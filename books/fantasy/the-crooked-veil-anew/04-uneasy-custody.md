## 4. Uneasy Custody

### Adric

I caught her on the third night, in the fishmongers' quarter, because I was the only inquisitor in Vessengard who thought to look where the poor actually went.

The others had spent two days kicking down doors in the silk districts, on the theory that anything miraculous must be expensive. High Inquisitor Serane had given me a list of names, and I had crossed off every name that owned more than one pair of boots. What remained was a short list of short lives: charm-sellers, hedge-healers, one woman who told fortunes out of a doorway on Candle Lane and charged less than the candle cost.

Silas Okafor. Scribe, third rank, indentured to the Temple of Ledgers for — I checked twice — another eleven years. A debt old enough to vote. Her file was thin as a wafer: no heresies, no fines, one cautionary note about "unlicensed blessings," which in Concord language meant she'd once made an old woman's knees stop hurting and hadn't charged enough for it.

I found her at a soup line near the tanneries, hood up, holding a bowl like it might be confiscated. When I stepped into her path and showed her my badge, she looked at it the way you'd look at a dead rat in your bread.

"Adric Vale," I said. "Inquisitor, second circle."

"I know who you are." Her voice was flat and tired and entirely unafraid, which unsettled me more than screaming would have. "You've been following me since the market. You walk loudly, inquisitor."

"I don't—"

"You have the particular tread of a man who has been taught to announce himself to rooms. It's very pious." She set down the bowl, unfinished. That bothered me. The soup was free, and she clearly needed it. "Am I under arrest?"

And here is the thing I have not confessed to anyone but this page: I did not know. My orders said bring in anyone connected to the miracle at the night market. The miracle had happened six feet from her stall; a beggar named Tam Wicke had prayed to a god with no name and stood up out of his own dying, and forty witnesses would swear to it, and witnesses were the one thing the Concord could not burn.

All I had to do was say yes.

"Detention pending inquiry," I heard myself say instead. "You'll come with me. Quietly."

Her eyes narrowed. She was doing arithmetic — I watched her do it, lips slightly parted, weighing alleys and odds and whether I was the kind of man who chased. Whatever sum she reached, she didn't like any of the answers.

"And if I run?"

"Then I chase you, and I'm better at it than I look, and you'll be dragged in by men who kick first and read files never." I paused. "Or you come with me and stay warm and fed until the inquiry decides what you are."

"What am I, then?"

"That," I said, "is what I intend to find out."

She studied me for a long moment, and whatever she saw — I still don't know what — made her sigh the sigh of a woman agreeing to a second-rate bargain.

"All right," she said. "Custody. But I want it noted that I'm coming voluntarily."

"Noted."

"And that your tread really is very loud."

### Silas

Here is what nobody tells you about being arrested by a doubting man: it's boring, and then it's strange.

Inquisitor Vale did not take me to the Bastion. He took me — I want to stress how absurd this sentence is — to a boarding house above a chandler's shop in Rope Walk, paid the landlady in advance for a week, and told her I was his cousin from the provinces, recovering from a fever. Then he locked the door from the outside and sat down against it in the hallway, where I could hear him not sleeping all night.

A cousin. From the provinces. Recovering from a fever. I lay in the narrow bed cataloguing everything wrong with the arrangement. Inquisitors did not rent rooms. Inquisitors did not pay in advance. Inquisitors did not buy their prisoners bread and honey on the first morning and knock before entering, which he did, every time, even though the door only opened outward into his custody.

On the third morning I decided to find out why.

"You're supposed to have taken me to the Bastion by now," I said, over the honey bread, which was good, which was another wrong thing.

"The inquiry is backlogged."

"You wrote the inquiry. Your handwriting is on the intake form you keep 'losing.'"

He had the decency to look briefly caught. Up close, Adric Vale had the face of a man built for standing in doorways — long, watchful, arranged around a permanent suspicion of himself. "There are questions about your case," he said.

"There are always questions about cases. That's what makes them cases."

"Not these questions." He turned his cup in his hands. He had large hands, ink-stained at the edges — a scribe's stain, or something close to it. "The god at the market. Ashen-Mother. She answered a prayer through a woman with no temple, no license, no training. Do you know what the Concord teaches about that?"

I knew exactly what the Concord taught, because I had copied it out four hundred times as part of my debt: *the gods are dead, slain in the Purification; all voices claiming divinity are fraud, fever, or fiend.* Chapter One, Verse One, the founding lie with the founding smell.

"That she's a fraud, a fever, or a fiend," I recited. "In ascending order of usefulness to the Bastion."

Something flickered across his face. Not amusement. Recognition, maybe — the look of a man hearing his own private heresy read aloud by a stranger.

"Yes," he said quietly. "But Tam Wicke was dying. I attended the examination myself. His lungs were rotted to lace; three physicians signed the certificate, and physicians will sign anything except an inconvenient truth. And then he wasn't dying." He looked up at me. "Frauds don't mend lungs, Mistress Okafor. Fevers don't last. So the Concord is left with fiend — and I've seen fiends. They don't answer prayers either. They answer *prices*."

"And what did the old woman at my stall pay?" I asked, before I could stop myself. "For her knees. What price?"

The silence stretched between us like a rope bridge.

"You've done this before," he said at last. "More than the cautionary note says."

"My whole life is more than the note says." I put down the bread. Here was the moment to stop — to be small, to be a scribe with a cough, to let him decide I was nothing worth burning. Twelve years of practice said stop. But Ashen-Mother's voice was still coiled somewhere behind my ribs like a banked fire, and Tam Wicke was alive and walking somewhere in Vessengard, and I was so tired of being careful alone.

"Inquisitor," I said, "what did they tell you happened at the Purification? Exactly. Word for word."

"They told us the gods were slain."

"By whom?"

"The founders. The first Concord."

"With what weapon?" I leaned forward. "Think. You've recited the liturgy ten thousand times. There's a hole in it, isn't there? Right at the center, where the killing should be. They describe the gods' deaths in detail — the weeping of the rivers, the silence of the oracles. But they never once say *how*. Because there is no how. You can't kill a god, Adric. You can only lock the door on it and tell everyone the house burned down."

He had gone very still. Men trained to stillness go stiller than other men, but his stillness had cracks in it, and through the cracks I could see something enormous shifting — the sound of a lifetime's architecture deciding whether to fall.

"That's heresy," he said. Without heat. Almost as punctuation.

"It's cartography," I said. "The maps agree with me, if you know how to read them."

### Adric

She showed me the maps that night, by candle stub, on the floor of the boarding-house room while the landlady's cat supervised from the windowsill with the air of a magistrate.

Not real maps. Copies of copies — pages she'd copied during her years in the Temple archive, when the debt-keepers weren't looking, of charts older than the Concord. Coastlines of countries that no longer appeared in any atlas printed after the Purification. Rivers with no names. And in the middle of one yellowed page, a shape like a closed eye, annotated in a hand two centuries dead:

*Ilvaren. Sealed. Let sleeping lie.*

"There was a country here," she said, tapping the eye. "It's not on any map younger than sixty years. Not sunk, not conquered — just *gone*, the way a word goes when everyone agrees to stop saying it. Ask any scholar why Ilvaren isn't on the charts and they'll say Ilvaren never existed. Ask the oldest chartmakers and they'll change the subject and pour you more wine."

"Where did you get this?"

"I copied it myself, third year of my debt, from a folio the archivists thought was too damaged to matter." Her mouth curved — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Damaged things are the safest things in any archive. Nobody guards what they've already mourned."

I should have taken the pages. I should have written the report that night: subject in possession of proscribed materials, heretical doctrine, evidence of contact with proscribed entity. Serane would have had me promoted for it. Instead I sat on the floorboards of a rented room, committing treason in installments, and asked the question that ended my career before I knew it had begun:

"Why show me this? You don't know me. I could burn these and you with them."

She was quiet for a while. The cat knocked a bottle cap off the sill, unrepentant.

"Because you bought honey bread," she said finally. "And knocked. And because when I said the founding liturgy back at you, you flinched in exactly the place I flinch. There's a hole in you, inquisitor, shaped exactly like the hole in the liturgy. I figure either we're both going to be burned for it, or—" she shrugged— "we're both going to be burned for it, together, which is marginally better company."

It was the closest thing to a proposal of alliance I had ever received, and it was delivered like an insult, and I found — this is the part that shames me least, actually — I found I was glad.

"The Concord will come for you," I said. "Serane doesn't backlog inquiries. She buries them, and then she buries the inquirers. When she notices I've misplaced a veilkeeper—"

"Misplaced," Silas repeated, delighted despite herself. "That's what I am now. A filing error."

"A detained filing error. Under uneasy custody." I gathered the maps, squared their edges, handed them back to her rather than keeping them, which was its own confession. "We move tomorrow. There's a route south along the coast road, pilgrim traffic, poorly policed. If the door you think exists is anywhere, it's where the drowned country used to be."

She took the pages, and for a moment her fingers stayed on mine, ink-stain to ink-stain.

"You understand," she said, "that if you do this, there's no ledger clean enough to write your way back out of it."

"I've read enough ledgers to know they're all written in disappearing ink eventually."

Outside, far off toward the Bastion, a bell began to ring — the wrong bell, the alarm cadence, three and pause and three. The cat left the windowsill. Silas went white, and pressed her hand flat to her sternum, and said, in a voice that was hers and underneath it someone much, much older:

"She knows we're leaving."
