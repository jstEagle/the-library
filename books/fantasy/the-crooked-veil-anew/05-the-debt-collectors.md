## 5. The Debt Collectors

There is a particular sound a temple makes when it decides to sell you. It is not dramatic. No bells, no thunder, no choir of outraged clerics. It is the sound of a quill scratching on vellum, somewhere above your head, in an office you have never been permitted to enter, and it sounds exactly like every other quill scratch in Vessengard — which is to say, like nothing at all, until you understand what it means.

I learned this from a fishwife.

I had come down to the Saltquay market before dawn to buy eels with the last of Adric's forged coin — a detail I will return to, because it still embarrasses me that the most wanted woman in the city was financing her flight from religious tyranny with a disgraced inquisitor's pocket change — when Maren Codling grabbed my sleeve with hands that smelled of brine and forty years of haggling.

"They posted it at the Chapterhouse," she hissed. "Your name, girl. On the transfer board."

"The what?"

"Contract transfers. Temple of the Quiet Ledger sells its paper debts twice a year. Yours went up yesterday." She looked around, then lower, conspiratorial. "Bought within the hour. There's only one buyer who pays cash for scribes these days."

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the morning fog off the river.

The Concord didn't just want me now. They *owned* the right to want me. Somewhere in the temple where I'd spent twelve years copying psalms in a hand too small to be read across a room, some administrator I'd never met had signed away my name, my labor, and my body's obligation to be wherever my contract said it should be. And since my contract currently said I should be nowhere except in Concord custody — well.

You cannot steal yourself if someone else has already bought you. That's the elegant cruelty of debt. It converts a human being into property so smoothly that even the property starts believing it.

"Thank you, Maren," I said, and meant it, and hated that I meant it, because thanking people for warning you that you are hunted is not the life I had planned at any point in my thirty-one years.

"Give us a blessing sometime," she said gruffly, "when all this nonsense is done," and pushed a string of smoked eels into my hands without letting me pay, which was either kindness or bribery against future miracles, and with Maren it was honestly impossible to say.

* * *

Adric found me two streets later, moving through the crowd with the particular walk he'd developed since burning his career — half inquisitor, half fugitive, a man whose body hadn't yet decided which laws it answered to.

"You've heard," he said.

"I've heard."

"Serane filed the transfer personally. That means it isn't administrative anymore. It's—" He stopped himself, jaw working.

"A leash," I finished. "She wants a leash with paperwork attached. Very tidy. Very Concord."

"It's worse than that." He drew me into the shadow of a fish-drying rack, close enough that I could see the exhaustion carved under his eyes. Three days we'd been running the city's grey veins together, sleeping in shifts in a smuggler's loft over Tanners' Row, and he still flinched slightly whenever our arms touched, as though duty were a bruise he kept forgetting he had. "A contract purchase gives them legal authority to take you anywhere. Not just a cell. Serane can ship you to one of the quiet houses upcountry. Places where veilkeepers get studied instead of questioned."

"Studied."

"Silas." His voice dropped. "I've seen the reports from the quiet house at Marrowfen. You don't want to know. Trust me as a man who used to file them."

I looked at him for a long moment. Here was the strange arithmetic of my new life: I trusted a former inquisitor more than I trusted the temple that had raised me, fed me, taught me my letters, and sold me like a bolt of cloth.

"When do they move?" I asked.

"Dawn tomorrow. They'll come to the places you're known. Your stall. Your old room." He hesitated, and I watched something painful move behind his eyes. "And the scriptorium. They'll check the scriptorium first, actually. Brother Elam requested the assignment himself."

Ah. There it was.

Brother Elam. Seventy-two years old, dry as August grass, keeper of the archive, the man who had taught me shorthand and scripture and how to hide a charm's true wording inside an innocuous phrase. The man who had once covered for me three times when the temple auditors found irregularities in my stall inventory. The man whose cough I knew by heart and whose tea I had brewed every winter for eleven years.

He would give me up for absolution. I knew it the way you know weather coming — not from evidence but from the ache in old scars. Because Elam believed in the Concord. Not the way zealots believe, loudly and with fire. He believed the way tired men believe in roofs: the rain is real, the roof leaks less than the open sky, and anyone who pries at the shingles deserves what falls on them.

"He'll tell them about the loft on Tanners' Row," I said quietly. "He knows I'd go somewhere with a lock I could pick. He taught me locks."

Adric stared at me. "You're certain?"

"I'm certain he loves me," I said. "That's what makes it certain. Love with a roof over it always wins. Ask any god."

* * *

We ran anyway. Of course we ran. But we ran toward Tanners' Row first, because I am apparently the kind of fool who goes back for her good boots and a stolen relic shaped like a knucklebone, and by the time we reached the loft the sun was fully up and the trap was already sprung.

I knew it before we turned the corner. The street was too empty. Markets have rhythms the way hearts do, and this one was holding its breath.

"Back door," Adric breathed, and we cut through the tannery yard itself — through the stink and the lime pits and the stretched hides hanging like flayed ghosts — and came out onto the frozen canal just as the first shout went up behind us.

Then it was simply ugly.

I have read forty accounts of heroic escapes and I can tell you now that every one of them lies. There is nothing heroic about running on ice in borrowed shoes while men in grey livery crash through a fence behind you. There is only breath like knives in your throat and the terrible mathematics of footing, and Adric grabbing my arm hard enough to bruise when I went down, hauling me up without a word because neither of us had breath to waste on words.

They herded us east, whether by design or accident, out of the lanes and onto the long frozen reach of the Verren — the river districts, where the poor of Vessengard live their whole winters atop ice thick as temple floors, in warrens of shanties and smoke-holes built right on the frozen flood.

For one blessed mile I thought we might lose them among the shanty-labyrinths. Then a crossbow bolt shattered on the ice six feet ahead of us, and the message was clear: stop, or the next one won't miss, and the Concord's marksmen do not miss.

So we did the thing desperate people do. We went down.

Not onto the ice. Into it.

There is a world beneath the frozen Verren that the city pretends not to know about — culverts, old millraces, the drowned foundations of buildings from before the Concord, back when Vessengard had been built greedily right to the waterline. Smugglers called it the Cold Vein. Adric, to his everlasting usefulness, knew its entrances from his inquisitor days; the Concord mapped everything, including the maps everyone else pretended didn't exist.

We went in through a smoke-hole in a fish-smoking shed, down a ladder slick with rime, into air that tasted of stone and old water and darkness. Behind us, boots hammered the ice overhead. Ahead, black water moved slow between shelves of frost, lit by Adric's shuttered lantern in a glow no wider than a prayer.

"This way leads under the Chapterhouse," he whispered, which was either brilliant or insane, and we followed it anyway, wading thigh-deep through channels that froze my legs into two wooden grievances, climbing over collapsed beams furred with ice like white fur, once freezing entirely — both of us, pressed flat into a niche — while a patrol crossed a grate overhead and their lamplight fell through the bars in long golden stripes across the dark water, close enough to touch.

In that light I saw Adric's face, and he saw mine, and there was no room in that moment for anything but the fact of each other. Two people crouched in a hole beneath the city that had made them both, wanted by it, soaked to the ribs in its coldest blood.

"My boots," I whispered, absurdly, when the light passed, "are ruined," and he laughed — a cracked, half-frozen, entirely genuine laugh — and I laughed too, helplessly, until it hurt, because grief and laughter live in the same chest and only one of them can breathe at a time.

* * *

We surfaced after nightfall in the cellar of a deconsecrated shrine in the Weft district, a place where the walls still bore scratched prayers from before the purges — little invocations to gods with names like Ashen-Mother, scraped but legible, patient as seeds.

I sat wrapped in a smuggler's blanket, drinking broth Adric heated over a candle-stub, and I grieved.

Not dramatically. Grief, I have found, is mostly domestic. I grieved for Maren's stall and the smell of frying dough at the night market. For Elam's cough and the ritual of his winter tea, which I would never brew again, because the man who drank it had chosen a roof over me and I could not even hate him properly for it. For the small, shabby, survivable life of compromises I had built — selling harmless charms, keeping my head down, paying a debt that was never mine in coin that was never enough — and which was gone now, burned off me like fog, and would never come back no matter how this ended.

Twelve years of being careful, and it had taken one dying beggar's answered prayer to unmake me.

"You're allowed to mourn it," Adric said. He sat across the candle from me, still damp, watching me with that careful attention he thought I didn't notice.

"Mourning implies it was worth keeping."

"Silas."

"It wasn't," I admitted, to the broth, to the scratched prayers, to whatever was listening behind the veil these days. "But it was *mine*. Every compromised inch of it. And they didn't even leave me the dignity of choosing to lose it. They just — purchased me. Signed a line and turned a person into paper." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it, once, briefly, the way you crack a window in winter: just enough to keep the room from suffocating.

Adric reached across and took my cold hand in his colder one, and neither of us remarked on it, and it was the warmest thing in the cellar.

"Then we make sure the signing costs them," he said.

I was still holding his hand, still cataloguing all the small things I'd lost and the one large thing I seemed to be finding, when the relic spoke.

The knucklebone — the stolen god-bit I'd carried since the raid, silent for weeks — warmed against my breastbone and pulsed, once, twice, like a second heart learning its rhythm. And through it, faint as a voice through deep water, came the Ashen-Mother's knowing, vast and unhurried:

*They come at moonset, little scribe. And your brother Elam walks beside them — weeping.*

Outside, above the shrine, the clouds were tearing open, and the moon was already leaning west.
