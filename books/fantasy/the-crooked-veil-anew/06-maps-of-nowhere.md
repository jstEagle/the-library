## 6. Maps of Nowhere

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the Concord's archive of confiscated maps, and I have always suspected it was deliberate. You cannot erase a country without erasing the sound of it too — the gulls, the market bells, the argumentative clatter of a harbor at dawn. The archivists cut Ilvaren out of every atlas with the same shears they used on heretical psalters, and what remains in that room is the hush of a wound.

I stood in it for three hours on the night I decided to become a traitor.

Not that I called it treason yet. Treason has a shape, a weight, a smell of hot iron. What I had then felt more like curiosity with a criminal streak — the same feeling, I imagine, that leads otherwise sensible men to lean over well shafts. Silas Okafor had been gone from my custody for six days, and every rational part of me should have filed the report that ended her. Instead I had spent those days learning how thoroughly the Concord had lied to me, and lying to myself about why I cared.

The map room kept its secrets badly, if you knew where to press. Under the floorboards where the oldest atlases were stored — the ones too dangerous to burn because burning them would admit they'd mattered — I found a coastal survey dated two years before the Purge. Ilvaren rendered in loving detail: a peninsula of grey hills and black harbors, its cities named, its roads drawn like veins. And across the whole of it, stamped diagonally in red wax, the seal of the First Concord: *REMOVED BY RESOLUTION NINE.*

Removed. Not sunk. Not drowned by divine wrath, as the catechism taught. Removed — the way you remove a stain, or a witness.

I sat down on the cold floor among dead cartographers' dreams and laughed until it turned into something else.

* * *

Forging travel papers is easier than people think, provided you have spent eleven years forging confession records, which I had. The trick is never to make anything too clean. A genuine document has coffee rings and hesitation marks; a forgery that gleams might as well be wearing a sign. So I aged my merchant's writ with tea, smudged the seal with my thumb, and misspelled one word — *Vessengard* becoming *Vessengaurd*, the error of a tired clerk near midnight. Inquisitors are trained to spot perfection. Imperfection waves them past like an old friend.

I did this work at my own desk, in the Hall of Inquiry itself, which strikes me now as either very bold or very stupid. Both, probably. Boldness and stupidity are conjoined twins; you rarely meet one alone.

The papers said I was Aldous Merrin, wool factor, traveling north to inspect flocks before winter closed the passes. They said nothing about a scribe with stolen relics and a god's voice in her mouth, because documents are honest that way even when their authors aren't.

It was while the tea was still drying on the third page that Serane found me.

High Inquisitor Maren Serane does not knock. Doors open for her the way mouths open for bad news. She stood in my office doorway with a lantern in one hand — unnecessary, since my lamp was lit — and looked at the papers spread across my desk with the expression of a woman reading a letter she'd already intercepted.

"Factor Merrin," she said. "How's the wool trade?"

"Volatile," I said. My heart was performing some kind of drum solo. "Prices are up."

"So I see." She came in and closed the door behind her, which was worse than leaving it open. Closed doors in the Hall of Inquiry mean one of two things: promotion or disappearance. "Sit down, Vale."

"I am sitting."

"Don't be clever. It doesn't suit you half as well as you think." She set the lantern down and studied my forged papers the way a jeweler studies paste. "You've been busy. Six days chasing a runaway through districts you have no jurisdiction in. Requesting archive access under three separate pretexts. And now this." She tapped the writ. "Tell me what you think you've found."

The correct answer was *nothing, High Inquisitor, a clerical confusion.* I heard myself say instead: "Ilvaren."

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. That frightened me more than anger would have.

"Say that again," she said quietly.

"The country that sank. Resolution Nine. Except it didn't sink, did it? Sinking leaves wreckage. Fishermen find wreckage. There's no wreckage because there was no sinking. You folded it away like a tablecloth after dinner." I stood, because sitting suddenly felt like surrender. "And the gods you burned us all for worshipping — you didn't destroy them either. You locked the door and threw away the maps."

For a long moment Serane simply looked at me, and I understood that I had just done something irreversible. There are doors that only swing one direction. I'd walked through one, and she was standing on the other side holding the lantern.

"You want the truth?" she said at last. "Fine. Sit down anyway. Your legs are going to want the option."

I sat.

* * *

What she told me took an hour, and I will spend the rest of my life wishing it had taken less.

The Concord's founders hadn't destroyed magic. They couldn't. What they had done — what Resolution Nine actually said, in language so dense with euphemism it took me years of training not to notice it said nothing — was negotiate. The old gods agreed to withdraw behind a door, and the veilmakers of Ilvaren, who had built their whole civilization on hearing the divine, were sealed inside with them. Hostages, or volunteers; the records disagreed. In exchange, the new nations got order. No miracles, no plagues of prophecy, no kings claiming heaven's mandate. Just law. Clean, human, accountable law.

"It worked," Serane said. "For thirty years, it worked. Do you know what Vessengard was before? Half the city speaking in tongues, the other half selling indulgences to stop the tongues. Children born marked. Rivers that changed course when someone important died. We bought peace, Vale. It cost a country. I've made my peace with the price."

"And now prayers are being answered in the night market."

"Now prayers are being answered," she agreed, "and the door is weakening, and in a few weeks the long winter comes and seals it for another century whether we like it or not. Which brings us to you." She leaned forward. "You found the veilkeeper. You lost the veilkeeper. And instead of reporting your failure, you're forging papers to chase her to the coast. So here is my offer, and understand that I am making it because you were the best student I ever trained, and it will pain me to hang you."

The word *hang* landed somewhere below my ribs and stayed there.

"Bring her in," Serane said. "Alive, willing, unharmed. The Concord needs a veilkeeper the way it needed the veilmakers — to maintain the seal from our side. Give her to me, and your little expedition becomes an authorized mission. Papers, escort, commendation. Refuse—" she spread her hands, an elegant shrug over an inelegant future, "—and tomorrow morning you're a fugitive with excellent forgery skills and no institution behind you. She'll run farther with you than without you, I'll grant. But you'll both die running, and I'll have gained nothing but paperwork."

I looked at her — at the woman who had taught me that doubt was a tool, that certainty was armor, that the Concord's lies were the load-bearing kind and therefore holy — and I asked the question I should have asked years ago.

"You've known. All of you at the top. The gods live, the country exists, the whole catechism is a management strategy. And you let people burn for believing it anyway."

"People don't burn anymore," Serane said. "That's rather the point. The pyres stopped when the truth started. You can hate the arithmetic, but the arithmetic held." She rose, straightened her coat, and paused at the door. "You have until dawn, Vale. Bring me the girl, or become the kind of man this chapter of history doesn't record."

The door closed. The lantern stayed, burning her oil on my desk, like a small accusation.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat with the forged papers and the stolen survey and Serane's offer arranged around me like the points of a triangle, and I tried to find the corner where I could stand and still be the man I'd been at breakfast.

Here is what I kept returning to: Silas Okafor owed the temple twelve years of her name, and paid them, and wanted nothing except to sell small charms and stay invisible. She wasn't a zealot. She hadn't sought the gods; they'd fallen on her like weather. And the moment she became useful, the institution I served decided she was inventory — a tool to maintain a lock built on a buried country. Serane hadn't even pretended otherwise. *Give her to me.* Not save her. Not free her. *Give.*

I thought about the ledger entry with her family's name in it, ink brown with age, the word *forfeit* written in a hand that hadn't trembled. I thought about her face in the tenement stairwell, lit by my own lantern, watching me decide what she was worth.

Dawn came grey through the shutters. I gathered the papers, the survey, the relic-sighting reports I'd falsified to buy her time, and I left the lantern burning on my desk — let them read whatever they liked into that.

At the Hall's gate, the duty warden asked my business. "Wool factor," I said. "Merrin. North before the passes close."

He waved me through. Somewhere behind me, Serane was already learning I hadn't come to her, and the machinery of my old life was beginning to turn against me with the smooth, patient efficiency of everything I'd once believed in.

The road north ran along the frozen river districts, past the stalls where Silas used to sell her harmless charms, past the temple whose debt had owned her. At the last crossroads before the coast road, I stopped and looked back once at Vessengard — its spires, its smoke, its clean lawful silence — and felt the strange lightness of a man setting down something heavy he'd mistaken for his spine.

Then I turned north, toward a country that wasn't supposed to exist, carrying forged papers, a stolen map, and the growing, terrifying understanding that I wasn't following a fugitive anymore.

I was following her. And the difference between those two things was going to get me killed — or saved, depending entirely on whether she opened the door.
