## 7. The Drowned Country

The sea at Greyharrow was the color of old pewter, and it did not crash so much as mutter.

I had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road north — Adric's forged papers in my coat, Ashen-Mother's relic warm against my sternum like a second heart. In every imagining, I crested the dunes and saw the drowned towers of Ilvaren breaking the waves, all that swallowed grandeur visible through green water. I would stand witness to the Concord's greatest crime and feel, if nothing else, vindicated.

What I actually felt, standing ankle-deep in freezing foam, was the distinct sensation of being lied to by geography itself.

Because there was nothing there. No towers. No spires. No sunken rooftops furred with weed. Just grey water running out to a grey horizon under a sky the color of unwashed wool, empty as a Concord hymnal.

"Adric," I said, not turning around. "If this is your idea of a romantic holiday, I want my papers refunded."

He came up beside me, boots hissing in the wet sand. Three weeks on the road had worn him down to something leaner than an inquisitor — stubble gone ragged, oath-medallion traded for a fisherman's oilskin he wore like an apology. But his eyes still did that thing where they catalogued everything and confessed nothing.

"The maps say it sank," he said. "Fourteen sources. Two of them pre-Concord."

"And how often have fourteen sources been right about anything the Concord wanted buried?"

He didn't answer, because we both knew. The Concord's history had more revisions than a tax code.

* * *

We'd come down to the shore at dusk because the cartographer's letter — the one Adric had paid half his remaining coin for in a coastal tavern reeking of herring and despair — insisted the veil thinned at twilight. *Look for where the gulls won't go,* the old man had written, in a hand that shook with either age or fear. *Birds know what maps don't.*

The gulls knew. That much was true. They wheeled and screamed along the whole coastline and then, at a line you could almost see if you squinted — a seam in the air, a wrinkle in the light about two hundred paces offshore — they turned back as one, like courtiers refusing to cross a threshold.

"There," I said.

Adric saw it too. I watched him try very hard not to see it, the way he'd spent three weeks trying very hard not to say certain words aloud. *Veilkeeper. Gods alive. Treason.* We'd developed a whole vocabulary of silence between us, and it said more than most marriages.

"Ilvaren didn't sink," I said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water filling a boot. "It was never in the water at all."

"They folded it out." His voice was flat, but I'd learned to hear the strain beneath the flatness, the way you hear wind behind a closed door. "When they sealed the door. They didn't just lock the gods away. They took the whole country with them. Every soul in it."

Every soul in it. I stood there doing arithmetic no one should have to do — a nation's worth of farmers, children, bakers, priests, all of them erased from the world the way you'd erase a ledger line that embarrassed you. And everyone on our side of the veil had agreed to forget. Had *been made* to agree. The maps were redacted; the songs had their verses cut out; the histories simply stopped mentioning a country that had, until one terrible generation ago, existed.

I thought of my mother teaching me letters from a primer with a page torn out. I'd always assumed she'd done it herself.

Maybe she hadn't.

"We can't swim it," Adric said. "Can't row it. There's nothing to row to. It's not distance, Silas. It's—" He gestured helplessly at the seam of wrongness hanging over the water. "It's a *not*."

"It's a door," I corrected quietly. "And doors open for people who knock."

* * *

Ashen-Mother had been quiet since the coast road — suspiciously quiet, in the way of gods and cats before pouncing. Now, as the last light failed and the seam offshore began to glow faintly, like a crack in the underside of the sky, she spoke up inside my skull with the warmth of a banked fire and the patience of someone who has waited a hundred years and intends to wait exactly no longer.

*Knock,* she said.

"With what? My knuckles? Against *what*?"

*With what you are. Walk out until the water takes your knees. The veil knows its keepers.*

Adric caught my arm. "Whatever you're hearing — whatever she's telling you to do — think first. Please. For once in this whole mad pilgrimage, think first."

"I am thinking first," I said. "That's the problem. I've been thinking first for twelve years, Adric. Thinking first is how a person ends up selling lucky copper pennies in a night market while her name sits in a temple ledger accruing interest." I looked at his hand on my arm, then at his face, which was doing its level best to remain an inquisitor's face and failing beautifully. "I'm going to walk into the sea now. If I drown, avenge me badly and blame the weather."

"That's not funny."

"No," I agreed. "But it's mine."

The water took my knees, then my waist, and it was cold enough to stop a heart — except mine wasn't stopping. It was speeding up. Because the closer I came to the seam, the more the world around me began to *pool*. That's the only word I have for it. Time went strange, thickened, ran slow and then fast and then sideways. A wave froze mid-curl beside my shoulder like a sculpture of itself. Somewhere behind me Adric shouted my name, and the shout stretched out into a long low note, a bell rung underwater.

And then the seam opened.

Not dramatically. Doors that matter never do. It simply became apparent that the space in front of me was no longer water and sky, but a shoreline — a real one, with dark sand and pale grass and hills rising inland under a sky that held two moons instead of one.

Two moons. I stood hip-deep in the surf of a country that had been deleted, and I looked up at a stranger's sky, and I laughed until the salt water stung my throat.

* * *

Ilvaren's beaches were littered with ships.

That was the first thing I understood about the place, wading ashore with my skirts frozen into armor: the Concord hadn't just folded the country away — they'd folded it away *mid-flight*. Fishing boats sat beached mid-haul, nets still trailing. A merchant vessel lay canted against the rocks with cargo crates burst open, spilling cloth that might once have been crimson and was now the grey of everything here, colors leached like the country itself had been left out in the rain for a century.

Because that's what it had been. Left out. Not destroyed — *abandoned*, sealed outside time like furniture under a sheet.

Except the sheet had holes.

I walked up the beach past the ghost fleet, and the strangeness pooled around me. Here, a patch of sand where grass grew and died and grew again in the space of my three steps, seasons cycling like a shuffling deck. There, a stretch of shore where rain fell upward. A child's wooden horse sat in the dunes, and when I touched it, I heard — faintly, from nowhere — a girl laughing, decades dead or decades delayed, I couldn't tell which.

"This is what they took," I whispered, and Ashen-Mother answered from inside my ribs:

*This is what they took from everyone. What they took from you is smaller. But you will find it weighs the same.*

Ahead, the land rose into hills, and in the hills stood a city. I could see it clearly even in the failing light: walls, towers, a harbor craned and silent. Ilvaren's capital, whole and hollowed, its windows dark as eye sockets.

And above the city, gathering like storm clouds that had decided to grow faces — the gods.

I don't know how else to describe them. They weren't figures. They were *weathers*. Pressure systems of presence, vast and slow-turning, each one carrying its own gravity. Ashen-Mother rose off my chest at last, unspooling into the sky like smoke finding its proper altitude, and joined them. Her relief was a physical ache, like a tooth finally pulled after years.

*They are waiting for you, little keeper,* she said, her voice thinner at a distance. *A hundred years of exile, and they are waiting for one girl who sells charms in a night market.*

"One scribe," I corrected. "With excellent penmanship."

Behind me, Adric came through the seam at a stumbling run, soaked, gasping, medallion-less and lawless and utterly out of his depth. He stopped when he saw the city. When he saw the sky. I watched the last of the Concord drain out of him — not the doubt, which had been leaking for months, but the *hope* that his order might still be saved, might be argued with, might be anything other than what built this empty harbor.

"Gods alive," he breathed. Then, wryly, because he'd been spending too long in my company: "Apparently literally."

"Welcome to Ilvaren," I said. "Population: pending."

We climbed toward the hills together, and the pooling time thickened until every step forward felt like walking through honey and memory. And as we climbed, the gathered gods turned their attention downward — all of them at once, the weight of a hundred years of exile settling onto my shoulders like a mantle made of lead and gratitude.

One voice rose above the rest, older than Ashen-Mother, colder than the winter coming behind us. It spoke without sound, directly into the marrow of me, and what it said was this:

*The door opens at the turn of the season, keeper. Or it seals for another century. Choose quickly — because the Concord has chosen already, and they are bringing the winter with them.*

Far to the south, beyond the veil, beyond the grey coast, I felt it begin: a cold that was not weather, rolling north like an army.

The long winter had started early.
