## 8. Winter's Bargain

The snow came wrong.

I want to be clear about that, because everything else that night was merely terrible, and the snow was *wrong*. It fell upward first — a slow silver exhalation off the veiled hills of Ilvaren, rising past us into a sky the color of old pewter, hanging there, refusing to fall. Then it changed its mind all at once, the way a mob changes its mind, and came down in sheets so fine and dry they didn't melt on skin. They just sat there. Accumulating. Like the world had decided to keep receipts.

"Concord work," Adric said. He stood at the edge of the drowned-not-drowned harbor with his collar up, watching the flakes settle on the black water without dimpling it. "Weather-seeding. It's in the oldest inquisitorial texts — the Rite of Long Winter. I always thought it was a fable they used to scare novices about the dangers of ambition." He laughed, short and joyless. "Turns out it was a supply list."

"How long?" I asked.

"If they're doing it properly? A century. Maybe more. Cold enough, long enough, and the door freezes shut on its own. No army needed. No inquisitors storming anything." He glanced at me. "They just wait for winter to do their killing for them. It's very economical. The Concord loves economical."

We had been inside Ilvaren for six days, and I still hadn't gotten used to the light. It came from everywhere and nowhere, a pale gold suffusion, like the last hour of sunset stretched into permanence. Time pooled here — you could see it pooling, actually see it, in the hollows between cobbles, in the doorways of houses whose owners had walked out a generation ago and left their bread on the tables. The gods gathered above the ruined capital like storm clouds with opinions. Ashen-Mother was the loudest. She was also, I was learning, the most like my grandmother: loving, enormous, and absolutely certain she knew what was best for me.

That night she came to me while Adric slept by a fire that burned without wood — one of the small lawless mercies of a country where magic had never been outlawed, only exiled.

*Little scribe,* she said, in the voice of banked coals. *Come walk with me.*

"I'm tired," I said, which was true, and also a stalling tactic, because when a god asks you to go walking, the walk is never the point.

*Tired is a debt you owe your body. You know about debts.* The gold light bent around her as she moved through the ruined square — a shape like a woman made of hearth-smoke and patience. *So let us speak plainly, as debtors do. The winter comes. In a month, perhaps two, this door will be sealed for another hundred years. My siblings and I will go back to being a rumor behind a wall. And you, Silas Okafor, will go back to selling charms in a market that hates what you are.*

"Cheerful forecast."

*I have watched you since the beggar in the market. I have watched you carry a debt that was never yours, pay it year after year with your name and your years, and ask nothing.* Her smoke-shape settled before me. *Here is what I offer, and I offer it once. Open the door alone. Not with him — he is Concord, whatever he pretends, and the door knows it. Open it alone, and the debt dies with the veil. Your family's contract, unmade. Your name returned to you whole. And more: you would be our keeper no longer but our equal. A woman who walks out of this country owning herself and everything she touches.*

The offer landed in me like a coin dropped down a well. I listened for the splash and heard nothing, which frightened me more than the offer did.

"And Adric?" I asked.

*Adric chose his order once already.*

"He unchose it."

*Then he can choose again, outside, in a world where you will be a legend and he will be a traitor, and see how long love survives being hunted together.* The coals in her voice banked lower, gentler. *I am not cruel, little scribe. I am old. And old things know that freedom offered halfway is freedom withheld. Come alone to the door.*

She withdrew, and the gold light went ordinary, and I stood in a dead queen's square with snow sitting on my shoulders like a judgment, and I did not sleep either.

* * *

Adric's account of that night, which he gave me much later and which I set down here because he'd want it counted, goes something like this:

He woke to the fire burning wrong — too steady, too gold — and knew the Mother had been to her. He'd spent twelve years reading people for the Concord; he could read a god's fingerprints on a person now the way he used to read forged seals. So he got up, and he found Silas standing in the square with snow that wouldn't melt on her, looking like a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

He didn't ask what the god said. He'd been an inquisitor long enough to know that asking makes people lie. Instead he said, "Run with me."

"That's not an answer to anything," she said.

"It's an answer to everything. Listen." He stepped closer, and the snow hung suspended between them. "There's a fishing village forty miles south of the grey coast. Pre-veil maps show a sea cave under the headland that doesn't appear on any Concord survey. We go tonight, before the winter closes the passes. There's a boat. We cross to the Free Isles, where nobody's ever heard of the Concord or its debts, and we find some town where nobody knows our faces, and you sell honest charms and I mend nets, and in twenty years we'll be the strange old couple everyone suspects and no one reports."

"You've thought about this."

"Every night since the river districts," he said. "Every night since I watched you bleed on the ice rather than go back to them." His voice did the thing it did rarely — dropped its officer's polish and went rough underneath. "Silas. The gods are offering you a crown made of a door. I'm offering you a leaky boat and the rest of your life. I know which one sounds bigger. But I've seen what belonging to something large costs. I paid it for twelve years. Belonging to someone — that's the only membership I've ever wanted."

And she looked at him — this is how he told it, and I believe him because I remember my side of it too — and for one whole minute neither of them spoke, and the snow hung in the air around them like a held breath, and the door in the mountain beneath the city hummed its low note that they were both beginning to hear in their sleep.

"The gods say the door won't open for two," she finally said. "It knows you. It knows what you were."

"I know what I was." He took her hands. They were cold; he was always cold now; the north of the world was coming down on us like a lid. "I'm asking if you know what I am."

"That's the problem," Silas said, and her voice cracked on it, wry even in breaking, because that's how she grieves — sideways, with a joke as a splint. "I do know. That's why this is impossible. Because if I open it alone, I get everything — my name, my freedom, them — and I lose you. And if I run with you, I keep you, and I spend another century listening to gods knock on a door I could have opened. Either way I pay. I have paid my whole life, Adric. I am so tired of being the currency."

"Then don't choose tonight," he said. "Choose tomorrow. Choose at the door. Just—" and here his duty and his love finally stopped fighting each other and shook hands, "—just don't choose *without* me. Whatever you decide. Let me be in the room when you decide it. That's all I'm asking. That's the whole bargain I have to offer."

Above them, the false winter thickened. Somewhere south, beyond the veil's edge, inquisitorial engines were breathing cold into the sky, and High Inquisitor Serane was writing orders in a hand as neat as frost.

* * *

Morning came gray and early, though morning hardly meant anything anymore under a sun that couldn't be bothered to rise properly. The gods gathered on the broken terraces of the old palace, all of them — Ashen-Mother and the Tide-Father, the twins of seed and salt, the nameless one who was only a warm spot in the air. They had stopped pretending to be subtle. Storm clouds with opinions, all of them, and every opinion aimed at me.

Adric stood at my shoulder. He hadn't mentioned the night before. Neither had I. But he was there, which was the entire content of his argument, restated.

*The winter quickens,* Ashen-Mother said. *You feel it. Decide, little scribe. Alone and free, or not at all.*

And I opened my mouth to give some answer — I genuinely don't know which one, I think I might have surprised us all — when the Tide-Father, who had been silent for six days, turned his attention toward the southern edge of the veil and said, in a voice like a hull taking water:

*They're early. The Concord is through the outer mark. Inquisitors, in white, carrying cold in iron cages.*

Adric went very still beside me. "Serane," he said. "She didn't wait for the winter to finish. She's storming the veil now — while the door is still soft enough to break from either side."

Below us, at the far edge of the folded country, the horizon filled with torchlight moving through snow — dozens of lights, orderly as a ledger, coming to end the age of gods one more time.

Ashen-Mother looked down at me, and for the first time since the night market, the great patient hearth of her sounded afraid.

*Little scribe,* she said. *The door is open to you now — but only for hours, not days. Choose.*
