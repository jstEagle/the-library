## 10. Anew

### Silas

The first thing magic did when it came home to Vessengard was ruin the bread.

I mean that almost literally. The bakeries along Cordwainer Row woke to find their ovens lit by nothing at all, their dough rising with ambitions no yeast should have, and one poor woman's sourdough attempt climbed out of its pan and had to be talked down from the rafters. By noon there were a hundred small miracles and a hundred small disasters, and no one could tell which was which, because that is what lawless means — it doesn't mean wicked. It means nobody's holding the leash.

Including me. Especially me.

I stood in the wreckage of my old market corner three days after the door opened, watching a charm-seller — a real one now, no more pretending her blessings were "novelty keepsakes" — sell luck to a queue that stretched past the fishmonger's. Twelve years I'd kept my head down on that exact cobblestone. Twelve years of harmless trinkets and whispered prayers I pretended not to hear. Now the Concord's seal was scraped off the temple doors like wax off a tablecloth, and the city smelled different. Like rain before it falls. Like a held breath let out.

"They're saying the High Inquisitor's finished," said a voice behind me. Old Marta, who'd bought fever-charms from me back when buying them could get us both drowned in the harbor. She looked older than grief and younger than she had in a decade. "Serane. They dragged her out of the Spire this morning. Her own people, if you can believe it. Half of them couldn't swear the oath fast enough."

I could believe it. Serane had built the Concord's lie with her own hands — *the gods are dead, magic is a crime, silence is safety* — and when the gods walked back into the world wearing the faces of storm clouds and drowned countries, a lie that size doesn't shatter quietly. It collapses inward. Takes its architect with it.

"What happens to her?" I asked, though I wasn't sure I wanted the answer.

"Trial," Marta said, and spat, delicately, the way Vessengard women do. "If you can call it that. Half the city wants her hanged. The other half remembers her putting soup kitchens in the river districts." She shrugged. "Vessengard never met a villain it didn't also want to forgive."

I thought about the frozen river districts. About Brother Emeric, my mentor, who sold my location for absolution and got neither — the Ashen-Mother found him first, and what she gave him wasn't forgiveness but something stranger: memory. Every prayer he'd ever smothered, played back in his head until he understood what he'd been burying all those years. He wept for two days, they say, and then started teaching children their letters again, only now the letters included the ones that used to be forbidden. That was the pattern everywhere, once the veil tore. Not vengeance. Reckoning. The gods weren't interested in punishment. They'd been locked away a century; they just wanted to be *counted* again.

I touched the place where my debt-mark used to sit, below my collarbone. It had been a brand of ink and binding-words, renewed every year on the anniversary of my family's fall — twelve little strokes for twelve years of servitude, one more than I could count without flinching. When I paid the door with my bound years, it hadn't burned away or bled. It had simply become skin. Ordinary, unclaimed skin.

Mine. All of it mine.

"You're staring at nothing," Marta observed.

"I'm staring at everything," I said. "It's very distracting."

### Adric

An oath is a strange thing to break. You spend years believing it's load-bearing — that if you pull it out, the whole structure of yourself comes down — and then you pull it, and discover the building stands fine without it. Better, even. Fewer drafts.

They made me do it publicly. I hadn't planned on the public part; I'd planned on resigning my commission in writing, which is the coward's method and would have suited me. But the morning after Serane's arrest, the Concord's remnant council convened in the great hall of the Spire — what was left of it, anyway, after the Ashen-Mother's passing had cracked the eastern wall from foundation to bell tower, a wound nobody proposed repairing — and someone put my name forward as the example.

"The inquisitor Vale," said Councilor Hesse, an old man whose voice sounded like paper being folded, "stood beside the veilkeeper when she unmade the Concord's greatest work. Let him stand before us now and say what he chooses."

There were perhaps four hundred people in that hall. There were also, I noted with the detachment of a man watching his own execution, several gods pressed against the high windows like moths — vast, patient shapes of ash and tide, come to see how the mortals would handle it.

So much for the written resignation.

"I choose Silas Okafor," I said. My voice carried better than I expected. Probably divine assistance; the gods have always appreciated theater. "Not instead of my oath. Instead of the lie the oath was built on. I swore to protect Vessengard from sorcery, and I have learned that Vessengard was never in danger from sorcery. It was in danger from silence." I unpinned the silver quill — the inquisitor's badge, worn dull from a decade of polishing — and set it on the floor between myself and the council. "I break the oath. I keep the duty."

Old Hesse studied me for a long moment. Around the hall, hands drifted toward sword hilts and then, one by one, drifted away again. What was anyone going to do? Arrest treason? The jails had already been opened; half the prisoners had walked out through walls that had politely stopped being walls.

"Duty to whom?" Hesse asked.

"That," I said, "is the question I intend to spend the rest of my life answering honestly."

Later — that evening, on the steps of the Spire, with the cracked bell tolling a note it had never been tuned to make — Silas found me. She didn't say anything at first. She just sat down next to me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and together we watched the city argue with itself about miracles.

"You know they'll write songs about this," she said eventually. "The disgraced inquisitor and the last veilkeeper. Terrible songs. Wrong in every particular."

"Every particular?"

"You'll be taller."

I laughed — really laughed, which felt like another oath breaking, a good one. "And you'll be modest."

"Not even slightly. I earned the immodesty. I paid for it in bound years." She turned her hand over in the fading light, palm up, the way she did when she was checking for something that wasn't there anymore. "Adric. I'm going to Ilvaren."

I knew. Of course I knew. You don't walk out of a drowned country with its gods at your back and leave it empty forever. "When?"

"When the passes clear. A month, maybe less." A pause, the length of a woman deciding whether to ask a question she already knows the answer to. "Come with me."

Here is what I want recorded, since apparently history has taken an interest: she asked. She didn't assume, didn't presume, didn't present it as a settled thing. After everything — the custody pretense, the frozen river, the door, the fire and snow — she still understood that belonging can't be seized. Only offered.

"I resigned this morning," I said. "You think I did it for the pension?"

### Silas

Ilvaren in spring is a country apologizing for a century of absence.

The hills came back green first — impossibly, greedily green, as if the land had spent its hundred folded years dreaming of color and meant to spend it all at once. Then the towns, whole and dusty, their shutters still latched against an evacuation no living soul remembered. We found tables set for meals. Letters in drawers, mid-sentence. A child's chalk drawing on a schoolhouse slate: a stick figure waving at a shape that might have been a god, or might have been a dog. Time pools strangely here, Adric says. I think time mostly just *waited*, the way a dog waits at a door, and is now making up for lost enthusiasm.

We took a house in the hills above what had been the capital — a low stone thing with a crooked chimney and a view of the sea road, which runs now to actual places, on maps that actually admit Ilvaren exists. The cartographers are having the decade of their lives. So are the priests, though most of them arrived expecting to lead congregations and discovered instead that the gods prefer to conduct their own affairs, thank you, and that worship in Ilvaren looks less like kneeling and more like tending.

That's what I'm doing, these days. Tending.

We built the temple ourselves, which is to say we argued with a mason, hired the mason, and then did half the work badly while he corrected us. It's small. One room, a hearth, a door that locks from the inside only — I insisted on that detail — and no altar, because altars imply transactions, and transactions imply debts, and I have developed something of an allergy.

Above the door, carved in stone that the Ashen-Mother herself warmed into softness for the chisel, are words in old Ilvareni that translate roughly as: *Nothing owed. Everything given.* Underneath, smaller, because I insisted: *No exceptions.*

People come. Refugees from the Concord's fracture, mostly, and pilgrims, and a surprising number of former inquisitors looking for honest work, whom Adric interviews with a severity that fools absolutely no one. They bring questions, and offerings, and sometimes their debts — and here is the part I am still learning, the discipline of it: we don't take the debts. We take the people. There's a difference, and it took me twelve years and one apocalyptic winter to learn it, so I am patient with everyone else who hasn't yet.

Yesterday a woman came with her contract in her fist, the temple-stamped kind, the kind I used to carry. She laid it on our bare table and waited to be told what it would cost to burn it.

"Nothing," I said.

"Nothing?" She looked at me the way I must once have looked at a god with no temple — wary, hungry, waiting for the trap.

"It costs nothing," I said. "It cost *me*, but that account's closed. Yours starts clean." I fed the contract to the hearth and watched the binding-ink flare green, the way it does now, the way it will forever, and the woman cried, and I let her, because some things shouldn't be hurried.

Afterward I went out onto the hillside where the light was going long and gold, and found Adric there, ostensibly surveying a fence line, actually watching the sun set over a country that existed again. He didn't ask how it went inside. He'd heard the woman crying. He always hears.

"Well?" he said.

"She's staying. They mostly stay."

"And you? Still staying?" He asked it lightly, but I caught the thread under it — the same thread he'd been carrying since the Spire steps, since the frozen river, since a night market stall where a tired scribe sold him a luck-charm and accidentally sold him her whole future.

I leaned into him, shoulder to shoulder, the way I'd learned to on cold nights crossing country that was learning to exist. Below us the temple windows glowed, and beyond it the hills ran green all the way down to a sea that remembered its own name, and somewhere in the middle distance the Ashen-Mother moved like weather, keeping her patient watch over everything we hadn't managed to ruin.

"I'm staying," I said. "For the first time in my life, it isn't a sentence. It's a choice."

He took my hand — the ordinary, unmarked, entirely mine hand — and we watched the dark come up gentle over Ilvaren, and if the gods were watching too, well.

Let them. I've got nothing left to hide.
