## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

There are three rules to guiding a smuggler's caravan through the Thornfell passes, and I have broken all of them at least once, which is why I'm still alive to tell you about them.

Rule one: never take a road you can't walk blindfolded. Rule two: never trust a client who pays half up front and smiles too much about it. Rule three — and this is the one that matters — never, under any circumstances, look up.

I'm joking about that last one. Mostly. But there's a reason mountain folk say the Thornfell sky is none of your business. The peaks up there are old in a way that makes kings look like mayflies, and the stars above them have been watching longer than anything has had words to be watched with. You learn to keep your eyes on the trail, your hand on your blade, and your business strictly horizontal.

That night, of course, the sky made itself my business whether I liked it or not.

* * *

The caravan was nine wagons deep, hauling salt-spirit and dreamtobacco through the Widow's Tooth pass, and its owner was a soft-bellied man named Corvus Dain who smiled exactly as much as rule two warned me about. He'd paid half up front. He'd paid *generously* half up front. In my experience, generosity from a smuggler is like sweetness from a wolf: interesting, and best observed from a distance.

"Storm coming," he said, squinting at a sky that held nothing but clean cold stars. He said it for the third time that evening. Smugglers fill silence the way rivers fill ditches — constantly and without much thought.

"That's not a storm sky," I said.

"You're the guide." He said it lightly, but his eyes did something small and quick, the way they always did when he mentioned my role. Like he was reminding himself I was a tool he'd rented, and tools don't get curious.

I let it go. Curiosity is expensive in my trade, and I've spent enough on it over the years to know when to keep my purse closed. Instead I rode point, as I had since dusk, and let the road do what roads do — which, on the smuggler paths of Thornfell, is more than most people would believe.

You've heard the stories, probably. That the old smuggler roads remember. That if you walk them long enough, honestly enough, they'll glow faint where your boots fall, a pale seam of light like frost catching dawn. Most people think it's superstition told by drivers who've had too much salt-spirit of their own cargo. Those people have never walked the Widow's Tooth at midnight with ten years of these passes written into their heels.

The road glowed for me. Just barely — a whisper of light under each footfall, gone before the man behind me could see it. The wardwrights used to say the border roads were woven into the wards themselves, that every honest step stitched the veil a little tighter. I didn't know if that was true. What I knew was that the road knew me, and I took an unreasonable comfort in that, the way you might take comfort in a dog that greets you at the door. Something in this world knows my name, even if it's only gravel and stone.

My blade knew my name too. It always does.

If you've never seen an oath-blade, imagine a sword that's been loved by too many people across too many years. Mine is called Vesper, though she was called other things before me — she carries the memories of every swordspeaker who ever swore an oath on her steel, going back further than any family tree I could draw. She doesn't talk, exactly. But she hums. A low note under the guard, felt more than heard, steady as a heartbeat, and it changes when things change. Rising before a fight. Sinking near a grave. On good nights, riding a familiar pass under familiar stars, she hums so quietly she's almost silent, and that silence is the sound of everything being fine.

She hadn't been silent since sundown.

* * *

We made camp in the hollow below Cragwatch, where the pass narrows and the old wardstones stand in their broken ring like teeth knocked out of a jaw. There are wardstones all along the Thornfell border — grey standing stones older than the kingdom, carved with spirals no scholar has ever satisfactorily explained, humming with a magic so old and so patient that everyone had collectively agreed to stop thinking about it. They were part of the scenery. Part of the world. Like weather. Like taxes.

One of the wagon drivers, a wiry woman called Sef who chewed licorice root the way other people chew fear, nodded at the stones as we passed. "They're quiet tonight."

"They're always quiet," I said.

"Not quiet like that." She spat. "Quiet like something's listening back."

I laughed at her, because that's what you do, and because laughing is cheaper than thinking. But I'll confess it here, in the dark of my own telling: I checked the stones twice after that. And they were quiet. Not the comfortable quiet of old things resting, but the quiet of a room after someone has just left it — or just arrived.

Vesper's hum dropped another note.

Corvus Dain broke out his private bottle and made a little speech about profits and partnerships, and the drivers drank, and I drank less than I poured, and somewhere in there the night got very still. Not cold-still. Held-still. The kind of stillness a rabbit makes when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

"Zephyrine." Corvus settled beside my fire with the bottle, wearing his generous smile. "Ever wonder what's actually behind those wards? What they're keeping out?"

"No."

"Come now. A woman like you — sword full of ghosts, roads that light up for you — you must wonder."

"A woman like me," I said, "wonders about tolls, weather, and whether her client is going to try to cheat her at the border. Everything else is decoration."

He laughed, and raised the bottle to me, and for one moment his eyes over the rim weren't smiling at all. They were hungry in a way I couldn't name yet, and I filed it away in the drawer where I keep things I don't want to look at. That drawer has saved my life more times than my sword has.

Then the stars went out.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, because everyone who tells this story gets it wrong. The stars didn't fall. They didn't dim or flicker or hide behind cloud. They went out the way a candle goes out when pinched between wet fingers — instantly, completely, with a small dark *absence* left where the light had been. One moment the Widow's Tooth was wheeling under a river of stars thick enough to swim in. The next, the sky was a black lid, close and total, and the mountains stood against it like bones against skin.

Nobody screamed. That's the strange part. Nine wagons' worth of hardened criminals, and nobody screamed. We all just stopped — mid-drink, mid-word, mid-breath — the way a house stops when the floor gives a creak it shouldn't.

Sef's licorice root fell out of her mouth.

"The wards," someone whispered. Not a question. A diagnosis.

And then the wards died, and I learned what that sounds like.

It sounded like glass. All of it at once. Imagine every window in the world breaking in the same instant — but inside your ears, inside your teeth, inside the wet dark of your skull. The sound came from everywhere and nowhere, from the wardstones ringing the camp and from the horizon itself, a single vast crystalline crack that rolled down the length of the border like a whip being cracked by a god with a grudge. The old stones flared white — one heartbeat of light so pure it threw our shadows hard against the rock — and then went dark. Truly dark. Darker than stone has any right to be, as if the darkness had been poured into them like lead.

Vesper screamed.

Not hummed. Screamed — a high thin note tearing up through the guard and into my palm, into my wrist, into the marrow of me, and underneath the scream, for the first time in twenty years, I heard *words.*

*"Zephyrine."*

I knew the voice. That's the thing I still can't put down, even now, even after everything. I knew it the way you know your mother's footsteps on the stairs. It was a woman's voice, warm and smoke-roughened, and the last time I'd heard it I was seven years old, sitting cross-legged on a kitchen floor while a woman with Vesper's hilt in her lap taught me the first oath.

My grandmother has been dead for twenty years.

"Zephyrine." Her voice again, closer now, or maybe just clearer, rising out of the steel like breath fogging a window. "Wake up, child. Wake up and *run.*"

Around me the camp was dissolving into chaos — drivers shouting, horses screaming and hauling at their lines, Corvus Dain standing very still in the middle of it with his face turned up toward the starless sky like a man listening to music no one else could hear. The wardstones were bleeding darkness. I swear that's what it looked like — thin threads of absolute black seeping off the monoliths and crawling downhill along the old ward-lines, moving with purpose, moving *fast*, converging on something south of us. Converging, I understood much later, on Cinderhollow. On the lowlands. On everything the wards had spent three thousand years pretending to protect.

"Everyone mount up!" My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. Guide's reflexes. When the world ends, bill accordingly. "We move north, now, torches lit, nobody straggles—"

"North?" Corvus turned to me, and his eyes were wrong. Not evil-wrong. Empty-wrong, like windows of a house where the fire's gone out. "But the pass south—"

"The pass south is *dying*, you fool. North. Now."

He looked at me a moment too long. Then he moved, and to his credit, he moved fast, bellowing his people into order while I ran the line of wagons with a torch, counting heads, counting horses, doing the arithmetic of survival because arithmetic is what I have instead of prayer.

And all the while, Vesper spoke in my dead grandmother's voice.

"They ate it," she whispered. "Child, they didn't break the wards. They *ate* them. From the inside. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Nothing broke in tonight. Something finished."

"I don't understand," I said, through my teeth, low, so no one else would hear me answering air. "Who ate them? What's *they?*"

Silence. The worst kind — the kind that means the answer exists and is terrible.

"Grandmother." I gripped the hilt until my knuckles ached, until I could feel the ancestral memories stirring beneath my palms like fish under ice, dozens of voices, dozens of lives, all of them restless now, all of them afraid. "Talk to me."

"When you swore your oath," she said, and her voice was fraying at the edges like cloth worn through, "what did I tell you the blade was for?"

"To hold memory. To hold *oaths*. So that—" I stopped. Because I remembered. Of course I remembered; some things you carry whether or not you want to. *So that the border never forgets what it's guarding,* seven-year-old me had recited, parroting the lesson. *The veil is woven from remembering.*

"Yes," my dead grandmother said softly, from inside the steel, from inside the dark. "And tonight, child, something on the other side of that veil got *hungry.*"

Down in the southern dark, far beyond the reach of any torch, something answered her. Not a sound, exactly. More a pressure — a vast slow attention turning toward the Thornfell passes the way a head turns toward a smell. I felt it pass over the camp like a shadow cast by nothing, felt the horses flatten their ears and the drivers go mute, felt the road beneath my boots go utterly, terribly dark.

The road that had glowed for me for ten years.

It wasn't glowing anymore. Every stitch of light, every remembered footprint of mine and every honest walker's before me — gone, drunk down, erased. The smuggler roads of Thornfell had forgotten me in a single breath, and I stood there mourning a strip of gravel like a fool, because grief doesn't check whether it's proportionate.

"Zephyrine." Grandmother's voice again, thinner now, being pulled somewhere I couldn't follow. "Listen. Find Merric Vale. Whatever they tell you about him — whatever crime they buried him for — find him. He's the only one who believed me. He's the only one who—"

The voice cut off like a snuffed candle.

Vesper's hum returned — but changed. Lower. Wrong-keyed. A heartbeat after a fever breaks.

Above me, the starless sky pressed down like a lid on a jar, and in the south, faint as a dying ember, I saw the horizon pulse once. Twice. Slow and rhythmic and patient.

Like breathing.

"Mount up!" I shouted, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I didn't care. "North! *Ride!*"

As the caravan lurched into motion, wheels grinding on stone that no longer knew us, Corvus Dain drew level with my horse. He looked at the southern dark, and then at me, and he smiled his generous smile.

"Told you a storm was coming," he said.

I didn't answer. I was busy memorizing the feel of my grandmother's voice, because I already knew — the way you know a wound is deep before the blood arrives — that I was going to need to hold onto it for a long, long time.

Behind us, in the dark where the stars used to be, something began, very softly, to laugh.
