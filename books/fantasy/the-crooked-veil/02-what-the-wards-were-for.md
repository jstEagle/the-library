## 2. What the Wards Were For

Cinderhollow smelled like wet ash and boiled turnips, which was fitting, because Cinderhollow had always smelled like something that had given up on being anything else. I'd passed through it a dozen times on the lowland runs, and every time I'd thought: *someday this town will either burn down or get its act together, and either way I won't be here to see it.*

The stars had been gone for two days. The wards had died with them. And now the town was full of people who had walked out of Thornfell with nothing — not even their own names, in some cases.

I came in off the smuggler's road with my hood up and my blade wrapped in oilcloth on my back, which is what you do when you're the last oath-bound swordspeaker in a province where oath-bound swordspeakers have suddenly become very interesting to very bad people. The caravan master had paid me off at the pass and taken his wagons south with the kind of speed usually reserved for men fleeing paternity. Smart man.

I should have done the same. Instead I stood in the market square of Cinderhollow and watched a woman scream at a child.

Not *at* him, exactly. That's the thing I keep coming back to, the thing that made me stay when staying was stupid. She wasn't angry. She was holding the boy by both shoulders and shaking him gently, the way you'd shake a locked box, and she was saying, "You're mine. You have to be mine. Look at me — don't you know me?"

And the boy, who couldn't have been more than six, said, "You're crying, lady," in the flat, patient voice of a child explaining rain to an adult.

She knew he was hers. That was the horror of it. She had the fact of him the way you have a sum you can no longer check — *this is my son, he is six, his name begins with a T* — but the face above her hands might as well have belonged to a stranger pulled from a crowd. Some clerk in her head had kept the ledger and burned the receipts.

I'd seen grief before. Plenty. Grief has a shape; it bends people in predictable directions. This wasn't grief. This was a woman standing in the wreckage of her own mind, holding the one piece of evidence left, begging it to testify.

A man near me muttered, "Third one today," and spat. "Ward-touched, they're calling it."

"Ward-touched," I repeated.

"Come down from the passes since the night the sky went black." He wouldn't look up when he said it. Nobody in Cinderhollow looked up anymore. Two days of starless sky and they'd already learned the trick of pretending the ceiling was normal. "They remember everything except what matters. Faces. Names. One fellow remembers every debt he's owed but not who owes them." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, like a hinge that needed oiling. "Town council says it'll pass."

"Do you believe them?"

"I believe the council meets behind a locked door now," he said, "and I believe I've started writing things down." He patted his coat, where a small book bulged. "My wife's face. My daughter's. Descriptions. In case."

"In case of what?"

He looked at me then, properly, and I saw that he was older than I'd first thought, or maybe just tired in a way that added years the way damp adds weight to wool. "In case I'm next," he said. "Whoever you are, swordswoman — and don't look surprised, the oilcloth fools nobody — you should write things down too."

He shuffled off toward the granary, where the town had set up cots for the refugees, and I stood there in the turnip-smelling cold and did not write anything down, because writing it down would mean admitting it was real, and I wasn't ready to admit it was real.

Here's what I knew, or thought I knew, in those first hours: the border wards of Thornfell were old. Older than the kingdom, some said, though kingdoms always say that about whatever was here first. They weren't walls. Walls keep things out. The wards were more like... a seam. A stitching between what the world is and what the world was before people agreed on what it is. Every child in Thornfell grew up knowing the rhyme — *the veil hangs crooked, the veil hangs thin, mind the seam and mind the wind* — and every adult stopped thinking about it, because that's what wards are for. You're not supposed to notice them working. A good ward is like a good servant: invisible, tireless, and only ever missed once it's gone.

And the wards hadn't broken. I want to be clear about that, because everyone in Cinderhollow kept saying *broken*, and the word sat wrong in my ear every single time. Broken implies force. Broken implies something hit them hard enough to crack. But I'd been on the pass that night. I'd felt it happen. It hadn't sounded like stone failing.

It had sounded like glass breaking, yes — but glass breaking *inward*. Like a window swallowing itself.

Like a meal finishing.

My blade had whispered when it happened. I haven't told anyone this yet, so consider it a confidence between us. Swordspeakers' blades hold memory — that's the whole trade, the whole sacred burden, the reason we swear oaths over steel instead of paper. Paper burns. Steel remembers. My ancestors live in this blade, their oaths and their deaths and, if I'm honest, a few of their worse decisions, and on the pass that night, when the stars went out, they had all spoken at once. Not words. A sound like a held breath finally released after three hundred years.

Then one voice, underneath the others, young and frightened, said: *she's awake.*

I didn't know who *she* was. I still don't, entirely. But I knew where to start asking, and the answer was sitting in a cell under the Cinderhollow tollhouse, where the crown put him four years ago for the crime of being right too loudly.

Merric Vale had been famous once, back when I was still learning which end of a sword to hold. Wardwright of the second rank, youngest ever elevated, the man who re-threaded the Cinderhollow boundary stones after the flood year. Then he'd written a report. I'd heard about it the way you hear about all scandals, in fragments, mostly wrong: that he'd claimed the wards were failing, that he'd accused the crown of negligence, that he'd used the phrase *the hungry thing behind the veil* in an official document, which even I could tell was career suicide dressed up as terminology.

They'd stripped his rank and locked him in the tollhouse cellar to think about what he'd done. Four years of thinking. As far as I knew, the crown had never sent anyone to hear the results.

The jailer was a fat, bored man named Ottery who charged me two coppers for entry and another two for discretion, and threw in, free of charge, the observation that "he's gone strange down there. Talks to the walls. Well — talks to the *stones*, if you take my meaning."

"I take your meaning," I said, and went down the stairs into air that tasted of chalk and mildew.

The cell at the bottom wasn't dark. That was my first surprise. Every surface — floor, walls, ceiling — was covered in ward-marks drawn in what I desperately hoped was chalk. Not random marks, either. They interlocked, layered, a lattice of geometry so dense it made my eyes water, and at the center of it all, cross-legged on a cot like a spider in the middle of its web, sat a gaunt man with grey in his beard and ink-stained fingers, talking quietly to a loose flagstone.

"—no, no, you're listening wrong, the third harmonic carries the load, if you'd just—" He looked up. His eyes were bloodshot and sharp as awls. "You're not a stone."

"No," I said.

"You're worse. You're armed." He didn't move. "If Ottery sent you to collect back rent, tell him the wall-markings constitute improvements to the property."

"Merric Vale?"

"That depends entirely on who's asking and whether they've come to add charges."

"Zephyrine Iversen. Swordspeaker." I let the oilcloth slip from the blade on my back, just enough that the edge caught the light of his little lamp. "Oath-bound. Last one, as far as I can find."

Something changed in his face. It wasn't fear and it wasn't hope, or rather it was both at once, fighting for the same ground. He stood slowly, and I noticed he braced himself on the wall as he rose, and that his fingertips found the chalk lines without looking, the way a blind man finds furniture.

"A swordspeaker," he said. "Do you know how long I've wanted to examine one of your blades? There are theories—"

"I'm not here for your theories."

"Everyone says that first." He wiped his hands on his ragged coat, leaving fresh chalk ghosts among the old ink ones. "Why are you here, Zephyrine Iversen?"

So I told him. The stars going out. The sound like glass breaking inward. The refugees coming down from the passes missing their children's faces. The woman in the square shaking her boy like a locked box. I told it plainly, because I'd learned on the road that plainness is armor, and when I finished, Merric Vale sat back down on his cot very carefully, the way a man sits when his legs have stopped being trustworthy.

"Say the sound again," he said.

"Glass. Breaking inward."

"*Inward.*" He closed his eyes. "Not shattered. Consumed. Do you understand the difference? If the wards had shattered, the fragments would still exist — dead, scattered, but present. Memory doesn't vanish, swordspeaker. It goes somewhere. Someone would be able to find the pieces and—" He stopped. His eyes opened. "Unless someone ate them."

"The hungry thing behind the veil," I said. "Your famous phrase."

"My infamous phrase." He laughed, short and bitter. "Four years ago I found irregularities in the Thornfell lattice. Consumption patterns. The wards draw on stored memory to sustain themselves — royal memory, specifically, that's what the old kings were for, did you know that? The crown isn't a hat. It's a reservoir. Every monarch swears the Founding Oath and pours a portion of themselves into the veil, and the veil spends it slowly, keeping the seam closed." He was talking faster now, his hands moving, drawing shapes in the air that I half-recognized from the walls around us. "But the consumption rates were wrong. Something was taking more than the allotment. Something was feeding *from inside*. I wrote it up. I used the word 'parasite.' I may also have used the word 'devouring.' The Lord Chamberlain's response was to ask whether I'd been drinking."

"And then they gave you a cellar."

"They gave me a cellar," he agreed. "Which, in fairness, has excellent acoustics for talking to stones." The humor dropped off his face like a mask slipping its cord. "I told them it was hungry. I told them exactly what would happen if it finished eating. And then I sat down here and waited for it to happen, and it happened, and do you know what the worst part is, Zephyrine Iversen? It's not that they didn't listen. It's that I *stopped trying to make them listen.* Year two, I decided the walls were better company. Year three, I decided I deserved the cellar. Guilt is very comfortable, eventually. It fits like an old coat."

I knew about old coats. "The king," I said. "The Regent. Is he—"

"Is he part of it?" Merric's voice went very quiet. "Swordspeaker, I have spent four years in this cell doing the arithmetic of my own cowardice, and the number I keep arriving at is this: the wards were woven from royal memory. The parasite feeds on royal memory. The king has worn the crown for ten years, and in ten years, no one — not one soul at court — has remarked upon anything unusual about him." He looked at me. "Either the crown is perfectly guarded by the luckiest accident in history, or the thing wearing the crown is the thing doing the eating."

The cellar was silent except for the drip of somewhere-water and, faintly, if I let myself imagine it, the whisper of three hundred years of ancestors in the steel on my back, all of them saying what they'd said on the pass:

*She's awake.*

"Why am I here?" I asked him, and it came out harsher than I meant. "That's the question you asked me. Here's yours. Why did I walk into a plague-town and climb down into a disgraced wardwright's cell instead of riding south until the sky made sense again? Because I don't know. Because I swore oaths to protect Thornfell and the veil fell down and I wasn't there, and because that woman in the market square is going to spend the rest of her life loving a child whose face she can't find, and because my blade woke up and spoke to me for the first time since I was a girl and I am *afraid*, Merric Vale, and I have discovered that fear plus guilt plus a sword makes a person do stupid, necessary things."

He studied me for a long moment. Then he got up, crossed to the cell door, and banged on it twice.

"Ottery!" he shouted. "Keys! The crown's business!"

"The crown hasn't sent anyone in four years," Ottery's voice drifted down, unconcerned.

"The crown is currently *indisposed*, you magnificent walrus, and this woman is about to be the most important person in Thornfell, so bring the keys before I redraw your door lintel into something that bites."

There was a pause, the particular pause of a man calculating profit margins against cosmic horror. Then keys jingled.

Merric turned back to me. Up close, in the lamplight, he looked less like a prisoner and more like a man who had been sharpened by four years of captivity down to a single point.

"I need my instruments," he said. "I need access to the boundary stones outside town — the dead ones. And I need you to understand, before we take one step, that I am not doing this out of nobility. I'm doing it because I knew, and I stayed in my comfortable cellar, and every faceless child down from the passes is a debt in my ledger. You carry guilt like a sword. I carry mine like a debt. Between us we might make one functional person." He almost smiled. It was not a comforting sight. "Also, I need to examine your blade. There are theories."

"There are always theories."

"Mine involve why a parasite that eats memories would wake up the moment the last swordspeaker walked past its front door." His eyes dropped to the oilcloth-wrapped hilt. "You're not carrying a weapon, Zephyrine Iversen. You're carrying a banquet. And I suspect the thing that ate the stars knows it."

Above us, through the grate at the top of the stairs, the starless sky pressed down on Cinderhollow like a lid. Somewhere in the refugee cots, a woman was teaching herself her own son's face from a description in a stranger's memory, and getting it wrong.

Ottery came down the stairs with the keys, grumbling about paperwork.

It was the last quiet hour any of us would get for a long time — though of course, none of us knew that yet. Especially not what the boy in the grain wagon outside the tollhouse had seen when he touched the fallen wardstone.
