## 3. The Boy With No Yesterday

Here is what I know about myself: I am cold, I am hungry, and my left hand hurts.

That is the complete inventory. Everything else — name, mother, whether I once liked the smell of rain or hated it — went somewhere, and nobody left a forwarding address. It's like waking up in the middle of someone else's dream and having to pretend you know the plot.

I'd been following the wagon for two days before I worked up the nerve to climb into it. Not because I was afraid of the woman driving it — though she had a sword that hummed when she wasn't looking at it, which is not a thing swords are supposed to do, and she held it like she'd been born holding it, which is not a thing people are supposed to do either. No, I was afraid of her hand. She kept touching the hilt the way you touch a wound to check if it's still bleeding, and something in me understood that whatever was wrong with her was the same kind of wrong as what was wrong with me, and I didn't want to be recognized. Broken things can smell each other. It's how they find out they're broken.

But the second night the frost came down off the hills like a dropped curtain, and I made the arithmetic every hungry person makes: the wagon has blankets, the woman has bread, and being dead is colder than being caught.

So I climbed in among the barrels of Cinderhollow salt-fish and pulled a tarp over myself, and I lay there with my fused hand throbbing against my chest like a second heart, and I listened to the wheels talk to the road.

The hand first, since you'll ask about it anyway. Everyone asks about it eventually, usually while backing away.

Three nights ago — I think it was three; time goes slippery when you have no yesterday to measure against — I woke in a ditch with this shard of grey stone grown into my palm like a splinter the size of a knife-blade. Wardstone. I know that word the way you know words in a language you don't speak: *wardstone*, *veil*, *oath*. They sit in my head with their boots on, refusing to explain themselves. The stone itself is warm when other people are near, and sometimes — this is the part I should probably keep quiet, but you've come this far — sometimes it *shows* me things. Little burning pictures behind my eyes. A woman kneading bread in a kitchen that smells of cardamom. A man burying a dog under an apple tree and crying so hard his shoulders shake. Strangers' moments, played for me by a rock stuck in my fist, and I can't turn them off, and I can't make them mine.

It's like being handed letters addressed to everyone in the world except you.

* * *

The woman stopped the wagon at midmorning, near a stream, and I heard her boots come around the back, unhurried, and then the tarp lifted and there she was against the grey sky with her sword on her hip and an expression I would learn to read later as *of course*.

"Out," she said.

I climbed out. My legs had opinions about the climbing but performed anyway.

She looked at me the way you look at a coin you suspect is clipped. "How long?"

"Two days."

"You ate my bread."

"Some of your bread."

"That's the same as eating my bread." But she said it without heat, more like reciting a rule than enforcing one, and her eyes had gone to my hand — to the shard, which was glowing faintly, the way it does around her specifically, which I did not yet understand and which frightened me more than her sword did. "Where are your parents?"

"I don't know."

"Where's your village?"

"I don't know."

"Your name?"

And here is the thing about that question. People ask it like it's easy, like the answer lives right behind your teeth where they keep theirs. I opened my mouth and reached for it and found the same smooth nothing I always find, a hallway with all the doors painted onto the wall.

"No," I said. Which wasn't an answer, but was the truest thing I owned.

Something changed in her face. Not softness exactly — I got the feeling softness had been amputated from her some years back and the stump still ached — but recognition. She looked at my hand again, longer this time, and I watched her decide several things in a row, fast, the way gamblers count cards.

"Wardstone," she said. "Fused. That's not possible."

"It's very possible," I said. "It happened to me."

Her mouth did something at the corner that might have been the ghost of a laugh. "You always this clever, or just with strangers who could throw you in a river?"

"Just with those, mostly."

She crouched, brought her eyes level with mine, and studied the shard. Up close I could see she hadn't slept properly in days; there were shadows under her eyes like thumbprints. The stone warmed against my palm and flickered — and there it was, uninvited, a picture: a girl of maybe ten, kneeling in a circle of candlelight, a sword too big for her laid flat across her knees, an old man's voice saying words like iron being drawn slowly through a whetstone. *Do you swear?* And the girl saying, in a voice that shook and meant it anyway, *I swear.*

I gasped. I couldn't help it. The picture burned and was gone, and the woman was staring at me with an expression gone carefully blank, which is its own kind of loud.

"What," she said quietly, "did you just see."

"A girl," I said. "Swearing something. On a sword. There were candles." I hesitated, because lying seemed suddenly both very easy and very rude. "Was it you?"

The silence went on long enough to have weather. Somewhere up the stream a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"Yes," she said finally. And then, standing, briskly, as if she could outrun the moment: "There's bread in the box by the seat. Eat it walking. You're coming to Cinderhollow."

"Why?"

"Because that stone in your hand is a piece of the border wards, boy, and the border wards are dead, and dead things don't fuse themselves to children." She swung up onto the bench and took the reins. "And because I knew a girl who swore an oath once, and I want to know why the stone remembers her better than I—"

She stopped. She didn't finish. She flicked the reins and the wagon rolled, and I scrambled up beside her, and neither of us said anything for a mile, and it was somehow the most comfortable silence I'd ever shared with anyone, which given my total sample size of one previous person isn't saying much, but it felt like saying much.

* * *

Her name was Zephyrine Iversen, which she told me the way you tell someone a password you intend to change soon. She asked me questions across the next day's miles, in the tone of someone doing sums: Did I remember anything before the ditch? (No.) Did the stone show me things often? (Constantly.) Did it hurt? (Only when I tried to hold onto what it showed.)

"And the oath-girl," she said, on the second morning, eyes on the road. "Did you see anything else? Around it. Details."

"There was snow coming through a window that wouldn't shut. The old man smelled like ink and horse. You cut your palm on the blade and didn't flinch, and he said—" I closed my eyes, reaching back into the borrowed bright. "*The blade will keep it safer than you will.*"

Zephyrine pulled the wagon to a stop.

For a moment she just sat there, hands loose on the reins, looking at the road ahead as if it had personally betrayed her. Then she laughed — one short, cracked note, nothing like joy.

"That's what he said." She rubbed her face with one hand. "I haven't thought about that day in years. I've *tried* to think about it, since the stars went out, and it's like groping through smoke. But you — a rock in your hand reaches into my head and pulls it out whole, candles and snow and all." Her jaw tightened. "The wards are dead. The stars are gone. And now memory itself is coming loose." She looked at me sideways. "What did you say your name was?"

"You didn't give me one yet."

That startled a real laugh out of her, brief and rusty. "Fair."

* * *

I should say something about the nights, because the days were for questions and the nights were for the stone.

When Zephyrine slept — lightly, one hand never far from the humming sword — I sat up in the wagon bed and let the shard play. It was the only way I had of feeling like a person. Not because the memories were mine, but because watching them, I could practice. Grief, in the apple-tree man. Tenderness, in the bread-woman. A soldier's terror and exaltation in the same heartbeat, running up a hill toward men who wanted him dead. I watched them like lessons in a language I'd forgotten I spoke, learning how a self is built, taking notes for the person I might someday be if I ever found any materials.

One night the stone showed me Zephyrine herself — not the oath, something older. A kitchen. A smaller girl than the oath-girl, maybe six, standing on a stool to reach a pot, and a woman's hands guiding hers, and a voice saying *stir, don't whip, we're making soup not enemies,* and small-Zephyrine giggling, and the smell of leeks—

Then it cut off, hard, like a door slammed, and the shard went cold in my hand for the first time ever, and I sat in the dark shaking, because I understood what the cutoff meant. That memory had a hole in it where the rest should be. Something had eaten forward from that kitchen, year by year, and the stone had shown me right up to the edge of the bite.

I looked over at Zephyrine sleeping with her hand on the hilt, and I decided two things. One: I was going to stop asking her questions that made her face go blank. Two: whatever was eating everyone's yesterdays — hers, the refugees' in Cinderhollow, mine — I was going to be there when it got found. Not out of bravery. Out of the simple fury of a boy who'd had his own plate cleared without being asked, and couldn't even remember what had been on it.

* * *

Cinderhollow came up out of the lowlands smelling of woodsmoke and fear, and I won't describe the refugees yet because that's another chapter's worth of horror, but one image stays nailed to me: a woman in the square holding a child's mitten, turning it over and over, asking everyone who passed *do you know whose this is, do you know whose this is,* and no one did, including, by the look in her eyes, possibly her.

Zephyrine went to find her disgraced wardwright in the town jail. I went along because going along was the only skill I currently possessed.

Merric Vale turned out to be a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who'd been right at the worst possible time. He took one look at my hand through the bars and went white as the wardstone wasn't.

"Where did you get that?"

"Grew it," I said.

"He woke in a ditch with it fused," Zephyrine said. "No memory. None at all."

Merric reached through the bars — hesitated, asked my permission with his eyebrows, which after Zephyrine's efficient interrogations felt almost courtly — and touched the shard with one fingertip. His eyes unfocused. His mouth moved around silent words. Then he snatched his hand back like he'd touched a stove.

"That's a piece of the Thornfell border lattice," he whispered. "First-rank anchor stone. It shouldn't exist outside the wardline, and it certainly shouldn't be *rooted*." He looked at me with an expression I couldn't parse — pity and hunger and fear braided together. "Boy. When it shows you things. Whose are they?"

"Strangers'. Mostly." I glanced at Zephyrine. "Some not."

"Mostly strangers," Merric repeated softly. "Because it's casting around. Looking." He sat back on his cot, and for a moment he looked older than the jail, older than the town. "Wardstone holds memory, boy. That's what a ward *is* — a promise the land remembers. And promises are made of memories, and memories are made of—" He stopped himself, visibly reorganizing. "Never mind. Zephyrine. We need to talk about what eats a veil from the inside. And you—" he pointed at me through the bars, and his finger was not entirely steady, "—you stay where I can see you. Because a shard that casts memories doesn't attach to just anyone. It attached to you for a reason, and I very much want to know whether the reason is that you're a victim..."

He trailed off. He was looking past me now, at the shard, which had begun to glow — brighter than I'd ever seen it, warm as a coal, and behind my eyes the pictures came flooding, faster than ever, dozens of them, hundreds, a flood of other people's yesterdays all shoving to be seen—

"—or that you're the bait," Merric finished.

The glow died. The wagon outside creaked. And far away, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of everything, something that had been patiently eating the world for a decade turned its attention — gently, curiously, hungrily — toward a nameless boy in a Cinderhollow jail, holding a stone that remembered too much.

I gripped the shard tighter. It pulsed once against my palm, almost like a heartbeat answering mine.

Almost like it was glad I'd finally noticed it was there.
