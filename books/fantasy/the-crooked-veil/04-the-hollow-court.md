## 4. The Hollow Court

There are three ways into a city that doesn't want you: over the wall, under the wall, or through the front gate smiling like you own the place and the place owes you money. The first two get you killed. The third gets you invited to dinner, which is slower, but the food is better.

I went through the front gate of Carreth Vale with a forged merchant's seal, a wagon full of legitimately boring wool, and a boy at my side who had been instructed to say nothing unless spoken to, and then to say less than that.

"They'll ask your name," I'd told him on the road down from Cinderhollow.

"Ash," he said.

"That's not a name, that's what falls out of a fireplace."

"It's the one I have."

Fair enough. It was more than most people in this kingdom had left. We'd passed three refugee columns on the road, families walking south with everything they owned on their backs and nothing they remembered behind their eyes. One woman asked me if I'd seen her daughter — described her in loving detail, the gap teeth, the habit of naming crows — and when I said no, she thanked me politely and asked me who I was. Twice. In the same conversation. She wasn't stupid. She was simply leaking, like a bucket someone had kicked over, and no amount of scooping was going to put the water back.

The guards at Carreth Vale's Sun Gate didn't leak. That was the first thing that unsettled me. They were crisp, courteous, well-fed, and absolutely certain of everything, which in my experience is the uniform of men who have recently been relieved of the capacity for doubt. One of them examined my seal, looked up at me with eyes like polished river stones, and said, "Welcome to the capital, mistress. His Radiance is holding court today. You're fortunate."

"Fortunate," I repeated.

"Everyone is fortunate," he said, with the serene conviction of a man reciting something he'd been told rather than something he knew. "Every day."

I thanked him and rolled through, and did not let myself shiver until we were three streets deep and surrounded by enough noise to hide in.

* * *

Carreth Vale was beautiful the way a painted portrait is beautiful. Everything in its right place, everything lit generously, every beggar swept off avenues that gleamed like they'd been licked by angels. Fountains ran wine-gold in the afternoon sun. Musicians played on corners without asking coin, because apparently the city paid them now, or because they'd forgotten what coin was for, and I honestly couldn't decide which explanation frightened me more.

And the people — the people *loved* it here. You could see it in their faces, this soft, unhungering contentment, like cats dozing in a patch of sun. A baker gave me a roll for free and couldn't tell me what kind it was. A child recited the king's titles with shining eyes — *Aldric the Beloved, Aldric the Radiant, Aldric Who Never Forgets His People* — and when I asked her how old she was, she smiled blankly and recited the titles again, word for word, same cadence, same breath.

"Zephyrine," Ash whispered, tugging my sleeve. "That girl just did that before. At the fountain."

"I know."

"She got all the words exactly the same."

"I know, Ash."

He was quiet a moment. Then, in the small voice he used when he was about to say something that made me want to sit down: "It's like they're all wearing the same coat. And it fits nobody properly."

Out of the mouths of boys with holes where their histories should be. I bought him a honey cake with the baker's forgotten coin and told myself the chill down my spine was just the mountain air following me down.

My blade disagreed. It had been murmuring since the Sun Gate — not words yet, just a low restless hum against my hip, the way it used to hum before border skirmishes back when I still took contracts worth the name. Swordspeakers learn to trust that hum the way sailors trust a dropping barometer. My ancestors' voices lived coiled in that steel, a hundred generations of oaths and grievances and terrible singing, and right now all hundred generations were awake and uneasy, like dogs hearing a whistle pitched too high for human ears.

*Something is eating*, the blade seemed to say. *Something is eating and it has set a very long table.*

* * *

Getting into court required exactly two things: a dress I couldn't fight in, and a lie I could. The dress cost me half my wool profits from a tailor who kept forgetting my measurements mid-pin. The lie was free, being merely an extension of the merchant's seal — a wool factor from the Thornfell passes, newly arrived, petitioning for a trade writ, humble, grateful, awed.

Awed was the easy part. Standing in the Grand Audience Hall of Carreth Vale for the first time, even a cynical old smuggler's sword finds awe lying around loose and picks some up without meaning to.

The hall was built to make you feel small in the flattering way, the way cathedrals do — pillars like trees, light falling in long gold shafts, the acoustics tuned so that a whisper at the throne reached the back benches clear as a bell. Courtiers packed the floor in silks I could have fenced for a year of comfortable living. Everyone faced the dais. Everyone was smiling.

And there, at the top of the steps, stood King Aldric the Beloved.

Understand: he was magnificent. I want to be honest about that, because what came after only works if you understand that I understood it too. He was tall without looming, silver at the temples without aging, dressed in white and gold that managed to look simple despite costing more than my home village. He moved among his petitioners like sunlight moving across a floor — touching shoulders, laughing, leaning in to hear the small worries of small people as though each one were the only worry in the world. When a farmer stammered his grievance about a flooded mill, the king took the man's hands in both of his own, and the man *wept*, openly, with gratitude, and walked away glowing like he'd been blessed by every god at once.

The hall loved him. Not respected — *loved*, the way you love a thing that has never once disappointed you, which is to say the way you love a story.

And I stood in my rented silk with my heart going like a war drum, because I had grown up on my grandmother's knee hearing about Aldric the Beloved, and everything was correct — the face matched the coins, the voice matched the ballads — and none of it was *right*.

Here's what I mean. Watch a man laugh a hundred times and you learn his laugh, the way it starts in his chest, the little variations, the true ones and the polite ones. This king laughed beautifully. He laughed perhaps forty times in the hour I stood there. And every single laugh was identical.

Not similar. Identical. Same length, same pitch, same warm rising finish, same exact placement of the little pause before it. Laugh number seven and laugh number thirty-one were the same recording played twice. I counted. Counting laughs is not a skill I ever expected to use, but smuggling teaches you to notice when a thing repeats, because repetition is what forgery looks like. A real signature varies. Only a stamp comes out the same every time.

I looked at Ash beside me in his borrowed page's livery. He was staring at the dais with his jaw tight, and his hand — the one with the wardstone shard fused to the palm — was curled into a fist against his chest, and the shard was glowing faintly, pulsing, like a second heartbeat out of rhythm with his own.

"It hurts him," Ash breathed. "The shard. It keeps reaching for the king and coming back wrong. Like dipping a cup into a well and pulling up sand."

"Hush."

"He's not—" Ash swallowed. "Zephyrine, under the shine there's nothing. I can see nothing. It's like looking at a house where all the furniture's been taken out and they've hung paintings where the windows should be."

I hushed him again, harder, because a courtier near us had turned — a plump, pleasant woman with rings on every finger — and was watching us with mild interest.

"You'll love the harvest feast," she told us, apropos of nothing, in the tone of someone continuing a conversation we hadn't started. "Last year's feast was wonderful. The king danced with my daughter. Do you remember it? I keep trying to remember it and it slips." Her smile didn't waver. Her eyes had gone briefly somewhere else, somewhere with no furniture. "It was wonderful. Whatever it was."

She drifted away, serene, and I watched her go and thought about the refugees on the south road, and about the woman who couldn't hold her own question in her head for ten breaths.

Small memories. That was the pattern I was starting to see. Nothing big enough to miss. No one screaming that their past had been stolen, because how do you mourn a hole you can't find the edges of? The king's courtiers left his presence lighter than they arrived — a dance forgotten here, a feast forgotten there, the taste of last winter gone like frost off glass. Death by a thousand tiny amnesias, administered by a beloved hand, in a hall full of music.

And the worst part — the part I still can't shake, the part I'll carry into whatever dark is waiting for me next — is that I almost joined them. There was a moment, one moment, when the king's gaze crossed the hall and touched mine, warm as bathwater, and my whole body wanted to step forward. Wanted to kneel. Wanted to open up like a door and hand him whatever he cared to take, gladly, gratefully, the way the farmer had given his hands, the way the woman had given her rings' worth of yesterdays. It felt like standing at the edge of a warm bed on a cold morning. It felt like *relief*.

My blade saved me. It flared cold against my hip — a shock like falling through ice — and the spell of him snapped, and I was myself again, sweating in my rented silk, with a hundred dead ancestors shouting wordlessly in steel.

Because that's the other thing I noticed, the thing that turned wonder to horror and horror to purpose. When the king's attention had brushed past me, the humming in my blade hadn't spiked in fear.

It had spiked in *hunger*.

Two hungers recognizing each other across a crowded room. The thing on the throne had felt my sword the way a starving man smells bread, and I had felt its pull the way a drowning sailor feels the current, and neither of us had needed an introduction.

* * *

We left the hall with the crowd, heads bowed, faces arranged into blissful nothing. In the corridor outside, the light went ordinary again, and my lungs remembered their business.

Ash leaned against the marble and shook, quietly, the way he did — grief with no address to send it to. "It saw us," he said. "Didn't it. It saw the shard."

"It saw the sword. The shard it just found annoying." I pulled him upright by the shoulder. "Listen to me. Tomorrow morning we're gone from this city before the fountains start flowing. Tonight we find Merric's contact at the Sign of the Broken Compass, and—"

"Mistress?"

One of the gate guards. River-stone eyes, courteous smile, standing far closer than a guard needed to stand. Behind him, two more, blocking the corridor's mouth without appearing to block anything at all.

"His Radiance noticed you today," the guard said warmly. "Among all those faces, he noticed yours. He wishes to extend an invitation. Supper, at the palace, tomorrow evening. Small gathering. Intimate." The smile widened by a precise, measured degree. "He so enjoys new friends. He remembers them forever."

Behind the guard, down the gold-lit corridor, faint and impossible and perfectly clear, I heard laughter.

His laugh. The same one. Forty times in an hour, and now once more, echoing down empty marble where no one stood — the same laugh, the same length, the same rising finish, repeating like water dripping in a cave, patient as anything that knows the stone will give out before it does.

Ash's shard flared so bright it showed through his glove.

"We would be honored," I heard myself say, in my best merchant's wife voice, while every ancestor in my blade screamed one wordless syllable at once:

*Run.*
