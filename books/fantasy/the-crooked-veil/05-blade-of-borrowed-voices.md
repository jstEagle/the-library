## 5. Blade of Borrowed Voices

I have spent most of my life being right at exactly the wrong moments.

It's a peculiar talent. Most people are wrong in comfortable ways — wrong about the weather, wrong about which horse will throw a shoe, wrong about the price of turnips. Their wrongness costs them a shoe and some coin. My wrongness cost me my title, my workshop, my standing before the crown, and eleven years of my life in a cell the size of a coffin with better ventilation.

So when Zephyrine Iversen came to my cell door with a set of smuggled keys and an expression like a woman who'd been chewing on a question for three days and finally swallowed it wrong, I knew — before she said a word — that she was about to be right too. And I knew what it was going to cost us both.

"You said," she began, before the door had even finished swinging open, "in your trial. You said the wards weren't built. You said they were *remembered*."

"Good morning to you as well."

"Merric."

"I was drunk at my trial," I said, which was true, though not in the way she thought. "Half of what I said was nonsense and the other half was treason. It's hard to tell them apart sometimes."

"The wards were remembered." She stepped into my cell — my cell, eleven years of my life, treated now as a hallway — and looked around at the walls where I'd scratched diagrams into the plaster with a bent spoon. Ward-lattices. Veil-geometry. The mathematics of memory made structural. The prison authorities had assumed they were prayers. In fairness, so did I, some nights. "You're going to explain that sentence to me, Vale, or you're going to find out how fast a disgraced wardwright can run with a swordspeaker's boot up his—"

"Yes, all right." I gathered the few things worth gathering, which was to say nothing, because eleven years of confiscation leaves a man beautifully unencumbered. "But not here. The walls in this place have ears, and two of those ears belong to men who report directly to Lord Chancellor Veyre, and Veyre smiles too much for a man whose king has been laughing at the same joke for a decade."

She stopped. Something went through her face then — not surprise. Confirmation. The look of someone who had seen the wrongness herself, up close, and had been told by everyone around her that she was imagining it.

"Your cell," she said quietly. "You've been scratching ward-work into the walls for eleven years."

"Keeps the hands busy."

"That's not ward-work." She touched one of the grooves, the long spiral near the window that I'd redone every spring when the damp ate it away. "That's a containment lattice. Small. Crude. But pointed *outward*."

I confess I felt something then that I hadn't felt since my arrest: pride. Here was a woman who'd never drawn a ward in her life, reading my desperate spoon-scratchings the way a musician reads a score.

"It kept the dreams out," I said. "Mostly. Come along, Iversen. If we're going to commit treason properly, we should do it somewhere with better light."

* * *

The safehouse was a cooper's cellar off Tanner's Row, which is to say it smelled of oak shavings and old brine and had exactly one exit, which I hated, but it belonged to a woman named Sera Doss who owed me her son's life and asked no questions, which I loved. Zephyrine barred the door behind us, checked the shutter twice, and then sat down across from me with her sword laid across her knees like a sleeping child.

And I mean that precisely. Like a child. She held it the way you hold something you love and are slightly afraid of.

"Show me," I said.

"Show you what?"

"The blade. The whispering. You heard it on the pass when the stars went out — don't make that face, the boy talks in his sleep and you talk in yours, and between the two of you I've pieced together half a confession." I leaned forward. "You heard your sword speak in a voice you hadn't heard since childhood. Whose voice, Iversen?"

Her hand tightened on the scabbard. For a moment I thought she wouldn't answer. Then:

"My grandmother's. Halda Iversen. She taught me my first oath-forms when I was six. She died when I was nine." A pause. "The sword sounded like her. Exactly like her. Like she'd been standing behind my shoulder on the pass, saying *not yet, little wren, hold the line, not yet*."

"And no one ever told you why?"

"They told me swordspeakers carry echoes. Training echoes. That the old masters left their forms in the steel and the blade plays them back to the student, like a music box." Her jaw worked. "It's a comforting story. Music boxes don't say things like *hold the line, wren, the veil is thinning*. Music boxes don't know the word *veil*."

"No," I said. "They don't."

I got up, found Sera's good lamp, and turned the wick high. If I was going to do this, I was going to do it under honest light, looking her in the face.

"What I'm going to tell you is the thing that put me in that cell," I said. "So understand that I've had eleven years to rehearse it, and it still doesn't get easier, and if you decide I'm mad, I'll genuinely not blame you. Madness would be tidier than the truth."

"Vale."

"Right. Yes." I sat back down. "Tell me first — what were you taught the wards *were*? The border wards of Thornfell. The great lattice. What does the guild teach?"

She recited it like a catechism, which meant she'd learned it young. "The Wards of Thornfell are a standing enchantment, anchored at thirteen gate-stones, maintained by the Crown Wardwrights' College, separating the settled lands from the Hollow Marches. They were raised after the Sundering by the first King Aldric and his college of—"

"Stop there. 'Raised by the first King Aldric.' Raised *how*? What power source? Every working needs fuel, Iversen. A candle burns wax. A forge burns coal. A lattice spanning four hundred miles of border, burning continuously for nine hundred years — what does it burn?"

She was silent. I watched her arrive at it, watch the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"Memory," she said.

"Royal memory. Specifically." I held up my hand and began counting fingers, because this was the part where I always counted, even alone, even in the dark, even talking to myself through a wall. "One: the wards cannot be woven from ordinary remembering. Ordinary memory fades, distorts, lies. The veil needs *true* memory — memory sworn under oath, witnessed, bound. Two: only royal blood can swear memory strong enough to anchor a working that size. Three: therefore, every king of Thornfell since Aldric the First has fed the wards pieces of himself. His coronation day. His mother's face. His children's names. The taste of his wedding feast. Whatever he could bear to give." I folded the fingers down one by one. "The Wards of Thornfell are not a wall, Iversen. They are a *biography*. Nine hundred years of kings, poured out stone by stone, holding the dark on the other side of the page."

"And the Regent—"

"Ate the biography." I let that sit. "From inside. He didn't break the wards. Breaking them would have taken armies and ten years of siege-craft, and everyone would have noticed. Instead he did something far more elegant. He found the royal line's memory — the living archive, the blood that still carried the pattern — and he consumed it at its source. The wards didn't collapse, Iversen. They were *digested*. When the stars went out, that wasn't the wall falling. That was the book being finished."

Zephyrine stood up. Walked to the shutter. Stood there with her back to me for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was very controlled, which in a swordspeaker is roughly equivalent to another man screaming.

"How long have you known?"

"Known for certain? Since the night before my arrest. Suspected since I was a journeyman and noticed the coronation oaths in the old liturgies read suspiciously like *fuel contracts*." I rubbed my eyes. "I sent the crown four letters. Polite ones. Then less polite ones. The fourth one used the phrase *the hungry thing behind the veil*, which I stand by as a description and regret deeply as a rhetorical choice, because it gave the court everything they needed to have me declared insane rather than inconvenient."

"And the Regent. The thing wearing the king. You know what it is."

"I know what it *isn't*. It isn't a demon, whatever the priests are preaching. Demons come from outside. This came from—" I hesitated. "Later. One horror at a time. Tonight the horror is yours."

She turned. "Mine."

"Your blade." I pointed at it, at the plain scabbard, the worn leather, the weapon that looked like a thousand soldiers' weapons because that was exactly what it wanted to look like. "You think swordspeaking is technique. Form-memory. Echoes. Let me ask you something, Iversen. Your grandmother died when you were nine. How old is the tradition?"

"Older than the wards."

"Older than the wards. And in all that time, how many swordspeakers? Hundreds? Thousands? Each one trained, each one sworn, each one carrying voices in her steel?" I stood, because I couldn't sit still for this part; I never could. "Technique doesn't survive like that. Technique dies with the body unless someone writes it down, and there are no books, are there? No manuals. No treatises. The guild teaches mouth to ear, hand to hand — and yet every generation, the voices get *clearer*, not fainter. Explain that with music boxes."

"Then what—"

"Oaths." The word came out of me harder than I intended, and I saw her flinch, and I didn't stop, because softening it would have been a lie and she'd earned the truth at full weight. "An oath is a binding. It takes a piece of the swearer and fixes it in place — in the witness, in the words, in the *object* the oath is sworn upon. Every swordspeaker swears her oaths on her blade. Every ancestor swore hers on hers. And the blades pass down, Iversen. Or the steel gets reforged, the old metal folded into the new, oath layered on oath layered on oath, nine hundred years deep. Your sword isn't carrying echoes. Your sword is a * reliquary*. Every voice in it is a whole person — their memories, their loves, their deaths, all the pieces of themselves they fixed into the steel when they knelt and swore. Your grandmother is in there. Her grandmother is in there. Some nameless woman who fought at the Sundering is in there. You've been walking around with a library of souls on your hip, and the guild let you believe it was a music box because the truth would have made you ask questions like *whose* memories feed the wards, and *where else* might such a concentration of sworn memory draw hungry attention."

The silence afterward had texture. Weight. Somewhere above us a cart rattled over cobbles, and the sound seemed enormously far away, happening in some other, kinder world.

Zephyrine looked down at the sword across her knees.

"She talked to me when I was a child," she said. "Halda. Before she died. She used to rest her hand on the flat of the blade and close her eyes, and I thought she was praying. And once — once she said, *they're all still here, little wren, every one of them, and someday you'll hear them too, and it will feel like drowning in family.*" Her laugh came out cracked down the middle. "I thought it was a figure of speech."

"It was a warning."

"It was a *family reunion*." She drew the blade — slowly, deliberately, laying it bare across both palms like an offering. Up close, in the lamplight, I could see what I'd been hoping not to see: the faint movement under the steel's surface. Not reflection. Not oil-sheen. A slow, deep shifting, the way darkness moves over water when something enormous passes beneath.

And the blade *hummed*.

Not loudly. A thread of sound, felt more than heard, rising toward her hands, toward her skin — and I watched her face change as it touched her, watched her pupils dilate, watched her breathe go shallow, because I knew what she was feeling. Recognition. Welcome. A hundred dead women saying *there you are, we've been waiting, come home.*

"Stop," I said sharply. "Don't answer it. Don't reach for it. Put your walls up — yes, I know you don't know how, imagine the pass, imagine the cold, cold is good—"

She wrenched her hands back. The hum faded to nothing, sullen, patient. She was shaking.

"Why did it do that?" Her voice was thin. "Why now? Why me?"

"Because you're the last," I said gently. "Because the line has thinned and thinned until it comes down to one woman and one blade, and because—" I stopped. Started again. "Because the thing wearing your king can smell her own dinner, Iversen. Think about what the Regent eats. Sworn royal memory. Where in all Thornfell is there a greater hoard of sworn memory than that blade? Where is there a *living carrier* of the oath-line, a woman whose own mind is braided into the same weave as her ancestors'? You're not just carrying the counterweight to the wards, Iversen. You're carrying a *feast*. Everything the Regent consumed to bring the stars down, condensed into one sword and one stubborn woman who refuses to put it down."

"So I'm a target."

"You're the target. The hunters, the hollow-eyed ones — they're not hunting the boy, they're not hunting me, though we're useful and I flatter myself we're entertaining. They're following *you*. Every road you walk remembers you; every step you take rings against that steel like a bell. You could strip naked, abandon the blade, change your name, and walk into the sea, and the hunger would still lean toward you like a compass finding north." I spread my hands. "I'm sorry. I truly am. I've had eleven years to find a gentler way to say it and there isn't one."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she picked the sword back up — and here is the thing I will remember about Zephyrine Iversen until whatever memory-devouring horror eventually comes for me: she did it without hesitation. She slid it home into the scabbard with the tenderness of someone buckling a child into a coat against winter, and she said,

"Then we move faster."

"*We*—"

"You knew," she said. "Eleven years ago you knew, and you wrote letters anyway, knowing what it would cost, because someone had to. That makes you the second-stupidest brave man in Thornfell, and the first is currently a king who ate his own biography for breakfast for nine hundred years of predecessors." She slung the scabbard across her back. "You're coming with me, Vale. To the capital. I want to see this thing wearing the crown with my own eyes, and I want a wardwright beside me when I do, because apparently I've been carrying a cathedral on my hip and nobody thought to mention it."

"Ah," I said. "Yes. About the capital."

"You already have a plan."

"I have the outline of a plan. The plan involves a wine merchant's cart, a forged manifest, and my personal guarantee that Lord Chancellor Veyre's mistress buys her claret exclusively from a man who owes me money." I was already moving, already gathering the maps I'd memorized rather than risk owning, because some habits survive even a coffin-cell. "But understand what you're asking. The court is where the hunger is thickest. Walking into that palace with that blade is like walking a bleeding lamb through a wolf convention — polite wolves, charming wolves, wolves who will compliment your boots while their eyes go glassy—"

"I've fought worse than wolves."

"You haven't fought *hungry*," I said. "Hunger is patient, Iversen. Hunger says please. Hunger wears your king's smile and asks after your mother, and you walk out of the audience chamber missing the sound of her voice and thanking him for the honor." I paused at the foot of the stairs, hand on the latch, and made myself say the last of it, the part I'd been circling all night. "There's one more thing. About the blade. About why the Regent wants it so badly — badly enough to burn the borders down rather than wait."

She waited.

"The wards were woven from royal memory," I said. "Which means the pattern of the wards — the true lattice, the original design — exists nowhere anymore except in the memories it was made from. The kings gave theirs away. The Regent ate them. The complete pattern survives in exactly one place." I nodded at the scabbard on her back. "Every oath those kings ever swore was witnessed, Iversen. And the witnesses were swordspeakers. Your ancestors stood at every coronation, every vow, every feeding of the wards. Their blades drank it all in. That sword doesn't just carry the counterweight to the veil." I met her eyes. "It carries the *instructions*. You are carrying the only surviving map of the Wards of Thornfell — and the thing that ate them knows it."

For a moment she just stood there, lamplight moving on the worn leather at her shoulder, and I watched her understand that the weight of inheritance I'd described was worse than a burden. It was a bullseye.

"Well," she said at last, with the ghost of the crooked grin that I would learn to dread and rely on in equal measure, "at least we won't need to stop and ask directions."

And that was when the knock came at the cellar door — three quick raps and one slow, which was Sera's signal for trouble — and through the wood, in a voice that was Sera's and yet somehow not, a pleasant, echoing laugh asked whether the swordspeaker was comfortable, and whether she might care to share what was in the pretty knife.
