## 6. Thirteen Turns

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a room where a man has been doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish. I knew it well. I had lived in that silence for eleven years, ever since the crown took my credentials and the dungeons took everything else, in roughly that order.

The cellar beneath Widow Sarn's dye-house was not built for scholarship. It was built for vats of mordant and the slow rotting of wool, which meant it smelled like a wet dog's regrets and stayed at a temperature designed to keep corpses honest. But it had one virtue: no windows. If the Hollow Regent's hunters looked for us by moonlight — and they did; I'd seen what the moons did to their eyes — then windowless was the only way to live.

So I sat among Zephyrine's smuggled ledgers, three stolen ward-law codices, and a candle stub I kept relighting because blowing it out felt too much like surrender, and I did the arithmetic.

Zephyrine came down the ladder just past midnight, moving the way she always moved, like the floor owed her money. She'd been out checking the safehouse rotations. She carried two bowls of something Widow Sarn called stew and I called an act of aggression against root vegetables.

"Eat," she said, setting one beside me. "You've been reading since dawn."

"I'm working."

"You're hiding behind work. There's a difference. I know it intimately." She pulled a crate over and sat across from me, elbows on knees, sword across her lap like a cat that thought itself a dog. "Tell me anyway."

I told her anyway. That was the trouble with Zephyrine Iversen: she asked questions the way other people drew blades, and you found yourself answering before you'd decided to.

* * *

Here is what the old ward-laws say, for those who enjoy ancient texts with the warmth of a tax audit.

The veil between Thornfell and whatever presses against Thornfell from outside — the codices call it the Far Dark, which tells you how much our ancestors liked naming things — was not built. It was *woven*. And the thread was memory. Royal memory, specifically. The first wardwrights discovered that a king's remembered years could be spun into lattice-work strong enough to hold back the hungry dark, and so every monarch of the old line gave portions of themselves to the border. Their childhoods. Their griefs. Their names, eventually. That was the covenant: the crown eats the king, and the king feeds the veil, and everyone else gets to sleep soundly.

I had written all this down once, in a report to the crown, eleven years ago. They had responded by arresting me for sedition, on the grounds that a veil woven from royal memory implied kings had been giving things up, and kings do not care to be reminded of their own generosity.

"The wards didn't break," I said to Zephyrine, turning the oldest codex toward her. The page showed a diagram of the lattice — concentric rings of script, each ring a decade of some dead king's life. "They were eaten. From inside. Whatever wears Aldric's face has been consuming the royal memory-reserves for ten years, ring by ring. The stars going out wasn't an attack. It was a *digestion*."

She was quiet for a moment. In the candlelight her face did the thing it always did when bad news arrived — nothing at all, which was worse than any expression. "And the boy's shard?"

"Fragment of the outermost ring. The last decade fed into the weave before the Regent started gorging. It still holds echoes. Which is why Ash sees memories that aren't his." I tapped the diagram. "Which brings me to the part you're not going to like."

"I never like any part. Say it faster."

"The veil can be reforged. The law is specific about how." I turned to the passage I'd copied out three times, as if repetition might soften it. "*By the unbound hand of the true king, upon the original seam, with craft and willing memory.* Four requirements. A true king — meaning Aldric, or what's left of him. The original seam — meaning the first citadel, which is now a ruin under forty feet of glacier. Craft — meaning me, God help us all. And willing memory, freely given, from an oath-bound blade."

Her eyes went to her sword. Then to me. Then back to the page, as if she could arrest the sentence for trespassing.

"No," she said.

"It doesn't have to be—"

"Merric." She said my name the way you'd set down something heavy. "My blade holds every oath my line ever swore. Every ancestor. Their voices, their roads, their faces. It's the only thing I have left of—" She stopped. Recalibrated. When she spoke again her voice was level, which cost her more than shouting would have. "How much?"

"That depends on how much of the weave survived. Could be half. Could be all of it."

"And if it's all?"

I didn't answer fast enough, and she read the answer in my face, because Zephyrine missed nothing except, historically, her own best interests.

"Then I pour myself into the veil," she said, "and stand there holding an empty stick."

"The alternative is the Regent finishing its meal."

That was when I put the second piece of arithmetic on the table, and I want it noted that my hands were steady. Cold, but steady. Eleven years in a cell teaches you to deliver bad news without flinching, mostly because flinching requires hope and hope was confiscated along with my tools.

* * *

The Regent's hunger is not random. Nothing about it is random. It follows the lunar calendar of the old faith — thirteen moon-turns, the full cycle the first wardwrights used to bind the veil in the first place. The wards were keyed to that rhythm. Thirteen turns, thirteen rings of memory, thirteen locks in one long chain.

The Regent has been eating along the same pattern. One ring per turn. Ten turns done. The stars went out at the start of the eleventh.

"When the thirteenth turn completes," I said, "the lattice is empty. Fully consumed. And here's the part the codices are very clear about, in language I can only describe as smug." I slid the page across. "*What is wholly eaten cannot be rehung. The veil, once unstrung entire, does not return.*"

Zephyrine read it twice. "Meaning?"

"Meaning after the thirteenth turn, there's nothing left to reforge. No anchor points. The Far Dark comes through permanently, wearing whatever face it likes, and it already likes ours." I leaned back against the cold stone. "Eleven weeks, Zephyrine. Give or take. The thirteenth moon completes its turn on the night of the Long Dark festival, which our ancestors presumably chose for the binding because they had a sense of theater, and which the Regent will choose for the ending for the same reason."

"Eleven weeks." She said it flatly, testing the weight. "To find a hollowed king who doesn't know his own name, dig him out of a glacier, march him to a ruined citadel, and convince you to do the finest wardwork of your career with stolen tools."

"When you list it like that it sounds ambitious."

"It sounds insane."

"Yes," I agreed. "But notice it's a very *specific* insanity. That's the difference between us and despair. Despair is general."

Almost, almost, she smiled. Then it passed, and she looked at the diagram again, at the rings, at the small notation where the twelfth ring should be — the one currently being devoured, right now, while we sat in a basement smelling of sheep.

"What happens during the twelfth turn?" she asked. "If we're mid-cycle when we reach him?"

"Then we're racing the Regent's appetite. Every week we lose, another ring goes dark, and the reforging needs more from your blade to compensate." I met her eyes. "Every delay costs you memories. That's the exchange rate. Time for self. The Regent eats the world's past; we'll be eating yours."

She was silent a long moment. Outside, somewhere above us, Cinderhollow's refugees were sleeping badly in the temple hall, dreaming of children whose faces slipped away from them like fish. I thought about that. I thought about how the Regent didn't need to hunt them. It had already taken what mattered most, casually, in passing, the way a fire takes a house while looking for the next one.

"There's one more thing," I said. "The part I've been circling since sundown."

"You've been circling since sundown? Merric, you've been circling since the stars went out. Out with it."

"The codices describe what happens to a person who gives memory to the weave willingly. It's not like being robbed. Robbery leaves the shape of the thing behind — you feel the absence. Willing surrender is different. The law calls it *unbinding*. You don't lose the memory. You lose the *claim* to it. The road you walked stops being yours. The face you loved becomes a stranger's face you happen to remember." I turned the candle stub, watching the wax pool. "The first wardwrights wrote that the ones who wove the original seam didn't survive it as themselves. They walked away whole-bodied and hollow-hearted, and they founded villages, and married, and died, and none of them ever went home, because home had stopped meaning anything."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm a disgraced wardwright living in a dye cellar. Cheerfulness would be dishonest."

Zephyrine stood. Paced the length of the cellar — four steps, turn, four steps back, the walk of someone who'd spent her life in spaces too small for her legs. Her hand rested on the sword's pommel, and I swear the blade hummed, low, like a dog hearing its name in another room.

"My ancestors gave their oaths to that steel," she said finally. "Willingly. Generation after generation. They knew what swordspeaking cost and they paid it anyway, because someone had to hold the line." She looked at me. "You're telling me the line ends with me pouring the last of them into a wall."

"I'm telling you the law offers no discount."

"Of course it doesn't. Laws never do. Only people pay." She stopped pacing. "Eleven weeks. Fine. We leave for the glacier in three days — I need two days to arrange the caravan routes and one day to argue with Widow Sarn about supplies. You'll spend them memorizing every ward-formula you can, because when we get to that citadel, you'll be working from a head that might get emptied, and I want redundancy."

"Redundancy," I repeated. "You want me to memorize my own craft in case I forget it."

"I want *copies*, Merric. Of everything. That's what survival is — copies, backups, a second knife in the boot. Haven't you learned that yet?" She picked up both bowls, hers untouched, mine untouched, and looked at the cold stew with the expression of a woman adding it to a very long bill. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we plan the route. Tonight—" she paused at the ladder, and for one unguarded moment she looked tired all the way through, "—tonight I'm going to sit with my sword and listen to my grandmother's voice, while I still know why it matters."

She climbed up. The trapdoor closed. The candle guttered.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat with the codices and did the arithmetic again, because arithmetic is a prayer for people who've lost the habit of praying.

Eleven weeks. Three days to organize. Call it nine weeks of travel and ruin-delving and whatever violence waited between here and the glacier. Nine weeks against a hunger that had been patient for ten years and would not tolerate being interrupted politely.

And underneath the schedule, underneath the route-planning and the supply lists, the true ledger, the one I kept in the place where my guilt lived rent-free:

*Unbound hand. Original seam. Craft. Willing memory.*

Four requirements, and I was the third. My craft. My hands, which had once signed the reports that got my family's names struck from the guild rolls, which had trembled in a courtroom while men in fine robes explained that warning the crown about the hungry thing behind the veil constituted treason against the crown's peace of mind. I had spent eleven years telling myself that disgrace was a debt I'd finished paying. Now the ledger reopened, and the currency was the same as it had always been: what I knew, given freely, to people who had every reason not to trust me.

Ash came down the ladder around the third hour, silent as smoke, the way he always did. He'd heard none of it — or all of it; with that boy it was impossible to say. He looked at the open codex, at the diagram of rings, at the candle burning down to a fingernail of wax.

"Is it bad?" he asked.

"It's a deadline."

"I don't know what those are." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. He crossed the cellar and sat beside me, and his shard-hand — the palm fused with fallen wardstone — caught the light and threw it back wrong, faintly green, like deep water. "You were talking about giving things up. The lady's voice went quiet when you said it. Quiet like the refugees go quiet."

"Ash—"

"I give things up all the time," he said. "Things I find. Things people show me. I don't miss them, because missing needs a before, and I don't have one of those." He held up the shard-hand and studied it, the way he studied everything, like a scholar of his own strange condition. "But she has a before. Lots of them. And you're saying she has to feed them to a wall."

"I'm saying the law asks it."

"Laws." He made a small disgusted sound, far too old for his face. "Whose laws? Who decided the wall gets to eat people?"

"The same people who built the wall to stop something worse from eating everyone. It's not a good bargain, Ash. It's just the only one on the table."

He was quiet for a while. Then he said, "When we do it — when she pours them out — will the voices in her sword go somewhere? Or just end?"

I looked at him. Thirteen years old, maybe, with no name of his own and a parasite's leavings fused to his hand, asking the question the first wardwrights had spent three volumes dodging.

"They go into the veil," I said. "Into the thing that keeps the dark out. Every road she remembers, every oath her ancestors swore — it all becomes part of what holds. They don't end. They become the reason anyone gets to have a tomorrow."

"Does she get to be part of that too? After?"

"That depends what 'she' means afterward."

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, and I realized with a lurch that he understood unbinding better than I did — better than the codices did. He was already unbound. He was what the process left behind, walking and breathing and asking kind questions. He was the invoice, made flesh.

"Then we should hurry," Ash said, and lay down on the floor with his arm under his head, and was asleep in moments, the way only the truly innocent and the truly ruined can sleep.

I watched the candle die. In the dark, I laid out the route in my head: north through the Cinderhollow hills, east along the remembering roads — Zephyrine's roads, glowing with her footsteps, betraying us mile by mile to anything that hunted by memory — then up into the teeth of the Whitereach, where the first citadel slept under its glacier with a king inside it who couldn't remember being one.

Nine weeks. Thirteen turns. One seam.

Somewhere above us, the twelfth moon was eating.

I got out fresh paper and began copying the ward-laws by touch, letter by letter, building my redundancy in the dark — because Zephyrine was right, survival is copies, and because I had a sudden, cold certainty that before this was over, I would need to know these words even if I forgot knowing them.

The pen scratched. The clock ticked in the only language clocks speak.

Eleven weeks. Cold hands. Warm ink.

God forgive us all, we were going to be on time.
