## 8. Ash's First Memory

I should probably explain something before the river, because everything that happened at the river will make more sense if you know it: I don't remember anything. Not my name — I've since picked one, but we'll get to that — not my mother's face, not the color of my own front door. Nothing before roughly three weeks ago, when I woke up in the back of a smuggler's wagon with a shard of broken wardstone fused to my palm like the world's least comforting birthmark.

What I *do* have is other people's memories. Fragments of them, anyway, leaking out of the shard in my hand at inconvenient moments. I know how a woman in Cinderhollow likes her tea, which is useless, because she's dead and so is her kettle. I know a shepherd's prayer for lost lambs. I know what it feels like to be very old and very tired and still afraid of the dark.

The shard doesn't ask before it shows me things. That's the part nobody warns you about. It just opens a door in your head and walks through with its boots on.

Zephyrine says everyone has something they'd trade for that — a door into other lives, a way out of their own for an hour. Zephyrine says a lot of things in a dry voice while sharpening a sword that whispers. I've decided she's mostly right about the world and entirely wrong about herself, and I haven't told her either of these opinions yet, because she's the only reason I'm alive and there are rules about biting the hand.

Merric says the shard is a piece of the border wards, the great woven veil that used to keep the hungry thing behind the sky from eating Thornfell. The wards broke. The stars went out. And a piece of them landed on me, or I landed on it, and now I'm carrying a splinter of a dying country around in my fist.

That's the setup. Here's the river.

* * *

We came down to the ford at Greywater late in the day, when the light had gone the color of old pewter and the mountains behind us looked like they were deciding whether to follow. The road remembered Zephyrine here — I could see it, faint gold lines waking up under her boots as she walked, like the ground was greeting an old friend. She hated it. She kept saying roads that remember are roads that tattle, and she was right, but more on that later, because the tattling hadn't started yet.

"Water's high," Merric said, crouching at the bank and doing his wardwright squint, which looks exactly like a man who regrets every choice he's ever made examining a river. Which is accurate. "Snowmelt off the eastern face. It'll be thigh-deep in the channel."

"Then we cross in the shallows by the stones," Zephyrine said, "and we cross fast, and Ash stays between us, and nobody stops to admire the scenery."

"I never stop to admire the scenery," Merric said. "Scenery and I have an understanding. It leaves me alone; I leave it un-warded."

I was carrying the packs. Both of mine and half of hers, because she let me, because letting me carry things was her way of saying I wasn't cargo anymore, I was company. I want to be clear that at this point in my life that distinction was the entire architecture of my self-worth. I had no name, no past, no country — but I carried the packs, and therefore I existed.

The ford was quiet. Too quiet, if you believe the stories, and I do, because I live inside one now. No birds. No insects humming over the reeds. Just water going over stones and wind going through alders and the particular silence of a place holding its breath.

Zephyrine stopped halfway across.

Her blade came out of its sheath without her drawing it — I know how that sounds, but I saw it. The whispering sword slid two inches free on its own, the way a dog lifts its head before the knock comes. The whisper got louder. It always whispered, but usually in a murmur, like an old woman talking in her sleep. This was different. This was urgent.

"Back," Zephyrine said. One word. She said most important things in one word. "Ash — *back.*"

Merric grabbed my collar. I dropped a pack. The river took it, and I remember thinking, absurdly, *there goes the dried plums,* and then the world ended.

Not really. But that's what it felt like, and I'm told honesty matters in narration, so: it felt like the end of the world, delivered personally, to me.

They came out of the treeline on both banks at once. Hollow-eyed hunters — the Regent's people, or what was left of people after the Regent got done with them. Their eyes weren't empty exactly. Empty would have been better. There was *something* in them, deep down, like looking down a well and seeing your own reflection at the bottom, except the reflection wasn't yours and didn't blink.

There were six on our side of the river. Later I would learn that was a hunting party, not an army, and that we were lucky. At the time, six felt like a number invented specifically to kill us.

"Run for the far bank," Zephyrine said, and then she did something I still think about. She turned *toward* them. Toward six of them. With a sword. Because we were slower than her, and she'd decided the arithmetic worked if she stood in the middle of it.

Merric hauled me upstream along the bank, toward the stones, shouting something about the crossing being shallowest there, and I went, because my legs are loyal to whoever shouts loudest. Behind us I heard the first clash of steel and Zephyrine's voice saying something in a language I didn't know — oath-speech, the swordspeaker tongue — and the air over the river shivered like it had been struck.

A hunter cut across the shallows toward us. He moved wrong. Not fast-wrong, not slow-wrong. *Remembered*-wrong, like a puppet performing the idea of running rather than the act of it. His sword came up. Merric shoved me. I fell in the shallows, cold water to my chin, and the man raised his blade over me, and his hollow eyes looked down at me with something almost like apology, and I thought: *this is it. Three weeks of existence, ending in a ford, and I never even learned my own name.*

And the shard woke up.

* * *

Here is what happened next, as best I can tell it, because I was there and I still barely understand it.

The shard in my palm opened — not physically, nothing bled, nothing broke — but something *opened*, the way a door opens, and instead of showing me a fragment, a scrap, a leftover taste of someone else's life, it showed me *everything.*

A soldier. A dead soldier — I understood that immediately, the way you understand things in dreams, completely and without evidence. He'd died somewhere near this river, years ago, maybe decades, and some residue of him had soaked into the wardstone lattice the way wine soaks into a table. The wards recorded him. The wards recorded *everything* — that's what they were for, holding memory, holding the shape of a country against the dark. And he had died fighting. He had died fighting *here*, and his last battle lived on in the lattice like a song stuck in a wall.

The shard gave him to me.

I felt his hands close around mine from the inside. I felt his grip — sword calluses, an old break in the left ring finger badly set. I felt the way he held a blade, which was low and patient and economical, a man who had learned to fight from a grandfather who learned it the hard way. I felt his fear, too. His fear was enormous and very calm, the fear of someone who had made peace with it years ago and simply carried it like a second sword.

His body knew what to do even though mine didn't. That's the only way I can describe it. My arms moved and it wasn't me moving them, and it also *was* me, because for those few seconds the boundary between me and him had gotten thin as wet paper.

I caught his descending sword on the flat. The impact went up both our arms — his ghost-arm, my real one — and I heard him say, in a voice worn smooth like a river stone, *low and turn, boy, low and turn,* and I did, and the hunter stumbled past me into the water.

Another came. Low and turn. Parry. Step through. His hip remembers where the man's ribs are; trust the hip. I stepped through and the second hunter folded over my — over *his* — pommel and sat down hard in the river and didn't get up, because whatever animated him, it needed the body intact, and bodies have opinions about pommels to the sternum.

A third. I was screaming. I don't know when I started. I think I was screaming his name, except I didn't know his name, so I was screaming the *shape* of his name, the hole where it should go. *Low and turn.* The third hunter fell. The fourth thought better of it and backed up the bank, hollow eyes fixed on me with something new in them now, and the new thing was recognition. They'd been sent for Zephyrine. Nobody had warned them about me. I watched the realization move across his ruined face like weather: *the boy is a door, and doors can be walked through from both sides.*

Then it was over. Four down, two fled, Zephyrine standing in the shallows breathing hard with her whispering sword bare at last and black ichor — or something like ichor, something that steamed and smelled like burnt sugar — smoking off the edge. Merric was on his knees in the water staring at me with his mouth open, which, for the record, was the most expressive I'd ever seen him.

"Ash," Zephyrine said.

I didn't answer, because I'd just realized two things at once, in this order:

First: I had named myself. In the middle of the fight, or just after it, or maybe during — sometime — I had called myself Ash, and it had stuck, the way names stick when you choose them yourself. Ash. For the burned things. For the wardstone, which crumbles grey when it breaks. For what's left after a fire that you can still hold in your hand. I was Ash. I existed. I had a name I'd made, out of wreckage, with my own mouth.

Second: I was crying so hard I couldn't stand up.

* * *

They got me to the far bank. Zephyrine built a fire — small, screened, smuggler-style — and Merric checked the hunters' bodies and reported, grimly, that they weren't dead, exactly, just switched off, and that they'd be walking again by morning unless he did something unpleasant with chalk and wire, which he did, unpleasantly.

I sat against a stone with my knees up and cried.

It wasn't elegant crying. It wasn't the single tear kind. It was the full-body kind, the kind that comes up from somewhere below the stomach, and I couldn't stop it and I couldn't explain it, and the worst part — the truly humiliating worst part — was that I knew *exactly* whose grief it was.

His. The soldier's. All of it, all at once, uncompressed. I had his last battle in my head now, complete: the mud of this same riverbank, the horns, the banner going down in the reeds, his brother-in-arms Daven taking a spear meant for him, the moment he understood the line was broken and the pass was lost and the wards above were starting to *sing wrong*. I had his wife's face — Marta, brown-eyed, laughing at something he'd said about a horse. I had the letter he never finished writing her. I had the exact weight of the moment the spear took him below the ribs, and the strange, floating surprise of it, and the grass against his cheek, and the sky — *the sky with stars in it*, hundreds of stars, a whole spilled sack of them — tilting away above him as he died believing someone else would finish the wall.

He'd been afraid his son wouldn't remember his face. That was the last whole thought he had. Not the pain. Not Marta. *Will Tam remember my face?*

And now I couldn't stop seeing Marta's face, and I had never met her, and she was dust, and her son was dust or worse, and this man had poured himself into the stones of his country like water into cracks, trusting the country to hold him, and the country had broken, and I was the crack he'd leaked into, and I was sitting by a fire holding a dead man's grief in my chest like a swallowed stone, and it wasn't mine, none of it was mine, and I would carry it forever because the shard doesn't give things *back.*

"Do you want to talk about it?" Zephyrine asked.

She crouched across the fire from me, sword across her knees, watching me with those grey road-worn eyes. She asks questions like that — flat, direct, no cushioning — and somehow they're kinder for it. Cushions are lies you sit on.

"He had a wife," I said. My voice came out wrecked. "Marta. She laughed at his horse jokes. He died thinking his son wouldn't remember his face." I wiped my nose on my sleeve. "I know what their house looked like. Blue door. Why do I know that? Why is *that* the thing that stayed?"

"Memory's strange about doors," Merric said quietly, from where he was packing away his chalk. He didn't look at me. Merric rarely looks at anyone directly; I think faces cost him something. "People keep the small things because the small things are load-bearing. You take the blue door away and the whole house falls down."

"He's *in me.*" I pressed my fist — the shard-fist — against my sternum. "He fought through me. I killed two men with his hands. I felt him die. He's been dead for — how long? How long has he been dead?"

Merric did the math on his fingers, which involves a lot of muttering. "The ford engagement. Forty years. Perhaps forty-five."

"Forty-five years." I laughed, and it came out horrible. "Forty-five years he's been sitting in a rock waiting for somebody to pick him up, and the somebody is the boy with no memories, because apparently the universe has a sense of humor, and it's a *bad one—*"

"You saved us." Zephyrine said it plainly, the way she says everything. "All three of us. With a dead man's sword-craft, in your first fight, at fourteen—" she paused, reassessed, "—fifteen? Sixteen?"

"I don't know," I said. "That's the point. I don't know anything. I don't know how old I am. I know a dead soldier's wife's laugh and I don't know my own birthday. Is that a victory to you?"

"Yes," she said, without hesitation, and that shut me up, because she wasn't being wry. She meant it. "You were going to die at that ford. Now you're not. Everything else is commentary."

I looked at my hand. The shard sat in the palm, grey and veined with faint light, looking — I swear — smug. "It didn't ask," I said. "None of them ask. It just opens me up and pours things in. It's been doing it since Cinderhollow and today it gave me a whole *person*, a whole dead man, and it didn't ask, and I can't give him back, and I can't put him down, and I don't even know if what I'm feeling right now is me or him, and that's — that's the part —"

My voice cracked. Zephyrine crossed the fire and sat down next to me, which she never does, because Zephyrine maintains distance the way other people maintain weapons. She didn't touch me. She just sat, shoulder a hand-span from mine, and looked at the fire.

"I know what it's like," she said finally, "to have a head full of voices that aren't yours."

"The sword."

"The sword." She turned it slightly so the firelight ran along the blade. It whispered, softly, the way it does when it's content, which is a sentence I never expected to think. "Forty generations of oaths in this steel. Some nights it's loud. Some nights I can't find myself in it. And some mornings I wake up grieving someone I never met, and I have to lie there and sort out whose sorrow it is before I can stand up." She glanced at me. "You learn to label them. That's the trick. *This one's Grandmother Vess. This one's the Oath of the Ford. This one's mine.* You put little signs on the doors. It doesn't make them quieter. But it means when you open one, you know who's going to walk out."

"And him?" I asked. "The soldier. What sign do I put on his door?"

She considered. Firelight moved on her face. "Tam's father," she said at last. "Brave at Greywater. Bad at horses. Loved a woman who laughed." A pause. "That's a good life to be the keeper of, Ash. Heavy. But good."

Ash. She used the name like it had always been mine, like she'd known it before I did. Maybe she had. Maybe that's what she was doing all those weeks, handing me pieces of myself and calling them obvious.

Merric cleared his throat from the edge of the firelight, wearing the expression of a man about to ruin a moment because ruining moments is his job. "There's a complication," he said. "Two, actually. The smaller one: the hunters will report what they saw. The Regent now knows the boy is a working shard-bearer, and it knows shard-bearers can be *used.* It will come for him specifically. It may already be listening through what's left of them."

"Wonderful," Zephyrine said.

"The larger one," Merric continued, with the relish of a scholar delivering bad news he finds genuinely interesting, "is what the boy did to the lattice. When he drew on the soldier, he didn't just borrow a memory. He pulled it *through* the ward-shard. Into the living world. Do you understand what that means?" He looked between us, and for once his eyes were bright with something other than regret. "The wards aren't just broken. They're *accessible.* If a shard-bearer can pull memory out of the weave — if the weave can be reached — then the weave might be repairable. From inside."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, the river kept saying the same thing it always says, the way rivers and grandmothers do.

"So there's a door," Zephyrine said slowly, "into the thing that ate our country."

"There's a door," Merric agreed. "And a boy holding the key. Who happens to be wanted by the hungriest thing in creation." He smiled, thinly, terribly. "So. Shall we discuss where we're going, or shall we wait for the moon to finish turning and skip the suspense?"

I looked down at my hand — at the shard, at the ghost-calluses I could still feel under my skin, at the little labelled door where a dead man waited with his unfinished letter and his blue-door house.

"North," I said.

They both looked at me.

"North," I said again, and my voice steadied, because it was his steadiness and mine too, and I was done sorting out whose was whose. "He died holding this river. Somebody should find out why it mattered."
