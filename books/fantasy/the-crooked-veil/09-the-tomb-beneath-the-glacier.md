## 9. The Tomb Beneath the Glacier

The glacier had a name once. Merric insisted on this, the way he insisted on most things nobody asked about — that everything in Thornfell had a name, and forgetting it was how the rot started. *Vaskrjod*, he called it, from a dead dialect of a dead province: *the weeping ice*. Somewhere in the last century the name had been eaten by disuse, and now the maps just said THE WHITE FIELD, in letters so apologetic they looked embarrassed to exist.

We'd been climbing toward it for six days.

Six days of Ash asking questions I didn't have answers to, Merric muttering calculations into his beard like a man arguing with an invisible accountant, and me doing what I do best, which is walking and pretending not to be afraid. The road had taken my mentor's face at the pass — I still caught myself reaching for it some mornings, that shape behind my eyes, and finding only a smooth white nothing where a person used to live. You don't get used to that. You just get quieter about it.

"You're limping," Ash said, falling into step beside me. He'd stopped limping himself two days ago; whatever the wardshard was feeding him, it apparently included other people's ankles.

"I'm favoring," I corrected. "Limping is for amateurs."

"Your left knee sounds like a door in a haunted house."

"That's character. Swordspeakers are supposed to creak. It intimidates bandits."

He grinned, and I let him have it, because one of us should get to be young today. The boy walked with the shard fused to his palm held slightly away from his body, the way you carry something both precious and mildly accusatory. It had been glowing faintly since dawn. It had never glowed before.

"Merric," I called up the line. "The shard's doing its lantern impression again."

Merric stopped so fast I nearly walked into his pack. He turned, and I watched his face do the thing it did when knowledge arrived that he'd have preferred to keep paying rent elsewhere. "It's not reacting to us," he said slowly. "It's reacting to *proximity*. We're close enough that it's... resonating." He wiped his gloves on his coat, which he does when he's frightened and wants his hands to look busy. "Zephyrine. How close are we, by your reckoning?"

I squinted up the valley. The White Field filled it end to end, a river of ice frozen mid-lunge down from the peaks, blue-veined and groaning. And there, high on its shoulder where the ice curved like a cupped hand—

"There," I said. "That hollow. That's not natural carving."

"No," Merric said quietly. "No, it isn't."

* * *

You don't climb a glacier. You negotiate with it. We roped together and went up the shoulder in the grey afternoon light, boots biting crampons into ice that groaned beneath us like an old man shifting in bed. The wind came off the peaks with nothing between it and us for a hundred miles, and it carried that particular silence glaciers have — a silence with weight, a silence that has been accumulating interest for ten thousand years.

The hollow in the ice resolved as we climbed. It wasn't a cave. Caves are accidents. This was architecture — a facade cut into the living glacier, columns and a lintel, all of it sheathed in ice so clear it might have been glass, so old that the frost inside it had grown in patterns no frost makes anymore. Spirals. Oath-marks. The knotwork of the first citadel, the same patterns Merric had shown us sketched in his stolen research, drawn here on a scale that made my neck ache to look up at it.

And in the center, a door.

Not a grand door. That was the strange part. After the columns and the spiraling frost and the sheer audacity of building a tomb into a moving glacier, the door itself was plain — dark wood gone silver with age, iron-banded, unmarked except for a single slot at chest height. Long. Narrow. About the width of a blade.

"An oath-lock," Merric breathed. He'd gone pale, or maybe that was the cold; with Merric the two were hard to distinguish. "I read about them. Pre-Regency craft. They don't take keys. Keys can be stolen." His voice dropped, reverent and horrified in equal measure. "They take *oaths*. The lock doesn't check the metal, Zephyrine. It checks the soul holding it."

"Then we have a problem," Ash said, "because none of us—"

"It'll open for me," I said.

They both looked at me. The wind filled the pause with its opinion, which was indifference.

"The blade's been whispering since the wards fell," I said. I drew it — slow, because drawing it near Merric always felt like undressing in church. The steel came out singing that low note it had found lately, the one underneath my hearing, the voice I hadn't heard since I was a child kneeling in the snow with my mentor's hands over mine. "It knows this place. Or this place knows it. Either way—" I shrugged, and hated how casual I made it sound, because nothing about this felt casual, nothing about this felt anything less than like standing at the edge of a very tall drop. "Either way, I'm the key. Let's find out what I unlock."

"Zephyrine." Merric put a hand on my arm. His grip was stronger than a disgraced drunkard's ought to be. "Whatever's behind that door — the research says the king was hidden here *alive*. Do you understand? Not dead. Hidden. Which means someone sealed a living man into a glacier and told no one, and the wards were woven to make even the *location* forgettable, and the only reason we found it is that the wards are dead and the veil is bleeding and the whole country is coming apart at the seams." He swallowed. "What's behind that door has been alone for ten years. With nothing but its own thoughts. If it even has thoughts left."

"Then it's owed a visitor," I said. "Ten years late, but owed."

Ash said nothing. But when I turned to the door, he moved to stand at my shoulder, and Merric took the other side, and I understood without anyone saying it that they were arranging themselves the way you arrange yourselves around a friend before a firing squad. Present. Useless. There.

I slid the blade into the slot.

For a moment, nothing. Then the sword *sang* — not the low whisper, but full voice, every ancestral memory in the steel rising at once like a congregation standing for the entrance hymn, and the sound came out of the door too, out of the ice, out of the mountain itself, harmonics stacking on harmonics until my teeth ached and the glacier shivered and somewhere far below, something vast shifted its weight.

The lock drank. I felt it happen — felt the oath leave the blade the way warmth leaves your hand into cold iron, a pull, a tasting, the door deciding whether I was worth believing. My knees buckled. Ash caught my elbow. The world went white at the edges, and in the white I heard — briefly, horribly clearly — my own voice, aged eight, swearing my first oath into the snow while my mentor's face watched me with pride, and I thought: *no, I paid for that one already, take something else* —

The door opened.

Just opened. Inward, smooth as a drawer, onto darkness and air that smelled of stone and candle smoke and, impossibly, of *lilies*.

* * *

There were candles burning inside.

That was the first wrongness, and it took me three steps to understand why it was wrong. Ten years. A sealed tomb in a glacier. And dozens of candles guttering in wall niches along a descending corridor, their flames steady in the dead air, each one burning without consuming — the wax unmelted, the wicks unchanged. Ward-fire. Someone had lit these lights with craft, and the craft was still running, patient as arithmetic, waiting for a guest who was a decade overdue.

"They knew someone would come," Ash whispered. His shard-hand was blazing now, throwing shadows that moved wrong. "Eventually. They built the door to wait."

"Doors are patient," Merric murmured. "It's people who aren't. That's rather the tragedy of the species." He was scribbling as he walked, because of course he was, sketching the corridor's knotwork by candlelight with numb fingers, because the man could not walk through a miracle without taking notes.

The corridor descended in a long spiral. The walls were carved — I saw it gradually, the way you see a face in a crowd — with figures. Thousands of them. A procession of kings, I realized. Every ruler of Thornfell, generation after generation, marching down the spiral in stone relief, each one labeled in the old script, each one shown performing the same act: pressing a hand to a seam of light in the air. Touching the veil. Renewing it.

Every king but the last ones. The final carvings grew cruder, hurried, the chisel-work degrading like handwriting in a letter written while crying. The last figure was barely finished — a king with his hand raised toward a seam that the sculptor had left rough and unfinished, and below it, scratched in later, in a different hand entirely, smaller and desperate:

*HE WOULD NOT LET US USE HIM. GOD FORGIVE US. WE USED HIM ANYWAY.*

Merric read it aloud, and then wished he hadn't, and then read it again anyway, because Merric's guilt has always been the kind that picks at itself.

"We used him anyway," Ash repeated softly. He was looking at the little scratched words like they were addressed to him personally. Maybe they were. None of us knew whose memories that shard had been showing him lately, and lately it had been showing him a great deal.

At the bottom of the spiral, the corridor ended at a chamber.

I want to describe it honestly, because awe is cheap and I've spent too much of my life trading in it. The chamber was round, domed, perhaps forty feet across. The ceiling was a single sheet of clear ice, and above it, compressed and preserved in the glacier's heart, was the sky — ancient sky, trapped air, bubbles of light frozen mid-fall, so that looking up felt like looking up from the bottom of a well into a heaven that had been saved for later. Stars, fossilized. A whole night sky, kept the way you'd keep a letter from someone dead.

Someone had built this room under a piece of the old sky on purpose. So that whoever slept here would not have to lie under the empty one.

In the center of the chamber stood a bier of black stone. On the bier lay a man.

He was breathing.

Ten years. Sealed in ice. Alive. He lay on his back with his hands folded on his chest like an effigy, dressed in a king's clothes gone grey with age, and he was *breathing* — shallow, slow, the rhythm of a man sleeping through winter — and his face was handsome and ruined and utterly, catastrophically blank. Not peaceful. Blank. A face with nothing behind it, the way a house with the furniture removed is not restful but *empty*, and you feel the emptiness in your teeth.

Around the bier, arranged with terrible care, were objects. A crown, set aside — not worn, *set aside*, deliberately, the way you put down a burden. A signet ring. A folded letter, wax unbroken. A child's wooden horse, painted gold, worn smooth by small hands. And scattered across the floor around the bier, hundreds of them, thousands, were slips of paper, each covered in writing, each one pinned under a pebble against a wind that never blew down here.

Merric knelt and picked one up. His hands shook. He read it, and made a sound I have never heard a grown man make, and handed it to me without being able to speak.

The slip said, in a fine, weakening script:

*My name is Aldric. I have a daughter. Her name is—*

The next slip: *My name is Aldric. I am the king. I have a daughter. Her name is—*

The next: *My name is Aldric. I am the—*

The next: *My name is—*

Hundreds of them. Thousands. A man writing his own name over and over as it drained out of him, pinning each attempt to the floor like a butterfly collection, trying to hold himself together with ink, and the ink losing, losing, losing. The oldest slips, nearest the wall, were confident and full. The newest, piled by the bier, were single strokes of a pen trailing off the edge of the paper.

"He wrote until he couldn't remember what he was writing," Merric whispered. "The Regent didn't kill him. It *fed*. Slowly. And they sealed him here with the hunger still in him, still eating, and the wards slowed it but couldn't stop it, and he—" Merric pressed the slip flat against his chest. "He fought it with a pen. For years. Alone. Under the ice."

Ash had gone to the bier's edge. He was staring down at the king's blank face, and the shard in his palm was flaring so bright it threw his shadow huge against the dome of fossil stars, and tears were running down his face — silent, bewildered tears, the tears of a boy crying a grief that had no address in him.

"Why does it hurt," he said. "Why do I *know* him?"

And the king opened his eyes.

They were pale eyes, washed-out, like cloth bleached too many times. They moved across Ash's face. Across Merric's. Across mine, and my drawn blade, and the oath-light still dying in its steel. And then King Aldric of Thornfell — the true king, the hidden king, the man who had fought a parasite with a pen for ten years in the dark — looked up at the three strangers who had finally come, and smiled with terrible, gentle courtesy, and asked:

"Forgive me. Are you friends of mine? I seem to have lost the list."
