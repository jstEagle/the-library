## 10. The King Who Forgot Himself

I woke to the sound of a door that had been waiting a long time for someone to knock.

That is not a metaphor. I have since learned that most of what I know arrived as metaphor first, because metaphor was all the ice left me. But the door — the glacier-vault door with its ring of oath-steel and its lock shaped like a closed hand — groaned open on hinges that had rusted in the dark, and the sound it made was exactly like a throat clearing after ten years of silence. *Ahem,* said the mountain. *Finally.*

I did not know my name then. I want that established at the outset, because everything else I tell you will be filtered through the peculiar embarrassment of a man who has to be introduced to himself.

Here is what I knew when the light came in:

I knew how to breathe, though breathing felt like a courtesy extended by strangers. I knew the taste of my own mouth, which was stale and mineral, like a coin kept too long under the tongue. I knew that I was sitting — had apparently *been* sitting for years — on a throne of black ice at the center of a vault carved into a glacier's heart, and that the throne fit me the way an old coat fits a man who has shrunk inside it.

And I knew, with the absolute certainty of a thing that cannot be unlearned because it was never learned in the first place, that I was waiting for someone.

Not rescue. Waiting is different from hoping. Hope requires a shape for the future, and I had none. I simply knew that the door would open one day, and that whoever came through it would be able to answer the only question I owned.

*Who am I?*

The question sat in me like a stone sits in a shoe. Not fatal. Just present, with every step.

* * *

Three figures came through the door, and the cold rushed past them into the vault as if it had been held back by courtesy and could no longer be bothered.

The first was a woman with a sword, which told me nothing and everything. In the world I could not remember, swords presumably meant something specific, because she carried hers the way a priest carries scripture — close, apologetic, essential. The blade was old and it was *loud*, though no sound came from it. I could feel it the way you feel a struck bell through a floor. It hummed in my teeth. Later I would learn why. That morning it simply made me want to weep, and I didn't know why, and the not-knowing made the weeping worse.

The second was an older man with wardwright's burns on his hands and the posture of someone who had spent years apologizing for being right. He looked at me the way a mason looks at a cracked foundation: grief and professional interest in equal measure.

The third was a boy, perhaps twelve, with a shard of pale stone fused into his palm like a splinter the world had decided he should keep. He looked at me with no fear at all, which frightened me more than fear would have, because I understood instinctively that a child who fears nothing has already lost something large.

They stood in the doorway, torchlight bending in the cold air, and the woman said — carefully, the way you speak to a wild animal or a grieving person, categories I have since learned overlap —

"Hello."

"Hello," I said. My voice was a ruin. Ten years of disuse had sanded it down to gravel and apology. "I've been waiting for you."

"We know," she said. "We think."

"I don't," I said, and heard the sentence come out wrong, and tried again. "I mean — I don't know anything. I know I've been waiting. I don't know for whom. I assumed you." I gestured at the throne beneath me, the vault around us, the dark. "This seems like a lot of trouble to go to for a stranger. So either I'm not a stranger, or someone went to a great deal of trouble to keep one very comfortable."

The wardwright made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"You're comfortable?" the woman asked.

"No," I said honestly. "But the *chair* is. Whoever built this wanted the occupant preserved. I've had a decade to inspect the workmanship." I looked down at my hands, which were pale as candle wax and steady as a surgeon's. "I haven't aged. I haven't hungered. I haven't dreamed, which I understand is unusual, because everyone dreams, even people who claim they don't. There is a hole where my sleep should be. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"No," said the boy.

"Neither do I," I said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The woman — Zephyrine, they called her, though the name meant nothing to me and I could see it meant a great deal to her, names being heavy things that their owners forget to notice — approached the throne slowly, sword still sheathed, which I later learned was a courtesy bordering on the sacrilegious.

"What's the last thing you remember?" she asked.

I searched. This is difficult to describe, the searching. Imagine reaching into your own chest to find your heart and finding instead a smooth cavity, recently vacated, still warm. Something had lived there. Something had been *removed*, not lost — removal leaves tool-marks, and mine were everywhere.

"There was a coronation," I said slowly. The memory surfaced like a drowned body, bloated and wrong. "No. There's the *idea* of a coronation. Bells. A crowd. A weight being set on my head. But it's like remembering a story someone told me. I can't find myself in it. I'm watching a man from outside, and the man looks like the reflection I see in the ice, but I don't—" I stopped. Started again. "There's a girl laughing. Somewhere there's a girl laughing, and I know — I *know* — that her laugh mattered to me more than the crown. And I can't see her face. I can't hear it properly. It's a laugh-shaped hole."

Zephyrine's hand went to her sword hilt. Not drawing. Holding. The way you hold a friend's hand at a funeral.

"The Regent," Merric said quietly behind her. "It didn't just take his memories. Look at him. It took them *years ago*. Then it put the rest back and sealed him here like — like a coat in a closet."

"A coat," I repeated.

"It's an expression," Merric said. "An empty coat. Something worn without anyone inside it."

"Well," I said, "I'm inside this one now, and I'd like to register a complaint about the tailoring."

The boy laughed. Short, startled, quickly smothered — the laugh of a child who has learned laughter is a resource to be rationed. But it broke something loose in the room. Even the cold seemed embarrassed.

"You're taking this well," Zephyrine said, and there was something raw under the words. She had expected, I think, a monster. Or a corpse. Or worse — she had expected a king, and found instead a man asking directions.

"I'm taking it terribly," I said. "I'm simply doing it politely, because I don't know what the alternative manners are. You must understand my position. Every person I have ever met, every place I have ever been, every reason I ever had for anything — gone. I am a house with the furniture removed. You can still see the pale rectangles on the walls where the pictures hung. That's what memories are like from the inside, incidentally. Pale rectangles. I keep finding them." I touched my temple. "Here. Something important stood here once. I can't tell you what. But the wall remembers the weight."

Merric stepped forward, and I watched him wrestle with something — guilt, probably; I have become an excellent reader of guilt, having so much empty shelf space for it — before speaking.

"Do you know what a Hollow Regent is?"

"No."

"Good," he said grimly. "Neither does anyone else. That's rather its genius." He told me, then, in the flat voice of a man reciting a diagnosis: the parasite, the veil, the wards woven from royal memory, the crown worn by a thing that ate the king from the inside out and wore what remained. He told me the stars were gone. He told me the thirteenth moon was turning.

He told me I was King Aldric of Thornfell.

And I sat there on my throne of black ice and felt absolutely nothing, which was the most horrifying sensation available to me, because a man hearing he is a king should feel *something* — terror, wonder, outrage, hilarity — and I felt the way a wall feels when told it used to be a window.

"Aldric," I said, testing it. The name sat in my mouth like a borrowed key. It turned no locks. "Is that supposed to mean something to me?"

"Yes," Zephyrine said softly.

"It doesn't. I'm sorry. I can say it. Al-dric. I can wear it the way I assume one wears a crown — outwardly, at arm's length, aware of its weight and ignorant of its purpose." I looked up at her. "You came a long way for this. For me. I can see it cost you — all of you — and I am standing — sitting — in the wreckage of whatever you hoped to find. I'd apologize, but I suspect I've been apologizing my whole life and simply can't remember for what."

* * *

They camped in the vault that night. I learned later this was dangerous, foolish, and entirely Zephyrine's decision, over Merric's objections, and that she won the argument by pointing out that the door only opened for an oath-blade and therefore nothing else was getting in. I mention this because it is the first fact I ever learned about her, and because it established the pattern of our entire acquaintance: Merric says *that's impossible*, Zephyrine says *good, fewer witnesses*, and the impossible thing happens while the boy watches with the particular patience of someone who has already accepted that adults are weather.

I could not sleep. There was a hole where my sleep should be, remember. So I sat on my throne and listened to them talk in low voices by the fire, and I eavesdropped shamelessly, because their conversation was the first world I had ever owned.

"—he's not what I expected," the boy whispered.

"What did you expect?" Zephyrine.

"I don't know. A king. Someone big."

"He's big enough," Zephyrine said. "It's what's missing that's big."

Silence. Fire crackle. Then the boy again, very quietly: "Is that what happened to me?"

And I confess I leaned forward on my throne of black ice like the worst sort of gossip, because the question landed in the hollow space inside me and rang there like a bell in an empty church.

Zephyrine didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice had changed — roughened, careful, the voice of someone handling broken glass.

"I don't know what happened to you, Ash. But if it's the same thing — if some of you got eaten and the rest got put back —" She paused. "Then you're both still here. Whatever took the rest. The part that's sitting by my fire is still here."

"That's not much of a part," Ash said.

"It's the only part that gets to decide things," Zephyrine said. "Believe me. I've checked."

I looked down at my hands in the dark. The part that decides things. I flexed my fingers, watching them move in the firelight like the hands of a stranger doing an impression of me.

*Aldric.* I tried the name again, silently. Still nothing. A pale rectangle on a wall.

But the girl — the laugh-shaped hole — stirred when I tried. Not returning. Just turning over in its absence, the way the bed turns over when someone who slept beside you for years finally leaves it. I knew the shape of what was gone. Which meant, some stubborn cellar of me insisted, that the shape itself was evidence. You cannot miss what you never had. Whatever had been taken from me had been *mine*, and mine enough to leave a mold of itself behind.

"They'll come for him," Merric was saying across the fire. "When the vault opens, the Regent will know. It always knows."

"Then we move at dawn," Zephyrine said.

"Dawn is eleven hours away and the pass is—"

"Merric."

"*Eleven hours,*" he repeated, with the weary dignity of a man who has been overruled by better people his entire life and expects to die the same way.

I cleared my ruined throat, and three heads turned toward the darkness where I sat.

"Pardon me," I said. "The king would like to weigh in."

"You're the king," Ash pointed out.

"Am I? I hadn't noticed. No one sent a memorandum." I rose from the throne — my legs worked, another small mercy I inspected with suspicion — and walked toward the fire, and I will tell you the truth of that walk: it was the longest journey of my life, longer than any I cannot remember, because I crossed it as no one at all, toward three strangers, betting what remained of myself on their faces.

I sat down on the stone beside them. A king on a floor. It felt, absurdly, like the first honest thing I had ever done.

"Tell me about her," I said.

Zephyrine frowned. "About who?"

"The girl who laughs. She's mine — I know that much. Whoever emptied me missed one shape, and it's hers." My voice cracked on the last word, and I let it. Manners are for men with something to protect. "If I'm going to walk out of this mountain and march toward a thing that eats memories, I want to know what I'm fighting for. Start anywhere. Her name. Her age. Whether she had my chin. Whether she laughed at my jokes or *despite* them — that one matters most, I think, though I couldn't say why."

The fire popped. Zephyrine looked at Merric. Merric looked at the floor. And Ash — Ash, the boy with no yesterday, the other empty coat in the room — looked at me with an expression I recognized immediately, because it was mine, reflected.

Hope with the receipts still attached.

"She had your chin," Zephyrine said at last, very quietly. "And she laughed despite them. Always despite them." She drew a breath that shuddered going in. "Her name was Elira. And Aldric — there's something you need to know about what happened to her, and I need you to hear all of it before we take another step toward that seam in the world, because if you learn it on the road, mid-crisis, it will break you in a place where we can't stop to pick up the pieces."

The stone-cold vault seemed to lean closer.

"Then tell me," I said. "I appear to be very good at hearing terrible things. It's the one skill I'm certain I own."

She opened her mouth—

—and the sword at her hip spoke first.

Not loudly. Not in words, exactly. A hum, rising, urgent — the bell-through-the-floor feeling again, but aimed, insistent, like a dog barking at a door before the knock comes. Zephyrine's hand snapped to the hilt.

"What is it?" Merric whispered. "What's it saying?"

She stared into the dark above the vault door, and her face did something complicated — dread and recognition tangled together — and she said:

"*Someone's coming.*"
