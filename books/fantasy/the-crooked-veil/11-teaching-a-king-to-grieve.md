## 11. Teaching a King to Grieve

The tomb had a hearth. That was the first absurdity I noticed once the horror of Aldric himself had stopped clawing at my throat — a glacier vault built to hide a king from a memory-eating god, and someone had thought to put in a fireplace. Merric said the wardwrights of the old order believed a man could not stay human without firelight. Ash just liked it because it was warm.

We'd been there two days when I finally admitted what we all knew: Aldric wasn't going to remember himself on his own. Hollowed men don't grow back like hair. You have to give them something to grow *from*.

Which meant me. Which meant the blade.

I sat across the fire from him that third evening, watching him turn his hands over and over like a man checking whether they were still attached. He did that a lot. He also apologized a lot — for taking up space, for not knowing things, for existing with such conspicuous incompleteness. A king who didn't know he was a king, apologizing for being a burden. Somewhere under all that ruin was a man who had ruled Thornfell for twenty years, and every hour he sat there blank and polite, the Regent's victory got a little more permanent.

"Zephyrine," he said, for perhaps the hundredth time. "I keep trying to thank you, and I don't know your name well enough for it to mean anything."

"It means plenty," I lied.

Merric caught my eye across the flames. He didn't say anything. He'd already explained the mechanics twice, in that lecturing tone he used when he was terrified: a swordspeaker's blade holds living memory, oath-bound and true. What the sword holds, the speaker can pour out. Into a person. Into a veil. Into whatever will take it.

And what you pour out doesn't come back.

"You don't have to do this tonight," Merric murmured.

"Yes, I do." I drew the blade and laid it across my knees. It hummed — it always hummed now, low and expectant, like a dog that smells dinner. "Every day we wait is a day closer to the thirteenth moon. And besides." I looked at Aldric's empty, courteous face. "Nobody should have to be nobody."

* * *

The sword speaks before it gives. That's the part nobody warns you about. When I wrapped both hands around the grip and reached down into the sheath of years where my ancestors' oaths lived, the memories came up talking — voices layered over voices, my grandmother's grandmother's grandmother, all of them murmuring at once like wind through a wheat field.

And among them, new since the glacier door had opened to my blade: *him*. Fragments of Aldric. The wards had been woven from royal memory, and some of the king had soaked into the lattice over the centuries, and some of *that* had soaked into the oath-line that guarded it. My sword had been carrying pieces of this stranger since before I was born.

I found one near the top, still warm. I let it rise.

The coronation. Not his — his father's — but Aldric was there, nineteen years old, standing in the second row of the great hall at Carrowmere while snow came down outside like the sky was shedding. I felt what he'd felt: the weight of the crown not yet on his head but already on his shoulders, the terror of it, and underneath the terror something so simple it made my chest hurt. He'd looked at his father kneeling before the archwarden and thought, *I hope I can be gentle enough. I hope gentleness counts as strength.*

He gasped. Actually gasped, there by the fire, both hands flying to his face.

"I was—" His voice cracked. "There was snow. My father was old. I was afraid of becoming him."

"That's right," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "That's you. That's Aldric."

"Aldric." He tried the name like a coin bitten for gold. Then he looked up at me with an expression I recognized, gods help me, from mirrors — the look of a man who has just been handed something precious and knows perfectly well it cost the giver. "Where did that come from? Where did *you* get that?"

"The wards," Merric said quietly. "Some of you leaked into them. She's been carrying you a long time, Majesty."

Aldric stared at me. I stared at the fire. There are conversations you can't have while looking at someone, and that was one of them.

* * *

Here's what I learned about giving away memories, in case you're ever foolish enough to try it: it isn't like losing a book from a shelf. It's like losing a room from a house. The walls around it sag. The hallway that led there now leads nowhere, and you keep walking down it anyway, out of habit, and finding nothing.

I gave him his daughter next.

I'd found her in the blade's deep places — not royal memory this time, but mine. I'd been there, ten years ago, a young oath-sworn guard doing rotation duty in the palace gardens, and Princess Isolde — six years old, gap-toothed, absolutely feral with joy — had spent an entire afternoon teaching me a game involving acorns, a fountain, and rules that changed whenever she was losing. Her laugh had gotten stuck in me somewhere between my ribs and my oath. Apparently it had gotten into the steel too, because when I reached for it, the blade offered it up eagerly, like a child presenting a dead bird as a gift.

I almost didn't do it. I want that on the record, wherever records go. I sat there with my hands on the hilt for a long time, feeling her laugh humming in the metal, knowing exactly what I was about to do.

Because here was the arithmetic, and it was ugly: the laugh lived in me. If it stayed in me, it stayed in one hollowing woman on a suicide errand. If I gave it to him, it went back to the only other person alive who had any right to it. A father who needed it more than I did.

That's what I told myself, anyway. Love makes excellent accountants of terrible math.

"Close your eyes," I told him.

"Why?"

"Because I said so, and I'm armed."

He almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile, and it nearly broke me, because I could see the ghost of another smile inside it — a father's smile, worn smooth by use, waiting for its owner to come home.

I poured.

It went through me on the way out. That's the thing nobody tells you — a memory leaving has to pass through everything you are, and it takes a little of each thing with it, like river water through cloth. I felt the fountain spray. I felt small fingers pressing acorns into my palm with tremendous authority. I heard the laugh — high, hiccupping, unstoppable — and then I heard it from the *other side*, heard myself laughing too, young and unguarded, and then—

Then it was going. I grabbed for it. I swear to every star that used to exist, I grabbed, and my fingers closed on nothing, on the shape where it had been, on a daughter-shaped hole in my chest with smooth edges and no bottom.

Aldric was crying.

Not elegantly. Kings presumably cry elegantly, with dignity and handkerchiefs; this man cried like a burst dam, folded forward over his knees, making sounds that weren't words. "Isolde," he kept saying. "Isolde, Isolde—" And then, worse, quieter: "She used to cheat at games. She cheated *constantly*. I let her win and she cheated anyway—"

"Yeah," I said. My voice worked. That surprised me. "She did."

He looked up, wet-faced, radiant, ruined. "You knew her."

"I guarded her gardens for a season." Past tense. I searched myself for the season and found the shape of it — duty roster, stone walls, the smell of roses — with a warm spot missing in the middle, like a tooth gone from a jaw. I knew I had loved that summer. I couldn't remember why. I remembered *that* I'd laughed until my sides hurt. I couldn't remember the sound.

Worth it, I told myself. Say it again. Worth it.

* * *

Ash watched me after that. Watched me the way kids watch adults do dangerous things — wanting to understand, hoping never to need the knowledge.

On the fifth night he sat down beside me while Aldric slept — actually slept now, deeply, instead of lying awake asking the ceiling who he was. Ash turned the wardshard over in his fused palm, its light pulsing faintly against his fingers.

"It hurts you," he said. Not a question. The boy who remembered nothing had turned out to notice everything.

"Some."

"Liar. Some." He mimicked my flat tone badly, and I would have laughed if laughing hadn't felt like opening a door I'd just nailed shut. "You walk different since the laugh. Like something's missing on one side."

"Sharp eyes for a boy with no yesterday."

"I figure somebody had to have them," he said. "So they might as well be mine." He was quiet a moment, turning the shard. "Does it work? Giving it away? Does he get more... *there*?"

"He remembered how to pray last night. Out of nowhere, mid-sentence, he just started — old words, formal ones. Merric nearly wept." I poked the fire. "So yes. It works. Each piece I give him, he stands up a little straighter inside his own skin."

"And each piece you give him, you sit down a little further inside yours."

Out of the mouths of babes, as my grandmother used to say. Except I no longer remembered my grandmother saying it — I only knew people said it, that it was a saying, that somewhere behind the saying was a woman whose voice I'd traded away without noticing which gift took it.

"Ash," I said carefully, "if I forget you someday — if I look at you like a stranger — don't take it personally. Just introduce yourself again. You're good at it. You've had practice."

He considered this with the terrible seriousness of the young. "Okay," he said. "But I'm picking a better introduction this time. Last one was pretty weak. 'Hello, I'm in your wagon.' Real memorable."

This time I did laugh. It hurt, in the place where the laugh used to live, and it healed something too, and I've decided those are often the same sensation.

* * *

By the ninth day I had given him eleven memories, and I'll tell you what they cost, because someone should write it down even if it's only me:

His wedding day. I never had a wedding, but I'd carried the memory of his — the vows leaking through the wards — and giving it up took with it my understanding of why anyone bothers. Marriage became, to me, a strange custom performed by strangers. I know that's sad. I can report it's sad the way you can report that a country you've never visited has lovely coastline.

His mother's lullaby. This one I'd inherited from an ancestor who'd served at court, and when it left, every song in the world went slightly flatter. Music still worked. It just stopped meaning anything extra.

The taste of the bread his baker made. Trivial, you'd think. But it took with it my whole category of *bread as comfort*, and now when I eat, I eat fuel, and I miss something I cannot name, which is the worst kind of hunger.

His grief for his dead brother. Yes — I gave him his grief. Merric argued against that one. "He's functioning," Merric said. "Grief might break him." And I said, "He's not functioning, he's *floating*. A man who can't grieve his own brother isn't whole, he's just intact." I won the argument. I lost the argument's price: I no longer fully believe I've ever lost anyone, though I carry evidence of mourning all over myself, scars of feeling with the feelings scraped out.

And the strangest one: his fear. One specific fear — the night before his coronation, age nineteen, certain he would fail everyone. I gave it back and discovered I'd been leaning on it for years without knowing. Courage, it turns out, is not the absence of borrowed fears. Take away a man's fear and you take away the proof that he had something worth being afraid for. I've been lighter ever since. Lighter and less sure the ground is real.

Eleven gifts. Aldric stood taller each morning. He remembered his wife's face (she was beyond the veil or dead; we didn't know which; he wept either way). He remembered how to read the old script, how to ride, how to command a room without raising his voice. He remembered, on the eleventh day, that he had sealed the vault himself — that he had *chosen* this, walked into the ice to deny the Regent his final meal, and the knowledge transformed him. The apologetic ghost started standing like a man.

Me, I got lighter too. Ask me about my childhood and I can tell you facts like a clerk reading an inventory: village, parents, mentor, roads. The facts are all present. The warmth has been sold off piecemeal, and I keep finding new gaps — rooms in the house I didn't know were gone until I walked into their absence.

Merric counted the costs silently. I saw him doing it, that ledger-keeper's flicker in his eyes, and on the eleventh night he finally said it aloud.

"Zephyrine. The seam will need memories too. Willing ones, poured into the weave itself. If you keep spending yourself on him—"

"Then I'll arrive at the seam light enough to pour everything," I said. "Efficient."

"That's not efficiency, that's erasure with extra steps."

"Merric." I looked at him across the dying fire, at the disgraced wardwright who'd warned a crown that wouldn't listen, and I made my voice gentle, because he deserved gentleness and rarely got any. "You've seen what he is now. You've seen him stand up. Tell me honestly — was any of it not worth it?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Aldric sleeping like a man instead of a coat, and at Ash curled against the wardshard's glow, and at me, sitting straight-backed over a sword that hummed a little thinner every day.

"No," he admitted. "But worth it and survivable aren't the same word."

"No," I agreed. "They're not."

* * *

On the twelfth morning, Aldric was waiting for me by the hearth, dressed, upright, with something in his face I hadn't seen before — a king's patience, the kind that waits out storms.

"Zephyrine," he said. "I remember something else. Something I recovered on my own, in the night." He held up a hand before I could reach for the blade. "Not from you. From *me*. The Regent cast back what it couldn't digest, and some of it's been settling, like silt. Last night it finished settling." His eyes were steady and very old. "I remember the Regent's beginning. I remember what it actually is — and you're going to wish I didn't, because it changes everything we thought we came here to do."

Behind him, Merric went very still. Ash's shard flared bright enough to throw shadows.

"What is it?" I asked. "What does it change?"

Aldric looked at the glacier wall, as if he could see through a mile of ice to the capital, to the thing wearing his crown.

"It changes who we're fighting," he said. "Because it isn't a demon. It never was. And it can't be killed — only refused. Come and sit down. All of you. We have eleven weeks, and I finally remember why that number matters."
