## 12. The Regent's Offer

The village of Lowmere had been on no map I ever trusted, which is how I knew it was still honest. Maps lie about roads; they never lie about the absence of them.

We came down out of the pine dark in the last grey hour before dawn, and I knew something was wrong with Lowmere before we reached the first fence post. Not wrong like burned or wrong like emptied — wrong like a room where someone has just finished tidying and left. Everything in its place. Smoke rising from chimneys in tidy columns. A dog that didn't bark at us, which in my experience is the single most damning piece of evidence a dog can provide.

"Maybe they're friendly," Ash said, walking beside the wagon with his bandaged hand tucked against his chest. The wardshard beneath the wrapping gave off a faint light these days, like a coal refusing to die. He'd stopped asking me if it was going to hurt him. I'd stopped pretending to know.

"Nobody's friendly before dawn," I said. "Friendly people are asleep."

Merric looked up from the ward-lattice he'd been scratching into the wagon's bench with a nail, his version of fidgeting. "The wards around this place are intact," he said slowly. "That's what worries me. Every settlement between here and the capital has gone dark, wards eaten down to the root. This one's burning clean."

"Could be an old garrison town," I offered.

"Could be," Merric agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing that a wolf could be a large dog.

I should have turned us around. Let the record show that I considered it. But Ash hadn't eaten a proper meal in two days, Merric's hands shook from rationing his own strength into the lattice-work, and the pass behind us was crawling with hunters who wore people's eyes without using them. Lowmere had bread in it, probably. Bread makes cowards of us all.

So I did what survival has always taught me to do: I walked toward the danger because it was standing between me and breakfast.

* * *

The villagers came out to meet us in the square, all of them, which was the second damning thing. No one in any village I'd ever passed through comes out to meet armed strangers at dawn unless there's grain to hide or daughters to marry off. They stood in loose rows by their well, forty-odd souls in good wool coats, and every one of them had the same eyes.

Hollow-eyed. That was the phrase Merric used, back when he was explaining the Regent's habits to us over campfire smoke. Eyes like windows in an empty house — you can see through them, but nobody's home behind the glass. I'd seen it once before, on a courier outside Cinderhollow who smiled at me for a full minute without blinking and then walked into a river.

These forty people smiled at me at once. It was like watching a field of flowers decide to bloom simultaneously. Beautiful, if you didn't think about it. Horrifying, if you thought about it even slightly.

I thought about it.

"Well met, travelers," said the woman at the front of the group. She was broad and grey-haired and her hands were floury, and she said the words with the careful precision of someone reciting a speech learned overnight. "You look tired. There is bread."

"Told you," Ash murmured.

"Hush." I dismounted slowly, keeping my left side — blade side — toward the crowd. "Bread would be kind, mistress. We'll pay."

"There is no need for payment." She tilted her head, and the motion was wrong — too smooth, no joint-hesitation, like a puppeteer testing a new string. "Payment implies debt. Debt implies memory. We have so little of that left, you see."

The square went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to step aside.

"You know what I am," I said.

"We know what you carry." Her smile widened by exactly the amount a smile can widen before it becomes something else. "Zephyrine Iversen. Last swordspeaker. Blade full of dead voices. We have been waiting for you since the stars went out."

Behind me, I heard Merric climb down off the wagon with the deliberate slowness of a man trying not to startle anything. Good instinct. Wrong target. You cannot startle a puppet by moving carefully; only the hand holding the strings can be startled, and the hand was not in the square.

It was everywhere. It was wearing the king's crown three hundred miles away, and it had apparently decided to drop in for a chat.

* * *

They fed us. That was the worst part, though I didn't know it yet. The bread was warm. There was butter, real butter, and honey comb still dripping, and hard-boiled eggs with salt, and a pot of tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. Ash ate like a small starving animal, which is to say efficiently and with constant glances up in case the food was a trick. Merric picked at his portion and watched the villagers, who stood around the edges of the inn's common room not eating, not drinking, just watching us with those borrowed smiles.

The flour-handed woman sat across from me. She'd introduced herself as Marta, though the name fit her like a coat worn by someone else.

"You have questions," she said.

"I have several," I admitted. "Starting with whether these people are alive."

"The bodies are alive. The memories are... relocated." She said *relocated* the way a landlord says *renovated*. "Some were taken weeks ago. Some were taken this morning, to make ready for your arrival. The woman whose mouth I am using had a daughter. The daughter remembers having a mother but cannot describe her face. It is efficient. Grief takes so much space, and grief serves nothing."

"That's a hell of a philosophy," I said.

"It is not a philosophy. It is hunger. Hunger requires no justification. Only appetite." She folded her floury hands on the table. "But you did not come here for theology, Swordspeaker. You came here because I asked you here. And I asked you here because I have an offer."

"I figured. Hollow-eyed couriers don't usually lead with breakfast."

"The breakfast is part of the offer. Eat while you consider it. Consideration goes better with honey."

I ate. Survival has always been my religion, and its first sacrament is: take the food when it's offered, because you don't know when it will come again. Even now, sitting across from the mouth of the thing that had eaten my kingdom's sky, I broke the bread and buttered it and chewed. Somewhere in my chest, my ancestors — the ones living in my sword, anyway — were presumably screaming at me. Or maybe they were hungry too. Dead voices get peckish, I imagine.

"Here is the shape of it," Marta-the-mouth said. "In eleven days, the thirteenth moon completes its turn. When it does, my hold upon the throne becomes permanent, and the veil between worlds dissolves entirely, and everything behind it comes through. You have seen the edge of that everything, Swordspeaker. The things that eat wards. The things that eat names. You have carried a shard of it in a boy's hand for weeks."

Ash's fork stopped halfway to his mouth.

"I am not cruel," she continued. "This is important for you to understand. Cruelty is waste. Cruelty spends energy to produce suffering, and suffering is not nourishing. Memory is nourishing. And you—" her eyes, those empty windows, fixed on my sword where it leaned against the bench, "—you are carrying a feast that has been aging for nine generations. Every oath sworn into that steel. Every ancestor's last battle, first love, dying breath. Do you know what that weighs, Swordspeaker? Do you feel it on your hip? Nine lifetimes of the richest vintage ever brewed, and you carry it like luggage."

"It's not luggage," I said. "It's inheritance."

"Inheritance," she repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. That is what kings called it too, when they traded theirs to me. Your King Aldric traded me twenty years of his reign — the tedium, the taxes, the funerals — to keep the pleasant parts. He did not understand that memory is not divisible. You cannot eat half a life. When I took my share, the rest followed, the way a wall follows its cornerstone." She leaned forward. "But you. You understand division. You have been dividing yourself your whole life. Rations on the road. Sleep in shifts. Love in secret, because loving openly gets people killed. You are an expert in spending less than you have."

"Flattery," I said. "From a parasite."

"An accurate inventory." She spread her hands. "Here is the offer. Give me the blade's memories — pour them out willingly, as you would eventually do at your precious seam, except to me instead of into some lattice. In exchange: safe passage. Not just tolerated passage — protected. My hunters will become your honor guard. And the boy and the wardwright live. Not merely survive — *live*. Fed, warmed, remembered. I will even return to this village its stolen mornings, its mothers' faces, its dog that barks. A whole town restored, purchased with one sword's worth of dead voices."

"And the king?" Merric asked quietly. He'd known. Of course he'd known — he'd spent ten years trying to warn the crown about exactly this conversation.

"What king?" Marta-the-mouth said pleasantly.

"The one you're wearing."

"I am wearing a coat with no one in it. The man you seek is a ruin in the ice. If you reach him — and you won't, but if — what will you give him? Coronations? He will watch them like another man's dreams. His daughter's laughter? It will make him weep for a child he cannot remember fathering. You call that rescue. I call it cruelty with extra steps." She turned those window-eyes back to me. "But you, Zephyrine. You would keep yourself. Your own memories. Your mother's kitchen. Your mentor's voice. Your first oath, the one you swore with bleeding knees in a shrine that no longer exists. Keep all of it. Lose only the dead weight. Is that not what you've been doing your entire life? Trimming yourself to survive?"

And there it was. The knife wasn't in the offer; the knife was in how well the offer fit.

* * *

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only thing I have left that isn't mortgaged. For a long moment — long enough for the tea to go cold in my cup, long enough for Ash to set down his fork — I wanted to say yes.

Not because I feared death. Death and I had been negotiating terms for fifteen years and had reached a comfortable détente. I wanted to say yes because I was tired. Because eleven days felt like a lifetime and I had already given up so much of myself on this road that the word *myself* had started to sound like a legal fiction. Because the Regent was right, damn it. Right the way a blade is right when it slides between ribs. Survival has always been my religion, and here was its god offering me the one thing no other faith ever had: survival *without further cost.* Everyone else demanded payment upfront. The Regent was willing to let me pay with money that was already mostly spent.

Nine generations of ancestors. What were they to me? Voices in steel. Teachers I'd never met. A chorus that whispered strategy at me in my darkest hours, yes, and had saved my life more times than I could count — but also a weight. A debt compounding across decades. Every swordspeaker before me had poured themselves into the blade expecting the next one to carry them forward. Had anyone ever asked the carrier whether she wanted to carry?

"You're quiet," Marta-the-mouth observed.

"I'm counting," I said.

"Counting what?"

"How much of me is already gone." I turned my cup slowly on its saucer. "My mentor's face — spent on a mountain road. My sister's wedding — spent on a river crossing. Little pieces, here and there, all the way from Thornfell. You know what I've noticed? The memories I've paid don't leave holes. They leave smooth spots. Like river stones. I can't miss what I can't remember missing." I looked up at her. "Is that what you're offering everyone? Smoothness?"

"I am offering peace."

"No." I set the cup down. "You're offering *oblivion with good manners.* I've met the difference. The difference has a name, and the name is Ash."

The boy went rigid beside me.

"He had everything taken," I said. "Whole self, gone, and what was left was a scared kid with a rock stuck to his hand. And you know what he did? He built himself a name out of nothing. Out of ash, because that's what was lying around. That's the thing you can't digest, Regent. You eat what people remember. You can't eat what they choose." I stood up, and my chair scraped, and forty hollow-eyed villagers turned their heads in perfect unison, and I swear on whatever's left of me that it was almost funny. "So here's my counter-offer. You give back this village — every face, every stolen morning — right now, today, free of charge. In exchange, I don't spend the next eleven days hunting you down with a sword full of everything you're afraid of."

Silence. Then, horribly, the whole square laughed — all forty mouths at once, the same laugh, the same pitch, an echo applauding itself.

"You misunderstand your position," Marta-the-mouth said, and the warmth drained out of her voice like bathwater. "You are not threatening me. You are a candle threatening the concept of darkness."

"And you're a hunger wearing a crown, threatening a woman who's been hungry her whole life." I picked up my sword. The blade hummed against my palm — low, angry, awake. Nine generations of voices, and for once they weren't whispering. They were roaring. "You made one mistake in your arithmetic. You priced my ancestors as dead weight. They're not. They're the reason I'm still standing, and they have opinions about you, and I've decided to start listening to all of them at once."

Marta-the-mouth rose. Around the common room, forty bodies straightened like soldiers called to attention, and the smiles finally, blessedly, fell off every face at once.

"The offer stands until the thirteenth moon," she said. "Hunger is patient. It is the one thing hunger is." Her head tilted, and behind those empty eyes something vast leaned close, close enough that I felt it like frost forming inside my skull. "But hear me, Zephyrine Iversen. Everything you refuse to surrender, I will take anyway. Piece by piece. Starting with the ones you love. You have given me such lovely pieces already — the wardwright's secrets, the boy's fear, the king's hope. Do you truly believe I haven't been collecting from your friends all along?"

Cold walked down my spine on small feet.

"Merric," I said, without turning. "How far to the high waystation at Grey Crag?"

"Half a day," Merric said. His voice had gone very flat and very careful, the way it did when his mind was running faster than his mouth wanted to follow. "Why?"

"Because I think," I said, watching Marta-the-mouth smile again — slow, satisfied, certain — "that we're about to find out what she's been doing with our secrets. Saddle up."

Outside, the sun was coming up over Lowmere, and every villager stood in their doorways watching us leave, and not one of them blinked.

Behind us, faint and terrible, the whole village began to sing. The same song. Forty throats. One voice.

It sounded like a lullaby I half-remembered, and couldn't remember forgetting.
