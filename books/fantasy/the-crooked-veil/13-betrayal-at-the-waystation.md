## 13. Betrayal at the Waystation

The waystation was called the Kettle, which I thought was a fine name for a building that looked like someone had dropped a kettle on a mountainside and then, out of politeness, added walls and a chimney. Smoke rose from its roof into air so thin it seemed embarrassed to hold anything up. Snow came down in the lazy, sideways way snow does when it has already decided to bury you and sees no reason to rush.

"We stop here," Zephyrine said.

"We could keep walking," Merric said.

"We could also lie down in the snow and die," Zephyrine said, "but I've done enough dying for one winter."

I kept my mouth shut, mostly because my teeth were chattering too loudly to permit speech, and partly because the shard in my palm had been humming all morning — a low, sour note like a struck bell that hadn't finished deciding whether it wanted to ring. The shard hummed when memories were near. It hummed loudest when they were *wrong* somehow. Old griefs, buried ones, the kind people stitch over and pretend are scars instead of open wounds.

It was humming at Merric.

I'd been noticing that for days, if I'm honest. Noticing and saying nothing, because noticing things about Merric Vale felt rude, the way you don't mention that a guest's coat smells of smoke. He was the one who knew things. He was the one who read ward-laws by candlelight until his eyes went red and explained the thirteenth moon-turn to us in a voice like a man reading his own death warrant. You don't suspect the man carrying the map just because your hand itches around him.

But the shard doesn't lie. It showed me a dead soldier's battle once; it showed me Zephyrine swearing her first oath on a hillside I'll never see. It shows what is. And what it showed me, whenever Merric leaned close to the fire, was a door.

Not a real door. A memory of a door — a tall one, black iron, set into a wall of pale stone, and behind it something vast and patient turning over in its sleep like a body adjusting under blankets. And standing before the door, holding it open a crack with one careful hand: a man with ink-stained fingers and a wardwright's satchel.

Merric's hands. Merric's satchel.

* * *

The Kettle's keeper was a woman named Brannoc who had exactly two expressions: suspicion, and suspicion with her arms crossed. She took our coin, gave us the corner room with the crooked stove, and told us supper was whatever was in the pot and complaints were extra.

We ate. The pot contained something brown and heroic in its determination to be stew. Aldric — the king who didn't know he was a king, who ate like a man relearning the concept — sat across from me and studied his spoon as though it might contain clues to his identity. Zephyrine cleaned her blade with an oiled rag, slow strokes, and I watched the light run along it and wondered how many voices lived in there now, and whether any of them were getting lonely.

Merric spread his maps on the table and started talking about the pass ahead. The Greytooth. Two days' climb, then the descent to the ruined citadel where the veil's first seam waited.

"—and here the road forks, but the left branch is a lie, it washes out every spring, so we take—"

"Behind the iron door."

I said it quietly, but quiet carries in mountain rooms. The stove ticked. Zephyrine's rag stopped moving.

Merric looked up. His face did something complicated and fast, like weather over water. "What?"

"The door." I held up my right hand, palm out, shard glinting. It had gone from humming to burning. "Black iron. Pale stone wall. Something sleeping behind it that isn't asleep. And a man opening it for that something. Ink-stained fingers. A wardwright's bag." I swallowed. My heart was going hard against my ribs, not from fear exactly — fear I knew, fear was an old roommate — but from the specific misery of watching a person's face come apart and having no idea what to do with the pieces. "Your fingers, Merric. Your bag."

Silence. Even the stove stopped ticking, or I stopped hearing it.

"You've been showing him things," Merric said to the shard, to my hand, his voice flat and strange. "All this time. I wondered why you flinched when I touched your shoulder."

"Tell me it isn't true." Zephyrine's voice was very even, which anyone who'd traveled with her knew was worse than shouting. "Tell me the boy's haunted trinket is wrong."

And here is the thing I will remember about that moment, when I remember anything at all: Merric didn't deny it. He didn't scramble for excuses or reach for a clever lie. He just closed his eyes, and let out a breath ten years long, and said:

"It's true. Sit down. Both of you. Please."

Zephyrine didn't sit. Her blade came off the table and lay across her knees like a sleeping animal that wasn't sleeping.

"I served him," Merric said. "The Regent. Willingly. For three years."

Aldric's spoon went down. Slowly. The sound it made against the bowl was very small and very loud.

"Why?" I asked. Because there had to be a why. There is always a why. The world runs on whys the way roads run on stones underneath.

"Because I have a family." His laugh came out broken, half-swallowed. "Had. Wenna. My wife. Two daughters, Sef and little Maren, who was born the year I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I was the crown's chief wardwright. I inspected the wards at court every season. I was the one who told them, eleven years ago, that the wards weren't fraying — they were being *eaten*. That something behind the veil was drinking the king's memory through the lattice itself. Do you know what they did? They thanked me for my diligence and posted me to a coastal garrison where the nearest ward was a tide-charm. And the thing I'd warned them about put on the king's face and smiled at everyone and nobody noticed, because nobody looks at a smile that closely."

He turned his hands over. Ink stains, faded to gray, still in the creases.

"So I went back. On my own. To study it. To find its weakness. And it found me first. It spoke to me through the mouths of servants — the way it speaks through hollow-eyed villagers now, you've seen it. And it offered me a trade. Ward-secrets. The deep lattice, the old craft, the keys to every sealed door in Thornfell. In exchange, my family would be left alone. Untouched. Unremembered-by-it, which is the only safety there is." His voice cracked clean through. "What would you have done? Don't answer. Everyone knows what they'd have done. They'd have done what I did."

"And then?" Zephyrine. Still level. Her knuckles white on the sheath.

"Then it took everything anyway." He said it simply, the way you report weather. "Three years of secrets bought three years of nothing. When I finally ran — when I finally understood there was no bottom to what it wanted — I went home and stood in my own doorway and my wife looked at me like a stranger. Worse than a stranger. Strangers you can introduce yourself to." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "The Regent had eaten me out of their memories. Every trace. Wenna remembered being married, remembered loving someone, remembered the shape of the hole. But the hole was me-shaped and she couldn't see it. My daughters drew pictures of their family and there were four figures in the house and none of them were me."

Nobody moved. Outside, wind dragged its nails along the shutters.

"That's why the wards fell," Zephyrine said at last, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't gentle. "You gave it the lattice. You gave it the seams."

"I gave it what it already had," Merric snapped, and for one moment he wasn't a broken man, he was the man who'd once shouted truth at a crown. "Do you understand? It was *inside* the wards from the beginning. Eating from within, season after season. My secrets were crumbs off a table it owned. If I hadn't given them, it would have taken them and taken my family besides. All I did was choose the order of the losing." He looked at each of us in turn, and his eyes were wet and furious. "You want a traitor. Fine. I'm one. Write it on my stone. But I've spent ten years trying to unmake what I helped make, and I am the only person alive who knows the Regent's lattice from the inside, and if you cut my throat tonight, Zephyrine Iversen, you had better do it before the Greytooth, because past the Greytooth you will need me more than you hate me."

* * *

That's when the windows blew in.

Glass first — a bright, singing scatter of it across the supper table, into the stew, into Merric's maps. Then cold, a fistful of mountain shoved through the frames. Then the hunters.

They came through the broken glass without cutting themselves, which should have been the first warning. Hollow-eyed men and women in road-gray clothes, moving with the terrible smoothness of things that borrow bodies rather than wear them. Four of them. Five. The keeper Brannoc was among them, her suspicion finally resolved into something worse than crossed arms.

"The Regent remembers you, wardwright," said the one who'd been Brannoc, in a voice like a well with something at the bottom. "It remembers every secret you sold. It has come to collect the last one."

"The boy," said another, and five pairs of empty eyes found me, and my shard went white-hot in my palm. "And the sword. Bring both."

Zephyrine was already moving. I have seen her fight smugglers and wolves and once, memorably, a tax collector, and I can tell you there is a difference between fighting to win and fighting like someone who has decided the world does not get to take anything else from her today. Her blade cleared the sheath and *spoke* — that ringing chorus-voice it makes when the ancestor-memories wake — and the nearest hunter staggered as though the sound alone had weight.

"Merric!" she shouted. "Lattice! Now!"

And here was the moment, I realize now, where everything hung. Where I stood in the wreckage of a window with a burning hand and a betrayal still fresh in my chest, and watched the traitor decide what he was.

Merric didn't hesitate. Later I would think about that a great deal — how he didn't hesitate, how hesitation was a luxury his guilt had never afforded him. He swept the fallen maps off the table with one arm, snatched the salt cellar and the candle and the poker from the stove, and began laying them in patterns I'd seen him sketch a hundred times in the dirt. Salt for the lines. Fire for the anchors. Iron for the spine.

"Boy!" he barked. "Shard! Give me your hand!"

Everything in me said no. Everything in me said *he opened the door, he sold the secrets, he's the reason your hand burns.* The shard pulsed against my palm like a second heartbeat, showing me flashes — the iron door, the pale wall, ink-stained fingers pushing it wide — and I hated him in that moment with a clean, total, childish hatred that felt wonderful and awful at once.

But the hunters were inside the room, and Zephyrine was bleeding from a cut on her arm, and Aldric — Aldric, the hollowed king, the man with no name — had picked up the poker and planted himself between the hunters and me with the clumsy courage of someone who didn't know why he was protecting us, only that he was.

So I gave Merric my hand.

His grip was ice and ink. He pressed my shard-palm flat against the salt line and spoke a word of ward-craft so old it sounded less like language than like a decision the world had once made. The lattice leapt. Light ran the pattern — up the walls, across the floor, a cage of remembering woven in a heartbeat from salt and candle-flame and a stolen boy's stolen sight.

The hunters hit the lattice like moths hitting a lamp. Where they touched it, the ward drank from *them* — pulled memory out of the borrowed bodies, and the bodies folded, and out of the folding mouths came sounds like a feast interrupted. The one wearing Brannoc's face collapsed first. Then another. The last two tore free and went through the shattered window into the dark, taking the cold with them, leaving snow drifting onto the ruined maps.

Silence. The stove ticking again. Blood on the floorboards — Zephyrine's, small dark coins of it.

Merric let go of my hand and stood there shaking, surrounded by his own salt-and-fire geometry, looking older than the mountains outside. "Well," he said. "Now they know exactly what I can build."

"Yes," Zephyrine said. She sheathed her blade. She was breathing hard. She looked at him for a long, long time, and I watched her weigh it — the door, the secrets, the wife with the hole where her husband used to be — against the lattice still glowing faintly on the floorboards, against the fact that they were alive because of it. I watched forgiveness arrive in her face the way dawn arrives in a valley: not all at once, and never completely, but arriving.

"You lied to us," she said.

"I lied to myself first. Longer." He wiped his face with a sleeve. "It seemed only fair to share."

She almost smiled. Almost. "When we reach the citadel, you weave the seam. And if one thread of that lattice serves the Regent instead of the veil, Merric Vale, I will spend my last memory hunting you, and my blade holds enough voices to make certain I never forget why."

"Fair terms," Merric said hoarsely.

They looked at me then. Both of them. And Aldric too, still holding the poker like a scepter he wasn't sure he'd inherited. Waiting to see what the boy with no yesterday would do with a man who'd traded away tomorrow.

I looked at my palm. The shard had gone quiet, or quieter. It wasn't showing me the door anymore. It was showing me something new — a small room, a woman's hands kneading bread, a girl's drawing pinned to a wall with four figures in it, and a fifth figure drawn later, in different charcoal, pressed so hard into the paper it had torn. Someone had tried to draw himself back into a family that couldn't see him. The paper had torn. He'd kept the drawing anyway.

Ten years of keeping it.

"I'm angry at you," I told Merric. My voice came out steadier than I expected. "I think I get to be angry for a while. That's mine."

"You do," he said. "Keep it as long as you need."

"But you built that lattice in about four seconds while monsters crawled through the window, and it saved us, and—" I flexed my burned, glowing, cursed, useful hand. "Ash needs you. Whoever I am. So." I shrugged, because I didn't have better words, because maybe forgiveness isn't a feeling at all but a thing you build out of salt and fire and choosing, one night at a time. "Don't make me regret it. That's all. Just — don't."

Merric nodded, and couldn't speak, and nodded again.

Outside, far off down the valley, something howled — not wolf, not wind, but the particular noteless cry the hollow-eyed things make when they're calling home to the thing that emptied them. It was answered from the north. Then the east. Then, faintly, from everywhere at once, like a chord tightening.

Zephyrine went to the broken window and stood looking out into the falling snow, her breath smoking, her hand resting on the hilt where a hundred ancestors slept.

"They know where we are," she said. "And they know what we're carrying. We ride for the Greytooth tonight, in the dark, ahead of the hunt — or we bury these bones in this kettle and let the thirteenth moon rise over our graves."

She turned back to us. Snow was melting in her hair.

"Pack fast," she said. "We ride."
