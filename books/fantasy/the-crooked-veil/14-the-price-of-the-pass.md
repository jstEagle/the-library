## 14. The Price of the Pass

The road found us before we found it.

That's not poetry — or rather, it is, but it's also literal fact, which is the worst kind of poetry. We'd been climbing for two days through country that didn't appear on any map Merric owned, which was saying something, because Merric owned maps of places that had been underwater since his grandmother's grandmother. Then the pines thinned, the fog peeled back like a curtain being drawn by a bored stagehand, and there it was: a track of pale stone winding up between two shoulders of mountain, glowing faintly gold where the mist touched it.

"The Old Toll Road," Merric said, and his voice had gone careful in the way it did when he was about to hand me bad news wrapped in scholarship.

"It's glowing," Ash said. He said it with the particular wonder of someone who has no memories to compare wonders against. Everything was either the most amazing thing he'd ever seen or a horror beyond reckoning, and often both at once.

"It remembers," I said.

I knew this road. Not from maps — from my mother's mother's stories, passed down through the blades and the blood. The smuggler roads of Thornfell were woven old, older than the wards, older than the crown. They were made to remember the feet that walked them honestly and forget the boots that came hunting. For centuries they'd kept our people one step ahead of every tax collector, bounty man, and righteous army that tried to squeeze the passes dry.

For centuries they'd been content to simply remember.

But the stars had been out for nine weeks now, and everything old was getting hungry.

* * *

We stopped at the foot of the ascent while Merric crouched and examined the first paving stone like it owed him money.

"Look here." He brushed frost off the stone's face. Beneath the ice, carved so shallow you'd miss it unless you knew to look, ran a line of ward-script — the same lattice-work that used to hold the border veil together, but cruder, older, more honest somehow. "This isn't decoration. It's a ledger."

"A ledger," I repeated.

"Every road keeps accounts. Who walked it, what they carried, what they left behind." He sat back on his heels. "Normally the interest is small. A footprint. An hour of your time. But the roads are starving, Zephyrine. The wards fed them — royal memory, poured down through the lattice like grain down a chute. When the Regent ate the wards, it cut the roads' supply too. And now—"

"Now they want payment in the original currency," I finished.

He looked up at me. He has a way of looking at me when we both know what he's about to say, like he's testing whether I'll make him say it.

"How much does a pass cost?" I asked.

"That depends." He stood, knees cracking — the man is forty and complains about it like he invented the condition. "On how badly you need to pass, and how much of yourself you're willing to put on the stone."

Behind us, down in the valley we'd crawled out of, something howled. It wasn't a wolf. Wolves have voices that belong to their throats. This sound belonged to nothing; it was a hole shaped like a noise, and it came from three directions at once.

The hollow-eyed hunters had picked up our trail again. Of course they had. The Regent had learned by now that the remembering roads glowed under my feet like festival lanterns, and it had learned to read the glow.

"They're four hours behind," Ash said quietly. He'd gotten good at judging them — the boy with no yesterday could measure other people's hungers with unsettling precision. "Maybe less. They don't sleep."

"No," I agreed. "They don't. Sleep is a memory of the body, and they've been relieved of those."

Merric wiped his hands on his coat. "Then we climb. And we pay as we go. Small payments. Scraps. The road will take what's loosest first — things you barely notice missing. The name of an innkeeper. The taste of a meal. You won't feel it."

I looked at the pale stones winding up into the fog, each one faintly gold, each one waiting.

"And if someone can't pay?" Ash asked.

"Then the road forgets them," Merric said. "Which sounds gentle until you understand that means it stops holding them *up*. People who default on the Old Toll Road tend to walk straight off the edge of it, mid-stride, because the path simply declines to be beneath their feet anymore."

"Wonderful," I said. "A road with the ethics of a loan shark."

"It predates ethics," Merric said. "Ethics had to be invented later, mostly to deal with roads like this one."

I stepped onto the first stone.

* * *

Paying a road feels like nothing, at first. That's what makes it monstrous.

You walk. The stones warm under your feet. Somewhere in the lattice beneath the mountain, a little door opens in you and something drifts out, and you keep walking, and you feel fine. Better than fine. Lighter. That's the trap — lightness feels like relief right up until you try to inventory what made you heavy.

By the third hour I'd lost the name of the innkeeper in Cinderhollow who used to water his ale. By the fifth, the taste of plum brandy, which apparently I had loved, because I felt its absence the way you feel a missing tooth — a smooth wrongness where something lived. By nightfall, camped in a shelf of rock halfway up the pass, I couldn't recall the color of the sky over my childhood village, though I could still see the village itself, all its roofs and chimneys rendered in perfect gray, like an etching someone had forgotten to tint.

Ash sat beside me at the fire, watching me test myself.

"What was your mother's voice like?" he asked. Gently. He asks gently, always. It doesn't help.

"I know she laughed," I said. "Low, in her chest. I know she sang off-key on purpose to make my father wince. I know she—" I stopped. Reached. Came back with smoke and no fire. "She had a voice. I'm sure she had a voice."

"You gave away her voice?"

"I gave away whatever was loosest." I fed the fire a stick harder than necessary. "The road takes what isn't nailed down. Turns out most of me isn't nailed down. Turns out a person is mostly loose parts held together by habit."

Ash was quiet a while. Then: "My whole self was taken at once. All of it. And I'm — all right? Mostly. So maybe losing it slowly is easier. Or maybe it's worse, because you watch it go."

"Thank you for that analysis. Deeply comforting."

He almost smiled. "You're welcome."

Merric, wrapped in his coat at the fire's edge, spoke without opening his eyes. "Slow down your payments tomorrow. Walk on the frost-line edges, off the center stones. The road charges less for the margins. And Zephyrine — pick what you pay. Don't let it choose. A road will strip a person down to their debts given half a chance, but it respects a deliberate offer. Old law. Older than the crown."

"Everything's older than the crown lately," I muttered. "Fine. Tomorrow I pay deliberately."

I should have thought harder about what I had left worth protecting.

* * *

The hunters gained on us through the second day. We could see them now, far below, moving up our own glowing trail like beads drawn along a thread — six shapes that walked wrong, joints bending at angles grief hadn't gotten around to yet. Their faces were smooth in the way of things that had been emptied and refilled with purpose. One of them had a swordspeaker's stride. I recognized the stride.

I recognized the man.

Corvess. My mentor. Twelve years he'd trained me — the only oath-blade left in Thornfell and the last student anyone would ever dare take. Twelve years of drills and proverbs and cold mornings, and then the Regent's hunters had caught him outside the capital, and whatever came back wasn't him, except in all the ways that mattered to my survival. His body remembered everything he knew about killing. Only Corvess himself was gone, scooped out like a gourd.

"We cut east," Merric panted. "There's a spur trail—"

"There's no spur trail," I said. "You're inventing geography again."

"I invent excellent geography."

But east was a cliff face and west was the hunters, and north was up, and up was the Old Toll Road, and the road was hungry, and behind us Corvess walked with my own footwork in his borrowed limbs.

So we climbed faster. And the road charged more.

It started asking for bigger pieces. I felt the difference — the little doors swinging wider, the withdrawals going deeper. I paid it the smell of woodsmoke, which hurt more than I expected, because woodsmoke turned out to be braided through half my life like a golden thread. I paid it the layout of a house I must have loved. I paid it the feeling of rain on a specific afternoon, standing under a specific eave, next to a specific someone whose shoulder I remembered touching without remembering the shoulder.

And then, near dusk, with the hunters close enough that I could hear the wrongness of their breathing, the road asked for something specific.

I felt it happen the way you feel a hand enter your pocket. Not violent. Almost polite. A pressure against the innermost door, the one I'd kept shut, the one behind which I'd stacked the load-bearing memories — the ones I'd decided, somewhere in the last weeks without ever saying it aloud, that I would lose last or not at all.

First among them: my mentor's face.

I want to explain why that mattered, and I find I can't fully, because the explanation requires the face, and — well. Hold that thought.

Corvess taught me the blade. Before him I was a smuggler's brat with quick hands and a temper; after him I was a swordspeaker, the last of a line that went back to the founding of the veil. Every lesson happened inside that face. The first time I disarmed him, aged thirteen, and he laughed so hard he had to sit down on the practice-yard fence — the laugh had a face attached. The day I swore my first oath and he pressed the flat of the blade to my shoulder — the pride had a face. The last time I saw him alive, turning away down a road I can no longer picture, toward a fate I already knew — even my dread wore his features.

You can lose a lot of yourself and stay upright. But some memories are foundation stones. Pull one and the whole building above it starts listing.

The road pressed against the door. The hunters were twenty minutes behind. The cliff was on the left. The drop was long.

"Zephyrine." Ash had fallen in beside me. He'd seen it too — seen me stumble, seen the stones under my feet flare brighter, greedy, the gold climbing my boots like rising water. "What's it taking?"

"Nothing," I said. "Not this one."

"Then give it something else. Fast. Give it anything—"

"It doesn't want anything else." I could feel the road's attention the way you feel a dog's eyes on the sausage in your hand. Patient. Certain. "It wants the deepest thing loose. And everything's loose now. Everything's been loose since the stars went out. I've been paying scraps for days and it knows the difference between scraps and treasure."

Merric called from ahead, voice fraying: "The ford! There's a bridge-stone ahead — tolls double at bridges, but it's also the only place it will accept a *lump sum*. One payment, one crossing, no further charge till the summit! Zephyrine, do you hear me? Decide what it's worth!"

I heard him. I heard the hunters too — that wrong breathing, closer now, and beneath it, faintly, horribly, a snatch of humming. Corvess used to hum when he sharpened blades. The thing wearing him hummed the same tune with nothing behind it, a song sung by an empty room.

Decide what it's worth.

Here is what nobody tells you about sacrifice: it never looks like the songs. In the songs you fling your arms wide and offer your heart and the heavens take note. In life it happens on a mountainside at dusk, with your legs burning and your enemies close, and you get about four seconds to choose, and the choice is ugly and small and permanent, and afterward you just keep walking because walking is the only thing left that works.

I reached the bridge-stone — a slab of white rock spanning a gorge where the road narrowed to a single lane, the ward-script on it blazing like a struck match. I put my palm flat on the stone. I opened the innermost door myself, before the road could kick it in.

"Take it," I told the road. "Deliberately. As offered. Old law."

And I thought, as hard as I have ever thought anything: *his face. Keep everything else. Take the face.*

The road took it.

* * *

People imagine forgetting is like a candle going out. It isn't. It's like a page being lifted cleanly out of a book — the binding intact, the neighbors still there, and a thin absence where the page used to be that you can run your thumb across forever.

I knelt on the far side of the bridge, and I reached for Corvess's face, and I found the shape of the reaching and nothing at the end of it.

I knew I'd had a mentor. The knowledge was intact, the page-neighbors undisturbed: twelve years, the practice yard, the oaths, the laughter, the humming. I knew he'd been tall — I knew that the way you know a fact from a ledger, not the way you know a person. I knew he had hands that moved like water over stone. I knew I loved him the way you love the ground you were built on.

But when I closed my eyes and asked for his face, my mind presented me with a smooth gray oval, patient and featureless, like the unpainted head of a practice dummy. Like an etching someone had forgotten to finish.

"That's the worst part," Ash said softly. He'd knelt beside me. He didn't touch me, but he was there, the way he's always there, the boy with no yesterday keeping vigil over someone else's loss. "It's not that the memory's gone. It's that you can *feel the hole*. I never got to feel mine. You get to feel yours every single time you reach for it."

"Lucky me," I said. My voice came out level, which cost more than crying would have.

"Do you regret it?"

I considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty and because lying to Ash is like lying to a knife — pointless and likely to end badly.

"No," I said. "Ask me again tomorrow, when I've had time to miss it properly. But no. Not tonight." I looked back across the bridge, at the hunters silhouetted on the far side, pacing the bank like wolves cheated of a kill. Corvess-that-was-Corvess stood among them, and I felt nothing looking at his shape. No recognition. No grief. Just assessment — height, reach, weapon, threat. He was a stranger wearing a stranger's body, and the man I would have mourned was a gray oval in my mind, and the road had been paid, and we were alive.

"He'd have understood," I said. To Ash, or to the oval, or to the mountain. "If there was ever any part of him left in there to ask. He always said a blade is just steel that agrees to be spent. Maybe people are too."

Ash said nothing. He just stayed kneeling beside me while the dark came down, and I think that might be the closest thing to prayer I believe in anymore.

* * *

We made the summit col by moonrise — the real moonrise, the twelfth-turn moon, fat and wrong and slightly too bright, counting down its final turn toward the thirteenth. Nine weeks since the stars went out. Three remain. The moon hung over the pass like an unpaid bill coming due.

Merric spread his charts on a flat rock and worked by shardlight — Ash holds the wardshard up now like a little lamp, and it glows for him the way the roads glow for me, and none of us comment on what that might mean.

"Past the col, the road forks," Merric murmured, tracing lines. "West goes down to the lowlands — safe, slow, useless. East climbs onto the glacier shelf. The old vault roads." His finger stopped. He didn't look up. "That's where the tomb is. If my research is right. Where they buried the king who isn't dead."

I looked east. The glacier shelf caught the moon's too-bright light and threw it back cold and blue, a road of ice running up into darkness, and somewhere up there, a man who didn't know his own name, sleeping in the ruin of himself.

"The vault roads are older than the Toll Road," Merric went on. "Older than the crown, older than the wards. Which means the toll will be different up there. Steeper. And Zephyrine—" Now he looked up, and in the shardlight his face was grim as winter. "After today, after what you just paid, I have to ask. How much more of you is there to spend?"

I opened my mouth to answer — something wry, probably, something deflecting, it's what I'm best at besides swordwork.

And then every stone on the summit col lit up gold at once, all of them, the whole mountainside flaring like a signal fire, and Ash said, very quietly:

"It's not the road. Something's answering it. Something under the ice."

Far below us, at the tree line, the hunters had stopped pacing.

They had all turned to face the glacier.

All of them. Even him.

Even the empty shape that used to be my mentor — the gray-oval ghost of him — stood motionless in the dark, staring up at the ice, and began, in a voice with nothing behind it, to sing.
