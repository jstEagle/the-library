## 15. What Wears the Crown

I woke to the taste of ash and someone else's grief.

That is not poetry. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only dignity left to me. The grief was not mine — I knew that the way you know a coat doesn't fit. It hung off my shoulders wrong. Someone had wept inside it before me, and their sorrow had soaked into the lining.

The woman with the sword sat across the fire from me, sharpening a blade that hummed like a struck bell when she wasn't looking at it. The boy slept curled against the glacier wall, his fused hand tucked under his chin like something he'd learned to be ashamed of. And the disgraced wardwright — Vale — was watching me the way a man watches a bridge he isn't sure will hold his weight.

"You're awake," he said.

"I'm not certain that's the word for it." My voice came out rusted. I tried again. "Where am I?"

"Under the Vharen Glacier. In your tomb." He said it without apology, which I would come to appreciate about him. "Or what everyone believed was your tomb. Including, apparently, you."

I looked at my hands. They were a king's hands — or they had been once. Now the knuckles were wrong somehow, the way a house is wrong after strangers have lived in it and moved all the furniture an inch to the left. "I don't remember," I said. "Any of it."

"No," said the swordswoman, not looking up from her whetstone. "You wouldn't. It ate you first."

* * *

Her name was Zephyrine Iversen. I know this because she told me four times over the following days, patiently, each time as if it were the first, because each time it was. That is what being hollowed means. Not emptiness — emptiness would be restful. It's a bucket with a crack in the bottom. Things pour in and drain away, and you stand there holding a vessel that has forgotten how to hold.

The boy — Ash, they called him, though even that was chosen rather than remembered — fed me broth and fragments. Fragments of me, actually. When the shard in his palm touched my skin, sometimes something would surface: a corridor. A bell. A girl laughing in a garden I did not recognize and recognized completely.

"That's her," Ash said on the third day, breathless. "The laugh. That's your daughter. You're smiling — do you feel yourself smiling?"

I felt my face do something. Whether it qualified as a smile or merely the memory of one, I couldn't say.

Zephyrine watched these sessions from across the fire with an expression I eventually learned to read, because it kept appearing: guilt wearing the mask of patience. She was giving me pieces of myself out of that humming blade of hers, and every piece cost her something she didn't name. I saw her reach up afterward and touch the place behind her ear where a memory might live, the way a man touches a socket where a tooth used to be.

On the fourth day, I was strong enough to sit up without the world tilting. On the fifth, I remembered my own name — Aldric, third of that line, crowned at nineteen, widowed at thirty-one — and I wept for an hour over it, which embarrassed us all.

And on the sixth day, Zephyrine sat down across from me, laid the blade flat between us, and said: "Tell me what it is. What actually wears the crown. Merric thinks it's a demon. The old texts say demon. The priests say demon. But demons don't eat memories selectively. Demons aren't patient. So tell me what I'm going to have to kill."

I looked at the fire for a long time.

"I can't tell you what to kill," I said finally. "Because there's nothing to kill. And that is entirely my fault. Mine, and every crown that came before mine."

* * *

Here is what I told them, and I will set it down plainly, because I spent ten years as a hollow thing and I refuse to spend whatever remains of my life speaking in riddles the way kings do when the truth would cost them something.

There is no demon behind the veil.

There never was.

What my ancestors called the Hungry Thing, what the ward-laws were built to hold back, what the Regent now embodies — it began as a practice. An accounting trick. The oldest magic in Thornfell is not fire or frost or any of the showy disciplines. It is the trade of memory for power. Every coronation oath contains it, folded small, disguised as solemnity: *I give my whole self to the realm.* The words sound like devotion. They are a contract clause. The throne takes. That is its function. A king who gives everything remembers nothing, and a king who remembers nothing cannot rule — so the founders built a relief valve. The crown stores what the king surrenders, holds it in trust, and returns it gently, spoon by spoon, so long as the king governs well.

Do you see the flaw? Of course you see the flaw. It took my line nine generations to see it, and we were proud of how cleverly we'd hidden it.

The crown does not store. The crown *digests*. Slowly, over years, the memories a monarch feeds it change. They lose their warmth. A memory held outside a living mind becomes a specimen, pinned under glass, and the throne grew fat on specimens. Every sacrifice of self for power — and kings make dozens, hundreds; we call them hard choices and sleep fine afterward — every one of those went into the same dark treasury. And hungers accumulate. Grief accumulates. Ten generations of rulers, each feeding the thing a little more of themselves, each taking a little more comfort in the numbness it traded back—

It gained weight. It gained appetite. And eventually it gained *shape*, because enough hunger, pressed together long enough, learns to want the way a person wants. It wanted to wear the office itself. Not the crown — the *crown-ness*. The being-fed. The being-obeyed.

"So it took yours," Merric said quietly. He had stopped pretending to take notes somewhere around the second generation of my family history.

"It took mine," I agreed. "Because I made it easy. You should understand this part, Zephyrine, since you're planning to fight it. It didn't breach the wards. It didn't devour them from outside like some beast at a gate. I opened the door. I was drowning in decisions — a war on the western march, a famine in the river valleys, a council full of men who needed managing — and it came to me the way temptation always comes: dressed as mercy. It offered to carry what I couldn't. One memory at a time. The face of the envoy I'd sent to die. The sound of my daughter crying when I left for the front. Small things. Necessary losses. I told myself I'd reclaim them later, when the realm was safe."

"How long before you noticed?" Zephyrine asked.

"Three years. Maybe less. The trouble with surrendering memories is that you also surrender the ability to notice they're gone. Each one takes a little of the map with it. By the time I understood what was happening, I couldn't find the door anymore, let alone the way out. It poured what was left of me back into—" I gestured at myself. "At this. An empty coat. And it stepped into the shape of me, and it has been wearing me to court ever since."

Silence around the fire. The glacier ticked and groaned above us, settling its ancient weight, and I found I envied it. Ice gets to just be ice.

"The refugees," Zephyrine said at last. "In Cinderhollow. Mothers who couldn't remember their children's faces. That's it feeding, isn't it? It's not satisfied with the court."

"It will never be satisfied." I heard my own voice go flat and cold, and I let it, because softness here would have been a lie. "That is the nature of what it is. It isn't a creature with a stomach. It's a debt with a mouth. Every ruler who chose numbness over grief, convenience over conscience, power over presence — every one of them is still in there, still hungry, still making the same choice. It is not evil the way your priests mean evil. It is worse. It is *reasonable*. Every bite it takes was once justified by someone sensible. That is why your blade frightens it, swordspeaker. Living memory — memory that stays warm because someone chooses to keep carrying it — that's the one thing it cannot digest. It can only envy it. And envy, in a thing that size, looks exactly like hatred."

* * *

Ash spoke up then, from the shadows near the wall, in the small voice he used when he was afraid of the answer. "Can it be killed?"

"No."

The word landed among us like a dropped stone. I watched each of them absorb it differently: Merric with grim confirmation, as if he'd known for years and been imprisoned for saying so; Zephyrine with a tightening around her jaw that meant she was already re-planning; Ash with something close to despair, because he was young, and the young still believe monsters have hearts you can stab.

"But it can be unbound," I said. "And refused. Listen carefully, because this is the whole of it."

The Regent is not a body. It is a pattern — an accumulation given temporary coherence by the throne's original working. Cut that coherence apart and the hunger doesn't die; it scatters, back into the lattice of the veil, back into the old contracts, dormant until the next generation of rulers starts feeding it again. Unbinding is possible. My ancestors built the release mechanism into the founding weave itself, at the first citadel, at the original seam — a failsafe, ironically, against the very thing they were creating. But unbinding requires three things, and I will name them plainly:

A king's unbound hand. Not a king under oath, not a king mid-contract — a sovereign who stands outside the trade, who owes the throne nothing and the throne owes nothing to. I am, at present, the only such hand in existence, and only because I've been scraped empty. There is a joke in there. I am not yet able to laugh at it.

A wardwright's craft, to unpick the lattice without collapsing the veil entire — and the veil, mind you, is the only reason anything on either side of it stays itself.

And an oath-blade full of willing memories. Memory freely given, still warm, still chosen. The one currency it cannot counterfeit and cannot refuse, because refusing it would mean admitting it was never owed anything at all.

Zephyrine looked down at the blade between them, at the faint light moving beneath its surface like fish under ice. She knew what I was asking before I asked it. I watched her decide not to flinch.

"There's more," I said, because she deserved the worst of it before the hope. "Unbinding alone won't save Thornfell. A scattered hunger waits. It regathers. Unless—" I paused. This was the part I had rehearsed in the dark for six days, and it still came out rough. "Unless it is refused. Openly. By the ones who created it. Every heir to every crown, every soul bound into those old oaths, must look at the bargain their ancestors made and say: *no more. We keep our griefs. We keep our names. We will rule remembering, and if remembering unmakes us, then we deserve to be unmade.* The Regent exists because we kept agreeing to it. It ends when we stop."

"And if they don't stop?" Merric asked. "If the lords of Thornfell hear your proclamation and decide a comfortable monster beats an honest throne?"

"Then it comes back," I said. "Maybe not in my lifetime. Maybe wearing someone kinder. And they'll have earned it."

* * *

That night I could not sleep, which was fitting; I had spent ten years doing nothing else.

I walked — as far as the tomb allowed, which was forty paces and back — and I thought about responsibility, that great stone kings are taught to carry without looking at. For years, while I wore the crown, I told myself the sacrifices were the realm's, that my losses were taxes paid on behalf of a grateful nation. It was a comfortable lie, and comfortable lies are the only currency the hungry thing accepts.

But sitting in my own tomb, stripped to the studs, I could see it clearly at last: no one made me feed it. No demon held my hand to the contract. Nine generations of my blood chose, one small sensible surrender at a time, to build the mouth that ate us. The realm's enemies were never beyond the veil. The veil was a mirror, and we could not bear what looked back.

So I would not ask forgiveness. I would not ask pity, though the boy gave it anyway, freely, the way he seemed to give everything. I would ask only this: to stand at the seam with an unbound hand and an honest ledger, and to speak the refusal aloud — not as a king reclaiming a throne, but as a man returning a stolen inheritance to its rightful owners, which is to say, to everyone.

Toward dawn, Zephyrine found me standing at the tomb entrance, watching the blue dark of the glacier breathe.

"You should sleep," she said.

"Later. There's something you need to hear before you finish deciding whatever you're deciding." I turned to face her. "The seam is beneath the first citadel. Three weeks' travel, if the roads haven't fully turned against us. And the thirteenth moon-turn completes in—"

"Nine weeks," she said. "We know."

"Then know this too." I took a breath, and it hurt, the way truth does after ten years of lies. "When we unbind it — when your blade opens and everything your ancestors gave you pours into the weave — it will not go quietly. A starving thing offered a feast does not say grace. It lunges. Everything it has eaten, everything it *is*, will turn on the hand that feeds it. It will offer you bargains. It will wear faces you love. It will show you the exact shape of what you're losing and ask if you're certain." I held her gaze. "It did all of that to me, and I was only surrendering scraps. You'll be surrendering everything. So before we take one step toward that citadel — are you certain?"

She was quiet a long moment. Somewhere above us, the glacier shifted, shedding a sheet of ancient ice that shattered on the slope below like the world clearing its throat.

"No," she said finally. "But I've never once been certain of anything, and it hasn't killed me yet."

Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Merric stood suddenly rigid, his head cocked toward the tomb's outer dark — and in his hand, the old wardwright's compass had begun to spin, wild and fast, pointing not north but *down*, toward the deep places of the world.

"It knows," he whispered. "Gods help us. It knows we're coming."
