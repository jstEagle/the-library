## 16. The Veil's Crooked Seam

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in ruined places, and I have spent enough of my life in them to know its moods. Some ruins are merely tired — the silence of a house whose family moved on. Others are ashamed. And a very few, the worst ones, are *watching*.

The First Citadel of Thornfell was all three.

We crested the ridge at what Ash solemnly informed us was "morning, probably," because none of us had seen the sky do anything so useful as brighten since the stars went out. The moon — the twelfth of thirteen, fat and wrong, hanging like a coin someone had bitten to test — threw just enough light to show us what waited below: a broken crown of walls around a sunken court, and beneath that, if my research was right and I had staked rather a lot on its being right, the seam itself.

"There," I said, because a wardwright learns early that pointing is cheaper than explaining and only slightly less useful.

Zephyrine squinted down the slope. "That's it? It looks like every other ruin we've walked through for three weeks."

"That's rather the point." I started down the scree before my knees could file a formal objection. "The first citadel wasn't built to be found. It was built to be *forgotten*. The wards were woven here, and the weavers understood something the later architects didn't — that a secret you build a monument to is already half-given away. So they made this place deliberately dull. Deliberately unremarkable. The most dangerous thing in the kingdom, dressed as rubble."

"Like you, then," Ash said cheerfully.

I chose not to dignify that. The boy had developed a talent for the observation-as-knife school of conversation, and I suspected he'd learned it from Zephyrine, who had never once in our acquaintance said anything kind to me without immediately following it with something true and much less comfortable.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about the veil, since apparently I'm the one narrating now, and a man should at least justify his presence in his own chapter.

The world is not one thing. It is two — or more, but two is the number that matters — stitched together along a seam that runs through everything like a scar through an apple. On one side: the world of matter, of stone and blood and weather. On the other: memory. Not metaphorical memory. Actual, living memory, the accumulated past of everything that has ever happened, coiled and breathing behind the fabric of the present.

The first wardwrights — and I use *wardwrights* loosely; they were more priests than engineers, though the distinction has always flattered the priests — discovered the seam and did the thing people always do with power. They used it. They drew memory through the weave and set it into standing stones and oath-blades and royal lines, and the kingdom of Thornfell grew fat and safe on borrowed recollection. The border wards weren't walls. They were *remembered promises*, woven so densely that nothing from the far side could slip through without being recognized, named, and refused.

And then, a decade ago, something on the far side learned to eat.

I warned the crown. I have told this part before and I will not apologize for telling it again: I stood in the Court of Bright Hours with my charts and my trembling hands and I said, "Your Majesty, there is a hungry thing behind the veil," and the king laughed — that warm, repeating laughter, each note identical to the last — and six months later I was in a cell, and ten years after that I was standing in a ruin with a smuggler, a boy, and a hollowed king, about to commit the largest act of unauthorized wardcraft in recorded history.

My mother would have been proud. She'd also have hit me with a spoon for taking this long.

* * *

The descent into the citadel took the better part of the day, because the ruin did not wish to be entered and had opinions about being hurried.

Staircases that should have gone down went sideways. Corridors doubled back on their own footprints. Twice I caught Zephyrine counting doorways under her breath and twice the count came out different, which she handled with considerably more grace than I did; I handled it by sitting down on a fallen lintel and having a small, professional crisis.

"It's not shifting," I said finally, when my hands had stopped shaking enough to hold chalk. "It can't shift — the lattice is dead, the Regent ate it. What's happening is it's *misremembering* itself. This place was built out of memory, and when the memory rotted, the architecture started... improvising."

"Improvising," Aldric repeated. He stood a little apart from us, as he always did — a tall man wearing his own body like a coat borrowed from a generous stranger. Ten days of fed memories had given him back fragments: his name, mostly. His daughter's laugh, which he asked Zephyrine to repeat more often than any of us liked to witness. But the whole of him was still missing, and he knew it, and he carried the knowing with a dignity that made me want to both salute him and weep into my collar.

"The building doesn't remember how to be a building," I explained. "So it guesses. Badly."

"Then how do we find the seam?"

I pulled out the one object I had left from my old life: a brass theodolite no larger than a snuffbox, its lenses ground from wardglass. "We ask it what it misses."

* * *

Here is a trade secret they don't put in the guild manuals: dead wards leave ghosts. Not spirits — impressions. When a lattice of woven memory collapses, the shape of what it *was* lingers in the stone the way a chair's shape lingers in carpet. A competent wardwright can read those impressions. A disgraced one, with nothing left to lose and a decade of prison arithmetic sharpening her mind, can read them *fast*.

I knelt in the sunken court as the false light failed and swept the wardglass lens across the flagstones, and there — faint as breath on cold glass — the ghost of the original lattice bloomed under my eye.

"Oh," I said. Then, with more feeling: "*Oh*."

"What?" Three voices at once. Even the king leaned in, and I felt briefly, absurdly, like a lecturer again — the good years, before the bad ones, when students hung on my diagrams instead of the crown hanging me.

"It's not hidden underneath the citadel," I said. "It's not a vault, a chamber, a buried anything. The seam runs through the *whole site*. Every foundation stone, every broken wall — the entire ruin is sitting on top of the seam like a bandage on a wound. They didn't build over it. They built *with* it. The First Citadel was never a fortress. It was a suture."

Ash crouched beside me, holding his lantern low, and the wardshard fused to his palm drank the light and gave back something else — a shimmer, a suggestion of threads running through the ground in patterns too vast and regular to be natural. "It looks like stitching," he said softly. "Crooked stitches."

"Crooked," I agreed. "Because the weavers were crooked-handed. Memory doesn't lie flat, you see — it pulls, it puckers, it wants to go home to whatever it remembers being. You can't sew the worlds together straight. You sew them together *willingly crooked*, and the crookedness is what holds." I sat back on my heels and looked at the pattern spreading out beneath us, beautiful and terrible, and heard myself add, in the flat voice I reserve for conclusions I hate: "Which means the reforging won't happen in a room. It'll happen across the whole ruin. All of us, spread out on the seam, working at once, while the thirteenth moon turns."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Somewhere above us, wind moved through broken colonnades with a sound like slow applause.

"And the price?" Zephyrine asked. Because she always asked. It was, I had come to understand, her defining virtue and her doom in a single habit: she never let hope arrive without checking its luggage.

* * *

I laid it out for them that night, in the shelter of a collapsed granary, with a fire small enough to hide and a diagram scratched in the ash floor. I have given worse lectures to better audiences, but never to one listening this hard.

"Three things," I said. "The old laws are explicit. First: an unbound hand. The king's hand, specifically — the hand that swore the founding oaths, touching the seam directly. Not bound by crown, not bound by office, not bound by anyone else's claim on him. Free to choose. That's why the Regent could never reforge the veil itself; everything it touches becomes bound to it, and a bound hand can't hold a needle."

Aldric looked at his own hands, turning them over as though checking them for bindings the way a man checks his boots for stones. "Mine are as unbound as hands get," he said dryly. "I'm barely bound to my own name."

"Second," I went on, before the pity in the air could thicken, "a wardwright's craft. Someone who can read the lattice, cut the new thread, tie the knots. Someone who understands that crookedness is strength." I tapped my own chest, because false modesty is for men who have something left to protect. "That's me. Prison taught me patience; disgrace taught me precision. I am, God forgive me, exactly the craftsman this job requires."

"And third," Zephyrine said.

She said it quietly, and I hated her a little for making me say it aloud anyway.

"Third: an oath-blade full of willing memories. The new weave has to be anchored in living recollection — that's the thread itself. Dead memory crumbles; coerced memory poisons the stitch. It has to be *given*, freely, by someone who carries oaths in steel." I met her eyes across the ash-diagram. "You are the last swordspeaker in Thornfell, Zephyrine Iversen. Your blade holds the memories of every oath-sworn ancestor you have. Pouring them into the seam will empty it. Emptying it will—"

"I know what emptying it will do."

"You don't, actually," I said, and my voice came out harder than I intended, so I let it stay hard, because softness would have been a lie and she deserved better than lies at this particular juncture. "You think it will cost you your ancestors' voices. It will cost you *yourself*. Those memories aren't stored in the blade like coins in a purse — they're braided into yours. Every gift you've already given the king took a piece of you with it. This takes the braid. Your roads. Your oaths. Your—" I stopped, because the next word was *name*, and saying it made it a plan rather than a possibility.

The fire cracked. Ash had gone very still, the way he did when he was using the shard without meaning to — reading the room's memories instead of its mood.

"Willing," Zephyrine said at last. "You said willing memories. Given freely."

"Yes."

"So it has to be my choice." She turned her blade over in the firelight — that plain, worn sword that whispered in voices older than the kingdom. "Nobody can spend me. Not even you. Especially not you."

"No one could spend you," I said, "and live long enough to enjoy it."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face. "Good. Keep it that way until I've decided."

* * *

I couldn't sleep after that, which surprised no one, least of all me. I climbed out of the granary and walked the sunken court with the wardglass lens, tracing the ghost-lattice, doing the arithmetic no one should do alone at night: the length of the new weave against the speed of my aging hands, the turn of the final moon against every delay the road had already charged us.

The Regent would know we were here soon. Perhaps it already knew. A thing that eats memory had spent ten years learning the taste of this kingdom, and we were four small flames moving across a field it considered its pantry.

"Merric Vale."

The king's voice, behind me. I turned. Aldric stood at the edge of the court, bareheaded, looking down at the invisible seam as if he could see it without glass. Perhaps he could. Kings were woven closer to the veil than the rest of us; that was the whole obscene bargain of crowns.

"You should sleep," I said.

"So should you." He came and stood beside me, and for a while we were just two middle-aged men failing at rest in a ruin. Then: "You said the founders wove the wards from royal memory. From *my* line's memory."

"Some of it. Yes."

"Then the Regent ate my memories twice over. Once out of my head, once out of the wards." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Tell me truly, wardwright. When we reforge the seam — when I put my hand on it — what will it take from me? What's left to take?"

I thought about lying to a king. It occurred to me that this was precisely how the Regent had been born — not as a demon, but as an accumulation, every ruler's convenient falsehoods compounding like interest until the debt grew teeth. I was not going to add my grain to that pile.

"Everything you've recovered," I said. "And everything you haven't. The unbound hand gives freely or not at all — but the seam doesn't distinguish between what a man treasures and what he's forgotten he treasures. It will take what it needs to hold the worlds together, and it will not send a receipt."

He nodded slowly. "And if I refuse?"

"Then the thirteenth moon completes, the binding finishes, and the Regent becomes permanent. The veil stays eaten. Everything behind it comes through. There is no second option, Your Majesty. There is only the invoice, and the question of who signs."

"Invoice," he repeated, tasting the word. "You have a merchant's soul, Merric Vale."

"My mother sold cloth," I admitted. "She said every honest transaction hurts a little, and every painless one is theft. I've tried to run my life accordingly. It has not made me popular."

"No," he said, and there was, unbelievably, warmth in it. "But it may make you the most honest man in Thornfell, which is a rarer accomplishment."

* * *

Dawn — or the gray approximation the sky offered in place of one — found Ash sitting at the rim of the court with his legs dangling over the seam-side of the world, the wardshard glowing softly against his palm.

I went to sit with him, because someone should, and because I had a professional question I'd been avoiding.

"The shard," I said. "It's a fragment of the original lattice. Fallen wardstone. Which means it remembers how the weaving was done — the actual handcraft, the knots, the tension. When we work the seam, I'll need it. I'll need *you*."

He was quiet for a moment. Below us, the ghost-threads pulsed faintly, keeping time with something.

"Okay," he said.

"Just like that?"

"You're asking. Nobody asks me for things — they either need me or they're worried about me." He glanced up, and there was an oldness in his young face that no child should own. "Asking is nice. I might get used to it."

"Ash." I hesitated, then did the brave thing, which for me means the honest thing. "When we open the weave, it will show you things. The seam holds everything the wards ever carried — including, possibly, the day the Regent first fed. Including, possibly, *you*. Whatever it offers you — whatever version of yourself it puts on the table — remember that you're allowed to refuse. Remember that a name chosen is worth more than a name returned."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I realized with a start that he wasn't reading my words. He was reading my memories, gently, the way you lift a letter from a table — and whatever he found there, he nodded once, slowly, like a man confirming an appointment.

"They'll come for us tomorrow," he said. "Or the day after. I can feel it through the stone. It's hungry, and we're the last meal on the menu, and it knows where we are now."

"Then we'd best finish the shopping list," I said, and got up to wake the others, because hope had arrived with its invoice, and there remained only the small matter of payment — and the smaller matter of surviving long enough to sign.
