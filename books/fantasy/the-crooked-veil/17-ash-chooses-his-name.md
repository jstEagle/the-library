## 17. Ash Chooses His Name

The night before the end of the world, I learned that I used to have excellent handwriting.

This is not the sort of revelation most people get. Most people get revelations like *you are loved* or *your life has meaning*. Mine came to me in the flicker of a wardshard's light, in the shape of a ledger page I apparently once copied out in a small, careful script — grain tallies for the royal kitchens, dated eleven years ago, signed at the bottom in a boy's neat hand: *Ashen Vael, page to His Majesty King Aldric the Second of That Name.*

I stared at my own signature for a long time.

"It's a good name," Merric said from across the fire. He was pretending to work on the lattice-frame, bending wire with fingers that shook more than he'd ever admit. "Vael. Old lowland family. They were stewards before they were anything else."

"I don't remember them."

"No," Merric said gently. "You wouldn't."

The shard sat in my palm where it had lived for months now, fused to the skin like a second knucklebone, warm as a held coin. It had shown me other people's memories since the day I found it — Zephyrine swearing her first oath, a dead soldier's last battle, a mother singing a lullaby in a language neither of us knew. Borrowed things. Secondhand grief. I had gotten good at telling what was mine and what wasn't, mostly because almost nothing was mine.

But tonight, camped in the shadow of the ruined first citadel with the thirteenth moon one turn from full, the shard had finally turned its glass eye inward. And it had found me.

*Ashen Vael,* it whispered against my skin, patient as water wearing down stone. *Ashen Vael. I can give him back.*

* * *

Here is what I know about myself, in the order I learned it:

I know I woke up in a smuggler's wagon outside Cinderhollow with no name, no past, and a burning stone stuck to my hand. I know Zephyrine found me instead of throwing me off the wagon, which by her standards was practically an adoption ceremony. I know Merric looked at me that first week with an expression I couldn't read then but can now — the look of a man seeing a debt he thought forgiven walking around on two legs.

And I know that when I needed a name, I picked one off a burned tree.

That's true, actually. The first village we passed through after Cinderhollow had a rowan tree scorched black by wardfire, and someone had scratched letters into the char — old ward-marks, half legible. One of them looked like *ASH*. I was eight years old, or thereabouts, or possibly nine; when you have no yesterday, every age is a guess. I pointed at it and said, "That's me," and Zephyrine had looked at me for a long moment with something complicated moving behind her eyes, and then she said, "All right, Ash. Try not to set anything else on fire."

So that's my name. Not inherited. Not bestowed by a priest or a parent or a king. Chosen, off a burnt tree, by a boy who had nothing else to choose from. It fits the way a coat fits when you buy it yourself instead of being measured for it — slightly wrong in the shoulders, entirely right everywhere that matters.

And tonight the shard wanted to take it away from me.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around midnight I gave up pretending and climbed out of the wagon, careful of the boards that creaked, and walked to the edge of camp where the citadel's bones rose against the sky.

The ruined first citadel is a strange place at night. The wards died here first — this is where the veil was first sewn, and so it's where it first tore — and the absence has a texture. Not silence exactly. Silence is just no sound. This was the memory of sound, worn smooth, like the dent a head leaves on a pillow long after the sleeper gets up. The stones remembered being a wall. The air remembered being guarded. Nothing here had forgotten its purpose; it had only lost the ability to fulfill it.

Which, I suppose, made it kin to about four people in this camp.

"You should be sleeping." Zephyrine's voice came out of the dark, and then Zephyrine did too, wrapping her cloak tighter, sword on her hip even at midnight, even on the eve of everything. She sat down on a fallen lintel without asking if the seat was taken. It was a ruin. Everything was available.

"Can't."

"The shard?"

"How'd you guess."

She snorted. "Because you're holding your hand like it owes you money, and because you've been making that face since sundown. The face you make when it's showing you something you don't want."

I turned my palm up. In the starless dark the shard glowed faintly, sullenly, like an ember refusing to admit the fire was over. "It found me," I said. "Not somebody else. Me. There was a ledger. Kitchen accounts. My handwriting was better than yours, by the way."

"My handwriting is a war crime," she agreed. "Go on."

"Ashen Vael. Page to King Aldric." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it does when I'm carrying something heavy and don't want to feel the weight yet. "First victim. That's what it keeps saying. First victim. The Regent ate me before it ate the wards, before it ate the king. I was — practice, I think. An appetizer." I laughed, because that's what I do instead of crying; I come by it honestly, probably, wherever honestly is. "Eleven years old, maybe. Copying grain tallies one minute, and the next — nothing. It took all of it. And it kept the shape of me somewhere, like a pressed flower, and now it wants to sell it back."

Zephyrine was quiet for a while. She's good at quiet. Most people fill silences like they're afraid of them; she treats them like rooms worth sitting in.

"What does it cost?" she asked finally.

"That's the thing. It says nothing. It says the memory is mine, it was stolen from me, taking it back is only justice." I flexed my fingers. The shard pulsed, eager, a dog hearing its leash. "It's lying. It always lies by telling the truth sideways. If I take Ashen Vael back — his mother, his brothers, whatever the Regent left of him — then who's holding the rest? Who's standing watch over the boy who chose his name off a burnt tree?" I looked at her. "He doesn't disappear, Zeph. He just goes back in the drawer. And I'd be — I'd be wearing him. Like the Regent wears Aldric's crown."

Something moved through her face then, fast, like a fish under ice. I'd said the thing we were all thinking and none of us said: the enemy's whole method was putting on other people's lives. Wearing the king. Wearing the mentor whose skills she'd have to fight tomorrow. Every horror we'd walked through for eleven weeks had been some version of *a self that isn't yours, worn well enough to pass.*

"You could say no," she said, "and still grieve him. Both things fit in one person. I'm told that's how people work." Her mouth twisted. "Though I'll admit my sample size is mostly ancestors in a sword."

"He was a child," I said. "Somebody's son. He had a mother who — " The shard flared, helpful, offering: a kitchen smelling of bread, a woman's hands braiding hair, a voice saying *Ashen, mind the candles.* I wrenched my attention off it like pulling a hand off a hot stove. "See? It does that. It keeps feeding him to me in pieces, hoping I'll get hungry."

"And are you?"

I wanted to lie to her. She'd earned better than that months ago, though, so: "Every minute. It's like standing outside a bakery when you haven't eaten in days. Except the bread is a dead kid's whole life, and eating it means he stays dead in a different way."

* * *

Merric found us near dawn. Or dawn-adjacent; the sky had been the color of dirty wool for weeks, stars gone, sun a rumor behind haze, and morning was more a consensus than an event. He lowered himself onto the lintel with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and he looked at my open palm, and he sighed the sigh of a man who had already had this conversation with himself and lost.

"The shard showed you," he said. Not a question.

"It showed me a boy with nice handwriting."

"Ashen Vael." Merric said the name carefully, the way you handle something fragile. "I looked for him, you know. After. When I still thought warning people would help. The palace rolls listed a page missing, presumed fled during the 'illness' that took the king's household. I pulled the records. There were no records. There was never any record of him at all — the Regent had eaten him so thoroughly that the ink forgot him. Eleven years old, and the world's paperwork couldn't recall he'd ever existed." He rubbed his eyes. "Except the shard. Wardstone holds what paper can't. It's been carrying him all this time, waiting for a hand to offer him back to."

"Why wait eleven years?" I asked. "Why not show anyone?"

"Because it needed someone empty enough to hold him," Merric said, and then winced. "I mean —"

"No, you're right." I almost smiled. "I'm the only person alive with a hole exactly his shape. Congratulations, shard. Excellent logistics."

Zephyrine shot Merric a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces. He raised his hands. "I'm saying it badly. What I mean is: the choice was always going to be real. You can't return a stolen thing to someone who was never robbed. You were robbed, Ash. That's not in question. The question is whether taking it back makes you whole or just makes you two people in one coat, and I confess I don't know the answer. I've spent eleven years wearing a man I used to be, and I can tell you it doesn't fit anymore either way."

That was the most honest thing Merric Vale had ever said in my hearing, and considering the man had confessed to treason-by-necessity at a mountain waystation three weeks ago, that was saying something.

"What would you do?" I asked him.

"To my eternal shame? Eleven years ago I'd have taken it back. I'd have said a self is a self, and hunger is hunger, and let the philosophers sort the difference." He looked into the grey nothing where the sunrise should have been. "Now I think a self isn't a possession. It's a decision you keep remaking. And I think the Regent's whole crime — the original one, the one all the others grew from — was deciding that what you were given matters more than what you choose. Every ruler who fed it memory for power made that same trade. What I inherited, over what I'll build." He shrugged, tired. "Take the boy back if you want him, lad. Refuse him if you want to be yourself. But make it a choice, not a reflex. That's all any of us get."

* * *

I went alone to the citadel's heart around midday, because some arguments you have to lose or win in private.

The great hall was roofless, floorless in places, a ribcage of a building. The seam was down below — Merric had mapped it, the crooked stitch in the world where the first wardwrights had sewn sky to earth — but I wasn't there for the seam. I was there because the hall's stones still remembered coronations, and I figured if anywhere could help me think about kings and pages and names, it was here.

I sat on a broken step and opened my hand and let the shard speak.

It didn't waste time. It poured him into me: Ashen Vael, age eleven, running messages through corridors that smelled of beeswax and cold stone. Ashen Vael learning to bow without wobbling. Ashen Vael stealing honey cakes and getting caught by the king himself, who didn't punish him, just laughed — *that laugh*, the real one, the one the court hadn't heard in a decade, warm as a hearth — and said, *Well, then, we'll share it, page, and be criminals together.* Ashen Vael's mother braiding his hair by candlelight. Ashen Vael's whole small, bright, ordinary life, offered up like a tray.

And underneath it, soft as a hook under bait: *take him back. Be him again. You never have to be nobody ever again.*

I sat with it. I let myself want it, fully, the way Merric said — not flinching. God, I wanted it. A mother. A history. A name that came with a family attached instead of a burnt tree. For about ten minutes I was the most miserable boy in Thornfell, sitting in a ruin wanting to be a ghost.

Then I noticed something, and it changed everything.

In every memory, I was watching Ashen Vael from the outside. Seeing him the way the shard saw him — archived, catalogued, complete. And in not one single memory was there anything of what happened *after*. No fear. No dark. No moment of the taking. The Regent had eaten that part first, or the shard had never held it, and so the boy on offer was a boy with the worst hour of his life edited out. A pressed flower. Beautiful, flat, and dead.

That's not a life, I realized. That's a portrait of one.

If I took him back, I wouldn't be a boy with a mother and a king's friendship. I'd be a boy with a portrait, wearing it like the Regent wears the crown — smiling a smile that repeats like an echo, radiant and utterly wrong. I'd be the smallest hollow thing in Thornfell, and I'd have done it to myself, gladly, because I was hungry.

"No," I said aloud. My voice bounced off the remembering stones. "Thank you. But no."

The shard went very still. Then it pressed harder, and I felt it for the first time as what it maybe had been all along: not a gift, but a mouth. The Regent's oldest trick, scaled down. *Everything you were, returned. All you must surrender is everything you chose.*

"Yeah," I said. "That's the deal it always offers. We've met."

I stood up. I held my hand out over the stones where a king had once shared honey cakes with a child, and I spoke to the shard the way Zephyrine speaks oaths — plain words, no ornament, the kind that stick.

"I'm keeping the name I picked. Ash. Off a burnt tree, in a town I barely remember, given to me by a woman who owed me nothing and gave me a place anyway. It's not as old as Vael. It's not as pretty. Nobody wrote it in a ledger." I closed my fist. "But I chose it. And I'm choosing it again right now. And I'll choose it again tomorrow, and the day after, until somebody writes it on my grave or I get bored of dying. That's the whole inheritance I need. Selfhood isn't what you're given. It's what you keep deciding. So — decision made. Again."

The shard flared — furious, grieving, hungry, I couldn't tell and didn't try — and then, slowly, it dimmed. Not dead. Dimmed. Like a dog that has finally understood the door is closed and lies down anyway. The warmth in my palm settled into something quieter. Almost companionable.

And in the last flicker before it stilled, it showed me one thing more. Not Ashen Vael. Not a memory at all — a fragment of the lattice, the veil's weave, the pattern Merric had been bending wire toward for weeks. A gap in it. A place where a shard-shaped piece of living wardstone might slot home, if a hand were willing.

Oh, I thought. *There* you are. That's why you showed me him. Not mercy. Positioning.

Well. Two could play at knowing what they were for.

* * *

They were waiting when I came out — Zephyrine and Merric both, trying very hard to look like they hadn't been waiting, failing completely. Beyond them, the camp was stirring. Aldric stood at the fire with a bowl in his hands, a king rebuilt from borrowed memories, watching me with the particular attention of a man who had been a blank once himself and recognized the weather.

"Well?" Zephyrine asked.

"I'm keeping the name," I said. "Ash. The burnt-tree special. Limited edition."

Her shoulders came down half an inch, which from her was a standing ovation. "Good. Because I've gotten used to yelling it across valleys, and I hate learning new words."

Merric studied my face, then my closed fist. "And the shard?"

"Oh, it's fine. Sulking." I held up my hand, and let the glow show — steady now, banked, obedient to me instead of the other way round. "Turns out it wasn't offering me a gift. It was showing me where it goes. There's a gap in the weave, down at the seam. A shard-shaped gap." I looked at each of them, and felt something settle in my chest that I suspected other people called certainty. "It's been waiting eleven years to go home. It just needed a boy empty enough to carry it there."

Zephyrine's eyes narrowed. "And are you going to?"

The thirteenth moon hung invisible behind the dirty wool of the sky, one turn from full, and somewhere beneath our feet the Hollow Regent was winding itself toward permanence, and tomorrow — or the day after, or however long the clock allowed — a king would put his unbound hand on the veil and a wardwright would reweave the lattice and a swordspeaker would pour herself out like light.

"I'm going to sleep," I said. "Tomorrow I'm going to put a piece of the sky back where it belongs. And then, if we somehow don't die—" I grinned, and it felt strange and new and entirely mine, "—somebody owes me a honey cake. I'm told it's traditional."

Above the ruin, in a sky that had forgotten how, the first thin crack of blue was opening — and inside it, faint as a pinprick, something that might have been a star, coming back.
