## 18. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The thirteenth moon came up the color of a bruise that hadn't decided whether to hurt yet.

I stood on the broken steps of the first citadel — the one the histories called the Cradle of Wards, though the histories had been wrong about most things lately — and watched it rise over the ruined seam of the world like an eye opening. Below us, out of the dark, the Regent's forces were coming. Not marching. *Coming*, the way water comes through a cracked hull: patient, inevitable, and utterly uninterested in your opinion about it.

"They're not hurrying," Ash said beside me. He had the wardshard cupped in his palm, and it was glowing faintly, the way it did when something hungry was near. Which, tonight, was everything.

"They don't need to hurry," Merric said from the doorway behind us. He was carrying an armload of lattice-rods and chalk-slates like a man moving house in a fire. "They know where we are. We know they know. Hurrying is for people who still believe in surprises."

"Cheerful," I said.

"I'm a disgraced wardwright about to attempt a forbidden reweaving on the corpse of the first citadel while a memory-eating parasite wearing my king's face sends its entire household to eat me. Cheerfulness would be dishonest."

I liked Merric best when he stopped pretending to be brave. It happened rarely, and I'd learned to treasure it the way you treasure a coin you're about to spend.

* * *

We had maybe two hours before the moon completed its turn. The seam itself lay beneath the citadel's shattered heart, down a stair that had been built by people who believed architecture should be a form of prayer — every step carved with a word I couldn't read anymore, because the words were made of memories, and memories were currency now, and I was nearly broke.

That's the thing nobody tells you about spending yourself piece by piece. You don't notice what's gone. You notice the *holes*. The shape of the absence. I knew there had been someone who taught me the sword — someone whose hands I could still feel correcting my grip, whose voice lived in the blade along with all the others — but when I reached for his face, I found smooth nothing, like a portrait someone had painted over. I'd paid him to the road at the pass. His face bought us three days of running. I hoped he'd have found that funny. I couldn't remember if he laughed easily.

His name was Dain Corvath. That much survived. Names are stubborn things; they hold on after everything else lets go.

"He's here," Ash said quietly.

I didn't ask how he knew. The shard in his palm had been flickering for an hour, showing him scraps — torchlight, armor, a sword held in a stance I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting. Ash had seen my teacher through other people's memories before I'd ever described him. He knew Dain's silhouette better than I did now.

"The champion," I said.

"Yeah." He hesitated. "Zeph — the thing wearing him. It doesn't move like the others. The hollow ones shuffle. This one—"

"This one remembers how to fight," I finished. "It kept the skills. Skills aren't memories, exactly. They're deeper. The Regent can't digest them, so it wears them."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Merric appeared between us, pressing a slate into my hands. "The lattice needs three hours. The moon gives us two. So I need you to do the thing you're worst at, Zephyrine."

"Which is?"

"Buy time without dying. Or worse, without *spending*. Every memory you feed that blade tonight is one less voice in the weave tomorrow. The seam wants them full. All of them. Do you understand me? Whatever happens up here—" he gripped my shoulder, hard, his wardwright's calluses catching on my cloak, "—you fight empty-handed as long as you can stand it."

"And if I can't stand it?"

"Then we lose everything anyway, just more slowly." He almost smiled. "Welcome to wardcraft. It's mostly math with feelings."

* * *

They came up the old processional road, and I will give the Hollow Regent this much: it understood theater.

A thousand hollow-eyed shapes climbed the switchbacks in perfect silence, torches burning without smoke, and at their head walked a single figure in the grey half-armor of the Thornfell border guard — the uniform Dain had worn for thirty years, back when the border still existed and so did the man guarding it.

He stopped fifty paces from the steps. The horde stopped with him, like a tide obeying a stone.

"Zephyrine Iversen," he said. His voice was Dain's voice. That was the cruelest part — the Regent hadn't bothered changing it, because why would it? It had eaten everything else. The voice was just the bowl the meal had come in. "Surrender the blade. Surrender the king. The Regent offers you a place at its side. Your ancestors' memories will be returned to you. Every oath. Every voice. You will never be empty again."

"You're offering me a feast," I said, "at a table where I'm also the dinner."

"The Regent does not distinguish. Neither should you."

Gods, but it even argued like him. Dain had taught me that the best lie was the one you delivered like a truth you were tired of repeating.

"Dain Corvath," I said, stepping forward, drawing no blade, "was the finest swordspeaker the border ever produced, and he told me once — I remember *telling myself* he told me once, which is what I have left — that a duelist's first duty is to know which parts of her opponent are still alive." I looked at the thing wearing his body, at the eyes like wells with the bottom kicked out. "So tell me. Is anything left in there? Anything at all?"

For a moment — a heartbeat, no more — the champion's head tilted. Something moved behind those empty eyes, something small and drowning, like a candle flame under water.

Then it was gone, and the thing smiled with Dain's mouth, and drew Dain's sword, and I stopped being able to breathe quite properly.

"Come, then, little oath," it said. "The Regent hungers. And you have been *such* a generous meal already."

* * *

Here is what I knew about fighting Dain Corvath, or the shape of him: I could not win.

Not because I lacked skill. Because he had built my skill. Every trick I owned, he'd given me, and then he'd spent thirty years learning how to beat people who used them. Fighting your teacher is like arguing with the person who taught you language — they know what you're going to say before you finish deciding to say it.

So I did the second-worst thing I'm good at. I fought dirty.

I came off the steps at an angle no honorable duelist would use, using the ruined masonry as ground he'd never trained me on, because Dain had trained me on grass and flagstone and the frozen lake at Veymar, and I'd paid the lake away too, somewhere back on the remembering roads, and gods, the debts were piling up faster than the miles.

He met me anyway. Of course he did. The Regent wore his reflexes like a glove fits a hand.

Steel rang. We broke apart. Rang again. Around us the horde stood in its patient circle, watching, and above us the thirteenth moon crawled toward the top of the sky like it was being paid by the hour.

"You favor the left," the champion said conversationally, parrying a cut I'd been rather proud of. "You always favored the left. I told you to correct it. Lesson forty-one."

"Lesson forty-one," I said, breathing hard, "was about footwork."

"Was it?" The thing paused, and for one strange instant its brow furrowed — Dain's frown, the one that meant a student had caught him in an error and he was deciding whether to be annoyed or delighted. "I... don't recall."

And there it was. The crack in the vessel. The Regent had eaten Dain's memories whole, but it had skimmed the surface — the lessons, the techniques, the catalog of my flaws — without keeping the *man*. It knew lesson forty-one existed. It didn't know what was in it. It was a thief wearing a coat full of labels and nothing else.

"You don't," I agreed, circling. "Because you're not him. You're a table of contents with a sword."

"I am what remains useful," it said, but the smile had gone slightly wrong at the edges, like a mask slipping in rain.

"Then let me show you what's useful." I feinted left — lesson forty-one, apparently, whoever taught it — and drove my shoulder into his breastplate instead, slamming him back against a fallen pillar. "Dain Corvath taught me that a swordspeaker's blade holds voices. Ancestors. Oaths. He told me—" I pressed in, steel grinding steel, our faces a hand-span apart, close enough to see the emptiness eating his eyes from inside, "—he told me that when he died, he wanted to go *into the blade*. Into mine. He asked me for it. Did the Regent keep that? Did it keep the promise he made me on the last night of the old world?"

Something flickered. Deep down. A candle flame under water, thrashing.

"*Liar,*" the champion whispered — and the voice that said it wasn't the Regent's flat courtesy. It cracked. It *broke*. "You forgot my face."

The world went very quiet and very cold.

"You forgot my face," it said again, and Dain's sword arm dropped an inch, and behind the emptiness something was clawing at the walls of its own prison, screaming in a voice only I could hear, because I'd heard it every day for ten years. "You paid me to a *road*, girl. I felt it leave you. I've been walking around inside this thing feeling you forget me one mile at a time, and I want you to know—" the blade came up again, trembling, torn between two masters, "—I want you to know I don't blame you. I blame *it*. Now hit me before it takes the wheel back."

I hit him. What else does grief weaponized look like?

* * *

We fought across the citadel's heart while the moon climbed, and I will not pretend it was clean or noble or anything but the ugliest work of my life. He was stronger — the Regent fed its vessels something like strength — and I was cleverer, and neither of those facts was enough, and both of them nearly killed us.

Twice I got him down and couldn't finish it. Twice he got me down and, for reasons the Regent would be chewing on for centuries, didn't either.

"You're fighting it," I gasped, third fall, my back against the stair-mouth, his point hovering over my throat. "Inside. You're holding it off."

"Barely." Sweat ran down Dain's face — real sweat, real effort, the body taxed by a war happening in its own skull. "It's big, Zeph. Bigger than the crown. It's every king who ever traded remembering for ruling, all of them folded together, and it's *hungry*, and I am one tired old man standing in a doorway." His eyes — the human ones, surfacing — found mine. "The seam. Down there. That's really it? That's the way?"

"That's the way."

"Then stop wasting time on me." He raised his voice, and it doubled for a moment, two speakers in one throat. "I can't hold this door much longer! When it opens again, put me down, and don't you dare waste a single memory doing it! Go give that poor bastard king his name back!"

"Dain—"

"*Go.*" And then the door slammed shut behind his eyes, and the Regent took the wheel, and the sword came down.

I rolled. It bit stone where my neck had been. And I finally, finally drew the blade — oath-blade, ancestor-vessel, the last living memory in Thornfell outside a buried king's skull — and its voices rose around me like a choir waking, and I thought: *Merric said don't spend. Merric said the weave needs them full.*

But Merric wasn't here. Merric was underground with his math and his feelings, and the moon was one hand's width from the top of the sky, and the only person standing between the Regent's horde and the seam of the world was a woman who had already sold her teacher's face and was about to sell everything else.

"Sorry, Merric," I murmured. "Mostly math. Some feelings."

The blade sang. I poured one voice into the edge — just one, a great-grandmother whose name I'd never known, whose oaths I carried like heirloom silver — and swung.

The wave of released memory went through the champion like dawn through fog. He staggered. The horde behind him recoiled as one body, hollow faces turning toward a sound only they could hear — the sound of a dead woman's courage, weaponized. Half of them simply fell over, emptied of whatever animating hunger had held them upright, like puppets whose strings had been cut by a song.

The champion stayed on his feet. Barely. On his knees, actually, sword planted in the rubble, head bowed.

"Zephyrine." Dain's voice. Only Dain's voice, ragged and human and fading. "Listen. It's pulling back to regroup — it doesn't like that song, it'll bring something worse next. You have minutes. Maybe less." He lifted his head, and I saw with a lurch that his eyes were clearing — the emptiness receding like a tide going out, leaving the man visible on the mudflats, exhausted and dying and *himself*. "One thing. Before it comes back. One thing I need you to know, since you lost it."

"Don't," I said. My throat was wrecked. "Don't spend your last minutes on—"

"My face," he said, and smiled, and it was the smile from the frozen lake at Veymar, the one I'd paid away, the one I'd never see again except now, right now, in the light of a bruise-colored moon. "It's ugly. Big nose. My mother said I looked like a shovel. There. Now you have it back, and it cost you nothing, and it cost me—" he coughed, wetly, "—nothing at all. Best trade either of us ever made."

Below us, the stair-mouth breathed cold air up from the seam of the world. Above us, the thirteenth moon touched the crown of the sky.

And all around the ruined citadel, out of the dark beyond the fallen horde, something vast began to arrive — not an army this time, not a champion, but a *presence*, a pressure, a hunger with a horizon, wearing at the edge of every torch flame. The Regent itself. Coming to see what was taking so long with dinner.

Dain got to his feet. Shovel-faced. Grinning. Raised his sword one last time, not against me but beside me, facing the dark.

"Well, little oath," he said. "Shall we make some noise?"
