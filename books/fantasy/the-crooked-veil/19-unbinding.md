## 19. Unbinding

**Zephyrine**

The seam was not what I expected.

I had imagined a wound in the world — a gash, a howling rent, something you could point at and say *there, that's where everything went wrong*. Instead it was a hairline crack in the air beneath the ruined citadel, thin as a scar on old glass, running crooked through the dark like a vein of lightning that had forgotten to strike. It didn't roar. It *hummed*. A low, patient note, the sound of a door left ajar for three hundred years.

"Of course," I said. "Of course it's small. Everything important in this country is smaller than it should be and twice as expensive."

Merric didn't laugh. He was kneeling at the edge of the cracked flagstones with his wardwright's kit spread around him — chalk, silver wire, a bowl of ash he'd carried four hundred miles for reasons he'd refused to explain until tonight. His hands were steady. That frightened me more than anything else, because Merric Vale's hands were never steady when things were fine.

"The lattice is still there," he murmured. "Underneath all of it. The Regent ate the wards, but it couldn't digest the original weave. Royal memory doesn't rot. It just... waits." He looked up at Aldric. "Your ancestors are still holding the door, Majesty. They've been holding it this whole time."

Aldric stood very still in the ruined nave, moonlight from thirteen moons pooling around him like spilled milk. Ten days ago he hadn't known his own name. Five days ago he'd wept over his daughter's laugh like a man being handed back his own heart in pieces. Tonight he looked like a king again — or like something better than a king, which was a man who knew exactly what a crown cost because he'd paid it twice.

"Then let's not keep them waiting any longer," he said.

Above us, the thirteenth moon began its final turn.

* * *

**Merric**

There is a moment in every wardwright's life — if he's honest, and I have been many things but rarely that — when he understands that craft is only grief with better posture. You build walls because something was taken. You weave protections because someone was lost. Every knot in every lattice I ever tied was a small apology to a world that kept breaking.

Tonight I tied my last one.

The chalk came first. Old script, older than the crown, older than the lie wearing it. I drew the circle wide enough to hold all four of us and then some, because I had learned — late, expensively — that you do not skimp on room for the people you love. The ash went into the grooves. The silver wire followed, singing faintly as it remembered its shape.

Ash crouched beside me, the shard fused to his palm glowing like a coal. "Tell me again what happens to it," he said. "After."

"After? It goes home. Back into the weave. Where it always belonged." I hesitated. "So do you, if you want. There's a place in the lattice for—"

"No." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not a piece of a wall, Merric. I'm a boy who picked his own name yesterday. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," I agreed. "They're not." And I thought: *there it is. The whole lesson, delivered by a child with no past. Everything I built walls to protect, and the protection itself was the thing worth protecting against.*

The hum of the seam deepened as I worked. Beneath it, faintly, I could hear voices — the royal dead, murmuring in their long vigil. My family had served them once. Then I had served the thing that ate them, trading secrets for lives that no longer remembered they were mine. I had told myself desperation excused it. Standing here, chalk dust to my elbows, I understood that desperation explains nothing. It only describes.

"You're doing it wrong," said a voice behind me, warm and wet and wrong.

I did not turn around. Turning around was how they got you. "Zephyrine," I said quietly. "It's talking again."

"I know," she said. "Ignore it. It hates being ignored more than it hates us."

"It doesn't hate us at all," the voice said, from the mouth of a dead hunter slumped against a pillar, from the mouths of three more filing out of the dark, hollow-eyed and patient. "Hate requires wanting you gone. We want you *here*. All of you. Forever is just a meal that hasn't finished."

The Regent's voice had gotten better over the weeks. At first it had sounded like a man reciting warmth from a book. Now it almost believed itself.

"Merric Vale," it said, using my name like a knife it had found on the ground. "You fed me once. Small bites. Ward-secrets, little maps of the veil's weak places. Do you remember how it felt? Giving me what I asked?"

"Yes," I said, tying the next knot. "It felt like being paid in coins I'd stolen from my own children."

"And yet your children don't remember you at all. So really, who did you steal from?" A pause, savoring. "The lattice will hold without you, wardwright. Walk away. Live unremembered but alive. Isn't that the bargain you've been making your whole life?"

My hands did not shake. For the first time in thirty years, my hands did not shake.

"No," I said. "That was the bargain I made. This is the one I'm keeping instead."

* * *

**Aldric**

I remember my coronation now. Zephyrine gave it back to me eleven days ago, pouring it out of her blade like water from a jug — the weight of the crown, the cold of the anointing oil, my daughter in the front row picking her nose with royal impunity. I remember my wife's handwriting. I remember the smell of the orchards at Vaelmoor. I remember failing. Gods, I remember failing — every compromise, every memory traded for leverage, every small surrender that fed the thing wearing my face. That is the tax on being restored: you get back your sins along with your name, and the sins weigh more.

But I also remember why the veil was woven in the first place. My grandmother's grandmother stood at this seam with her hand pressed flat against the dark and swore that no ruler after her would ever pay for power with forgetting. And every ruler after her broke that oath a little, and the broken pieces pooled beneath the throne like blood under a floorboard, and after ten generations the pool stood up, put on my crown, and smiled at my court.

I am not innocent. But innocence was never the requirement. An *unbound* hand was the requirement — a hand that belongs to the man wearing it, freely given, freely placed. Not a hand bought by hunger. Not a hand worn like a coat.

Mine. Scarred, ordinary, shaking slightly despite myself.

The hollow-eyed ones ringed the ruin now, dozens of them, standing in the moonlight like furniture arranged by something that had only ever seen furniture described. The Regent spoke through all of them at once, a chorus with one throat.

"King of nothing," it said. "You were so easy. You gave me memories like a man paying ransom — gladly, quickly, hoping I'd stop taking. Did you think I wouldn't notice the relief in you? Part of you *wanted* to be empty. Emptiness is rest. I am offering everyone here the same rest. Ask the swordspeaker. Ask her what survival has cost her. Ask her what she has left."

"Don't," Zephyrine said. Not to the Regent. To me. Her knuckles were white on the hilt of her blade, and the blade was singing — all those ancestral voices at once, a choir crammed into steel. "Don't ask me. Ask yourself whether you'd take the crown back, knowing everything. Because that's the only question that matters now."

I looked at my hand. Unbound. Mine.

"Yes," I said. "Knowing everything."

I stepped forward and laid my palm flat against the crooked seam.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The veil opened under his hand like an eye.

Not light — *recognition*. Three hundred years of held breath exhaling through a crack the width of a hair, and inside it, the weave: the original lattice, vast and dim and starving, threads of royal memory stretched across the gap between worlds like a bridge built by people who trusted their descendants and were wrong.

"Now," Aldric said, and his voice came out steady even though tears were cutting lines down his face. "Merric. The girl with the sword. Quickly, before I lose my nerve — I remember losing my nerve, it's one of my best-remembered skills."

Merric moved. I'll give him that; the man had spent a decade being a coward and it had taught him exactly how cowards move, which is to say he now moved like no coward alive. Silver wire sang. Chalk flared. The lattice woke thread by thread beneath his fingers, and each thread reached toward the seam and stopped, waiting, hungry for the one thing missing.

Me.

The blade knew before I did. It had always known. It grew hot in my grip, and the voices rose — my mother teaching me the first forms, my grandfather swearing his road-oath under a sky full of stars, a woman named Iversen three centuries dead pressing her lips to cold steel and promising to remember, to carry, to keep. Every oath ever sworn into this metal. Every memory entrusted to it by people who believed remembering was a sacred act.

And it was. That was the joke. Remembering *was* sacred, and the thing beneath the capital had known it too, and that was why it ate.

"Zephyrine." Aldric's voice, gentle. "You don't have to empty it all. Some—"

"All of it." I heard my own voice and was surprised it didn't shake. "That's the trick, isn't it? The Regent takes. Taking leaves holes. It's been gorging on holes for ten years and calling it a kingdom." I lifted the blade. "This isn't taking. Nobody's taking anything. I'm *giving*. Willing memories, freely poured. That's the third condition. That's the whole spell."

The Regent understood one heartbeat too late. Its chorus faltered mid-sentence — the hollow-eyed ones all turning their heads at once, like birds, like puppets, like mouths around a single rising panic.

"*No,*" it said. "*No, that is MINE, that hunger is mine, you cannot feed what I have already claimed—*"

"Watch me," I said, and turned the blade point-down over the seam, and let go.

* * *

**Merric**

I have tried, since, to describe what it looked like. Language keeps failing me, which I suppose is fitting, since language was the least of what we spent that night.

The memories left the blade as light, but not clean light — *lived* light, light with weight and grain and grief in it. Generations of it, pouring down into the lattice, and the lattice drank and blazed and wove, and Zephyrine stood in the middle of it with her arms locked straight and her eyes open because she refused, absolutely refused, to give anything away blind.

I saw the cost land on her in waves. Each ancestor's gift passing through her took a piece of her passage with it — I watched her forget, in real time, the name of the pass where she'd learned to walk a cliff face. Watched her mouth shape a word and find nothing there. She didn't scream. She laughed once, short and terrible, and said, "Good riddance," to something none of us would ever know she'd lost.

The Regent came apart the way a lie comes apart. Not all at once — in *layers*. First the court voice failed, and the chorus of hollow mouths went silent mid-bargain. Then the beautiful false radiance guttered out, and what stood in the moonlight above the ruin was the true shape of it: a mass of every surrendered memory ever traded for power, tangled and starving and suddenly, catastrophically *offered* everything it had ever wanted, freely given, no taking required.

It gorged. That was the horror and the mercy of it. It could not refuse. Hunger was all it was. It plunged into the flood of willing memories like a starved dog into a river, drinking down coronations and lullabies and deathbed forgivenesses, and each willing memory was different from the stolen kind — they *filled*, they didn't hollow — and the creature that had been built entirely of appetite discovered, all at once, the sensation of being full.

Full things do not persist. Appetite without lack is just... a person. And a person, even a monster made of ten generations of royal failure, must finally do the one thing the Regent had never done.

It ended. Quietly. Almost gratefully. The last of it sighed out through the seam like smoke finding a chimney, and the crooked line in the air sealed itself behind it with a sound like a book closing.

The stars came back.

All of them. At once. As if the sky had been holding its breath since the night the wards died and had finally, finally been allowed to exhale.

* * *

**Aldric**

Dawn found the four of us sitting in the ruins like beggars at a wedding.

The lattice hummed overhead, whole and bright, woven through with new-given memory. Somewhere in that weave my ancestors' voices had gone quiet at last — not silenced, *finished*, their long watch relieved. I put my hand against the sealed seam and felt nothing but cool air and, distantly, the ordinary morning.

"We did it," Ash said. He was staring at the sky, at the stars fading politely into daylight, at the sun coming up honest and gold over the mountains. "We actually—"

He stopped. We all turned.

Zephyrine was looking at her sword. The blade hung loose in her grip, plain steel now, silent, empty — and she was looking at it the way you look at a stranger wearing a friend's coat. Then she looked at her own hands. Then at the ruins. Then at us.

Her brow furrowed — that quick, sharp frown I had seen her wear at crossroads and ambushes, the look of a woman calculating a route.

"Which way," she said slowly, "is Cinderhollow?"

Merric made a sound like a man swallowing glass.

"South," Ash said gently. "Zephyrine. Your name is Zephyrine. You're a swordspeaker. You brought us here. Don't you—" He stopped himself, because she was looking at him with perfect courtesy and not one flicker of recognition, and some questions are crueler than silence.

She stood, sheathed the empty blade, and rolled her shoulders like a traveler at the start of a long road.

"Well," she said, to no one in particular, to the dawn, to whatever was left of herself. "Someone want to tell me who I'm supposed to be now?"
