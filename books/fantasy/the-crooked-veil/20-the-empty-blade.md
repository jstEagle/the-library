## 20. The Empty Blade

I woke to stars.

That was the first wrong thing, though I didn't know it yet. I woke the way you wake in an unfamiliar room — that cold half-second where the world has no edges and you have to rebuild it from scratch. Except for me the rebuilding never came. The world stayed edgeless. I lay on my back in the ruined citadel with frost in my hair and a sword beside me that weighed nothing at all, and I looked up through the broken dome at a sky absolutely crowded with stars, thousands of them, arrogant and bright as spilled salt, and I felt nothing about them whatsoever.

Somewhere nearby, someone was weeping quietly. It took me a long moment to understand it wasn't me.

"Zephyrine." Merric's voice, hoarse as gravel under a boot. His face swam into view above mine, grey-bearded, lined, streaked with soot and something darker. "Zephyrine. Can you hear me?"

"I can hear you," I said. My own voice sounded like a stranger's. "Who are you?"

His face did something complicated. Grief and relief fighting over the same ground, neither winning. "Merric Vale," he said carefully, the way you'd hand something fragile to a child. "I'm a friend. We came here together."

"We came here together," I repeated. The words had no furniture in them. No rooms behind them. Just words.

I tried to sit up and discovered my body worked fine — better than fine, actually, every muscle loose and rested as if I'd slept a year — but there was a hole where things should have been. Not pain. Holes don't hurt. That's the trick of them. You only notice what's missing by reaching for it and finding nothing, like running your tongue along a gap where a tooth used to be.

I reached for my name and found *Zephyrine* waiting, worn smooth like a river stone. Just that. No mother saying it across a kitchen. No smuggler barking it over a mountain pass. No mentor correcting my grip on a practice blade, saying *again, girl, again* — except even as I thought that, I knew there had been a mentor, because there was a shape where he should be. A mentor-shaped absence. I could see the outline of him against the light, the way you can see the outline of a burned thing on a wall.

I couldn't remember his face.

I sat up too fast and Merric steadied my shoulder, and I looked down at the blade lying beside me in the frost. My oath-blade. I knew that much — knew it the way you know your own hand — but when I picked it up it was the lightest thing I'd ever held. Swordspeakers' blades are heavy. They carry weight the way rivers carry silt: every oath ever sworn into them by every ancestor who held them, generations of remembered promises layered into the steel until the metal practically hums with borrowed voices.

Mine hummed with nothing. It rang faintly when my thumb brushed the flat, a clean empty note, like a bell with its clapper out.

"It's gone," I said. Stupidly, obviously.

"You gave it away," Merric said. "All of it. Every memory in the blade, and—" He stopped. Swallowed. "And most of yours besides. Into the weave. To unbind the Regent."

I looked around the broken dome then, properly, and saw the others.

Ash was sitting against a fallen pillar with his knees drawn up, and he was the one weeping — silently, steadily, tears cutting tracks through the grime on his face while his mouth stayed set and calm, like his body had decided crying was necessary and hadn't consulted him. The wardshard was still fused to his palm, but it had gone dark and dull as river glass. Dead, or sleeping. When he saw me looking, he wiped his face with his sleeve and gave me a smile that cost him visibly.

"You did it," he said. "We won."

"Did we?" I asked. And meant it as a question.

*

The king was standing at the far end of the chamber, facing the resealed seam.

I say *king*, and some part of me that no longer had any business knowing things knew it anyway — Aldric, true king of Thornfell, restored, unbound, whole. He stood very straight in front of the place where the veil met the world, where the crooked seam ran beneath the shattered first citadel, and the air around it shimmered now the way air shimmers over summer stone. Whole. Mended. Behind it, presumably, all the ordinary machinery of the universe ticking along as it should.

He turned when he heard us approach, and I got my second shock of the morning, because the man from the tomb — the hollowed ruin who had asked strangers *who was I?* in a voice like wind through an empty house — was gone. This man filled his body. His eyes were dark and present and terribly awake. Memory had been poured back into him drop by drop over the past weeks, each one a piece I'd cut out of myself, and now he stood there wearing all of it, and the difference between him and the hollowed thing we'd found was the difference between a lit window and a boarded one.

"Zephyrine," he said.

"That's me," I said. "So I'm told."

He flinched. I watched him flinch, and I understood — dimly, abstractly, the way you understand a battle you never fought — that he knew exactly what those pieces had cost. He was wearing them. Her coronation, someone said she'd given him. A daughter's laugh. Roads and oaths and names of people long dead, ancestors stacked like firewood in her blade, all of it spent to make this man remember how to be a man.

"You don't have to do that," he said quietly.

"Do what?"

"Make it a joke."

I considered that. Considered telling him that I didn't know what else to do with it, since the part of me that had carried grief like a trade skill appeared to be among the missing inventory. I felt the loss the way you feel a phantom limb — aware of the shape of everything I'd surrendered, unable to touch any of it. I knew I had loved people. I knew I had sworn things. I knew there had been a road through the Thornfell passes that glowed under my feet because the road itself remembered me, and that knowing it made my chest ache in a way I had no story for.

"I don't actually remember why it would be a joke," I said. "Or anything else. In case that matters."

"It matters," Aldric said.

*

We walked out of the broken dome together, the four of us, into the first honest dawn Thornfell had seen in a year.

It should have been triumphant. I want to be clear that I understand it *was* triumphant — the sky was stitched back up with stars going out one by one as the sun rose, the border wards humming again somewhere north of us like a tuning fork struck and held, the Hollow Regent gorged and unraveled into nothing at the seam, eaten by the very feast it had schemed a decade to obtain. Merric kept stopping to press his palm against stones and laugh, actually laugh, a wet disbelieving sound, because the old lattice answered him now instead of ignoring him. Ash walked close to me without touching, the way he'd done the entire journey, two broken people orbiting each other at a polite distance.

And I stood in the middle of all this resurrection feeling approximately the way a fencepost feels.

"The roads will remember you again," Ash offered at one point. We were picking our way down toward the valley. "The smuggler roads. Now that the veil is mended, they'll— they'll glow for you. Like before."

"Will they?" I said. "I don't remember them glowing."

He stopped walking.

"They glowed everywhere you stepped," he said. "The whole way here. You hated it. You said belonging somewhere means being findable there."

I turned that over. It sounded like a good line. It sounded like something I would say. But hearing it was like being told a story about a stranger who happened to share my name — interesting, faintly flattering, entirely secondhand.

"Then I suppose they'll glow for someone who appreciates it," I said, and kept walking, and heard him fall in behind me with a small wounded sound he probably thought I didn't catch.

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving at the cost of everything survival was for: the victory doesn't care. Victory arrives fat and satisfied regardless. The sun comes up whether or not you can remember why you bothered. Somewhere south of us the capital was already, I imagined, waking to a sky full of stars and a decade of small stolen memories returning to the courtiers like birds coming home to roost — the name of a childhood dog, the tune a mother hummed, the taste of a particular winter apple. All the little meals the Regent had taken and hoarded, released back to their owners when it came apart.

Everyone in Thornfell was getting their lives returned.

Everyone except the woman who'd paid the bill.

*

Aldric caught up to me at the treeline, where the glacier's meltwater ran bright and loud over stones. He dismissed his escort — and yes, there was already an escort; word traveled fast when a dead king walked out of a mountain — and stood beside me watching the water for a while.

"I've been trying to find the right way to say this," he said finally. "There isn't one, so I'll just say it. Come to court. Take whatever place you want. Ward-captain, sword-mistress, my own personal shadow if that suits you. Anything you name."

"Why?" I asked.

He blinked. "Because you saved the kingdom. Because you saved *me*. Because I owe you—"

"You owe me memories I can't feel the absence of," I said. "You're offering me a place I can't remember wanting. Do you see the problem? You could put me in gold armor on a marble dais and I'd stand there thinking, well, this is nice, I wonder whose idea this was. I wouldn't know why I'd want it. I wouldn't know why I'd earned it. I'd just be a woman in a costume standing in your palace, waiting for a feeling that isn't coming."

He was quiet a long time. The water talked to itself over the stones.

"When I was hollowed," he said at last, slowly, "in the tomb. There was one thing left. One fragment the Regent missed, buried so deep it survived. Do you know what it was?"

"No."

"Not my coronation. Not my daughter. Not my crown or my kingdom or my name." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of everything he'd almost lost. "It was the feeling of being cold on a hunting trip when I was nine, and my father giving me his cloak without a word. I didn't know who the man was. I didn't know where we were. I just had the *cloak*. Warmth, out of nowhere, from someone whose face I'd lost. And it was enough. Zephyrine — it was the only enough thing I had, and it was enough."

"That's a lovely story," I said. "I mean that sincerely. I remember nothing like it."

"I know."

"And the offer stands anyway?"

"The offer stands anyway."

I looked at my hands. They were steady. They were always steady; apparently that much of me had survived. Swordswoman's hands, scarred, competent, attached to a woman who owned perhaps forty memories total, most of them from the last three weeks. I inventoried myself the way you'd count coins after a robbery: Ash asleep in the wagon. Merric arguing with a wardstone. A boy who chose his own name. A cloak-shaped kindness from a stranger-king. The clean ringing note of an emptied blade.

It wasn't nothing.

But it wasn't a court, either. Courts were for people with histories to defend.

"I'll come to the capital," I said, and something in Aldric's shoulders dropped with relief. "For now. For winter, maybe. But I'm not taking a title, and I'm not taking armor, and I'm not standing on any dais. I'll take a room and a table and I'll see whether anything grows back. Memories. Wants. Whatever." I turned to look at him straight on. "And if nothing does — if this is all there is, forty memories and an empty sword — then I'll walk out of Thornfell in spring and go find out who I am when I'm not anyone's oath-bound anything."

He studied me for a long moment, this stranger who had bought his life with her own.

"That sounds less like surrender and more like a road," he said.

"I'm told I used to be good with roads."

*

Ash found me at dusk, at the edge of the camp the soldiers had pitched below the citadel. He had two bowls of stew and handed me one without asking, which I gathered — from the flicker of surprise on his own face — was a habit older than either of us could verify.

"You're really going to the capital," he said.

"For now."

"With the king. Who remembers everything. Including—" He hesitated, turning his bowl in his hands. "Including you. Everything you gave him. He gets to carry all of it, and you get to carry none."

"Yes."

"Doesn't that make you angry?"

I thought about it honestly. Searched myself for anger the way you'd search a drawer for a tool. There was a shape where it might live, a hollow with anger's outline, but the drawer came up empty — not numb, exactly. More like a room after the furniture's been sold. You can still walk around in it. The floorboards remember the weight, sort of.

"I think the woman who would have been angry about it," I said, "is one of the things I spent. She was expensive. Anger at the king, love of the road, my mentor's face, my mother's voice — all of it went into the weave, and the Regent choked on it, and the sky works again." I shrugged. "I keep waiting to feel robbed. Mostly I just feel... light. Bad light. The kind of light in a house when the thieves have been through."

Ash was quiet. Then he moved closer on the log, careful as a cat, until our shoulders touched. He was warm. I registered that the way I seemed to register most things now: factually, gratefully, without history.

"He told me what you gave him," Ash said. "Your memories. He said the last one — the one that finished filling him up, right before the Regent tore — was you at fourteen, swearing your first oath in the rain. You were crying and you swore anyway. He said it was the bravest thing anyone ever fed him."

"Huh," I said.

"Doesn't that—"

"Mean anything to me? No." I ate some stew. It was good. I was fairly confident it was good; I had a general sense that food could be good, even if I couldn't have named five other meals I'd eaten. "But it means something to *you*, clearly, and to him, and apparently to whoever wrote that story. So maybe that's what stories are for. Keeping the parts of us we spend."

He leaned his head against my shoulder. I let him. Somewhere above us the new stars were coming out, one by one, punctual as debts repaid, and I looked at them and felt the ghost of an ache — the outline of wonder where wonder used to live — and I decided, deliberately, the way you decide to take a step, that I would learn to fill these shapes with new things.

Not restore. Fill. There was a difference, and I suspected the difference was the rest of my life.

"Ash," I said.

"Yeah?"

"In the spring. If I leave — if nothing grows back and I walk — will you come?"

He didn't answer right away. I felt him smiling against my shoulder before he said it.

"Wherever you're going," he said, "it won't be anywhere either of us remembers. So yes. Obviously yes."

Below us, the campfires were lighting, and far off to the south, past the mountains, the lights of the capital were kindling too — the city where a restored king waited with an offer I couldn't remember wanting, and a court full of people getting their lives handed back, and a throne room where, I somehow knew without knowing how I knew, something was already waiting for the woman with the empty blade.

I finished my stew. I picked up my sword, and it rang that clean hollow note, and for the first time all day I almost smiled.

Forty memories, I thought. Well.

Time to start earning more.
