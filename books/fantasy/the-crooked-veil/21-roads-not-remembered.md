## 21. Roads Not Remembered

Here is the thing nobody tells you about getting your memory back and then refusing it: everyone treats you like a miracle with legs.

The healers of the capital came to my sickroom in shifts for three days after the unbinding, pressing cool hands to my forehead, asking me questions in the gentle voices people use on the dying and the very young. *Do you remember your mother? Do you remember the palace kitchens? Do you remember the king's daughter, the one who used to braid ribbons into the hems of her brother's cloaks when he wasn't looking?*

I remembered none of it. I remembered choosing not to remember it, which is a different thing entirely, and no healer in Thornfell has a salve for that.

*You could have had it all back,* they said, over and over, in voices that meant *why would you refuse a gift?* And I never found an answer good enough for them, because the true answer was this: the boy in those memories — the page, the Regent's first victim, the small frightened thing who watched a king get eaten from the inside — that boy was a stranger. He'd been hollowed out at seven years old and filled up with someone else's hunger. If I took him back, I wouldn't be recovering myself. I'd be moving into a dead man's house and calling it mine.

Ash was the name I chose in a wagon full of stolen turnips, hiding from a woman whose blade whispered secrets. Ash was the name that fit. You don't trade a coat that fits for one that merely belonged to somebody once.

So on the fourth morning, while the court was still arguing about what to do with me — the lost page returned! the living relic! put him in silk! give him a title! — I packed a bag that contained almost nothing, went down to the stables where Zephyrine was pretending to inspect horses she had no intention of buying, and said:

"So. Which way are we going?"

She didn't look surprised. That's the thing about Zephyrine now — most of her is gone, burned away feeding a king his own life back, but what remains of her is exactly the part that never needed memory in the first place. She still reads people like weather. She looked at me sidelong, and something in her face did a thing I want to call relief but she'd deny under torture.

"Out," she said. "Any direction that isn't here."

"Good answer."

"The only one I've got." She patted the nearest horse, which snorted at her with the frank disrespect of all horses everywhere. "They want to give me rooms. A pension. The king—" she stumbled slightly on the word, because Aldric was still mostly a stranger to her, a man wearing memories she'd paid for, "—the king says I've earned rest."

"Have you?"

"I don't know. I can't remember earning anything." She said it lightly, the way you say things that would shatter if you touched them properly. "That's the joke of it. They hand me a medal for sacrifices I can't recall making. Feels like being thanked by a stranger for a meal I never ate."

"And so you're leaving."

"And so I'm leaving." She finally turned to look at me fully. Her eyes were the same as they'd been in the wagon, that first night — sharp, tired, amused at the world's expense. Everything else about her had been sanded down, but not the eyes. "You could stay, you know. They'll find a place for you. You're practically royalty-adjacent. There are worse lives than being a curiosity with a pension."

"I had a life here once," I said. "It didn't take."

* * *

We walked out of the capital through the south gate on a morning so bright it felt like the sky was showing off. The stars were back — had been back since the dawn after the unbinding, filling the night like salt spilled across black cloth — and people kept stopping in the streets to stare upward, weeping, pointing, as though they expected the stars to apologize for their absence and were graciously accepting it.

Zephyrine walked beside me with her sword slung across her back, and I noticed she carried it differently now. Lighter. It was empty, or nearly — every ancestral voice poured out into the veil's new weave, every oath dissolved into the lattice that held the stars up. Merric had told us it might regrow, slowly, the way a forest grows back after fire, one sworn promise at a time. But for now it was just steel. Just metal and weight.

"It keeps humming," she said, catching me looking. "Quietly. Like a kettle thinking about boiling."

"What does it hum?"

"No idea. Something new, maybe." She shrugged. "First time in its long ugly life it's had room to make up its own mind."

The road south out of the capital was wide and well-tended and utterly forgettable, which suited us fine. We'd both had enough of roads that remembered. For eleven weeks last autumn, the smuggler paths of Thornfell had glowed beneath Zephyrine's feet like lit fuses, betraying her position to the Regent's hunters mile after mile, until she'd learned to walk on the edges, on the stones, anywhere her history couldn't find her. She still walked on the edges sometimes. I'd catch her doing it — drifting toward the verge of a perfectly good road, stepping on roots and rocks for no reason — and then she'd notice herself doing it and force herself back to the center, jaw tight, like a woman relearning how to breathe through her nose.

"You don't have to do that anymore," I told her once, somewhere past the second milestone.

"Do what."

"Walking like you're sorry for existing."

She gave me a look. "You're very mouthy for someone who lived in my wagon rent-free for two months."

"You liked having me there."

"I tolerated having you there. There's a difference, and it's called dignity." But her mouth did the thing — the almost-smile, the one that lived in the corner and never committed to the whole journey. The almost-smile survived the unbinding too. I think it might be the last original piece of her, along with the eyes. I intend to protect both.

* * *

Here is what we discovered in our first week on the road, walking south through country neither of us knew: being nobody is a kind of freedom that comes with a bill attached.

Nobody knew Zephyrine Iversen, because Zephyrine Iversen was mostly gone — her name survived, but the woman it referred to had been spent, coin by coin, to buy a king back from nothing. At a waystation inn outside Marrowford, a carter squinted at her and said, "Haven't I seen you before? Weren't you the one who guided the Corvane caravan through the passes, back before the dark?" And Zephyrine said, honestly, "I don't know. Was I?" And the carter laughed and bought her a drink anyway, because there's something disarming about a stranger who admits to being a stranger.

At the same inn, a woman asked me where I was from, and I said, truthfully, "Not sure yet," and she looked at me with such naked pity that I had to go stand outside for a while.

That's the bill. Freedom from your past means freedom from the answers, too. Every question — *where are you from, who are your people, what did you do before* — lands in empty air. We were two blank pages walking side by side, and the world kept trying to write on us, and half the time what it wrote was *poor things.*

But the other half of the time — the other half was ours.

Because here's what else we discovered: when you have no past, every new thing counts double. The first time Zephyrine saw the southern lakes — great flat sheets of silver lying between hills like dropped mirrors — she stood at the shore for an hour without speaking, and when she finally spoke she said, "I think I've seen mountains before. I'm nearly sure. But I don't think I've ever seen water lie down like that." And it was the first thing either of us had seen her claim as new, genuinely new, hers alone, unbought and unpaid-for. She looked like a woman being handed a gift she hadn't traded anything for, and didn't quite trust.

I drew it in the map book.

The map book was my idea, back in the capital. I'd bought it from a stationer with money Aldric pressed on us despite Zephyrine's protests — "consider it wages," he'd said, "you were both on my payroll, historically, in ways I'm told were significant" — and on the first page I wrote, in careful letters: *Roads Not Remembered.* Underneath: *Everything past this point, we learn together.*

Every day since, I've drawn what we crossed. The bridge at Marrowford with the chipped lion. The hill where the wind made the grass lie down in waves, all pointing east, like the land itself was gesturing us onward. The lake. The orchard where a farmer gave us apples for no reason except that we looked hungry and he'd had a good year. Each drawing is bad. My trees look like broccoli. Zephyrine teases me about it mercilessly — "that mountain appears to be melting, Ash; is the south known for its geology in crisis?" — but she looks at every page anyway, longer than bad drawings deserve, running her thumb along the ink like she's checking the lines will hold.

"It's not really a map," she said one evening, watching me sketch a crooked church spire by firelight. "Maps are for places that already exist. This is more like... evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

"That we went through. That it happened. That somebody saw it." She poked the fire. "Memory's a luxury. Ink's cheaper."

* * *

We argued, that second week, about whether to visit Cinderhollow.

Zephyrine wanted to. Or part of her did — the part that remembered, dimly, through other people's accounts rather than her own experience, that Cinderhollow was where it started, where the refugees couldn't remember their children's faces, where she'd pulled a disgraced wardwright out of a cell and set the whole mad journey in motion. She stood at the crossroads with the signpost pointing west — CINDERHOLLOW, 2 DAYS — for a long time.

"They might remember me," she said. "Merric. The innkeeper. People I helped." A pause. "People I might have helped. I'm working from secondhand sources on my own heroism, which feels obscene."

"And if they do?"

"Then they'll expect me to be someone. The woman who saved them. And I'll stand there nodding like a thief wearing another man's coat." She kicked a stone off the road, harder than the stone deserved. "Worse — what if they tell me things? What I said, what I promised, who I was to them. What then? I can't honor promises I can't remember making."

"We could go anyway," I said. "And let them tell you. Let them have their version of you back. You don't have to keep it. You could just... listen. Take notes. Put them in the book."

She looked at me for a long moment. "When did you get wise?"

"I'm not wise. I'm just the only person you know whose entire personality was assembled recently, from parts. I know how it works. You don't have to accept delivery of everything people offer you."

We went to Cinderhollow.

Merric Vale met us in the street outside the wardwright's hall he was rebuilding — actually rebuilding, sleeves rolled, mortar on his hands, a man putting his craft back together plank by plank the way we were all doing. He saw Zephyrine coming up the road and stopped, trowel in midair, and his face did several complicated things at once.

"You came back," he said.

"Did I promise to?"

"No." He set the trowel down carefully. "But I hoped. Listen — I know you don't remember any of it. The cell, the argument, the wagon, the glacier. Aldric wrote to me. Explained. As much as anyone can explain." He wiped his hands on his apron, and then, awkwardly, formally, held one out to shake. "Merric Vale. Wardwright. Apparently we were friends."

Zephyrine looked at the offered hand. Then she ignored it and hugged him instead, which startled him so badly he dropped the trowel anyway.

"There," she said into his shoulder. "Now we're friends again. New model. No warranty."

He laughed — wetly, I noticed — and over his shoulder he looked at me and mouthed something that might have been *thank you,* and I pretended not to see, because dignity.

We stayed three days. Merric told us stories — the cell, the escape, the road that glowed, the river ford where a boy with a shard in his palm had weaponized a dead soldier's grief and saved them all. He told them gently, watching Zephyrine's face the whole time, ready to stop if it hurt too much. It hurt some. She listened anyway, chin on her fists, like a child hearing about a brave stranger.

"That doesn't sound like me," she said at one point.

"It was exactly like you," Merric said. "You just can't audit the account anymore. The spending shows in the ledger even if you've forgotten the purchases."

On the last night, he brought out a wardstone shard — smooth, pale, humming faintly — and offered it to me. "For the road. In case."

I looked at it a long time. The last shard had shown me other people's memories, including a girl swearing her first oath in a rain-soaked yard, and I'd carried fragments of strangers inside me for months. Some of them I still carried. The soldier's grief from the ford had never fully left; it lived in my chest like a tenant who pays rent in sighs.

"No," I said. "Thank you. But no."

"You're sure?"

"I'm building a person here," I said, tapping my own chest. "Slowly. Out of new parts. I can't afford tenants."

Merric nodded like I'd said something profound, and maybe I had, and maybe I'd just said the obvious thing that everyone pretends isn't obvious. Either way, he wrapped the shard back in its cloth and gave Zephyrine a plain iron knife instead, which she accepted with much more enthusiasm.

* * *

South of Cinderhollow the country changed — softer, greener, roads narrowing to cart-tracks and then to footpaths and then, some days, to nothing at all except the suggestion of passage, a gap in the hedgerow, a stile worn smooth by generations of boots that weren't ours.

We got lost twice. Properly lost, the kind where you walk an hour in the wrong direction and have to admit it out loud. The first time, Zephyrine took it hard — stood in the middle of a nameless field with her hands on her hips and said, "This is humiliating. I used to guide caravans through mountain passes in fog. Passes that *ate* people. Now I can't find a village with a church spire visible from three miles off."

"You used to have roads that remembered you," I said. "And maps in your head, and a sword full of ancestors shouting directions. All that's gone. You're navigating with the same equipment I am, and I got lost in my own hometown, allegedly, at age six."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to make us equal. Comfort's extra."

She laughed — a real one, the whole-face kind, rare enough that I stopped and stared, and she threw a pinecone at me for staring, and I caught it, and we walked on.

The second time we got lost, we found something. A valley the maps didn't have — well, no maps had it, since our map was the only one and I hadn't drawn it yet — a fold of green hills with a stream running through and an abandoned shepherd's cottage with a good roof and a hearth that needed only minor persuasion. Wild onions by the stream. A view south that went on forever.

We stayed a night. Then two. On the third morning, Zephyrine stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, looking at the valley, and I could see her doing arithmetic she didn't want to do.

"It's too easy here," she said. "That's what worries me. Easy is how the Regent got everyone. Not with hunger — with comfort. With the version of your life that asks nothing of you." She shivered, though the morning was warm. "I keep waiting for the trap."

"Maybe it's not a trap. Maybe it's just a valley."

"Maybe." She didn't move from the doorway. "Ask me in a year whether I stayed."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a *maybe* with furniture," she said, and went back inside to fix the hearth.

* * *

We're in the southern hills now, weeks out from the capital, and the nights have turned cold enough for fires, and tonight the fire is good and the stars are outrageous — more of them than the old stories promised, as if the veil's reforging had let a few through that were always meant to be hidden. Zephyrine is sharpening the iron knife Merric gave her, slow rhythmic strokes, and the empty sword leans against her pack, humming its quiet kettle-thought, and I am drawing today's miles in the book: a ford, a fox, a hill with a crown of standing stones that Zephyrine swears watched us pass with what she chose to interpret as approval.

"Read me yesterday's," she says, without looking up. It's become a ritual. She never remembers to ask; she just holds out her hand at the right moment, and I've learned to have the book ready.

I read her the fox. I read her the ford, and the old woman at the ford who'd given us bread and called Zephyrine "soldier" and Zephyrine hadn't corrected her, because she couldn't remember whether she'd been one, and decided it didn't matter.

"Add the stones," she says. "Write: *the stones approved.* Future historians will thank us."

"Future historians will think we were mad."

"Future historians weren't there." She sets the knife down and looks at the fire, and her voice goes quieter, the way it does maybe once a week, when the almost-smile steps aside and lets something realer stand in its place. "Ash. Do you ever regret it? Refusing it back. The name, the life, the little page in his fancy coat."

I think about it honestly, because she'd know if I lied. "Sometimes. At night. When the soldier's grief gets loud." I turn a page. "But then I think — the boy who lost everything didn't get to choose any of it. Losing him was done *to* me. Being Ash is the first thing that was done *by* me." I shrug. "I'd rather own a small thing than inherit a big one."

She nods slowly, and pokes the fire, and sparks climb toward all those returned stars.

"Same," she says simply. "Mostly same."

And that's when we see it — or I see it first, and grab her arm, and she follows my finger south. Down the far slope of the valley, where the darkness should be empty, lights. Many lights. Moving. Organized. A column of torches winding along a road that, as far as I know, I haven't drawn yet — because as far as I know, there is no road there at all.

Zephyrine's hand finds the empty sword. It stops humming.

"Well," she says softly. "Either that valley grew a road overnight..."

"...or somebody's using one that was never on any map," I finish.

The torches are heading north. Toward us.
