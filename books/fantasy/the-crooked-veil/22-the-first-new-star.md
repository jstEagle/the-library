## 22. The First New Star

The pass had no name on any map I owned, which was easy, because I owned no maps. Ash had drawn this one himself, on the back of a bill of lading he'd lifted from a caravaneer who hadn't been watching his satchel closely enough. The mountains were rendered as a row of grumpy triangles. A river was a blue worm. Our position was marked with an X and, beneath it, in his careful boy's handwriting: *here, probably.*

"Probably is my favorite word," I told him.

"You say that every time."

"It stays true every time. That's what makes it a favorite."

We'd been walking for six weeks. Or seven. Time had gone soft around the edges since the citadel, since the seam, since I poured everything I was into a lattice of light and watched it hold. Six weeks of mornings where I woke and had to lie still for a moment, taking inventory. *Zephyrine.* Sometimes the name came slow, like a word in a language I'd studied but never spoken. *Zephyrine Iversen.* Sometimes only the first part arrived, and I had to wait for the rest like a late ferry.

The blade rode my hip all the same. Empty — truly empty now, no ancestors whispering in its steel, no oaths stacked in it like cordwood. Just a sword. And yet.

That morning, climbing the switchbacks with our breath smoking in the thin air, it had begun to hum. Not the old hum, not the chorus of the dead. Something smaller. One note. One voice.

Mine.

* * *

Here is what I remember, in the order I recovered it, which is to say the order strangers and documents and one very patient king insisted on telling me:

I was a swordspeaker. The last one. There had been others once — an order, a tradition, women and men who carried memory in steel — and they were all gone before the stars went out, though whether they went before or after the Regent began its long meal, nobody living could say. Including me.

I had a mentor. His name was Dain Corvath. I know this because Aldric told me, gently, over dinner in a palace that still felt like someone else's house. He told me that Dain had taught me the forms, that Dain had loved me like a daughter, that Dain had stood against me at the ruined citadel hollowed out and empty, and that I had fought him weeping. I listened to all of this the way you listen to news from a foreign country. It was sad. It was terrible. It happened to a woman with my name, and I felt for her, the way you feel for anyone in a story.

But his face — Dain's face — was a blank coin in my mind. Smooth on both sides. I had paid it at a tollgate somewhere on the remembering roads, and whatever hand took it gave me back nothing but the knowledge that something was missing.

Merric says that's the cruelest arithmetic of memory: you don't lose the thing itself, you lose the hole where it was, and holes are heavier than things.

He would know. He's spent ten years carrying his own.

* * *

The pass narrowed where two walls of granite leaned toward each other like conspirators. Old travelers' marks covered the stone at the narrowest point — initials, dates, crude arrows, a carving of a stag that some artist with too much time and too little talent had given the antlers of an octopus. Centuries of people saying *I was here* to nobody in particular.

Ash ran his fingers over them. "You're going to do it, aren't you."

"Do what."

"The thing you've been not-talking-about for three days. You get quiet when you're about to do something, and you've been quiet since the last ridge."

"I'm always quiet. Quiet is my defining feature. Ask anyone."

"I did. Merric laughed until he coughed."

I unslung the blade. The humming had grown louder as we climbed, a single warm thread of sound, and when I drew it the edge caught the afternoon light and threw it back gold. An empty sword should feel light. This one didn't. It felt full of exactly one thing, held carefully, the way you carry a cup of water across a moving deck.

"What are you going to carve?" Ash asked. "An oath?"

"No." I turned the blade in my hands. "No more oaths. Oaths are how I got here. Oaths are how everyone got here — sworn into shapes they couldn't take back, carrying loads other people packed for them." I thought of Aldric, whole again and hating every minute of the throne room, doing his penance in public sessions and private letters. I thought of Merric, who had traded secrets for his family and lost them anyway, and who now rebuilt wards for villages that remembered him only as a stranger with kind eyes and steady hands. "Just a name. Freshly chosen."

"You already have a name."

"Do I? Let's test it." I looked at him. "Say it."

"Zephyrine."

Something moved behind my ribs. Not recognition exactly. More like a door swinging on hinges that needed oil — it worked, but you heard it work. "Again."

"Zephyrine Iversen."

"Hm." I tapped the flat of the blade against my palm. "It fits the way borrowed boots fit. Close enough to walk in. But I've been thinking maybe I should try it on deliberately instead of just wearing whatever I woke up in."

Ash considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, which was either endearing or exhausting depending on how much walking we'd done. "What else would you be called?"

"I don't know yet. That's rather the point. First time in my life — or what I'm told was my life — that nobody's handed me a name with obligations sewn into the lining. Swordspeaker. Last oath-bearer. She Who Must Reforge the Veil." I snorted. "Half those titles weren't even mine. They were the sword's. I was just the arm it happened to be attached to."

"And now the sword's empty."

"Now the sword's empty." I raised it so the point rested against the granite wall, between the octopus-stag and somebody's weathered declaration of love for a woman named Bess. "And it's got one memory in it. Want to guess which?"

"That one," Ash said. "This one. Right now."

"Clever boy."

"I'm not clever. I just pay attention. There's a difference."

"There really isn't, but I'll let you have it."

* * *

I want to set down what happened next properly, because it's the only memory I own outright, purchased fair and square with everything else I had, and I find I care about keeping accurate records now. When your ledger has been wiped, you guard the new entries.

I carved my name into the stone.

Not quickly. Not with a swordspeaker's flourish. I carved it the way you'd write a letter to someone you might never meet — slowly, pressing hard, each stroke deliberate. And here is the strange part, the part I keep turning over like a river stone: I chose the letters myself. Stroke by stroke. Nobody taught me this version of my name. Whatever hand had trained me, whatever academy or master or long line of oath-bound ancestors shaped the way I move and fight and speak, none of that survived. So these letters were mine. Ugly, maybe. Slightly crooked, definitely. But crooked in a direction I picked.

*ZEPHYRINE*

No surname. No house sigil. No date, because I honestly wasn't sure of the date. Just the name, standing alone in the granite like a person standing alone in a doorway, deciding whether to step out.

The blade sang.

Not loudly. If Ash hadn't been watching my face he might have missed it entirely — a single clear tone, low and warm, the sound a struck bell makes after the strike, when the metal finally settles into being itself. I felt it travel up my arm and settle somewhere behind my sternum, and I understood, with a certainty that required no evidence, that the sword had taken the moment in. Filed it. Kept it.

One memory. This one. The first entry in a book I was writing myself.

"Well," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "That's done, then."

Ash stepped up beside me and put his hand flat on the stone, below the letters, where the granite still held a little of the vibration. The wardshard in his palm — fused there since the night the stars went out, dimmed almost to nothing since the Regent came apart — flickered once. Pale gold. Like a candle deciding whether to bother.

"It liked it," he said.

"Swords don't like things."

"This one does. I've been listening to it for six weeks. It hums when you're happy. It goes quiet when you dream about the mentor whose face you lost." He said it simply, without cruelty, which is Ash's particular gift — he can hand you the truth like it's a cup of tea. "It's been waiting for you to make something new. All that room in there, and nothing but grief rattling around."

"Grief isn't nothing."

"No," he agreed. "But it's not furniture, either. You can't live in it."

I looked at him. Twelve years old, maybe thirteen — he'd stopped aging visibly somewhere around the time he refused his own history back, as if identity had been the only thing holding him to a calendar. The king's lost page. The Regent's first victim. He could have walked back into that palace and been surrounded by people who'd known him, records of him, a whole life laid out like a table set for supper.

He'd chosen the road instead. Chosen me, which frankly remained the most questionable decision anyone had made in this entire affair, including the Regent's decision to eat the border wards of a country containing me.

"Why?" I'd asked him, back in the second week, on a night cold enough to crack stones. "Why stay with someone who can't even remember why she cared about you?"

And he'd shrugged, that infuriating boy-shrug, and said: "Because you fed me first. Before you knew anything about yourself, before you knew I mattered, you split your bread and gave me the bigger half. People show you who they are before they know it themselves. I'm just returning the courtesy."

I still didn't fully understand it. But I'd stopped trying to argue with it.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of the pass wall, under the shelf of rock where centuries of travelers had scratched their passing. Merric would catch up tomorrow — he'd stayed behind in the last village to reweave a failing boundary-stone, muttering about shoddy pre-collapse craftsmanship, secretly delighted to be needed. The three of us had fallen into a rhythm of splitting and rejoining, like a river braiding around islands. None of us said anymore that we were a family. Saying it would have made it smaller somehow.

I sat with the sword across my knees while Ash slept, wrapped in both blankets because he refused to admit he was cold and I refused to make an argument of it.

The blade hummed softly. My single memory, resonating in all that emptiness.

I thought about what I'd lost. I made myself do it, most nights — an inventory of the vanished, read aloud in my head like a merchant checking crates. My roads, every smuggler's path and mountain track I'd ever known, gone; I navigated now by asking locals and by Ash's drawings, which meant navigating by trust, which was its own strange education. My oaths, all of them, the architecture of duty that had held my spine straight for twenty years — dissolved, and good riddance, mostly, except on nights like this when I missed having instructions. My mentor's face. My mother's voice — I learned of her existence from a palace archivist, and learned her name, and could not summon so much as the sound of her. Whole years of my childhood, sealed off behind glass I couldn't break.

And underneath all of it, the strangest loss: the person who had carried those things. She'd been exhausted, that woman. I could tell from the shape of what remained — from the way relief kept ambushing me in ordinary moments, a hot spring welling up through snow. She had been tired the way stone is tired, from bearing weight so long it forgot it was weight. Everyone told me she'd been magnificent. I believed them. I also suspected she'd been magnificent the way a dam is magnificent, and that nobody had ever once asked the water how it felt about the arrangement.

I did not miss her, exactly. I missed *for* her, the way you grieve for a stranger whose story ends well.

But sitting there in the dark with an empty sword and a sleeping boy and a name carved fresh into ancient stone, I found the inventory didn't hurt the way I'd expected. Because at the bottom of the ledger, in the column where the totals go, there was this:

The stars were back.

I tipped my head against the rock and looked up at them — all of them, the whole spilled-salt scatter of them, the constellations whose names I'd apparently once known and now got to learn again like a child. Somewhere up there, past the mended veil, the sky went on forever, and it was full, and it was lit, and every star was a small fire someone had bothered to keep burning.

Some things, I thought, are worth surviving for after all.

Not the survival itself — I'd been good at survival, and it had cost me everything survival was supposed to protect. But the *for*. The bread split before you know why. A boy's crooked maps. A friend rebuilding wards village by village, asking nothing. A king learning to rule again with his failures pinned to his chest like medals. A name cut into stone by a hand that chose every letter.

Those things added up. I checked the sums twice.

* * *

Near dawn, Ash stirred and sat up, hair sticking out sideways, and squinted at the eastern sky where the first true star of morning — the stubborn one, the one that outlasts the dark — was still hanging above the pass.

"That's a good omen," he said.

"We don't believe in omens. We believe in breakfast."

"That's also a good omen. There's a village on the other side of this pass. Merric's map says so."

"Merric's map says *probably*."

"All the best things are probably." He was already rolling his blanket, already moving, because Ash treated every morning like a door worth opening. He paused at the edge of camp and looked back at me, at the sword on my knee, at the fresh-carved letters catching the first grey light. "Come on, Zephyrine. The road's waiting."

And the blade hummed — one note, warm and low and entirely mine — as I stood, and shouldered the empty scabbard full of one perfect memory, and followed him down into the morning, drawing the map as we went.
