## 1. The Date That Wasn't Hers

The waystation had been dead for thirty years, maybe forty, and Tamsin Thornbury liked it that way.

Dead buildings didn't lie to you. They didn't promise hot meals and warm beds and then hand you a bowl of gray barley and a straw tick full of fleas, the way the last three inns on the Salt Road had. A dead building just stood there with its roof half-caved and its hearth cold, asking nothing, offering nothing, and Tamsin had learned a long time ago that nothing offered freely was the only honest currency in Thornfell.

She kicked the door open with her boot heel because the handle had rotted off sometime before she was born, and she stood a moment on the threshold while the wind at her back tried to shove her inside like it was eager to be shut of her.

"Charming," she said, to no one. Talking to herself was another habit the road had given her, along with the scar across her knuckles and the reflexive count of exits — two here: the door, and the gap in the back wall where a cart had gone through the plaster sometime in her grandmother's day.

Outside, the pass was going dark. Not sunset-dark — wrong-dark. The kind of dark that came down out of the high country ahead of weather, or ahead of something worse than weather. She'd been watching the ridgeline all afternoon, watching the crows lift off the scree in long ragged sheets, one after another, like someone was walking up the mountain shaking every tree as they came. Crows don't spook for wolves. Wolves are old news to a crow.

She stepped inside and let the door swing shut behind her.

* * *

The hearth still drew, which surprised her. She built a small fire anyway — small, because smoke could be seen from the pass on a clear night, and this night was clear as glass — and while it caught she did what she always did in an abandoned place: she searched it. Not for treasure. Treasure in the free countries was mostly a rumor soldiers told each other to keep from thinking about home. She searched for information. Rats told you if a building was sound. Old bedding told you who'd slept there last and how recently. And books —

Books told you things people had died wanting kept secret.

There were three rooms and a cellar that smelled of turnips long since turned to soil. The main room gave her a rusted spit, a child's wooden horse with one leg, and a shelf over the hearth holding exactly one book, lying flat, dust-furred, spine toward her.

She almost left it. She was tired, her boots were wet through, and the last thing she wanted after nine days on the smuggler's roads was to read somebody's grandfather's sermons. But the spine had no title, and untitled books in abandoned houses were either ledgers or worse, and ledgers meant coin, and coin meant she could stop walking north and start riding.

She pulled it down and blew the dust off in a gray cloud that made her sneeze twice.

Not a ledger. Leather covers, cracked like dried river mud, sewn binding gone loose at the front so the first pages fanned out crooked. Handwritten throughout, in a cramped brown ink, by at least four different hands — she could see where the script changed, tightened, loosened, changed again, generations of some family or order passing the thing along like a bad debt.

The early pages were accounts. Grain tallies. Births. A list of names under a heading that read *Taken by the fever, the winter the bridge went out.* Ordinary. The kind of book that got written in by lamplight by people who never imagined anyone would care.

Then, a third of the way in, the handwriting changed to something older and more careful, and the accounts stopped, and the prophecies began.

Tamsin knew about prophecy the way she knew about sea serpents: everyone claimed to have seen one, nobody could produce the body. The free cities had doom-sayers on every corner, charging a copper to tell you your fortune and a silver to unsay it. She'd cut a fortune-teller's purse once in Vell's Landing when he told her she'd die loved and rich. It had felt like the honest thing to do.

But she turned the pages anyway, because the fire was small and the night was long, and reading cost nothing but light.

Most of it was the usual rot. Kings born under crooked stars. A red winter. A serpent beneath the capital. She skimmed, half-smiling, until her eye caught on a word she hadn't seen since she took her oath seven years ago, inked into a mercenary contract by a priest who never once spoke aloud:

*Vael.*

She stopped smiling.

Vael, the silent god. The god with no temples worth the name, no priests who spoke, no scripture except the oath itself, sworn in silence, sealed in silence. Half the free companies carried his mark somewhere on their bodies — hers was a thin line of pale scarring around her left wrist, like a shackle that had healed into the skin. Nobody knew what Vael wanted. Nobody claimed to. That was the whole point of him. He asked nothing, promised nothing, and in a world where every other god had a price list nailed to his door, that silence had bought Vael a hundred thousand mercenaries' loyalty without spending a word.

She read the passage. Read it again. Her mouth had gone dry, and she noticed it the way you notice a stair missing in the dark — too late, already falling.

*When the crown-spirit wakes in the usurper's house,* the old hand wrote, *the silent god will spend himself to cage it, and spend is the true word, for he will pour all that he is into the oaths of the sworn, and be no more. And in that generation shall come the Crooked Wolf, marked by Vael's shackle, walking the smuggler's roads, and she shall stand before the true king in his gilded cage, and —*

Tamsin looked at her left wrist.

The pale line caught the firelight.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the empty room, too loud, too young.

*— and whether she frees him or devours him, the book does not say, for the wolf chooses, and the choosing is not yet written.*

She set the book down on her knee very carefully, the way you set down a snake you're not sure is dead.

It was coincidence. Of course it was coincidence. There were thousands of sworn mercenaries in Thornfell; the mark was common as a soldier's tattoo; *crooked wolf* was the kind of image any half-drunk scribe might reach for. And yet her thumb moved on its own to the margin, where a later hand — newer ink, browner, sharper — had added a single line beneath the prophecy, dated in the old reckoning:

*The Wolf comes to the pass on the night the crows flee the mountain. She shelters where the road forgets. This I have seen.*

Tonight the crows had fled the mountain.

Tonight she was sheltering where the road forgot.

Tamsin sat very still and listened to the fire tick and the wind worry the loose shutter, and she felt something she hadn't felt since she was six years old and her mother's boat went down off the Teeth — that vertiginous sense of the floor of the world tilting, of all the solid ordinary rules she'd built her life on quietly sliding sideways.

She had never believed in gods. Gods were what kings hired to make their wars smell holy. She had never believed in fate. Fate was what cowards called the things they hadn't the spine to change. She was Tamsin Thornbury, orphan of the Salt Road, sworn sword of the Grey Meres company, and every mile of her life she had walked on her own two feet, and no book written by dead men in brown ink got to say otherwise.

But someone had written her down.

That was the part that wouldn't sit still in her chest. Not the god, not the wolf, not even the king in his cage — but the simple, obscene fact that somewhere, sometime, a person had sat with a pen and put *her* on a page. Her nameless, unimportant, mercenary life, described in advance, like she was a delivery expected at a warehouse. Like she'd never had a choice at all, only the illusion of one, walking obediently along roads some scribe had drawn for her a hundred years before she was born.

Her hand closed around the book hard enough to crease the cover.

*Frees him or devours him.* Even the prophecy couldn't decide what she was. Fine. Neither could the prophecy. She'd spent twenty-six years deciding what she was, and she wasn't going to surrender the job now, least of all to a dead man with good handwriting.

She fed another stick to the fire, deliberately, calmly, and settled back against the wall with the book open on her knees, because whatever else this was, it was information, and information was the one treasure she'd ever believed in. If someone had written her future, she intended to read every word of it — and then do her damnedest to prove the author a liar.

* * *

She woke to silence.

Not the friendly silence of the empty station — a held-breath silence, the silence of the mountains themselves listening, and Tamsin came up off the floor with her knife already in her hand and no memory of reaching for it.

The fire had burned to coals. Through the broken shutter, the sky over the pass was the deep black-before-dawn, starless where clouds had begun to stack against the peaks. And above the ridgeline, moving against the lesser darkness, passed a shadow.

She'd seen shadows thrown by clouds all her life. This was not that. Clouds moved with the wind, ragged and formless. This moved against the wind, slow and deliberate, and it had a shape — a ring, a band, a circlet of darkness wider than the whole pass, crowned with points, eight or nine of them, jagged against the stars, blotting them out one by one as it traveled.

A crown. A crown the size of a storm front, drifting south over Thornfell, unhurried, as though it had all the time in the world and every world after this one.

Tamsin's oath-mark burned. Not hurt — *burned*, a cold white heat crawling around her wrist like a bracelet of frost tightening, and beneath it, faint as a pulse heard through a wall, she felt something she had never felt in seven years of wearing the silent god's shackle:

Pressure. As if something enormous was leaning on the far side of her skin, trying to get out. Or in.

The shadow crossed the moon's path and the stars went out behind it, and in that blackness she heard — or didn't hear, felt, in her teeth, in the scar tissue of her wrist — a voice that was not a voice, vast and patient and amused:

*Found you, little wolf.*

Then it was past, sliding down behind the southern peaks, and the stars came back, and the ordinary night resumed as if nothing had happened, and Tamsin discovered she was on her feet with her back to the wall and the knife shaking in her grip.

Down in the pass, where the smuggler's road bent around the shoulder of the mountain, torchlight bloomed. One torch. Then five. Then a dozen, strung out in the disciplined spacing of soldiers on the move — the usurper's scouts, the vanguard of the army she'd been hearing rumors of for a month, come at last to the pass below her, close enough that she could hear the creak of harness on the night air.

They weren't looking for her. Not yet. They were just moving, the way a flood moves, indifferent to the mice in its path.

Tamsin sheathed her knife. She picked up the prophecy book, wrapped it in her oilcloth spare shirt, and shoved it into the bottom of her pack, because whatever it was — trap, truth, or dead man's joke — it was the only map she had to the shape of what was coming, and Tamsin Thornbury had never yet run from a fight she could see coming.

She killed the coals with her boot heel, checked her exits — door, gap in the back wall — and stood a moment in the dark, flexing her left hand, feeling the mark cool slowly against her skin.

"Frees him or devours him," she murmured to the empty room. "We'll see whose book is right."

Then she shouldered her pack and slipped out into the dark, north along the road that forgot itself, toward the capital, toward the caged king, toward whatever had written her name a century before she was born — and behind her, far below in the pass, the first torches of the usurper's army began to climb.
