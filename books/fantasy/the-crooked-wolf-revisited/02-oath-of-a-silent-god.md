## 2. Oath of a Silent God

The waystation had no name anymore. Whatever sign once hung above its door had been burned for warmth by some traveler before her, and the hearth held nothing but ash and the ghost of a fire. Tamsin sat with her back to the wall where she could see both the door and the broken window, the prophecy book open across her knees, and she did not touch it.

She had not touched it in an hour.

Outside, the wind came down off Thornfell carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet and smoke that shouldn't be there — woodsmoke, cookfire smoke, too many fires burning in a pass where only shepherds and smugglers traveled. The usurper's scouts. They were closer than she'd thought. She filed that away with all the other things she'd file away and survive, and looked back down at the page.

*And on the third night of the Frostmoon, when the crooked wolf comes down from the high roads, the choice shall stand: the wolf who frees the king, or the wolf who devours him.*

Her name was written beneath it. *Tamsin Thornbury.* Not a name like hers — *her* name, spelled right, which almost nobody bothered to do. And beneath her name, a date. Tonight.

"You're not real," she told the book quietly.

The book said nothing back. Books never did. That had always been her problem with them — they kept talking after you stopped listening, and they never answered when you asked what gave them the right.

* * *

She'd sworn the oath at fourteen, kneeling on frozen mud in a mercenary company's camp, blood from a cut palm soaking into the dirt while the company's oathmother stood over her with a face like weathered oak. Tamsin hadn't chosen Vael, exactly. You didn't choose a god the way you chose boots or a blade. The company served Vael, so you swore to Vael, and Vael — silent Vael, the god who had never spoken to anyone living — took your word and kept it, and in exchange you got something no other company could offer.

You got certainty.

Not guidance. Vael never guided. But every mercenary under Vael's silence carried the same quiet weight in the chest, like a stone worn smooth by a river, and it meant that when you gave your word, it *held*. Contracts sealed in Vael's name didn't break. Employers paid a premium for it. Other soldiers trusted it. Tamsin had built an entire life on the foundation of that stone — twenty years of contracts kept, promises honored, oaths unbroken even when breaking them would have been easier, kinder, smarter.

She had told herself it was discipline. She had told herself it was professionalism. She had told herself a great many things over twenty years, and none of them had ever included the possibility that the stone in her chest was something more than habit.

* * *

Finding Wren Calloway took three days and cost her most of the coin she'd been saving against a bad winter.

The archivist lived at the bottom of a cistern in Lowmarsh, in a room the city had forgotten to fill in when it paved the rest. He was a narrow man, ink-stained to the elbows, with spectacles mended twice with wire and the hunted look of someone who had been exiled long enough ago that he'd stopped expecting it to end. When Tamsin climbed down his ladder and put the prophecy book on his table, he went white as paper.

"Where did you get this?"

"A smuggler's cache under Ravenrock." She watched his hands. Hands told you more than faces; faces could lie, but hands always flinched first. "Your seal is stamped inside the cover, Archivist. The Royal Archive's mark, struck through. Someone wanted it forgotten badly enough to exile you over it."

"They didn't exile me over the book." Wren sank onto his stool like a man whose legs had given notice. "They exiled me because I read it. Reading is the crime, mercenary. It's always been the crime. The book itself has been in the archive for a hundred years, catalogued, dusted, ignored. Forbidden texts aren't dangerous because they exist. They're dangerous because somebody might *read* them."

"And when you read this one?"

He laughed, but there was no humor in it — just the dry rattle of a man who had spent too many years alone with books. "When I read this one, I found my own name in the margins. Written by an archivist dead two generations. A note: *Find the wolf. Warn her.*" He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, though they weren't dirty. "So I went looking for the wolf. And then Mavros's people went looking for me, and I ran, and now the wolf has found me instead, and I confess I'm not sure that's better."

"Talk," Tamsin said. "Start with who wrote it."

* * *

The author had been an oracle of Vael — the last one, Wren said, from before the Silence. A woman named Ilsette who had gone up to the shrine in Thornfell's deepest fold and come back down with a hundred pages of prophecy and a voice that never worked again.

"She wrote it in the old tongue, sealed it, and died within the year. The archive shelved it and forgot it. Prophecies are like that. Most of them are wrong, and the ones that aren't wrong are usually vague enough to be wrong in practice." Wren spread the book open between them, careful as a surgeon. "This one isn't vague. This one gives a date. Your date. And a name. Yours. And then—" he turned pages with fingers that trembled slightly, "—it does something I've never seen another prophecy do."

"Which is?"

"It offers a choice." He tapped the passage. *The wolf who frees the king, or the wolf who devours him.* "Every other prophecy in the archive is a single thread. Fate doesn't negotiate, that's the whole terror of it. But Ilsette wrote two endings, side by side, as if she genuinely didn't know which one would happen. As if the outcome depended on something she couldn't see." His eyes came up to meet Tamsin's. "As if it depended on *you*."

"I don't believe in fate."

"I know. It says so here." He turned to the flyleaf, and there, in a cramped hand much newer than the rest — Wren's own hand, she realized, written during whatever fevered weeks had followed his reading — were transcribed fragments. "Ilsette describes the wolf. *A soldier bound in silence, who keeps faith as others keep breath.* That's you, Thornbury. Every line of it. She saw you a century before you were born, and what she saw was your oath."

Tamsin stood up. It wasn't a decision; her body simply refused to stay seated.

"No."

"Read it again—"

"*No.*" The word came out harder than she intended, and the stone in her chest — the smooth, familiar, lifelong stone — shifted, just barely, like something rolling over in its sleep. She pressed her hand flat against her sternum without meaning to. "My oath is mine. I made it. I've kept it for twenty years because keeping it is who I am, not because some dead woman scribbled it into a book. Take your prophecy and—"

"To what do you swear?" Wren asked quietly.

She stopped.

"It's a simple question," he said. "Soldiers swear to companies, to paymasters, to kings. You've severed from three companies, buried two paymasters, and I'd wager you've never sworn allegiance to any crown. So when you knelt in the mud at fourteen and gave your word to Vael — a god who has never answered a single prayer in living memory — what exactly were you swearing *to*, Tamsin Thornbury? What did you get? What did Vael give you, in exchange for everything you are?"

"The stone," she said, before she could stop herself. "Certainty. My word holds."

"Yes." Wren's voice was very gentle now, and she hated how gentle, hated that it was the tone you used on the wounded. "And hasn't it ever struck you as strange? That a silent god — a god who gives nothing, says nothing, appears to do *nothing* — should have power enough to bind ten thousand mercenaries' oaths across four centuries? Gods spend themselves on miracles, Thornbury. Healing. Fire from heaven. Crops in drought. Vael spends nothing. Vael has been spending *nothing* since before the usurper's grandfather wore a crown. Where did all that power go?"

The room was cold. The cistern dripped somewhere in the dark below, each drop landing with a sound like a small, patient clock.

"I don't know," Tamsin said.

"Neither do I. That's the truth of it — I read a hundred forbidden texts looking for the answer and found nothing, which is why I'm alive and in exile instead of dead and decorated." He closed the book and rested his hand on the cover. "But I'll tell you what frightens me, mercenary. Ilsette went silent the day she finished writing. Vael went silent centuries before that. And the prophecy names a crowned shadow — a spirit wearing the true king like a coat — and says the wolf will either free him or devour him. Everything silent. Everything spent. Everything poured out into something else and never refilled." He looked up at her, and behind the cracked spectacles his eyes were wet. "I think the answer is in Thornfell. In Vael's shrine, where the god last spoke. And I think we need to go there, and I think you need to come with me, because if that stone in your chest is what I'm starting to suspect it is—"

"Then I'm not a person," Tamsin said. "I'm a vessel."

The words tasted like iron. Like blood from a fourteen-year-old palm soaked into frozen mud.

Wren didn't contradict her. That was the worst part. He just picked up the book, wrapped it in oilcloth with the practiced care of a man who had lost too many books to carelessness, and said, "There's one more thing. The prophecy's margins — Ilsette's later notes, the ones the archive never catalogued — mention a daughter. *The princess sees what the father cannot.* If Aldric has a living heir—"

"Sera Aldraine," Tamsin said. "Struck from the succession six years ago. Exiled to the free cities. They say she hunts the usurper's couriers along the Salt Road for sport."

Wren blinked. "How do you—"

"I keep contracts in the free cities. Everyone knows the stories." She slung her pack over her shoulder and felt the weight of it settle into the grooves her body had worn into it over twenty years, and thought about how many other grooves twenty years wears, and into what. Outside, the smoke-smell was stronger. Somewhere down-valley, a horn sounded — one long note, then two short ones. Usurper signals. She'd heard them her whole life and never thought about who taught her their meaning.

"We move tonight," she said. "Ahead of the scouts, through the high roads. And we find the princess before Mavros's riders do."

"Tamsin." Wren paused at the ladder, the oilcloth bundle clutched to his chest. "For what it's worth — Ilsette wrote two endings. Two. Fate doesn't offer choices. Whatever that stone in your chest is, whoever wrote you into that book — the ending is still yours to pick."

Tamsin looked at her open palm, at the thin white scar across it where a knife had drawn blood for an oath to a god who had never once, in twenty years, said a single word.

"Then let's hope," she said, "that whoever I am without the oath is someone worth picking."

She climbed the ladder into the smoke-colored dark, and far to the north, past the pass, past the burning free cities' horizon-glow, the shrine of Vael waited in Thornfell's deepest fold — silent, patient, and full of answers that would cost more than either of them knew.
