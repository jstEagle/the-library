## 3. The King in the Gilded Cage

Wren Calloway had spent thirty years among books and exactly four days among thieves, and he was beginning to understand why the archivists had exiled him rather than sent him anywhere useful.

"Stop fidgeting," Tamsin murmured beside him in the shadow of the tannery wall.

"I'm adjusting my collar."

"You're broadcasting our position to every guard on the street." She didn't look at him. She never looked at him when she spoke; her eyes were always moving, cataloguing rooftops, doorways, the rhythm of the patrol lamps along the Processional. "You wanted to come. You don't get to be bad at it."

He wanted to point out that wanting a thing and being competent at it were unrelated conditions — he'd written half a treatise on the distinction before his exile — but the words died as the bells of the Citadel began to ring the hour of Vespers, and the sound rolled down through the capital like a stone dropped into still water.

The capital had not been still for a long time.

Wren had left Ashvale Archive seven years ago, dragged out by six brothers of the order while he clutched a codex to his chest like an infant. He remembered the city then: loud, gilded, indifferent. Now it was quiet in the way a held breath is quiet. Banners of black and bone-white hung from every balcony — Mavros's colors, though the general had not yet taken the throne, only everything around it. The usurper's soldiers stood at each corner in pairs, faces hidden behind visages of hammered tin shaped like serene, eyeless masks. Wren found them worse than any face. A face could be read. These were pages torn out.

"The servant leaves by the postern at Vespers," Tamsin said. "Every night. Buys bread at the chandler's stall, walks the river path alone for two hundred paces. That's our window."

"And if she screams?"

Tamsin glanced at him then, briefly, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Then I'll have already covered her mouth. Try to keep up."

* * *

The servant's name was Ilsabet, and she did not scream.

She froze instead, back against the river wall, a loaf of dark bread clutched to her chest, as Tamsin stepped out of the reeds with a knife that seemed less drawn than simply *arrived*. Wren stumbled after, mud sucking at his boots, holding up both hands in what he hoped was a scholarly gesture of harmlessness.

"We're not going to hurt you," he said. "We need to know about the king."

Ilsabet's eyes went from the knife to Wren's ink-stained fingers to the archive sigil half-worn from his satchel strap. Something in her face collapsed — not into fear but into relief, which frightened Wren more than screaming would have.

"You're from outside," she whispered. "You've seen the sun. Real sun."

"It's been overcast," Wren said carefully.

"Not here." Her laugh was small and terrible. "Not since the coronation. Ask anyone. It's been grey for three years, and the grey *leans*, master archivist. Some nights it leans so close to the windows you can hear it breathing."

Tamsin lowered the knife an inch. "The king. Is he alive?"

"He's alive." Ilsabet said it the way one confirms a wound is not fatal. "That's the cruelty of it. He's alive, and some mornings — some mornings he's *there*. He knows me. He asks about his daughter. He weeps and apologizes and asks about his daughter, and then the crown—" She stopped. Pressed the bread harder against her sternum as if it were armor. "You should see for yourselves. No one believes the telling of it. They think us mad, or traitors, or both."

"How?" Tamsin asked. "How do we see?"

Ilsabet looked at them for a long moment. Then she tore the loaf in half and handed the pieces over like a password. "Laundry goes up to the royal apartments at Matins. Two carts. There's room under the linens if you're thin and you can hold your breath past the gate wardens. One condition." Her voice steadied, hardened; here was a woman who had made a decision long ago and been waiting for someone to hand it to. "If there's anything left of him in there — anything at all — you find a way to give it back to him. Or you end it. Either mercy. But no more watching."

* * *

They went up in the second cart, beneath sheets that smelled of lye and something else, something faintly sweet and wrong, like flowers arranged over a sickbed. Wren counted the turns of the stairwell by the echoes — eleven landings, each guarded, each warden wearing those serene blind masks — and pressed his face into the linen as light swept across the cart bed twice.

When the cart stopped and the canvas lifted, they were in a laundry yard high above the city, walled on three sides, open on the fourth to a view Wren would have wept over in any other circumstance: the whole of the capital spread below, the river a pewter ribbon, the free cities' road winding south toward hills that were, even now, burning somewhere beyond sight.

A single door led inward. It was not locked. That, more than anything, told Wren what kind of prison this was. No one fled this place. No one *could*.

Inside, the corridor was hung with mirrors.

Dozens of them, tall and narrow, framed in blackened silver, lining both walls of the passage to the royal apartments. Wren stopped dead. His breath fogged the nearest glass, and the reflection that looked back was his own — but delayed. A half-heartbeat delayed. He raised his hand; the man in the mirror raised his a moment later, like an echo learning obedience.

"Don't touch them," Tamsin breathed. She had gone very pale. "Don't look at them longer than you must."

"What are they?"

"Cage bars," she said quietly. "For something that lives in reflections."

She was a mercenary who worshipped a silent god and claimed to believe in nothing, and yet she knew things like this without being told. Wren filed it away, the way he filed everything, and followed her down the corridor of lagging selves.

The doors to the king's chambers stood open at the corridor's end.

* * *

King Aldric Vane sat at a writing desk positioned before a tall window, quill in hand, a blank page before him. He was not what Wren expected. Prophecy and rumor had painted a raving beast, a hollowed husk, a madman howling at ceilings. Instead there was a broad-shouldered man in his forties, grey coming in early at the temples, dressed in clean but unfashionable clothes — someone had dressed him like a private gentleman, deliberately, the way one dresses a portrait to hide the sitter. His hands were steady. His eyes were the problem. They moved like a man reading a page no one else could see, tracking something just above the horizon of the visible world.

And above his brow, resting on hair gone iron-grey, sat the crown.

It was a modest thing as crowns went — old gold, unadorned, the circlet of the first kings. It should have looked absurd indoors, worn at a desk, worn *always*, if the indentations in his hair meant what Wren thought they meant. Instead it looked like a mouth. Like something fastened over the upper half of his face to keep it from speaking.

"Aldric," Tamsin said softly.

The king did not turn. "It doesn't work," he said. His voice was hoarse, ordinary, heartbreakingly sane. "The letters. I write them and by morning the page is blank and my hand hurts and I don't remember what I was trying to say. But I know I was trying to say it. Do you understand? I know there was something I needed someone to know, and the knowing is all that's left. The words go. The knowing stays." He set down the quill with terrible care. "Whoever you are, please. Please tell me if my daughter is alive. Just nod or shake your head. It can't stop a nod. It's never once managed to stop a nod."

Tamsin nodded.

Aldric closed his eyes. His shoulders shook once, twice — a man wringing out grief the way one wrings a cloth, efficiently, because there was no time to do it properly. "Sera," he whispered. "My fox. She used to hide in the mews and make the falcons lie to the falconers." A wet, broken laugh. "Thank you. Whoever you are. Thank—"

He stopped.

Wren watched it happen and would spend the rest of his life failing to describe it accurately. The change came not from within but from *above* — as if a lamp were lowered over the king's head, as if a second face settled onto the first like a mask of water. Aldric's spine straightened. His hands flattened on the desk. When his eyes opened again they were the same color and entirely different, patient and vast and cold as the far side of a mirror.

The crown gleamed. Somewhere in its old gold, Wren thought he heard — felt rather than heard, a vibration in the teeth, in the breastbone — a slow, satisfied breathing.

"No," Aldric said, in a voice that was his voice played backward through a dream. "No visitors today."

"We're not visitors," Tamsin said, and her hand had found her knife without Wren seeing it move. "We're readers. We read what was written about you, a hundred years before you were born."

Something flickered behind the king's eyes. Beneath the mask of water, faint as a drowning man's palm against ice, the real Aldric pressed upward — and for three seconds, three impossible seconds, both faces were present at once, layered, the king looking out through the thing that wore him.

"*Run,*" Aldric said. Both mouths said it. "*While it's listening elsewhere — run — and if you find my daughter, tell her the shrine — tell her her grandmother's shrine —*"

The pressure in the room changed. The mirrors in the outer corridor sang, one clear high note, all together.

"—is *mine*," the crown-spirit finished, alone again, smooth as poured wax. It rose from the desk. It smiled with Aldric's mouth, and the smile fit poorly, like a child wearing a father's coat. "But you knew that already, didn't you, little reader? I felt your book the moment you crossed the gate. Old paper. Older words. You carry forbidden things the way other men carry plague."

Wren's mind, treacherous scholar's mind, was already cataloguing even as terror flooded him: *it speaks through him but perceives independently; it fears documentation; it said 'my shrine,' which means the shrine threatens it; it let Aldric speak because it was listening elsewhere — where, where else is there to listen—*

"Tamsin," he whispered. "The corridor. Now."

The crown-spirit tilted the king's head, birdlike, studying Tamsin with those vast cold eyes. And then, softly, almost tenderly, it said:

"Wolf."

Tamsin went white to the lips.

* * *

They ran. Down the corridor of lying mirrors, their reflected selves lagging and stumbling and reaching after them with hands that were not quite their own; down eleven landings while wardens turned those serene blind masks in perfect unison, like a field of flowers tracking the sun; out through the laundry yard as the grey sky leaned close enough to touch, close enough, Wren swore, to breathe against the back of his neck.

They reached the river as the bells began to ring alarm — not the measured hour-chime but a jagged, arrhythmic peal, the sound of a city being told to hunt.

At the postern gate, Ilsabet waited with a lantern and a bundle of bread, and pressed both into Tamsin's hands without a word.

"They'll question me," she said calmly. "I'll tell them a merchant's clerk and his wife. They'll believe me. I'm nobody; nobody is invisible." She looked up at the Citadel, at the lit window of the royal apartments, and her jaw tightened. "Did you see him? Was he there?"

"For a moment," Wren said. "Long enough. He has a message for his daughter. About a shrine."

Ilsabet exhaled shakily. "Then it wasn't for nothing. Go. *Go.*"

They went, down the river path, into the tanner's warrens, into the dark. Only when they were three streets deep did Wren dare speak, low and shaking.

"It recognized you. Tamsin — the spirit looked at you and said *wolf*. The prophecy names you, but prophecy is paper. That thing *knew* you. Which means it isn't reading fate." He swallowed. "It's reading *you*."

Ahead, at the mouth of the alley, torchlight bloomed — a dozen torches, and before them a figure in black and bone-white, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back, waiting as though he had known precisely where they would emerge.

General Mavros smiled.

"I believe," he said pleasantly, "you have something of mine."
