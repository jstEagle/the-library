## 4. Daughter of Two Crowns

Sera had been watching the road for three days before they came.

She'd chosen the spot herself: a bend where the old pilgrim track narrowed between two walls of granite, close enough that a thrown knife could cross it and low enough that the pines above gave her shadow to vanish into. Her father's maps — her grandfather's maps, really, annotated in a dead woman's hand — said this was where travelers bound for Thornfell's interior would funnel, whether they liked it or not. The mountains did not offer choices here. They offered a throat.

And into that throat, at last, came two figures.

The first was small and gray and moved like an apology, hunched under a satchel that seemed to contain every book ever banned in the capital. Wren Calloway. Sera knew the archivist's face from a wanted placard she'd burned rather than let it hang in a tavern where loyalists might study it. *Exiled for reading forbidden texts.* The usurper's men called him a heretic. Sera suspected he was something worse: a man who had actually read things.

The second figure made her breath go shallow.

Tall. Broad-shouldered. A sword worn low on the hip in the manner of someone who expected to use it before supper, and a mercenary's oath-knot braided into her dark hair — the silver thread of Vael woven through it, dull as old pewter. Tamsin Thornbury. The name from the prophecy. The name her father had wept once, lucid and drowning, before the crown-spirit dragged him back under: *the wolf comes, the wolf comes, do not let her in.*

Sera had spent six years learning not to flinch when her father screamed. She did not flinch now. She nocked her feelings away like arrows and waited until the pair reached the narrowest point of the pass.

Then she stepped out of the treeline with a blade at Wren Calloway's throat.

* * *

"Move and the archivist bleeds," she said.

To her mild surprise, neither of them panicked. The archivist went very still in the way of a man who had clearly been threatened before and had developed opinions about which threats deserved fear. And the mercenary — the Crooked Wolf herself — simply stopped walking, tilted her head, and looked at Sera the way one looks at weather.

"You're holding him wrong," Tamsin said. "Blade angled down, you'll catch his collarbone when he flinches. He will flinch. They always do."

"I won't flinch," Wren said, offended.

"He'll flinch."

Sera adjusted the angle without meaning to, then hated herself for taking instruction from a stranger with a knife at her companion's neck. "Drop your weapons. Both of you. Slowly."

The archivist set down his satchel with the tenderness of a father laying down an infant. The mercenary unbuckled her sword belt and let it fall, but her hands stayed loose at her sides, and her eyes never left Sera's face. Reading her. Cataloguing her. Mercenaries did that; Sera had grown up among soldiers who could price a person at a glance.

"You have my father's jaw," Tamsin said quietly. "And his way of standing like the ground owes him money."

The words landed harder than any blade could have. Sera kept her voice flat. "You've seen the king?"

"I've seen what wears him." Something flickered across the mercenary's face — pity, maybe, or its uglier cousin, recognition. "In the capital. Three nights ago. He was lucid for perhaps a minute. Long enough to beg a servant to take his head off with a meat cleaver."

Sera had imagined a hundred versions of this conversation on three cold nights of watching the road. In none of them had it hurt quite this much. "He asked that?"

"He asked politely. Apologized for the mess." Tamsin's voice didn't soften — it couldn't, Sera thought, it had been built too thick for softness — but she held Sera's gaze while she said it, which was more courtesy than most mercenaries managed. "I'm sorry. I'm telling you because you deserve the truth more than comfort."

"And who decided I deserve anything?" Sera stepped closer, letting the blade kiss skin, feeling Wren swallow against the steel. "You. A sellsword whose name shows up in a century-old prophecy as either my father's salvation or his devourer. You walk out of nowhere toward the deepest shrine in Thornfell, and I'm meant to trust you because you're sorry?"

"No," Tamsin said. "You're not. Trust is earned. So earn mine back — who are you?"

"Sera Aldricsdotter." She let the name fall like a stone down a well. Watched it land. Even the archivist's composure cracked; even he had heard what happened to the princess after the coronation, how she'd cut her way out of the palace with a kitchen girl's help and vanished into the hill country, and how the usurper's criers called her *dead* with such cheerful confidence.

"The crown-spirit can't be killed by steel," Sera continued, before they could find their voices. "My father has tried. His guardsmen tried. Half the college of sorcery tried, before Mavros fed them to their own experiments. But it has a weakness. Everything caged has a door, and everything poured has a vessel." She lowered the blade at last, though she didn't sheathe it. "Vael went silent at the shrine beneath Thornfell's roots. That silence wasn't death. It was a locking. And whatever key opens that lock, I intend to find it and turn it myself — with or without the wolf of the prophecy."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath a shelf of rock, a fire small enough to hide in cupped hands. Sera sat opposite the other two with her sword across her knees, because trust was a currency she had stopped minting years ago, and she told them so.

"Test us, then," Wren said. He had recovered his satchel and his dignity in that order, and was warming his hands over the coals. "That's what you're doing, isn't it? Every question is a little blade. You're deciding where we fit."

"I'm deciding whether you're worth dying beside. It's a short list. Currently it contains me."

"It should contain your father," Tamsin said.

"My father is a crown with a man trapped inside it." The words came out sharper than intended, edged with six years of grief she hadn't permitted herself. "I loved a man who used to carry me on his shoulders through the palace gardens and name all the roses after generals he'd defeated. What sits on the throne now wears his face and eats his mind by inches. If freeing him means burning the throne, the city, and half of Thornfell, I have already decided I'm willing. Have you?"

The fire popped. Tamsin looked into it for a long moment, and Sera watched the mercenary weigh the question honestly — not performing resolve, not reciting a creed, just weighing, like a woman counting coin she wasn't sure she had.

"I don't know yet," Tamsin said finally. "But I know I don't break contracts halfway. Someone wrote me into a prophecy. Until I understand why, I'm not putting that book down."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have. Take it or stab me."

Despite herself — despite everything — Sera felt the corner of her mouth twitch. It had been years since anyone had spoken to her without either terror or flattery, and there was something almost restful about being threatened by a woman who meant it. "Wren," she said. "Your assessment."

The archivist had gone pale in the firelight, and not from cold. He was staring at Tamsin's oath-knot, at the pewter thread, and his lips were moving as though reading something written in the air above it.

"Wren."

"Hm? Yes. Assessment." He blinked twice. "She should come with us. Also, Princess, I need to see that shrine immediately, and I need to see it before Mavros's riders do, and I need you to tell me everything you know about the rite of Vael's silence, because I believe the second half of the Thornbury prophecy isn't lost — I believe it's *hidden*, and I believe it's hidden somewhere only a sworn mercenary can read it."

"Why only her?"

"Because," Wren said gently, the way one delivers a diagnosis, "every oath sworn to Vael carries a fragment of the god. And if the god poured itself into those oaths to build the cage — then the wolf isn't the lock or the key, Princess. The wolf is part of the cage itself."

The fire crackled. Somewhere up the slope, a night bird went silent all at once, the way birds do when something large passes overhead.

Sera looked at Tamsin Thornbury — at the woman the prophecy named liberator or devourer, at the woman who admitted she didn't know which — and made the decision the way her father had taught her to make decisions: quickly, completely, and without looking back.

"All right," she said. "You're both on the list."

Tamsin raised an eyebrow. "The list of people worth dying beside?"

"The list of people I'd kill for. It's longer. Don't make me add you to the other one."

For the first time since the pass, the mercenary smiled — a crooked, unpracticed thing, like a door opening in a wall that had been solid for years. "Fair terms," she said. "Princess."

"Sera. If we're going to die together, you may as well use my name."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, and it was Tamsin who heard the horses first — many horses, moving fast along the valley road below, riding with the disciplined rhythm of Mavros's outriders sweeping the passes for fugitives.

"They've picked up our trail from the capital," Sera breathed, pulling Wren down behind the rocks.

"Can we outrun them?" the archivist whispered.

"Not on the roads." Tamsin rose to a crouch, eyes narrowing at the ridgelines, and Sera saw the look on her face — the look of a woman greeting terrain the way other people greet old friends. "But there are roads that aren't roads. Smuggler's ways through the high fells. My company ran them for years. They don't exist on any map, and half of them don't exist at all unless you know where to step." She glanced back at them, and the crooked smile returned, thinner now, sharpened by danger. "Stay close to me, Princess. And whatever you hear in the fog up there — don't answer it."

Above them, the first light touched the peaks, and far below, the riders turned onto the mountain road, coming for them.
