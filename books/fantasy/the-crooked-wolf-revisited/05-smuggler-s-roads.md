## 5. Smuggler's Roads

The road behind them died at moonrise.

Tamsin heard it happen — the distant crack of Mavros's riders forcing the pass below, torchlight blooming like a wound in the dark — and she did not look back. Looking back was a habit for people who still believed the world would give them time.

"Off the trail," she said, and turned her mare straight at the cliff face.

Wren pulled up short. "There's no road there."

"There is." She said it flatly, the way you said things you needed to be true. "There just isn't a road anyone's supposed to find."

* * *

Her father had shown her the first of them when she was nine: a seam in the rock above the Ashen Fork, no wider than a handspan, invisible unless you knew to look for the lichen that grew only where old hands had chiseled. He'd called them smuggler's roads, though smugglers hadn't cut them. Smugglers had only found them, the way water finds cracks in stone. Older than the kingdom. Older, maybe, than the crown that sat on Aldric's head and ate him from the inside out.

She'd spent twenty years learning every one of them, because a mercenary who could vanish was a mercenary who lived to spend her pay. She had never once imagined she'd be using them to run with an exiled archivist who kept tripping over his own satchel and a princess who rode like she was punishing the horse personally.

"Left here," she called back. "There's a drop on your right. Don't admire it."

"I wasn't admiring it," Sera snapped. "I was noting its tactical value."

"You were admiring it."

Behind them, faint but persistent, the horns of Mavros's outriders threaded through the passes. They'd lost the riders' line of sight two hours ago, but sound carried strangely in Thornfell's high country, folding around peaks and arriving from directions that didn't exist. Tamsin had learned long ago not to trust her ears here. She trusted the lichen instead, and the memory of her father's voice, and the cold arithmetic of how far they could go before the horses foundered.

They climbed through the night along a shelf of stone that no map had ever recorded. Below them, the valley filled with the usurper's campfires — hundreds of them, then thousands, a spilled constellation marking the slow strangulation of the free cities. Wren stopped once to look down at it and made a small sound, like a man reading his own death warrant in a language he'd only half learned.

"They're moving faster than the histories said armies could move," he murmured.

"The histories didn't have Mavros." Tamsin kept her eyes forward. "He doesn't feed his army. He feeds it *to* something. You've seen what the crown-spirit does to a king. Now imagine what it does to ten thousand men who've already agreed to stop being people."

Sera's jaw tightened. Tamsin saw it in profile, lit by the thin starlight — the princess carrying her father's ruin like a stone sewn into her chest.

"Then we don't stop," Sera said.

"We don't stop," Tamsin agreed, and hated how much she meant it.

* * *

Dawn caught them in a hanging valley that appeared on no chart, ringed by walls of granite streaked white with old snowmelt. There was a spring there, and a lean-to some dead smuggler had built from driftwood carried up three thousand feet, and Tamsin let them rest because the horses were done pretending otherwise.

She was checking hooves when Wren crouched beside her, spectacles fogged, voice pitched low enough that Sera — asleep sitting upright against the lean-to post, sword across her knees even in exhaustion — wouldn't hear.

"The prophecy," Wren said. "I've been rereading the passage about the roads. 'The wolf walks ways that were never walked.'" He tapped the satchel. "It isn't metaphor, is it. It's this. These roads. The book knew where you'd take us."

Tamsin's hands went still on the mare's fetlock. "Then the book can tell me what happens next, because I'm making it up as I go."

"That's rather the point I'm trying—" He sighed, pushed the spectacles up. "Every step we take, you're walking inside something written a century before you were born. Doesn't that frighten you?"

She thought about lying. She was good at lying; it came with the trade.

"Yes," she said instead, and Wren went quiet, because apparently honesty was more alarming than either of them had budgeted for.

* * *

The ambush came at the narrows above Greywater Falls, where the smuggler's road ran between a cliff and a drop so sheer the river below looked like a silver thread someone had dropped.

Tamsin smelled them first — woodsmoke where there should have been no smoke, leather and oil, the sour note of men who'd been waiting in the cold too long. Mavros's scouts hadn't followed the road. They'd guessed it. Someone in the usurper's intelligence had grown up in these mountains too, or interrogated someone who had.

"Ambush," she breathed, and had time to say nothing else.

The first crossbow bolt took her saddlebag off her mare's flank. The second buried itself in the packhorse's neck, and the animal screamed and went down sideways into the narrows, taking their grain, their rope, their winter cloaks, and most of their food with it in a long clattering fall toward the river.

"Down!" Tamsin hauled Wren off his horse by the collar as bolts stitched the air where his head had been. They hit the scree together and rolled behind a fallen slab. Above them, Sera did not take cover. Sera stood in her stirrups, drew, and put an arrow through the eye-slit of the first scout leaning out from the rocks — clean, professional, furious.

Then the second scout's blade found her.

Not a killing blow. A sweeping cut across the thigh as she wheeled her horse, and the princess folded sideways with a sound that was less a scream than a snarl, grabbed her own reins with her teeth, and somehow stayed mounted while her blood painted the stones red.

Tamsin moved without deciding to move. That was the thing about the oath — Vael's silence lived in her muscles, in the space between thought and act, and when she gave it leave it carried her like a current. She came up the rocks from the flank the scouts hadn't guarded because no road was supposed to exist there, and she killed the archer with her knife and the swordsman with his own dropped blade, and it took perhaps six heartbeats, and she felt nothing about any of it until it was over.

That was the part that frightened her. Not the killing. The nothing.

"Sera!" She slid down the scree to where the princess had finally dismounted, gray-faced, pressing both hands to her thigh. Blood welled between her fingers, bright and fast. An artery-adjacent cut. Bad. Not death-bad. Yet.

"It's shallow," Sera said, in the tone of someone informing a subordinate of a fact.

"It's deep. Shut up and lie down."

"I do not take orders from—"

Tamsin pushed her flat with one palm on the sternum and started tearing strips from her own shirt, and Sera, to her credit, shut up.

* * *

They lost four hours to the wound. Four hours in which Mavros's main column closed the distance like a fist closing.

Tamsin bound the leg tight, fed Sera willow bark tea from her dwindling kit, and watched color crawl back into the princess's face by degrees. Wren hovered uselessly, offering medical advice sourced from a treatise two hundred years out of date, until Tamsin told him gently that if he mentioned leeches again she'd feed him to the falls.

When Sera slept — truly slept, for the first time since Tamsin had known her — Wren sat beside Tamsin at the edge of the ledge, looking down at the thread of river.

"You went up those rocks," he said quietly, "before you decided to."

"I decided fast."

"No. I watched you. There was no decision. There was Sera bleeding, and then there was you moving, and nothing in between." He turned to look at her, and behind the smudged lenses his eyes were kind and terrible, the way only archivists' eyes could be. "That's the oath, isn't it. That's what Vael left in you. Something that acts before you do."

Tamsin looked at her own hands. Steady now. Always steady.

"When I took the oath," she said slowly, "they told me it was a discipline. Silence, service, no ties that bind tighter than the contract. I thought I was choosing it." Her thumb traced the faded scar on her wrist where the vow-mark sat, cool as riverstone. "What if I never chose anything? What if I'm just... a vessel that thinks it's steering?"

Wren was quiet a long moment.

"Then I suppose," he said at last, "the question is whether a vessel can care what happens to the cargo." He nodded toward the sleeping princess. "Because I watched you climb those rocks, Tamsin Thornbury, and whatever wrote you into that prophecy a hundred years ago — it didn't write that. That was new."

* * *

They moved again at dusk, slower now, Sera riding double with Wren because her leg couldn't grip a saddle alone. Tamsin led them onto a road so old it had forgotten it was a road — a slot canyon where the walls pressed close enough to touch on both sides, roofed in places by fallen slabs, running under the mountains themselves.

She knew a dozen routes to the shrine at Thornfell's heart. She'd been planning to take the fastest.

Instead she took the longest. The hidden one. The one that doubled their travel time and tripled their hunger and would keep them beneath the earth, unseen, while Mavros's riders swept the high passes like wolves after deer.

Wren noticed. Of course he noticed; noticing was his whole ruined life. But he said nothing, only adjusted the princess's weight against his back and kept walking.

And Tamsin walked ahead with her knife loose in its sheath and her heart doing something complicated and unwelcome in her chest, and understood, with the clarity of cold mountain air, that she had just traded speed for safety, advantage for caution, the mission for the people.

Any secret road in Thornfell, she thought. Any shortcut, any edge, any card she'd ever held close. She'd trade all of them.

For these two idiots, she'd trade everything.

Which was, she suspected, exactly what the prophecy had counted on.

Far behind them, muffled by a hundred feet of living stone, she heard the horns change their song — no longer searching the passes. Turning south. Toward the free cities. Toward the last wall.

And beneath her feet, so faint she might have imagined it, the mountain hummed — like a god clearing its throat after a century of silence.
