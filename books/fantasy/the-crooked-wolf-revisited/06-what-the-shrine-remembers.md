## 6. What the Shrine Remembers

The shrine of Vael did not look like a place where a god had died. It looked like a place where a god had simply stopped speaking, the way a man stops mid-sentence when he hears something terrible behind him.

Wren Calloway felt it before they crested the last ridge: a silence with weight to it, pressing against the ears like deep water. Twenty years in the Royal Archive had taught him that silence came in varieties — the hush of dust and forgotten shelves, the held breath of a sealed reading room, the dead air of texts burned before he could reach them. This was none of those. This was the silence of a mouth that had been full of words.

"Stop gawking," Sera said behind him, though her voice had gone quiet too, as if the mountain itself demanded it. "It's just a ruin."

"It is not," Wren said, "just anything."

They came down through black pines onto a shelf of stone above a frozen lake, and there it stood: a crescent of standing stones around an altar of grey rock, unadorned, uncarved, older than any masonry Wren had ever catalogued. No statues. Vael had never permitted images — the god who spoke only truth could not be captured in lies of stone. Only the altar, and the stones, and snow filling the spaces between them like unwritten pages.

Tamsin stopped at the edge of the shrine circle and would not come further.

Wren turned and watched her. She stood with her hand pressed flat against her sternum, over the oath-mark every sworn mercenary carried — the small grey sigil burned into the skin at binding, the mark of Vael's covenant. He had seen hundreds of such marks in his years cataloguing military contracts. He had never once thought about what they *were*.

"Tamsin?"

"I can hear it." Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice she used when reporting casualties. "Or I can't hear it. There's a shape where sound should be." Her eyes came up, and Wren saw something in them he had not seen before: fear without anger under it. Tamsin's fear always came armored in fury. This was naked. "Wren. What is this place doing to me?"

"That," Wren said slowly, "is precisely what I intend to find out."

* * *

He built his work the way he always did: methodically, kneeling in the snow at the base of the easternmost stone with his satchel open and his tools laid out in order of use. Chalk. Rubbing paper. The wax impressions kit he'd bought with money meant for food. Sera watched him with her arms crossed and her sword loose in its scabbard, the posture of a woman who trusted no one's competence but her own.

"You're going to take rubbings," she said. "Of rocks. While Mavros's army burns its way down the valley."

"The prophecy has two halves, Lady Sera." He smoothed the paper against the stone. "We have read only the first. The cache where Tamsin found it was a smuggler's strongbox — smugglers do not carry half-documents. The second half is here or nowhere. Vael's shrines were the archive of the faithful; when the god went silent, the shrines were sealed, defaced, or forgotten. This one was forgotten. Forgotten things keep their secrets."

"And you were exiled for keeping secrets yourself."

"For *reading* them." Wren set the chalk to the stone and began, stroke by careful stroke, to lift the inscription from beneath two centuries of lichen. "There is a difference, whatever the Archonate claims. They burned forty thousand volumes because the texts described what the crown-spirit actually is. Not metaphorically. Actually. A thing that wears kings. A parasite of office. And do you know what the first line of the oldest text said?" He glanced up at her. "*It enters through the crown, but it is fed by the oath.*"

Sera went very still. "Fed by the oath."

"Every king swears oaths of service. Every knight, every official, every soldier bound to the crown. I assumed it meant the spirit drew strength from loyalty." He returned to his rubbing, but his hands had slowed. "I assumed wrong, I think. Look at her."

They both looked. Tamsin had not moved from the edge of the circle. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she did not brush it away. Her lips moved silently, once, twice — counting breaths, perhaps, the way soldiers counted when pain got bad.

"She looks like someone praying," Sera said.

"She looks like someone being *read*."

* * *

The rubbing came clean by late afternoon, and what it held made Wren sit back on his heels in the snow and forget, for a long moment, that he was a grown man with frostbitten fingers and enemies within a day's ride.

The script was Old Thornic, pre-Imperial, the hand of a shrine-scribe from the last generation before the Silence. Wren translated aloud, haltingly, because some part of him needed to hear the words in the air to believe them:

*"In the year the crown was doubled, the god Vael looked upon the usurpation and saw that steel could not cut a spirit nor walls contain one. So Vael chose the lesser death: the god poured itself out — not into heaven, not into the earth, but into the oaths of the faithful. Every mercenary sworn in Vael's name carries a shard of the divine. Every oath spoken is a bar in the cage. The crown-spirit is not imprisoned in the palace. It is imprisoned in the keeping of ten thousand sworn strangers, each carrying a piece of the lock."*

Sera read over his shoulder, her breath quick and shallow. "That's not possible. That's — the whole Order of the Silent Oath, the entire mercenary tradition—"

"Was founded the same decade the crown-spirit took Aldric's grandfather," Wren finished. "Yes. I checked the dates years ago and assumed coincidence. Historians are trained to assume coincidence. It keeps us sane." His voice cracked slightly on the last word. "But listen. There is more. Listen to what it says about the shards."

He traced the next lines with a numb finger, translating as he went, and felt each sentence land like a stone dropped into still water:

*"The shards are not inert. They remember. They hunger toward wholeness, as all broken things do. In the bearer who keeps faith perfectly, the shard sleeps. But faith broken — faith betrayed, faith sold, faith shattered by the bearer's own hand — wakes the shard, and wakes with it the cage-door it holds shut. The prophecy speaks of the Crooked Wolf because the wolf is the one whose breaking will matter most. The largest shard sleeps in the largest faith. And the largest faith in a generation belongs to the woman named in the first half of this text."*

The wind moved through the standing stones, and for the first time since they'd arrived, Wren heard something in it — not words, nothing so kind as words, but a pressure, a vast attention turning over in its sleep.

Behind them, Tamsin made a sound.

* * *

She was on her knees in the snow at the circle's edge, and the grey oath-mark on her chest was glowing.

Not brightly. Not dramatically. It shone the way embers shine under ash — a dull, deep light, pulsing slow, in time with nothing so simple as her heartbeat. Wren scrambled toward her and Sera was faster, dropping to her knees in the snow, catching Tamsin's face in both hands.

"Tamsin. Hey. Look at me. What's happening?"

"I don't know." Tamsin's voice came out strangled. "It's — Wren, it's *moving*. Under my skin. Like something turning over." Her hand clawed at her chest, and where her fingers touched the mark, the light flared. "Twenty years I've worn this thing. Twenty years it's been a scar. Now it's—"

"A shard of a god," Wren said quietly. "The largest one. Sleeping inside you because you kept your faith perfectly."

Tamsin laughed, and it was the worst sound Wren had ever heard from her — no humor in it at all, just a raw, scraped hollow. "Kept my faith. You mean my *oath*. You mean every contract I honored, every employer I didn't rob, every night I stood watch when desertion would've been easier. That wasn't me being decent. That wasn't me choosing anything." She looked down at her own chest as if it belonged to a stranger. "I'm not a person, Wren. I'm a *lockbox*. Someone wrote me into a prophecy before I was born because I'd make good masonry."

"That isn't—"

"Isn't it?" She surged to her feet, and Sera rose with her, hands half-raised. "Think about it. My mother sold me to the Order at nine years old. The recruiters tested me for three days and then told me I was *chosen*. I thought I was chosen because I was good. Because I was fast, and loyal, and mean enough to survive." Her breath fogged the air in ragged bursts. "They chose me because the god needed somewhere to put itself. My whole life — the training, the discipline, the way I can't break a promise even when breaking it would save people — that's not character. That's *architecture*. I was built to hold a door shut."

"Tamsin." Sera's voice cut low and hard. "Look at me."

Tamsin looked. And Wren, watching, saw something pass between them that had nothing to do with prophecies.

"You stood watch over me for six nights on the smuggler's road," Sera said, "when you could have sold me to Mavros for enough gold to retire. Nobody wrote that in a book. Your god didn't make you do it. You were tired and cold and you did it anyway." Her jaw tightened. "A lockbox doesn't argue with me about tactics. A lockbox doesn't laugh at my terrible jokes at three in the morning because she's too polite to say they're terrible. Whatever's sleeping in your chest, it's sleeping in *you*. It doesn't get to be the whole story."

For a long moment Tamsin said nothing. Then, quietly, in a voice scraped nearly bare: "And if waking it up breaks everything? If I'm the devourer, Sera? If the reason the prophecy can't decide whether I free the king or eat him is that *both endings are true*, depending on what I do next?"

"Then we find out together," Sera said. "That's what family does."

Wren looked away, suddenly, blinking hard at the frozen lake — twenty years an archivist, and still unprepared for the moment a text became a life.

When he turned back, Tamsin had gone very still, her head tilted, listening to something none of them could hear. The ember-light under her skin had steadied.

"Wren," she said. "The second half of the prophecy. Read me the ending. All of it. Now."

His stomach dropped. "Tamsin, the final passage is damaged, the stone's been cracked—"

"*Read it.*"

He knelt, found the fractured lines, and translated the surviving fragments aloud, his voice growing quieter with each word:

*"...and the wolf shall stand between the crowned and the crown... and the choice shall not be written, for the god spent its voice to buy the cage, and what the god cannot speak, the wolf must..."*

The rest was a smear of broken stone.

Above the shrine, far to the south, a red glow was rising against the clouds — distant, patient, the color of a city burning. The free cities' last wall.

And Tamsin Thornbury stared at that glow with the light of a sleeping god pulsing in her chest, and said, in a whisper Wren almost missed:

"I know how it ends. I think I've known since the waystation. I just need someone to tell me I'm allowed."
