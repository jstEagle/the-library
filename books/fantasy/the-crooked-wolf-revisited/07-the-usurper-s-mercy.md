## 7. The Usurper's Mercy

General Mavros had learned long ago that mercy was a weapon like any other — it simply required better maintenance than steel.

He stood at the edge of the burning field below Vane's Crossing and let the wind carry the smell of it to him: charred grain, scorched leather, the sweeter stink of burned men. The free cities' army had made their stand here because the maps said the ground favored them. The maps were not wrong. The ground *had* favored them, for perhaps two hours.

Now the wheat was black to the horizon, and what remained of the free companies' banners hung from his own standards as trophies, and somewhere in that ash were four thousand men who had believed that courage was a kind of armor.

"Signal from the eastern wing, General." Captain Ilsen reined up beside him, her face gray with dust and something worse than dust. "The Thornbury company broke at the third charge. Most of them ran north."

"Most," Mavros said.

"Some of them fought well. Too well. We lost two hundred taking their line."

"And Thornbury herself?"

Ilsen hesitated. That hesitation told him more than any report could have. "Gone, sir. Vanished between the second and third charge. Some say she cut through our flank single-handed. Some say—" The captain stopped herself, wisely.

"Some say she's dead. Some say she's a ghost. Some say she's the Crooked Wolf out of the old songs." Mavros turned from the field. "I've heard all three from men who claim to have seen it themselves, which means none of them saw anything."

But he did not entirely believe his own dismissal, and that irritated him.

* * *

In his command tent, lit by a single lamp and the distant glow of the fires, Mavros spread out what he knew on the map table the way another man might lay out cards he suspected were marked against him.

Three fugitives moving west along the smuggler's roads into Thornfell: a disgraced archivist named Wren Calloway, whose exile papers Mavros had signed himself five years ago and never thought about again until this week; a girl calling herself Sera, who was either a merchant's daughter or the estranged child of King Aldric, depending on which informant you believed; and Tamsin Thornbury, mercenary, oath-sworn to the silent god, once the finest captain Mavros had ever commanded and ever lost.

He had files on all of them. He had read each file so many times the ink was going soft at the folds. But files were bones; they didn't tell you why a woman like Thornbury would throw away a pardon already offered, already earned by ten years of good service, to run off into the mountains with an archivist and a girl with a sword.

Unless the prophecy was real.

Mavros did not believe in prophecies. He believed in supply lines, in weather, in the quality of a soldier's boots. He had built his career on refusing to believe in things — gods, ghosts, the crown-spirit his own emperor claimed wore Aldric like a glove. And yet he had seen the king at the winter court, lucid for eleven minutes, weeping and begging a servant for a knife, and Mavros had walked out of that hall knowing that some things wore belief whether you gave it to them or not.

"You sent for me, General."

Vess stood at the tent flap. His spymaster was a narrow man with a voice like dry paper and a talent for being where he wasn't wanted. Mavros gestured him in.

"The Thornbury party. Where?"

"Kettle Gorge, as of two days ago. They've taken the smuggler's roads — the ones that aren't on any map worth trusting. My riders lost them twice. The mountain woman knows those paths the way a spider knows its web."

"She grew up there."

"She did." Vess produced a folded paper. "There is also this. A copy of a page from Calloway's confiscated notes, recovered from her rooms before we burned them. It concerns a certain book found in a smuggler's cache. A prophecy. It names Thornbury."

Mavros read it twice. It was exactly the sort of thing he refused to believe in, written in exactly the hand of a scholar who had been exiled for believing in too much. *The wolf crooked shall come upon the crowned... to unmake the false crown or to swallow the true king whole.* Two endings. Liberator or devourer.

"Superstition," he said.

"Of course, General." Vess's dry voice suggested nothing at all. "Though I note that the emperor believes it. And the emperor has ordered that the girl Sera be taken alive above all other considerations. If the prophecy is superstition, the order costs us little. If it isn't—"

"If it isn't, then whoever holds the girl holds the shape of the war." Mavros set the page down. "And if Thornbury delivers her willingly, we don't need to catch anyone at all."

"That would be the elegant solution."

Mavros almost smiled. "Elegant solutions are usually traps. Prepare my escort. Six riders, no banners. I'm going to meet Captain Thornbury myself."

Vess raised one eyebrow — a full sentence, from him. "She'll try to kill you."

"No," Mavros said. "She won't. That's what makes this delicate. She'll want to kill me. What she does instead will tell me everything I need to know about which wolf she is."

* * *

They caught her alone, which was the first surprise, because Tamsin Thornbury was never alone unless she chose to be.

Mavros found her at dusk on the shoulder of a fell above the Kettle Gorge road, sitting on a stone with her sword across her knees, watching his six riders come up the switchbacks with the unhurried attention of someone deciding whether the day was worth the trouble. She looked thinner than the file photographs — no, not photographs, he corrected himself, the sketches; this country hadn't surrendered to such luxuries — thinner, harder, with a new scar along her jaw that hadn't been there when she'd served under him. Her eyes were the same, though. Gray as frost over slate. Eyes that counted exits before they counted faces.

"General." She didn't stand. "You're a long way from your army."

"My army is burning the free cities' last field army to ash as we speak. I can spare an evening." He dismounted, waved his riders back to a respectful distance, and stood before her with his hands visible at his sides — an old habit from negotiating with mercenaries, who judged sincerity by the hands. "You left Vane's Crossing before the third charge."

"I left when staying stopped meaning anything."

"Four thousand men stayed."

"Yes." Something moved behind her eyes — grief, or its armored cousin. "Yes, they did. I trained half of them. I know their names, Mavros. Don't do me the courtesy of pretending you brought me up this mountain to discuss tactics."

He had always liked that about her: she cut through the scaffolding of conversation the way she cut through a shield wall, directly and without apology. He had also, once, trusted her with his life, and she had saved it twice, and neither of those facts had anything to do with what happened next.

"No," he agreed. "I came to offer you back your life."

"It wasn't lost. It was spent elsewhere."

"On a prophecy." He took the copied page from his coat and held it out. She didn't take it, so he let it fall into her lap. "A century-old book in a smuggler's cache. Your name. Tonight's date, if the archivist's arithmetic holds. You've read it. So have I. Two endings, Captain. The wolf that frees the true king, or the wolf that eats him."

"You don't believe in that."

"I don't," Mavros said honestly. "But my emperor does, and my emperor's belief is currently marching forty thousand men toward the free cities, so belief has become a matter of logistics." He crouched down to her level, deliberately, making himself smaller than a general should be. "Here is what I offer, and I will only say it once. Bring me the girl who calls herself Sera. Alive, unharmed, willing or otherwise. In exchange: full pardon. Restoration of rank. Your old company, rebuilt under your command — the survivors of Vane's Crossing, the ones who ran north, the ones who still paint your sigil on their shields and pretend you're coming back. And Thornfell itself spared the torch when we pass through. Every village. Every waystation. Your mountains, unburned."

The silence stretched. Wind came up the gorge carrying smoke, and her cloak lifted around her like wings folding.

"You'd burn Thornfell anyway," she said quietly.

"I'd prefer not to. Scorched provinces make poor taxes and worse recruits. But I will burn them if the girl runs, because my orders give me no choice, and orders are the only scripture I keep." He let that sit. "You cannot win this, Tamsin. I've seen the musters of every city from here to the sea. I've seen what's left of them tonight. The resistance is a candle arguing with a forest fire. The only question left in this war is how much burns before it ends — and you, of all people, know what I do with questions that have only one answer. I change the terms."

"And the girl? What happens to her in your emperor's hands?"

Mavros held her gaze. Lying to Tamsin Thornbury had never worked; she read falsehoods the way other soldiers read tracks. So he told the truth, or the portion of it that served.

"I don't know. The emperor says she'll be treated as a princess. I have seen what my emperor calls treatment." He paused. "I know what you think of me. That I'm the usurper's dog. That I burned my conscience years ago along with everything else. Perhaps true. But I am offering you the only exit from this mountain that doesn't end in a mass grave, and I'm offering it because I remember what you were, and I would rather have you back than bury you. Deliver the girl. Save your home. Live."

Tamsin looked down at the prophecy page in her lap for a long time. When she spoke again her voice was low, almost gentle, which from her was more frightening than shouting.

"When my mother died, I carried her body down out of these fells on my back because there was no cart and no one to help. I was twelve. I passed through four villages and every door stayed shut, because my mother had been a smuggler and smugglers' children learn early what mercy costs. Then a waystation keeper — a stranger, a Vael-sworn woman who owed me nothing — fed me, buried my mother, and taught me the roads. She never asked for payment. She said the god kept accounts she couldn't see." Tamsin looked up. "That's the only reason I'm alive, Mavros. Someone's mercy with nothing in it for her. Everything I am is built on top of that. So when you offer me a trade — a girl's freedom for a province's safety, my honor for my company — understand that you're not tempting me. You're asking me to become the closed door."

"The door is already closing," Mavros said. "I'm merely offering you a place on the right side of it."

"And I'm telling you no."

It was quiet, final, and utterly without drama, and it landed on him like a hammer blow anyway. Not because he'd expected yes — some stubborn part of him had known better — but because hearing it confirmed the thing he had ridden up the mountain hoping to disprove.

She was not going to be bought. Which meant she was either the liberator or the devourer, and either way she was now the most dangerous person in his war.

"I'm sorry," Mavros said, and meant it, and stood. "Six days. I'll give the girl six days of grace before I move on Thornfell. After that, the orders take me, and I stop being the man who remembers what you were." He turned to his horse, then paused. "For what it's worth — I showed you the burning field because I wanted you to see it. Four thousand men. That is what hope looks like after it meets my emperor. Whatever you're carrying down into that shrine of yours, Captain, weigh it against that."

She watched him mount. Her hand rested on her sword, loose, deliberate — choosing, visibly, moment by moment, not to draw.

"Mavros," she said as he wheeled the horse. "The field at Vane's Crossing. Did any of the Thornbury company survive the fighting?"

"Some. Running north. Toward you, I'd guess." He nudged the horse forward, then added, unable to help himself, the last card he hadn't planned to play: "They're singing your name, Tamsin. The Crooked Wolf. My own soldiers. That's what I'm riding down the mountain ahead of."

Behind him he heard her breath catch — a small sound, quickly mastered, but he had spent thirty years listening for exactly such sounds, and he carried it down the switchbacks with him like a coin he'd stolen.

By dawn he would send the riders to seal the gorge. By week's end, Thornfell would burn or bend.

But tonight, watching her silhouette stay motionless on the fell against the smoke-red sky, Mavros permitted himself one honest thought, the kind he buried beneath ledgers and orders:

He had just shown a wolf the size of the fire coming for her, and she had looked at the flames the way a person looks at a horizon — not with fear, but with recognition.

Whatever she carried down into the deep places of Thornfell, it was not going to be mercy.
