## 8. Broken Oaths

The tent smelled of cedar and blood, and Tamsin hated how much she wanted to say yes.

Mavros sat across the campaign table from her, unhurried, a man who had already won and was merely deciding what to do with the leftovers. His armor was polished to a mirror's patience. Behind him, through the open flap, the horizon burned — the free cities' armies at Ashfall Reach, ten thousand torches of a resistance dying in real time.

"Your company," he said again, letting the words sit like coins on a table. "The Thornbury Irregulars. Reformed under your name. Your pardon. Full pay, back wages for every year they called you traitor. All you have to do is walk out of here with Princess Sera on a lead and hand her to me."

"And if I don't?"

"Then in three days there will be no free cities left to shelter her, and you'll have watched it happen from whatever hole you crawl into." He smiled without warmth. "I'm not asking you to betray her, mercenary. I'm asking you to be honest about what you are. You've sold your sword your whole life. This is simply the highest bidder."

Tamsin's hand found the oath-mark at her wrist — the pale scar where Vael's covenant lived under her skin, cold and silent as it had been silent her entire career. *No contract broken. No faith forsaken.* She had taken that oath at seventeen, broke and starving, and it had never once spoken back to her. It had only ever held.

That was the thing about silence. You could pretend it was agreement.

"I need to think," she said.

"You have until dawn." Mavros turned back to his maps, dismissing her the way kings dismiss weather.

She walked out past the sentries who pretended not to see her, down the burning-lit slope toward the picket lines, and found her horse where she'd left it. Sera was not in camp — thank every indifferent god — but waiting two miles north in the ruins of an old watchtower with Wren, exactly as planned. Exactly as Mavros had guessed she would be.

Tamsin stood in the dark with her reins in her fist and understood, with terrible clarity, which way this was going to go. Not because of honor. Honor was a story bards told other people's children. But because when she imagined handing Sera over — the princess who'd put a blade to her throat their first meeting and then, three weeks later, given her the last strip of dried meat without being asked — something in her chest closed like a fist around a hot coal.

*You'd trade any secret road for these two people,* she thought. *When did that happen?*

Her oath-mark pulsed. Once. Like a warning. Like a door being tested from the inside.

She rode hard, took the ridge trail Mavros's scouts couldn't follow in the dark, and reached the watchtower an hour before dawn. Sera met her at the base of it, blade half-drawn before recognition caught up.

"You're late," Sera said.

"I was negotiating." Tamsin swung down. "We ride. Now. He knows where we're going — the shrine, the pass, all of it. He gave me until dawn to decide, and my decision is going to make him very unhappy."

Wren emerged from the tower shadow, clutching a satchel of texts like a shield. "What did he offer?"

"My old company. A pardon." Tamsin tightened the girth strap harder than she needed to. "Sera."

Sera went very still. "And you came back instead."

"Don't make it strange," Tamsin said. "Get on your horse."

They were half a mile out when the horns started behind them — three long notes, then a scramble of drums. Mavros's riders, maybe forty, fanning out across the low country between them and the shrine road. Tamsin knew this land the way she knew her own scars: the dry creek that looked fordable and wasn't, the sheep track through the bracken, the collapsed quarry tunnel the smugglers used when the toll men got greedy.

"The tunnel," she said. "Two hours ahead of them if we push."

They pushed. The horses complained. Wren prayed quietly to a god who had never once answered him either. And when the riders cut the angle and appeared on the ridge above the quarry mouth — torches like a dropped necklace of fire — Tamsin made the second decision of the night.

"I'll hold the mouth," she said. "You two go through. There's a fork a hundred yards in; bear right, come out at the shepherd's bothy. Don't wait for me."

"No," said Sera.

"Sera—"

"You don't get to die holding a door for us. That's not how this works anymore." The princess drew her sword, and there was nothing royal in her stance, only the flat practicality of someone who'd learned to fight because no one else would do it for her. "We go through together or not at all."

There was no time to argue. The first rider came down the scree, and Tamsin met him with her shield rim and put him off his horse into the rocks. Then it was noise and blood and the ugly close arithmetic of a rearguard action — three riders, five, seven — Wren screaming something behind her, Sera's blade flashing at her shoulder. They fought back step by step into the tunnel mouth, and Tamsin took a slash across the thigh she barely felt, and then they were through, in the dark, the riders' torches blocked by the fallen lintel stone that hadn't moved in sixty years.

They walked the horses through blackness, all four of them breathing hard.

"That," Wren said shakily, "was remarkably stupid."

"It worked," said Tamsin.

"You refused a general's pardon to fight his soldiers in a quarry."

"I said it *worked*."

But even as she said it, she felt it — a wrongness, deep under her skin, at the oath-mark. It had gone from cold to *hot*, and the heat was spreading, and somewhere far away — or very near, she couldn't tell — something vast shifted in its sleep.

"What did you do?" Wren asked suddenly. He had stopped walking. In the faint phosphor-light of the cave fungus, his face had gone the color of old paper. "Tamsin. What exactly did you refuse?"

"A pardon. An employment offer. I didn't sign anything, I didn't—"

"You were sworn to your employer's contract when you took this job. Protecting Sera *is* your current contract. But Mavros offered you terms, and you entertained them — and then you refused him with violence against his men." Wren's voice dropped to a whisper. "An oath isn't just paper, Tamsin. For people like you, it's architecture. You broke faith tonight. Not with Mavros. With the *covenant*."

The pain hit her then — not in the thigh, but in the wrist, in the mark, a white-hot wire pulled through the center of her. She went to one knee. The cave seemed to flex, stone breathing.

"Tamsin!" Sera was beside her, hands on her shoulders.

Through gritted teeth, Tamsin heard herself say words she didn't understand and somehow had always known: "*The cage holds while the wolf keeps faith. Break the wolf, break the bars.*"

Silence. Even the horses had gone still.

"What was that?" Sera asked. "Where did that come from?"

"Inside me." Tamsin stared at her own wrist. Under the skin, the pale scar of the oath had split — a hairline fracture, glowing faintly, like ice about to give. "It's the oath. Something's *in* the oath. Wren — the prophecy. Read me the line again. The one about the devourer."

Wren hesitated. Then he opened the satchel with hands that trembled, and found the page, and read by touch and fungus-light:

"*And the Crooked Wolf shall stand before the caged crown, and by her keeping the cage shall stand; and by her breaking, the Wolf shall open her jaws upon the king, and devour what remains of him.*"

"No," Tamsin said.

"Tamsin—"

"No. That's not—" She got to her feet, swaying. "I've kept every oath I ever swore. Every single one. Twenty years. If the cage runs on my faithfulness, then the cage has been *solid* my whole life, and one bad night doesn't—"

"Then explain the crack," Sera said quietly.

Tamsin looked at her. In the dark, the princess's face was unreadable, but her voice wasn't cruel. It was worse than cruel. It was afraid.

"You felt it yourself," Sera said. "You spoke those words in a language nobody uses. You're not just carrying the cage, Tamsin. You might be its lock — or its key. And tonight you chose us over a contract. Which is the first decent thing you've ever done, probably, and it might be the exact thing that ends the world."

The crack in the mark pulsed again, and this time Tamsin felt something answer it — far away, in the capital, behind the usurper's throne, behind a gilded cage and a king's lucid, pleading eyes. Something pressed against the bars. Something patient. Something that had just heard, across a hundred miles of mountain, a lock begin to turn.

Wren whispered, "The crown-spirit. It felt that. Gods help us — it *felt* you choose."

Tamsin rose, and the pain settled into a dull burn, and she looked at the two people she had just damned by saving.

"Then we stop talking and start riding," she said. "Because if I'm the devourer, I want to reach that shrine before whatever's inside me decides to eat."
