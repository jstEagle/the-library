## 9. The Crooked Wolf

The wall of Harrowgate did not fall so much as exhale.

Tamsin felt it from the ridge above the city — a shudder through the stone, through the earth, through the soles of her boots — as Mavros's siege engines finally cracked the last gate. Dust bloomed gold in the dying light, and beneath it came the sound she had heard on a hundred battlefields and never once gotten used to: the particular silence of a thousand people deciding at the same moment to run.

"They're in," Sera said beside her. Her voice was flat, scraped clean of everything but fact. Below, the usurper's columns poured through the breach like water finding a crack in a dam, black armor glinting, banners bearing the coiled crown that made Tamsin's teeth ache to look at.

Wren clutched the prophecy book against his chest as if it might shield him. "The shrine gave us the answer. It has to be now. Tamsin — if you're going to do this, it has to be *now*, before he's fully—"

"Before the thing wearing him finishes eating what's left," Tamsin said. "I know."

She had known since the shrine. Since Wren had read aloud the second half of the text with shaking hands: *The god was not slain. The god was spent. Poured vessel by vessel into every sworn blade, until the cage was built of oaths instead of stone.* Every contract she'd ever signed, every silent vow whispered over a mercenary's coin — she had been a brick in a prison. She had spent her whole life believing her word was the one clean thing about her, and it had turned out to be someone else's wall.

And when she'd broken faith with Mavros — when she'd refused his pardon and fought free of her own former company, blades singing in the dark — something inside her had torn. She'd felt the cage groan. She'd seen it in Sera's eyes afterward, the question Sera hadn't spoken: *Are you the wolf who frees my father, or the wolf who devours him?*

Tonight she would find out which. Tonight, maybe, everyone would.

"The bond," Wren said. "You'll have minutes at most. The crown-spirit will feel you the moment you touch his mind, and it will not be gentle."

"It never is." She pulled off her gauntlets, finger by finger. Her hands were steady. That surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. "If I don't come back—"

"You'll come back," Sera said. It wasn't a plea. It was an order, delivered in the voice of a woman who had been raised to give them and had learned, recently and painfully, that orders were not the same as love. "You don't get to write yourself into their prophecy, Tamsin. You don't get to die on *their* page."

Tamsin looked at her — at the soot on her cheek, the split lip from the ambush on the smuggler's road, the stubborn set of her jaw — and felt the truth rise in her like water finding its level.

"No," she agreed. "I don't."

She closed her eyes and reached for the oath.

* * *

It was not like entering a room. It was like falling into a winter lake, except the cold was *old* — older than kingdoms, older than the language of the prophecy itself. The crown-spirit's mind was a palace built of hunger, corridors of gilded appetite stretching away into dark, and somewhere in its deepest chamber sat a man chained to a throne by his own skull.

*Aldric.*

He was smaller than his statues. A thin man in a king's body, gray-faced, eyes sunken but — gods, still *lucid*, still burning — fixed on her the instant she appeared. He knew what she was. Maybe he'd been waiting a century for someone to arrive.

"You're the wolf," he rasped. His voice was a ruin. "The crooked one. I've dreamed you. Both endings. I dreamed you setting me free, and I dreamed—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I dreamed your teeth."

"I know." She knelt in the dream-space before the throne, and the act cost her something — pride, maybe, or the last illusion that she knelt for no one. "I've come to find out which dream is true."

"Then hurry." His gaze flicked upward, past her, and Tamsin felt it then: the weight of the thing above them, coiling through the palace of hunger like smoke through rafters. It had noticed her. It was turning. "It lets me speak only when it's feeding elsewhere. When the wall broke, it — it feasted. There were so many dying at once, and it drinks fear like wine—"

The temperature dropped. The gilded corridors began to move, rearranging themselves, and Tamsin understood with animal clarity that they were being *hunted* inside a mind.

"Aldric." She gripped his chained wrist. The shackles were not iron; they were words. She could read them now, glowing faint along his skin — vows sworn a century ago by men long dead, binding him to the throne, binding the throne to the spirit, binding spirit to cage. "I need you to listen. What I'm about to do will hurt. It might kill us both. But there's a version of tonight where you walk out of here, and I intend to find it."

"What must I do?"

"Nothing. For once in your miserable crowned life — nothing. Just stay *you*. Stay the man who begged a servant for mercy. That stubbornness is the only weapon in this room that isn't mine."

Something flickered across his ruined face. Almost a smile. "My daughter gets that from me, you know. The stubbornness."

"I know," Tamsin said. "She told me. At knife-point, twice."

Then the crown-spirit arrived.

* * *

It did not have a face, so it gave itself hers.

It rose out of the corridor wearing her own shape — taller, crowned, armored in mirrored plates that reflected not light but *choices*, every path she might have walked glittering across its surface. When it spoke, it spoke in her voice.

*TAMSIN THORNBURY. THE CROOKED WOLF. WRITTEN BEFORE YOUR GRANDMOTHER WAS BORN.*

"I've read it," she said. "Bad prose. Wren agrees."

*YOU COME TO MY HOUSE ARMED WITH WHAT? AN OATH YOU ALREADY BROKE? A GOD ALREADY SPENT?* It circled her, slow, savoring. *I HAVE EATEN KINGS. I HAVE EATEN CENTURIES. AND YOU — YOU ARE A CRACK IN A WALL THAT IS ALREADY FALLING. I WILL POUR MYSELF THROUGH YOU INTO THE WORLD, AND I WILL WEAR YOUR FACE WHILE I DO IT.*

"That's the plan, is it?" Her heart was hammering somewhere far away, back on the ridge, in a body kneeling in the snow. In here she was only will, and she found she had more of it than she'd ever suspected. "Through me. You need a vessel. An open door."

*THE BROKEN OATH LEFT YOU OPEN. YES.*

"Good." She smiled, and saw the spirit flinch — actually flinch — at the sight of it. "Because I have something to say, and I wanted an audience."

She turned from the thing, from the throne, from the hunger, and faced Aldric. And she spoke her truth — all of it, out loud, in a voice that shook the gilded corridors.

"My name is Tamsin Thornbury. I never believed in gods. I never believed in fate. I took my first oath at fourteen because it came with food and a roof, and I took every oath after because my word was the only thing I owned. I broke faith with Mavros to save two people I love, and I have regretted nothing, and I am *not sorry*." Her voice cracked and she let it. "I am not the liberator. I am not the devourer. I am not a page in a dead man's book or a brick in a dead god's wall. I am a woman who chooses — and I choose *now*, and I choose *this*, and no prophecy, no crown, and no god gets to hold the pen but me."

She drew breath. The whole palace of hunger was screaming now, the spirit tearing toward her like an avalanche given intent.

"And so I renounce it. Every vow. Every silent word. Every fragment of Vael I ever carried — I *give it back*."

She broke the oath the way you break a bone: all at once, deliberately, knowing it would never sit right again.

* * *

The world went white.

Not light — *presence*. A century of poured-out godhood came home through her all at once, every mercenary's vow across Thornfell igniting like fuse-cord, and Tamsin stood at the center of it holding nothing, protected by nothing, because she had just made herself the one thing the crown-spirit could not wear:

An empty door. A person with no oath left to twist.

Vael poured through her and did not possess her — could not; there was nothing in her left to grip — and became, for three seconds or three centuries, a wolf the size of a cathedral, crooked-jawed and silver-eyed and *furious*.

It fell upon the crown-spirit the way winter falls on a fire.

Tamsin felt the battle rather than watched it — a wrenching in the roots of the world, a century-old cage shattering outward, the spirit shrieking in ten thousand stolen voices as the god tore it loose from Aldric's skull like a thorn from flesh. The shackles of words burned off the king's wrists. The throne crumbled to dust that smelled, impossibly, of spring.

And through it all, Tamsin held on — to the memory of soot on Sera's cheek, of Wren's shaking hands smoothing an old book's pages, of a smuggler's road under stars — because that was the anchor Vael needed. Not faith. Not fate. A woman who chose.

The last thing she heard, before the whiteness took her, was Aldric weeping — freely, humanly, for the first time in a hundred years.

* * *

She woke to snow, and to Sera's hands on her face, and to her own blood on someone's cloak — hers, she realized distantly, from the nose, the ears, the price of carrying a god through a keyhole.

Below the ridge, the black columns had stopped. All across the breached wall, Mavros's soldiers stood motionless as the coiled-crown banners slipped from their poles like shed skins. Somewhere in the burning city, a king was walking out of his own palace under his own power.

"Tamsin." Sera's voice was wrecked and fierce. "Stay with me. Look at me. *Look at me.*"

"I'm looking," Tamsin managed. Her mouth tasted of ozone and old prayers. "Did we—"

Wren was laughing. Actually laughing, tears freezing in his beard, the prophecy book open in his hands to a final page that was — Tamsin squinted at it — blank. Cleanly, utterly blank, as though the ink had simply given up and gone home.

"It's empty," Wren breathed. "Every ending. Gone. She's not in it anymore. *None of us are.*"

Sera pressed her forehead to Tamsin's, snow melting between them. "You stupid, impossible wolf," she whispered. "You came back."

"Wasn't written anywhere that I would," Tamsin said. "That was the point."

But even as she said it, she felt it — faint, at the edge of hearing, at the very bottom of the silence where Vael used to live. Something stirring. Not a voice. Not an oath. Something newer than either, watching her with interest, waiting to see what she would choose tomorrow.

And down in the smoking city, a horn sounded — deep, royal, unmistakable — and a herald's cry rose over the ruins, carried on the wind up the ridge:

*"The king lives! The true king walks! Long live King Aldric!"*

Long live, Tamsin thought, drifting back toward darkness in Sera's arms. But for how long — and what would a man do with a kingdom, after a century in a cage?

She was almost asleep when she heard Sera say quietly, to no one, to the snow:

"He's going to abdicate. I can see it in his face already. Gods help me — he's going to hand it all to *me*."
