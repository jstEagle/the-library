## 10. A Crown of Her Own Choosing

The throne room still smelled of smoke and old incense when my father walked into it for the first time in eleven years.

He came slowly, one hand on Wren's arm and the other pressed flat against the wall as though he needed to confirm the stone was real. The crown-spirit was gone — torn loose three days ago by a god's scream and a mercenary's truth — but I watched him flinch at every shadow anyway, the way a man flinches at a door that once ate him alive.

"Your Majesty," the chamberlain began, bowing so low his chain of office swung forward, "the council is assembled, the banners are—"

"Aldric," my father said.

The chamberlain blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"My name." He let go of Wren's arm and stood alone in the middle of the hall, small and stooped and unbearably human. "You will call me Aldric. There has been no Majesty in this room for eleven years. Whatever wore this crown while I screamed behind my own eyes — that was not a king. And I will not pretend the kingdom cannot tell the difference."

I had imagined this moment a hundred times during the march home: my father restored, the usurper's banners burned, the long nightmare ending in trumpets. I had not imagined him looking at me across the length of that hall with an expression like an apology too large for words.

"Sera," he said. "Come here."

I went. Of course I went. My boots rang on marble I'd been dragged across as a child, past courtiers who had watched me grow up half-orphaned by a thing no one could see, and stopped in front of the man who had taught me to ride before he taught me to fear.

"I have ruled for six days," he said, "and in those six days I have learned something worse than anything the crown-spirit ever showed me." His voice did not shake. That was the worst part. It was steady the way a surgeon's hands are steady. "I have learned that they all knew. The council knew. The generals knew. Half the court knew their king was a cage with a crown on top, and they knelt to it anyway, because kneeling was safer than looking."

"They were afraid," I said.

"Yes. So was I, for eleven years, and no one came." He smiled, and it broke my heart cleanly, like bread. "Until three people did. A disgraced archivist. A mercenary who didn't believe in gods. And you." He took my hands. They were rougher than I remembered; the spirit had used his body hard. "I am done being a vessel, Sera. Done being a symbol. If I sit on that throne, every day of my reign will be a monument to what was done to me, and this kingdom deserves better than a monument. It deserves a ruler who chose it."

"You're abdicating." I said it flatly, because if I said it any other way I would have started shouting or weeping, and I refused to do either in front of the chamberlain.

"I am handing you a kingdom," he corrected. "Not because of your blood. Blood put the last three kings on this throne and two of them were monsters. I am giving it to you because when Mavros offered you your father's life in exchange for walking away, you stayed. Because you held Thornfell's gate for nine hours with forty soldiers against four hundred. Because you are the only person in this room who has never once asked what the throne could do for her." He turned to the assembled court, and his voice carried the way it used to carry over battlefields, back when he was young and whole. "Hear me. Princess Sera of House Aldric accepts the crown freely given, or she does not accept it at all. No prophecy. No oath. Her choice, made now, in front of all of you."

Every eye in the room turned to me.

I thought about refusing. I thought about how easy it would be to walk out of this hall, out of these walls, out of the whole suffocating machinery of a state that had watched my father be devoured and called it governance. Tamsin would have gone with me. She'd have gone anywhere, that woman, with her crooked smile and her crooked god and her absolute refusal to be written down by anyone.

But then I looked at the faces. Not the courtiers — the servants along the walls, the guards at the doors, the kitchen girl who had smuggled bread to my father's cell for years on the gamble that somewhere inside the gilded cage there was still a man worth feeding. They weren't looking at a princess. They were looking at someone who had come back for them.

"I choose it," I said. "Freely. And the first law of my reign is this: no one in this kingdom will ever again be required to kneel to survive."

The sound that went up wasn't a cheer. It was quieter than that and louder — a long exhale from a thousand throats, like a city remembering how to breathe.

* * *

Vael came to us at dusk on the seventh day.

Wren saw it first — of course Wren saw it first; the archivist had spent thirty years cataloguing gods and now got to meet one personally, which they described as "the single greatest professional humiliation of my career." We were on the eastern terrace, the four of us, eating cold chicken off a shared plate because formal dinners still made Tamsin itch like wool, when the light changed.

It came up the mountain road without hurry. It did not walk so much as *arrive*, incrementally, the way dawn arrives — and where it passed, the scorched fields below the walls greened over in real time, grass uncurling through ash. It had no face. It had no shape you could hold onto. But it was unmistakably, achingly familiar, and when it reached the terrace it stopped, and the air itself seemed to lean toward Tamsin.

She set down her chicken leg. "You're supposed to be resting," she told it. "Recovering. Whatever gods do instead of sleeping it off."

The god said nothing. Vael had poured its entire voice into her oath a century before anyone in this room was born, and though it was reborn now — vast, patient, growing back into itself like a forest after fire — it had come back wordless. Wren theorized it might find language again in a few decades. Tamsin said it was probably just sulking about the whole "broken oath" business.

It extended something toward her. Not a hand. More the idea of a hand, rendered in evening light.

Tamsin looked at it for a long moment. Then she laughed — that short, disbelieving laugh she did when the universe confirmed her worst suspicions about itself.

"No," she said. "Absolutely not. You are not putting another oath on me. I just spent a month digging myself out from under one. I know exactly what I am now, and it isn't a vessel, and it isn't a wolf in anybody's book, and it is certainly not going to be your sworn sword for the next hundred years. Find someone else. Write someone else's name down."

The light rippled. If a wordless god could look wounded, this one managed it.

Then it did something none of us expected. It withdrew the offered hand — and instead laid something small and warm against the back of Tamsin's wrist, the way you'd touch a friend's shoulder passing by. A mark bloomed there: not a sigil, not a brand, but a simple line, like a path drawn on a map. Crooked. Going somewhere.

"What's that?" I asked.

Tamsin turned her wrist in the fading light. "I think," she said slowly, "it's tomorrow's." She looked up at the god. "This resets, doesn't it. Every morning I get to choose again whether I keep it."

The light brightened — briefly, warmly, unmistakably yes — and then it thinned out across the sky like smoke and was gone, leaving the first stars and a hillside of impossible new grass.

Wren was already scribbling. "A covenant renewed daily rather than sworn once. Do you understand what this does to three centuries of theological scholarship? I'll have to burn half my own books. I've *dreamed* of burning half my own books."

Tamsin flexed her wrist, watching the little crooked line catch the starlight. "It's not an oath," she said, mostly to herself. "Oaths hold you even when you'd rather let go. This is just—" She paused, hunting for the word, and I realized I was holding my breath waiting for it. "It's showing up. Same as I'd show up for any of you. It just happens to be a god asking."

"And will you?" Wren asked. "Show up? Tomorrow?"

Tamsin grinned, crooked as her new mark. "Ask me in the morning."

* * *

We rode out on the ninth day, before the coronation crowds could turn gratitude into ceremony.

My father came to the gates to see us off — Aldric, plain-cloaked, already talking about traveling north to see the free cities rebuild, since apparently abdication agreed with him enormously. He embraced Wren, who went stiff as a fence post and pretended not to be moved. He embraced Tamsin, who endured it with the expression of a woman being hugged by weather.

Then he embraced me, and held on longer than protocol allowed, and whispered, "Send word when you can. Not reports. Just word."

"I will," I said. "Father."

He stepped back, and for a moment he was the man from before — the one who'd taught me to ride, who'd laughed at his own terrible jokes, who existed underneath eleven years of horror like a coin under silt. "Your mother would have liked them," he said. "The loud one and the quiet one and the one who apologizes to books."

"The one who apologizes to books is standing right here," Wren said, "and I resent the accuracy."

We rode east through the new grass, the four of us — me, Tamsin, Wren, and the mule Wren insisted on calling Archimedes despite the animal's evident objections. Behind us Thornfell's walls caught the morning sun, and my kingdom, my impossible inherited kingdom, went about the business of learning to stand up straight.

We had no destination yet. That was the point. Tamsin had declared that after a month of prophecies, ambushes, and divine possession, we had earned the right to travel badly — wrong turns, bad inns, roads chosen by throwing a knife at a map. Wren wanted to see the drowned libraries of Mereth. Tamsin wanted to check on her old company, half of whom presumably believed she was dead and deserved to be surprised about it. I wanted nothing more complicated than to keep riding beside these people until belonging stopped feeling like something that could be taken away.

We crested the first ridge at midmorning, and Tamsin reined in.

"There's a road down there," she said, pointing to a thread of track winding through the valley, "that isn't on any map. Smugglers cut it sixty years ago. It goes nowhere important and it's beautiful the whole way." She glanced at me sideways, that crooked smile starting. "Your majesty."

"Don't," I warned.

"Your *majesty*," she repeated, insufferable, "shall we take the road that doesn't exist?"

I looked at Wren, already fumbling for charcoal to add the smuggler's road to a map out of pure archival compulsion. I looked at the crooked line on Tamsin's wrist, catching the sun. I looked east, at all that unwritten country.

"Yes," I said. "Let's go be nobody for a while."

And we rode down into the valley, four travelers on a road no one had written down, choosing it — every one of us, every mile, every morning — freely.
