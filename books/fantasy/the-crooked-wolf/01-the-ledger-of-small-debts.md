## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts the way other institutions kept their secrets: carefully, and in a locked room.

Marguerite Dravenmoor had held the key for eleven years. It was not a grand key — brass, small enough to swallow, worn smooth at the bow where her thumb had polished it through ten thousand turnings — but it opened the Ledger Room on the third floor of the weighing house, and the Ledger Room contained, in Marguerite's considered opinion, the only honest things in the building.

She climbed to it now as the light went amber over the rooftops of Corvess, her boots finding the worn hollows in the stairs without her looking. She knew this building the way she knew her own hands. The third stair from the landing canted left; the banister had a splinter she'd never reported because reporting it would mean someone else fixing it, and then it wouldn't be *her* splinter anymore. There were perhaps four objects in the world that belonged to Marguerite Dravenmoor outright, and one of them was a splinter.

At the top of the stairs, the iron door with its seven locks — six of which were decorative, because no thief alive could get past the seventh, which was a lock that only opened for someone who intended to put everything back exactly as they found it — swung inward at her touch.

Inside: shelves. Rows upon rows of them, floor to vaulted ceiling, holding ledgers bound in grey calfskin, black calfskin, and, for the accounts nobody wanted to read twice, a leather so dark it drank the lamplight. The air smelled of vellum, iron-gall ink, and the faint mineral coldness of things that had been bargained away and never reclaimed.

Marguerite lit the lamps. Three of them, always three, set in a triangle around the central reading table, because two left shadows in the corners and four made the room feel like a courtroom.

Then she sat down, uncapped her pen, and began to close the day's accounts.

This was the work, and she loved it, and she would have denied loving it under torture.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things did not, despite its name, deal primarily in impossible things. It dealt in *nearly* impossible things: doors that opened onto places that didn't exist yet, debts owed by the dead to the living, bargains struck in languages that required the speaker to give up a vowel per sentence. Someone had to keep track. Without records, a bargain was just noise, and a door was just wood, and a debt was just a grudge with ambition.

So the Guild kept ledgers. And the Guild's founder — a woman named Ondile Vash, whose portrait in the entrance hall showed her with eyes that had been painted looking slightly off-canvas, as if she couldn't quite be bothered to face the viewer — had decreed that the ledgers would be kept by one person only. A single Keeper of Accounts. One hand, one style, one memory. Because a record kept by committee was a rumor, and a rumor was worth nothing at the moment of reckoning.

Eleven years ago, Guildmaster Corvane had put a pen in Marguerite's hand and said, "You will know where everything is written."

Not *you will write everything down*. Anyone could write things down. Corvane's instruction was stranger and heavier than that. *Know where.* Be the index. Be the map of the maps. If a debt came due in forty years, if a door needed finding, if a name had to be produced or disproved, there would be exactly one person in the world who could say, without hesitation, *that is written here, in this book, on this page, in this hand.*

Marguerite had been twenty-three and hungry for exactly that kind of weight. She had accepted before he finished the sentence.

Now she was thirty-four, and the weight fit her like a second skeleton, and some evenings — most evenings, if she was honest, which she was, professionally, at all times — she found the weight comfortable. That was the part she'd deny under torture. Not the love. The comfort.

She worked through the day's entries with the steady rhythm of a loom.

*A bargain of silence, purchased by the widow Anselm of Tarrow Street, price: her second-best memory of her husband. Filed under Sound, Absence of. Shelf nine, black binding, folio 212.*

*A door, sold once, to a merchant who wished to step briefly into his own future. Price negotiated. Terms standard. Return clause intact.*

*A debt of gratitude, incurred by a midwife in Lowmarsh, owed to something that had helped her deliver a child during a flood. Interest accruing. Nature of creditor: unconfirmed. Filed under Gratitude, Aquatic.*

Her pen moved. Her mind was quiet. This was the hour of the day when Marguerite Dravenmoor was most nearly happy, though she would have called it something else — *satisfied*, perhaps, or *in order*, words that sounded like virtues instead of appetites. Outside the high windows, Corvess went gold and then bruise-colored, and the bells rang the hour, and she did not hear them, because hearing them would have meant counting the hours since she'd last spoken to anyone, and she preferred not to keep that particular account.

There was loneliness, of course. She was aware of it the way one is aware of a draft under a door: a persistent thinness in the air, easy to ignore if you stayed wrapped up. She had colleagues. The Guild's clerks nodded to her in corridors. Enforcers tipped their hats with the careful respect one shows a well-armed stranger. Once a year, at the founding feast, someone told her the ledgers were the backbone of the Guild, and she thanked them, and meant it, and went home alone to rooms where every object was exactly where she had left it, because she had left it there, deliberately, years ago, and nothing in her life ever moved unless she moved it first.

That was the pride. Not vanity — she owned no mirror she trusted — but the deep, structural satisfaction of a woman who knew where everything was written. Every bargain, every door, every debt in the Guild's long and crooked history had a place, and she held the place in her head the way a lighthouse holds its beam. Nothing escaped her. Nothing had ever escaped her.

She was, therefore, the last person in the world who should have been surprised by what she found at the bottom of the day's stack.

But she was surprised. Badly.

* * *

It happened near dusk proper, when the lamplight had gone from warm to necessary. She had reached the final folder — the small debts, the loose ends, the entries too minor for the great bindings. The Ledger of Small Debts was a modest thing, quarter-bound, its spine patched twice with her own stitching. Most Keepers treated it as an afterthought. Marguerite treated it as a garden: weeded daily, inspected leaf by leaf, because small debts were how big ones started. Every catastrophe in the Guild's history, she maintained, had begun as a line item somebody hadn't bothered to read.

She turned to the day's page. Four entries. She read the first three without incident.

On the fourth, her pen stopped.

*One (1) door, oak, iron-banded, standing in the alley behind the weighing house of Corvess. Sold this date to Master Ellery Voss, chandler, for the sum agreed. Purpose of purchase: buyer wishes to visit a version of his shop in which his late wife still keeps the accounts.*

Marguerite frowned. Not at the entry — grief-purchases were common, and the Guild served them without judgment — but at something underneath it. A texture in the ink. She tilted the page toward the lamp.

Beneath the fresh entry, showing through the paper like a bruise through skin, was another entry. Older. Same hand — *her* hand, unmistakably, the slight rightward lean she'd never managed to correct — but dated fourteen months prior.

*One (1) door, oak, iron-banded, standing in the alley behind the weighing house of Corvess. Sold this date to Master Ellery Voss, chandler, for the sum agreed. Purpose of purchase: buyer wishes to visit a version of his shop in which his late wife still keeps the accounts.*

The same door. The same buyer. The same sum, to the copper.

Sold twice.

Marguerite sat back. The chair creaked, the only sound in the room besides her own breathing, which had gone careful and shallow, the way it did when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

Doors were not sold twice. This was not policy; it was physics. A door, once sold, ceased to belong to the Guild, and a door that did not belong to the Guild could not be entered into the inventory, and an item not in the inventory could not appear in the day's accounts. She had written both entries. Both were in her hand. Both were dated correctly, cross-referenced correctly, filed under Doors, Grief-Motivated, shelf eleven. And yet the same object had passed out of the Guild's possession twice, fourteen months apart, and the ledger — *her* ledger, the one honest thing in the building — had permitted it.

Which meant one of three things.

Either Master Ellery Voss had bought the same door twice, which was possible only if he had somehow returned it between purchases, and there was no return entry, and Marguerite would have known about a return entry because she wrote all the return entries.

Or the door had come back on its own. Which happened, occasionally, with doors. They were the Guild's most temperamental stock. But a returning door generated a resonance — a faint shudder through the seventh lock downstairs, a taste of tin in the Ledger Room's water jug — and there had been no resonance, no tin, nothing.

Or.

Marguerite did not finish the third possibility. She picked up her pen instead, because that was what she did with problems: she wrote them down, gave them a number, and filed them where she could watch them. She drew a fine red line beneath the duplicate entry and annotated it in her smallest hand:

*Discrepancy noted. Door sold twice. Investigation pending. — M.D.*

Then, because thoroughness was a form of prayer to her, she went to shelf eleven and pulled the great binding for Doors, Grief-Motivated, to check the master record against the day-book.

The master record confirmed the sale. One sale. Fourteen months ago. Signed, witnessed, closed. No subsequent transaction. As far as the permanent record was concerned, today's entry had never happened.

As far as today's entry was concerned, it had.

Two records, both hers, both correct, disagreeing with each other. In eleven years, this had occurred exactly zero times. Marguerite stood in the lamplight holding the great binding and felt something shift in the floor of her contentment — not crack, not yet, but settle, the way a house settles, with a sound you feel in your teeth more than hear.

She was still standing there when the bell below rang the closing hour, and footsteps started up the stairs.

* * *

They were not the Guildmaster's footsteps. Marguerite knew those: measured, deliberate, each stair given its full value like notes in a slow hymn. These were quicker, lighter, uneven — someone hurrying, someone favoring one leg.

At this hour, no one came to the Ledger Room. The clerks had gone home. The enforcers changed shift in the yard. The building emptied from the top down, and Marguerite was always the top, always the last lit window, always the one who locked the seven locks and carried the key home against her sternum.

The footsteps reached the landing. Stopped.

A knock. Two raps, a pause, a third — the old courier's pattern, out of fashion for a decade.

"Keeper Dravenmoor." A man's voice, roughened, pitched low. "I need the cartographer."

"Weighing is conducted on the ground floor," Marguerite called back, in the tone she used for beggars and salesmen. "Records are closed until morning."

"It isn't weighing I want." A wet cough, badly suppressed. "I want the one who remembers."

Marguerite's frown deepened by a degree. *Remembers.* The Guild employed cartographers, yes — she had trained as one, before the ledgers, and still consulted on boundary disputes when a bargain required terrain that didn't officially exist. But nobody asked for *the cartographer who remembers*. Cartographers remembered coastlines. The phrase as spoken meant something else, something older, something that snagged on a hook in her mind she couldn't see.

"I remember everything that's written down," she said. "If your matter is written, make an appointment."

A pause. Then, closer to the door, quieter, almost gentle:

"It isn't written. That's rather the point."

Marguerite looked down at the open ledger in her hands. At the red line beneath the duplicate entry. At the annotation in her own smallest hand. *Investigation pending.*

Everything in her life was exactly where she had left it. Nothing moved unless she moved it first.

She crossed the room and unlocked the iron door.
