## 2. A Stranger at the Weighing House

The weighing house of the Guild of Impossible Things smelled of brass polish and old rain. It was the first room any visitor saw, and it had been designed — by someone with a cruel sense of theater — to make them feel measured before they were met.

Great scales lined the walls, some tall as gallows, their pans hung from chains thick as a man's wrist. On one side of every scale sat a standard weight; on the other, nothing, waiting. Above the door, carved into the lintel in letters that had been gilded so many times they'd gone soft-edged, ran the Guild's motto: *ALL THINGS ARE WEIGHED TWICE*.

Marguerite Dravenmoor crossed this room twice a day, every day, on her way between the ledgers' office and the archives, and she had long ago stopped seeing it. That evening she noticed only that the floor needed sweeping again — grit tracked in from the courtyard, catching lamplight like spilled stars — and that the third scale on the left had developed a faint tick when it swung, which meant someone would need to be sent for, which meant a requisition slip, which meant ink.

She was thinking about ink when the doors banged open.

The man who came through them did not walk so much as negotiate passage with his own body. One hand pressed hard against his ribs; the other held his hat, crushed flat, against his hip like a shield he no longer believed in. His coat had been good once — Guild grey, she noted automatically, though the cut was thirty years out of date — and it was dark now across the whole left side, dark in a way that water is not.

"Evening," said the night porter, Hobb, rising from his stool with the unhurried caution of a large man who has learned that most emergencies are smaller than they look. "Weighing's closed till morning."

"Weighing." The stranger laughed, and the laugh cost him something; Marguerite saw him pay for it in the way his shoulder dipped. "No. No, I'm not here for weighing." He looked past Hobb, past the scales, past everything, until his eyes found hers across forty feet of polished floor.

Marguerite had been looked at by strangers before. It came with the office; people who bargained with the Guild always wanted to know who kept the books, and knowing made them feel less adrift. She knew how those looks went: a flicker up and down, an assessment, done.

This was not that. The man stared at her face the way a drowning man stares at a shoreline. His lips moved around something — a name, maybe — and whatever the name was, it wasn't hers, because nobody knew a name for her but Dravenmoor, and he didn't look like a man saying *Dravenmoor*.

"You," he said. His voice cracked down the middle like a dropped plate. "You're her. You're — gods. You're exactly—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter, as if volume might break something fragile in the air between them. "I need the cartographer who remembers."

Hobb glanced back at her, apologetic. "Ma'am?"

"I'm right here," Marguerite said. "What is it you want remembered?"

And the man flinched.

It was small — a tightening at the eyes, a half-step back that his ruined balance immediately punished — but Marguerite had spent twenty years reading people across ledger desks, and a flinch was a signature. People flinched when they were caught lying. People flinched when they were caught telling a truth they'd sworn off.

He did neither, quite. He flinched like a man hearing a song he'd paid to forget.

"I've come a long way," he said instead. "I need — there should be a record. A door, and what was paid for it. There should be a ledger entry."

"There are entries for nearly everything," Marguerite said, in the tone she used for explaining arithmetic to the slow. "If you can tell me the date, or the parties involved, or even the color of the wax—"

"No date." He laughed again, that terrible paying laugh. "That's rather the point, isn't it. No date. No parties. The whole transaction's been — filed somewhere I couldn't read if my life depended on it." He took another step forward and Hobb moved with him, easy and enormous, and the stranger seemed to remember where he was and what he looked like. He straightened. It clearly hurt. "Forgive me. My name is Aldous Wren. I have business with your cartographer. The one who remembers."

"The Guild employs eleven cartographers," Marguerite said. "I keep the ledgers. If there's a record to be found, I'm the one who finds it. What door? Whose bargain?"

Something shifted in his face — disappointment, or its twin, relief — and she would think about that shift later, much later, turning it over like a coin worn too smooth to read.

"Aldous," said a new voice from the doorway behind him, gentle and urgent, "we haven't time for introductions, let alone—"

But Aldous raised a hand, and the younger man behind him — rain-slicked, sharp-faced, hovering like a gull over a catch — fell silent.

Aldous Wren came toward her across the weighing-house floor. Each step was deliberate now, a man spending his last coins carefully. Up close he was older than his voice had sounded, sixty perhaps, with white stubble and a smell of horses and iron underneath the blood. His eyes were pale grey and very steady, and they never left her face, not for a moment, not even to find his footing.

"Eleven cartographers," he repeated softly. "And which of them drew the northern coast of the Cinder Reach?"

"That territory isn't charted," Marguerite said. "It's rumor. Rumors go in the annex, cross-referenced under disputed claims."

"And who keeps the annex?"

"I do."

"Then you're the one." He smiled. It transformed him briefly into somebody kind, somebody who had once been young in a place worth being young in. "Twenty years I wondered what you'd look like. They always said—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "They said a great many things. Most of them wrong, I expect."

"Whoever *they* are," Marguerite said, "they told you true about the annex. Now. You mentioned a door sold twice. I assume that's why you came through my door bleeding, instead of using the front gate like a civilized petitioner."

Behind him, the younger man made a sound — half gasp, half protest.

Aldous didn't turn. "Sold twice?"

"There's an entry." Marguerite heard herself say it and marveled slightly, because it was true, and because she had been turning it over all day like a stone with something underneath. "Dated the ninth of Harvestmonth, forty-one years back. A door, unspecified location, sold twice. Once to a buyer whose name has been struck through, once to a buyer whose name was never written. Both payments recorded. Only one door accounted for." She folded her hands. "It shouldn't be possible. Everything is weighed twice, Mr. Wren. Nothing is written twice without reason."

For a moment Aldous Wren simply looked at her, and something in his expression was so nakedly, unbearably fond that she took a step back herself.

"Oh, you *are* her," he breathed. "Same way of talking. Same — you count things when you're nervous, don't you. You're counting the scales right now. Twenty-two, and two of them hang crooked."

Marguerite stopped counting the scales.

"I don't know what you imagine," she said coldly, "but I was born in Corveth Hollow, apprenticed at fourteen, and I have never in my life been anywhere interesting enough to be remembered for it. I am a bookkeeper. If a door was sold twice, I will find out why, because that is my work and I am good at it. But I do not know you, sir, and I do not know what you think I've forgotten — and I'll thank you to stop looking at me like a man reading a will."

The words came out level. Professional. Perfect.

And beneath them, quietly, in the cellar of herself where she kept things she didn't inventory, something turned over.

*Corveth Hollow.* She had said the name smoothly, the way you say anything you've said ten thousand times. But standing there under the swaying scales, she realized she could not, at this moment, conjure a single image of the place. Not the shape of a street. Not her mother's kitchen. Not one face from before fourteen. There was just the name, sitting in her memory like a label on an empty jar.

She was tired, she told herself. She had been closing accounts since dawn. Anyone would feel strange, saying their birthplace to a bleeding stranger who looked at them like a returned ghost.

"Miss Dravenmoor," Aldous said, and her name in his mouth was wrong too — careful, weighted, the way you handle a word you know means more than the hearer thinks — "may I ask you one question? Just one. Then I'll trouble you no further tonight, and we'll do this properly, with tea and forms, whatever the ritual requires."

Hobb cleared his throat. "Ma'am, I can call the watch if—"

"It's fine, Hobb." She didn't move her eyes from the old man. "One question."

Aldous Wren leaned in. Blood dripped from his coat hem onto the swept floor — one drop, two, three; she counted, hated herself for counting — and when he spoke it was barely above the whisper of the scales settling behind him.

"When you close your eyes at night," he said, "and you try to picture the place you were born — what color is the sky?"

The question was nonsense. It was the sort of thing drunk men asked philosophers, the sort of riddle children tortured each other with. There was no correct answer. There was no answer at all.

Marguerite opened her mouth to say so.

And found nothing there.

Not *grey*, which was surely right, Corveth Hollow being a lowland town under lowland weather. Not blue, not black, not any color at all. When she reached for the sky of her own childhood, her memory offered her the blank white of unwritten paper — and worse, it offered the *shape* of the absence, a rectangle of missing sky with clean edges, as if someone had razored it out of her and mounted the remainder neatly.

Her heart was suddenly very loud.

"Grey," she said. "It was grey."

"Liar," Aldous said, with infinite gentleness, and the kindness in it was the cruelest thing anyone had ever done to her. "It wasn't. And you don't know how I know that, and I can see it eating you already. I'm sorry. I am. We came here because you were the only one left who might—" His breath caught. He pressed his hand harder to his ribs, and fresh color welled between his fingers, black in the lamplight. "Might remember. Before it finishes going."

"Sir," Hobb said, "you need a surgeon, not an archive—"

"He needs a ledger," Marguerite said, and surprised everyone including herself. Her voice had gone quiet and precise, the voice she used for entries that would be audited. "Bring him inside. Properly. There's a bench by the annex stairs."

"Miss Dravenmoor—" began the younger man.

"You. Gull-face. You'll wait by the door and touch nothing." She was already moving, skirts gathered, mind assembling procedure around the hole in the evening the way water assembles around a stone. "There are rules about bleeding on Guild premises and we are going to follow all of them, starting with mine."

Aldous Wren watched her go with that unbearable tenderness, and then his knees gave out, and Hobb caught him, and the scales overhead swung gently in the draft from the open door, weighing nothing, waiting.

Tobias Fenwick stood by the entrance with rain running off his chin, and thought: *she doesn't know.*

He had imagined this meeting forty times across four hundred miles — on ferries, in ditches, once upside-down in a flooded culvert with Aldous cursing above him. In none of those imaginings had she been so *ordinary*. Small, neat, severe, ink-stained at the second finger of her right hand. Eyes the brown of strong tea. A woman built entirely of margins and marginalia.

In none of them had she lied about the sky.

He'd seen her reach for it — everyone in the room had seen it, if they'd known to look — that half-second hitch, the blank cast of a woman consulting a page and finding it torn out. And then *grey*, delivered smooth as a receipt, and Aldous's face folding up around grief and hope in equal parts.

*Told you,* Aldous had said on the road, a hundred times, in a hundred tones. *They don't take the country, boy. They take the remembering of it. Cleanest theft there is — the victim does the guarding for you.*

Tobias looked at the woman now, kneeling by the bench, sleeves pushed back, issuing clipped instructions to the porter about pressure and linen and the location of the surgical kit, and felt the plan wobble under him like a bad bridge.

Because here was the problem, the problem he hadn't confessed to Aldous, the problem that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone:

He'd spent six years hating the Guild. Six years learning that every misfortune in his family's history traced back, eventually, to a signature on a document he'd never seen, in a ledger he'd never read, written by hands he'd never known. And now here was one of those hands, small and competent and bossy, ordering a porter to boil water, and she had saved Aldous's route home with a single sentence — *bring him inside* — and Tobias did not know what to do with a hatred that kept getting interrupted by gratitude.

"She's lying to herself," he muttered. "That's all. That's the whole trick of it."

"Talking to yourself, sir?" Hobb asked, passing with an armful of linen.

"Practicing," Tobias said.

Across the room, the cartographer looked up from the wounded man — looked *through* the room, through the scales, through forty years — with an expression Tobias recognized from mirrors. The look of a person standing at the edge of something and refusing to name it edge.

She caught him watching. Her chin lifted. Even at forty feet, the message was unmistakable: *I see you, gull-face. Touch nothing.*

Tobias touched nothing. He stood in the doorway of the Guild of Impossible Things, dripping, and watched the woman who had unmade his world kneel beside the man who was dying to unmake her forgetting, and he counted the seconds until one of those two facts would have to give.

Above them all, twenty-two scales swung slow in the lamplight, weighing nothing, weighing everything, and on the third one from the left — the one with the tick — something that was not quite a weight settled softly into the empty pan.

Nobody noticed.

Almost nobody. Marguerite Dravenmoor, kneeling in blood and lamplight, felt the small wrongness in the room the way she felt a misaligned column in a ledger: a prickle along the spine, an itch in the arithmetic. She looked up at the third scale. Its pans hung empty and level.

Its pans should not have been level. One of them was a thumb's weight heavier than the other, and had been for twenty years, and she had never once thought about it until now.

She looked away. There was a dying man to deal with, and questions forming in her like frost on glass, and tomorrow there would be ledgers to search, and somewhere in the deep annex, under *disputed claims* and *rumors*, she was almost certain there was an entry she had never read, in a hand she was beginning to fear she knew.

"Water," she said. "Boiled. Quickly."

And under her breath, so quietly that not even she could swear afterward to having said it, she spoke a word she had no memory of learning.

"*Vellharrow.*"

The third scale ticked.
