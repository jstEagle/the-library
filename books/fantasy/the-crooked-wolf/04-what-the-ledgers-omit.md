## 4. What the Ledgers Omit

I did not sleep.

This is not a confession of grief or terror — I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only thing I had left that night. I did not sleep for the same reason a locksmith does not sleep when someone has picked his own front door: not because he is afraid, but because the lock has stopped being trustworthy, and if the lock cannot be trusted then nothing behind it can either.

The word *Vellharrow* sat in my head like a stone in a shoe. I turned it over and over as I walked home through the rain-slick streets, past shuttered stalls and the gutter-lamps of the Weighing District, and every time I reached for it — *Vellharrow* — it slid away from meaning. It was a sound shaped like a place. It was a place shaped like nothing at all. I have spent twenty years learning the difference between a thing that does not exist and a thing that exists but refuses to be written down, and I could not tell which one was sitting in my chest, heavy and wet as the dead man's map folded inside my coat.

The map itself I locked in the tin strongbox under my floorboards, beneath the false bottom where I keep the Guild's duplicate seals. I am not proud of that decision. A proper cartographer would have brought it back to the archives at once, logged its acquisition, entered it into the Register of Recovered Documents in her neat, upright hand. But the man had died holding it, and his blood had soaked through three folds of the paper, and when I'd unfolded it by lamplight in the weighing house while the watchmen argued over the body, the ink had still been moving.

Not smearing. Moving. The rivers were running their courses again, slow as honey, and the mountain ranges breathed like sleeping animals, and along the southern coast a road appeared where no road had been, drew itself line by patient line, and vanished before I could fix its position in my mind.

I know what you would say, if you were one of my apprentices — I have never taken an apprentice, but I imagine one anyway, on bad nights, someone to explain things to. You would say: *ink migrates, mistress, especially blood-ink, especially old paper.* And I would say: yes. And I would say: but rivers do not run uphill between heartbeats, and roads do not build themselves out of spite, and I have catalogued eleven thousand documents in this Guild's keeping and never once seen ink behave like something trying to be remembered.

So I locked it under my floorboards like a coward, and I went to bed, and I lay there listening to the rain, and around the third hour of the morning I admitted the truth to myself, which is that I was not going to sleep, and got up, dressed, and walked back through the empty city to the Guildhall with my keys cold in my fist.

If a country had been erased from every record I kept —

— and I want to pause here, because that sentence is wrong, and I knew it was wrong even as I thought it. *Every record I kept.* Not every record the Guild kept. Mine. As though the ledgers were an extension of my own skull, as though forgetting were a clerical function I performed personally. That is how it feels, after twenty years. The archive is not a building. It is a habit of mind. And somewhere in that habit there was a hole, and the hole had a name, and the dying man had known it.

* * *

The archives occupy the lower three floors of the Guildhall, and they are mine in every way that matters, which is to say legally the Guild's, spiritually mine, and practically nobody else's because nobody else has the patience. Twelve thousand volumes. Forty-one registers of bargains. Nine hundred and six recorded doors, of which perhaps two hundred still open onto anywhere. The Index of Erasures — thin, always thin; erasure is expensive and the Guild is frugal — shelved alone in the iron cabinet at the end of the third row, because even the other books seem to keep their distance from it.

I lit the lamps. I pulled the Index of Erasures off the shelf, and my hands were steady, and I remember being grateful for that, the way you are grateful for small mercies in a house fire.

The Index is organized chronologically. Each entry records four things: the subject erased, the authority authorizing the erasure, the fee paid, and the page number of the master ledger where the full record resides. In thirty years of entries I expected to find — I don't know what I expected. A gap. A burn mark. Something dramatic. Archives are honest in the way that graves are honest: they tell you exactly where the body isn't.

What I found instead was worse than a gap.

There was no entry for Vellharrow. There was no torn page, no scraped vellum, no burned section. There was simply a stretch of the Index — years of it, decades, I counted them twice with my finger on the margin — in which the entries continued in an unbroken hand, dates ascending, fees recorded, subjects named and unmade, and the hand was mine.

Not similar to mine. Mine. The particular way I cross my sevens. The slight forward lean that gets worse toward the bottom of a long page. The little hook I put on terminal r's that Master Ellery tried to beat out of me when I was nineteen and failed to, thank all the patient gods of paper.

My hand, writing entries for erasures I had no memory of conducting, in years I remembered clearly — the flood year, the year of the twin comets, the year Corvane took the chair — none of which contained, in any corner of my recollection, a single hour spent holding a razor to a book.

I set the Index down very carefully on the reading table, the way you set down a snake you suspect is venomous, and I made myself think.

Possibility the first: forgery. Someone had learned my hand well enough to pass a casual inspection. Against this: no one alive knows my hand that well, because I have never let anyone see enough of it, because a keeper of ledgers who lets her script become predictable might as well leave the vault door propped open with a brick. Also against this: the forger would have needed access to the iron cabinet, which requires my key, which has not left my neck in eleven years except to be cleaned.

Possibility the second: I had written those entries and forgotten writing them. And here my reasoning did something I did not like. It flinched. It offered me, quickly, helpfully, a dozen alternative explanations — exhaustion, enchantment, a fever in some year I misremembered — the way a frightened animal offers a predator several exits at once, hoping one of them works. I noticed the flinch. I noted it down mentally, in my own hand, so to speak. *Marguerite Dravenmoor declined, briefly, to consider the obvious.* That is not like me. That is the opposite of like me. My whole life is built on the discipline of looking directly at the thing.

So I looked directly at the thing.

*You cut these pages out,* said the thing, in a voice that sounded unpleasantly like my own. *You erased a country, and then you erased the erasing, and then you went back to work.*

"No," I said aloud, to the empty archive, because I am precise even in madness, and madness deserves accurate reporting.

But my voice echoed off twelve thousand volumes, and twelve thousand volumes said nothing back, and silence in an archive is never neutral. Silence in an archive is a verdict pending.

* * *

I worked until dawn. This is what I know how to do when the world comes apart: I check the arithmetic.

If Vellharrow had been erased, the erasure would leave fingerprints everywhere, because nothing in this world is deleted cleanly — deletion is just a tidier word for moving. Debts do not vanish; they migrate. Doors do not close without a record of closing. If a whole country had been unwritten, then everything that touched it — trade agreements, tolls, marriages across its borders, refugees, mercenaries, letters of credit — all of it would have been amended, and amendments leave scars in paper. Old ink sits differently on the page than new. A line written over a scraped patch catches the light wrong. Twenty years of handling these books has taught my fingers things my eyes don't notice.

I started with the trade registers. Year by year, backward from the present, I read for the scar-tissue of amendment, and I found it, and I found it, and I found it.

Here: a toll agreement with the port of Greyharbor, amended in a year I could date to within a season by the water damage, the original clause struck through and replaced. The original clause had concerned tariffs on goods arriving "from the eastern marches." The replacement read "from points east." Innocuous. Except that no clerk in the history of this Guild has ever used a phrase as vague as *points east* when a specific port was available, because vagueness is how disputes start, and the Guild's entire fortune rests on disputes not starting.

Here: a mercenary company discharged and paid off, the record noting service "overseas." Overseas. Which sea? The Guild records seas by name. Always. There are nine of them and we are proud of every one.

Here: a marriage contract annulled, both parties compensated, the bride's origin given as "the interior." The interior of what? The world has no interior. The world has places, and places have names, and I have written most of them down.

Do you see what was done? Whoever performed the erasure had not simply removed Vellharrow. They had gone through the entire archive and sanded down every edge where Vellharrow touched the rest of the world, replacing the specific with the vague, the named with the unnamed, until the country dissolved into a fog of general directions. It was beautiful work. It was the best erasure I have ever seen, and I include in that assessment the ones I have performed myself, of which there are — I checked, twice, hands flat on the table — seven. Small ones. A debt forgiven improperly. A door sold twice, once to a man who should never have been sold anything. Nothing larger than a town.

Seven entries in my hand. Seven. And now a country's worth of sanding-down, also in my hand, that I had never done.

Unless I had.

* * *

Somewhere in the fourth hour, my lamp found the razor marks.

I had moved on to the master ledgers — the great bound volumes where the full records live, the ones referenced by the Index — and I was checking the pagination, because a sander-down of edges must also remove pages, and removed pages leave stubs. You cannot tear a page from a Guild ledger; the binding is sewn too tight and the paper is too good. You must cut it. And a cut page, however carefully done, leaves a hairline ridge at the spine, a pale seam where the blade passed, visible only if you tilt the page against the light and run your thumb along the gutter the way — the way I was doing, the way I have done ten thousand times, checking other people's work.

Volume nine. The seam. Volume ten, two seams, close together. Volume fourteen, a cluster of five, and when I tilted the fifth against the lamp I saw, ghosted into the next page by the pressure of the blade, the mirror-image impression of handwriting that was not there anymore.

I could not read it. Mirror-writing defeats me the same as it defeats anyone. But I could measure it. The letter-height. The slant. The lean that worsens toward the bottom of a page.

I fetched a blank sheet and wrote the alphabet in my own hand, and held it up beside the ghost, and compared them the way I compare any suspected forgery, because that is the procedure, that is the discipline, that is the cold clean machine I have made of myself.

The slant matched to a degree.

The letter-height matched to a degree.

The hook on the terminal r — the hook Master Ellery failed to beat out of me — was there, mirrored, unmistakable, a signature of habit deeper than any signature of ink.

I stood in the archive with the lamp trembling in my hand — my hand, steady all night, finally trembling — and I understood that I had come looking for a criminal and found a fingerprint, and the fingerprint was mine, and there were only two explanations left standing, and one of them was that someone had committed the most perfect forgery in the history of the Guild purely to frame a middle-aged cartographer who owned nothing worth stealing, and the other one was—

The other one was that I had done it. That I had stood in this room, in some year my memory insisted was ordinary, with a razor in a hand as steady as the one holding the lamp now, and I had cut my country out of the world, page by page, seam by seam, and then — this was the part that made me sit down on the reading bench like an old woman — and then I had cut the memory of doing it out of myself, and the cutting had been equally clean, equally professional, and I had woken the next morning and gone back to work.

Who does that? What kind of person erases a nation and then files the erasure under things she doesn't need to know?

A careful one. A thorough one. Someone who understood that the most dangerous loose end in any cover-up is the cover-up artist herself, and tied it off accordingly.

Someone like me.

* * *

The lamps were burning low when I heard the footsteps above — the watch changing, the day-clerks arriving, the Guildhall waking into its ordinary lies — and I realized I had been sitting motionless for a length of time I could not account for, which frightened me more than anything else that night, because gaps in my own attention were suddenly no longer safe things to have.

I rebindinged nothing. I reshelved everything precisely where it had been, angles exact, spines flush, because whatever else was true about me, true or forged, I was not going to add disorder to the evidence. I locked the iron cabinet. I hung the key back around my neck. And as I turned to go, my eye caught the last thing, the small thing, the thing I have turned over every night since.

On the reading table, where I had worked, there was a faint crescent of wax — sealing wax, crimson, high-grade, the kind the Guildmaster's office uses — dried in a little droplet, as if a seal had been broken near that spot. Not by me. I had sealed nothing. I had broken nothing.

But the droplet was old. Months old, maybe years. It had been there all along, on the table where someone once sat with a razor and a stack of pages and a pot of crimson wax, sealing something shut — a packet, a report, a memory — before carrying it upstairs to whoever keeps the things that must never be found.

And I knew — the way you know the shape of your own hand — that if I climbed the stairs to the Guildmaster's office and asked Corvane what he kept in his black lacquered cabinets, I would find the missing pages, and the missing memories, and probably the missing country, filed and labeled in a hand steadier than mine.

I was halfway to the stairs when the summons came down to meet me: a junior clerk, breathless, sent by the Guildmaster himself, requesting — requesting, Corvane never requests — the Keeper of Ledgers' presence in his office, first thing, regarding "a matter concerning a deceased visitor."

He knew about the weighing house already. Of course he did. He had probably known before the man finished dying.

And as I followed the clerk up through the waking Guildhall, past the portraits of every keeper who had served before me, I caught my reflection in the glass of a display case — tired, rumpled, ink-stained to the second knuckle — and for the first time in twenty years I looked at my own face and could not swear to whose it was.

Corvane's door opened before I knocked.
