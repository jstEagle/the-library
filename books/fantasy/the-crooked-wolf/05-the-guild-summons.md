## 5. The Guild Summons

The summons came at dawn, which was wrong. Guild business arrived by ledger-runner at the third bell of afternoon, when accounts were open and witnesses plentiful. Dawn was for funerals and fires.

Marguerite stood in her doorway holding the slip of grey paper as if it might bite. *Guildmaster Corvane requests the cartographer Dravenmoor at the ninth hour. Come alone. Bring nothing.*

Bring nothing. As though she were a debtor instead of the woman who kept every debt the Guild had ever incurred.

She dressed carefully — her good grey coat, the one with the brass buttons that said *I am precise and I am not afraid* — and she did not bring the map. The map lay folded beneath the false bottom of her ink drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, and all night it had whispered against the wood like something breathing. She had not slept. The dead man's face had not left the inside of her eyelids: the blood on his teeth, the way he had said *Vellharrow* like a man saying the name of a lover he'd been told to forget.

At the ninth hour she climbed the spiral stair to Corvane's office at the top of the weighing house, where the great scales hung silent and the windows looked out over the city like eyes.

Corvane was pouring tea.

That was the first thing about him that unsettled people: the gentleness. Guildmaster Aldous Corvane was a narrow man with a scholar's stoop and a voice like a well-read uncle, and he poured tea the way other men performed surgery — total attention, no wasted motion. He did not look up when she entered.

"Marguerite." He said her name the way she wrote entries: correctly, completely, with no abbreviation. "Sit. You look tired."

"I closed the ledgers late."

"You always close them late. It's why I trust you." He set the cup before her. "Drink. It's the good tea. The kind we don't record."

She drank because refusing would be a statement, and statements were evidence. The tea was excellent. That frightened her more than anything else had all week.

* * *

Corvane watched her drink and thought: *she knows already.*

He had known Marguerite Dravenmoor since she was nineteen and hungry, a girl from nowhere in particular with handwriting so clean it looked typeset. He had promoted her past three senior clerks because she had a quality the Guild prized above diligence, above honesty, even above loyalty: she believed in the records. Not in the Guild — the Guild was an instrument, and instruments could be replaced — but in the idea that everything true could be written down, and everything written down could be trusted, and everything unwritten deserved its fate. Give him ten more like her and the world would run quiet as a balanced column.

Which was why the dead man in the weighing house troubled him. Not the death itself; deaths were line items. What troubled him was what the man had been carrying, and who he'd been asking for.

*The cartographer who remembers.* Corvane had read the watchmen's report four times. Four times was excessive. Excessive attention was how errors crept into otherwise sound systems.

"Tell me about last night," he said now, pleasantly.

"There isn't much to tell. A man came in off the street, bleeding. He asked for me. I sent for the physicker. He died before she arrived."

"What did he ask you?"

"He asked—" She paused. Corvane noted the pause the way he noted an entry out of sequence: not alarming on its own, but filed. "He asked if this was the Guild of Impossible Things. I said yes. Then he stopped making sense."

"Stopped making sense," Corvane repeated. "What did he say?"

"Words. Names, maybe. He was dying; people say names when they're dying." She met his eyes. Hers were steady. Good. "I didn't write any of it down, if that's your next question. There was nothing worth recording."

*There was nothing worth recording.* Corvane turned the sentence over. In thirty years he had never heard Marguerite Dravenmoor declare anything unworthy of recording. She had once catalogued, in full, the contents of a beggar's pockets found drowned in the canal — including a button, a tooth, and half a love letter — because, she said, someone had carried those things, and therefore they mattered.

And now a bleeding stranger dies at her feet speaking names, and she declares it *nothing worth recording.*

He refilled her cup. His hands did not shake. His hands never shook; that was the point of him.

"The man had a wound here," Corvane said, touching his own left side, below the ribs. "Made by a Guild enforcer's knife. Do you know why a Guild knife would be in a stranger's ribs, Marguerite?"

"No."

"Do you have a theory?"

"I keep ledgers, Guildmaster. Theories are above my station."

It was almost insolent. Almost. He smiled at her, and let it go, and hated himself a little for the letting-go, because he knew — he had known since she walked through the door smelling of lamp oil and sleeplessness — exactly what she was doing. She was lying to him. Badly, by her standards. Which meant she was lying for the first time, which meant whatever she was protecting, she was protecting it with her whole unpracticed heart.

He had built this. He had taught her that the record was sacred. And now the record had come back around wearing her own face, asking her to choose.

* * *

Marguerite set down the cup. Her hand was steady. She had practiced steadiness in front of the mirror at dawn until it stopped being a performance and became a fact.

Corvane rose and went to the window. Below, the morning market was assembling itself — carts, awnings, the small commerce of a city that believed it was permanent. He stood there a long moment with his hands clasped behind his back, and when he spoke again his voice had changed register, dropping into something older and quieter.

"When I was a young clerk," he said, "there was a country in the eastern ledgers. Fertile. Stubborn. Full of people who worshipped their own history — literally, they had churches where they recited names all day, as though names were bread. And it sat on the only corridor between two empires that wanted very much to kill each other." He turned. "Do you know what happened to that country?"

"It isn't in the ledgers," Marguerite said. Carefully. "If it ever was."

"It was scheduled." Corvane came back to his desk and sat, folding his hands. "Erasure is not violence, Marguerite, whatever the romantics say. It is bookkeeping. A war over that corridor would have killed two million people inside five years. We checked the arithmetic twice. Instead, the Guild spent eleven years and the finest cartographic mind of the century, and the country simply... concluded. No war. No graves. Two million people alive today who will never know they were saved, because most of them were moved before they could be missed, and the rest—" he spread his hands, "—the rest are a rounding error. Grief without a name attached. The kindest thing anyone has ever done with a pen."

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because a man died in our weighing house last night with a Guild knife in him and a word in his mouth, and the word was Vellharrow."

The name went through her like a hook through cloth. She felt it catch somewhere behind her sternum and pull. But her face held. Her face had held all night against the whispering map; it would hold now.

"I don't know that word," she said.

"No," Corvane agreed softly. "Nobody does. That's rather the point."

* * *

He let the silence do its work. Silence was the Guildmaster's best tool; ink was only the second-best.

He watched her sit in it — straight-backed, hands folded, the very portrait of a clerk with nothing to hide — and he felt something in his chest that he refused, professionally, to call affection. He had signed off on erasures before. He had erased men, whole families, once a fishing village that had seen a door it shouldn't have. He had never enjoyed it. Enjoyment was corruption. But he had learned to hold the cost in one hand and the arithmetic in the other and to notice, always, which one was heavier.

With Marguerite, the arithmetic was simple. If she had the map — and she had the map; the dead man's companion had been seen heading toward the cartographers' quarter, and maps of that kind went to people who kept them — then she was either a traitor or a victim. Traitors were processed. Victims were recovered. Both procedures ended with the object in Guild custody and the person either restored to useful ignorance or entered into the ledger under *assets, expended.*

He preferred, for her sake, victim.

"Here is what happens next," he said aloud. "You will go home. You will continue closing the ledgers. If anyone approaches you — anyone — about countries that don't exist, you will send for me before you speak to them. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"If you find anything unusual among your effects — papers you can't account for, documents that seem wrong — you'll bring them to me. Not to the archive. To me."

"Yes, Guildmaster."

He studied her a moment longer. Then, gently: "You lied to me just now."

Her breath stopped. He saw it stop — a full second where the machinery behind her face seized and restarted.

"About the man's words," Corvane continued, in the tone of a tutor correcting sums. "You know what he said. You've known since he said it. I'm not angry, Marguerite. I want you to understand that. I am the least angry man in this building. But I've read your hand for twenty years, and I know what your lies look like. They look exactly like your truths. That's what makes them dangerous — to you, mostly." He leaned forward. "One lie, in a career like yours, is a crack in a dam. It doesn't matter what the lie was about. The water doesn't care. So I'm going to ask you one more time, and then we're going to have tea like colleagues, and none of this will ever be recorded. What did the man say to you?"

* * *

Marguerite looked at the old man across the desk — the man who had hired her, promoted her, taught her that a well-kept record was the closest mortal thing to justice — and understood, with terrible clarity, that he already knew everything. He knew about the map. He probably knew it was under her ink drawer. He was giving her a door marked *confession* and watching to see whether she'd walk through it.

And some cold, clear part of her — the part that had cut pages out of archives with a steady hand, though she couldn't remember doing it — said: *he knows everything except whether you'll break. Don't give him that. It's the only thing you have.*

"He said," Marguerite heard herself reply, "Vellharrow."

The name sat in the air between them. Neither of them flinched. Outside, the market noise rose and fell like breathing.

"Thank you," Corvane said quietly. "Was that so difficult?"

"Why does a dead man's word frighten the Guild of Impossible Things?"

"Frightened?" He laughed — genuinely, warmly, horribly. "My dear girl. Nothing frightens us. Things are either recorded or they aren't. Vellharrow isn't. Therefore it cannot hurt anyone." He stood, signaling the audience was over, and shook her hand with both of his, dry and warm and careful. "Go home. Sleep. Close your ledgers. And Marguerite —"

She paused at the door.

"— if the word comes up again, in any mouth, in any hand, in any ink — you'll remember which side of the ledger you're written on."

* * *

She walked down the spiral stair with her spine straight and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she did not exhale until the street door closed behind her.

In the office above, Corvane sat alone with two cooling cups of tea and opened a slim black notebook that appeared in no inventory. On a fresh page he wrote, in a hand as neat as hers:

*M. Dravenmoor. Contacted by carrier of the Vellharrow map. Denies knowledge. Lied once — first instance. Under observation. Recovery window: forty-eight hours.*

He blotted the page, considered it, and added a final line:

*Note: she gave up the word too easily. Either she trusts me still — or she has already decided I am the enemy, and is learning to spend truth like coin.*

He closed the notebook and looked out the window, at the city, at the morning, at the small figure in the grey coat crossing the square below with a cartographer's unhurried stride.

"Come back to me, girl," he said to no one. "Whatever you're carrying — put it down. I've buried better countries than that one."

Below, Marguerite turned the corner and was gone.

And in her rooms across the city, beneath the false bottom of an ink drawer, the folded map stirred against its oilcloth — and began, patiently, to draw a road that ended at the Guildmaster's door.
