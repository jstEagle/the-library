## 6. The Companion in the Rain

The rain found Tobias Fenwick three streets from the Guildhall and followed him the rest of the way like a debt collector.

He didn't mind. Rain was honest. It fell on everyone equally and told you exactly what it intended to do to you. He had spent the last six days learning how rare that quality was — in people, in cities, in himself. Six days since Aldous Vane had walked into a Guild weighing house and walked out, in every way that mattered, dead. Six days since Tobias had stood across the square and watched the cartographer take the map from a dying man's hands, fold it into her coat, and go inside as if she were filing a complaint about a leaky roof.

He'd spent those six days telling himself she was just a clerk. A functionary. A woman who kept books for an institution that had killed his best friend, or at least arranged the weather that killed him. He'd rehearsed what he would say: *Give me the map. You don't know what it is. You don't know what it costs.* Clean sentences, easy threats.

Then he'd seen the enforcers.

Two of them, gray-cloaked, standing under the awning of a chandler's shop near her lodgings on Candlewick Row, doing a very poor impression of men buying candles. One of them had the particular stillness of a person who has been told not to be noticed and has decided that stillness itself is a kind of camouflage. The other kept glancing up at a third-floor window — hers, presumably — the way a man checks a clock.

Tobias crossed the street, bought a candle he did not need, and asked the chandler, in the bored tone of a neighbor, whether the mapmaker upstairs was in trouble again. The chandler, delighted by gossip and indifferent to its consequences, said: *Trouble? That one? She's been there eleven years, never so much as a late rent. Though I'll say — funny thing — she only took the rooms four months back. Before that she was over on Tanner's Stair, and before that—* He'd shrugged. *Guild moves its people around.*

Eleven years. Four months. Both could not be true, and yet both had been said without a flicker of doubt, which meant the chandler's memory had been tidied. Someone had edited him.

Tobias knew what that looked like. He'd seen it done to whole villages.

So now he stood in the rain across from Candlewick Row, watching the third-floor window, holding his unnecessary candle, waiting for either the cartographer or the enforcers to make the next mistake.

It was the enforcers who moved first. They went in through the building's front door with the unhurried confidence of men who owned the lock, the door, and the law of the country the door stood in. Tobias counted to forty out of pure spite, then followed them into the stairwell, where the smell was wet plaster and tallow, and where, above him, boots were moving with purpose toward a woman who had no idea — or every idea, he corrected himself; she'd taken the map, hadn't she, she'd known exactly what she was taking — that her life had just changed weight.

He climbed.

* * *

Marguerite Dravenmoor heard the boots two floors down and had time to do three things well, because precision under pressure was the one skill the Guild had given her that she actually valued.

First, she banked the fire and set the kettle on the cold plate — a room that smelled of recently made tea read as innocent, and innocence bought seconds.

Second, she took the map from its hiding place beneath the false bottom of her ink drawer — because a cartographer's drawers all had false bottoms; it was practically a professional courtesy — and put it where no searcher thinks to look: in plain sight, rolled among seven ordinary maps of ordinary places, tied with the same drab ribbon, its living ink stilled, she hoped, by the cold.

Third, she opened her ledger to the day's page and began to write, because a clerk interrupted mid-entry is furniture, and furniture is ignored.

She got nine words down — *Reconciled quarterly totals against the Weighing House tallies; noted* — when the door came open without a knock.

There were two of them. She catalogued them the way she catalogued everything: the tall one had wet shoulders and a warrant-tube slung crosswise, which meant authority written somewhere; the short one had ink-stained fingers, which meant he handled documents, which meant whatever they wanted was administrative, and administrative problems were solvable. Her heart performed its fear quietly, in the background, like a clerk humming while he works.

"Marguerite Dravenmoor?" the tall one asked, though they both knew the answer, which told her this was theater, and theater meant someone above them wanted a record of politeness.

"You're dripping on my floor," she said. "Yes."

"We're instructed to ask you some questions regarding a death at the Weighing House. Six days past."

"I heard about it," Marguerite said. "A stranger. Collapsed. The Guild physician signed it as heart failure." She turned a page with a steadiness she was genuinely proud of. "I don't examine bodies. I balance accounts. If you want the death entered properly, give me the physician's certificate and I'll see it's filed by morning."

The short one, the ink-fingered one, smiled the smile of a man who has done this many times and enjoys the small variations. "We're less interested in the filing, mistress, than in the man. Did he speak to you?"

"He spoke to everyone. Dying men are conversational."

"What did he say?"

Something in the room shifted — a pressure change, like the moment before weather turns — and Marguerite realized with a cold, clean clarity that she was about to lie for the first time in her professional life, and that she was going to do it well, and that she would never afterward be quite the person the ledgers described.

"He said," she replied, dipping her pen, "'where am I,' and then he said nothing else worth recording."

The ink-fingered one drifted toward her map rack. Her pulse did not speed up. She made it not speed up through sheer clerical will. His hand touched the roll of maps, the drab ribbon, the seven ordinary countries — and paused, because underneath the smell of old paper there was something else, faint and wrong, the smell of ink that hadn't finished deciding what it was.

And then the window broke.

Not the glass — the shutter latch, snapped inward by a hand reaching through the rain, and then a man was coming over her sill in a cascade of gutter-water and bad decisions, and every trained instinct in the room turned toward him, which was, Marguerite would understand later, exactly the point.

"Run!" the man bellowed, in the voice of someone who has thought very carefully about what to shout and chosen the least helpful possible word. He threw the candle he was inexplicably carrying. Not lit. Just threw it. It bounced off the tall enforcer's warrant-tube with a hollow wooden bonk, and in the half-second of universal bewilderment that followed, the man grabbed Marguerite's wrist and said, "Sorry about your floor," and pulled her toward the stairs.

She went. Not because he pulled hard — he didn't; he pulled like a man asking permission with his whole body — but because in the instant of chaos she had seen the ink-fingered enforcer slide the map-roll into his coat, and because the arithmetic was instantaneous and merciless: *the map leaves this room in his pocket or in mine, and I know which one I trust less.*

Also, someone was shouting run, and after eleven years of sitting still, part of her found the instruction almost restful.

They went down the stairs two at a time, past the chandler's bewildered face, out into the rain, left at the corner, left again, through a cooper's yard where the man — running ahead of her now, gasping — kicked over a stack of hoops behind them with the timing of long practice, and out onto Tanner's Stair, where the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators and the rain came down in ropes.

He stopped in the lee of a doorway, hands on knees, wheezing.

"Six days," he managed. "I've watched your window for six days, and the first time I look away to warm my hands, they move on you."

"They weren't moving on me," Marguerite said. "They were visiting. And you have cost me a map."

"A map." He straightened. He was perhaps thirty-five, rain-flattened brown hair, the build of a man who carried heavy things for a living and the eyes of a man who counted exits. "You mean the map they just stole off you? Good. Let them have it. It's cursed anyway."

"It is not cursed." The correction came out of her before any decision to make it, sharp and proprietary. "Cursed things are static. That map is *active*. And it is now in the coat of a Guild document-handler named—" she replayed the man's hands, his posture, the cut of his collar— "well, I don't know his name, but he files under Corvane's office, third tier, which means by dawn it will be on the Guildmaster's desk, which means we have perhaps ten hours before someone reads it correctly, and whoever reads it correctly will understand what it is, and then everything your friend died for becomes a line item."

The man stared at her. Rain ran off his chin.

"How," he said slowly, "do you know about Aldous dying for anything?"

"Because he pressed a bloodstained map into my hands and died saying a word that does not exist in any ledger I keep, in a city where I have filed every ledger for eleven years." She pushed wet hair from her face and discovered her hands were shaking now that it was over, which annoyed her. "I know more about your friend's death than you do, sir, because unlike you, I write things down. Who are you?"

"Tobias Fenwick." He said it the way people say names they've stopped being sure belong to them. "Aldous Vane was my — we traveled together. Eleven years. He was the one who could read things. Charts, signs, faces. I'm the one who could carry things and hit things." A bitter breath of a laugh. "Turns out hitting things mostly makes more things to carry."

"And you've come to take the map from me."

"Yes." No hesitation, and then, visibly surprising himself: "No. Apparently not." He rubbed rain out of his eyes. "Aldous said, *find the cartographer who remembers, and if she won't help you, she'll help herself, and either way follow her.* I thought he was being poetic. He wasn't a poetic man. He was a precise man. Which means he knew something about you I'm only now working out."

"Which is?"

"That you lied to those enforcers." Tobias stepped closer, rain drumming on the awning between them. "I was on the stair. I heard it. 'He said nothing else worth recording.' You said it like a woman reading from a ledger, and your voice didn't shake until after — after, when it couldn't hurt you. That's not fear, mistress. That's craft. I've spent eleven years around liars — good ones, the kind the Guild trains — and I know the sound of a first lie done well." His jaw tightened. "You've never done it before. But you're going to do it again. A lot. Because you looked at that map and something in your face said *home*, and you don't even know it yet."

The rain filled the silence between them, thorough and impartial.

"You presume a great deal," Marguerite said.

"I do. It's my one skill besides carrying." He held out his empty hand, palm up — the gesture of a man offering nothing and asking for the same. "Here's my presumption, then. Your name is in danger. Mine already burned six days ago along with Aldous's. Neither of us can go home. The map knows where it's going and we don't. You can read it and I can keep us alive getting there. That's not trust. That's just two people falling in the same direction."

Marguerite looked at his open hand and thought about ledgers. Every bargain she had ever recorded came down to this shape: two parties, each concealing something, each needing something, terms unwritten. The Guild called those bargains dangerous. The Guild was right. That was rather the point of them.

"The map redraws itself," she said. "Nightly. The roads it shows exist only while observed. It is navigating toward something, Mr. Fenwick, and I don't yet know what, and I don't enter bargains whose terms I haven't read."

"Then read fast." He finally smiled, small and crooked and tired. "Because the wind's coming. I felt it this morning, out past the east fields — a wind that smells like erasure. Like a schoolroom slate. Aldous felt it too, near the end. He said Vellharrow was running out of time." He caught himself. Too late. The word sat between them in the rain, enormous.

Marguerite did not ask what Vellharrow was. She found she couldn't; the question stuck in her throat like a splinter, and behind it, absurdly, unbearably, rose the smell of woodsmoke and river water and a street she had never walked, and her eyes stung in a way she chose to blame entirely on the weather.

"East fields," she said instead. "Dawn. Bring nothing you'd mourn losing."

"And you?"

"I'm going back for my ledger." She was already turning up the collar of her coat, mind racing ahead through corridors and locks and the specific weight of the archive-room key. "No one runs from the Guild carrying nothing, Mr. Fenwick. They run carrying proof. And tomorrow, when the Guildmaster opens that stolen map and finds the roads have moved — because they will have moved, they always move — I intend for him to find something else as well. An entry. In his own records. In my own hand."

"What entry?"

Marguerite allowed herself the smallest of smiles, the first in six days, thin and cold and entirely new to her face.

"The truth," she said. "Properly filed. He'll never suspect it."
