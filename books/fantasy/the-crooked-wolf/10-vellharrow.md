## 10. Vellharrow

There was no border. That was the first thing I understood, and the last thing I believed.

We had been walking since dawn through country that couldn't decide what it was — hills that softened into meadow when you weren't looking at them, a stream that ran uphill for a hundred yards and then pretended it hadn't. The map had stopped redrawing itself somewhere in the night. Instead it lay flat and still across my palms like a thing asleep, its ink settled into roads I could almost name.

Then Tobias stopped walking, and I walked two more steps before I noticed, because my body knew something my mind didn't.

"What is it?" I said.

He didn't answer. He was staring at the ground ahead of us — at nothing, as far as I could see. Just grass, just the same gray-green light we'd been walking through for hours. But he stood there with his fists at his sides like a man at a graveside.

"The air's different," he said finally. "Feel it."

I did feel it. That was the terrible part. The air on our side of the invisible line had been provisional all morning, thin, apologetic, the air of a place that wasn't sure it deserved to exist. And here — here it was *thick*. It smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and something sweeter underneath, something like apples left too long in a cellar. It smelled like a country that had been cooking dinner for itself for years with no one to eat it.

"Cross," Tobias said. Not to me. To himself. He said it the way you'd say a prayer you didn't believe in anymore, just to hear the shape of it.

I crossed first. I don't know why. Perhaps because the map had gone still, and stillness in that map meant certainty, and certainty meant *here*.

The moment my boot came down on the other side of nothing, every hair on my arms stood up, and a sound went through me that I can only describe as a chord — one low note struck somewhere behind my ribs, deep and resonant and unbearably familiar, like hearing your own voice played back to you from a room you lived in as a child.

"Oh," I said. That was all. Oh.

Tobias caught up to me. His face was gray. "You felt it."

"I felt it."

"That's what it's like," he said. "Crossing in. That's what it's like for everyone." He looked at me strangely then, the way he'd been looking at me since Ashenwell's innkeeper called me by another name, but he didn't say anything else, and I didn't ask. We were both getting very good at not asking.

* * *

The road appeared within the hour.

Not appeared — *resolved*. One moment we were following a sheep track that might have been a sheep track or might have been a suggestion; the next, the ground beneath us was cobbled, worn smooth in two parallel lines by cart wheels, and the sheep track behind us had simply never existed. I turned around twice to check. The second time, Tobias put a hand on my shoulder.

"Don't look back too often," he said. "Out there it matters. In here—" He gestured vaguely at everything. "In here, looking is how things stay."

The cobbles led us down through alder woods, past a shrine so small it was barely a niche in a stone, holding a clay figure whose face the weather had taken. Someone had left fresh bread at its feet. Bread, in a country being unwritten. I stood over it longer than I should have.

"You're crying," Tobias said.

"I'm not." I touched my cheek and found he was right. "It's the wind."

"There isn't any wind."

There wasn't. I wiped my face with my sleeve and kept walking, and the grief kept coming anyway, rising in me like water into a dry well — slow, steady, from some source far below anything I could see. Grief without an object. Grief with no memory attached to it, no funeral, no name. Just the shape of loss with the contents scooped out, and my whole body pouring itself into the mold.

I had mapped countries that didn't exist before. That was the work; that was the pride of it. I had drawn coastlines for seas no ship had sailed and named mountains that would never trouble a climber, and I had never once cried doing it. So why did this unmapped road under my feet feel less like discovery than like *return*?

Why did I know, before the village came into view, that its well would have a crooked roof?

* * *

The village had no name on any map, because there was no map, but it had a name anyway: I heard it from a woman drawing water, who nodded to us as we passed and said, "Roads'll take you to Marrowgate if you keep the river on your left," and the word *Marrowgate* went into me whole, untranslated, warm as a swallowed coal.

The language was not one I knew. I want to be precise about this, because precision was the last thing I had left. The sounds were foreign — rounded, dark vowels, consonants like stones turning over in a current. If a Guild examiner had played those words for me a week ago, I'd have failed to place them on any atlas of tongues. And yet I understood them the way you understand your own heartbeat: not by translation but by recognition. There was no step between hearing and knowing. The meaning was simply *already in me*, waiting, the way a key waits in a lock it was cut for.

*Keep the river on your left.* I knew which side the river would be on before I saw it. Left. It was always on the left, going to Marrowgate. Of course it was.

"Tobias," I said quietly, when the woman had gone back to her well. "What language is that?"

"Vellharri," he said. "You've never—"

"I know. I've never."

He watched me for a moment. Rain was starting again, fine as sifted flour, beading on the map case at my hip. "And you understood her."

"Every word."

Something moved across his face — hope and fear fighting for the same ground. "That's good," he said, in a voice that suggested it was anything but. "That means the country knows you."

"Or I know the country."

"Same thing, here." He pulled his collar up. "Maybe everywhere, if you're honest about it."

* * *

We took shelter in the village's one inn, a low stone building with a sign that showed a black bird drinking from a cup. The innkeeper was old and broad and moved like a woman who had carried everything in this building at least once. She looked at us, looked at our soaked cloaks, and asked where we'd come from, and I answered her — answered her in Vellharri, fluently, without thinking, before my conscious mind could raise a hand and object.

I heard my own accent. That was the worst of it. Not only the words, but the *accent*, the particular way Vellharri speakers swallow their final vowels, and my mouth did it naturally, easily, like a dancer returning to steps learned in childhood. The innkeeper smiled at whatever I'd said and gave us a room and barley soup and bread, and charged us in coins she bit absently, and none of it — none of it — surprised her.

"Your Vellharri is good," Tobias said, when she'd gone. He said it carefully, the way you'd hand someone a blade point-first.

"Don't."

"Margo—"

"*Don't.*" My own voice frightened me. I lowered it. The common room was empty except for an old man asleep by the fire, but walls in a dying country seemed thin in more ways than one. "My name is Marguerite Dravenmoor. I was born in Corvess, in the Guild quarter, to a Guild clerk and his wife. I have records. I have a childhood. I remember it."

"Do you?" Tobias asked gently. "Remember it? Or do you remember the ledger of it?"

I opened my mouth to say the difference was nothing, and found, to my horror, that I could not say it quickly enough to make it true. Somewhere in the last three days the floor of my certainty had developed a crack, and now, sitting in an inn in a country erased from every record including my own, drinking soup I recognized from nowhere, I could feel cold air moving through that crack.

Because here is what I remembered of Corvess: a street, a schoolroom, a mother's hands. All correct. All complete. All arranged with the tidy perfection of entries in a ledger — and I was beginning to understand that real memories are not tidy. Real memories have gaps and wrongnesses and smells out of season. Mine had the smoothness of something written by a careful hand.

A hand steady as my own.

"Eat," Tobias said. "Then sleep. Tomorrow we follow the river."

"To Marrowgate," I said, and the word sat in my chest like a lamp.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat by the window of our room instead, with the map open on my knees, watching the moon come and go behind cloud.

The ink had begun to move again. Slowly, drowsily, the way a sleeper turns over: roads thickening, a river finding its line, small marks blooming along the banks that I slowly understood were villages — this village, the next one, Marrowgate ahead. The map was not showing me the country. I understood that now, watching it. The map was *remembering* the country, stroke by stroke, the way the priestess in Ashenwell had recited names to keep them alive. It was a record keeping itself against the forgetting, and it had led us here because here was where the record was thickest.

Here was where there was still something left to save.

Below the window, the village slept. Smoke rose from chimneys. A dog barked twice at nothing and thought better of it. Ordinary life, stubbornly continuing inside a wound in the world — and I pressed my forehead against the cold glass and wept, silently, thoroughly, for reasons I could not have written down in any ledger.

Grief arrived before explanation. That is the truth of that night, and I have never found better words for it than those. I grieved the way you grieve at a stranger's funeral when the hymn is one your mother sang: not for the dead man in the box, but for something the music unlocked, some door in you standing open onto a dark room full of everything you've lost and cannot name.

Somewhere in that dark room was a well with a crooked roof. I was sure of it. I had never seen it. I was sure of it the way I was sure of north.

* * *

Near morning, I dreamed.

I dreamed of a signing desk — green leather, brass corners, a wax seal cooling beside my right hand — and of a pen moving across vellum in handwriting I knew intimately, because it was mine. I woke with the taste of wax in my mouth and the dream already dissolving, leaving only its weight, the way a bell leaves silence shaped like sound.

Downstairs, the innkeeper was banking the fire. She saw me on the stairs and said something in Vellharri, soft and kind, and I understood it perfectly:

*You have your mother's way of standing in doorways.*

I thanked her. I do not know what possessed me to thank her. She nodded as though thanks were owed, and went back to her ashes, and I stood in the doorway — stood there exactly as long as I needed to prove her wrong, and failed entirely, because I had no idea what my mother's way of standing in doorways was, or whether I had ever had a mother at all beyond a ledger entry.

Tobias came down with the map rolled and slung across his back, and he read my face the way he was learning to.

"It gets worse before it gets better," he said. "Ilsa told me that. About coming home to a country that's forgetting."

"Is that what this is? Coming home?"

He didn't answer directly. He opened the door onto a morning the color of pewter, and the river was there, running bright and certain on our left, exactly where I had known it would be.

"Marrowgate by dusk, if the roads hold," he said. "And Marguerite—" He hesitated, and for once the wariness in him dropped away and left something younger and more afraid. "Whatever you find out about yourself in the next few days. However bad it is. I need you to remember that the man who died giving you that map chose *you*. Out of everyone alive, he looked at your face and knew the country still had a chance."

"And if my face is the reason the country needs a chance?"

The river said nothing. The rain began again, gentle, insistent, like a question asked over and over by someone who will not leave until it's answered.

"Then we're even," Tobias said. "Come on. Keep it on your left."

We walked. And half a mile downstream, at a bend where the water slowed, we found the first sign that the Guild had been here before us: a boundary stone, ancient and lichen-crusted, freshly defaced — the old carved letters gouged out by a chisel, recent and precise, though the stone itself refused to forget, the ghost of the name still legible beneath the wounds if you knew how to read shadow.

I knew how to read shadow. I had made my living reading shadow.

The name beneath the gouges was *VELLHARROW*, and beneath it, smaller, in the same ancient hand, a phrase I translated without effort and without permission:

*Whoever crosses, carries.*

Tobias crouched beside it, touching the fresh cuts. "Enforcers came through here," he said. "Days ago. Maybe a week." He looked up at me, and the rain ran off his chin. "They're not chasing us, Marguerite. They're finishing the job. And we're walking straight down the throat of it."

Behind us, faint but unmistakable, the village dog began to bark — and stopped mid-bark, as if it had forgotten why.
