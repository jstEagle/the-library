## 11. The Town That Remembers Her

Tobias Fenwick had spent eleven days learning not to trust Marguerite Dravenmoor, and he had gotten quite good at it. He distrusted the way she folded maps — always along the same creases, as if the paper had opinions she was humoring. He distrusted the way she counted things under her breath when she thought no one was listening: fence posts, crows, the seconds between lightning and thunder. He distrusted, most of all, how calm she stayed whenever the map in her satchel shifted its ink overnight, redrawing a road they'd walked yesterday into a river that hadn't existed at dusk.

But he had never once seen her lie. Not to him, anyway. She lied to innkeepers about their names — they traveled under false ones, obviously; you didn't carry a dead man's map across three provinces under your own — but with Tobias she was blunt to the point of rudeness. *I don't know what Vellharrow is. I don't know why your friend gave me this. I don't know why the ink moves.* She said it like a woman reading figures aloud from a ledger: here is what is true, here is what I owe you, we are square.

So when the innkeeper of the Drowned Bell squinted at her through the rain and said, "Margo? Margo Vale, as I live and breathe," Tobias was watching her face.

He saw it happen.

It took less than a second. A flicker — not of confusion, which would have been fine, which would have been *right* — but of recognition being shoved down a well. Her shoulders dropped half an inch, the way a person's do when they come home and hear a voice they know through the door. Then she caught it, hauled it back up, and put on the polite blankness she used for customs officers.

"I'm sorry," she said. "You've mistaken me for someone else."

The innkeeper — a broad woman with flour on her forearms and rain beading in her gray hair — laughed. Actually laughed, warm as a stove. "Mistaken! Twenty years I pulled pints for you, girl, and you tell me I'm mistaken. You used to sit right there—" she pointed at a corner table by the window, "—with your ink pots lined up like soldiers, drawing the town from memory because you said your eyes made it prettier than it was."

"Marguerite," Tobias said carefully, "this is—"

"Dravenmoor," she said. "My name is Marguerite Dravenmoor. I'm a cartographer out of Corvane's Landing." Her voice didn't shake. That was the thing he'd remember later, lying awake in whatever bed this town offered: her voice didn't shake at all. "I've never been here before."

The innkeeper's smile faltered, then reassembled itself into something older and sadder. "Well," she said. "Well. First time for everything, I suppose. Room's two silvers, bath's one more, supper's included if you can stomach mutton again." She slid two keys across the counter and looked at Marguerite a moment too long. "Corner table's free, if you want it. No charge for sitting where you always sat."

"We won't be staying long," Marguerite said.

They always said that. It had stopped meaning anything.

* * *

Upstairs, while the bath filled, Tobias sat on the window seat and did what he did best, which was watch.

He'd been watching people since he was nine years old, when watching was safer than being watched. His father had been a debtor, his mother a liar of great local renown, and Tobias had learned early that faces told on themselves if you were patient. Halloran — poor, stupid, brave Halloran, who'd bled out in an alley in Corvane's Landing pressing a folded map into a stranger's hands — Halloran had never learned to watch. Halloran had walked straight at things his whole life, doors and debts and Guild enforcers alike, and it had finally killed him.

So Tobias watched Marguerite Dravenmoor dry her hair by the fire and catalogued everything wrong.

One: she'd known the layout of the inn. When the innkeeper said *corner table*, Marguerite's eyes had gone there before the woman finished pointing — not searching for it, *finding* it, the way you find your own hand in the dark.

Two: the name hadn't frightened her. Startled her, yes, but fear has a particular flavor, and this wasn't it. This had been grief. Grief arriving ahead of its explanation, like thunder before you find the storm.

Three — and this was the one that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone — when she thought he wasn't looking, she'd mouthed something to herself in the mirror. Two syllables. He read her lips badly, but he'd have bet the map itself on *Margo.*

"You're staring," she said, without turning around.

"Occupational habit. I used to mind a tollhouse. You learn faces or you get robbed."

"And what does my face say?"

He considered lying, decided against it, and then considered the truth, and decided against that too, and landed somewhere in the middle, which was where he lived these days. "It says you're tired. It says you don't trust this town. And it says you knew that woman's table before she pointed at it."

Her hands went still above the fire. Just for a moment. Then they resumed, wringing water from her hair, steady as ever. "Towns are towns. Inns are inns. There's always a corner table."

"Not always one someone saves for you."

She turned then, and her face was the ledger face, balanced and closed. "Tobias. I understand what you're doing. Your friend died handing me a country I've never heard of, and now strangers in a town I've never visited are calling me by another name, and every rational bone in your body is screaming that I'm lying to you. I'd think the same. But I am telling you the truth: I have never been called Margo Vale in my life. I don't know this town. I don't know that woman. If my face did something strange down there, it's because the last two weeks have been strange, and a person's face will eventually start doing strange things whether she permits it or not."

It was a good answer. It was a *very* good answer. It was so good that it circled all the way around to suspicious, because honest people usually answered worse than that. Honest people got flustered. Honest people said *what? no* instead of delivering a small speech with perfect punctuation.

"That's fair," Tobias said.

Something flickered behind her eyes — relief, maybe, or disappointment that he'd let it go so easily. "Thank you."

"I'm still going to sleep with my boots on."

"You've slept with your boots on since the salt marshes."

"And I'll keep doing it until one of us tells me why this map keeps pointing at Ashenwell like a dog pointing at a rabbit."

She didn't answer that. She turned back to the fire, and Tobias lay down on the narrow bed with his boots laced tight, and neither of them spoke again for a long time.

* * *

He dreamed of Halloran, which was normal now. What wasn't normal was that in the dream, Halloran was standing in the corner of the Drowned Bell's common room, at the corner table, ink pots lined up like soldiers, and when Tobias tried to reach him the tables multiplied and rearranged themselves and the floorboards showed him street maps of cities he'd never entered, and Halloran kept saying, over and over, patient as a schoolteacher: *You brought her home, Fenny. You brought her home and she says she doesn't live here.*

He woke before dawn to the sound of the map rustling.

That was the other thing he'd learned not to trust: the map only moved when it wanted to be seen moving. He'd taken to sleeping with it under his pillow — Marguerite thought he didn't know she'd noticed — and now he drew it out into the gray light and unrolled it on the floor.

Ashenwell had appeared.

Not near the edge, the way new places usually did, creeping in from the margins like frost. It was at the center. The ink had gathered itself into the middle of the map overnight and drawn the town in fine, confident strokes: the millrace, the church spire, the crooked lane behind the market, and — he checked twice, three times — the Drowned Bell, rendered in such loving detail that he could see the little bell engraved above the door, the one he'd walked past yesterday without noticing.

And beside the inn, in handwriting that was not the map's usual hand — smaller, rounder, hurried — two words:

*her room*

Tobias sat very still on the cold floorboards and listened to Marguerite breathing in the next room, slow and even, asleep or pretending to be.

*Her room.*

Whose room? Whose hand?

He rolled the map up before she could wake and find him holding it, and that was its own small betrayal, and he noted it without flinching, because he was keeping a ledger of his own now. He'd learned ledgers from the Guild's enforcers, who kept them on everyone, and from Halloran, who'd kept one on the Guild. Every debt gets written down. Every debt gets collected.

Downstairs, the common room smelled of woodsmoke and mutton and wet wool. The innkeeper was banking the fire, and she looked up when Tobias came down alone, and her face did something complicated.

"Just you?" she said. "Where's—"

"She's sleeping."

"Mm." The innkeeper wiped her hands on her apron. Watched him. Decided something. "You'll think me foolish," she said. "Or drunk. Probably both."

"Try me. I've been thinking both about myself for weeks."

She came closer, lowered her voice to the register women use for serious things. "That girl. Your Marguerite." She pronounced the foreign name carefully, like a coin she was checking for clipping. "I know it wasn't her. I know that. But twenty years ago I poured beer for a girl with her exact face, and that girl's name was Margo Vale, and Margo Vale drew maps of this town that were better than the surveyor's, and one day Margo Vale was gone, and nobody remembered her except—" the innkeeper stopped. Started again, quieter. "Nobody remembered her except me. Do you understand? Her room upstairs, third door, we kept it. Her friends forgot her inside a season. Her landlady sold her things. Even the parish book — her name's still inked there, but Father Aldous swears he never wrote it, swears the page was always like that. I'm the only one, and some mornings I wake up and I have to work at it, like remembering a dream. Like the name's trying to leave me too."

Tobias's mouth had gone dry. "What happened to her? To Margo?"

"That's just it." The innkeeper gripped his wrist, flour-soft fingers, urgent. "Nothing happened. That's what I keep telling myself. Nothing *happened* to her, because nothing can happen to a person who was never here. But the room's still made up. Twenty years, and I still make the bed. Because if I stop, I think—" She let go of his wrist and stepped back, embarrassed, brisk again. "Third door on the left. Don't tell her I told you. She looked at me like I was mad, and maybe I am, but I'd rather be mad than be the last one to forget."

* * *

He stood outside the third door on the left for a long time.

It was a plain door, pine gone honey-colored with age, and there was nothing written on it, which was somehow worse — every other room in the corridor had a chalked number, and this one had a faint rectangle of cleaner wood where a nameplate had once hung and been removed.

He should knock. He should get Marguerite. Whatever was behind this door belonged to both of them, the way the map did, the way Halloran's death did, the way the truth did — split down the middle, each holding half.

He opened it instead. He'd never claimed to be a good man, only a careful one, and careful men are curious in the worst way.

The room was small and neat and preserved like a held breath. A bed made with corners sharp enough to cut. A washstand, a jug, a chair. And a desk beneath the window, and on the desk, maps.

Maps of Vellharrow.

He knew it was Vellharrow the way you know your own name in a stranger's mouth — the coastline matched the moving map's oldest state, the one it wore the first night, before it started shifting. Here was the same coast, drawn by hand, annotated in a small round hurried script: depths, currents, a note reading *ask the ferryman about the third channel — H. insists it floods in autumn.* H. Halloran. Halloran had grown up here. Halloran had never said a word about it, and now here was his initial in a dead girl's margin, twenty years old.

There were more papers beneath. Chorographies. Street plans of Ashenwell. A half-finished map of the whole country, the borders left open on the east side, waiting, with a note pinned to the corner in the same hand:

*Guild wants the eastern ranges by month's end. They won't tell me why. Corvane's new apprentice says the surveys are "for the archive." Since when does the archive need passes no army could hold? Ask Ilsa. Ask anyone. Something is wrong.*

Corvane. The word hit him like cold water. Everyone knew Corvane — the Guildmaster, the gray eminence, the man whose seal opened any door in the civilized world. And here he was, decades young, named in a marginal note by a girl who had then vanished so thoroughly that her own town had to strain to remember her.

Tobias lowered himself into the chair, because his legs had opinions of their own, and looked at the desk drawer. It was slightly open. Inside: ink pots, a pen knife, and a journal with a broken clasp.

He didn't touch it. He honestly meant to not touch it. But the journal had fallen open on its own, the way books do when they've been waiting a long time, and the page facing up bore a single line, dated twenty years past, in that small round hand:

*If anything happens to me, and I forget why, look for the map that moves. I hid the country in it. — M.V.*

Behind him, on the landing, a floorboard creaked.

He turned. Marguerite Dravenmoor stood in the doorway of a room she had sworn she'd never seen, wearing the expression of a woman reading her own obituary, and behind her, in the corridor, the morning light came through the window and lit the clean rectangle on the door where a nameplate used to hang.

"Tobias," she said, and her voice finally shook. "How long have you known?"
