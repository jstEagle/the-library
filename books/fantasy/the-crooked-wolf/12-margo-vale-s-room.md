## 12. Margo Vale's Room

The innkeeper's key was iron and cold as a confession.

Marguerite held it in her palm the whole way up the stairs because she could not make her fingers close around it properly. They kept opening. A small betrayal of the hand, over and over — grip, release, grip. Tobias walked behind her on the narrow stair, close enough that she could hear him breathing, and neither of them spoke. What was there to say? *Your name is not your name.* The stairs said it for them, creaking in a rhythm she almost recognized.

*Almost.* That was the whole country, wasn't it. Almost.

The room was at the end of the corridor, third door on the left, under a window with a cracked pane that someone had mended with wax paper and patience. The innkeeper had pressed the key into Marguerite's hands back downstairs with an expression Marguerite was still trying to file away somewhere it wouldn't keep resurfacing: not pity, not suspicion. Recognition. The look you give someone returning to a house they'd sworn they'd never see again.

"Been kept clean," the innkeeper had said. "Thirty years, more or less. We dust around things." And then, softer, the way one speaks to the bereaved: "We thought maybe you'd come back when the wind stopped."

The wind hadn't stopped. But Marguerite had said nothing, because the alternative was to ask *what wind*, and some questions you already know the shape of the answer before you finish asking them.

Now she stood before the door and the key trembled against the lock like a compass needle near iron.

"You don't have to go in," Tobias said.

"Yes I do."

She meant it three ways and he heard all three. She turned the lock.

* * *

The door opened onto thirty years of dust arranged by devotion.

It was a boarding room — small, square, one window facing the street where Ashenwell went about its stubborn, impossible business of existing. A bed with a quilt folded back at one corner, as if its owner had only stepped out to fetch water. A washstand. A chair drawn up to a desk, and the desk —

Marguerite's knees nearly went.

The desk was covered in maps.

Unfinished maps. Maps in progress, pinned at the corners, layered over one another like fallen leaves: coastlines half-drawn, mountain ranges shaded in hatching that stopped mid-stroke, rivers traced in blue ink that trailed off into pencil guesses. A country taking shape under someone's patient hand. She knew the coastline before she knew why. She had been reading it for days now, moving across it inside a map that redrew itself nightly, and here it was again — Vellharrow — rendered in a hand she knew better than any handwriting in the world.

Her own.

"No," she said. Out loud. To the room, or to herself; the distinction was becoming difficult to maintain.

Tobias came up beside her but didn't touch anything. He was learning that about her — that she needed objects left where they were, needed the world's evidence undisturbed while she interrogated it. "Is it yours?" he asked. Gentle. Which was worse than if he'd been triumphant. He had every right to be triumphant. He'd watched her deny being Margo Vale in the common room below with the flat certainty of a woman who keeps ledgers for a living and knows what her own name is worth.

"I don't know."

"That's your writing."

"I know that's my writing." Her voice cracked down the middle like the windowpane. "That's exactly the problem."

Because it was. She bent over the desk and there it was in every stroke: the way she crossed her sevens, the little hook she put on terminal r's, the habit of annotating in the margins in a cramped shorthand she'd invented at — at when? At fifteen? At twenty? At some age in some year in some life, and the room sat there holding all of it like water in cupped hands.

A map of the eastern marches lay on top. In the margin, in her own shorthand, someone — she — had written: *the pass at Karst closes in autumn; note for the Guild survey; Corvane will want this confirmed.*

Corvane.

Guildmaster Corvane's name, in her handwriting, casual as a grocery list, dated in a calendar system the Guild hadn't used since before she'd joined it.

Marguerite sat down. Not gracefully. Her legs simply stopped participating, and the chair received her, and the chair knew her weight. That was the worst detail yet, worse than the maps: the chair did not creak in surprise.

* * *

She worked. It was the only thing she knew how to do with horror — organize it.

That was the joke, wasn't it. That was the whole joke of her life. When the world became unmappable, Marguerite Dravenmoor mapped harder. She lifted each sheet from the pile with two fingers and laid them out across the bed in chronological order, or what she judged to be chronological order, using ink color and paper stock and the slow evolution of confidence in the line work. This she could do. This was the one language she had never once doubted.

The earliest sheets were student work. Exercises. A coastline copied from memory, then corrected, then corrected again. Margin notes getting shorter as competence grew, the way notes always do. Then the exercises became surveys, and the surveys became ambitious — whole regions attempted from rumor and secondhand report — and then there were the later sheets, and the later sheets were different in a way that made her hands go cold.

They weren't drawings anymore. They were arguments.

One showed the border of Vellharrow with the boundary line drawn and redrawn, erased, redrawn, until the paper had worn thin and someone had pasted a patch over it and drawn the line a final time. Beside the final line, in her shorthand: *they will not accept this. draw it anyway.*

Another showed a river — the same river, she realized, whose absence the Forgetting Wind had recently revealed, a village downstream that had lost its water overnight. On the sheet, the river ran full and certain, labeled in careful capitals, and beneath the label: *if we stop naming it, it stops. keep naming.*

Keep naming.

Marguerite stood very still in the middle of the room, in the middle of thirty years, in the middle of somebody else's life wearing her handwriting like a stolen coat.

"Tobias." Her voice sounded far away even to her. "Who was the man who died? Your friend. What was his name?"

"Anselm." Tobias had lowered himself onto the windowsill, watching her with the wariness of a man watching a fire decide which way to spread. "Anselm Vale."

Vale.

"And he was looking for—" she began.

"The cartographer who remembers," Tobias finished. "Those were his words. He'd been saying them for weeks, delirious half the time, after we crossed the border. *Find the cartographer who remembers.* I thought he meant someone in the Guild. Someone official." He paused. "I think he meant you. I think he meant Margo Vale."

"But I'm not—"

"Look at your hand."

She looked at her hand. It was shaking. Beyond the shaking, though — she forced herself to actually look — there was an ink stain in the web of her right thumb and forefinger. A cartographer's stain, permanent, the kind you stop seeing after ten years of daily work. Except she had always assumed she'd earned it in the Guild scriptorium, copying other people's borders. A borrowed stain. A stain from someone else's labor.

Except the room disagreed. The room said the stain was hers, earned here, in this chair, over these maps, in this country that officially did not exist.

"I need you to tell me everything," she said. "About Anselm. About how he got the map. About why you crossed the border. All of it. Now, while I can still—" She stopped. Started again. "While I'm still me enough to hear it."

* * *

He told her what he could, and it wasn't much, and both of them felt the shape of what he wasn't telling her.

Anselm had been a surveyor. That much was true. He'd worked the southern provinces — Tobias said the word *provinces* carefully, watching her face, and her face gave nothing away because her face no longer reported to her. Anselm had started keeping records the villages couldn't keep for themselves, names and boundaries and births, chalk and ink and repetition, because the wind took things that went unnamed too long. Then the wind had started taking faster. Then people had started selling memories to survive it — the priestess would tell them that tomorrow, at the church, but tonight it hung unspoken in the room like weather.

Then Anselm had found the moving map. Or been given it. Tobias's account went vague there, and Marguerite noted the vagueness the way she noted everything: precisely, helplessly, in a ledger only she could read.

"And he carried it out through the Crooked Wolf," she said. "Out of the country. To find me."

"To find the person who drew it," Tobias said. "The map has a maker, Marguerite. Everything does. Anselm believed whoever made it could use it. Could bring it back. Restore it." His jaw tightened. "And then he died on your floor with the word Vellharrow in his mouth, and you told me you'd never heard of the place."

"I hadn't." She heard how it sounded. She heard it exactly the way he heard it. "I hadn't, Tobias. There is no version of me in any ledger I keep that has ever—"

"No," he agreed quietly. "There isn't. That's rather the point, isn't it? Somebody edited you."

The words landed in the room and stayed there.

Somebody edited you.

Marguerite Dravenmoor had spent eleven years keeping the Guild's ledgers. She knew what editing looked like. She knew the difference between a page that had aged empty and a page that had been made empty — the razor's kiss against vellum, the faint ridge where text had been scraped, the ghost of pressure where a pen had once pressed hard. She had caught forgers with subtler work than whatever had been done to her.

And standing in Margo Vale's room, surrounded by thirty years of her own absent life, she understood something with the sickening clarity of a seal cracking:

You could edit a ledger.

You could not edit a room.

The Guild could cut pages, burn records, rewrite memories down to their roots — she had no proof yet, only the terrible hypothesis assembling itself behind her eyes — but it could not reach into a boarding house in Ashenwell and unmake a quilt folded back at one corner. It could not unpick thirty years of a landlady's dusting. Somewhere, outside the ledgers, the evidence of Margo Vale had survived the way a river survives underground: unseen, unnegotiated, running.

"I lived here," she said. It didn't sound like a discovery. It sounded like a surrender.

"It looks like it."

"Don't be kind to me right now. I can't—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "If you're kind I'll fall apart, and I need to not fall apart until I've finished cataloguing."

A breath of a laugh from the windowsill. Almost fond. "Cataloguing."

"It's what I have."

* * *

So she catalogued.

The wardrobe held clothes in her size — in Margo Vale's size, and she refused to test whether those were the same size, except her eyes kept measuring anyway and reporting that yes, yes they were. A coat with chalk dust ground so deep into the seams it would never wash out. Boots worn at the outer heel, the way she wore her own boots now, the way she had always assumed came from Guild corridors.

On the nightstand, a journal. Locked. Small brass clasp, no key anywhere in the room, and she searched the room twice with a thoroughness that frightened her — under the mattress slats, behind the loose brick in the hearth (she knew about the loose brick; she had known about the loose brick before her fingers found it), beneath the floorboard by the washstand that sat a finger's width proud of its neighbors.

She knew the hiding places. That was the sentence she kept circling without landing on. *She knew the hiding places.* Not guessed them. Not deduced them, the way she could deduce most things. Knew them, in her hands, in the specific grammar of muscle and instinct that no forgery reaches.

The journal stayed locked. Some part of her was grateful. Whatever was written in it, she wasn't sure the woman who wrote it and the woman holding it could survive meeting just yet.

The last thing she examined was pinned to the wall above the desk, alone, apart from the working maps. A single sheet, older than the others, yellowed soft as cloth. Not a survey. A drawing — crude, a child's rendering done by a hand that had not yet learned to be precise. A house. A crooked tree. Two figures holding hands, stick-limbed, smiling in the way children draw smiles, all curve and no anatomy.

In the corner, in enormous childish letters:

*MARGO & ANSELM — OUR HOUSE — KARROW'S END*

Karrow's End. A village she had never heard of, in a country she had never seen, and grief arrived in her chest like water finding a crack — sudden, total, ahead of any explanation. She grieved for the house. For the tree. For the small hand that had drawn them, and the smaller hand beside it, and the boy who had grown into the man who died on her floor pressing a map into her arms as if it were the last warm thing in the world.

As if she were the last person in the world who might know what it was.

"Marguerite." Tobias was standing now. Careful. "What is it?"

"My brother," she said.

The word came out of her before any decision to speak it, before permission, rising from somewhere below the floor of her conscious mind like a drowned thing finally surfacing. She stared at the child's drawing and felt her identity split along a seam she hadn't known was there — Marguerite on one side, Margo on the other, and the tear running straight through the middle of her chest.

"I had a brother." Her voice was very steady, which is how she knew nothing was steady at all. "Or I didn't. One of those is true and I don't know which, and the ledgers won't tell me, because the ledgers are the ones that lied."

Below them, faint through the floorboards, the innkeeper was setting the common room to rights for the evening. Ordinary sounds. Dishes, a chair dragged, someone laughing. A country going about its evening while it forgot itself, and somewhere in that ordinary noise Marguerite heard — or imagined she heard, and could no longer tell the difference, and that inability was itself a kind of wound — a wind picking up beyond the shuttered window.

Not the ordinary wind.

The other one.

Tobias heard it too. He went still, head cocked, and his face did what faces do when hope and dread arrive together. "That's early," he said. "It shouldn't come for another—"

Outside, down the lane, a bell began to ring. Not alarm. Not celebration. The particular urgent cadence of a town calling everyone out of their houses — and beneath it, rising from the streets of Ashenwell, voices. Dozens of them. Hundreds. People pouring into the square below her window, and Marguerite leaned toward the cracked pane and saw them gathering in the dark, faces tilted upward, mouths open, and she understood before anyone explained it, because understanding had stopped requiring her consent hours ago.

They were reciting names. All of them, together, into the teeth of the coming wind — a town defending itself the only way the room's margins had ever taught anyone how.

*If we stop naming it, it stops. Keep naming.*

And in the crowd, at the edge of the lamplight, a figure stood who was not shouting with the others. A tall figure in traveling clothes gone gray with road dust, hood back, face upturned toward her window with an expression of such patient, terrible recognition that Marguerite's breath stopped against her ribs.

The figure raised one hand. Not a wave. Something older than a wave. A summons, or a greeting, or a warning.

Tobias followed her gaze and swore softly. "That's a Guild cloak," he whispered. "Under the dust. Marguerite — that's a Guild enforcer, and she's not here for the wind."

The figure mouthed something up at the window. No sound reached through the glass. But Marguerite read lips the way she read terrain, automatically, professionally, and the shape the stranger's mouth made was two syllables long, and both of them belonged to a name she had spent all evening failing to disown.

*Margo.*
