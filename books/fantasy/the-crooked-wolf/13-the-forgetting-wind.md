## 13. The Forgetting Wind

Tobias Fenwick had learned to read weather the way other men read faces, and what he read in the sky over Vellharrow that morning he did not like at all.

The clouds were moving wrong. Not fast, not slow — *wrong*, the way a clock is wrong when its hands tick backward. They came out of the east in long gray ribbons, and they came silently, without the usual argument of wind in the trees, as if the air itself had been told to keep quiet about something.

"Marguerite," he said. She was ahead of him on the road, the moving map open across her forearms like a bird she was trying not to startle. "Look up."

"I see it." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used when she was frightened and didn't want the fear to have edges. "It's coming down the valley. Toward Ashenwell."

"Toward us, you mean."

"That too."

They had been three days out of Ashenwell, walking north along a road that the map insisted did not exist until Marguerite looked at it, at which point it existed with a smugness Tobias found frankly offensive. The country around them was beautiful in the way of things that don't know they're dying: orchards heavy with late fruit, stone walls stitched across green hills, a river glinting off to the west where it had no business being, since the map showed no river there at all.

That river, as it turned out, was going to matter.

* * *

They took shelter in a barn belonging to a farm whose name, chalked on a board by the gate, was Harrow's End — though when Tobias asked Marguerite if she knew the place from before, she only frowned and said the name felt like a word on the tip of her tongue, familiar and unreachable. He had stopped asking her such questions. Each one seemed to cost her something.

The wind arrived an hour past noon.

It came through the gaps in the barn boards with a sound Tobias had never heard before and hoped never to hear again: not howling, but *reading*. A dry, papery whisper, page after page after page, as if something enormous were leafing through the world looking for a particular entry. The horses in the far stall pressed themselves against the wall and made no sound at all. Even the rats went quiet.

Marguerite stood in the middle of the barn floor with her arms wrapped around herself, and Tobias watched her hair lift in the draft and watched her lips move, silent, counting or praying or reciting — he couldn't tell which, and he was beginning to suspect she couldn't either.

"What's it doing?" he asked.

"Unwriting." She said it the way you'd say *drowning*. "Can't you feel it? It's going through everything. Checking. Striking things out."

The wind passed over them. Through them. Tobias braced for it to hurt, and it didn't — that was the worst part. It felt like nothing. Like being skimmed. Like a finger running down a list and pausing on your name just long enough to decide *not yet*.

Then it moved on, south and east, toward the village they'd walked through that morning, and the silence it left behind was worse than the sound.

* * *

They found the first thing missing within the hour.

Harrow's End's well stood in the farmyard, a low stone circle with a bucket on a chain, and when the farmer's wife drew water for them — a broad, kind woman named Bess who wouldn't take payment — the bucket came up empty. Not dry. Empty. She stared into it, laughed, and said the rope must be short, and dropped it again, and again it came up with nothing but a wet gleam on the boards, as if the water had been there a moment ago and had thought better of it.

"There's a spring under this yard," Bess said, and her voice changed halfway through the sentence, the certainty draining out of it. "There's — I've drawn from this well my whole life. My mother drew from it. There's a spring."

Tobias looked at Marguerite. Marguerite looked at the map.

She had it open on her knee by the gate, and her face, when he saw it, made his stomach drop. The map's ink was moving faster than he'd ever seen it — rivers of it, actual rivers, thinning and fading even as he watched, their blue going gray going transparent going gone, leaving blank paper where water had been drawn for — for however long the map had been drawing itself.

"The river," Marguerite whispered. "The one west of here. Look."

He looked. On the map, where the river had run, there was now a pale scar of nothing, and the fields on either side of it were beginning to fade too, their careful hatching dissolving like sugar in rain.

"It lost the river," she said. "The wind came through and the country let it go. And now—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Now the well has nothing to draw from, because upstream of every pipe and bucket and memory of water, there is no water anymore. It doesn't work forward, Tobias. It works *backward*. It takes the source and lets the rest dry up on its own."

Bess was still lowering the bucket. Up and down, up and down, with the doggedness of a woman who had done a thing ten thousand times and refused to be told otherwise. Tobias wanted to tell her to stop. He didn't know why. Something about the rhythm of it — the faith in it — made his chest ache.

"Bess," he said gently. "When did you last hear from your neighbors downriver? The mill folk?"

"What mill?" she said.

And then she stopped cranking, because she heard herself, and her face did something terrible and slow, like ice cracking on a pond.

* * *

The village of Lowmere — though by evening nobody could agree that it had ever been called anything — lay two miles downstream, and they reached it as the light was failing.

Or rather, they reached where it should have been.

The houses were still there. That was the horror of it, the thing Tobias knew he would carry for the rest of his life however long that turned out to be: the houses were *still there*. Stone walls, slate roofs, chimneys, a church with a bell in it. Doors stood open. Laundry hung on lines, gone stiff with evening cold. A cart sat in the lane with its shafts lowered, as if the horse had been led away five minutes ago.

But the millrace was dry. The millpond was a bowl of cracked mud with a boat sitting in the middle of it, high and helpless, listing against nothing. And the people —

The people were there too, and that was worse than if they hadn't been.

They moved slowly, the way people move in dreams. A man stood in his doorway holding a lantern that wasn't lit, looking out at the road with an expression of patient confusion, the look of someone waiting for a word that's on the tip of his tongue. A woman knelt in a garden pulling weeds from soil that had already gone gray and dusty, planting nothing, harvesting nothing, weeding out of pure habit because habit was all she had left. Children sat on a wall, quiet, not playing, not speaking, watching the road with eyes that had stopped expecting anything to come down it.

"They're not dead," Marguerite said beside him. Her voice was shaking. "That's the thing. They're not dead, they're *unwritten*. The wind took the river, and the river was the reason for the village, and the village was the reason for the name, and the name was the reason for — for them, in a way. People are stories too, Tobias. We're the stories we're part of. Take enough of the story away and—"

A man approached them. Older, stooped, with a woodcutter's shoulders and a face like a folded map himself, creased and faded. He came up the lane at that slow dream-pace, and stopped six feet away, and looked at them both with terrible politeness.

"You'll be wanting directions," he said. "Everyone who stops here wants directions. I'm afraid I can't help you. I can't remember where anything goes anymore. I can't remember—" His brow furrowed. "I had a trade. I know I had a trade. My hands remember it." He held them up, palms out, as if presenting evidence. "But the word for it is gone. Everything's going. First the water. Then the mill. Then—" He gestured at the village, at the laundry, at the children on the wall. "We wake up in the morning and something else is missing. Yesterday it was the bell. The bell used to ring. I'm sure it used to ring. Nobody rings it now because none of us can remember what it's *for*."

"What's your name?" Tobias asked.

The old man smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing Tobias had ever seen, because it was warm and human and utterly hollowed out, a lamp burning with no oil in it.

"That's the strange thing," he said. "I almost had it this morning. It was right there. Two syllables. Started with a sound like—" He made a shape with his mouth and nothing came out. He tried again. Nothing. He lowered his hands. "I've started writing it on things. So I won't lose it. But the writing fades too. Faster every day."

He showed them his forearm, where letters had been scratched into the skin with a knife, shallow and desperate. Most of them had healed smooth and blank. One remained, half-legible:

**TOB**

Tobias felt the ground tilt.

"That's not a name," he said quietly. "That's half of mine."

"Is it?" The old man peered at the letters with genuine curiosity. "Well. Then perhaps you knew me. Perhaps you could tell me who I am." He waited, hopeful, courteous, a man asking a stranger the time.

And Tobias opened his mouth to say *I don't know you, I've never been here, I came through this morning and you weren't — you were fine, you had a name, everyone had names* — and found that he couldn't. Because he *hadn't* known the man. He had walked through this village twelve hours ago and bought bread from a baker and joked with a child about a dog, and he had not learned a single person's name, because he had been in a hurry, because the map was moving, because Marguerite wanted to make distance before dark.

He had walked through a village full of people and treated them as scenery.

"No," he said. His voice came out rough. "I'm sorry. I don't know your name. I should have asked. I should have—"

"Tobias." Marguerite's hand closed on his arm. "Don't. Don't waste it here. We can't give back what the wind took by standing in the road feeling things."

"Can't we?"

"No." She said it flatly, and then, softer, with something breaking underneath the flatness: "I've tried. You don't understand — I've *tried*. Names are the only currency this country has left, and I—" She stopped herself. He saw her stop herself, saw the sentence die behind her teeth, and filed it away, because there was something in that unfinished sentence that mattered, something about what she had tried and what it had cost, and he was learning that Marguerite Dravenmoor's unfinished sentences were where she kept her worst truths.

The old man was still standing there, waiting politely to be told who he was.

Tobias reached out and gripped his forearm, over the carved letters, the way men grip arms when they mean a thing.

"Your name starts with Tob," he said. "Hold onto that. Write it bigger. Carve it deep. And when the wind comes again — and it will come again — you say it out loud. Every day. Scream it if you have to. Names are harder to take from a mouth that's using them. I don't know why I know that, but I know it."

Something flickered in the old man's eyes. Small. Faint. A coal remembering it had once been fire.

"Tob," he repeated. He said it like a man testing ice. Then again, firmer. "Tob. Yes. *Tob.*" He straightened, and for one moment the dream-slowness fell away from him and he was simply an old man standing in his own village, furious and alive. "Thank you," he said. "Whoever you are. Tell whoever sent you that we haven't finished yet."

* * *

They walked out of Lowmere in full dark, because there was no longer any point calling it anything, and camped in the lee of a drystone wall with no fire, because fires drew attention and attention was the one thing they could not afford.

Tobias lay on his back and watched the wrong clouds crawl east, and listened to Marguerite not sleeping beside him. He could always tell when she wasn't sleeping. She breathed like a woman doing sums.

"The wind's heading for Ashenwell," she said finally, into the dark.

"I know."

"And after Ashenwell, the archive-church. The priestess. All those names she keeps by saying them." A pause. "If the wind reaches her before we find what we're looking for—"

"We'll get there first."

"You don't know that."

"No," Tobias admitted. "But I know the old man back there carved half my name into his arm so he wouldn't lose himself, and I walked past him this morning without asking what it was. I'm not doing that twice. Whatever's left of this country when the wind gets done with it — somebody's going to be standing in it saying *here, I'm here, I'm still here* — and I want to be the kind of person who asks their name while there's still time."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had that careful flatness, the fear-without-edges.

"There's something you should know about the wind," she said. "Why it feels familiar to me. Why I said I'd tried to fight it before."

Tobias turned his head. In the starlight he could just see her profile, and the map, which she had not put away, lying open on her chest, its ink crawling in slow circles like something pacing a cage.

"When I was a girl," Marguerite Dravenmoor said, "in a country that doesn't exist — I remember the wind. I remember hearing it come down the valley. And I remember that it stopped. For years, it stopped, right at the borders, like a dog hitting the end of a chain. Someone built a fence it couldn't cross." Her breath caught. "Tobias, I think I know who built it. I think I might be able to feel the shape of the work. And I think whoever they were, they're dead now, or gone, or *unwritten* — and that's why the wind is loose."

The map shuddered under her hand. Its ink surged, all at once, every line on it straining toward the northeast — toward Ashenwell, toward the church, toward whatever waited there — and then, unmistakably, deliberately, it formed a single word in the margin, in handwriting Tobias recognized with a chill, because it matched the letters on the old man's arm and the maps in Margo Vale's room and, though he had never said so, the signature at the bottom of every Guild ledger he'd ever seen forged in his smuggling days:

***HURRY.***

Tobias sat up.

"Marguerite," he said. "The map's talking to us."

"No," she said, and her voice was very small. "It's talking to *me*. And I think I wrote it."
