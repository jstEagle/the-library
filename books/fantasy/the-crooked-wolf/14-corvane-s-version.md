## 14. Corvane's Version

The council chamber of the Guild of Impossible Things had no windows, which Corvane considered its finest feature. Windows admitted weather, and weather admitted opinion. The chamber admitted only the eleven members of the Inner Table and the matters they were sworn to weigh.

He stood at the head of the long table with the dead man's file open before him, though the file was thin — one page, mostly redactions, a name he had struck through himself years ago in ink that was supposed to be permanent. Guildmaster's privilege. Some entries were not for reading; they were only for keeping.

"Before we begin," Corvane said, "I want each of you to remember what this room is for. We do not decide what is true. Truth manages itself. We decide what is *recorded*, and recording, gentlemen and ladies, is the only immortality on offer to anything — person, place, or promise. When we unrecord a thing, we are not destroying it. We are declining to save it."

Old Mistress Halloway, who held the Chair of Debts, folded her hands. "You called an emergency session over a dead courier, Corvane. I trust there is more."

"There is always more," Corvane said. "There is more every single day. That is the burden of our office." He turned the file around so they could see the redaction. "A man named Aldous Fenwick entered our weighing house two nights ago and died on the floor of it, asking for the cartographer who remembers. Before dying, he passed her a document. I want to be precise: not a map. A document that behaves like a map. There is a difference, and the difference is why we are here."

* * *

He told it in order, because order was the discipline that separated the Guild from mere collectors of wonders. He told them about the ledger entry Marguerite Dravenmoor had flagged at dusk — a door sold twice, a small irregularity she had noted in her immaculate hand and set aside for morning review. He told them how the stranger had bled out across her clean floor, how the enforcers had raided her rooms within the day, and how she and a companion — Tobias Fenwick, brother to the dead man — had escaped through a door recorded but never built.

"That last part should interest you most," Corvane said. "She used our own records as a weapon. She found a door we catalogued and never constructed, and she walked through it. Which means either our records contain errors—" he let the word sit until several members shifted uncomfortably, "—or our records contain *inventory* someone has been drawing on without authorization. Both possibilities end in the same place. Someone inside this Guild has been keeping a second set of books."

Mistress Halloway again: "And your suspicion?"

Corvane did not answer immediately. He took from his coat a slim volume bound in grey calfskin, worn soft at the corners, and laid it on the table. It was not a Guild ledger. It was older than any of them, older than the table, possibly older than the room.

"This is the Erasure Schedule," he said. "Every country, city, or covenant we have ever declined to save. Forty-one entries. You will notice the pages are not numbered consecutively. That is deliberate. A schedule you can read straight through becomes a history, and histories invite grief, and grief invites reversal. We keep it fragmented so that no one member ever holds the whole shape of what we have done. Each of you knows your own decisions. None of you knows all of them. This is mercy, of a kind. Ours."

He opened it to a gap between entries — a razored stub where a page had been removed with a blade steady enough to leave a clean line.

"Entry thirty-one," Corvane said. "Vellharrow."

The name landed in the chamber like a stone into still water. He watched them each refuse, in their own fashion, to recognize it. Halloway's hands tightened. Young Master Quill, who held the Chair of Doors, actually said, "That country doesn't exist," with the flat confidence of a man reciting something he'd been taught rather than something he knew.

"No," Corvane agreed. "It doesn't. That was the point of the exercise, and by every measure available to us, the exercise succeeded. Vellharrow was scheduled for erasure forty-three years ago. The motion carried unanimously. The boundaries were drawn by our own hand, the borders ratified by this table, and the unwriting executed over eleven years by three generations of enforcers who were told they were performing routine boundary maintenance. Most of them died never knowing what they had done. That, too, was mercy."

"And now?" asked Master Dree, Chair of Bargains. His voice had gone careful. "Now there is a moving map in the possession of a junior cartographer, and the country's name in a dying man's mouth. What does that make the exercise?"

"Incomplete," Corvane said.

* * *

He walked them through it the way he had rehearsed it, because the truth required choreography if it was going to survive contact with eleven independent minds.

The problem with Vellharrow had never been the country itself. Countries were problems the way weather was a problem — vast, indifferent, survivable. The problem was what Vellharrow sat on top of: the confluence of four treaty-lines, three faiths, and one door — the last standing door out of a country that had spent two centuries being everyone's battlefield and no one's ally. The Crooked Wolf, the old texts called it. A door bent from being forced. Every power on the continent wanted it. Two wars had already been fought for the ground it stood on, and the third war — the one the auguries promised, the one with casualty projections that had made even the Chair of Debts go quiet — would have been fought over whether the door could be closed.

"So we closed it first," Corvane said. "Not the door. The *question*. You cannot fight a war over a country that no one can prove exists. We didn't kill Vellharrow. We made it unverifiable. And unverifiable things cannot be claimed, cannot be invaded, cannot be mourned publicly. The armies went home. The treaties lapsed into irrelevance. The auguries went quiet because auguries require an object, and we had removed the object." He tapped the razored stub. "Forty-three years of peace, purchased at the price of one nation's continuity. I have never lost sleep over it. I want that minuted."

"It will not be minuted," Halloway said dryly. "Nothing in this room is ever minuted."

"Then remember it," Corvane said, "which is the same thing."

But the chair at the far end of the table had been empty since the session began, and everyone had been carefully not looking at it, and finally Master Dree said what they were all circling: "The cartographer who drew the boundaries. Our finest apprentice. Where is she in all this?"

Corvane closed the Erasure Schedule.

"She is sitting in my holding room," he said, "under guard, having been taken on the salt flats yesterday with the moving map in her possession. And before anyone asks — yes. Marguerite Dravenmoor drew Vellharrow's borders when she was nineteen years old, with a precision this Guild has not seen since or matched. She then voted, at twenty-two, for its erasure. She then requested — insisted — that her own memory of both acts be sealed, along with everyone else's, because she argued — correctly, I might add — that a Guild full of people who remembered what they had done would eventually find a reason to undo it, and undoing it meant the war."

He let that settle. Let them do the arithmetic themselves: the finest apprentice, the perfect crime, the perfect amnesia, and now the perfect fugitive carrying the one artifact that could unravel everything.

"You sealed her memory," Halloway said slowly. "So she doesn't know what she did."

"No one knows what they did. That was the design. But memory is a debt, Mistress, and debts in this building have a way of coming due. Something called her back. A dying man with a map, a brother with questions, a country that apparently declined to finish forgetting itself. Whatever moved her onto those salt flats, it wasn't curiosity. Curiosity I could have managed. This looks like *recall*."

Master Quill leaned forward. "Then the question before this table is simple. Do we complete the erasure — take the girl, take the map, take the companion, and finish what we started forty-three years ago? Or do we—"

"There is no *or*," Corvane said, and his voice did not rise, which was how they knew he meant it. "There is no version of this Guild that survives Vellharrow's restoration. Not because of wars. Because of precedent. If a country can come back, then every erasure can come back. All forty-one entries. Every debt we declined to pay comes due on the same afternoon. Gentlemen, ladies — we are not guarding a secret. We are guarding the *concept* of finality itself. If finality fails here, it fails everywhere, and everything we are becomes a suggestion."

Silence, then. The good kind — the kind that meant the argument was over before it started, which was the only kind Corvane trusted.

"The vote," he said, "is a formality, but we will take it. All in favor of completing Entry Thirty-One — full erasure, all persons and artifacts associated, including the former apprentice — signify."

Eleven hands. Twelve, counting his own, raised around a table that had no windows and needed none.

"Carried," Corvane said. "I will lead the expedition personally. I want no intermediaries between this table and the work. Enforcers muster at dawn. We go through the salt flats, we recover the map, and we close the account."

He gathered his papers, and paused, one hand flat on the grey calfskin book, feeling through the leather the absence of the page he had cut out himself, decades ago, with a steadiness he had told himself was duty.

One thing he did not tell the council — one thing he would carry alone, because a Guildmaster's burden was precisely the weight no one else could be permitted to share:

When they had sealed Marguerite Dravenmoor's memory, she had made them seal one more thing alongside it. A condition, written in her nineteen-year-old hand at the bottom of the consent order, in ink that was supposed to be permanent: *If the country ever calls me back, I am to be allowed to answer.*

Corvane had signed beneath it. He had always assumed the clause was sentiment — a child's hedge against regret.

Standing in the windowless chamber, listening to eleven hands fall away from the table, he understood for the first time that it might have been something else entirely. A clause like that wasn't sentiment. A clause like that was a *door*, recorded but never built.

And somewhere out past the salt flats, in a country that refused to die quietly, his finest apprentice was already walking through it.
