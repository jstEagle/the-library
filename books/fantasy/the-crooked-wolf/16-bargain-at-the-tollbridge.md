## 16. Bargain at the Tollbridge

*Tobias*

The river appeared an hour before the bridge did.

Tobias noticed it first as a sound — a low, patient rushing somewhere to their left, like a crowd murmuring behind a wall. Then the ground began to tilt toward it, gently, the way a table tilts when a leg is short, and Marguerite stopped walking so abruptly that he nearly collided with her pack.

"That wasn't there yesterday," she said.

"Which part?"

"Any of it." She crouched at the lip of the slope and studied the water below with the expression she wore when reading a ledger — narrowed eyes, lips slightly parted, one finger tracing a line in the air as if following columns only she could see. "Rivers don't just arrive, Tobias. They're born in high country and they run downhill and they take centuries deciding where they belong. This one has decided overnight."

"Maybe it's in a hurry."

"Everything here is in a hurry." She stood, shouldered her pack, and started down the slope without waiting for him. That was Marguerite all over: fear converted instantly into forward motion, grief compressed into footprints. He'd known her three weeks now — since the rain, since the alley behind the weighing house where he'd put a knife against her ribs and she hadn't even flinched, just said *you want the map or you want answers, pick one* — and in three weeks he had learned exactly two things about her for certain. She never slept past dawn. And she lied the way other people breathed: constantly, fluently, and without any sign of effort.

He was fairly sure she'd started telling him the truth somewhere around Ashenwell. He was less sure she knew the difference anymore.

The bridge, when they reached it, was the wrong kind of old.

Not ruined-old, not moss-and-crumbling-old, but *kept*-old, swept-old, the way a church is old. Its stones were pale as teeth and fitted so precisely that the mortar lines looked drawn rather than built. A single lantern hung from an iron post at its center, burning with no visible flame, giving light the color of weak tea. And standing beneath the lantern, hands folded on the top of a walking stick, was a figure that might once have been a man.

It was tall and thin as a fence post, wrapped in a coat of grey felt gone soft with age, and its face —

Tobias made himself look at the face. He'd learned that much in Vellharrow. You looked at the faces, because the ones you didn't look at were the ones that followed you.

The face was smooth. Not young-smooth — *unfinished*-smooth, like wax before a seal is pressed into it. There were the suggestions of features, a nose's shadow, the dimples where eyes would go, but nothing committed. It was a face that had been paid away, or never collected, and what remained was a kind of polite absence.

"Travelers," it said. The voice came from everywhere and nowhere in particular, the way a bell's voice belongs to the whole bell. "The crossing is open."

Marguerite stopped three paces from the bridge's near end. Tobias came up beside her and kept his hand loose near his belt, though he suspected a knife would do about as much good here as a strongly worded letter.

"What's the toll?" Marguerite asked. Direct. No haggling posture, no feigned ignorance. He admired that about her even while it made his teeth ache. In his experience the fastest way to be robbed was to ask a keeper what something cost.

"The toll," said the figure, "is a memory."

The word dropped into the air between them and sat there.

"A memory," Marguerite repeated.

"One. Freely given. The bridge does not take; it receives." The unfinished face tilted slightly, and Tobias had the horrible sense that it was smiling, or performing the shape a smile leaves behind. "You will know which memory it wants when you offer. Some are too heavy. Some are too light. The bridge weighs."

"Of course it does," Marguerite said under her breath. "Everything in this country weighs."

Tobias stepped forward before he could think better of it, which was, he reflected later, the story of his entire life.

"How much for two crossings?" he asked.

"Two memories. One from each."

"And if we refuse?"

The figure turned its blank face toward him with unhurried courtesy. "Then you may walk upstream to the ford at Marrowfen. It is four days' walk, and the ford has been unwritten since the spring wind, and the water there does not remember being crossed, and neither, afterward, will you." A pause. "But the toll is fair. It always has been. I have crossed this bridge myself, in both directions, more times than I can recall." Another pause, longer. "That is not an expression. I cannot recall."

Something in Tobias's chest went cold and quiet. He thought of the stranger — Callum, dead in Marguerite's rooms with a bloodstained map pressed into her hands, whispering a country's name like a prayer or an apology. Callum had crossed this bridge. Tobias was almost sure of it. Callum had come out of Vellharrow carrying nothing but the map and a wound that wouldn't close, and if he'd paid a memory at the water, maybe the memory he'd paid was the one that would have told him how to survive what came after.

Or maybe the bridge took the memory of how to die, and that was worse.

"I'll pay," Tobias said. "Both of us. Take mine first."

"Tobias—" Marguerite's voice cracked like a whip, low and furious.

"You're the cartographer. You need your head full of roads and borders and whatever else is rattling around in there. I need—" He gestured vaguely at himself. "Less."

"You don't get to decide what you can spare. Nobody does. That's the whole trick of it." Her eyes had gone bright and hard, and he realized with a small shock that she was afraid — actually afraid, not for herself. For him. When had that happened? "What are you going to offer? Think carefully. Think about what a man like you is mostly made of."

He didn't have to think. That was the terrible part. He'd been thinking about it since the figure finished speaking, some practical clerk-part of his mind sorting through the inventory of himself and marking items: *this one, this one is expendable.*

"My childhood," he said.

The word came out steady. He was almost proud of it.

"Before I met Callum. All of it. The farm, my mother's kitchen, the dog, the winter the well froze, all of it. It's twenty years of nothing that ever helped anybody. I don't use it. It just sits there taking up room and making me sad on rainy nights. Take the whole crate."

The figure regarded him. The lantern flickered, or seemed to — the flameless light bending toward him like grass toward wind.

"The bridge accepts bulk," it said. "Though it prefers precision. A childhood is not one memory. It is ten thousand wearing a single coat."

"Then take the coat."

"*No.*"

Marguerite moved between him and the bridge so fast he didn't see it happen — one moment she was beside him, the next she was a wall of grey wool and fury, her back to the keeper, her face inches from his chest, her finger jabbing up at him like a stylus coming down on a ledger line.

"You absolute fool. You beautiful, useless fool. Do you know what you're offering? You're offering the reason you flinch at church bells. You're offering the reason you eat bread crust-first because someone taught you the ends were the best part. You're offering every dream you've ever had that smelled like a kitchen you can't find anymore." Her voice shook. "I've watched you sleep, Tobias. You talk in your sleep. You say a name — *Mara* — and you smile. If you sell your childhood, who is Mara? Who holds her? Where does she live when there's no room left?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Somewhere inside him, a door he hadn't known was load-bearing quietly buckled.

"She's my sister," he said finally. "She died when I was nine. Fever."

"And you'll trade her. For a bridge crossing." Marguerite turned to face the keeper, and Tobias saw her square her shoulders the way she did before lying to Guildmasters. "I'll pay. Both crossings. From me."

"The toll is one from each," the figure said, mild as milk. "The bridge does not accept substitution. Debts are not transferable. That is the first law of—"

"Then hear me out." She lifted her chin. "I'm a ledger-keeper by trade. Eleven years with the Guild of Impossible Things. I know how bargains work, and I know that 'one memory each' is a *formulation*, and formulations can be renegotiated by parties with standing. So here is my offer, and I'll make it only once." She drew a breath, and Tobias watched her reach deep into herself for something — he could see the reaching, like watching someone draw a knife across their own palm. "I have a secret. One secret, kept so long and so carefully that it isn't a memory anymore. It's a *load*. I carry it the way a mule carries ore. It has weight, it has shape, it has years of interest accrued. It is worth more than a childhood, because a childhood is only what happened to you, and a secret is what you *did*."

The lantern-light bent toward her.

"The bridge weighs," the figure said slowly. "Yes. Yes, it does."

"Take my secret. Both tolls. Weigh it yourself."

"No—" Tobias grabbed her arm. "Marguerite, you don't even know what it is yet. Whatever it is — you said you don't remember what you—"

"I know exactly what it is," she said, and did not look at him, and that frightened him more than the blank-faced keeper had. "I know its weight. I've carried it blind for eleven years. Let them weigh it."

The figure was silent for a long moment. The river rushed beneath the stones, patient as arithmetic.

"It is irregular," the keeper said at last. "But the bridge was built irregularly. Very well. Approach."

*Marguerite*

Later — much later, on the far bank, with the bridge behind them and her hands still trembling — Marguerite would try to reconstruct what had happened on those pale stones, and find that the reconstruction refused to hold.

Here is what she could hold:

She walked onto the bridge. The stones were warm underfoot, blood-warm, which was wrong and she chose not to think about it. The keeper stood aside with the courtesy of a servant opening a door. The lantern swung gently though there was no wind.

"Name what you offer," the keeper said.

"I offer my secret," she said. "The one I keep. The heavy one. The one I signed."

Even saying it aloud was strange — she didn't know what she'd signed, only that somewhere in the sealed vaults of herself there was a document with her name on it and a weight attached, a black stone she'd swallowed whole, and everyone who looked at her too long seemed to see the shape of it through her skin.

"Bare the weight," said the keeper.

And the bridge *reached into her*.

There was no gentler way to describe it, and Marguerite was a professional describer. It was not pain. It was inventory. Something vast and patient paged through her — not her memories, not exactly, but the *ledger of her*, the hidden accounts, the entries she'd locked and walled and plastered over — and she stood perfectly still and let it read, because she had offered, and an offered thing must be allowed to be taken, and because some starving, buried part of her wanted desperately to know what was written there.

The reading lasted a moment or a year.

Then the bridge paused — she felt it pause, a held breath in the stones — over one entry. One sealed page. And she heard, or imagined she heard, or perhaps the bridge simply let her hear what it weighed:

A signing desk. Wax, cooling. An order bearing her own hand.

And underneath it, fainter, almost lost: *a copy. Keep one copy. Hide it where they'll never look — hide it in motion.*

The bridge withdrew.

"That," said the keeper, and its unfinished face turned toward her with something that in a finished face would have been wonder, "is very heavy."

"Is it enough?"

"For two crossings?" The keeper considered. "For two crossings and change. But the bridge takes only what is offered, and you offered the secret entire." The blank face inclined. "It is taken. Both tolls are paid. Cross."

And Marguerite felt it go.

Not ripped — *released*, the way a knot releases, the way a held breath releases. Eleven years of carrying, and suddenly her chest was — lighter. Cleaner. Emptier. She could breathe in a way she hadn't breathed since she was a girl, since before the Guild, since before—

Since before what?

She stood on the warm stones and reached for the secret, the way you reach for a tooth that's been pulled, probing the gap. Nothing. No desk. No wax. No order. Just a smooth place where the weight had been, and a faint afterimage, already fading, like the ghost of a number you'd been staring at all day.

She couldn't remember what she'd paid.

She remembered paying. She remembered the reaching-in, the reading, the pause over the sealed page. But the page itself was gone, and with it — she understood with a vertiginous lurch — whatever the page had contained. Whatever she'd done. Whoever she'd been when she did it.

"Marguerite." Tobias's voice, from the near end of the bridge, tight with alarm. "Hey. Hey. What happened? What did it take?"

She turned. Her legs carried her off the bridge on their own, one step and another, until she stood beside him on the far bank and the river rushed behind them like applause or like warning.

"I don't know," she said honestly, which was becoming an unpleasant habit. "I paid. It's gone. I can feel the hole where it was, but the hole doesn't have a shape anymore." She laughed — a short, unsteady sound that surprised them both. "Eleven years I carried it. I built my whole life around its weight. And now it's gone and I don't even miss it, because missing requires remembering, and—"

She stopped.

Because something else was surfacing in the emptied space. Not the secret itself — that was gone, spent, sunk into the bridge like a coin into dark water. But the edges of it. The residue. Fragments too small to be memories, clinging like silt:

Wax, cooling. The smell of sealing-wax, specific and sudden, as if someone had broken a stick of it under her nose.

Her own handwriting — not seen, but *felt*, the muscle-memory of it, the particular pressure of a signature written slowly, formally, the way one signs things that matter.

And a phrase, arriving whole, in her own voice, from somewhere impossibly far inside:

*Keep one copy. Hide it in motion.*

"Tobias," she said quietly. "When we get wherever we're going, I need you to watch me. If I start doing anything strange with paper — anything at all — don't stop me."

"Why? What does that mean?"

"I don't know." She looked back at the bridge. The keeper still stood beneath its tea-colored lantern, and as she watched, it raised one thin hand — not in farewell, she thought, but in the gesture of a bookkeeper closing a folio. "I paid a secret I can't remember for a crossing I barely needed, and all I got in change was an instruction I don't understand."

She turned east, toward the mountains, toward whatever the moving map was pulling them toward now. The ink had shifted again in the night — she'd checked at dawn — and the route it drew no longer ran along roads. It ran along rivers. Along this river, in fact, upstream, into the high country, toward a mark that was not a town or a ruin or a border, but a small crooked symbol she'd never seen on any map in her life.

It looked, she thought, like a door. Drawn badly. Drawn *bent*.

"Come on," she said. "We're losing daylight, and this country loses faster than we do."

They walked. Behind them the lantern burned on, and the keeper of the tollbridge stood alone on the stones with its new possession, and if it whispered anything to the river, the river kept it the way rivers keep everything — moving, always moving, toward the sea.
