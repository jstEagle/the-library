## 18. The Crooked Wolf Legend

Tobias Fenwick had never trusted churches, and the Church of Recorded Names was doing nothing to improve his opinion of the breed.

It wasn't the blind priestess. Sera Venn — she'd introduced herself with a bow so shallow it was almost an insult, as if her eyes had once done all her bowing for her — had been nothing but kind since they'd arrived at dusk, feeding them barley soup thick enough to stand a spoon in and asking no questions that mattered. It was the building itself. Every church Tobias had ever entered had been built to make him feel small before something large. This one was built to make him feel *unrecorded*.

The walls were shelves. Floor to vaulted ceiling, every surface, crammed not with books but with slats of pale wood, each one burned with a name in a hand too small to read from more than a foot away. Hundreds of thousands of them. Millions, maybe. The air smelled of cedar and tallow, and beneath both, faintly, of dust that had given up being anything else.

"They're names," Marguerite said quietly. She stood with her fingers hovering an inch from the nearest shelf, not touching. She hadn't touched anything since Ashenwell's innkeepers had called her Margo Vale, and Tobias had begun to notice that about her — when the world pressed too close, she retreated into observation, precise as a woman taking bearings.

"Every person born in Vellharrow," Sera Venn said. "Or nearly. The recitation never stops." She gestured with her chin toward the nave, where three robed figures sat in a circle around a single candle, their lips moving in slow unison. "Dawn shift, day shift, night shift. When one tires, another begins. If a name goes unspoken past a certain span of days—" She shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "You've seen what happens past a certain span of days."

Tobias thought of the village on the road out of the flats, the one where the river had gone overnight. Where the innkeeper had stared at his own hands as though trying to remember whose they were. His stomach did something complicated.

"And it works?" he asked. "Talking at them keeps them—"

"It works the way a dam works against the sea." The priestess smiled without much humor. "But dams matter. Ask anyone downstream."

Marguerite turned from the shelf. "You said you knew why we came. You said it before I told you anything."

"I said I knew why people come." Sera Venn folded her hands. Her eyes, milky-white and steady, found Tobias with unsettling accuracy. "People come to this church for three reasons. To be remembered, to remember someone, or to ask about the Wolf." She tilted her head. "You two are the third kind. You have the look of it. The map-look."

"The map-look?"

"People who've been navigating by something that shouldn't work." She said it mildly, the way another woman might say *you've been walking in the rain*. "Come. Sit. If you're asking about the Crooked Wolf, you should hear it properly, because everyone hears it wrong the first time, and wrong versions get people killed."

*Wrong versions already got someone killed*, Tobias thought, and didn't say. Dell. His friend Dell, who had bled out on the Guild weighing-house floor pressing a moving map into a stranger's hands, whispering a country's name like a prayer he didn't believe in anymore.

They sat on benches near the recitation circle. The candle between the three murmuring priests threw long shadows up the name-shelves, so that the whole nave seemed to lean over them, listening.

Sera Venn sat facing them, back straight, hands loose in her lap.

"First thing," she said. "There is no wolf."

Tobias waited. Beside him, Marguerite went very still — the stillness she got when facts were rearranging themselves, which he was learning to read on her like weather.

"There is no wolf," the priestess repeated, "and there never was. No beast, no spirit, no guardian hound of the old kings. That story is what the story became when people who needed it to be a beast carried it out of the country and told it badly." Her mouth curved. "Words wander. Especially words about doors."

"Doors," Marguerite said.

"The Crooked Wolf is a door." Sera Venn pronounced it plainly, without ceremony, which somehow made it worse. "A standing door in a standing frame, out past the salt flats, in a valley that most maps of the world decline to acknowledge. It is old — older than Vellharrow, possibly older than the idea of countries. And it is crooked because it was opened wrongly, once, long ago."

"Opened wrongly how?"

"Not by its keeper. Not by invitation. Forced." She let the word sit. "A door like that isn't carpentry. It's a promise. A door says: here is an edge, and here is the other side of the edge, and crossing is possible because both sides agree to it. When you force such a door — break the agreement — the door doesn't heal. Doors don't heal. It bends, the way a bone bends, and stays bent, and everything it touches afterward carries the bend in it."

Tobias found himself leaning forward despite himself. "And that's why it's called—"

"A crooked wolf? Because the men who forced it were soldiers, and soldiers see teeth in everything. They came through it expecting conquest and found a country, and the country looked back at them, and what came out of the dark of the doorway to meet them — in their telling — had teeth." She shook her head slowly. "It was probably just wind. Or it was probably the country itself, deciding what shape to wear for strangers. Either way, they ran, and they named what chased them, because naming is how frightened men stop running. And the name stuck to the door like a burr."

*Myth becoming itinerary*, some dry corner of Tobias's mind supplied, and he almost laughed. Dell would have liked that phrase. Dell, who had spent three years collecting stories about the Wolf in dockside taverns, sorting them like a man panning gold — discarding the beast-stories, the ghost-stories, the ones where it granted wishes — hunting always for the grain of repeatable fact underneath. *It's a way out, Toby. Everyone agrees it's a way out. Everything else is decoration.*

He missed Dell so suddenly and completely that the candle seemed to dim.

"You said it was guarded," Marguerite said. Her voice had changed — lower, faster. Tobias recognized the tone. It was the voice she used for ledgers, for entries that balanced. She was fitting this into accounts somewhere behind her eyes. "By whatever remains of the country's will. That's what you said when we met. What does that mean? What remains?"

Sera Venn was quiet for a moment. In the circle, the three reciters reached the end of a roll of names; one of them turned the slat-board with practiced economy and began again, unhurried, tireless, like water finding its level.

"When the erasure started," the priestess said at last, "the Guild didn't burn anything. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. They did something cleaner. They persuaded the country to forget itself — or they persuaded enough of it, and let forgetting spread like rot through wet timber. Rivers forgot their courses. Roads forgot their destinations. People—" she touched her own breastbone briefly, "—people forgot hardest of all."

"But not everything forgot."

"No. Not everything. Some things held. A stone that has stood in a wall for six hundred years remembers being a wall, even if it forgets the house. And the door — the Crooked Wolf — held better than anything, because it was never only Vellharrow's. It belonged to the edge itself, and the Guild couldn't persuade an edge to forget having two sides." She spread her hands. "So whatever will the country still has — whatever part of it refuses, still, after all these years, to lie down — that will gathers where the gathering place is. At the door. Guarding it. Testing whoever comes."

"Testing them how?"

"That depends on what the country thinks it needs." Sera Venn's white eyes were unreadable. "Sometimes the door asks riddles. Sometimes it asks for memories. Once — this was before my time — it asked a man for his shadow, and gave it back to him forty years later, worn thin as gauze. But lately, in my lifetime, it has asked only one thing, always the same thing."

Tobias realized he was holding his breath.

"It asks for a true name," the priestess said. "The true name of the one who opens it. Not the name on your letters. Not the name your mother used. The name that would survive if every other record of you burned — the one word that is genuinely, irreducibly yours. Give it, and the door opens, and you pass, and the name stays behind with the country. Walk away, and the door stays shut, and you keep yourself whole." She paused. "Most people, hearing that price, decide they weren't leaving after all."

The silence stretched. Somewhere high in the vaults, a slat creaked as the building settled, and a hundred thousand small wooden names seemed to murmur in response.

Tobias looked at Marguerite.

She was staring at the recitation circle, but he could tell she wasn't seeing it. She was seeing something else — something internal and vast, the way she'd looked at the moving map on its first night, when the ink crawled and reshaped and she'd said, half to herself, *it's not showing me roads, it's showing me intentions.* Her hand had drifted, without her seeming to know it, to rest flat against her own sternum. Over her heart. Over, perhaps, wherever a person keeps the word that would survive a fire.

*Margo Vale,* Tobias thought. *Or Marguerite Dravenmoor. Or neither. Or both.*

She had denied the name at the inn. He had watched her deny it — watched her lie with that same ledgerkeeper steadiness, watched something flicker behind her eyes that was either guilt or grief, and he still didn't know which, and the not-knowing had been sitting in his chest for days like a swallowed hook. Dell had known. Dell had recognized her face on sight and said *find the cartographer who remembers*, and Tobias had followed that instruction like a compass bearing ever since, and now the trail was bending toward a door that ate names, and he still didn't know what she was remembering or refusing to.

He should ask her. Tonight. Before they walked out onto the flats with a legend wearing a map's clothing.

Before he could, Sera Venn rose.

"You'll want to leave at first light," she said. "Not because the distance is great — it isn't, if you walk honestly. But the flats change, past the last village, and the changes favor early travelers. Also—" she hesitated, and for the first time the blind woman looked uncertain, "—there is a storm coming. Not a rainstorm."

Marguerite stood. "The wind."

"You've felt it, then."

"We've *seen* it." Tobias's voice came out harder than he intended. "We saw what it left. A village that lost its river in a night. An old man who couldn't finish his own name."

"Then you understand the deadline." Sera Venn nodded slowly. "The recitations hold the town. Chalk and habit hold some of the outlying farms. Nothing holds the open country. Every day, there is less of Vellharrow to stand in, and the door stands in what remains. Eventually—" she made a small gesture, closing a book that wasn't there, "—the last page. So if you mean to reach the Crooked Wolf, go now, and go knowing what you'll pay, and go knowing that the door may refuse you."

"It won't refuse us." Marguerite said it quietly, but with total certainty, and Tobias heard something in it that raised the hair on his arms. Not confidence. Recognition. As if she were saying *I've been there before* — which was impossible, because she had never set foot in this country, except that the innkeepers of Ashenwell disagreed, and the boarding room with her handwriting disagreed, and the unfinished maps in the drawer disagreed, and every hour the disagreement got louder.

"How do you know?" Tobias asked her.

She didn't answer. She was looking at the name-shelves again, at the millions of small burned words, and her expression had gone somewhere he couldn't follow.

Sera Venn walked them to the church doors herself. Outside, Ashenwell lay under a sky bruised purple at the edges, stars coming out over rooftops that existed tonight and might not next month. The priestess pressed something into Tobias's hand — a slat of pale wood, smooth, blank.

"For the journey," she said. "If the wind comes for you, speak any name into it. Yours. A friend's. The country doesn't care whose — a spoken name is a spoken name, and it buys moments. And if you reach the door—" her grip tightened on Tobias's wrist, brief and fierce, "—remember that it is crooked for a reason. Bent things are not broken things. There is a difference, and the country is betting everything that you'll understand it."

They walked back through Ashenwell's empty streets toward the inn. Lamps guttering. Somewhere, faintly, from the direction of the archive-church, the night-shift recitation beginning again, patient as tide.

Tobias turned the blank slat over in his fingers and made himself say it.

"Margo Vale."

She stopped walking. Didn't turn. The lamplight put her shadow long across the cobbles, pointing east, toward the flats, toward the door.

"Dell knew you," Tobias said. "On sight. He sent me to find 'the cartographer who remembers,' and I've been following that ever since, and I'm done pretending I haven't noticed that this country remembers you better than you do. Tomorrow we walk into a place that takes true names as payment. So tonight, before the road, you tell me which one is real — the name you carry or the name they buried — because I can't walk into that valley trusting a stranger I've bled for."

Marguerite stood very still. When she finally spoke, her voice was steady as a ruled line, and it frightened him more than shouting would have.

"Tobias. I don't know yet. But I think—" her hand rose again to her chest, that unconscious gesture, "—I think the map does."

Behind her, above the rooftops of Ashenwell, the bruised sky split with silent heat-lightning, and far off across the darkening salt flats, something answered: a low, resonant groan, like a hinge the size of the horizon turning over in its sleep.

Neither of them mentioned it. Neither of them slept.
