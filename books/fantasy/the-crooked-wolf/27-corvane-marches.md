## 27. Corvane Marches

Corvane had not left the Guildhall in eleven years.

He knew the number precisely because he had written it down himself, in his own ledger, in his own hand: *Departed the Hall for the last time, autumn of the Year of Salted Fields. Reason: none required.* That was the thing about being Guildmaster — you stopped needing reasons. You only needed entries.

Now he stood at the Weighing House door with a traveling cloak on and forty enforcers mustered in the yard behind him, and he found that the old habit held. He did not need a reason. But he wanted one, and wanting was worse.

"You could send us," said Deputy Marrin, beside him. She had her hood up against the drizzle and her ledger already open, as if the march itself were an entry to be recorded in real time. "Forty is more than sufficient. The country is mostly gone. What's left of it can't fight."

"That," Corvane said, "is exactly why I'm going."

He let that sit in the rain between them for a moment, then stepped out into the yard. Forty faces turned toward him. He had trained most of them personally. He had signed their commissions, set their wages, written the eulogies of the nine who had died on Guild service in the last decade. He knew each name the way a carpenter knows his tools — by weight, by wear, by the sound they made when used correctly.

He did not tell them where they were going.

That was the first lie of the expedition, and it would not be the last, and Corvane had made his peace with lies the way a ferryman makes peace with water: not fondly, but daily.

* * *

The road south passed through country that remembered them.

He noticed it at the second night's camp. A scout came back from the ridgeline reporting a village below with lights in its windows — a village, Corvane knew, that had been struck from the record thirty years ago. Not destroyed. Struck. There is a difference, and only people who work in ledgers understand how violent the difference is. Destroying a place leaves ruins. Erasing one leaves a hole shaped like everything that used to be there, and holes have a way of filling themselves when no one is watching.

"Name of the village?" he asked the scout.

The scout frowned. "It didn't — I didn't see a sign, sir."

Of course it didn't. Signs were the first thing to go. Names went next, then roads, then rivers, then the memories of anyone foolish enough to have loved the place. The Guild's method was patient as rot. It had taken Vellharrow apart over decades, page by page, and it had nearly finished. Nearly.

Nearly was the word that had put Corvane on this road.

* * *

Here was what he told the council, and here was what was true, and here was the space between those two things where he lived:

He told them that Dravenmoor had gone rogue, stolen Guild property, crossed into restricted territory, and was attempting an unauthorized restoration. All true.

He told them she was compromised, possibly coerced, certainly deceived by the moving map's design. Probably false. He suspected she had never been clearer about anything in her life.

He told them that restoring Vellharrow would resurrect the border war, the succession claims, the three powers who had spent a generation learning to live without the country between them. True — or true enough. The war had been unwinnable. He had read the casualty projections himself, in his apprentice days, before he'd been given the authority to act on them. Forty thousand dead in the first year, the assessors wrote, and the numbers after that grew less like arithmetic and more like weather.

What he did not tell them: that he had voted for the erasure too. That the order bore four signatures, and one of them belonged to a young man named Aldous Corvane who had believed, sincerely, that he was saving lives. That he still believed it. That belief, sustained across thirty years, becomes indistinguishable from identity, and a man will defend his identity the way he defends his own throat.

And what he did not tell even himself, not in words, though it walked beside him the whole march like a third deputy: that Marguerite Dravenmoor was the finest cartographer the Guild had ever produced, and that he had let her carry the guilt of Vellharrow alone because watching her carry it was easier than carrying it himself.

* * *

They crossed the border on the fourth day.

There was nothing to cross. That was the uncanny part. No wall, no marker, no customs post — just a stretch of ordinary moorland where the grass changed its mind about which direction the wind bent it, and suddenly every enforcer in the column felt the particular lightness of walking on unrecorded ground. Corvane saw Marrin stumble, catch herself, and look down at her own boots as if they had betrayed her.

"The ground isn't holding our weight properly," Marrin said.

"The ground is fine. The records aren't." Corvane checked his compass out of habit. The needle spun once, twice, and settled pointing at him. He closed the case. "From here on, we navigate by ledger. Nothing else out here is reliable."

They made camp in the lee of a hill that appeared on no map, because there were no maps. Around the fires, the younger enforcers were quiet in the wrong way — not disciplined quiet, but the quiet of people trying not to hear something. One of them, a boy called Pell, barely twenty, kept looking north toward the horizon as if expecting the dark to arrive early.

Corvane sat across the fire from him. "Something troubling you, Ensign?"

"No, sir." A pause. The boy was honest to the bone; it was his defining flaw. "Only — sir, I think I had an aunt from around here. And I can't remember her name, and I can't remember her face, but I remember that I remembered. Does that make sense?"

Corvane looked into the fire for a long moment.

"It makes perfect sense," he said. "Get some sleep."

*That*, he thought, walking back to his own tent. *That is what we are marching to defend.* An ensign with a hole in his memory shaped like an aunt. A country full of holes shaped like everything. He had told himself for thirty years that erasure was painless because it was clean — that a debt removed from the ledger harmed no one, since no one remembered being owed. He had known better. He had always known better. Clean was just a word for wounds you couldn't see.

He lay awake listening to the wind come off the moor, and the wind carried sounds that had no right to exist: a bell, distantly. A dog. Someone singing in a language the Guild had struck from its language registers in the Year of Salted Fields, and which Corvane understood anyway, because he had learned it once, before, when he was young and the borders were still drawn and someone in Ashenwell had taught him a drinking song he now could not stop hearing.

*The crooked wolf, the crooked wolf,* the song went, *who swallowed the sun and spat out the stars—*

"Damn it," Corvane said softly, to no one, and finally slept.

* * *

On the fifth day they found the first village that was fighting back.

It stood in a river valley that officially contained no river, and the river ran through it anyway, bright and insolent. The houses stood. Smoke rose from chimneys. And along every doorpost, every fence, every flat surface, chalk marks covered everything — names, thousands of names, written and rewritten until the chalk dust lay on the ground like snow.

Marrin rode up beside him. "Someone's been maintaining them."

"Someone has been maintaining them for years." Corvane dismounted and read the nearest doorpost. A woman's name. Below it, a man's. Below that, children. Below that, in smaller letters, as if the writer had run out of confidence but not conviction: *the miller's family, all six, and the dog whose name was Bristle.*

He recognized the handwriting. Of course he did. He had seen it in the Guild archives a hundred times, on survey charts and boundary drafts and one sealed confession locked in the deepest drawer of the records room. Ilsa Fenwick. The stranger's sister. The second cartographer, keeping a country alive by force of habit and chalk.

"She's doing our work in reverse," Marrin said. There was something almost like admiration in her voice, quickly suppressed.

"She's doing *my* work in reverse," Corvane said, "badly, beautifully, and thirty years too late — except it isn't too late, is it? That's the problem." He touched the chalk letters. They came off on his glove, white as bone. "Erasure isn't a state, Marrin. It's a process. And processes run both ways."

"Then we finish it."

"We will." He heard his own voice do the thing it did in council chambers — settle, flatten, become authoritative. He hated that he could still do it on demand. "But understand what we're finishing. Look at this valley. This is what restoration looks like. This is what Dravenmoor wants to give them back." He turned in a slow circle, taking in the river, the smoke, the names. "A living country means a disputed border. A disputed border means mobilization on three fronts. Three fronts means the war we erased this place to prevent. Every name on this doorpost is a soldier who dies in year one. That is the arithmetic, and no amount of chalk changes it."

Marrin was silent. Then: "And if the arithmetic is wrong?"

"Then I have marched forty people into a foreign country to murder a river," Corvane said, "and I will write that in my ledger myself."

* * *

The resistance began small, as such things do.

A bridge they needed was simply not there in the morning, though it had been there at dusk, and the scouts swore the gorge had not existed either. A pass closed itself with rockfall no one heard. Their supply wagons lost their axle-grease to something that ate it overnight, leaving forty men and women with carts that screamed like living things. And everywhere, everywhere, the landscape rearranged itself with the sullen patience of a debtor who knows exactly what he is owed.

Corvane began to suspect the truth on the sixth night, and confirmed it on the seventh: the country wasn't resisting them randomly. It was resisting them *specifically*. His tent leaked while others stayed dry. His compass pointed at him. The singing followed his column, staying just beyond the firelight, and the songs were ones he knew.

"It recognizes you, sir," Pell said, the honest boy, unable to help himself. They stood together at the perimeter, listening to the distant bell that rang from a tower no survey had ever recorded. "The land. It knows you're here."

"Then it remembers more than it should," Corvane said. "Which means someone has been feeding it. Which means Dravenmoor is closer than my scouts report, or the priestess in Ashenwell is busier than we thought, or—" He stopped. He had been about to say *or the country itself has decided to wake up,* and he found he could not say it aloud, because saying it aloud made it an entry, and entries had weight, and weight had consequences, and he was not yet ready to be the Guildmaster who recorded the awakening of a dead nation.

"Yes, sir," said Pell, who had understood anyway.

* * *

On the eighth day, the deserter problem began.

Not desertion exactly — no one fled. But three enforcers reported dreams. Then nine. Then, by the eleventh day, the entire column dreamed the same dream: a city with white walls and green bridges, a market square where a fountain sang instead of played, and a woman's voice reading names aloud in a stone church, unhurried, tireless, the voice of the blind priestess of Ashenwell reciting the rolls of the recorded dead. Except they weren't dead. That was the unbearable part of the dream. In the dream, everyone she named was alive, and waving, and calling back to her by name, and the names were warm the way bread is warm, and waking up felt like walking out of a lit house into snow.

"I want it noted," Marrin said, gray-faced over the morning fire, "that I don't believe in any of this. The dreams are shared suggestion. Mass psychology. There are papers on it."

"Noted," Corvane said. "Write it in your ledger. Write it large."

She looked at him. "You don't believe it either."

"No," Corvane agreed. "I believe something much worse. I believe the dreams are accurate."

* * *

He wrote his real report that night, the one for no eyes but his own, by the light of a lamp whose flame leaned toward Ashenwell as if reading over his shoulder.

*Marguerite,* he wrote. *I am coming. Understand what I am coming to do, because you will not believe it and you should: I am coming to save you from the worst thing I ever did, which was letting you survive it. If the borders return, the war returns. I watched the assessments. I signed the order because the alternative was forty thousand dead, and I have spent thirty years telling myself that a clean wound is a mercy. Your map says otherwise. Your map says we cut off a limb and called it surgery, and the limb has been waiting in the dark all this time, growing back.*

*If you restore them, I will stop you. I want you to know that I will stop you with everything I have, and that I hope — God help me, I hope — that you are fast enough to finish before I arrive.*

*That is the whole of my position. I am a man standing in a doorway trying to hold it shut against the spring.*

He read it twice. Then he fed it to the lamp, because some entries should never be filed, and watched the flame lean hungrily over the words.

Outside, far to the north, the bell rang again. Closer tonight. Or perhaps the country was simply leaning toward him now, the way the flame leaned toward Ashenwell — everything in this place straining toward the moment when the door would open and the arithmetic would finally be tested against the truth.

Corvane banked the lamp, settled his cloak around his shoulders, and gave the order to break camp in the dark.

"To the Crooked Wolf," he told Marrin. "We march through the night. Whatever happens at that door, the Guild will be there first — or it will be there in time. Either way, history gets an entry."

"And if the door opens before we arrive?"

Corvane looked north, where the horizon had begun, very faintly, very impossibly, to glow — not dawn-light, not firelight, but the pale gold of ink drying on fresh paper, as if somewhere ahead a country were writing itself back into the world line by line, faster now, urgent now, racing them to the threshold.

"Then we arrived late," he said, "and we'll have to decide, on the doorstep, whether we're the ones who shut doors—" he paused, and the old song rose unbidden in his memory, the drinking song, the true one, *the crooked wolf who swallowed the sun* — "or the ones who knock."

He gave the column the signal, and forty enforcers marched north into a country that was remembering them, and above the ridge ahead the glow brightened like a signature being written.
