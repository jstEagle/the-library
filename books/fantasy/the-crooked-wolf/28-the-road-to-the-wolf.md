## 28. The Road to the Wolf

Tobias had learned, in the weeks since Ashenwell, that Vellharrow did not forget all at once. It forgot the way a man forgets where he put his keys — first the small things, then the reasons for them.

The road east of the Salt Flats proved it. Three days ago there had been milestones along it, squat stone posts carved with distances. This morning they were blank. Not worn — blank. The numbers had been taken out of them, and out of whatever memory had once held the numbers, because when Marguerite tried to recall how far it was to the pass she opened her mouth and nothing came, and he watched her face do something he'd never seen it do: go empty.

"We're losing the country," Ilsa said. She said it the way other people said *it's going to rain*. Factual. Tired of saying it.

"No," Marguerite corrected, without looking up from the moving map. "We're losing our record of it. There's a difference. The country is still here." A pause. "For now."

That was the sentence Tobias hated most in the world now. *For now.* He'd started hearing it everywhere — in the wind, in the way the light came down too briefly over the hills, as if the sun itself were checking its ledger before committing.

They moved fast. Faster than they should have been able to, because the map kept shortening the road for them, redrawing the route each dawn so that what had been forty miles on Monday was thirty by Tuesday, as if the country were folding itself up around them like a letter being sealed. Marguerite said this meant the land recognized her. Tobias privately suspected it meant the land was dying and contracting toward its heart the way a hand contracts toward the palm.

Either way, they ran.

* * *

The village was called Brant's Hollow, or it had been until recently, and they had saved it — or thought they had. Two nights past, when the Forgetting Wind had come down off the northern ridges like something with an appetite, Ilsa had stood in the village square with her chalk and recited names until dawn: every soul in Brant's Hollow, their mothers' names, their grandmothers', the names of their dead buried behind the church. She had filled both sides of her slate twice. The wind had passed over the rooftops and found nothing loose to take, and the villagers had wept and pressed bread into their hands and called Ilsa a saint, which made her scowl, which made Tobias love his sister more than he could safely say.

Now they crested the ridge above Brant's Hollow again, on the run, and Tobias stopped walking so suddenly that Marguerite walked into his back.

Below them, where the village had been, there was a valley.

Not ruins. Not ash. A valley — green, unbroken grass sloping down to a stream, wildflowers going gold in the late light. No square. No church. No chalk dust on any doorstep. As if two hundred people and their two hundred years had been lifted out of the world by careful hands, leaving no scar, because a scar would have been a record, and records were precisely what the erasure refused to leave.

"I stood there," Ilsa said. Her voice was very flat, the flatness of someone holding a door shut against something enormous. "Two nights ago. I know every name in that—"

She stopped. Tobias watched her reach for the names and find the shelf bare. Her lips moved. Nothing came. She had spent those names; she had poured them out into the wind like coins into a toll, and the toll-keeper had taken them after all.

"Brant's—" she tried again. "It was called Brant's..."

"Hollow," Marguerite said quietly. "I have it. Here." She tapped her temple. Then, more gently: "I'll keep it until you can."

Ilsa looked at her for a long moment — suspicion and gratitude fighting openly on her face, the war that had defined their whole company since Ashenwell — and then nodded once, sharply, and started down into the valley as if she could walk straight through the absence and prove it hadn't happened.

Tobias followed, and the grass under his boots felt obscene. So soft. So certain of itself.

At the bottom, where the well should have been, there was only a ring of stones with nothing inside but darkness and, faintly, the smell of bread. Someone had baked here yesterday. Someone had laughed here yesterday. The darkness in the well didn't even echo when he dropped a pebble in. It simply accepted it, the way a ledger accepts an entry, and gave back silence.

Marguerite stood at the edge of the vanished square with the moving map open across her arms, and Tobias saw her go still in the particular way she went still when the ink told her something terrible.

"It's not random anymore," she said. "The wind isn't sweeping. It's *herding*. Look." She turned the map toward him. The roads of eastern Vellharrow had redrawn themselves into a pattern Tobias had no training to read, but Marguerite read patterns the way other people read weather, and her voice went thin. "Every surviving road now runs one direction. Toward the Wolf. Everything else is being closed behind us. We aren't fleeing through the country, Tobias. The country is funneling us. Whatever's left of its will — it's spending its last strength getting its cartographer to the door."

"Then we shouldn't waste its money," Ilsa said, already climbing the far slope.

* * *

They traveled through the night, because night had become safer — the forgetting seemed tied somehow to witness, to daylight's insistence on confirming details. Under stars, the land held. Under stars, Tobias could almost pretend the hills were permanent.

He walked beside Marguerite, because that had become his place without either of them deciding it, and because he had promised himself, back in Ashenwell when trust between them had cracked and been badly mended, that he would not keep secrets from her again. Which left him with nothing to talk about except fear, and he'd learned she preferred fear stated plainly.

"My brother died asking for you," he said, as the path bent along a ridge. "The stranger with the map. Halvard. He knew your face. I've never asked you what you think that means."

Marguerite was quiet long enough that three switchbacks passed beneath them.

"I think," she said finally, "that I knew him. Before. Before the Guild, before Dravenmoor. And that some part of me arranged all of this — the map, the dying man, the route — years ago, and then hid the arrangement from myself so thoroughly that I'm following my own instructions like a stranger reading another stranger's hand." She glanced at him. "You wanted me to say something comforting."

"I wanted you to say something true. Comforting's optional."

"That *is* the true version. The comforting version is that fate loves us." She almost smiled. Almost. "In my experience fate keeps excellent ledgers and pays out very little."

Ahead of them, Ilsa called out — a low warning note — and Tobias saw why. On the next ridge, black against the last of the starlight, stood figures. Five of them. Six. Enforcers, gray-cloaked, patient, spaced like punctuation along the crest. Not attacking. Not pursuing.

*Escorting.*

"They're not trying to catch us," Marguerite breathed. "Corvane doesn't need to catch us. The country's doing his work. He just needs us to arrive — tired, spent, out of names and roads — and he'll be waiting at the door."

"Then we arrive first," Tobias said, "and rested enough to fight," and he heard in his own voice a hardness that surprised him, that he suspected had been growing in him since a bleeding man fell into a weighing house and changed everything.

They cut off the road. The map, obligingly, revealed a shepherd's track that had certainly not existed an hour earlier, dropping steeply through pine shadow toward the river gorge below. The country, spending itself. They took the gift without thanking anyone, because there was no one left to thank, and ran.

* * *

Dawn caught them in the gorge, and with dawn came the sound.

Tobias heard it before he understood it: a rushing, a vast papery whisper, like ten thousand pages turning at once somewhere beyond the southern rim. He climbed the gorge wall to see and wished he hadn't.

Across the southern sky, where the mountains of central Vellharrow ought to have stood, there was a horizon. Flat. Featureless. White-gold and empty as an unwritten page. And it was moving — slowly, with the patience of institutions — northward, eating the mountains as it came. Where it passed, there was nothing to remember having been there. That was the worst of it. His eyes slid off the erased terrain like water off glass. He couldn't even hold the shape of what had been lost, because the loss itself was smooth.

"The end of the atlas," Marguerite said behind him. She had climbed up beside him, and her face in the early light was the face of someone reading a diagnosis. "It's not chasing the villages anymore. It's taking the geography itself. Borders, ranges, watersheds — everything I ever drew." Her hand rose unconsciously to the strap of the map case at her chest, the way a person touches a wound. "Everything I am."

"How long?" Ilsa asked.

Marguerite calculated. Tobias watched her do it — the tiny movements of her lips, the cartographer's arithmetic running behind her eyes — and understood that whatever number she produced would be a kindness or a cruelty depending on nothing but accuracy.

"At this rate? The Wolf stands at the head of the Vale of Ostenmere, four days' hard travel." She swallowed. "The page arrives in three."

Ilsa laughed — one short, ugly syllable. "Then we're running out of country."

"Yes," Marguerite said. Simply. Without ornament. That was the thing about her, Tobias thought, the thing he had come to trust past all evidence and betrayal: she did not decorate the truth. "So we stop sleeping, we stop grieving, and we spend the country while it lasts."

* * *

They spent it. All that day and into the next they ran through a landscape that was dissolving around its edges like sugar in rain, and the country gave what it had left. A bridge appeared over a flooded ravine, old and mossy and utterly unmapped, standing exactly long enough for three travelers to cross before groaning into mist behind them. A field of late berries fed them at dusk. An abandoned watchtower offered a few hours' shelter from the wind, and inside it, scratched into the stone in a language Marguerite read without translating, someone centuries dead had written *the wolf remembers what the men forget*, and none of them spoke for a long time after that.

On the second night, high on the shoulder of the last mountain range — the range the white horizon had not yet reached — they paused on a spur overlooking the Vale of Ostenmere, and Tobias saw Marguerite go rigid.

"What?"

"There's supposed to be a town there." Her finger stabbed at the valley floor, where lamplight glimmered in a scatter that might have been a market town, might have been anything. "Fennwick's Crossing. Population nine hundred. I mapped it when I was nineteen." Her voice did something complicated. "My *first* commission. My own hand drew every street of that town, and I don't remember drawing it, but the map does, look—" She thrust the moving parchment at him and there it was, Fennwick's Crossing in fine brown lines, every street rendered in a young woman's precise hand, and beneath it, in smaller script, a signature: *M.V.*

"You named it after yourself," Tobias said stupidly.

"I named it after *him*, whoever he was, and then the Guild took my name away and left the town wearing it like an orphaned coat." She folded the map with shaking fingers. "If the page takes that valley tonight, then the first thing I ever made will be gone, and I won't even mourn it properly, because I won't remember making it."

"Then we go down there," Tobias said. "Tonight. Tonight we remind them."

They ran down into the lamplight, and Ilsa stood in the market square of Fennwick's Crossing with her slate and her chalk and her ruined throat, and she named them — all nine hundred, house by house, child by child — while Marguerite walked the streets touching doorposts like a priest administering something, and Tobias stood guard at the valley mouth watching the white horizon and hating it with a hatred so clean it felt like a kind of faith.

And the page came anyway.

It came at moonrise, silent, total, and Tobias grabbed Marguerite's arm and pulled her up the far side of the valley while Ilsa's voice cracked and broke and finally failed, and behind them Fennwick's Crossing went out — lamps, streets, nine hundred lives, the first thing she ever drew — not burning, not screaming, just *unhappening*, the lamplight smoothing over into ordinary dark, the valley filling with grass that had always been grass, that had never been anything else.

Marguerite did not cry. She stood at the top of the valley with the wind pulling at her coat and said, in a voice scraped down to bare wire:

"I'm done letting it take things while I watch. Tomorrow we reach the Wolf, and I open that door if I have to give it every name I own."

Above them, on the ridge road they would have to take at first light, torches flared — many torches, ranked and orderly, and among the gray cloaks a figure in black who did not need torches to be recognizable, whose silhouette Tobias had seen in nightmares since a weighing house and a single gentle lie.

Guildmaster Corvane had reached the door first.
