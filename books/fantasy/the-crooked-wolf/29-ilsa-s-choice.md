## 29. Ilsa's Choice

*This is my hand writing this, though it shakes now, so forgive the letters their crookedness. Chalk first, ink after, the way my brother taught me. If you find this page and the pass is empty and the wind is singing, then it worked.*

*I am Ilsa Vane, of the chalk houses of Ashenwell, sister to Tomas Vane who died in a doorway asking a cartographer to remember. I was born in Vellharrow and I will die in Vellharrow and between those two facts I intend to fit every name I can carry.*

*Here is how we came to this pass, so that whoever reads understands that no one abandoned me, least of all me.*

* * *

The wind caught us on the third morning out of Ashenwell, and by noon it had eaten a village.

I want to write that sentence again because even now, holding the memory up to the light, it refuses to sit still. We were climbing through the hill country above Lowmere — Lowmere, which smelled of woodsmoke and wet sheep and the particular sourness of its cider, which had a church with a leaning spire and a miller named Odell Brack who beat his wife and apologized to his horse — and the Forgetting Wind came down out of the north like weather, except that weather leaves when it's done.

Marguerite felt it before any of us. She stopped on the track so suddenly that Tobias walked into her back, and she said, in a voice flat as a ledger line: "Get down. Get under something. Don't let it take your breath."

We crouched beneath an overhang of granite while the wind poured over the valley below us. It made no sound I could describe then and cannot describe better now — a sound like pages turning, thousands of them, very fast. Below, Lowmere sat in its bowl of hills, ordinary and complete. The wind passed through it the way light passes through water.

When it was gone, Tobias stood up too fast and stared, and I looked where he looked and understood why he'd gone quiet.

Lowmere was still there. But it had changed. Not ruined — *reduced*. The leaning spire stood straight, which sounds like an improvement but wasn't; the lean was part of what Lowmere *was*, the thing every child in three parishes could have drawn from memory. And the mill was gone. Not collapsed, not burned — absent, its plot a smooth green absence, as if the ground had healed over the idea of a mill. And standing in the lane was a woman I didn't know, staring at her own hands, turning in a slow circle, mouthing something.

"She's looking for her husband," Marguerite said. Her voice did something I hadn't heard it do before — cracked, just once, in the middle. "The miller. Odell. She can't remember why the empty plot hurts. She only knows it hurts."

"How do you know that?" Tobias asked.

"Because I'm the one who erased him," she said, "and erasure leaves seams."

We went down into Lowmere because Marguerite insisted — she was already unrolling the moving map across a fence rail, and the map had redrawn itself overnight, and where yesterday there had been one road north through the pass called the Shepherd's Throat, today there were two, and one of them glowed faintly, the way a vein glows when you hold your hand near a lamp.

"The country's fighting," she said. "It's rerouting us. Look — it wants us at the Throat by tomorrow night." She looked up, and for a moment she was not the Guild clerk with the precise hands and the careful face; she was someone younger, someone who had loved maps before she learned what they could do to places. "Vellharrow is spending itself to keep us moving. Every road it opens is a piece of itself it won't be able to hold onto later."

"Then we don't waste what it's buying," Tobias said.

That was my brother's friend entire. Give him a hard fact and a harder choice and he would stand in front of both like a wall. He had carried grief since the doorway where Tomas bled out, and he had carried it the way mules carry loads — without complaint, without setting it down, without ever quite looking at it directly. I loved him for it and I worried about him for it, because grief you never set down eventually sets you down.

We spent what remained of the day doing what I do. What I have done for eleven years, with chalk and habit and stubbornness, in whatever village would tolerate a strange woman writing names on walls.

You need to understand what I mean by that, reader, because it is the whole reason I am alive to write this and the whole reason I will not be alive much longer.

When the Erasure began — when the borders were unwritten and the trade roads forgot themselves and the neighboring kingdoms' atlases quietly lost a country the size of a fist — the land did not vanish. Land doesn't. What vanished was *holding*. A house whose street no longer existed could not be found by anyone coming to it. A man whose name was unwritten could walk into the next town and arrive as a stranger even to his mother, because the thread that connected them had been snipped at the source. People drifted apart like beads off a cut string.

But a name spoken aloud, written fresh, insisted upon — that re-tied the knot. Weakly. Temporarily. The way a splint holds a broken bone. So eleven years ago I started walking Vellharrow with a box of chalk, and I wrote names on walls. Street names. Family names. The names of dead people on houses where they'd lived, because a house that remembers its dead remembers itself. I taught children games built on recitation. I bribed priests with salt. I got thrown out of more taverns than I can count and welcomed into more kitchens than that, because somewhere underneath everything, the people of Vellharrow knew exactly what I was doing and why, and they were hungry for it the way the starving are hungry.

Tomas thought I was mad for the first year. Then he saw a village I'd chalked survive a Wind that took its neighbor, and he stopped thinking anything except *more chalk*.

So: Lowmere. We went down into it and I chalked the town from gate to gate while the light lasted. MILL AND MILL-RACE. ODELL BRACK, MILLER. THE CHURCH OF ST. FERREOL, WHOSE SPIRE LEANS BECAUSE THE SAINT SHRUGGED. Names, dates, small absurd particulars — the particulars matter most, reader, remember that. Erasure takes the general and leaves the ache. Only the specific fights back. *He beat his wife and apologized to his horse.* Ugly, true, specific. The woman in the lane read it, and read it again, and sat down hard on her doorstep, and wept, and said, "Odell. His name was Odell," and then, worse and better: "And I left him. Three years ago. Because of the beating." Memory is not mercy. It is only memory. But she was *whole* again, seam and all, and she pressed bread and cheese into our hands and told Marguerite — who had introduced herself with a hesitation I noticed and pretended not to — "You've a Vellharrow look about you, miss. You should be proud of it."

Marguerite said thank you in Vellharrow's tongue, the accent perfect, and did not explain, and I did not ask, because by then I had begun to guess, and guessing was easier than knowing.

* * *

The ambush came at dusk in the Shepherd's Throat itself, which meant the map had led us true and the enemy had simply been faster.

There were six of them. Enforcers of the Guild of Impossible Things, in grey coats the color of unwritten paper, and they came over the scree above the pass with the unhurried competence of people who have done this many times and been paid well for it. No challenge, no parley. Crossbows first.

Tomas — no. *Tobias.* Grief writes my brother's name into my mouth sometimes. Forgive me. I'll get it right here, where it counts.

*Tobias* shoved me behind the boulder and took a bolt meant for me through the meat of his shoulder, and Marguerite — Marguerite Dravenmoor, Guild cartographer, keeper of ledgers, the woman who signed the order — did the last thing any of us expected and possibly the last thing the enforcers expected, which was to shout the pass's name at it.

"SHEPHERD'S THROAT!" she screamed, in the old tongue, in the formal cadence the priestesses use for boundary-cries. "THROAT OF THE SHEPHERD, WHO LED NINETY-NINE HOME AND WENT BACK FOR THE HUNDREDTH! YOU KNOW YOUR NAME! HOLD!"

And the pass held.

I don't have another word for it. The scree shifted. The narrow way narrowed further. A shelf of rock that had hung in balance for ten thousand years chose that moment to come down, precisely, deliberately, between us and four of the six enforcers, sealing the throat's upper end like a cork. The other two kept coming on foot, blades out, and Tobias fought them with a sword he was too hurt to lift properly, and I broke a bottle of Lowmere cider over one's head, which is not heroic, merely effective, and the second one had Marguerite by the collar with a knife at her throat when the mountain dropped the rest of the rockfall and the whole pass rang like a struck bell.

Afterward there was dust, and coughing, and blood — mine, as it turned out. I hadn't noticed taking the blade. These things are shy about announcing themselves. A slash across my side, deep, ugly, from hip toward ribs. Tobias bound it with strips of his shirt and his hands were steady and his face was white and he said, "You'll live," in the tone of a man negotiating.

"I'll live," I agreed, "for a value of live."

Because I knew. Reader, I knew by the second mile. I have seen wounds enough in eleven years of hard roads. This one was beyond splinting and beyond stitching and past anything short of a surgeon in a city we did not have time to reach. I could walk tonight. I might walk tomorrow morning. By tomorrow afternoon the walking would stop being mine to choose.

Marguerite worked it out around the same fire I did. She watched me the way she watches everything — as if reading fine print — and then she said nothing, which was its own confession, and I decided to spare her having to say it aloud. One kindness per day. That's a rule I kept for eleven years. It keeps you human in a country being unwritten.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic of the Shepherd's Throat, laid out plainly, because I was raised bookkeeper's daughter before I was anything else:

Behind us: the Guild. Corvane himself now, if the reports the land whispered to Marguerite were true, marching enforcers up the low road to finish the Erasure in person, because a restored Vellharrow frightened him more than any war. Ahead of us: the high route to the Crooked Wolf, two days' hard going through country the Wind had chewed thin. Between the two: this pass, the only choke point where a handful could delay a company.

The land could hold the pass — we had seen it. But the land was spending itself everywhere at once, defending rivers and towns, and each defense cost memory it couldn't replace. Marguerite said the mountains were forgetting their own geology in patches, holding passes open by rote, like old men telling stories they no longer believed.

What the pass needed was not stone. It needed *naming*. Sustained, specific, relentless naming — the kind that doesn't fade when the chalk fades. The kind that has to come out of a living throat.

I have been the living throat of half of Vellharrow for eleven years. There is no one else who knows the recitations. The blind priestess in Ashenwell knew some, but Ashenwell was two days behind us and the priestess was ninety. Marguerite knew the forms but not the names — she had erased them; her memory of them had been sealed in wax and locked in a Guild vault, and though pieces were coming back to her, they came back like ice melting, slow and partial. Tobias knew three hundred names, maybe, the ones I'd drilled into him on the long roads, but he was wounded too, and the recitation isn't a song you hum. It's a labor. Hours of it. Days, if needed. You give it breath and rhythm and you do not stop, because the moment you stop, the Wind finds the gap.

So the arithmetic resolved itself while I was pretending to sleep by the fire. Someone stayed. Someone who could recite until they couldn't, and then keep reciting in whatever way remains after that.

I did not make a speech. Speeches are for people with time. I woke Tobias and told him, and he argued the way I knew he'd argue — offering himself instead, citing his shoulder as if a bolt wound equaled a opened belly — and I told him the truth, which is that his recitations are competent and mine are native, and that the pass would know the difference the way a lock knows the right key. Then I told Marguerite, and she went still, and then she said the most dangerous sentence I heard in this whole affair:

"There may be another way."

"There is not," I said. "Or if there is, it costs you, and I know what you're planning to pay at that door, Margo Vale, because I've read the room you slept in and the order you signed, and you've spent enough of yourself on my country. This one's mine. I've been paying in installments for eleven years. Let me settle the account."

She argued. Of course she argued. She had the Guild's gift for argument, the terrible fluency of people trained to make the false seem orderly. But I had my father's ledgers in my bones, and ledgers teach you one thing above all others: a debt deferred is not a debt forgiven.

At dawn I wrote my final chalk lines on the great stone at the mouth of the pass. Not instructions. Names. All the ones I could recall of everyone I'd met on these roads, and then, because the specifics matter most, the small truths attached to them. *Marta of the tollbridge, who cheated strangers and fed beggars. Odell Brack, who beat his wife and apologized to his horse. The leaning spire of St. Ferreol.* Let the Wind come and find a wall it had to work through.

Then I embraced my brother's friend, who cried, which surprised neither of us, and I embraced the woman who had signed away my country and then smuggled it out inside a folded map, which surprised me very much, because forgiveness arrived sometime during the night without my permission, the way dawn does.

"Recite," she told me at the edge of the rocks, her voice not quite level. "As long as you can. And then—" she hesitated, hating herself for the question— "and then what happens?"

"Then the Wind takes my name," I said. "And the pass forgets it was ever defended, and walks free behind you. But it will have held long enough. That's authorship, cartographer. The only kind left to us. You taught the country that trick yourself, whether you meant to or not."

* * *

They are gone now. I watched them from the height until the path bent and took them, Marguerite limping slightly and upright as a surveyor's pole, Tobias's arm bound and his stride long, two figures getting smaller against a landscape getting less certain. The map glowed faintly in her hand even at that distance. The country lighting its own way home.

Now it is midday, and the Wind is coming up the valley, and I have been reciting since they disappeared.

I will record here what I have said, or the shape of it, because a record is a record even when no one audits it. The roll of Ashenwell, four hundred and eleven souls, beginning with Mother Cassia at the church and ending with the unnamed baby due in spring, whom I named anyway — Wren, if a girl; Alder, if a boy; I hope whoever survives gets to argue with me about it. The roll of Lowmere, restored this week, Odell Brack first among them, may his wife's leaving stand and his apology to his horse be remembered forever. The roll of the tollbridge, of the chalk houses, of the shepherd villages, of every kitchen that fed me and every tavern that threw me out — especially those, reader; even anger is a form of remembering, and I hoard it gratefully.

My voice is going. I notice it the way you notice a candle guttering — not by silence but by effort. The words are starting to taste like blood, which the healers would say means the wound has reached the chest, which I believe.

The Wind is close. I can hear the pages turning.

Here is the thing I understand now, at the end, that I only suspected at the beginning: the Wind does not destroy what it takes. It *collects*. Somewhere in that turning-page sound there is a library, and everything it has ever unwritten — every river, every spire's lean, every beaten wife's grievance and miller's apology — is filed there. And when a name goes in, it does not go out. Nothing comes back out. But it is *kept*. And kept is not nothing.

So I will not say I am afraid of becoming unread. I will say instead: I am Ilsa Vane. Sister. Chalker. Keeper of four hundred and eleven names plus several thousand besides. I was born in Vellharrow, and Vellharrow was born in the minds of people like me, and if the price of keeping either is the other, then let the account close balanced.

Let the last line of my ledger read: *paid in full.*

The Wind is at the stones now. My voice is nearly gone. I am switching to the silent recitation, the one the priestess taught, lips and breath and heart, which the Wind must work harder to steal — good. Make it work. Make it earn every syllable.

Wren, if a girl. Alder, if a boy. Marta who cheated strangers and fed beggars. Odell Brack. St. Ferreol, who shrugged. My brother Tomas, dead in a doorway, whose face I can still—

Still.

Keep.

Far below, past the bend of the pass, two small figures climbed toward a door grown crooked from being opened against its nature — and behind them, faint but unmistakable, the sound of a mountain remembering its name.
