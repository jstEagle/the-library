## 31. Which Name to Give

The Crooked Wolf stood at the end of a road that had forgotten it was a road.

It had been a highway once — Marguerite could read the ghost of it in the land, the way the ground still leaned toward some old intention, ruts of memory running under the grass like veins under skin. Now it was a track, then a suggestion, then nothing at all: just a hillside climbing toward a doorway that stood alone in the open air, framed by no wall, hinged on no building, opening onto nothing she could see from here.

A door with no house around it. A door that had been forced once and never forgiven anyone for it.

Tobias stopped three paces behind her. She heard him breathing hard, heard the wet rasp of the wound Ilsa's sacrifice had bought them time to outrun but not to heal. He didn't speak. He'd learned, somewhere on the salt flats or somewhere before that, when to let her have silence.

Marguerite walked the last stretch alone.

Up close, the door was worse than crooked. It was *wrong* the way a word is wrong when you've stared at it too long — every line of it true and none of them agreeing with each other. The wood was dark ironwood gone silver at the edges, grain running in directions wood does not run. The frame listed fifteen degrees east, as if leaning away from something. The handle was shaped like a wolf's jaw, half-open, and the keyhole beneath it was not a keyhole at all but a slit, narrow and patient, like an ear listening.

Ink moved across its surface. Not painted ink — living ink, the same ink that had crawled across the dead man's map in her hands a lifetime ago, back when her ledgers balanced and her name was a simple thing. Rivers of it slid along the grain, pooling and parting, and where they pooled they formed letters, and the letters said:

**WHO KNOCKS?**

"I do," Marguerite said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. She cleared her throat and tried again, the way she'd been taught to speak to Guildmasters and difficult clients: level, precise, unafraid. "Marguerite Dravenmoor. Cartographer, second chair, Guild of Impossible Things."

The ink stirred. Swirled. Reformed.

**THAT IS ONE ANSWER. THE DOOR ASKS FOR ONE NAME.**

"One name," she repeated. "Yes. I know."

She did know. The priestess had told her, in the archive-church in Ashenwell, in that voice like paper being turned: *The Wolf was bent because someone opened it against its nature, and doors remember what they're made to swallow. It will take a name to straighten. Not a word — a name. The whole of one. And what you give, you do not get back.*

**YOU CARRY TWO,** the door said. The ink flowed like something considering. **I TASTE THEM BOTH.**

"You taste them both," Marguerite agreed. Her mouth was dry. Behind her, Tobias shifted his weight, and she raised one hand without turning — *wait* — and he waited, because he was better than she deserved and he knew it and she knew it and neither of them said so anymore.

The wind came down off the hills then, the Forgetting Wind, and she felt it pass through her like a hand riffling pages. Somewhere behind them, east, the village of Harrow's Bend — the one they'd reached at dawn, the one whose children had chanted their street names to keep them — was either still standing or was not. There was no way to check. There was less country left to check *with* every hour.

She looked at the door and the door looked at her, which is a strange thing to say about a door, but this was Vellharrow, and everything here looked back.

"Ask me," she said.

**ASK YOU WHAT?**

"The question. You're a door. Doors ask." She stepped closer, close enough to see herself reflected faintly in the wet black shine of the moving ink — two women superimposed, one in Guild grey, one in a stranger's coat, and she could not tell which reflection belonged to which name. "You want to know who I am. So ask."

The ink went very still. Then it began to move faster than she'd ever seen it move, rivers braiding into rivers, and the letters came slow and deliberate, each one placed like a seal pressed into wax:

**MARGUERITE DRAVENMOOR. WHO GAVE YOU THAT NAME?**

"I earned it," she said. "I was nobody when I came to the Guild. An orphan out of the border provinces with a gift for drawing coastlines that hadn't been surveyed yet. Corvane found me. The Guild made me. Every map I ever signed, every ledger I kept true, every debt I recorded honestly even when honesty cost — twenty years of that. Dravenmoor isn't a thing I was given." Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she pushed through anyway. "Dravenmoor is a thing I *built*. Stone by stone. It is the only thing I have ever built."

**AND THE OTHER?**

The wind picked up. Far below, at the foot of the hill, the road was visibly shorter than it had been five minutes ago — grass closing over it like water over a drowned man.

"Margo Vale," Marguerite said.

The name tasted like bread and woodsmoke and a kitchen she could almost see. Like a woman's voice calling up stairs. Like chalk dust and the smell of the river at Ashenwell and a boarding room with unfinished maps pinned to every wall, drawn in a hand that was hers, in a language she understood without translating.

"My mother named me that," she said. "Before I could earn anything. Before I knew a name was something you could lose. Margo Vale of Ashenwell, Vellharrow. Born on a street that is currently forgetting itself while we stand here. That name wasn't built. It was *given*, freely, by someone who loved me, which is more than I can say for any name I've earned since."

**THE DOOR WILL TAKE ONE.**

"I know."

**NOT BOTH. NOT HALF OF EACH. THE DOOR WAS BENT BY PEOPLE WHO TRIED TO HAVE IT BOTH WAYS — OPEN WHEN THEY WISHED, SHUT WHEN THEY FEARED. IT DOES NOT NEGOTIATE.**

"I know that too." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "You'd think I'd have learned. I'm the one who keeps the ledgers. Debts are debts. Terms are terms. I've spent my whole life telling other people there's no such thing as a bargain without a price, and now—" She stopped. Breathed. "Now the bill has come to my desk."

The ink rippled, almost gently.

**SPEAK THE CHOICE ALOUD. THE DOOR REQUIRES TO HEAR IT IN FULL BEFORE IT DECIDES WHAT YOU ARE OFFERING.**

"All right." She squared her shoulders. This, at least, was familiar — the weighing house, the scales, the moment before the number settles. She had spent twenty years watching people decide what their debts were worth. She had never once been on the pan herself.

"If I give you Dravenmoor," she said slowly, "then Marguerite Dravenmoor ceases. The cartographer. The keeper of ledgers. The woman who signed—" her stomach turned, but she made herself say it— "who signed the order. Who drew your borders and then erased them. She ends at this threshold. Whatever walks through is Margo Vale, born of Ashenwell, who remembers her mother's kitchen and her own handwriting and nothing of the Guild. No maps. No craft. Just a woman coming home to a country she helped kill, with no skill left to mend it."

**AND IF YOU GIVE VALE?**

"Then Margo Vale dies instead. The girl in the boarding room. The daughter. The one who loved this country before she understood what it would cost to betray it." Her throat worked. "What walks through is Dravenmoor — all craft, all guilt, all precision, and no home. A cartographer with no birthplace, mapping a country that will never be hers again. She restores you, maybe. She saves you, maybe. But she does it as a foreigner. As a stranger holding a map of somewhere she's never been."

The wind screamed down the hillside then, sudden and hard, and the letters scattered and reformed, and for a moment — she would swear this later, to Tobias, and he would believe her because he had seen the same thing — the ink did not form words at all.

It formed a picture.

A kitchen. A small stone kitchen with a blue door, and a woman at the table with flour on her hands, looking up, saying something Marguerite couldn't hear across the distance of thirty years and one erasure. Then it was gone, and the letters returned:

**THE DOOR SHOWS WHAT IT HOLDS. YOUR MOTHER SOLD HER MEMORY OF YOU TO THE PRIESTESS'S CHURCH, THAT SOMETHING OF YOU MIGHT SURVIVE THE ERASURE. SHE NEVER STOPPED SETTING TWO PLATES.**

Marguerite's knees nearly went. She put a hand flat against the cold ironwood to steady herself, and the wood was warm under her palm, warm as a living thing, and she snatched it back.

"That's not fair," she whispered.

**DOORS ARE NOT FAIR. DOORS ARE HONEST. THERE IS A DIFFERENCE.**

"Yes," she said. "There is. I've spent my career confusing the two myself."

Behind her, footsteps. Tobias came up beside her, close enough that she could feel the heat of him, smell blood and rain and road-dust. He looked at the door for a long moment, jaw working.

"You don't have to choose tonight," he said quietly.

"The country doesn't have tonight." She nodded east, where the horizon had gone strangely smooth, the way paper goes smooth when the writing leaves it. "Look at it. There's less of Vellharrow every hour. Ilsa bought us a pass with her voice and I am not going to waste it standing here rehearsing."

"So choose fast. Fine." He turned to face her fully. "But hear me first, because I've carried this since the weighing house and I'll not carry it past this door. Whichever name you give — I'll know you by the other one."

She blinked at him.

"Tobias—"

"No, listen." His voice was rough and low and absolutely certain, the way it had been the night he demanded the map from her in the rain, the night two liars recognized each other. "If you walk through as Vale, then I'll be the one who remembers Dravenmoor. Someone should. Someone owes it to her — she was hard and careful and lonely and she lied exactly once in twenty years, and it was to protect a dead man's map. If you walk through as Dravenmoor, then I'll be the one who remembers Vale. The girl with the chalk. Because somebody should remember that she existed before the Guild got hold of her, and it shouldn't only be a door."

The ink had gone still again. Listening.

"That's not how names work," Marguerite said, and her eyes were burning, and she let them. "That's not how *doors* work. What's given is gone. Even you can't hold it."

"I held a map of a country that officially never existed for six years," Tobias said. "I held it through two interrogations and a winter in the Saltmarsh gaol. I'm very good at holding things the world says are gone." He shrugged, wincing at his wound. "Give the door whichever name you can bear to lose. I'll keep the other one. Between us, you'll arrive complete."

For a long moment Marguerite could not speak at all.

Then she laughed — really laughed, ragged and wet and helpless — and wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and turned back to the door, because the horizon was getting smoother and Ilsa's pass would not hold forever and there is a moment in every bargain when the terms must finally be spoken.

"All right," she said to the door. "Here is my answer. Here is what I've decided."

The ink gathered. The wolf-jaw handle trembled. The whole crooked frame leaned toward her like a listener bending close.

And that was when the light came up the road behind them — lantern-light, many lanterns, arranged in the precise formation of a Guild column — and a voice she knew better than her own called out across the dying grass:

"Marguerite."

Corvane.

He was walking alone, ahead of his enforcers, hands empty and visible, and his voice carried no threat at all. It carried something far worse. It carried the tone of a man who had come to beg.

"Don't open it," he called. "Whatever you've decided — don't. Let me show you what it costs. Let me show you the arithmetic first."

The door's ink flared, and the letters burned across the ironwood:

**CHOOSE.**

* * *

Marguerite did not turn around.

"Keep walking, Guildmaster," she said, "and you'll watch me do it."

Behind her, boots on grass. Behind her, forty enforcers halting in perfect order, lanterns swaying. Behind her, the last honest man in the Guild asking her, in a voice worn down to the grain, to let the dead stay dead.

Ahead of her, the door waited, patient as a ledger, hungry as a debt.

Two names. One hand.

She lifted it toward the wolf-jaw handle.
