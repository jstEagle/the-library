## 37. Cartographer of a Restored Country

The river came back on a Tuesday.

I know because I was standing in the shallows of it when it did — the Aldenmere, the maps said, though for weeks there had been nothing there but a dry channel and a smell like old pennies. One moment I was sketching the absence of water; the next, my boots were soaked to the ankle and a fish the color of hammered tin flicked past my calf as if it had never been anywhere at all. Somewhere upstream, a mill wheel that had been a ruin yesterday began to turn.

Tobias, on the bank with the surveyor's chain coiled over his shoulder like a tame serpent, laughed until he had to sit down.

"You're laughing," I said. "You've been here six weeks and you still laugh."

"Six weeks of watching rivers un-erase themselves." He wiped his eyes. "If I stop laughing, Marguerite, I'll start weeping, and then neither of us will get any work done."

He was right, which was becoming an irritating habit of his. So I turned back to my board and did what I had crossed two worlds to do: I drew the water in.

Not the moving map's water — that had always been provisional, ink that swam, roads that existed only under observation. This was real cartography, honest work, the kind the Guild taught first and forgot last: chain and compass, triangulation from the bell tower at Ashenwell to the broken spire at Cael Morren, measurements taken three times and argued over twice. The country was holding still now, mostly. Long enough to be drawn. Long enough, if you were careful, to be *kept*.

That was the word I kept circling. Not restored — restored implied something finished. Kept. The way you keep a fire. The way you keep a promise. The way, once upon a time and in another handwriting entirely, someone had kept a copy of a whole nation inside a folded sheet of paper and let it wander the world looking for the person foolish enough to open it again.

My hand did not shake. Six weeks ago it would have. Now it moved the pen the way Tobias moved the chain: steadily, professionally, with only the small tremor underneath that meant *alive*.

* * *

We worked the eastern valleys that morning, Tobias pacing out distances while I shot angles, and by noon we had the bones of the Aldenmere watershed down on vellum. It was good work. I want to be clear about that, because I have spent most of my life mistaking precision for virtue and I am trying to be more precise even about that. The map was *good*. Every line earned. And when I looked at it — really looked, the way I used to look at nothing, because for years everything worth looking at had already been recorded by someone else — I felt the old thing stir in my chest. The one from before the Guild, before Corvane, before the signing desk. The feeling of standing at the edge of a landscape and thinking: *no one has written this down yet, and I am going to be the one.*

It is a greedier feeling than people think. Cartographers are not humble. We just hide our appetite behind instruments.

"There," Tobias said suddenly, pointing north along the valley. "Do you see it?"

I saw it. A village assembling itself out of the haze — rooftops first, then smoke from chimneys, then, faintly, a bell. Hollenbrook. Chapter twenty-nine's ledger had it lost; the wind had taken its name in the spring and the place had thinned to a rumor of itself, and Ilsa had stayed behind near there, at the pass, reciting names into a gale until the gale took hers too. I do not write about Ilsa much. My pen refuses, and I have learned to let it.

Hollenbrook was coming back. Slowly, like a photograph developing, like a sleeper deciding whether to wake. And at its edge, where the fields began, a figure stood watching us watch it, and raised one arm — not waving exactly. Acknowledging. The gesture of someone confirming: *yes, you see me too, therefore I am here.*

"That's the fourth this week," Tobias said quietly. "They come back faster when they know someone's mapping them. Did you notice? Places we've surveyed stay put. Places we haven't—" He shrugged. "The wind gets them first."

"I noticed." I had noticed more than that. I had begun to suspect, with the particular dread of a woman whose hypotheses keep coming true, that the country wasn't simply healing. It was *cooperating*. Roads straightened where my sightline needed them. Rivers returned in reaches I could measure. Twice now a fog had lifted precisely when my instruments required it, and both times I had the distinct sensation — absurd, unscientific, undeniable — of being *helped*.

Vellharrow knew I was drawing it. And Vellharrow, stubborn, wronged, alive Vellharrow, seemed to have decided I was on its side.

I did not feel entitled to that decision. I have a signed order in my past that says otherwise, in wax and iron gall ink, in my own young hand.

"Tobias." I capped my ink. "Tell me again why you're staying."

* * *

We sat on the mill wall above the returning river and ate bread and hard cheese and the small sour apples that grew wild along the Aldenmere and that no orchardist had planted because no orchardist remembered planting them — the trees had simply come back with the water, pre-inhabited, as it were, by their own fruit.

"Why am I staying," Tobias repeated. He turned an apple over in his fingers. "Because my brother died getting this country's name out of his mouth, and the least I can do is learn to say it every day. Because Ilsa held a pass with nothing but a list of names and I intend to be the kind of man who'd have stood next to her. Because—" he gestured broadly at the valley, the mill, the tin-colored fish, the bell of Hollenbrook ringing its uncertain noon, "—because somebody has to carry the chain. You can't triangulate alone."

"You could go home," I said. "There's a world with tea in it. Proper tea. And beds that don't smell of chalk."

"My home," he said, "is a rented room above a weighing house in a city where I owed eleven months' rent to a guild that tried to arrest me." He smiled, but it didn't reach all the way up. "Also my sister is buried somewhere near that pass, or she would be if the ground could decide to keep her. No. I'm staying. Someone should live here who isn't paying off a debt. Someone should just *live* here."

"And that's enough? Living?"

"It's everything," he said, simply, and ate the apple, core and all, the way people eat who have stopped being particular about abundance.

I looked west, where the Crooked Wolf stood — visible now from miles away, since the door had straightened and become less a door than a landmark, a gate between worlds with a road running through it that went nowhere unless you intended somewhere very specific. Pilgrims were already finding it. Refugees too. And Guild clerks, occasionally, arriving with ledgers under their arms and expressions of profound institutional indigestion, because the Guild's official position remained that Vellharrow did not exist, and the Guild's clerks kept having to file reports from a place that disagreed.

Corvane was gone. Resigned in ink, walked through as a private man, and the last anyone heard he was farming — actually farming, hands in actual soil — in a valley south of Cael Morren. I think about him more than I expected to. His arithmetic was wrong, but it was *arithmetic*, honestly kept, and there are worse sins. There is mine, for instance.

"So," Tobias said. "And you?"

"Me what?"

"You're leaving on Thursday. Don't pretend the packing isn't happening. I've watched you oil the hinges on that case for three days like it's a religious observance."

I said nothing, which confirmed everything, because silence has been my native language since before I knew I spoke it.

"The blank paper," he said. "The last unmapped region. You drew it on every chart — *still deciding* — and everyone thinks it's a joke or a humility or some cartographer's poetry. But you're going to walk into it, aren't you. Through the Wolf. With nothing but blank sheets."

"The region exists on none of my surveys," I said. "It resists measurement. Believe me, I've tried. Compasses sulk. The chain comes back a different length each time." I paused. "A country that just fought its way back into existence deserves a cartographer who will admit what she can't draw."

"That's the speech," Tobias said. "Now the reason."

The river ran beneath us, tin-bright, insistent. Hollenbrook's bell finished ringing. Somewhere a dog barked at a returning world.

"The reason," I said slowly, "is that I have been two women for a long time. Margo Vale, who signed an erasure. Marguerite Dravenmoor, who kept the ledgers of things that shouldn't exist. And I stitched them together with a hyphenated signature and told myself the country holding both names meant I was whole." I turned the apple in my hand without eating it. "But there's a third one. The one who comes after. The one who isn't defined by what she erased or what she restored, but by whatever she draws *next*, on paper that hasn't decided yet. I don't know her. I'd like to meet her somewhere with no borders."

Tobias was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was gentle in the way that men are gentle when they have already lost the argument and know it.

"And the peace?" he asked. "This valley, the work, the river coming back on a Tuesday with fish in it. You could have this every Tuesday for the rest of your life. Isn't that worth choosing *out loud*, instead of wandering into a blank looking for a self?"

Peace. I tested the word the way I test a bearing: against known points, against the fixed stars of what I actually wanted. And I found, to my considerable irritation, that it fit. That was the trap of it. Peace fit. The work fit. Tobias's stubborn companionship fit. Hollenbrook assembling itself out of gratitude fit. Everything fit, and fitting is precisely what I have never trusted, because the Guild fit too, once, beautifully, right up until I understood what the ledgers were *for*.

"I don't accept peace I haven't surveyed," I said finally. "Bad practice. Things that look settled are usually just unwalked."

He laughed, that laugh of his, half despair and half delight. "You're impossible."

"I'm a cartographer. Same thing."

* * *

Thursday came gray and soft, the kind of morning Vellharrow specialized in now — weather with texture, weather that seemed to have opinions. I packed the case properly this time: instruments, blank vellum, the new atlas of the restored country in fair copy (all but its final page), and, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom like a relic, which it was, the original moving map. Its ink had gone still. It lay flat and quiet, a finished thing at last, its purpose discharged — but I carried it anyway, because a map that saved a country deserves retirement among its own kind, and because I am sentimental about documents and have stopped apologizing for it.

Tobias met me at the gate of Ashenwell, which was no longer crooked, though the town refused to call the Crooked Wolf anything else. Some names are load-bearing.

"You'll come back through it," he said. Not a question. He had learned that about doors and about me.

"When the blank page has something on it worth showing you. Or when it doesn't. Whichever teaches me more."

"And your choice?" he said. "The one you've been not-making all week?"

I looked east, at the valley, at the river that had come back on a Tuesday, at the life with his laughter in it. I looked west, at the gate, and beyond it at the road that faded into a country no instrument could fix, waiting for its third cartographer.

"I'm choosing," I said, "to keep choosing. For a while. It's the only method I trust."

He took my hand, pressed something into it, and stepped back before I could protest: a stub of surveyor's chalk, worn round, the same kind Ilsa had used to write names on walls until the wind ran out of wind.

"Incase you find anything out there that needs keeping," he said.

I closed my fist around it. Then I turned to the Wolf, laid my palm flat against wood that had been bent twice and straightened once, and pushed — and the door swung inward onto a horizon that was not blank, exactly, but *undecided*: hills sketched in possibility, a sky the color of fresh paper, and far off, at the limit of seeing, a line of mountains that shifted slightly when I blinked, as if they were still conferring among themselves about where, precisely, they wished to be.

I stepped through with my blank pages and my chalk and my two names, and behind me — faintly, unmistakably — I heard the mountains begin, at last, to move.
