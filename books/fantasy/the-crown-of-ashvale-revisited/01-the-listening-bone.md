## 1. The Listening Bone

The bone had been soaking in lye and rainwater for nine days when Wendell Blackwood finally judged it ready to listen.

She lifted it from the crock with both hands, letting the gray water sheet off its curve, and held it up to the lamplight of her workshop above Greyhollow. A sheep's scapula, broad as a serving platter, still carrying the faint sweet-rot smell of the bath that stripped it clean of everything it had been in life. She turned it slowly. The light moved across its surface like water over river stones.

Most carvers would have seen only a shapeless thing, a blank to be worked until it surrendered to their design. Wendell saw a door.

"Right," she said softly, to no one, to everyone. "Let's see what you've got to say."

She set the bone on her workbench on a pad of felt, clamped it gentle at the edges — too tight a grip made the voices flinch, she'd learned that before she was ten — and took up her finest chisel. The blade was older than she was, its handle worn to the color of honey by three generations of Blackwood hands, and it fit her palm the way her own teeth fit her jaw.

Outside, Greyhollow went about its evening business below her window: cart wheels grinding over cobbles slick with sea fog, a fishwife arguing price with a man who smelled of tar even from two streets away, gulls screaming their endless grievances off the harbor pilings. The town clung to the cliffside like a limpet, all slate roofs and crooked chimneys, half smuggler's den and half honest fishing village, depending on which constable you asked and how much coin changed hands first. From up here, through the diamond panes warped by age, the lanterns coming out along the harbor road looked like a string of drowned stars.

Wendell touched chisel to bone.

The first cut always felt like knocking. She carved a shallow arc where the shoulder blade's spine would rise, following the grain, and beneath the small bright sound of steel on calcium she felt the other sound begin — the one that wasn't sound at all, that lived behind her ears and under her tongue, that rose up through her wrist like cold water through a well rope.

*Cold,* said something in the bone. *It was so cold.*

She didn't startle. She'd stopped startling at voices twenty years ago, when she was seven and her grandmother pressed her first carving into her hands and told her, *They only want to be heard once more, little wren. Give them that, and they'll give you the roads.* But her chest tightened all the same, because she knew this voice already. She'd been expecting it for nine days, since Tam Corrigan had come up her stairs with his hat wrung in his fists and his eyes red as butcher's meat, and laid a pouch of silver on her bench without counting it.

His boy. Little Joss, six years old, swept off the breakwater by the spring tide three weeks past. They'd found him two days later tangled in the nets off Gull Rock, his small face gone soft and strange, and the priest at the low chapel had said all the proper words over a box too short for a grown man's coffin. But Tam Corrigan was not an honest fisherman, whatever he told the crown's tax men, and honest priests could not do what needed doing. So he'd climbed the outside stair to the woman above the chandler's shop, the one who kept to herself, the one who was said to carve *memorials*, and he'd paid her in silver that smelled of other, less lawful transactions.

Wendell carved. The arc became the suggestion of a wave. The wave curled, and within it, guided by nothing but her hands' old knowledge, a small figure began to emerge — a boy running, arms out, mouth open in laughter rather than terror. That was the trick of it, the gift and the craft braided together so tightly that even she sometimes couldn't say where one ended. The dead spoke through the bone, but they spoke in shapes, in pressures, in the way the grain resisted or yielded. Her job was to translate pressure into beauty.

*The water pulled me down and I wasn't scared till I couldn't find the top.*

"I know," Wendell murmured. Her voice came out rough. It always did, during the listening. "I'm sorry, love. I'm sorry we were slow."

*I looked for Mama's red shawl. I thought if I could see the red I'd know which way was up.*

Wendell closed her eyes. This was the part no one who paid her ever understood, the part that made the gift a curse dressed in comfort's clothing: she did not hear these things at a distance. She heard them the way you hear your own heartbeat, intimate and inescapable, and for the length of the carving she was six years old and drowning, and there was no red shawl anywhere in the green dark.

Her chisel did not stop. That was the discipline her grandmother had beaten into her — gently, with stories instead of switches, but beaten in all the same. *Grief is a current, wren. You don't fight a current. You carve a channel for it.* So the chisel moved, and the boy in the wave grew clearer: the cowlick at his crown, the gap where he'd lost his first tooth, the little wooden boat he'd been holding when the tide took him, which the sea had kept and his mother mourned almost as much.

*Tell Papa the boat wasn't his fault. He told me not to take it past the posts and I did it anyway. Tell him I'm sorry I didn't mind.*

"He knows," Wendell whispered. "He'll know, when he sees this. You'll make sure of it yourself." That was the other half of the craft, the half that let her sleep: a memorial carving done true did not just record the dead. It carried them. Set it on a mantel, and the grief in the house would find somewhere to go besides sideways into anger or down into silence. Tam Corrigan would look at this wave and this laughing boy every morning for the rest of his life, and every morning the carving would say what the boy needed said, in the wordless way that bone remembered.

*It's warm where the light comes through the water. Did you know that? Everybody thinks it's cold but near the top it's warm like summer.*

"I didn't know," Wendell said. "Thank you for telling me."

That was the secret she never spoke aloud, not to clients, not to the few friends she allowed herself, not even in prayer, if what she did in the dark hours counted as prayer: the dead were mostly kind. Mostly frightened, mostly lonely, mostly full of small observations and unfinished tendernesses. The plague-talk that crept up from the lowlands lately — restless corpses, gifted devils raising the dead to walk, the crown's criers reading proclamations in market squares three valleys over — none of it matched anything she had ever heard in thirty years of listening. The dead she knew wanted what any tired traveler wanted. Rest, and someone to tell the journey to.

She worked until the candle had burned down two finger-widths, and then some more, because the boy's voice was fading now, going thin and peaceful the way they always did as the carving took their weight. The wave curled complete around the running child. In the last pass of the chisel she added what her grandmother had called the listening line — a single hair-fine groove tracing the bone's natural grain from edge to edge, invisible unless you knew to look, the channel down which a voice could travel forever.

*There's my mama's shawl,* said Joss Corrigan, wonderingly. *Oh, there it is. There's the red.*

And then the bone was only bone, warm under her hands, silent in the way that a finished song is silent.

Wendell sat back and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, impatient with herself. Thirty years of this, and still it undid her every time. She blew out a long breath, trimmed the candle, and examined her work with the cold professional eye she showed to clients. It was good. It was very good, though she'd never say so; modesty was another wall in the fortress of her careful life. A laughing boy in a curling wave, carved from a plain sheep's scapula that any farmer's wife might have bought for soup stock. Nothing in it that a magistrate could call witchery. Nothing that a frightened neighbor could point to and say *there, that's the mark of the gifted.*

That was the whole art of surviving in Greyhollow, Wendell had learned. Not hiding the gift — hiding the evidence of it. Carvings that looked like folk art. A reputation for being odd but harmless, the spinster carver who talked to herself over her bench. And underneath, threaded through the town's other economy, the real work: the memorial pieces, yes, but also the walking-sticks sold to certain travelers whose notches marked safe paths through the Thornfell passes, paths no living map recorded because she'd learned them from dead miners and dead smugglers and one very chatty highwayman who'd died in a snowdrift in the year of her birth and never stopped complaining about it.

The dead remembered everything. Roads, grudges, where bodies were buried, where bodies were *hidden*. A hearer with good sense could live comfortably forever on that knowledge, provided she stayed quiet, stayed useful, and stayed small.

*Provided,* said the sour voice in her head that sounded like her grandmother, *the world stays quiet too. And the world, wren, has been getting loud.*

Wendell pushed the thought away and reached for the beeswax and linseed oil, warming them between her palms before working the finish into the carving. The bone drank the light and began to glow, soft as a pearl. Tomorrow she would carry it down to the Corrigan house herself, wrapped in cloth, and refuse the extra coin Tam would try to press on her, and say the things one said. *He's at peace. He wanted you to know it wasn't your fault.* The liturgy of consolation, performed so many times she could do it in her sleep.

A knock at the workshop door made her hands go still.

Nobody knocked at this hour. Nobody knocked at all, usually — clients came by day, sent up by the chandler below with a nod and a name, and her smuggler clients came after midnight and knew to whistle the harbor tune twice rather than knock. A knock at dusk, heavy and official-sounding, sat wrong against her skin like a coat cut for someone else.

She covered the carving with its cloth, unhurried, though her pulse had picked up. In the small mirror by the door she checked her face: calm, mildly curious, a middle-aged woman interrupted at her needlework, essentially. Then she opened the door a hand's width, the chain on.

Marta Quill stood on the landing, breathless, her shawl pinned crooked. Marta was the chandler's wife, a solid sensible woman who had known Wendell for fifteen years and asked no questions, which was why Wendell bought her tallow and her gossip from the same shop.

"You've a visitor waiting at the back of the shop," Marta said, low and fast. "Came in off the pass road not an hour ago, mud to the knee, wouldn't give a name. But Wendell—" she glanced over her shoulder at the darkening stairwell, and when she turned back her sensible face had gone pale, "—he asked for you by the *other* name. The one nobody uses. And he said to tell you the shrine at Coldmere burned on Tuesday, and the riders are asking after hearers in every town from here to the capital, and there's a bounty posted with a description that fits you closer than your own looking glass."

The fog pressed in through the window behind Wendell, gray and cold and smelling of the drowned sea. On the bench under its cloth, the boy in the wave waited to bring comfort to a grieving house, and for the first time in thirty years, Wendell Blackwood was afraid to touch a finished carving — afraid of what the last whisper in it might be, afraid the dead had already begun trying to warn her.

"Tell him," she said quietly, "I'll come down directly."

* * *

She took the stairs slowly, buying herself the length of the climb to compose her face, and found the back room of the chandler's shop lit by a single storm lantern. The man waiting there was Dunc Ferris, a mule-train runner she'd guided over the high passes eleven times, a weathered slab of a man who feared nothing alive. He was standing with his back to the wall, both boots planted, the stance of a man who'd ridden hard through country that had stopped feeling safe.

"Wendell," he said, and didn't waste breath on greetings. "We need to talk about the winter stores you're so fond of. Because I don't think you're going to want to be here when the snow flies."

Behind her, up the dark stairwell, the finished carving sat wrapped in its cloth, holding a drowned boy's last warm words — and somewhere beyond the pass, in the burning dark where a shrine had stood, the first smoke of the hunt was already drifting toward Greyhollow.
