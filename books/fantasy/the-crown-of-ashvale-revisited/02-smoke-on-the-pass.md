## 2. Smoke on the Pass

The child's femur had been drying on the shelf for nine days when it finally spoke.

Wendell Blackwood was grinding walnut ink at her workbench when the sound came—not a sound in her ears, exactly, but in the small hollow behind them, where a word might live if a word were made of cold water and river stones. She set down the pestle. The workshop above Greyhollow was dim with afternoon, light coming slantwise through horn panes gone amber with age, and in that amber light the bone lay pale as a peeled birch branch.

She had carved it for Tam Wicker's boy, drowned in the millrace three weeks past. A memorial piece: the femur scored with running water, with reeds bending, with a small dog chasing something just out of reach along the shaft. Nine days of fine work, and now the bone wanted to say what the boy had not managed to say to anyone living.

Wendell wiped her hands on her apron out of habit, though there was nothing to wipe. Then she lifted the bone in both palms, the way one lifts a bird that has flown into a window—carefully, ready for anything.

*Cold,* said the dead boy, in the voice of cold water and river stones. *It went so fast. I didn't mean to go under. Tell Da the dog knew. The dog tried.*

She sat with it a long while, the way she always did, letting the words settle into her like sediment into still water. This was the part no one who envied the gift ever understood—the sitting. The hearing itself took less time than a sneeze. It was the carrying afterward that took days.

When she finally set the bone down, her eyes were wet, and she was already composing what she would tell Tam Wicker tomorrow at the market: that his son had not suffered, that the dog had stood at the race's edge and howled until they pulled the body out, that the boy wanted him to know the dog had known. All of it true. All of it bearable, if you did not look too closely at the machinery underneath.

That was the trick of her whole life, Wendell thought as she trimmed the wick of her lamp against the early dark. Bearable, if you did not look too closely.

Outside, Greyhollow was lighting its evening fires, smoke rising from chimneys cut into the mountainside like the town itself was exhaling. Below her workshop, the smuggler road wound down through scree and pine toward the valley floor, invisible from any crown road, known only to those who paid her to walk it. She was, by every record the crown kept, a carver of memorial bones. Nothing more.

She blew out the lamp and began packing the finished carving in wool.

* * *

Brannick Hale arrived two nights later, smelling of woodsmoke that was not his own hearth.

He came up the outside stair without knocking, which he never did, and stood dripping in her doorway with snowmelt on his boots and a look on his face she had seen only twice before—once when the plague carts came through the low country, and once the night his brother died in the pass.

"Shut the door," he said.

She shut it. "You're early. You're not due till the thaw."

"I'm not here about the pass." He lowered himself onto her stool by the stove without asking, another thing he never did, and held his hands out to the coals. They were shaking. Brannick Hale had carried kegs of Ashvale brandy over the Thornfell shoulder in a whiteout without his hands shaking. "Two valleys over. Correnvale. You know the shrine there—the stacked ones, the old cairn-shrine where the drovers leave tokens."

"The Weeping Cairn." Everyone knew it. Three generations of travelers had wedged bone charms into its crevices, little carved hopes and griefs, left anonymous because leaving your name felt like tempting fate.

"It's ash," Brannick said. "Crown riders put it to the torch a week ago. Ground the bones under their heels after. There's a woman in Correnvale who kept the shrine—old Mother Ilse, read tokens for copper, nothing more than a fortune-teller really—and they took her out of her house in front of the whole village and—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "They didn't hang her. Hanging's too clean for hearers now, apparently. They're posting bills in every market town between here and the capital. Fifty crowns silver for information leading to the arrest of anyone who *hears the dead through carved or uncarved bone.* Their words, near enough. I copied one down." He fumbled in his coat and produced a scrap of paper, soft with handling, and smoothed it on her workbench beside the half-finished reed-carving she'd been doing for Tam Wicker's commission.

Wendell read it twice. The letters were official, blocky, printed from a press somewhere—the crown did not hand-write its cruelties anymore, it typeset them. *By order of the Lord Inquisitor's office: the restless dead are a plague upon the realm, spread by traffickers in forbidden arts.* Her own trade, described in a single line. *Hearers are carriers of this corruption. Sheltering one is treason.*

"Traitors get the rope," Brannick said quietly. "Hearers get worse. They're saying the Inquisitor's men have instruments. Questions that don't stop."

Wendell set the bill down. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were perfectly steady, which seemed like someone else's hands. "Who else knows you've seen this?"

"The usual circle. And the usual circle knows better than to speak of it." He looked at her then, directly, and she understood that the usual circle included an understanding about her—that everyone who bought her guidance through the dead passes had long ago worked out why her paths were always right, and had agreed, silently and completely, never to name it. That agreement was the wall that kept her alive. Walls could burn like shrines.

"You need to run, Wendell."

"Where?" She heard her own voice come out flat and practical, and was grateful for it. "South is the capital's shadow. West is sea. East is the Thornfell interior, and you know what the interior's been like since autumn." Since autumn. Since the first reports of graves opening. Since the corpses found walking, not raging, not hungry—just *walking*, uphill, all of them uphill, toward the high passes, as if summoned or fleeing, no one could agree which. That was the plague the bill spoke of. That was what had lit the fire under the crown's fear and turned it into torches.

"Over the shoulder, then. I'll take you myself. First hard frost-glaze, we move."

"And leave everything?" She gestured at the workshop around them—at the shelves of drying bones each tagged with a name and a fee paid or owed, at the stores below stairs she'd spent the whole autumn laying in: salted pork in its crocks, barley and dried apples, tallow, lamp oil, a cord of split pine stacked to the rafters. At the cellar where forty years of her grandmother's carvings slept in oiled cloth, generations of the dead of Greyhollow and beyond, entrusted to her family because her family listened. "Leave them for whoever breaks in? Leave my grandmother's bones for the first rider who kicks the door?"

"Bones can be buried deeper."

"They can also be *burned*," she said, sharper than she meant to, "by whoever finds them, if I'm not here to keep them quiet." She hadn't told him that part. That the dead in her keeping did not sleep unless someone tended them—that the carvings were less memorials than anchors, each one holding a soul gently against the current that had been pulling things uphill all autumn. If she ran, and the collection was found and destroyed, every anchor broke at once. She did not know what that would do. She suspected the crown did not either, and was burning shrines anyway, cheerfully, by printed order.

Brannick studied her across the stove-light. "How bad is it," he said. Not a question about the stores.

"Bad," she admitted. "Worse than the bills say. Worse than the crown knows, maybe." She almost told him about the direction the dead walked. Uphill. Always uphill. Toward Thornfell, whose mines had been sealed since the founding war, whose gate-town still stood garrisoned at the mouth of roads no map admitted to. But telling him would make it real in a way she wasn't ready for, and besides—she had learned long ago that the gift was a knife you showed people one edge at a time.

"So stay and be taken," Brannick said. "Is that the plan?"

"The plan is winter," Wendell said. "Nobody rides the high valleys in deep winter. The riders are burning shrines in the low country because that's where they can ride. Come thaw—" She heard herself building the argument the way she built a path through scree, testing each stone before putting weight on it, and hated how much of it was hope dressed as arithmetic. "Come thaw, I'll have moved the collection. Come thaw, there'll be new routes even they don't have. I've got until spring, Brannick. Everyone's got until spring. Snow keeps secrets better than I do."

He was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the town bell rang the ninth hour, and a dog answered it, and life in Greyhollow went on being ordinary for one more hour, one more night.

"When I cross the shoulder next," he said at last, standing, "I'm bringing you out whether you pack or not. Say whatever you like. I'll carry you if I have to."

"Get home safe first," she said. "Then make threats."

He paused at the door, snowmelt still dark on his shoulders, and gave her the ghost of his usual grin. "Burn anything with names on it," he said. "Starting tonight."

Then he was gone down the stair, and she was alone with the bill and the lamp and the shelves of patient bones.

* * *

She did not sleep. She spent the night doing what Brannick had told her, but slower, and more.

Names came off the tags first—she fed them to the stove one by one, watching the ink curl and vanish, feeling each small unlacing as a name left a tag and stayed only in her memory, which was safer than paper and harder to search. Then the ledger of commissions, pages of clients and fees going back eleven years, into the fire in handfuls. Then her grandmother's teaching notes, the diagrams of which grain of bone held which kind of voice, written in a cipher of her grandmother's own devising that Wendell alone could read—but alone was a thinner protection than it used to be, with instruments and questions that didn't stop.

By the third hour the workshop smelled of burning paper instead of pine, and she sat back on her heels among the ash and let herself feel, for one unguarded minute, the shape of what was happening. Eleven years of careful life, and it fit in a stove. That was the lesson of the night, she supposed. Everything careful fits in a stove, eventually. Either you burn it yourself or someone burns it for you.

The bones watched from the shelves. She had taken down only the ones with voices still in them—the finished pieces awaiting delivery, the anchors of her keeping—and wrapped them in oilcloth, and now they lay in three traveling chests like sleeping passengers. Forty-one souls, give or take the few too faded to count. She pressed her palm flat against the nearest chest and felt nothing, which was how it should be; the dead only spoke through the carving, never through the box, and she was grateful tonight for the silence of mere wood.

*Toward Thornfell,* whispered something that was not quite a voice, in the hollow behind her ears where the cold-water words lived. *All of us. You know where. You have always known where.*

"Sleep," she told it, the way her grandmother had taught her, palm on the lid. "No one's asking you anything tonight."

But the whisper stayed, the way a draft stays in a room with one bad pane, and near dawn she gave up on sleep entirely and climbed the outside stair to the roof, where she could see the pass.

Snow-veiled, blue in the pre-dawn, the shoulder of Thornfell stood against the paling sky like a sleeping animal's back. And beyond it, fainter than the stars going out, a smudge of grey rose from the far side of the ridge—not cloud, not morning mist. Smoke. Too steady for a campfire, too wide for a chimney. Someone was burning something large on the other side of the mountain, and had been for hours, and the wind that carried it to her nose smelled wrong for timber. Sweeter. Fatter. Like resin and rendered fat and old, old bone.

Wendell stood on her own roof in the freezing dark and breathed it in, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling that spring was a lie she had sold Brannick and half-sold herself. The riders were not waiting for the thaw. The riders were already in the high valleys—her valleys, the ones no living map recorded—and something ahead of them was driving the dead uphill faster every night, and sooner or later both floods would break over Greyhollow at once.

She went down, banked the stove, and took out the one thing she had not yet packed: her grandmother's oldest chisel, whalebone-handled, worn to the shape of her grip. She wrapped it in wool and slid it inside her boot, against the ankle, where a knife would go if she were the kind of person who carried knives.

Three chests. One apprentice due at midmorning. One town full of neighbors who had, every one of them, some reason to wonder what she really was, and fifty crowns' worth of temptation now standing in every market square between here and the capital.

The bell below rang the dawn hour. Wendell Blackwood blew out her lamp, and in the grey light beginning at the window, she started counting what she could carry—and what, come what may, she could not.
