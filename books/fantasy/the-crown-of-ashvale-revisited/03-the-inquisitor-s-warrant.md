## 3. The Inquisitor's Warrant

The bells of Greyhollow rang wrong.

Wendell Blackwood heard it from her workshop above the chandler's row, where she had been sanding the spine of a whalebone comb for the ferryman's wife. Greyhollow's bells were lazy things, rung by a boy who pulled the rope the way he did everything—halfheartedly, with his mind on breakfast. But this morning they came fast and hard, three strikes and a pause, three strikes and a pause, and beneath them she could hear hooves on the causeway stones, many horses, moving in formation rather than in the loose scatter of traders or the careful quiet of smugglers.

She set down the comb. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that had learned to shake when it was safe to do so, alone, behind a locked door, after the danger had passed.

*Three and a pause,* she thought. *Riders.*

The workshop smelled of tallow and bone dust and the particular sweetness of boiled linseed oil, and for one absurd moment she wanted only to stay here, to finish the comb, to live inside the small warm circle of lamplight and craft as though the world outside were still the world she had spent thirty years arranging. She looked at the shelf above her bench—the memorial pieces waiting for delivery, each wrapped in wool—and at the piece she had finished two nights past, the drowned child's whalebone bird, which she had not yet been able to deliver because its mother could not yet bear to hold it.

The bird sat in its box like a held breath.

"Tam," she called down through the floorboards. "Tam, are you up?"

No answer. The boy's cot behind the house was empty, which was nothing unusual—Tam rose before dawn to fetch water and gossip in equal measure—but today the emptiness of it felt like a door left open in winter.

She moved through the workshop with the economy of long practice. The false back of the coal cupboard swung out; behind it, the iron box went into the flour barrel, the flour scooped over it in a white shroud. Her grandmother's letters—written in the old hearth-script, the kind that got women burned in the southern provinces—went into the stove, already lit against the morning chill. She watched the paper curl and blacken and thought: *Grandmother, you told me this day would come. You told me to be smoke.*

But she could not be smoke. Not entirely. Winter stores filled her cellar—salt pork, hardtack, a cask of pickled eel, forty pounds of barley bought at autumn prices she'd have to pay again if she fled now and came back to find it spoiled or stolen. And there was Tam. And there was the bird in its box, waiting for a grieving mother who deserved better than an empty workshop and a rumor.

The hoofbeats stopped below. Voices, sharp and official, in the street.

Wendell put on her plainest apron, the one stained with ordinary work, and went down the stairs to open her door before someone kicked it in.

* * *

They had come to Greyhollow the way weather comes—suddenly, from the north, inevitable. There were twelve of them in the square by the well, crown riders in ash-grey cloaks, their livery marked with the sunburst-and-scales of the Inquisitorial Office. The town had turned out the way towns do: shutters cracked, faces pale in doorways, the fishwife Marta standing too close to the well with her arms crossed as though she might defend it personally.

At the center of them stood a woman on foot—not tall, but carrying herself as though height were a technicality. Grey hair cut short as a soldier's. A long coat the color of wet slate, and across her breast the warrant-chain of office, heavy links catching the grey morning light. She held a scroll in one hand and read from it in a voice pitched to carry without shouting, the voice of someone who had done this many times and expected no more resistance than the tide expects from sand.

"—by order of the Regency Council of Ashvale, in the matter of the plague of unrest among the dead, all persons practicing the illicit art of hearing—that is, communing with the dead through carved remains—are hereby commanded to surrender themselves for examination. Sheltering a hearer is itself a crime. Concealment is itself a confession."

*A hearer.* Wendell stood in her doorway, half in shadow, and felt the word go through her like a splinter finding the quick. Not *witch*, not *bone-speaker*, not any of the soft regional names her trade had worn for generations like comfortable old coats. *Hearer.* Someone in the capital had written this warrant carefully. Someone had chosen the word that made the gift sound like an affliction.

The inquisitor lowered the scroll. Up close, in the flat light, her face was younger than her hair suggested—forty perhaps, maybe less, with lines around her mouth that looked carved rather than earned. Her eyes moved across the assembled town the way Wendell's own eyes moved across a fresh piece of bone: reading grain, reading flaw, reading what the material wanted to become.

"I am Serah Vane," she said. "Inquisitor of the Third Warrant. I will take testimony in the meetinghouse, beginning now. I would remind the good people of Greyhollow that the dead do not rest in Thornfell, that they walk the high passes, and that every day of concealment costs lives." A pause. "Bring me your hearers, and I will bring your dead their peace."

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. Somewhere a gull screamed over the harbor, obscene and alive.

Then Tam stepped forward.

Wendell saw it happen the way one sees a cart wheel come loose on a steep road—with total clarity and no power whatsoever to stop it. The boy was fourteen, all elbows and earnestness, his apprentice's smock buttoned crooked as always. He had been standing near the meetinghouse steps, and when Vane's gaze swept over him, something in his face answered it. Fear, yes, but also a terrible eagerness, the look of a child who knows a secret and has just been given permission, any permission, to set it down.

"He—" Tam's voice cracked. He pointed, and his finger found Wendell's doorway across the whole width of the square, steady as a compass needle. "She hears them. Mistress Blackwood. She carves the bones and they talk to her. I've seen it. I've *heard* it."

The square turned toward Wendell like a single animal scenting wind.

Later—much later, in the wagon, in the mountains, in the years after—she would try to remember whether she hated him in that moment. She never quite could. What she remembered instead was how young he looked, and how relieved, as though he had been carrying her secret in his chest for two years and it had grown too heavy, and Vane's question had been the first hand ever offered to help set it down. Betrayal, she learned, rarely looks like malice up close. It looks like exhaustion. It looks like a boy who wants, just once, to be the one who isn't hiding.

Vane looked at Wendell across the square. And Wendell, because there was nothing else left to be, stepped out of her doorway into the open light.

* * *

The meetinghouse smelled of cold stone and old incense, and it was full of her neighbors, which was somehow worse than being full of strangers. Vane sat behind the altar table with the warrant-scroll unrolled before her and two clerks flanking her, quills scratching. Wendell stood where she was told to stand, on the worn flagstone where brides and penitents had stood for two hundred years.

"Your name."

"Wendell Blackwood. Bone-carver. Licensed chandler's-row artisan." She gave the trade words weight, made them a wall.

"Carver." Vane turned a page. "And what do you carve?"

"Memorials. Keepsakes. Buttons, if the commission pays." A ripple through the room—someone almost laughed, then strangled it. "Whalebone mostly. It takes a fine edge."

"And does it speak?"

The question hung there. Wendell met the inquisitor's eyes and measured her life against the next ten seconds. Deny, and Tam had already named her before a dozen witnesses; denial became a second crime. Confess, and she became property of the warrant. There was a third road—the one her grandmother would have taken, the lie so brazen it bent back around to truth—but Vane's face offered no purchase for it. This was not a woman who could be charmed. This was a woman who had already decided, and was merely assembling the record.

"It speaks," Wendell said quietly. "When I carve the dead true enough. They leave... an echo. In the work. I hear it while I carve. It fades when the piece is done." That much was nearly honest, and honesty, she had found, was most useful in small doses, like arsenic.

Murmurs ran the pews. Old Cobb the netmender crossed himself with a fisherman's sloppy flourish. But Marta the fishwife, whose son's finger-bone ring Wendell had carved three winters past so the boy could say goodbye from the bottom of the sea—Marta looked at her and did not look away, and that was its own kind of testimony.

Vane raised a hand and the room stilled. "The charge is hearing. The penalty under the Unrest Statutes is forfeiture and examination." She let *examination* sit in the air until it curdled. Then she did something Wendell did not expect. She came around the table.

Up close, Serah Vane smelled of horse and ink and something faintly medicinal, camphor perhaps. Her eyes were brown and very tired and very awake, all at once, like a woman who had learned to sleep standing.

"But I am not here for you, Mistress Blackwood," she said, and her voice dropped to something meant for Wendell alone, though the whole room strained to hear. "If I wanted another pyre, I'd have brought kindling. I'm here because three weeks ago the graves at Thornfell Gate opened themselves. Because the dead there don't rise—they *flee*. They come down the mountain in the night, dozens of them, and they stand at the walls looking back the way animals look back at a fire. And every hearer we've sent up there has either gone mad or gone silent, and the last one died with her hands over her ears." She paused. "You've worked the Thornfell passes. The smugglers' roads. Don't insult us both by denying it; the toll-records name you."

"I guide carts," Wendell said. "I know paths."

"You know paths the living didn't make." Vane's gaze was relentless and, strangely, not unkind. "I need someone who can listen to what's up there and tell me what it wants. Not a zealot. Not a court hearer who'll tell me what the Council pays to hear. Someone who's kept her head down for thirty years because she understands exactly what this gift is worth and what it costs." Her jaw tightened. "Help me, and you return to your shop when it's done. Refuse, and you go to the capital in irons, and the examiners there are not me."

From the pews, Tam made a small sound, half sob, half protest, and Wendell looked at him—at the boy who had unburdened himself and found the burden only changed hands—and felt something settle in her that was not forgiveness, not yet, but was perhaps its raw ore.

"The dead are running," Wendell said slowly. "Not rising. Running."

Something flickered across Vane's face—there and gone, a crack in plaster. "You know this."

"I know what the mountain feels like lately." Wendell chose each word like a step on river stones. "Even deaf folk cross the street when the Thornfell wind comes down. Something's wrong up there, Inquisitor. Wronger than a warrant can fix."

"Then you'll help me fix what a warrant can't." Vane straightened, turned to the room, and her voice resumed its public register, flat and final. "Wendell Blackwood is remanded to the custody of the Third Warrant for the duration of the Thornfell inquiry. Her property is sealed, not forfeit. Any interference with the inquiry will be treated as interference with the Crown." She rolled the scroll with a snap. "We ride within the hour."

As the clerks packed their things and the townsfolk filed out past her—some avoiding her eyes, some unable to stop staring—Wendell caught one last sight of Tam by the door, wringing his cap in both hands, mouth working around words that wouldn't come. She gave him the smallest nod she could manage. *Go home. Lock my shop. Feed yourself.* The nod said all of it, and his face crumpled with the weight of receiving it.

Marta pressed through the crowd at the door, and in the press of bodies her hand found Wendell's and pushed something into it—a small shape in cloth. Wendell knew it by feel before she looked: the finger-bone ring, the son's farewell, carried three winters in the fishwife's pocket.

"For listening," Marta whispered, fierce and low. "Come back and give it back to me, you hear? Come back."

Then the riders were forming up in the square, and the bells of Greyhollow rang again—wrong still, but a different wrong, the slow toll of a town watching one of its own led away—and Wendell Blackwood climbed into the supply wagon between a crate of salt beef and a cage of carrier pigeons, and looked back once at the roofline of her workshop, where the chimney smoked gently, obediently, over everything she had hidden and everything she had burned.

The wagon lurched north. Toward Thornfell.

And beneath the rattle of wheels and the creak of harness, so faint she might have imagined it, so faint it might have been only the salt beef settling in its crate—something sighed.

Like a held breath, finally beginning to be released.
