## 4. What the Dead Refuse to Say

The wagon smelled of tallow and wet canvas and the sour iron of her own chains.

Wendell had counted the links twice—fourteen from wrist to wrist, enough to let her hands meet but not part, enough to work a small knife if she'd had one, which she did not. The manacles were good crown steel, cold even through the wool of her sleeves. Whoever had fitted them had done it with care, without cruelty, and somehow that was worse.

She sat with her back against a crate of dried apples, knees drawn up, watching the world through the gap where the wagon's canvas flap had been tied wrong. Greyhollow was already behind them—a last smear of chimney smoke against the grey shoulder of the pass—and ahead the road climbed through pine shadow toward Thornfell, toward whatever it was that Inquisitor Serah Vane wanted her to hear.

The wagon lurched over a root. Somewhere ahead, a rider's voice carried back: *mind the axle, mind the axle*, repeated like a prayer against bad roads.

Wendell closed her eyes and did what she always did when fear came: she listened.

Not with her ears. Her ears gave her only wheels and wind and the mutter of soldiers who believed their prisoner couldn't hear them anyway. She listened with the other sense, the one that lived somewhere behind her sternum, the one that woke whenever bone was near.

And there was bone near. There was always bone near, if you knew how to feel for it—the little reliquaries soldiers wore at their throats, the knuckle-bones in dice cups, the teeth of horses ground into the road grit by ten thousand hooves. Most of it was quiet. Old bone forgot its words the way old stone forgets rain. But every few miles something would brush against her awareness like a fish against a line, and she would flinch, and school her face, and hope no one noticed.

Someone had noticed. That was the whole of her trouble now.

*You'll ride with us to Thornfell,* Vane had said, not an hour after the arrest, while Tam wept in the doorway of the workshop and the riders went through her carvings like men looting a church. *You won't be harmed. You'll listen, and you'll tell me what you hear, and then we'll see about your chains.*

*I don't hear anything,* Wendell had said, because lying was free and truth was expensive.

Vane had looked at her the way a smith looks at iron that claims to be wood. *Then you're no use to me alive or dead, and I should leave you in Greyhollow to be dealt with by the town that sold you.* A pause, precise as a chisel-tap. *But I watched your face when my sergeant brought out that soldier's token. So we both know.*

Now, in the swaying dark of the wagon, Wendell turned the memory over and found the shape of her mistake. The token. Of course. Vane had carried it into the workshop like bait on a hook, a scrap of lead stamped with a company sigil, and Wendell—tired, frightened, arrogant in her craft—had let her eyes linger half a heartbeat too long. Half a heartbeat was all it took. Hearers were caught not by what they said but by what they failed to hide.

She flexed her chained hands. Cold seeped up through the wagon boards. The light through the flap-gap was going the color of pewter, which meant evening, which meant they'd make camp soon, and camp meant questions.

Better, then, to choose her own question first.

* * *

They stopped at a ford where the river ran loud and white over stones, and the soldiers built a fire under a leaning spruce and boiled barley in a dented pot. Two of them brought Wendell down from the wagon and set her near the flames—not kindness, she understood, but practicality; frozen prisoners stop being useful—and left a bowl within reach of her chained hands.

Vane sat across the fire, hood back, writing in a ledger by the light. She wrote the way she did everything: without wasted motion, without looking up. Around her the soldiers kept a careful distance, the way men keep distance from a dog that has bitten once.

Wendell ate. The barley was scorched and there was too much salt, and she thought absurdly of her workshop above Greyhollow, of the memorial carving she'd been finishing for the drowned boy when the riders came—half-done, his small face still waiting for its mouth. Someone would find it. Or no one would. Either way it would sit unfinished forever, a child's grief suspended mid-stroke, and that thought hurt more than the manacles.

*Finish him,* she told the boy silently, wherever he was. *I'm sorry. Finish yourself.*

"You looked at the fire," Vane said, without raising her head. "For four days you've barely looked at anything. But you looked at the fire."

"The fire is warm."

"The fire is made of pine, and pine grows on graves up here. Every tree on this pass is rooted in someone." Now Vane looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were the color of wet slate. "You hear them. Through bone. My sources in Greyhollow were specific."

"Your source in Greyhollow," Wendell said quietly, "was a fourteen-year-old boy who wanted his mother to be proud of him."

Something moved at the corner of Vane's mouth—not quite a wince, not quite agreement. "Yes. They usually are." She set down the ledger. From inside her coat she drew something wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapped it with two fingers, and held it out across the fire so the flames lit it from below.

A token. Lead, palm-sized, stamped with the sigil of the Ninth Foot—a tower between two crossed spears. It had been pierced once, near the edge, for a cord. The metal was blackened as though it had been in a fire, and bent, as though something had stepped on it.

"Dalren Ostwick," Vane said. "Corporal, Ninth Foot. One of forty-two men lost on the Weeping Stair three months past. Not killed—we found no bodies. Lost. Their patrol went down into the old galleries chasing reports of walking corpses, and they simply ceased to report. This token was found on the surface, a mile from the stair, laid neatly on a flat stone. Laid, understand? Arranged. As if someone wanted it found."

"And you think I can tell you where he is."

"I think you can tell me what he wants." Vane turned the token slowly in her fingers. "Because here is what troubles me, Blackwood. The corpses plague the lowlands. They walk into villages, they frighten cattle, they stand in doorways at night. But they do not attack. Do you understand? Forty-one incidents since midwinter, and not one confirmed killing by the restless dead. Not one. They walk, and they stand, and they *watch*, and the people panic, and the crown sends soldiers, and the soldiers go missing beneath Thornfell." She held the token out further, over the fire, its lead surface drinking the orange light. "So before I take you into that mountain, I want to know what I'm taking you into. Read it. Tell me what the corporal has to say."

"And if I say I can't?"

"Then tomorrow I turn the column around, ride back to Greyhollow, and hang you from the shrine-post as a hearer, and the hunt continues exactly as it would have anyway." No heat in it. No threat performed. Just arithmetic, done aloud. "Or you read it, and perhaps you become useful enough that hanging you becomes inconvenient. I'm told you value your life. Most people do."

Wendell looked at the token. The fire cracked and spat. Behind her, one of the soldiers shifted his weight, and his boot scraped over something buried in the road-grit—a horse tooth, maybe, or a fragment of rib from some beast dead fifty years—and it whispered at the edge of her sense, wordless, like wind through a keyhole.

Here was the trap within the trap. If she read the token truly, she proved herself, and proof meant deeper into Thornfell, closer to whatever waited there. If she refused or lied badly, she died on a shrine-post in a town that had already sold her once.

But there was a third thing, and it was this: she *wanted* to know. She had heard the restless dead at night through the floorboards of her workshop, all winter, a sound like many people murmuring in a room below. She had told herself it was the plague, the curse, the crown's excuse for murder. She had never once put her gift against it directly, because some listening cannot be taken back.

Fear sat in her chest like swallowed stone. And beneath the fear, patient as water finding a crack, curiosity.

"Give it here," she said.

* * *

The token was colder than the night justified.

That was the first thing, and it was the wrong thing. Bone held warmth the way sand holds sun—it remembered the living. Even old bone, even dust, carried a faint residue of body-heat that her gift read as presence. But this was lead, and lead should have been neutral, dead matter, silent as a spoon.

It was not silent.

The moment her chained hands closed around it, the whisper rose—not in her ears, but up through her palms, through the bones of her wrists, as if the manacles themselves had become part of the circuit. Wendell breathed out slowly and let it come, the way she let it come over her workbench, the way she'd let it come for the drowned boy: opening that inner door just wide enough.

*Cold,* said the token. *Cold cold cold, and the dark moves, the dark isn't empty, the dark is full of us, we are pressed together like grain in a sack—*

She steadied herself. Names helped. She gave the voice a name: *Dalren Ostwick, Corporal, Ninth Foot.* Naming anchored the reading, kept her from drowning in the general murmur of the dead. She pictured the man: young, probably; a soldier's token worn soft at the edges from touching.

*Ostwick. Where are you?*

The whisper sharpened, gathered itself, became almost a voice.

*Down. Down and down. We went down the Stair after the walking ones, following orders, and the walking ones let us follow—they walked ahead of us like guides, like shepherds, and we laughed about it, we joked, and then the gallery opened and—*

The voice broke. Not faded—*broke*, the way a rope breaks, sudden and total, and in the gap Wendell felt something else press against the reading. Something vast. Something that was not one dead man but many, hundreds, thousands, a congregation packed shoulder to shoulder in the dark beneath the mountain, and all of them—

All of them *afraid*.

Not angry. Not hungry for vengeance, not clawing at the world of the living in envy or rage. Afraid. The restless dead of Thornfell, the plague the crown had built its hunt upon, were not rising.

They were *fleeing*.

*We run,* the voice said, and now it was not only Ostwick, it was others layered beneath him, women and men and voices too old to name, a chorus speaking through one dead corporal's token because he was nearest, because he was willing, because someone had to say it. *We run upward. We push toward the light because below us—below us it wakes, below us the seal thins, below us He begins to count His due, and the pact is failing, the pact our king made with the thing under the roots, a generation, He asked a generation and the king said yes, the king said yes, the king said yes—*

Wendell's eyes flew open.

She was on her knees in the trampled snow beside the fire and she did not remember falling. The token lay in her palms, burning-cold, and every soldier in the camp was staring at her, and Vane had risen, ledger forgotten, slate eyes wide and fixed.

"What did you hear?" Vane's voice was level, but her hand was not steady, and Wendell saw that the inquisitor had gone pale as tallow. "Blackwood. What did you hear?"

Wendell's breath came in white plumes. Her heart slammed. She had read tokens a hundred times—grief, mostly, and love, and the small unfinished business of ordinary lives, a debt unpaid, a daughter's name spoken one last time. She had never once been *spoken through*. She had never once felt a hundred voices use a single scrap of lead as a doorway, all of them shoving to get their warning into the world of the living before the door swung shut.

"They're not rising," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away, scraped thin. "Your plague. Your restless dead. They're not attacking anyone because they aren't coming *for* anything. They're running. All of them. Out of the deep galleries, up toward the surface, toward the light—" She stopped. Swallowed. "They're fleeing something beneath Thornfell. And they wanted me to tell you: the pact is failing. The pact your first king made. He promised them a generation, and the thing below has begun to collect."

Silence, except the fire and the river.

One of the soldiers made a small involuntary sound, quickly smothered. Another spat, reflexively, as if to ward something off.

Vane crouched slowly until she was level with Wendell, and this close Wendell could see the war happening behind those slate eyes—faith against evidence, doctrine against a shaking woman kneeling in the snow holding a dead man's token.

"That's impossible," Vane said. "The founding histories say the first king won the war at Thornfell by valor. By sacrifice. There's no mention of any—"

"There wouldn't be," Wendell said. The cold of the token was climbing her arms now, settling into her chest like a second heart. "Who writes down the price when the price is shameful? Who carves *we sold a generation of souls* into the triumphal arch?" She looked down at the token, and the whisper had gone quiet again, but she could still feel them behind it, the whole packed congregation of the dead, waiting, listening to see what the living would do with the truth.

"Every corpse that walks out of those galleries," she said quietly, "is a soul trying to escape the collection. And your hunt is dragging them back."

Vane stared at her for a long moment. Then she stood, wrapped the token away in its oilcloth with careful, deliberate hands, and turned to her sergeant.

"Break camp an hour early. We make Thornfell Gate by tomorrow's dusk." Her voice was perfectly controlled, and that control was the most frightening thing Wendell had seen yet. "And Sergeant—if any man repeats what the prisoner said tonight, he runs the rest of the way."

The soldiers scattered to the wagons. Vane paused beside Wendell, looking down at her, and for a moment the inquisitor's mask slipped—not into cruelty, but into something rawer and more exhausted.

"If you've lied to me," Vane said softly, "no power under heaven will save you."

"I wish to every god I had lied," Wendell said.

Through the token's oilcloth wrapping, faint as a pulse, the whispering continued. And beneath the whisper, deeper than the corporal, older than the chorus, something else had begun—very slowly—to pay attention. Wendell felt it the way a rabbit feels a hawk's shadow cross the grass: not a sound, not a word, only the sudden absolute certainty of being *noticed* from below.

Far up the pass, in the dark where Thornfell's peak cut a notch from the stars, a single light appeared on the mountainside. It burned green, and it did not flicker, and none of the soldiers seemed able to see it at all.

Only Wendell, and, she suspected, the dead—who had all gone silent at once, the way a forest goes silent, the way prey goes silent.

Holding its breath. Waiting to see if the thing below had finally found a door it liked.
