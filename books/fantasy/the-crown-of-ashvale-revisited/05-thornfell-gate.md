## 5. Thornfell Gate

The mountains changed their voice before the town came into view.

Wendell had spent three days in the wagon learning its language—the creak of the axle like a bored elder, the rattle of chains against her wrists like impatient children, the groan of iron-rimmed wheels on stone like something old turning in its sleep. She had spent her whole life listening to things that could not speak, and so she heard it first: a thinness in the wind coming down from the pass ahead, as if the air itself had been wrung out and hung to dry.

"Thornfell Gate," Vane said from horseback, not turning around.

The town sat in the throat of a valley like a swallowed stone—walls of grey granite stained black with centuries of coal smoke, watchtowers crowned with frost, and above everything, everywhere, the mountain. Thornfell did not loom over the town; it *pressed*. Wendell felt the pressure against her skin like a hand laid flat between her shoulder blades. Beneath the snow and scree and the old mine shafts honeycombing its flanks, something waited, and the whole valley leaned toward it the way wheat leans before a storm.

And beneath the silence of the living, she could feel the other thing now—the hum she'd been aware of since the pass, growing stronger by the mile. The dead of Thornfell were not quiet. They were not even properly restless. They were *listening*, thousands upon thousands of them, turned inward toward the mountain's roots like a congregation facing an altar she could not see.

She had never felt anything like it. It frightened her more than the chains.

* * *

The gates opened for Vane's warrant without argument, but the town that received them was not the town Wendell expected.

She had imagined fear—shuttered windows, averted eyes, the sullen silence of a place under occupation. Instead the streets were full. Miners stood in doorways with their helmets still on. Women in mourning black lined the walls like crows on a fence. Children peered from behind skirts with the frank, terrible curiosity of the young. And when the wagon rolled through the gate square, the crowd did not scatter.

It pressed forward.

"Inquisitor." An old man pushed through first, hat in his hands, knuckles white around the brim. "Inquisitor Vane, they said you were coming. They said you'd brought someone who can *hear*."

The word went through the square like a spark through dry grass. *Hearer.* Wendell shrank against the wagon's rail, but there was nowhere to shrink *to*. Every face turned to her as if she were a well in a drought-stricken land.

"One at a time," Vane said, dismounting. Her voice carried the practiced authority of a woman who had learned that volume was cheaper than violence. "There is procedure. There will be order—"

A bundle hit the wagon bed beside Wendell. Then another. A knitted mitten, small, child-sized, stiff with frost. A wedding ring strung on a leather cord. A lock of hair tied with ribbon gone brown. A pipe, charred. A boot. A tooth.

Relics. The grieving of Thornfell Gate came armed with relics, and they pressed them into the hands of anyone who would take them, and the wagon took them all because there was no one to refuse.

"Please." A young woman gripped the wagon wheel, breath fogging, eyes fixed on Wendell with an intensity that hurt to look at. "My husband. Tam Wicker. Third gallery east, the collapse in autumn. They sealed the level with him inside rather than dig. Please. I only need to know if he—" Her voice broke and reassembled itself into something harder. "I need to know if he's *angry*."

"He isn't angry," Wendell said before she could stop herself.

The woman stared. Around her, the square went still as held breath.

"He's tired," Wendell went on, because it was true, because she could hear him, faintly, through the mitten clutched in the woman's fist—a man's voice worn down to a thread, saying nothing but *cold, cold, cold* in a rhythm almost like sleep. "He isn't angry at anyone. He wants you to stop standing outside in the winter waiting for him. That's what he wants. Go home. Light your fire."

The woman made a sound Wendell had heard before, from the mothers of drowned boys and the widows of lost smugglers—that single cracked note between grief and gratitude that no language had ever bothered to name. She stepped back. She went home. Half the square watched her go as if she were a message from the future.

Vane looked at Wendell for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—not warmth, nothing so generous as warmth, but a recalculation, the way a merchant recalculates when he discovers his goods are worth more than he thought.

"The town hall," Vane announced. "One hour. Those with relics will be seen in order. There will be *order*."

* * *

They set her up in the town hall like a priestess at an altar, and Wendell understood, sitting behind the trestle table with her chains hidden beneath a wool blanket, that she had just made a bargain she hadn't agreed to.

Vane had arranged it precisely: Wendell at the table, two soldiers flanking her, Vane standing behind with her warrant scroll held where the crowd could see it. The relics were laid out in rows on a cloth—dozens of them, then scores, then too many to count, as families filed past and laid down their dead's small possessions like offerings. A miner's tally-stick. A christening spoon. A doll with one eye. The hall filled with the smell of wet wool and woodsmoke and grief, which has a smell, Wendell thought, though no one speaks of it—something like candle smoke and turned earth.

"You'll read what I point to," Vane murmured, "and nothing else."

"And if I read wrong?" Wendell asked softly. "If I say nothing? If the dead are silent?"

"Then these people will decide you're a fraud, and I'll have a riot, and I'll have to declare martial law in a mining town that produces half the crown's iron." Vane's jaw tightened. "Read *well*, bone-carver."

*You're using me,* Wendell thought. *Good. Because I'm about to use you right back.*

She took up the first relic—a brass button polished smooth by a thumb—and let herself listen.

The trick of it, the part no one who feared her gift ever understood, was that hearing the dead required no ritual, no blood, no chanting. It required only the same thing any craft requires: attention. She held the button the way she held fresh bone, feeling for the grain of it, the place where a life had soaked in and left its mark. And there it was—a man's voice, cheerful and clipped, complaining about the price of ale and singing three bars of a song about a miller's daughter, over and over, because that was the last evening the button remembered and the dead wear their final hours like coats they cannot take off.

"Your husband liked to sing," she told the grey-haired woman across the table. "'The Miller's Daughter.' Badly. He knew the words were wrong and sang them anyway, and his workmates laughed, and he counted it a good night."

The woman wept. Not the tearing grief Wendell had feared, but the other kind—the kind that comes when a wound that has been open for months is finally allowed to close. She thanked Wendell in a whisper and drifted away lighter than she'd come.

Word traveled fast in a small town. By midday the line stretched out the hall doors and down the street, and the soldiers stopped pretending to guard her and started helping sort relics instead.

She read a miner's lamp whose last owner had counted his remaining oil by habit until the dark took him. She read a girl's hair-ribbon that remembered skipping rope and a mother's voice calling her in to supper. She read a foreman's whistle that held nothing but the memory of orders shouted into dust, and she told its widow, gently, that some men left nothing behind but duty, and duty was not nothing.

Each reading cost her. She had explained this to Vane once, in the wagon: the voices were not loud, but they were *close*, and letting them close enough to speak meant letting them close enough to touch, and the touch of the dead was cold in a way that accumulated, that banked itself in the bones like debt. By afternoon her hands shook. By late afternoon she was reading through a haze, and the voices were blurring together, and that was dangerous, because blurred voices said things she could not verify—

Which was how it happened.

A boy of perhaps ten years old climbed onto the bench across from her, too short to be seen over the table, and set down a carved wooden bird. A wren, roughly done, the wings unfinished. A child's carving. A dead child's carving.

"My brother made it," the boy said. "Colm. He was eleven. The fever took him at midwinter, and Da says the fever-dead don't come back restful, and Mam won't say anything at all anymore, and I thought—" He pushed the bird an inch closer. "I thought maybe you could tell me if he's scared."

Wendell picked up the little wren.

The cold came in faster than usual, sharper, the way it always did with children, because children burned bright and brief and left deep marks. She let it rise through her fingers, through her wrists, and listened.

*Colm. Colm. Colm.*

But the voice that answered was not a child's.

It was old. Old the way bedrock is old, old the way the mountain pressing down on this town was old, and it did not come from the wren alone—it came from *through* the wren, from somewhere far beneath the hall's foundations, far beneath the mine levels, from the place the whole valley leaned toward. It spoke through the boy's brother the way water speaks through a crack in a dam, and every other relic on the table, every token and trinket and bone, began at once to murmur, faint as wind through a keyhole, all of them carrying the same word outward like runners passing a flame:

*"Ashvane. Ashvane. Tell the ash-vane. Tell her the crown remembers her name."*

Wendell's vision whited. When it cleared, the hall was silent. Every face was turned toward her. She was standing—she had no memory of standing—with the wren clenched in both fists hard enough to bite her palms, and her lips were still moving around words she had already spoken aloud.

"What did you say?" Vane asked quietly. She had come around the table. Her hand rested on the hilt of her sword, but her eyes were not hostile—they were hungry, and that was worse.

"I—" Wendell's mouth was dry. The hall's fire crackled. Somewhere a baby fussed and was hushed. She looked down at the boy, at his waiting face, and gave him the only truth she had that belonged to him. "Your brother isn't scared. He says the bird needs finished wings. He says you should finish them."

The boy nodded solemnly, took his wren, and left. And Wendell turned to Vane, and lowered her voice so that only the inquisitor and the two soldiers flanking the table could hear, and spoke the name again, deliberately, watching it land:

"Ashvane. The dead are asking for someone called Ashvane. They say the crown remembers that name."

The effect was immediate and strange. Vane did not move. But the older soldier, the one named Brakkus who chewed bark-tar and had spoken perhaps ten words in three days, went grey around the mouth. And the younger one, Pell, made a small sound like a man swallowing his own tongue.

"That's not possible," Pell whispered.

"What isn't?" Wendell asked.

"It's a founding-era name," Brakkus said slowly, staring at her as if she'd produced it from a wound. "Ashvane. First royal house of Ashvale. King Aldric Ashvane—the First King. It died out before the founding war ended. Nobody's borne that name in six hundred years. It's not in any parish roll. It's barely in the histories."

"Then the dead have long memories," Wendell said. "Longer than yours."

She set the wren down on the cloth among the relics, and her hands were steady now, steadier than they'd been all day, because terror had burned through the exhaustion and left something colder and clearer underneath. She had walked into Thornfell Gate as a prisoner, a tool, a fraud kept alive for convenience. But she had just spoken a name that no living person should know, heard through a dead child's toy, from a depth of the mountain no map recorded—and every soul in this hall, living and otherwise, had watched her do it.

Her secret was no longer a thing to be hidden. It was a thing to be *spent*.

"They'll want to know what else you can hear," Vane said. Her voice had changed too—all the magistrate's crispness gone out of it, replaced by something quieter and more careful, the voice of a woman standing at the edge of a drop she'd only ever seen from maps. "The crown. My superiors. They'll want everything you can pull out of this mountain."

"Then we should discuss terms," Wendell said. "Because I don't work for the crown, Inquisitor. And after tonight, neither of us can pretend I'm just along to point at graves."

Vane studied her for a long moment. Outside, the light was failing, and the lamplighters were moving through the streets, and above the town the mountain stood black against the last grey of the sky, patient as a creditor.

"Terms," Vane repeated at last. "Tomorrow. After we've descended to the Gate galleries. Bring your terms." She paused at the edge of the firelight, and for just a moment her composure slipped, and Wendell saw what lay beneath it—not zealotry, not ambition, but fear, worn smooth as a river stone from years of handling.

"And bone-carver. If the dead ask for Ashvane again—" Vane's hand closed briefly, unconsciously, over the warrant scroll at her belt. "Ask them why."

That night, chained in the hall's storeroom among sacks of barley and salted fish, Wendell lay awake and listened to the mountain listen back. Thousands of voices, all turned toward the deep places, all murmuring the same syllables over and over like a prayer or a warning or a summons.

*Ashvane. Ashvane. Ashvane.*

And threaded through them, once, unmistakably, in a voice like her grandmother's, like her own:

*Come down, little carver. We've been keeping your seat.*
