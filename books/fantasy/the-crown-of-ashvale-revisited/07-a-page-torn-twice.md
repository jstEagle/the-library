## 7. A Page Torn Twice

The camp slept badly, the way it had slept every night since Thornfell Gate. Wendell lay in the wagon's shadow with her wrists unbound—Vane had allowed that much after the soldier's token, a concession dressed up as pragmatism—and listened to the fire settle into its bones. Pine popped. A horse stamped. Somewhere beyond the pickets, the mountain breathed in and out with a sound too slow to be wind.

She had learned the rhythm of the camp in three nights: which sentry walked his post twice as long because he feared his own thoughts more than raiders; where Vane slept, apart from the soldiers, her warrant case beneath her head like a child's doll; how Odo Fenwick arranged his maps each evening, weighted at the corners with river stones, squared to true north even here, even now.

It was the map case that mattered tonight.

Fenwick had told her, two days past, in that mild voice of his that never quite committed to anything: *The orders travel with the inquisitor. Sealed wax, but seals are for men who trust their masters.* He had said it while tracing a smuggler's path on his oldest chart, one she'd known by feel before he named it, and he had not looked at her when he said it, which was how careful men said dangerous things.

*If you want to know what you're walking toward, girl, read what they wrote about you.*

Wendell rose without sound. She had spent twenty years moving through other people's houses at hours when honesty was asleep; grief-work taught a body quietness, because the bereaved slept light and woke weeping. She took the chisel from her boot—the small one, bone-handled, more tool than weapon—and went into the dark like smoke going home up a chimney.

* * *

Vane's tent stood at the camp's edge, facing the mountain. Of course it did. The woman slept looking at the thing that haunted her, as if she could hold it under watch even unconscious. A lamp burned low inside; through the canvas came the scratch of a nib, the small wet sounds of ink.

Wendell crouched in the juniper shadow and waited. Patience was the first lesson of her trade. Bone did not give up its voices to the hurried. You soaked it, you waited, you listened until listening became the only thing you were.

The scratching stopped. Boots crossed the tent floor. The lamp guttered as the flap opened and Vane stepped out into the cold, wrapped in her grey cloak, and stood a moment with her face tipped up toward the peaks where the moon sat fat and wrong-colored, a coin left too long in vinegar. Then she walked toward the pickets—not toward the sentries but away from them, out to the edge of the dark where the horses dozed, and Wendell understood: the inquisitor could not sleep either, and had her own confessor, and it was the mountain.

Wendell counted to three hundred. Then she slipped inside.

The tent smelled of ink and tallow and something underneath both—astringent, medicinal. A wound salve. Vane had been hurt somewhere along the road and told no one, or tended herself, which amounted to the same confession. The writing desk held its modest kingdom: an inkpot, sand-shaker, a psalter worn soft at the spine, and the warrant case, iron-banded, unlocked because Vane trusted God and iron equally and saw no reason to spend suspicion on either.

The orders lay beneath the warrant itself, folded twice, sealed with grey wax stamped by a sigil Wendell did not know—a crown above a closed eye. Not the Ashvale arms. Something older. Something that predated the hunt.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Her heart hammered like a client pounding on her door at midnight, but her hands belonged to the work, and the work was this: lift the seal whole. She'd done it with letters for smugglers, once or twice, in years she didn't dwell on. Heat the blade, slide it flat, breathe. The wax parted like skin giving up a splinter.

She unfolded the page and read.

*To Inquisitor Serah Vane, from the Office of the Closed Eye, by command of the Regency Council in trust for the rightful heir:*

*You are reminded that the object of your commission is not the suppression of hearers, who are instruments and nothing more. The unrest among the dead of Thornfell is the decay of a binding laid down at the founding of the realm. The seal of that binding is the regalia of Aldric the First, called the Crown of Ashvale, interred with the king beneath the mountain. The Crown is the property of the throne. It will be recovered intact. It will be borne to the capital. It will be set upon the head of the rightful heir, and the dead of Thornfell will be bound again as they were bound before.*

*Hearers are to be used to locate the vault and discarded after. The hearer currently in your custody is of particular value, being of the line that served the first kings. Her grandmother's name appears in the founding rolls. See that she reaches the vault alive.*

*See that she does not reach anywhere else.*

*The plague of restless corpses is to be attributed publicly to hearers' malice. This attribution is essential to the succession. The realm must believe the gift is a corruption, so that no claimant arises who might contest the heir's authority over the dead.*

*Burn this letter.*

Wendell read it twice. The second time was not for comprehension. It was because her body refused to accept the first reading, the way a hand refuses a burn until the pain arrives late and total.

They knew. They had always known. The hunt was theater. The pyres, the burned shrines, the bounty for hearers—all of it stagecraft for a succession, a lie fed to a frightened realm so that one more crown could sit on one more head with the dead leashed beneath it. And her grandmother's name in the founding rolls. Her line had not merely served the kings. Her line had been part of the binding. Part of the pact. The voices she had carried all her life like stones in her ears were not accident. They were inheritance. She was not a hearer who happened to walk near Thornfell. She was a key that had wandered home.

And they meant to use her to open the lock, and then—

*Discarded after.*

The word was gentle as everything cruel is gentle. Discarded. As if she were a whetstone worn smooth, a candle burned to its socket.

She looked at the letter and felt something in her chest go very still and very cold, colder than the mountain air, colder than the drowned boy's last breath had been. Not fear. Fear had been her companion so long it barely registered anymore. This was the thing on the far side of fear: clarity. The absolute quiet of a person who has just seen the shape of the trap entire, floor and walls and lid.

She could run. Fenwick's offer sat in her mind like a coin on a table. The smuggler roads were hers; she could be over the pass in two nights, into the low country, gone. But the letter would still be true. The crown would still be sought. The lie would still be fed to the realm, hearer by hearer, pyre by pyre, until there were no more hearers and no more truth-tellers and the dead of Thornfell lay bound forever under a king's ambition renewed.

Or she could stay. Walk with them to the vault. Let them use her—let them think they were using her—and see with her own eyes what lay beneath the mountain, what her grandmother's generation had paid, what the whispering thing actually was. Knowledge first. Judgment after. Her mother had taught her that much before dying: *Never carve blind, Wendell. The bone tells you where the grain runs. Follow the grain or the piece shatters.*

Follow the grain.

She refolded the letter along its original creases, pressed the wax back down with her thumb warmed over the lamp, and held the blade's flat side against it until the seam forgot itself. It would not survive close inspection. It would survive a tired woman at dawn, and Vane was tired; Wendell had heard it in her footsteps, that dragging half-beat on the left side, an old injury favoring itself.

Then she did the harder thing. She took the letter outside, around the back of the tent where the fire pit held its bed of coals, and fed it to them page-corner by page-corner, watching the Office of the Closed Eye curl and blacken and go up in sparks that the wind carried toward the mountain like tiny fleeing souls. Burn the letter, it had commanded. She obeyed. There was a sour comedy in that, and she let herself taste it, because the alternative was to be sick, and she could not afford to be sick.

The knowledge stayed. Knowledge was the one thing no warrant could seize, no inquisitor could burn, no crown could reclaim. It lived in the bone of her now, and bone kept what it was given.

* * *

She was back in the wagon's shadow, breath steadying, when the flap of Vane's tent opened again across the camp. The inquisitor stood a moment in the cold, looking at the mountain, and then—Wendell saw it plainly in the moonlight—her hand went to her belt, to the little iron case, and pressed there, checking. Checking that it was shut. Checking that her world was still nailed down.

Then Vane turned and went back inside, and the lamp went out.

Wendell lay down and stared at the wagon's underside, at the grain of the boards silvered by years of weather, and thought about grain. Wood grain, bone grain, the grain of a life. Everything ran a certain way, and the carver's whole art was submission to it—until the moment came when the art was knowing exactly where to set the wedge and strike.

They wanted her to open the vault. Very well. She would open it. But she would decide what came out of it, and whose hands it ended in, and whether the rightful heir ever felt the weight of that old crown—or whether it went into a forge fire hotter than any the Office of the Closed Eye had ever dreamed of.

Above the peaks, the moon climbed, and it seemed to Wendell—lying very still, listening to the mountain breathe—that it had a faint ring around it tonight, thin as a hairline crack in glaze.

Thirteen turns, the astronomers had murmured at supper, arguing softly about the almanacs. Thirteen turns, and the old bindings weaken.

She closed her eyes and began, silently, to count.
