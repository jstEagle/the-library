## 8. Voices in the Deep Road

The sealed gate at the mouth of the Deep Road had not been opened in three hundred years, and it announced this fact with a groan that Wendell felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears. Odo Fenwick's keys—six of them, iron and black bronze, carried on a ring worn smooth as river stone—turned in locks designed by men who had trusted no one, least of all their successors. The gate swung inward on hinges weeping orange rust, and the air that came out was old. Not stale. Old. The way a tomb is old, or a promise.

Wendell stood at the threshold with her wrists still chafed from the wagon chains and did not want to go in.

She had known these mountains her whole life—the smuggler roads above them, the goat paths, the collapsed adits where her grandmother had taught her to read stone the way other folk read weather. But the Deep Road was not on any map she'd ever memorized, living or otherwise. It ran *under* Thornfell, through galleries cut during the founding war and then deliberately, ceremonially sealed, as if the kingdom had buried something it could not bear to look at. The cartographer's maps showed it in faded red ink, annotated in a hand that trembled: *Do not unseal below the Third Gallery. By order of the Crown. By order of anything.*

"Torches won't hold past the second gallery," Vane said behind her. The inquisitor's voice had changed since the Weeping Stair, since Wendell had turned corpse-raiders aside with nothing but the dead's own terror. There was something new in it—not warmth, exactly. Recognition. "Fenwick says there are oil rooms along the route. Royal stores."

"Fenwick says," Wendell murmured.

Odo Fenwick himself was already through the gate, holding his lantern high, his shadow enormous against the first gallery's vaulted ceiling. He was seventy if he was a day, with a cartographer's stoop and hands stained permanently with ink, and he moved into the dark like a man entering his own house after a long absence.

"The masons used Ashvale granite," he called back. "It holds the cold. And other things." He glanced at Wendell, and there was apology in it, or its cousin. "You'll hear them better down here than you ever have, bone-carver. Stone remembers longer than bone."

"I know what stone does," Wendell said, and stepped inside.

* * *

They descended for six hours.

The party had shrunk since the Weeping Stair—fourteen now, where there had been twenty-two. Soldiers mostly, hard-faced crown men who would not meet Wendell's eyes anymore, though whether from fear of her or shame at needing her, she couldn't say. Vane walked at the front beside Fenwick. Wendell walked where she always walked: in the middle, listening.

At first the galleries were only dark and dripping stone, the sound of fourteen pairs of boots swallowed whole. The royal oil rooms were where Fenwick promised—niches cut into the walls, clay jars still sealed with pitch, and the oil inside burning strange and sweet when they refilled their lanterns, with a smell like myrrh and something underneath it like blood left too long in a copper bowl. The light it gave was wrong, too. Amber shot through with green, throwing shadows that moved a half-beat late.

By the third gallery, Wendell began to hear the stones.

Not voices yet. Not words. A pressure, rather—a hum beneath hearing, the way a struck bell keeps singing after the ear gives up on it. She had felt it all her life in the mountains' bones, faint as breath on the back of her neck. Here it was a tide. Every loose stone in the walls was a throat waiting to be cleared.

"You feel it," Vane said quietly, falling into step beside her. It wasn't a question.

"I feel it."

"In the wagon, you read Corporal Hesk's token. You told me the dead near Thornfell weren't rising in anger." Vane's lantern light made hollows of her face. "You told me they were running."

"I told you what I heard."

"And now?" Vane's voice dropped further, until it was nearly lost under the boots and the drip of water. "Now that we're walking down into whatever they're running *from*?"

Wendell looked at her. The inquisitor who had burned bone-shrines and posted bounties for hearers, who had dragged her out of Greyhollow in chains—that woman had been carved from certainty. This one, walking deeper into the mountain with every step, was being hollowed out in real time, and Wendell recognized the process because she'd watched it happen to herself over years of listening to things that should not speak.

"Now I think we should turn around," Wendell said. "And I think you know we won't."

Vane didn't answer. But her hand went to the warrant in her coat—the folded paper with the crown's seal—and stayed there, the way a man touches a wound to confirm it still hurts.

* * *

The lanterns failed at the boundary of the fourth gallery.

All of them. At once.

One moment the amber-green light stretched long shadows down the corridor; the next, darkness so complete that Wendell's open eyes told her nothing at all. Someone shouted. Steel whispered from scabbards. Somewhere a jar of oil shattered, and the smell of it bloomed rich and useless in the black.

"No one strikes flint," Vane commanded, and her voice cracked like a whip across the panic. "Hold. *Hold.*"

Wendell stood very still.

Because in the dark, without the lanterns to drown them out, the stones began to speak.

It came up through her boot soles first, through the bones of her ankles and shins, a vibration resolving into syllables the way mud resolves into footprints. Then it was in the air, in the water-drip, in the rasp of every soldier's breathing. Hundreds of voices. Thousands. Not shouting, not wailing—*murmuring*, the way a congregation murmurs before the service, the way a crowd murmurs when it is waiting for something to begin.

"They're here," someone whimpered. "Saints preserve, they're in the *walls*—"

"Quiet," Vane said.

Wendell closed her eyes, which changed nothing, and listened, which changed everything.

The dead of Thornfell were not like the dead she knew. The drowned boy of Greyhollow had spoken in fragments, in the simple grammar of a child's last afternoon. These voices were layered, braided, speaking over and through one another in a cadence almost liturgical—and beneath the murmur ran a single thread of feeling so strong it needed no words: *fear*. Old fear. Worn smooth by centuries of repetition, the way a stair is worn by feet. They were afraid the way people are afraid who have been afraid for so long they've forgotten anything else.

And threaded through the murmur, names. Names being passed from voice to voice like buckets in a fire-line, each one caught and repeated and passed on, as if the saying of them was the only work left to do.

"—Aldous, Aldous, Aldous—"

"—Maren who kept the bees—"

"—the children of the low fields, the children of the low fields—"

"Bone-carver." Vane's hand found her shoulder in the dark, gripped hard. "What are they saying?"

Wendell opened her mouth to answer—and stopped.

Because one voice had come free of the murmur. One voice had separated from the tide the way a single wave lifts from a swell, and it was close, closer than the others, close enough that it seemed to come not from the walls but from the air an inch from her ear, and it said:

"*Maren.*"

Her grandmother's name.

"Maren Blackwood," the voice said again, gentle as a hand on a child's hair. "Maren's daughter's daughter. We kept your name too."

Wendell's knees nearly went. She put her palm flat against the gallery wall to steady herself, and the stone was warm. Warm as skin. Warm as a forehead when you check a child for fever.

"My grandmother died on the surface," she whispered. "In her bed. In Greyhollow. I carved her memorial myself."

"We know," the voice said, and around it the great murmur faltered, hushed, the way a room goes quiet when the important speaker rises. "We knew Maren. Maren walked these galleries when the sealing was young. Maren carried our names up and out, so they would not be lost when the pact took us. Maren promised." A pause, vast as a held breath. "You have her hands. We felt you carving. All these years, every time your chisel touched bone, we felt it, and we hoped."

"Hoped what?" Wendell's voice came out ragged.

"That you would come down. That a hearer would finally come *down*, instead of only listening at the top of the well." The voice drew nearer, or seemed to; the wall beneath her palm pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of geology. "The crown sends soldiers with fire and steel. The crown sends inquisitors with warrants. But the crown has never once sent anyone to *listen*. Three hundred years, daughter-of-Maren. Three hundred years we have been calling up through the stone, and the only ones who ever answered were smugglers and grave-robbers and girls like your grandmother, who paid for their hearing with everything."

"What happened here?" Wendell asked. "What did the first king do?"

The murmur surged—fear again, but different now, sharper, fresher, the fear of a thing about to be said aloud for the first time in centuries. And beneath it, another sound began to rise from far below, down through the fifth gallery and the sixth and the sealed vaults beyond even Fenwick's maps: a slow, rhythmic grinding, like a millstone turning, like teeth.

"He will wake early if you keep asking," the voice said urgently. "Listen instead. Take the names. Maren's work is unfinished—three hundred years of names, and we cannot carry them alone anymore. Take them up into the light. Say them. That is all rest we ask. That is all we have ever—"

The lanterns flared back to life.

Every one of them, all at once, flames leaping tall and green-edged, and in the sudden light Wendell saw the soldiers pressed back against the walls with their swords drawn, saw Vane staring at her with an expression stripped of everything but awe and dread, saw Fenwick—

Saw Fenwick standing apart from the others, lantern raised, watching her with an expression she had no name for. Not surprise. Something older and more terrible than surprise. As if he had been waiting for exactly this, and had known, all along, precisely whose granddaughter he was leading down into the dark.

"Witch-girl," he said softly. "Your grandmother sealed something in these walls besides names. Did she never tell you why the Blackwoods hear the dead?" His ink-stained hand rose, pointing down the corridor, toward the grinding sound that still echoed up from the deep. "Did she never tell you what your family was *for*?"

Behind him, at the far end of the fourth gallery, where the lamplight could not reach, something in the sealed dark shifted its weight—and waited.
