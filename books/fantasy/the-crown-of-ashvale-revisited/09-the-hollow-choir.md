## 9. The Hollow Choir

The passage narrowed for half a mile and then opened like a throat.

Wendell felt the change before she saw it—a shift in the way the air moved against her face, the way the lantern light stopped dying at six feet and began to travel. The dead had gone quiet in the last hour of walking, which was its own kind of warning. In her experience silence underground was never emptiness. It was attention.

"Hold," she said, and the column behind her stilled. Eleven of them now, since the Weeping Stair: Vane, four soldiers steady enough to keep walking, two sappers, the astronomer Pell with his brass instruments wrapped in oilcloth, old Odo Fenwick shuffling at the rear with his map case hugged to his chest like a grandchild, and a pair of porters who had not spoken since the ambush and whose eyes never stopped moving toward the walls.

"What is it?" Vane came forward, torch high. The bandage on her forearm from the stair had soaked through again; Wendell could smell the blood under the pitch-smoke smell of the torch. "Another gallery?"

"No." Wendell turned her head slowly, the way she did when she wanted to listen with more than her ears. The bone tokens in her pocket—three now, each one a small dead weight of someone's grief—had begun to warm. Not the fever-warmth of the restless dead below them, but something else. Something older. It reminded her of standing near an oven after it had been banked for the night: heat without flame, purpose without hunger. "There's a room ahead. A large one."

"How large?"

"Large enough that the echo has nowhere to go." She frowned at the dark ahead, where the lantern light simply gave up, swallowed by a blackness that seemed to take the light rather than fail to reach it. "And large enough that whatever is in there already knows we've come."

Pell made a sound that might have been a prayer or might have been arithmetic.

Odo Fenwick pushed past the porters, breathing hard, and stood beside Wendell peering into the dark. His eyes were bad in lantern light but his memory was not, and she watched him work through it—the same way she watched him work through every junction they'd passed since the sealed galleries began—lips moving, counting turns, comparing what he saw against maps that existed only inside his skull.

"This isn't on my charts," he said at last, quietly, and Wendell heard something in his voice she hadn't heard before. Not fear exactly. Shame. "I have the founding-war surveys. Every shaft the crown ever cut in Thornfell, every gallery sealed after the Purge of the Hearers. I have all of them." He looked at her, and in the torchlight his old face looked suddenly much older than sixty years. "This road isn't in any of them."

"Maybe your maps are wrong."

"My maps are never wrong, girl. That's the whole point of me." He said it without pride; he said it the way a man says the tide comes in. Then, softer: "Which means this place was made to be forgotten. Not lost. Forgotten. There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and the name is *order*."

Vane's jaw tightened. Wendell watched the inquisitor's hand go to the warrant tube at her belt—an old habit, Wendell had learned, the way another woman might touch a locket—and then fall away again. Since the stair, since Wendell had stood in the middle of corpse-raiders and called the dead by name and watched them kneel back into the dark, something in Serah Vane had come loose. She still gave orders. But she asked questions now too, and she waited for the answers, and that waiting was a crack in her that light got through.

"Forward," Vane said. "Slowly. Wendell—if anything speaks, you tell me before you answer it."

"The dead don't wait their turn, Inquisitor."

"They'll learn."

* * *

They went in with three torches and every lantern they had, strung out in a line like beads pulled along a wire, and the dark took them one by one and then, all at once, let them go.

The cavern was vast.

It was not natural—or rather, it had been natural once, some slow cathedral of water-carved stone, and then hands had finished it. The floor had been leveled and paved with slabs of grey slate worn smooth as river stones in their centers, worn by feet, by centuries of feet, all of them following the same paths from the entrance to the places where people used to stand. Tiered benches rose along the eastern curve of the wall, carved from the living rock, wide enough to seat hundreds. Above them, galleries—balconies of stone supported by pillars carved in the shapes of women holding lamps, though every lamp was empty, every cup of every lamp hollow and cold.

But it was the bones that stopped them all.

They lined the walls in niches cut with mason's precision, thousands upon thousands of them, and they were not heaped and they were not hidden. They were *carved*. Every skull bore work: fine lines incised across the cranium like the grooves of a fingerprint, spirals and ladders and running script in no alphabet Wendell knew, patterns that caught the torchlight and threw it back in tiny trembling rivers. Ribs strung like harp strings in some of the deeper alcoves. Finger bones laid out in long sentences along the bench backs, row after row, so that anyone seated there would rest their hands on writing made of the dead.

"Oh," said Pell, faintly, and sat down on the nearest step without appearing to decide to.

"It's an audience hall," one of the soldiers whispered. "Saints preserve us, it's a *court*."

"No." Odo Fenwick had gone very pale. He walked out onto the smooth center of the floor, his boots loud in a way nothing else in this place was loud, and turned slowly around, reading the tiers, the galleries, the pillar-women with their empty lamps. "Not an audience hall. A *choir*. Look at the benches—they're angled. Every seat faces the center but also the walls. You don't sit here to watch something. You sit here to listen to something together." His voice cracked on the last word. "This is where they worked."

"Who?" Vane demanded. "Who worked here?"

"The hearers." Odo said the word carefully, the way a man handles a blade he respects. "The Royal College of Listeners. Founded by Aldric the First King himself, if the chronicles are true—and I always assumed they were embellishment. *The king keeps a choir of the quiet kind*, the old texts say. I thought it was poetry." He laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "It was a staff roster."

Wendell had not moved from the entrance.

She could not move. The warmth in her pocket-tokens had become a hum, and beyond the hum—beyond the three souls she carried like coins in a beggar's purse—there was the rest of it. The room itself was speaking. Not the restless dead, not the clawing desperate things below them in the deep road who scratched at the world's skin trying to get away from something worse. These voices were settled. Layered. They spoke over one another the way rain speaks over a roof, and underneath the rain of them was one long sustained tone, patient as geology, and when Wendell finally let herself hear it clearly she understood that it was not many voices at all.

It was one voice, sung by many mouths. A chord held for five hundred years.

*You hear us.*

She swayed. One of the soldiers caught her elbow and she shook him off, because being touched while listening was like having a page slammed shut in the middle of a sentence.

"Wendell." Vane was beside her now, close, her voice pitched low and hard—the voice she used when she was frightened and would die before admitting it. "What do you hear?"

"You wouldn't believe me if I told you."

"Try me. My week has been very strange."

Wendell almost laughed. Almost. Instead she stepped forward onto the worn slate, into the center of the choir, where the paths of all those ancient feet converged, and she lifted her lantern, and she looked up at the carved skulls in their thousand niches looking down.

Every skull faced inward. Every carved spiral on every cranium wound toward the center of the room. Toward this exact spot, where she stood.

"They sang," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept her anchored, kept her *Wendell Blackwood of Greyhollow, bone-carver, smuggler's guide, nobody*, instead of whatever she was becoming in this place. "Not with throats. With the gift. This was a—" she groped for the word and found it waiting, as if the room had placed it in her mouth— "*conservatory.* They trained hearers here. Dozens of them. Hundreds. They sat in those seats and they listened together, and when they listened together they heard—" 

*More,* offered the chord, gently, like a teacher completing a student's faltering recitation. *We heard more.*

"—more," Wendell echoed, and hated how her voice shook. "One hearer alone hears one dead soul at a time. A murmur. But many hearers, joined—" She pressed her palm flat against the cool slate. "Joined, they heard everything. All of Thornfell. Every grave, every gallery, every soul the mountain holds. At once. Like an orchestra hearing the whole score instead of one instrument."

Silence behind her. Then Pell, in a whisper full of horrified awe: "That's why the founding war was won. The histories say the First King always knew where the enemy armies slept. Always. Every battle. They called it providence."

"It wasn't providence," Wendell said. "It was payroll."

"And then?" Vane's voice had gone flat and careful. "Where is this college in the histories? Why does no chronicle mention it? I served the Crown Archive for nine years before my warrant. I have read everything. There is *nothing* about this."

"That," said Odo Fenwick softly, "is because of the Purge."

He had drifted to the eastern wall, to the first tier of benches, and his lantern was raised toward the bench-back where finger bones lay laid out in rows—writing, Wendell had thought, writing made of the dead. Now, closer, she followed his gaze and saw what the rows actually spelled. The script was old, older than any alphabet she knew, but Odo's lips moved as he read, translating the way he translated everything, automatically, helplessly, and Wendell listened to the chord in the walls sing along beneath his voice like a congregation remembering a hymn.

"*Here sat Maren of the Vale, third voice of the western bench, who heard the drowned of Lake Sorrel and gave them back their names*," Odo read. "*Here sat Ivett, who heard her own son among the fallen of Red Ford, and stayed at her post until the war was won.* Here sat—" He stopped. His lantern hand trembled.

"Read it," Vane said.

"I can't."

"*Read it.*"

Odo Fenwick closed his eyes and read it. "*Here sat Ysolde Blackwood, first voice of the high bench, who heard the pact beneath and told the truth of it, and was silenced for the telling.*"

The word *Blackwood* went through Wendell like a chisel through green wood—clean, fast, all the way to the heart.

Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother's *name*, carved into a bench in a buried cathedral five hundred years old, in a room that appeared on no map, in a history that contained no such college, no such purge, no such woman. And yet the chord in the walls rose when the name was read—she felt it rise, a swelling in the sustained tone, recognition, grief, *welcome*—and every carved skull above the benches seemed to tilt, just slightly, the way a crowd tilts when someone they love enters late.

"She was real," Wendell said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "My grandmother's mother's mother, back and back—we always said the gift came down the Blackwood line, but we thought—I thought—" She turned to Odo. "The Purge of the Hearers. In your maps. What was it? What really happened?"

Odo lowered himself onto the bench beside Ysolde Blackwood's name, slowly, like a man kneeling. "The chronicle version is two sentences. *In the eleventh year of the reign of Aldric the Second, the College of Listeners was dissolved for reasons of state, and its members dispersed.* Two sentences, girl. I copied that sentence fifty times as an apprentice and never once asked why a royal institution that won a war needed dissolving for *reasons of state*." He looked up at the carved skulls, at the empty lamp cups of the pillar-women. "Now I'm standing in the reason."

"They didn't disperse them." Wendell's fury arrived all at once, hot and total, and she welcomed it because it was warmer than the fear. "Look at the wear on these floors. Centuries of use, and then—look." She strode to the far end of the eastern tier, lantern high, and showed them what she'd seen from the entrance and understood only now: a seam in the wall where the niches simply stopped. Where carvings gave way to rough stone. Where a section of the choir had been *walled off*, the bricks mortared over bone and bench alike, sealing away whoever had sat in those seats. "They bricked them in. Some of them. The ones who—" 

*The ones who heard the pact,* sang the chord, gentle and terrible. *As Ysolde heard it. As you will hear it.*

"—the ones who heard too much," Wendell finished. "The crown didn't erase this place because the hearers failed. They erased it because the hearers *succeeded*. Because they heard what was bought and paid for under this mountain, and some of them had the gall to say it out loud. And then the crown wrote two sentences, and burned the shrines, and posted bounties on their descendants, and taught five hundred years of children that the gifted are plague-bearers and corpse-witchers—" She rounded on Vane, and the lantern light made her shadow enormous on the tiered wall. "Your hunt, Inquisitor. Your warrant. It isn't the first hunt. It's the *latest* one. The same order, renewed every few generations, so that no one ever learns what their great-grandmothers did with the same gift. So that girls like me grow up hiding in smuggler towns, carving memorials in attics, believing we're alone."

Vane stood very still. Torchlight moved on her face and she let it, unblinking, and Wendell—who had spent weeks learning to read this woman the way she read bone—saw the moment the inquisitor's certainties shifted weight, like ballast sliding across a deck in heavy water.

"My sister," Vane said, and stopped.

The chord in the walls waited. Even the mountain seemed to lean in.

"My sister heard the dead," Serah Vane said at last, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "She died for it. I built my whole life on the belief that the law that killed her was *right*, and that she must have done something, must have crossed some line, because the crown does not burn innocent women." She looked up at the walled-off section of the choir, at the sealed-away benches, and Wendell watched her do the arithmetic that Odo had already done, the terrible simple sum of it: *an institution that bricks up its own servants will lie about them forever.* "How many," Vane said hoarsely. "How many hearers are behind that wall?"

*I can show you,* the chord offered, and it was so kind, so terribly kind, that Wendell put her hand against her own sternum as if to hold her ribs shut.

"Don't," she said aloud, to the room, to herself. "Don't show her. Not like this. Not all at once." She turned back to Vane. "Some knowledge is a door that only opens inward. Once it's open you can't close it. Believe me."

"And you?" Vane's eyes were wet and furious. "Is yours open?"

"Mine," Wendell said, "was never closed. That's the whole problem with me."

* * *

They camped in the choir because there was no better choice—the galleries gave defensible sightlines, the central floor held a fire pit so old its stones were fused glassy-black by a thousand vanished flames, and the alternative was the deep road, and no one would say the deep road's name aloud anymore.

Wendell did not sleep.

She sat in the center of the floor with her carving kit open on her knees and the three tokens laid out before her—the drowned child's whistle-bone, the soldier's knuckle-token, the grieving wife's ring-bone—and she worked, because working was how she prayed. Around her the others slept in the tiers: soldiers with their backs to the warm stone, Pell muttering lunar figures into his notebook even unconscious, Odo curled against Ysolde Blackwood's bench like a man keeping vigil at a stranger's grave that had turned out to be his own.

And the chord sang to her.

Not in words, mostly. In *showings*. Fragments, offered slowly, respectfully, the way one offers food to someone who has been starving: a young woman with Wendell's own heavy brow seated on the high bench, eyes closed, hands flat on the slate, her lips moving with a hundred other pairs of lips. Lanterns in the pillar-cups, lit, hundreds of them, the whole cavern golden and alive. A king on the floor below—she knew him by the crown, a plain circlet, unadorned, nothing like the thing the whispering dead feared—kneeling, asking, and the choir answering as one voice, and the king weeping at what they told him.

Then the showing changed. The lanterns going out one by one. Soldiers in the galleries—not the First King's soldiers, these wore the livery she'd seen burned into her memory on the riders who raided Greyhollow. Bricks. Mortar. Screaming that the chord replayed with terrible fidelity, because the chord remembered everything, because remembering was the entire purpose of its existence.

*They made us into a secret,* the chord sang, when Wendell could bear it no longer and set down her chisel with shaking hands. *Then the secret into a sin. Then the sin into a story about monsters. Your grandmother carried our memory out of here in her blood, little carver. Five hundred years, mother to daughter to daughter, and every one of them told to hide, and hide, and hide. You are the first of our line to sit in this room since the wall went up.*

"I'm not sitting," Wendell whispered. "I'm passing through."

*Are you?*

She looked down at her hands. At the chisel. At the tokens. And the chord—patient, ancient, achingly gentle—showed her one final thing before letting her be: a shape in the deepest dark below this room, below the deep road, below everything. A vault door. And upon it, incised in stone worn nearly smooth, a spiral.

The same spiral that wound through every carved skull in the choir. The same spiral her grandmother had taught her to cut into the handle of her first knife, calling it *just a pattern, child, a luck-mark, nothing more.*

A family mark. A guild mark. A *keyhole mark.*

Above her, at the mouth of the cavern, boots on slate. Vane stood wrapped in her cloak, sleepless too, watching the fire, watching the walled-off tier, watching Wendell.

"The dead below," Vane said quietly. "The restless ones. They've stopped scratching tonight. Did you notice? All evening—nothing. As if they're waiting."

Wendell closed her hand around the token of the drowned child and felt, beneath its small familiar sorrow, the vast patient chord begin to change key—tightening, rising, gathering itself the way a congregation gathers before the sermon.

"They're not waiting," she said. "They're deciding whether to trust us. Tomorrow, when we go down past the last seal—" She met Vane's eyes across the fire, and the firelight, and the five hundred years of silence between them. "Tomorrow we find out what my grandmother died for saying. And God help us, Inquisitor, I think we're going to find out she was right."
