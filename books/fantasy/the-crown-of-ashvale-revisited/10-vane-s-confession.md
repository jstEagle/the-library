## 10. Vane's Confession

Serah Vane had learned to sleep sitting upright, back against stone, one hand on her sword hilt. It was a habit from the border campaigns, where a woman who slept deeply did not wake. But tonight, in the hollowed gallery two hundred feet beneath Thornfell Gate, she could not have slept if the saints themselves had sung her down.

The dead were louder here.

She had told no one this—not the soldiers who trusted her, not Odo Fenwick with his rolled maps and his too-soft hands, and certainly not Wendell Blackwood, whose knowing eyes missed nothing and who would understand it all too well. But Vane heard them now, faintly, the way one hears rain through a roof: not words, exactly, but pressure. A murmur under the world's skin. The pick-marks in the tunnel walls seemed to pulse when she looked away from them.

She rose, took a lantern, and walked the perimeter of their camp because walking was what she did instead of praying. Twelve sleeping shapes. Two sentries at the gallery mouth, their silhouettes cut thin by the light of the deep-road fungus that grew along the ceiling in pale veins. And near the far wall, wrapped in a threadbare cloak with her wrists still chafed raw from three days of manacles, the bone-carver sat awake too, turning a scrap of antler over in her fingers as though it might speak to her.

Perhaps it did. Perhaps everything spoke to her. That was the whole horror of hearers, wasn't it—that they could never be alone, never be silent, never be safe even inside their own skulls.

Vane should have hated her. For weeks she had tried to hate her, the way one grinds a blade against stone to keep an edge. It had worked poorly.

"Carver," she said quietly. "Walk with me."

Wendell looked up. In the lantern light her face was all sharp planes and watchfulness, a face carved by someone patient and not entirely kind. "Is that a request or an order, Inquisitor?"

"A request." The word tasted strange in her mouth. She could not remember the last time she'd made one.

* * *

They walked the gallery together without speaking for a long while, boots scuffing dust that had lain undisturbed since the founding war, past rusted pit-cages and the sockets where support beams had rotted away. The fungus-light gave everything the color of drowned things. Somewhere below them—far below, past galleries the maps marked only with warnings—the air carried a sound like slow water, though Fenwick swore there was no river on any chart of the deep roads.

"You haven't chained me in four days," Wendell said at last.

"The chains stopped meaning anything."

"They stopped meaning anything the moment you decided I was more useful walking free."

"Yes," Vane said. "That too." She stopped beside a niche cut into the wall, one of dozens they'd passed, and raised the lantern. Inside lay bones. Not heaped—*arranged*. A skull resting on folded forearms, ribs curled around something small and long-corroded that might once have been a flute. Whoever had laid these remains to rest had done it with love, and with skill, and with the unmistakable tenderness of ritual. A shrine. One of hundreds, she suspected, in the deep places of Thornfell. One of the shrines her own warrant ordered burned.

"Do you know who tends these?" she asked.

"No," Wendell said. "Do you?"

"No." Vane let the silence sit. "My warrant says every shrine is an act of sedition. Every bone kept above ground unburned is evidence of gift-work. My orders are explicit." She turned the lantern so the flame threw long shadows across the skull's quiet face. "I have executed that order eleven times in two years. Shrines in cellars. Shrines in hedgerows. A shrine inside a millstone, of all places, ground smooth by a family who turned it every morning for sixty years."

Wendell said nothing, but her stillness had changed quality—the stillness of someone listening very hard.

"There was a twelfth," Vane said. "I didn't burn it."

"Why not?"

Because it had been my sister's. Because it held her hands. Because I stood in front of it for an hour and could not make my arm move.

"Because I am tired," she said instead, which was true, which was also a coward's version of true.

Wendell's gaze stayed on her, patient as stone. This was the thing about the carver that unsettled her most—not the hearing, not the whispered names, but this: she waited. She let silences open like wounds and did not rush to close them. Most people feared silence. Hearers, perhaps, lived in it.

Vane found herself speaking before she had decided to.

"My sister's name was Liss. Elissa Vane. She was four years older than me, and she had—" Her voice snagged. She started again, flatter, the way one reads a report. "She heard the dead. Our mother found out when Liss was nine, singing a lullaby in the voice of our grandmother, who had been dead two years and whom Liss had never once met alive. Mother did what mothers in the villages did then. She hid it. Taught Liss to hide it. Told her the voices were a sickness of the ears, that if she never spoke of them, no one would ever know, and no one would ever come."

"And did anyone come?"

"Not for nineteen years. Liss married. She had a son. She carved little tokens—bone buttons, mostly—and sold them at market, and people bought them because they were pretty, and some people bought them because they *worked*, and those people told other people, quietly, the way such things travel. And then the plague came. The restless corpses. You'll remember it; everyone remembers it. The crown needed a cause. The gifted were convenient." Vane's hand tightened on the lantern handle until the wire bit her palm. "A neighbor sold her name for a pardon he never received. They came at dusk. There was a trial—it lasted less than an hour. They burned her tokens first, in the square, so she could watch. Then they burned her."

The gallery was very quiet. Even the murmur beneath the stone seemed to lean closer.

"She went to the fire singing," Vane said. "Not screaming. Singing. And afterward I understood—I understood with a clarity that has never left me—that she had been singing to someone. Some poor restless soul nearby, frightened of the flames, and my sister spent her last breath comforting it." She set the lantern down on the ledge beside the niche, carefully, because her hands were no longer entirely steady. "Her son was eight. He lives with our aunt in Lowmarsh. He has his mother's eyes. I pray nightly that he has none of her ears."

"You joined the hunt," Wendell said softly. It was not a question, but Vane answered it anyway, because the confession had broken its banks and there was no holding it now.

"I joined the hunt to control it from inside. To stand between hearers and pyres wherever I could do it invisibly. A misplaced file. A warrant delayed. A prisoner who dies in transit and is secretly released. Eleven shrines I burned because I had to, and four I lost from the inventory, and two prisoners who drowned tragically in a river that ran nowhere near the road." Her laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I told myself I was a knife pointed inward. That every soul I saved balanced the ones I couldn't. That God—or the saints, or whatever listens—would weigh the ledger kindly."

"And now?"

"Now I am two hundred feet underground, leading a hearer in chains toward a thing my own orders call the First King's Crown, and I have read enough between the lines of those orders to know the crown does not intend to destroy it." She looked at the skull in its niche, at the small corroded flute cradled in its arms. "And now I hear them, carver. Down here. Faintly. I have no gift—I've been tested twice, scraped and starved and prayed over—but I hear *something*, a pressure under the stone, like a crowd behind a door. And they are not angry."

"No," Wendell said. "They're afraid."

"Fear of what?"

"That is the question I was sent to answer," Vane said. "But I begin to suspect it is not the question the crown wanted answered."

* * *

They returned along the gallery, and Vane noticed things she had trained herself not to notice: how Wendell's steps slowed near each niche, how her fingers brushed the cool stone in passing like a woman greeting acquaintances in a market crowd; how the sentries watched the carver with fear and, increasingly, with something else—something that looked embarrassingly like hope. A soldier named Brakkis, whose brother had died at the Weeping Stair two winters past, had asked Vane yesterday, in a voice pitched low with shame, whether the carver might... ask. Just ask. Whether Tam was at rest.

Vane had refused him. Refused him in her coldest inquisitor's voice, and then lain awake all night hearing her sister sing.

At the camp's edge, Wendell paused. "Why tell me this? Now? After keeping me chained and blind for weeks?"

"Because tomorrow we descend past the last mapped gallery," Vane said. "Fenwick's charts end at a stair the founding-era engineers sealed with poured lead, and beyond that, everything either of us knows is guesswork and grave-dust. If something down there means to kill us, I want—"

"You want a hearer at your side who trusts you."

"I want," Vane said, and the honesty cost her like pulling a tooth, "to stop lying to the one person in this expedition who has never lied to me. You told me the dead are running from something. You gained nothing by telling me that. A liar would have hoarded it."

Wendell studied her for a long moment. The fungus-light moved across her face like shallow water. "Your sister," she said at last. "What was the song? The lullaby she sang at the end."

"*Sleep, small ember, banked in ash,*" Vane said. The words came out whole, untouched by the years, as if they had been waiting in her chest all this time for exactly this terrible audience. "*The dark is kindlier than the lash.*"

Something shifted in Wendell's expression—recognition, and beneath the recognition, grief. "That's a hearer's song," she said. "Older than the purges. My grandmother sang it. She said it was for guiding them down—so the newly dead wouldn't lose their way in the dark. It's not a lullaby for children, Inquisitor. It's a lullaby for the dead." She hesitated. "It hasn't been sung openly in Ashvale for three generations. Your sister learned it somewhere. From someone who remembered."

From someone like her, Vane thought. From a lineage. From a tradition her warrant called sedition and her heart called mercy. The hunt had not merely killed hearers—it had murdered a language and called the murder justice, and she had carried the torch for it up and down the borderlands for two years, burning shrines that were not fortresses but *churches*, libraries of bone and love.

Her faith in the crown had been cracking for months, hairline fractures she'd refused to look at directly. Here, in the dark, with her sister's song hanging in the cold air, she felt the crack run suddenly from end to end.

"Inquisitor," said a voice from the gallery mouth. Young Aldric, one of the sentries, his lantern trembling. "You'd better come. The astronomer—Master Corvel—he says you need to see the moon."

"The moon is underground, boy," Vane said.

"No, sir. I mean—" Aldric swallowed. "There's a ventilation shaft. A natural chimney, goes up and up. Corvel's been taking readings off it each night. Tonight the light coming down it is wrong. He says the moon's gone red at the rim, and the count is—" the young man's voice dropped, superstitious and scared, "—he says the count makes it the thirteenth turn. Early. Days early. And the dead in the lower galleries, ma'am—we can hear them moving."

Far beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, came a sound like a thousand hands beginning, very slowly, to push against a door.

* * *

Vane stood at the shaft's edge and looked up the long throat of stone to the coin of sky at its top, where the thirteenth moon hung swollen and rimmed in red like a wound seen through water. Beside her, Wendell Blackwood pressed her palm flat against the rock, eyes closed, listening—and whatever she heard made her go white to the lips.

"How long?" Vane asked.

"Corvel says three nights," Wendell murmured. "Three nights until the turn completes. Whatever pact sleeps down there, Inquisitor—whatever your crown is so desperate to claim—" She opened her eyes, and they were full of a pity that frightened Vane more than any threat. "It isn't going to wait politely for us to arrive."

Below them, in the dark, the pushing sound grew steadier. Like a heartbeat. Like knocking.

Like counting down.
