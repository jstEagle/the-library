## 11. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The dead had been getting louder for three days.

Wendell felt it first in her bones—the ones she carved, the river of pale material she worked whenever her hands needed steadying. She'd taken a rib bone from her pack that evening, meaning to shape a simple charm, and the moment her knife touched it the voices came through like water through a cracked dam. Not whispers now. Not the murmured confessions and half-remembered lullabies she'd spent her life learning to sort and soften. These were cries. Scratching. The sound—carried in vibration up the knife's spine and into her wrist—of fingernails on stone, of bodies pressing against doors that were never meant to open from the inside.

She dropped the knife. It rang against the flagstones of the waystation's cellar where they'd made camp, and in the ringing she heard, distinctly, a woman's voice say: *It's coming early. It's coming early this year.*

Wendell sat very still in the lantern light and did not sleep.

* * *

By morning the whole camp knew something was wrong, though none of them could name it the way she could.

They had sheltered in an old assay office at the edge of a mining terrace called Sorrow's Ledge—a name Wendell knew from no map but from the dead themselves, who had told her once, years ago, through a smuggler's lucky talisman, that the ledge had been named by a widow who threw herself from it with her husband's pay still sewn into her coat. The building leaned into the mountain as if listening. Its walls were lined with slate shelves where ore samples had once been sorted, and now Vane's soldiers slept between them like coins in a merchant's tray.

Wendell climbed the narrow stair to the ground floor and found the astronomers already there.

There were two of them—crown men, quiet ones, attached to the expedition since Thornfell Gate with their instruments wrapped in oilcloth like coffins. Master Ellery was young and nervous, with ink-stained fingers and a way of blinking too fast when spoken to. The other, old Corvus Hale, was a dried-out stick of a man whose eyes had gone the pale grey of cataracts or of looking at bright things too long. He sat before a brass instrument mounted on a tripod, its long tube angled through the broken window toward the western sky, where the last stars were drowning in dawn.

"The moon set late last night," Hale said without turning. He had a voice like dry leaves. "Did you notice, hearer?"

"I don't watch the sky," Wendell said. "I watch the ground. The sky has never lied to me."

That earned her a ghost of a smile. "No. I suppose it wouldn't." He stepped back from the eyepiece and rubbed his ruined eyes. "But it lies to everyone else. Every almanac in Ashvale says the twelfth turn ended nine days ago. A new month should have begun with a new moon. And instead—" he gestured at the instrument as if it were evidence in a trial, "—it lingers. Full past its fullness. Hanging in the west three hours after it should be drowned. We've measured it four nights running. It is not following its path. It is *waiting*."

Ellery spoke from the corner, where he'd been scribbling figures. "Thirteen turns. The old calendars all mention it. Before the founding war, they counted thirteen. Afterward, twelve. Nobody wrote down why."

"Nobody was allowed to write it down," Wendell said quietly.

Hale turned his blind-grey gaze on her, and she saw that behind the cloudiness there was a sharpness that missed nothing. "You know the stories. From your grandmother's people."

"My grandmother's people kept their stories in bone and stone, not paper. Paper burns." She looked out the broken window at the fading stars. "But yes. There was a thirteenth turn once. My grandmother called it the Turn of Doors. The one night in the turning of the year when what is sealed grows loose, and what sleeps turns over in its bed." Her mouth went dry. "She said it came every seventh year. She said her mother's mother had seen it only twice in her life, and both times, the mountain wept."

"And this year?" Hale asked.

"This year it isn't supposed to come at all." Wendell wrapped her arms around herself. "The thirteenth turn died with the old calendar. That's what everyone believes. That's what the crown wanted everyone to believe." She thought of the burned page, the orders she'd read by candleflame in Vane's wagon—*the First King's Crown, recovery of, upon alignment*—and felt the cold crawl up her spine. "Unless someone wanted it forgotten so that no one would be watching when it came back."

Hale was silent a long moment. Then: "It hasn't fully begun. Not yet. Three nights, perhaps four, while the moon drains toward its false new-dark. But the binding—" He stopped himself, glancing at Ellery.

"The binding is already weakening," Wendell finished for him. "I know. I've been listening to it for three days."

* * *

Vane summoned her at midmorning.

The inquisitor had claimed the assay office's counting room, a stone cell of a place with a heavy door and a single high window, and she stood at the table beneath that window with maps spread across it, her hands flat on the parchment as though holding down living things. She looked worse than she had at Thornfell Gate. The hard certainty that had carried her through the arrest, through the deep road, through the ambush at the Weeping Stair—all of it had been sanded away layer by layer, and what remained was something rawer and more dangerous: a woman beginning to suspect that everything she'd built her life upon was load-bearing lies.

"You felt it," Vane said. No greeting. No title. They had passed beyond such things somewhere in the dark below the world.

"The dead are pressing upward." Wendell stayed near the door. Old habit. "Like floodwater under a floor. Last night I heard scratching through a rib bone I wasn't even trying to read. Through solid rock, Serah. They're clawing at the seals."

"How many?"

Wendell laughed, and hated the sound of it. "How many stars? How many grains in a riverbed? I can't count them anymore. For years the dead around Thornfell were sparse—scattered farmers, lost miners, the ordinary lonely dead. Now it's like standing in a market square. Thousands. Tens of thousands. And they're not calling for vengeance. They're not even calling for help." She swallowed. "They're calling to each other. Counting each other. Making sure everyone's awake before—"

"Before what?"

"Before the doors open." Wendell met Vane's eyes. "You've known about the thirteenth turn. Your orders mentioned it. Alignment. So tell me what the crown actually knows, because I promise you the crown knows less than my grandmother did, and my grandmother has been dead twenty years and knows considerably more than she did alive."

Something flickered across Vane's face—grief, or guilt, or the memory of a sister's name spoken once in the dark of the deep road. "The archives have fragments. Calendars from before the founding war, seized and sealed. Astronomical tables that only make sense if you assume a thirteenth lunar period. And one document—" her jaw tightened, "—one document that names the phenomenon and gives instructions. Sealing protocols. Containment rites. Written in the hand of the first king's own hearers."

"Hearers." The word tasted like ash and honey at once. "So the crown always knew. Knew about the turn, knew about the pact, knew about the souls—and buried it all, and then two hundred years later started burning hearers for knowing less than its own founding documents."

"Yes," Vane said simply. There was no defending it. She didn't try. That, more than anything, told Wendell how far the woman had fallen from her own certainties.

"So what do the protocols say happens when the binding weakens?"

"They say the seal must be renewed. By a rightful wearer of the crown." Vane's hands curled slowly against the maps. "Or the seal must be broken entirely, which the document calls *unbinding*, and describes in terms that would curdle milk. Renewal requires the crown and royal blood. Breaking requires—" she hesitated.

"Requires what?"

"A hearer willing to stand inside the pact's threshold and unmake it from within. The document is specific about that part. Specific and—" Vane's voice dropped, "—specifically silent about whether the hearer survives it."

Wendell stared at her. Outside, far below the terrace, wind moved through the black pines, and beneath the wind—if she let herself listen, and she couldn't stop listening, not anymore—she heard the vast slow breathing of the mountain full of dead.

"Well," she said at last. "Isn't that convenient. The crown hunts us for generations and then writes us into its own emergency instructions."

"Wendell—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't apologize again. Apologies don't hold back ten thousand dead." She turned toward the door, then paused. "Three nights, your astronomer says. Maybe four. Whatever we're going to do, we'd better decide what it is before the moon finishes dying."

* * *

That night the moon rose wrong, and everyone saw it.

It came up swollen and copper-stained over the eastern shoulders of Thornfell, hanging lower than any moon should hang, so large that soldiers stopped mid-task to stare and cross themselves and murmur prayers to saints who had never once answered anyone. It was nearly dark—the thinest sliver of itself remaining—and yet it shed a light that was somehow deeper than brightness, a reddish gloom that made the snow on the high peaks look like old blood and made every shadow fall the wrong direction.

Wendell stood at the edge of the terrace with the whole expedition gathered behind her—soldiers, muleteers, the two astronomers with their instruments, Odo Fenwick hovering at the rear with his cartographer's case strapped to his back and his eyes doing their quick merchant's arithmetic—and she listened to the mountain.

The seals were singing. That was the only word for it. Deep beneath them, under a mile of stone and older darkness, the bindings that held the pact were straining like rigging in a storm, and each strained note came up through the rock and into her feet, her knees, her teeth. Around her, she realized, others heard it too—not the words, not the voices, but the vibration, the subsonic groan of ancient things loosening. A soldier dropped his mess tin. A mule screamed and had to be cut loose from its tether before it broke its own leg.

And then the first of the restless dead reached the surface.

It happened at the old mine adit forty yards along the ledge—a collapsed tunnel mouth that the party had given a wide berth since arriving, the way travelers give wide berth to a doggone hole. The rubble there began to shift. Not dramatically; nothing so merciful as drama. Just stones rolling, one by one, downhill, as though something beneath were gently, patiently, pushing them aside. Dust breathed out of the gap. And from within came a sound that Wendell had heard only once before, through a carving, years ago, and had prayed never to hear in the living air:

A voice, human and hollowed, saying: *Let me see the sky. Please. I only want to see the sky before—*

Then another voice joined it. Then five. Then dozens, overlapping, harmonizing into a chord of desperation that made grown soldiers sit down hard in the snow. The rubble shifted further. A pale hand emerged—not grasping, not reaching for anyone, just rising into the copper light and spreading its fingers as if testing whether the air was real.

"*Wendell.*" Vane was beside her, sword drawn, though a sword was a joke against this. "What do we do?"

What did they do. Wendell closed her eyes and opened herself fully to the chorus for the first time since the deep road—dropped every wall, every filter, every careful craft of separation she'd spent thirty years building—and let the whole desperate ocean of the dead crash through her.

They weren't attacking. Gods, they weren't even angry. They were *afraid*. Beneath the mountain, in the darkening halls of the pact, something was changing—the old bargain decaying, the anchors dissolving—and the dead who had slept so long in their enforced rest were waking to find the roof coming down. They clawed toward the surface the way drowning men claw toward light. Not to take the living world. To *see* it one last time before whatever came next took them.

"They're fleeing," Wendell said aloud, her voice shaking. "Serah—they're not rising against us. They're running from what's happening below. If the seal fails without being either renewed or broken properly, it won't release them. It'll—" she groped for the image the voices gave her, and it was terrible enough that her stomach turned, "—it will grind them. All of them. A generation of souls, pulped between the failing pact and the dark underneath. That's what they're afraid of. That's why they're climbing."

Vane lowered her sword. Slowly. She looked at the pale hand in the moonlight, at the fingers spread against the copper sky, and Wendell watched the last pillar of the woman's faith crack clean through.

"Then we go down," Vane said. "Tonight. Now. While the turn is opening and the ways are... whatever they are."

"The ways are *loose*," Wendell corrected. "Which means passable, and also means nothing down there is staying where it belongs. Including us."

Behind them, Fenwick cleared his throat. When Wendell turned, the old cartographer wore his most reasonable expression, the one she had learned to distrust above all his others. "A night descent in these conditions is suicide," he said smoothly. "Surely wiser to wait for daylight, to prepare—"

"There is no daylight that helps us," Wendell snapped, "and every hour we wait, the grinding comes closer. You know that as well as I do, Odo. Better, maybe. Since you seem to keep such current accounts of everything." She let the accusation sit there between them, unproven and undeniable, and watched him blink.

Vane was already shouting orders. Soldiers scrambled. Torches were lit against a moon that mocked them with its dying red light.

Wendell walked to the mine adit. The pale hand had withdrawn—or been drawn back—but the voices swirled in the gap like cold air, and among them, clearer now, threading through thousands, one spoke directly to her, in a tone of terrible gentleness:

*Hearer. Daughter of hearers. Come down and choose. The thirteenth turn will not wait, and neither, child, will we.*

Wendell pulled her hammer and chisel from her belt, kissed the worn wood of the hammer's handle the way her grandmother had taught her—for luck, for courage, for the dead—and stepped into the mountain.
