## 12. Ambush at the Weeping Stair

The thirteenth moon had been climbing for two nights when the Weeping Stair announced itself—not by sight but by sound. Water fell somewhere ahead of them in thin silver threads, striking stone that rang like struck glass, and between each drop Wendell heard the dead holding their breath.

They had descended for three days through galleries the cartographer's maps called *the Long Descent*, and Odo Fenwick had grown quieter with every turn of the wheel. Even Serah Vane rode less like a woman leading an expedition and more like a woman being led, her gloved hand resting on her sword's pommel as though it were a rosary. The soldiers behind them muttered prayers to saints whose names had changed twice since the founding war.

Wendell walked at the front because Vane made her walk there. *You hear what we cannot,* the inquisitor had said on the first morning, not unkindly, and so Wendell walked where the listening was best, chains gone from her wrists since Thornfell Gate but never truly gone from her ankles, if one counted the way everyone watched her hands.

The stair was a marvel of the old kingdom: a thousand steps cut into the throat of a vertical fissure, descending in switchbacks beside a waterfall that wept from a crack in the mountain's shoulder. Someone had carved faces along its length—kings, perhaps, or saints, or simply miners who wanted to leave something of themselves behind—and centuries of mineral water had streaked their cheeks so that every face appeared to be crying.

"They call it the Weeping Stair in the oldest charts," Fenwick said, unrolling his lantern-lit page against the rock. His voice was thin. He had been thin-voiced since the voices in the deep road. "There's no way around it. And there's no cover on it. If anything means us harm, we'll meet it here."

"Nothing means us harm," said Corporal Brannic, too loudly, the way soldiers talk when they want to believe themselves.

The dead disagreed.

Wendell stopped at the top of the first switchback and laid her palm flat against the cliff. Through the stone she felt them—the restless ones, the desperate climbers of the last few nights, souls clawing upward toward air. But beneath that tide was something else, something she had been trying all day not to name.

Fear. Not the dead's fear of her, nor their fear of the crown's iron. A fear aimed sideways, along the dark. The dead were afraid of something living.

She had heard men's fear before, in bone and in stone. It tasted different from the dead's longing. The dead longed; only the living *dreaded*.

"Inquisitor." She kept her voice level. "We're being watched."

Vane came up beside her, and Wendell watched the woman do the thing she always did: swallow her doubt, straighten her spine, decide to trust the tool because the alternative was trusting nothing. "Watched by the dead?"

"By the living. Men who are afraid of the dead." She turned. "Deserters, I'd guess. Or worse."

"Worse?"

"There are people who follow the plague," Wendell said carefully. "Who harvest it."

She saw the word land. Everyone in the column had heard the stories by now—crown deserters who had discovered that a corpse touched by the unrest did not rot properly, that such corpses could be driven like oxen, sold to garrisons as labor, to plague-towns as... other things. Corpse-raiders, the smugglers called them, and spat after saying it. Even Greyhollow's hardest men would not take that work.

Vane's jaw tightened. "How many?"

"I don't know. Many enough to be afraid of the dark, and few enough to be hungry." She hesitated, then gave the inquisitor more than she strictly needed to. "They've been following us since the deep road. They know we have supplies. They know some of us won't come back from this descent, and they mean to take what the mountain leaves."

Fenwick made a small sound and began rolling up his map with hands that shook.

"Then we cross fast," Vane said. "Tight column. Lanterns doused to three. No one stops on the stair for any reason." She looked at Wendell, and in the lantern light her eyes were very pale. "Can you tell if they'll strike before we reach the bottom?"

Wendell pressed her hand to the stone again, and the stone answered.

*They wait below,* the rock whispered, in the layered voice the dead used when many of them spoke at once. *They have put their dead among the rocks like traps, and they wait below, and they are afraid, oh they are so afraid, because they have heard the crown coming down through the mountain wearing iron and fire, and they think the crown is the plague, they think you are the plague—*

"They're below," Wendell said. "At the base of the stair. They've seeded the switchbacks with their own risen dead—hidden among the scree, probably bound with the same foul craft that drives the plague-corpses." She met Vane's eyes. "And they think we're the source of it. They think the crown sent us."

For a moment the inquisitor looked almost amused, in the gray, exhausted way of someone who has stopped expecting justice from the world. "The irony is noted."

"It isn't irony," Wendell said quietly. "It's what happens when the crown burns shrines and hunts hearers. You taught the mountains to be afraid of you. Now everything down there fears you, and fear makes men stupid, and stupid men with crossbows on high ground kill a lot of people before anyone explains anything."

Vane held her gaze a long moment. Then: "What would you have me do? Send a herald?"

"No." Wendell looked out over the stair, at the weeping faces streaming in the lantern light, at the darkness pooled below like standing water. "Send me."

* * *

They argued about it, of course. Vane argued longest, which surprised Wendell until she understood the argument wasn't really about tactics—it was about the inquisitor's ledger, about the debt Vane believed she owed a sister she had failed, being paid now in the coin of keeping one hearer alive. Wendell did not pretend she couldn't see it. She let the woman keep her debt and took the concession anyway.

So they rigged it carefully. Three lanterns stayed lit at the top of the stair, hung on poles to suggest a camp settling in for the night. The rest of the expedition withdrew into a side gallery fifty yards back—a dead-end tunnel, Fenwick swore, that appeared on no chart he trusted, which was exactly why it wouldn't occur to raiders to search it. Wendell would walk down alone, openly, unarmed, carrying nothing but her carving hammer at her belt because she refused, absolutely, to go anywhere without it, and Vane—after a long silence—had agreed, saying only, "It doesn't look like a weapon anyway."

"It isn't," Wendell said. "That's rather the point."

She went down the Weeping Stair alone in the dark, feeling her way by the water-slick stone and by the dead, who rose around her like fish rising to a dropped crust. They knew her now. Word of her had passed down through the mountain faster than any rider could carry it—the hearer, the carver, the granddaughter of Maren Blackwood whose name was still spoken in the hollow choir three levels above. They crowded close without touching her, and their attention was like sunlight on skin after days underground: warm, almost unbearable.

Below her, at the foot of the stair, the raiders waited among the tumbled blocks where the fissure opened onto a lower gallery. She could feel them the way she'd learned to feel the living through their relics—hot spots in the cold, hearts beating out little rhythms of dread. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen of them, and among the scree on the switchbacks, six risen corpses crouched like stones with teeth, bound by cords of ugly craft that prickled against her senses like nettle-wire.

She stopped on the fourth switchback, where a carved king wept mineral tears over her left shoulder, and spoke aloud to the darkness.

"I know you're there."

Silence, except for the falling water. Then, from below, a voice roughened by cold and distrust: "Come down slow, then. Hands wide."

"I'm alone. I'm unarmed. I'm the one the crown drags in chains, if that helps you hate me properly." She kept descending, step by step, palms open. "I'm the hearer you've heard about. The one who reads the dead."

Another silence. When the voice came again it was closer, and younger than she expected. "Prove it."

"You have a man named Aldous Rennick with you," Wendell said. "Formerly of the Third Border Company. He carries his mother's wedding ring on a cord around his neck because he couldn't bury it with her when the fever took her, and he's been meaning to return it to her grave for four years and hasn't, and the guilt of that is louder in him than the hunger. Your lookout on the eastern shelf has frostbitten toes and hasn't told anyone because he's afraid you'll leave him behind. And the corpse you've got bound at the third switchback—that binding is failing. It will break loose within the hour, and when it does, it won't care whose throat it finds first, yours included."

The dark below her went very still. She heard breathing—one man's, ragged. Rennick himself, then, close enough to speak.

"How," he said, and it wasn't a question so much as a wound.

"Because I listen," Wendell said. "It's what I am. It's why the crown wants me. And it's why I'm telling you that you're wrong about what's happening in this mountain, and you're wrong in a way that's going to get every one of you killed."

Torches flared below her—three, four, five points of light rising from behind the rubble. She saw them then: gaunt men and two women in pieced-together armor, crown tabards worn inside-out or dyed black, faces sharpened by hunger into something older than their years. Their leader was a broad woman with a deserter's brand scarred over her collarbone and a crossbow held low but not holstered. Her eyes went over Wendell like a butcher assessing meat.

"The crown sent a hearer down the mountain," the woman said. "Right when the dead started walking. Forgive us if we don't find that comforting."

"The crown didn't send me to raise anything. The dead were walking before I ever came near Thornfell." Wendell stood on the last step above them, water misting her hair, torchlight catching the weeping kings above her head. "But you already know the dead here aren't like the plague-dead in the valleys. You've seen them. They run *from* something. They climb. They flee downward things. Haven't you wondered why your bound ones keep trying to climb even while you whip them back down?"

One of the women shifted. Another muttered something to Rennick. Fear moved through the group like wind through wheat, and Wendell seized it—not cruelly, but the way one seizes any rope thrown across a gap.

"You're afraid of the dead," she said. "Good. Be afraid. But aim the fear correctly." She lifted her voice, let it ring off the wet stone. "I can show you. I can show you what your own bound dead are terrified of. That's the difference between me and the crown—I don't drive them. I listen to them. And right now, every dead soul in this mountain is screaming about what wakes beneath us, and you're standing on the lid."

The branded woman's crossbow dipped a fraction. "Lid of what?"

Before Wendell could answer, the third switchback screamed.

It came from above and behind her—the bound corpse, its rotten cord of craft snapping early, exactly as she had promised and sooner than she'd hoped. She spun in time to see it lurching down the steps, a gray horror in a rotted soldier's coat, moving with that terrible wrong-jointed speed the plague lent its puppets. Torchlight slid off its milky eyes. One of the raider women shrieked. Crossbows came up in a clatter of panic, bolts flying wild, one sparking off the rock a hand's width from Wendell's cheek.

And Wendell did the thing she had sworn she would never do in front of witnesses.

She reached into the dead.

Not gently. There was no time for gentle. She seized the fleeing corpse the way a carver grips a knotted piece of oak—finding the grain, finding the soul-shard still trapped in the rotted flesh, a soldier's remnant, a boy from somewhere green who had died at a ford twenty years ago and been dug up by fools. Around it, the foul binding writhed like burned wire. She crushed it. She *unmade* it, singing through the bone the counter-song every hearer's grandmother had forbidden and every hearer's grandmother knew, the song that unravels another's claim, and the corpse staggered, shuddered—

—and knelt.

On the wet stone of the Weeping Stair, in the torchlight, in front of twelve armed deserters, the plague-driven corpse knelt to Wendell Blackwood like a penitent, its milky eyes clearing to something almost human, and its ruined mouth opened and said, in a voice like wind through winter reeds:

*"Green. It was green, where I drowned. Wasn't it?"*

"It was green," Wendell told it softly, kneeling too, heedless of the crossbows, heedless of everything but the shaking remnant in front of her. "It was green, and the ford is still there, and you can rest now. You were only ever running. I know. I know."

She put her hand on its brow, and the remnant sighed out of the body like smoke off a snuffed candle. The corpse folded down gently onto the steps, finally, merely, dead.

When Wendell raised her head, twelve crossbows pointed at her, and not one of them wavered—but not one of them fired either, and the silence on the stair was total except for the weeping water and the ragged breathing of frightened men.

The branded woman stared at her as though Wendell had stepped out of one of the carved faces. "What are you?" she breathed.

"A bone-carver," Wendell said. Her voice did not shake, though her hands, she noticed distantly, had begun to. "From Greyhollow. And I'm the least frightening thing that's going to happen to you tonight."

She turned toward the darkness beyond the raiders' torchlight, toward the lower gallery and the vault-road beyond it, because she could feel it now through the soles of her boots—had been able to feel it for an hour, growing, rising, patient as root-pressure under pavement.

Something far below had noticed the binding break. Something far below had noticed *her*.

The dead crowding the stair all turned their attention downward at once, a thousand invisible faces tilting in unison, and their fear poured through the stone into Wendell's bones like icy water:

*It heard. It heard the song. It knows a hearer stands above it. It is waking up to listen back.*

Behind her, boots thundered on the stairs—Vane and the soldiers, drawn by the screams, bursting into the torchlight with steel drawn, shouting orders that dissolved into confusion as they took in the scene: the kneeling corpse, the surrounded hearer, the deserters frozen mid-murder. Vane's eyes found Wendell's, and Wendell saw the exact moment the inquisitor understood how much of her tool had been hidden from her.

But there was no time for that reckoning either. Far beneath the Weeping Stair, deeper than any map Fenwick carried, something vast exhaled—and the waterfall above them stuttered, faltered, and for three full seconds stopped falling altogether, hanging in the lamplight like a curtain of held breath, before crashing down again all at once, as if the mountain itself had swallowed.

Every torch on the stair guttered.

"We need to move," Wendell said, to deserters and crown soldiers alike. "Now. All of us. Together."
