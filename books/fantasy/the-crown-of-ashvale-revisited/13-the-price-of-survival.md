## 13. The Price of Survival

The Weeping Stair had taken its toll in blood.

Wendell knelt in the lee of a collapsed gallery two turns below the ambush site, her hands red to the wrist, and counted the wounded the way she once counted coins she could not afford to spend. Four of the crown riders still breathed. One of Vane's own soldiers did not, though his chest rose and fell in shallow, wrong rhythms, like a bellows with a split seam. And Tam—the youngest of them, a boy who had joined the expedition for a soldier's pay and a soldier's story—lay with a spear wound through his belly that no needle and thread in any kingdom would mend.

He was dying by inches, and the inches were running out.

"More water," Vane said behind her. Her voice was steady the way a bridge is steady—with load-bearing effort visible at every joint. She had taken a gash along her forearm binding it herself, refusing help, and now she moved among the wounded with a field surgeon's economy, doing everything that could be done and knowing it was not enough.

Wendell pressed her palms flat against Tam's forehead. His skin was cold already, cold in the way stone is cold, and beneath it—not faintly, not distantly, but *loudly*—she heard nothing at all.

That was the horror of it. The dead spoke to her through bone and token and carved thing, through the relics of lives finished. But a dying man was neither alive nor dead, and he sat in the one silence her gift could not reach. She might as well have been deaf as any other woman in this gallery.

"He's going," she said quietly.

"I know." Vane did not look up from the bandage she was winding. "The others may follow if the wounds fester. Deep Road rot sets in fast. I've seen men lose arms to scratches down here."

"And you've no surgeon."

"I *am* the closest thing to a surgeon this expedition has." A bitter edge crept into her voice. "The crown sends inquisitors and cartographers and astronomers to find its buried treasure. It does not send healers. The dead are supposed to do the dying for us."

Wendell wiped her hands on a rag that was past saving. Around them the gallery stretched away into dark, its walls ribbed with old tool-marks where miners of the founding era had chased some vein of metal and abandoned it. The lanterns hung low, their light pooling yellow and unsteady. And everywhere—seeping up through the floor, breathing out of the walls—came the murmur of the restless dead, that vast uneasy tide she had been hearing since they passed the first sealed gate. Since the thirteenth turn began, it had grown worse. Closer. More *urgent*. Like water rising against a door.

They were afraid. All of them, the whole drowned congregation of Thornfell's stolen generation, and their fear pressed against Wendell's skull like a second skull.

But fear was not all they were. She had learned that much in the Hollow Choir, standing among the bones arranged in their patient audience rows. They had been hearers once, some of them. Servants of the crown. Women like her grandmother, whose name a voice in the deep road had spoken as if it knew her.

And above them all—if the murals in the threshold vault were true, and every whisper she'd heard since confirmed it—there was an echo that still wore the shape of authority. The dead queen. Ashara, the first king's bride, whose soul had gone into the seal alongside her husband's crown, or so the oldest carvings hinted. Whose voice Wendell had heard once already, distinct from the chorus the way a lighthouse stands distinct from the sea: *You are listening. Good. Listen further.*

"She can help us," Wendell said.

Vane looked up sharply. "Who?"

"The queen. The echo in the vault below." Wendell flexed her stiff fingers. "The dead here aren't mindless, Serah. They're people. People can be bargained with."

"You want to bargain with a ghost." Vane said it flatly, testing the weight of the words. "For what? What could we possibly offer the dead?"

And there it was. The question Wendell had been circling since the ambush, since she'd stood on the Weeping Stair and turned the raiders aside with the dead's own terror, since she'd understood that her gift was not a window but a door, and doors open both ways.

She thought of her mother's face.

The thought came unbidden, the way such thoughts do—as if some part of her already knew what the price would be and was saying goodbye. Her mother, Maren Blackwood, who had died when Wendell was nine, who had taught her the first knots and the first carvings and never once flinched at the things her daughter heard. Wendell could see her still: the crooked smile, the burn scar on the left cheekbone from a forge accident, the grey coming in early at the temples. She could see her because the memory was hers, worn smooth and bright with twenty years of handling.

What would a queen of the dead want with gold? With land? With anything the living valued?

*Memory,* whispered something far below, soft as settling snow. *We keep what the living forget.*

* * *

They carried the worst of the wounded down to the threshold vault itself, because Wendell insisted and Vane, after a long silence, overruled her own instincts and agreed. It was madness to bring bleeding men closer to the source of the unrest—but the vault was warm, impossibly warm, warmed by whatever ancient engine the pact had become, and warmth slowed the rot. That was the practical reason. The true reason Wendell kept behind her teeth: the echo had called to her, and echoes answered better in their own halls.

The vault opened around them like the inside of a bell.

Murals covered every surface, painted in pigments that had not faded so much as *settled*, grown quiet with age. The founding war marched across the north wall in ochre and rust-red: armies, burning fields, a king with a crown raised high. On the east wall, the same king knelt before a figure made entirely of small carved souls, thousands upon thousands, each one a tiny human figure rendered in bone-white paint—and the king was smiling. That was the detail Wendell could not stop returning to. He was smiling as he signed the pact. As he pledged a generation. As he bound the dead of Thornfell into service against enemies already broken.

The west wall showed the queen. Ashara, crowned, standing at the vault's heart with her arms open—not in triumph, Wendell thought now, studying the faded line of her mouth. In welcome. In *witness*. Someone had to stay and hold the door while the souls went through. Someone had volunteered.

At the center of the chamber, on a plinth of black stone, lay the crown itself.

It was smaller than Wendell had imagined. Iron and silver, plain almost, the kind of circlet a frontier lord might wear—except that the air around it bent, the way air bends above a fire, and the murmur of the dead gathered around it in slow spirals like moths around a flame they both loved and feared. She did not touch it. She had learned enough in the last days to know that touching it would be a conversation she could not end.

Instead she knelt at the vault's center, set her carving hammer and her knife on the stone before her—the tools of her trade, laid out like an offering—and closed her eyes.

"Ashara," she said aloud. Her voice sounded very small under all that painted history. "Queen of the sealed hall. Wife of the oath-breaker. I'm told you listen."

The temperature dropped. Not the cold of mountain stone—the cold of attention, of something vast turning its regard toward one small kneeling woman. The lantern flames leaned sideways, all together, as if bowing.

*I listen,* said the voice, and it was everywhere, in the stone and the marrow of her own bones. *Few ask me anything anymore. They ask the crown. The crown only takes.*

"My people are dying." Wendell kept her eyes closed. It was easier to speak to the dark than to the murals. "Four wounded. One of them a boy. Wounds fester, and we're days from any healer. You hold power over what lives and dies in this mountain—I've felt it. I'm asking you to lend it."

*A bargain.* The echo tasted the word. *The living always come to the dead wanting something. My husband did. Look how that ended.* A pause, long as a held breath. *What will you give, bone-carver? Your gold means nothing here. Your blood I cannot use—you are not done living, and I do not take the unfinished.*

Wendell's throat tightened. She had known. She had known since the thought of her mother's face rose unbidden on the stair, and knowing had not made it easier to say.

"My memories," she said. "A year of them. Take any year you choose."

Silence. Even the murmur of the restless dead seemed to lean in.

*You understand what you offer?* The voice was gentler now, which was somehow worse. *A year is not a page torn from a book. A year is a room in the house of you. Board it up and the house is smaller forever. You will know something is missing. You will walk past the door and feel the draft and never remember what was kept there.*

"I understand."

*Do you?* Something moved through the vault—a pressure, a sorrow older than kingdoms. *I watched my husband trade a generation because he believed victory was worth any price. I stayed behind to hold the seal because I believed someone must pay for the sins of kings. I have had four hundred years to learn what memory is worth, bone-carver. It is the only currency the dead respect, because it is the only thing the living truly own.*

"Then take mine," Wendell said. "One year. In exchange, my wounded live. All of them. The boy most of all—he hasn't made enough memories yet for anyone to steal."

Another silence. When the echo spoke again, Wendell could have sworn she heard something like approval, or perhaps only weariness worn smooth into something kinder.

*Name your year. I will not choose for you. That much mercy I retain.*

And there was the trap within the trap. Because if Wendell named a year, she named what she would lose, and she would spend the rest of her life knowing exactly what had been taken. But if she refused to choose—

"No," she said slowly. "I won't name it. Choose yourself. That's the bargain. I trust you to take what the dead need more than what I'd miss least."

*Foolish girl.* But the words were warm. *Or wise one. It has been so long I've forgotten the difference.* The presence gathered itself, drew closer, and Wendell felt it like winter pressing at a window. *Then hear the terms, and hear them once. I will take one year from your remembering. In exchange, life returns to the four who falter, and the fifth—* a hesitation, delicate as a spider's step, *—the fifth I cannot save. His wound is past any power short of the crown itself, and the crown I will not use. Do you accept terms less than you asked?*

Wendell opened her eyes. Across the vault, Tam lay on a bedroll, grey-faced, his breath a thread about to snap. Vane stood over him with her hand on his shoulder, and the inquisitor—who had hunted women like Wendell, who had burned shrines and posted bounties—was crying without any change in her expression at all, tears simply falling, as if her face had given up pretending.

"One life instead of five," Wendell said. "Yes. I accept."

*Bargain struck,* said the queen of the sealed hall. *Now sleep, bone-carver. Payment is collected in dreams.*

* * *

She dreamed of Greyhollow.

Of the workshop above the harbor, salt wind rattling the shutters, the smell of linseed oil and bone dust. Of a memorial commission for a fisherman lost off the Teeth—no, that wasn't right, that was last month, that was real, why was she dreaming of things that were real? The dream kept sliding, offering her days like a merchant offering cloth: here, the morning she carved her first swallow; here, the night the smugglers' lanterns came up the cliff path; here, a table in the Drowned Bell inn with rain on the windows and someone laughing across from her, someone whose laugh she knew, whose face she—

The dream took it.

She felt the taking. That was the terrible part, the part no warning could have prepared her for. It didn't hurt. It was like watching a tide go out over ground she loved: the water withdrawing, the sand gleaming wet and bare, and then the sand drying, and then the sand looking like it had never been underwater at all. A year of her life drained away through her cupped hands and she stood on the shore of herself waving goodbye to something she could no longer name.

When she woke, the vault was quiet and the lanterns burned steady, and Vane was shaking her shoulder with both hands.

"Wendell. *Wendell.* Wake up—they're breathing. All four. The fever's broken, the wounds are—" The inquisitor stopped, staring. "What did you do? What did it cost?"

Wendell sat up. Her body ached as if she'd been carved and reassembled slightly wrong. The wounded were indeed breathing—deep, even breaths, color back in cheeks that had been clay-grey. Tam slept like a child, his bandages clean, the spear wound in his belly closed to a pale seam as if months of healing had been folded into a single night.

Alive. Four of five. The bargain had held.

"I paid," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, flattened, like a voice heard through a wall. "It's fine. It's done."

"What did you pay?" Vane again, relentless, kneeling to grip her wrists. "Wendell. What did you give them?"

And Wendell reached for the answer and found the room in the house of herself boarded shut.

She knew she had a mother. The knowledge remained, a fact like a fact of geography: Maren Blackwood, born under the eaves of Greyhollow, dead of winter fever when Wendell was nine. She knew it the way she knew Ashvale lay south of the mountains. But when she reached for the face—the crooked smile, the burn scar on the left cheekbone, the early grey at the temples—there was only smooth blankness, a portrait with the paint scraped down to bare wood. She remembered the funeral. She remembered the grave on the headland. She remembered grieving. But the woman herself, the living warm particular woman—gone. A draft through a boarded door.

"Oh," Wendell said. Very quietly. It was the smallest sound she had ever made.

Vane read her face the way soldiers read battlefields. "She took memories. A year of them." Her grip tightened. "Which year?"

"I don't know." Wendell laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "That's rather the point, isn't it? I don't know what I've lost. I just know the house is smaller." She pressed her palms against her eye sockets until sparks bloomed in the dark. "There was someone. There was—my mother, I had a mother, I know I loved her, I know she taught me knots, and I can't—" Her breath hitched. "I can't see her face, Serah. I can't see my own mother's face."

The vault said nothing. The murals said nothing. Somewhere in the walls, very far down, the chorus of the dead murmured on—but softer now, she thought. Softer, as if even they were ashamed.

Vane did something Wendell would never have predicted. She sat down on the cold stone beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and said nothing either. For a long time. The inquisitor who had a sister executed in the last purge, who had joined the hunt to control it from inside, who had watched faith crack and fall away from her like old plaster—she simply sat, and let the silence be company instead of judgment.

"She knew," Vane said at last. "Your mother. About your gift. Did she?"

"Yes." Wendell lowered her hands. "I think—I believe she did. I remember that she never flinched. I remember *that*, at least."

"Then she'd say it was worth it." Vane's voice was rough. "Four lives. She'd say it ten times over."

"Maybe." Wendell looked across the vault at the crown on its black plinth, at the bent air around it, at the slow spiral of whispers it fed on. "Maybe. But that's the lesson of this whole cursed mountain, isn't it? Everyone pays, and everyone decides afterward what the payment was worth. The first king decided a generation was worth a war. I've decided a year was worth four lives. And somewhere down the road, someone will decide the crown is worth everything, unless we stop them."

She said it without thinking, and then heard what she'd said, and Vane heard it too. The inquisitor turned, and in the lantern light her eyes had gone sharp and bright with a purpose that had nothing left of zealotry in it—only grief, honed to an edge.

"*Them*," Vane repeated. "Not the deserters. Not the raiders. Them."

Wendell nodded slowly, and as she nodded, a cold slid through her that had nothing to do with the dead.

Because Odo Fenwick was not in the vault. She had assumed the old cartographer was sleeping in the upper gallery, keeping his distance from ghosts as old men reasonably did. But he had been asking questions for days—quiet ones, mapmaker's questions, about distances and gates and the layout of the threshold vault. He had seen the crown. He had seen the route to it. And three nights ago, over the campfire, he had asked her, idly, seemingly idly, how long a rider would need to reach the capital if he left the Weeping Stair by the western fork.

She stood up too fast. The vault tilted.

"Serah," she said. "Where's Fenwick?"
