## 14. Odo's Treason

The map fragment came back to us in the mouth of a dead man.

He had been a muleteer in life, one of the corpse-raiders' unwilling porters, and when the fighting at the Weeping Stair was done he had simply lain down among the wounded and stopped pretending to move. Vane ordered him searched like any other fallen soldier, and it was Corporal Ives—hands shaking, breath still ragged from the ambush—who drew from the dead man's coat a roll of oilcloth no bigger than a prayer book.

Inside was the fragment Odo Fenwick had recovered from the drowned gallery three days before: a rectangle of cured hide, brittle as autumn leaf, inked with the winding approach to the threshold vault. The Pact Beneath. The place where we had stood with our lanterns held high and learned what the first king had bought with a generation of souls.

"It should have gone into the strongbox," Ives said, staring at it as though the leather might bite. "Inquisitor, sir—it was in the strongbox. I locked it myself."

Vane took the fragment without a word and turned it over twice, her thumb moving along its edge the way she moved her thumb along the spine of her warrant when she was thinking. Around us the Weeping Stair dripped its endless mineral tears, each drop striking the stone with a small, patient sound, like a clock counting toward something.

I felt the dead man before I touched him. That was the trouble with my gift these days; since the vault, since the murals, the voices came louder, closer, as though the mountain had loosened its teeth and let them slip through. He was not angry. He was not even afraid anymore. He was *relieved*, in the dim way of the recently dead, and beneath the relief ran a single thread of memory, bright as wire:

*A thin old man with ink-stained fingers, pressing this bundle into his hands. A promise of coin for his widow. Go quickly, go quietly, and don't look back at me.*

I knew those ink-stained fingers. I had watched them unroll charts across a camp table only that morning, tracing routes with a stylus worn smooth by sixty years of service.

"Odo," I said.

Vane's head came up sharply. "You're certain?"

"I read him." I crouched beside the muleteer's body, careful not to touch the cold flesh, though touching was not truly necessary anymore—the dead near the vault spoke through stone and air and the wet dark itself. "Fenwick gave him the fragment and paid him to carry it down the service stair. There's a widow in some valley town who'll never see her coin."

"Then Fenwick is already gone." Vane rose, and her voice carried the flat calm I had learned to fear more than shouting. "How long?"

I closed my eyes and listened past the muleteer, past the other dead scattered down the stair like dropped stones, listening for the living. It was a strange, inverted thing to do—hunting warmth instead of whispers—but the dead marked the living the way water marks a wading bird. *There,* said a chorus of faint voices far below, echoing up the shaft. *Quick feet. Old feet. He stumbles on the left side but he does not stop.*

"Gone before dawn," I said. "Three hours, perhaps four. He's taking the drainage galleries west—they flood with meltwater by midday, so he'll push hard while they're passable."

"And where do the drainage galleries lead?"

"To the old survey camp at Cindermark. From there, the pilgrim road." I swallowed. "From there, the capital."

* * *

They convened the war council in the shrine chamber we had claimed for our wounded—a hollow in the rock where hearers of the old crown had once kept their vigil, its walls still scored with the listening-marks of my grandmother's grandmother's generation. Vane stood at the head of the table with the map fragment pinned under her palm. Captain Aldery sat to her right, gray-faced, his arm bound and slung since the ambush. Ives and two others filled out the benches. And at the far end, wrapped in a blanket because the deep cold had gotten into her bones, Serah Vane's shadow-self: the woman who had confessed to me two nights ago that her sister had died on an inquisitor's pyre for the crime of hearing voices in bone.

That confession hung between us now, unspoken, as it always did. It made us allies. It did not make us friends. I was not sure anything would ever make us friends, and I had stopped being certain I wanted it.

"Fenwick has served the cartographers' office for forty years," Aldery was saying. "He mapped half the sealed galleries himself. If he's turned, it's not treachery of the heart—it's treachery of the purse. Someone in the capital offered him something."

"Someone in the capital already knows about the crown," Vane said. "My orders said as much. *Recover and convey.* Not destroy. Convey." Her jaw tightened around the word. "I burned my copy of those orders. Apparently someone else remembered theirs."

"Then we ride after him," Ives said. "Six of us mounted, he's seventy years old and alone—"

"On foot, through galleries that flood by noon, with half our company wounded and the thirteenth turn waxing overhead." Vane shook her head once, a hard, economical motion. "We cannot catch him. The question is whether we try anyway, or whether we do something smarter."

"Which is?" Aldery asked.

All eyes came to me. I had grown used to that over the weeks—used to being the strange instrument they consulted, the bone-carver who heard what no living ear could. But lately the looks had changed. Since the Weeping Stair, since I had stood in the middle of a corpse-raider ambush and spoken the dead into turning aside, they looked at me less like a tool and more like a storm front. I could not decide which unsettled me more.

"The question," I said slowly, "is what Fenwick is actually selling. Because it isn't a map. A map of the approach is worthless without the vault's own defenses, and the vault's defenses—" I hesitated. The truth of it still sat in my chest like swallowed ice. "The vault's defenses are the dead themselves. They gather there because the pact is failing and the crown is the seal. Anyone who goes in expecting to *take* something will walk into a city of the restless. Fenwick knows that. He helped seal half those galleries; he knows better than anyone what waits below."

"Then why sell the location at all?" Ives asked.

"Because whoever sent him doesn't believe in the dead the way we've seen them." I looked at Vane. "Because there are men in the capital who think the unrest is a rebellion to be put down, or a resource to be harvested, and a crown that commands the dead sounds to them like a weapon worth any number of graves. Your orders didn't say *destroy*, Inquisitor. They said *convey*. Someone wants it carried home in triumph."

The silence that followed had weight, like snow loading a roof.

"So we pursue," Vane said at last. "Not to catch him—to get ahead of him. Cindermark sits above the pilgrim road where it narrows at the Widow's Span. If we take the hunters' cut across the shoulder of the mountain, we can be there before him, and we can make sure whatever message he carries dies with him or is discredited."

"No." The word left me before I had decided to say it, and every face at the table turned again. I made myself go on. "Killing the messenger changes nothing if the buyer already expects delivery. Fenwick fails to arrive, and the capital sends a real expedition—soldiers, priests, siege-craft. Ten times our number. You cannot stand at the Widow's Span forever, Serah. Sooner or later they come down into the mountain, and everything we learned in the vault tells us what happens then: they disturb the seal, the pact decays faster, and every soul buried under Thornfell wakes screaming."

"Then what would you have us do?" Vane's voice was quiet, which meant it was dangerous. "Let him carry the crown's location home? Let them come and *claim* it, as their orders say? You saw the murals, Blackwood. You know what that crown is. Do you imagine the men in the capital will wear it gently?"

"I imagine they'll try to wear it at all, which is worse." I pushed back from the bench and stood, because sitting still had become impossible. "Listen to me. All of you. The crown isn't a treasure. It isn't even a weapon, not really. It's a *knot*—a knot tied around a generation of souls to hold a war-pact together, and the knot is rotting. Every hand that reaches for it pulls a thread. Fenwick selling its location isn't the disease. It's a symptom. The disease is that the crown exists, and there is exactly one cure, and it has to happen here, in this mountain, on the thirteenth turn, or not at all."

"You're talking about destroying it," Aldery said. "Alone. With six soldiers and a handful of wounded."

"I'm talking about it being the only act that ends this. Not pursuit. Not messages. Not politics." I looked at Vane and made myself hold her gaze. "If Fenwick reaches the capital, the hunt doesn't end—it *justifies* itself. Everything you've done, everything your sister died for, becomes the crown's excuse to dig deeper. You want to control the hunt from inside. Fine. Then help me make sure there's nothing left inside to control."

For a long moment Vane said nothing. The drip of the Weeping Stair counted time somewhere behind the walls. Then she looked down at the map fragment under her palm, and I saw her thumb find its edge again, worrying it, the way it worried her warrant.

"If we let him go," she said, "and we fail—if the shatterturn fails, if the crown survives us—then we will have handed the capital its prize with both hands and no witnesses."

"Then we'd better not fail."

"That is not an answer, Blackwood."

"It's the only answer there is." My voice cracked on it, and I hated that, and I let it stand. "You told me once that you joined the hunt to keep it honest. Well. Here is the moment it stops being honest and starts being a salvage operation. Choose."

* * *

She chose, in the end, neither the way I wanted nor the way I feared.

At dusk she assembled the able-bodied in the gallery mouth and announced that the expedition would split. Four men—Ives among them—would take the strongest mules and ride hard for Cindermark, not to kill Odo Fenwick but to reach the capital ahead of him, bearing Vane's own sealed testimony: the truth of the vault, the murals, the pact, the crown. Truth against truth. If the old cartographer sold a location, Vane's report would arrive behind him like a blade between the shoulders, telling the council what the location actually contained.

"And if they don't listen," Ives said. "If they read your testimony and send the expedition anyway."

"Then they were always going to send it," Vane said, "and at least we will have spent our strength where it mattered—below, at the seal, rather than chasing one old man across a mountain." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that I recognized, because I had seen it in my own reflection for years: the look of a person setting down a thing they have carried so long they've forgotten its shape. "I am done serving a hunt I cannot steer. What remains of my warrant, I intend to spend well."

The four riders went down into the evening gloom with Vane's packet sewn into Ives's coat lining, and I stood watching until the last torchlight winked out among the pines, feeling the mountain breathe around me. The dead were louder tonight. The thirteenth turn was waxing toward its fullness, and beneath our feet, all through the black galleries of Thornfell, the souls of a murdered generation stirred like sleepers turning toward a window before dawn.

Odo found me there, or I found him—or perhaps the dead arranged it, as they seemed increasingly to arrange things.

He came up the gallery path leaning on his stylus-staff, breathing hard from the climb, and for a moment we simply regarded each other: the traitor and the hearer, the old servant of maps and the young servant of voices. He did not look like a man fleeing with stolen goods. He looked like a man who had finally set down something heavy and discovered his arms remembered nothing else.

"You knew," he said. "Before Ives opened the oilcloth. You read it off the dead man."

"I read it off the dead man."

"Then you know I didn't do it for coin." His voice was dry as the old hide he trafficked in. "Forty years I drew the crown's roads. Forty years I watched them seal the galleries my own masters ordered opened, burn the shrines, purge the hearers—and I told myself it was prudence, it was order, it was the price of peace. Then you walked into my camp reading names off a dead soldier's token, and I understood that everything I had mapped, I had mapped wrong. The roads weren't hiding treasure, girl. They were hiding a wound." He looked out at the darkening valley. "So yes. I'm carrying the location to the capital. Because the men there need to see the wound with their own eyes, and they will only come to see it if they think there's a crown at the bottom of it."

"They won't come to mourn it," I said. "They'll come to take it."

"Some of them." He smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle smile. "But not all. I've lived long enough to know that no institution is one thing. Somewhere in that marble pile is a clerk, a scholar, one honest fool who will read the truth and act on it. I'm gambling the mountain on that one fool. It's a better wager than leaving the crown's fate to a bone-carver with a hammer, and I'll thank you not to argue."

"I wasn't going to argue." I hesitated, then asked the question that had been sitting in my chest since the oilcloth unrolled. "The muleteer. Did you know he'd die carrying it?"

Odo was quiet for a long moment. Below us, the Weeping Stair dripped its patient count.

"He was already dead," the old man said at last. "Walking-dead, dead-dead—it hardly matters, the way things are going. I gave his widow's name to a friend in Cindermark, and the coin will find her whether the message does or not." He straightened, wincing. "Everything costs, Blackwood. You taught me that, if nothing else. Now go break your crown. And if you fail—" he tapped his temple with one ink-stained finger, "—remember that somewhere in the capital, one old fool remembers the true maps. The ones underneath."

He went down into the dark, and I let him go, and I did not tell Vane I had spoken with him, because some mercies are also betrayals and I was too tired to sort which this was.

* * *

That night I could not sleep. I climbed instead to the gallery mouth and sat with my back against the cold stone, my carving hammer across my knees, and I listened to the mountain.

It was not silence. It had never been silence, not since I was a child whittling my first sparrow-bone in my grandmother's kitchen. But the voices tonight were different—closer, more urgent, a tide coming in. *Soon,* they said, in ten thousand overlapping whispers, the way wind says it through a winter forest. *Soon, soon, soon.* The thirteenth turn was nearly full. The pact was fraying thread by thread. And somewhere below, in the vault where a king had once traded a generation for a victory, the Crown of Ashvale waited on its stone, whispering its own slow poison into the dark.

Vane found me there in the small hours. She did not sit; she never sat when the matter was serious. She stood looking down the mountain toward the road Fenwick had taken, and when she spoke, her voice was stripped of rank and rhetoric, down to the woman beneath.

"If your way fails," she said, "if the hammer breaks and the crown survives—what then?"

I turned the hammer over in my hands. Its head was stained with resin and old blood and thirty years of memorial dust.

"Then I pick it up," I said. "And I put it on. And I become the thing we both swore to prevent—because a just tyrant of the dead is still kinder than a free one wearing a king's seal." I looked up at her. "Pray with me, Inquisitor. To whatever you still believe in. Pray the hammer strikes true."

Far below us, faint as a heartbeat, the mountain answered: *soon.*
