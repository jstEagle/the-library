## 15. The Pact Beneath

The vault door was not locked. That was the first wrongness.

Wendell had expected iron, wards, the stubborn engineering of a kingdom that meant to keep its worst bargain buried. Instead the threshold stood open before them—a slab of mountain granite taller than three men, carved through its center with a keyhole shape that held no lock at all, only darkness, and on either side of that darkness, murals.

Vane raised a hand and the lanterns stilled. Behind them, the expedition's remnants—eleven souls now, after the Weeping Stair—waited in the gallery with the wary quiet of people who had stopped believing they would leave this place alive.

"Light on the walls," Vane said softly. "All of it."

They moved the lanterns along the stone, and the murals woke.

The first panel showed a young king—Ashvale's first, crowned in gold, sword raised over a field of the fallen. Wendell knew the founding war from chapel tiles and schoolroom songs: the king who united the broken valleys against the tide-lords of the coast. Here he was every inch the hero, his face rendered with such tenderness that some dead carver must have loved him.

The second panel showed the same king kneeling before a mountain.

Not before an altar. Not before any god Wendell recognized. Before Thornfell itself, its peak drawn with ribs of stone like a crouching beast, and beneath the mountain, in cutaway, a chamber—and in the chamber, a circle of small figures. Children, she thought at first. Then she looked closer, and her stomach turned, because they were not children. They were adults drawn small, drawn *dim*, their faces blank as unfinished wood, arranged in rows like goods in a merchant's cellar.

A generation. Drawn as inventory.

"The founding war," Odo Fenwick murmured beside her. The old cartographer had gone gray around the mouth. "The histories say the tide-lords were broken in a single season. Impossible odds. Every scholar says so. A single season." His finger hovered over the rows of small dim figures without touching them. "You don't win impossible odds in a single season unless something *pays* for it."

Wendell said nothing. She didn't need to. She could hear them—the mural's subjects, or what remained of them. Not voices here so much as pressure, the way deep water presses on the ear. A thousand souls pressed flat under stone, not screaming, not raging. *Waiting.*

The third panel showed the crown.

It was smaller than she'd imagined. A circlet of black iron set with seven pale stones, and around it the carver had ringed a band of text in the old script—the script her grandmother had taught her to read by candlelight, calling it the hearers' letters, saying it came down from before the crown ever sat a brow.

Wendell read it aloud because she couldn't help herself. The words came out of her like water finding a crack.

"*What is pledged below shall reign above. What reigns above shall hold below.*"

Silence in the gallery. Then Vane: "Translate the rest."

"There isn't rest," Wendell said. "That's all of it. That's the whole pact." She traced the old letters with one fingertip, feeling cold come up through the stone into her skin. "The king pledged a generation of souls to whatever sleeps under this mountain—in exchange for victory. And the crown is the seal. It isn't a symbol of authority. It *is* the authority. Whoever wears it holds the pledge. Holds *them*."

"And the plague of restless dead?" Vane asked. But her voice had changed. Wendell heard it—the flatness draining out, the zealot's certainty cracking along some fault line she'd been carrying since the Weeping Stair, maybe since her sister.

Wendell turned back to the fourth panel, and there it was, the answer Vane already dreaded.

The fourth panel showed the crown again—but cracked. And rising from the fields above, drawn upward out of graves and barrows and river-mud, the small dim figures. Not marching on towns. Not clawing at the living with the mindless hunger the broadsheets described. They were *fleeing*—the carver had made it unmistakable, their bodies angled away from the mountain, faces turned back toward it in terror. Fleeing the decaying seal the way tenants flee a burning house.

"They're not rebels," Wendell said. Her own voice sounded far away. "They never were. They're collateral. The pact is failing—the thirteenth turn, the binding weakening—and when a seal cracks, everything it held starts to leak out. The dead aren't rising in anger. They're running. From *this*." She looked at the rows of small dim figures, at the blank faces, and grief rose in her throat like floodwater. "And we hunted them for it. The crown burned shrines. Executed hearers. Blamed the victims for escaping a fire."

Vane was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was low and stripped. "My order's doctrine says the gifted raise the dead out of malice. That the hunt protects the living."

"Your order's doctrine," Wendell said, "was written by men who knew exactly what was under this mountain, and needed someone to blame while the seal rotted."

She felt it then, more than heard it—a shift in the pressure behind the walls, like a great body turning over in shallow sleep. The lanterns guttered though there was no wind. Somewhere far below, stone settled against stone with a sound like a sigh.

Fenwick crossed himself with the old sign, the one from before the crown's church. "Then whoever wears the crown—"

"—holds the collateral," Wendell finished. "Commands the dead justly or unjustly, forever, because the pact doesn't end when the wearer dies. It passes on. That's what *reigns above, holds below* means. It's not a throne. It's a chain with a crown on it."

She stood before the open threshold a long time, listening—not with her ears but with the bone-sense her grandmother had sharpened in her, the sense that had never once lied to her. Beyond the door, in the chamber the murals promised, something waited. The crown waited. And around it, patient as sediment, a thousand souls pressed flat under four hundred years of stone, waiting to learn whether they would be owned again, or freed, or simply abandoned when the last of the seal gave way.

One of them, faintly, pressed a thought against her mind like a child pressing a palm against a window from inside a dark house:

*Choose well, carver. Choose soon. The moon turns.*

Wendell closed her eyes. When she opened them, Vane was watching her, and Fenwick was watching her, and both of them, she realized, had already understood that whatever happened next belonged to her.

The vault door stood open. The moon was turning.

She stepped toward the darkness.
