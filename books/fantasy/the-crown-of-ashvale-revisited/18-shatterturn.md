## 18. Shatterturn

The thirteenth moon did not rise so much as bleed.

It came up over the throat of Thornfell like a wound in the sky, swollen and rust-colored, and its light poured down through the great fissure above the vault until the whole cavern seemed to swim in it. Wendell stood at the threshold with her hammer in her hand and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she listened—for the first time in her life she listened *on purpose*, all of her at once—to what the mountain was saying.

It was saying too much. It had been saying too much for three days now, since the pact's binding began to fray and every soul buried in the dark had found its voice again. The dead of Thornfell were not a chorus anymore. They were a flood. They pressed against the walls of her skull like water against a dam, ten thousand murmurs braided into one long moan that had a shape to it, a direction: *out, out, out.*

And beneath them, deeper, older, the crown itself had begun to sing.

Not words. Not even a whisper. A kind of gravity, a pull, the way a hook sings through the flesh of a fish. *Come down. Come be counted. Be mine.* The murals around the vault told her exactly what that meant—the king on his throne of ash, the generation of souls pouring like grain into his open hand, the seal set upon their mouths. She had read those walls until her eyes burned. She knew what was being offered to whoever lifted the crown, and she knew what it would cost, and she knew—God help her—how easy it would be to say yes. To hold all this grief in two hands and order it. To make the weeping stop by becoming its master.

She turned the hammer in her grip instead. Bone-handled, horn-set, made by her grandmother's grandmother for work no harder than shaping a child's memorial dove. It weighed nothing. It would have to be enough.

"Wendell." Vane's voice came from the dark behind her, rough as gravel. The inquisitor leaned against the vault door with one arm bound to her chest where the deserters' blades had opened her shoulder at the Weeping Stair, and her face was grey as tallow in the moonlight, but her eyes were still lit, still burning with whatever was left after faith dies. "They're coming up the switchbacks. I count forty torches. Maybe more behind."

"Fenwick's torches?"

"Some of them. The rest are garrison regulars out of Thornfell Gate. They'll have crossbows and they'll have priests, and the priests will have told them you're a witch who means to curse the mountain." She pushed herself upright with a grunt. "I told my own people the truth. Some stayed. Most didn't."

"How many stayed?"

Vane was quiet a moment. "Four. Two of them boys younger than your apprentice."

Wendell looked back down the long gallery, where four torches burned small and stubborn among the dark. Behind them, filling the gallery from wall to wall like mist given intent, stood the dead.

They had come up out of the deep road when the binding weakened—not as corpses, not as the shambling horror the crown had named them, but as something else. Echoes wearing shapes. In the moon-bleed light they looked like people seen through smoke: miners still in their chains, soldiers in armor green with age, children, a whole generation of them, faces turned toward Wendell with an expression she would carry to her own grave. Not anger. Not hunger. *Hope.* Terrible, exhausted hope, aimed at her like a weapon pointed at the wrong end.

The dead queen's echo stood nearest of all. She had been a queen once, before Ashvale was Ashvale—before the founding war, before the bargain—and she wore her death the way other women wore crowns, patiently and without apology. When she spoke, her voice arrived inside Wendell's ears rather than through them, which was somehow worse and somehow kinder.

*They will try to take it from you. The living always think the dead can be governed.*

"I know," Wendell said aloud, because saying things aloud kept her brave. "Stay behind us. All of you. If any soldier crosses that threshold, frighten them—but do not touch them. Do you hear me? No one dies tonight. No one else."

*You cannot promise that.*

"I can promise anything I want," Wendell said. "That's the whole point of tonight."

* * *

They came at the turn of the hour, when the red moon hung directly over the fissure and the light ran deepest into the vault.

Wendell heard them long before she saw them—boots on stone, the creak of crossbow stocks, the mutter of men trying to talk themselves brave. But she also heard the thing beneath their footsteps, the sound that made her stomach clench: the crown singing louder as its servants drew near, pulling at them too, wrapping the soldiers' fear in purpose. Fenwick had sold more than a map. He had sold a story—that the witch meant to steal the crown for herself, and that whoever brought it back to the capital would be rich beyond counting and safe from the restless dead forever.

Lies stacked on lies. They always were.

Torches bloomed at the far end of the gallery, and then the soldiers came, forty strong, spreading out across the stone with the drilled ease of men who had done this before. At their head walked Odo Fenwick, the old cartographer, his maps gone, his hands trembling, his face arranged into an apology he clearly believed himself capable of meaning.

"Miss Blackwood." His voice echoed off the murals. "Please. Put down the hammer. No one wants blood—not even yours. The crown is kingdom property. It belongs to the throne, and the throne will know how to use it rightly."

"Use it," Wendell said. "There it is. You finally said it plainly."

"The plague of corpses ends the day it is worn. Every family in Ashvale sleeps soundly. Is that not worth one woman's pride?"

Behind him, Wendell felt the dead press closer, and felt the crown's song swell to meet them, and understood in one cold flash that this was how it worked, how it had always worked: the bargain dressed itself in mercy. The first king had probably wept real tears when he signed away a generation. She thought of the drowned boy whose last words she had carried out of Greyhollow, and of Vane's sister, executed for hearing, and of her mother's face, which she no longer owned, and she found her voice steady when she answered.

"Every soul in this mountain paid for that sleep already," she said. "Forty years of them. A whole generation, ground down to whispers under your founding king's heel, so the living could have peace bought with someone else's children. And now the bill's come due and the crown wants a new payer." She raised the hammer. "I'm not paying it. And neither are they. Go home, Odo. I mean that kindly. Go home while your legs still obey you."

Fenwick's jaw tightened. He glanced back at the officers, and whatever he saw in their faces decided it for him, because he stepped aside and said, quietly, almost gently: "Take her, then. Gently, if you can."

The crossbows came up.

What happened next happened fast, the way terrible things do—one breath of ordinary time and then everything at once. The first volley loosed. Wendell dropped flat behind the fallen lintel-stone, and the bolts went over her head and struck the murals, punching holes through the painted faces of kings. Vane was shouting orders in a voice gone hoarse and hard, and her four loyal soldiers came up the flanking galleries with drawn steel, and the two forces met in the narrow throat of the vault with a crash like a mountain shrugging.

But the soldiers did not break because of Vane. They broke because of the dark behind her.

The dead came forward—not running, never running, simply *arriving*, unfolding out of the shadows between one heartbeat and the next, a hundred smoke-shaped figures standing suddenly among the living. Miners with chain-marks on their throats. Soldiers with old arrows still in them. Children. The soldiers' torches guttered blue. Men screamed and fired bolts that passed through the echoes like shots through fog, and the fog only stood there, looking at them, patient as winter, and the crown's song rose to a shriek in Wendell's skull—*mine, MINE, they are MINE*—as if it could feel its collateral slipping loose.

"No one touches them!" Wendell screamed over the din, to the dead, to everyone. "Show them. Only show them!"

And the dead showed them. That was all. A miner held up his chained wrists to a sergeant's face. An old woman turned slowly, showing the place where the binding-seal had been cut into her spine. The children simply stood in front of the soldiers' boots and looked up, and looked up, and looked up, and it was the children that broke the line. Wendell watched a hardened officer drop his sword and sit down on the stone and put his face in his hands, and she watched others follow, and within the space of twenty heartbeats the assault on the vault had become forty men kneeling or fleeing in the moon-bleed dark, sobbing or praying or simply staring at the evidence of what their kingdom's peace had been built on.

Only Fenwick remained standing, backed against the far wall, white as bone. "It doesn't change anything," he whispered. "The crown is still—"

"The crown is *sealing them in*," Wendell said. "Look at them, Odo. Look at what your maps served."

*Now,* said the queen's echo, close as breath. *While the binding is weakest. Now, carver, or wait another turning of years and lose them to it entirely.*

Wendell got up. Her legs shook. The vault floor seemed to tilt and sway beneath her, and the crown's voice crashed against her like surf, offering, pleading, threatening, promising her the power to make every grief orderly forever, and she walked toward it anyway, one step, another, the hammer growing heavier in her hand with each stride until it felt like she carried the whole mountain's weight in her fist.

The crown lay where it had lain since the beginning, on the stone plinth at the vault's heart, beneath the fissure, bathed in the red light of the thirteenth moon. Up close it was beautiful. That was the trap of it. Iron and gold grown together like roots through a coffin, worked into a circlet of thorned towers, and every point of it humming with the stolen voices of a thousand souls packed so tight they sang in harmony. It was a choir, she realized. The Hollow Choir had been practice for this. Hearers had served it, tuned it, kept it singing. Her grandmother's name had lived in these walls. Her name could have lived here too, forever, comfortable and honored and *used*.

"You're afraid," Wendell told it, and meant it, and took a strange fierce comfort in meaning it. "All that power and you're afraid of one woman with a hammer."

She lifted it high.

The crown screamed. Not in her ears—in the air, in the stone, in the teeth behind her eyes, a sound like a nation drowning. The fissure-light flared. Somewhere behind her Vane was shouting her name, and Fenwick was shouting something else, and the dead were pressing forward with their smoke-hands reaching, and Wendell planted her feet the way her grandmother had taught her to plant them over a carving bench, hips and shoulders and breath all in one line—

—and she brought the hammer down.

* * *

The first blow rang off the iron like a bell tolling for a funeral, and cracked it.

The second blow split the band.

On the third, the crown of Ashvale's first king broke apart with a sound that was not metal at all but something vast and living exhaling—a sigh that began in the vault and traveled outward through every gallery, every shaft, every sealed door and forgotten stair in Thornfell, a long-held breath released at last after four hundred years of being held.

The voices went with it.

Wendell felt them leave. That was the only word for it—they *left*, all of them, streaming past her like wind through an open window, brushing her mind on the way out, and some of them touched her in passing, brief as moths. *Thank you. Thank you. Remember. Thank you.* Then they were gone, out beyond the edges of her, out beyond everything, into whatever waits where the binding used to be, and the silence that followed was so enormous that she staggered under the weight of it.

Because it wasn't empty silence. That was the price. She had known it would be, and knowing had not prepared her.

The world had not gone quiet. The world had gone *deaf*. She stood in the middle of a cavern full of weeping soldiers and groaning wounded and Vane's ragged voice calling her name, and she heard none of it—saw mouths move, saw throats work, saw the whole human orchestra playing, and caught nothing, nothing, nothing. The gift her grandmother had passed down her blood like a candle flame, the voices that had tormented and companioned her since childhood, the drowned boy's last word and the dead queen's patient counsel—all of it snuffed out with the crown, gone into the dark along with the souls it had held.

Her knees gave. Stone met her palms. She knelt in the broken light, in the scatter of iron and gold that had been a kingdom's oldest sin, and her body shook with sobs she could not hear herself making.

Then arms came around her. Vane, kneeling beside her, saying something over and over—Wendell read the shape of it on her lips, *it's done, it's done, it's done*—and holding her the way one holds a survivor of a flood, and Wendell gripped the inquisitor's ruined shoulder and let herself shake.

Around them, the dead were thinning. Not fleeing—*fading*, gently, like frost in morning sun, each echo easing out of sight as the last of the binding's tension left the mountain. The miner lowered his chained wrists at last. The old woman straightened her bent spine. The children looked up at Wendell one final time, and though she could no longer hear a single word of it, she saw the youngest of them smile before the smoke of her took her, and that smile said everything the silence couldn't.

The queen's echo lingered longest. She stood over the shattered crown, and looked at Wendell for a long moment, and inclined her head—the smallest, gravest courtesy, sovereign to sovereign—and then she too went out, like a lamp at dawn, and Thornfell belonged to the living and the quiet.

* * *

Dawn, when it came, was ordinary. That seemed the most miraculous thing about it.

Grey light crept down the fissure and found the vault littered with broken weapons and kneeling men. The surviving soldiers had not fled. They sat in clusters, dazed, some weeping, and when Vane walked among them—arm bound, warrant still folded in her belt—they watched her without hatred, and one by one, as if moved by a single thought, they began to gather up the pieces of the shattered crown and bury them under the rubble of the threshold vault, unasked, unsanctioned, a burial conducted by men who had come to seize a treasure and stayed to inter a sin.

Wendell stood apart, at the mouth of the vault, with her hammer hanging loose in her hand. She could feel nothing through the stone. Nothing through the air. Nothing through the bones in her own pocket, the little carved tokens she had carried for years, each one a voice, each one now just—bone. Smooth. Silent. Finished.

She had spent her whole life listening to the dead. She did not know yet who she would be when she stopped. The not-knowing terrified her more than the soldiers ever had.

But the mountain was free. Whatever else, whatever cost, whatever silence stretched ahead of her like an unmapped country—she had done that. The dead of Thornfell had walked out of their long captivity, and no one would hunt them again, and no one would wear them.

Vane came to stand beside her. Her lips moved; her eyes asked a question.

"I can't hear you," Wendell said. Her own voice sounded strange and enormous in her skull, the only sound in the whole world she could be sure of. "I won't. Ever again. So you'd better say what you mean, Serah Vane, because I'm going to be reading lips for the rest of my life."

Vane smiled—crooked, tired, real. She said something slow and deliberate, shaping each word so Wendell could follow it:

*"Then I'll always speak plainly."*

Below them, in the gallery, the last of the buried crown-dust settled. And somewhere far off, down in the deep road where no lantern reached, Wendell felt—or imagined, or hoped—one faint pressure against her awareness, like a fingertip tapped twice on a closed door. A knock. A promise.

She gripped her hammer, turned toward the long climb home, and started walking.
