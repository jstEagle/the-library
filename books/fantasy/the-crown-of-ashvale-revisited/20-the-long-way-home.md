## 20. The Long Way Home

The mountain let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long—slowly, finger by finger, with a reluctance that could be felt in the stone itself.

Wendell stood at the mouth of the Weeping Stair and listened to nothing.

She had been listening to nothing for six days now, and still her body had not learned to stop trying. Her ears strained at every pause in conversation, at every gap between footsteps on scree, reaching for voices that were no longer there the way a tongue returns again and again to the socket of a missing tooth. The silence was not empty. It was *full*—full of wind and water and the small ordinary sounds of living things—and none of it spoke. None of it *said* anything. A raven called from the crags above the stair, and it was only a raven, and Wendell stood in the cold morning light with tears freezing in the corners of her eyes because a bird being only a bird was the loneliest sound she had ever heard.

"Carver." Serah Vane's voice came from behind her, stripped of its inquisitor's iron. Six days underground, a hammer-blow, a shattering, a vault full of soldiers who had fled or knelt or simply wept when the dead rose up around them not as horrors but as *witnesses*—all of it had worn the woman down to something plainer and more honest. "The others are asking which way."

Wendell turned. The pass above Thornfell Gate opened like a grey throat, choked still with winter's leavings, but the sun had been at the snowfields for three days running and the meltwater was talking in every gully—a hundred small silver voices that would have told her, once, everything she needed to know about what lay beneath them, what had died there, what still remembered. Now they were only water.

She looked at the mountain the way she hadn't looked at it in twenty years: with her eyes.

"The high path is gone," she said. "The thaw took the switchbacks under the Red Scree two winters back—I heard about it from a dead shepherd whose flock went over the edge with him. He was very bitter about it." She stopped. The habit of saying such things aloud, of letting the dead's testimony pass through her mouth as casually as weather-talk, would take longer to die than the gift itself. Vane flinched anyway; she always did now, hearing the old ease in Wendell's voice and knowing what it cost. "So. Not the high path. We go down through the Hollow Combe and up the far side past the standing stones. Three days to Greyhollow if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then four days," Wendell said, "and I'll be right, just slower."

* * *

They were eleven, out of the thirty-two who had gone down. Vane counted them each morning with a soldier's grim arithmetic, and each morning the number held, and each morning something in her shoulders loosened another degree. There was Tam the mule-driver, who had never once set foot off a road in his life and now walked through trackless country because the alternative was staying in Thornfell, and no one stayed in Thornfell anymore. There were two of Vane's riders, Brannic and the young one called Pell, who had laid down their warrant-staves at the vault's mouth and not picked them up again. There was Sister Maren, the expedition's chirurgeon, who had sewn Wendell's split palm without asking how she'd come by the wound. And there were five miners' widows and one miner's son who had joined them at the Gate with bundles of relics and nowhere else in the world to go, because the crown that had promised to quiet their dead had instead taught those dead to scream, and then taught them to stop.

The boy was named Osric. He was nine. He walked beside Wendell most days and asked questions no adult dared.

"How do you know where the path goes? If you can't hear them anymore."

"I don't know where it goes," Wendell said. It was midday, and they were picking their way along the lip of the Hollow Combe, where the snow rotted into lace over black rock. "I know where it *went*. That's different."

"But you've never walked this exact path."

"No."

"So how do you know it's still there?"

Wendell crouched, brushed the crusted snow from a shelf of granite, and showed him. There, faint as breath on glass: a chisel-mark, three strokes like a bird's footprint. Twenty years old at least, cut by her own hand or her grandmother's before her, renewed whenever either of them passed this way.

"Stone remembers better than bone," she said. "Bone holds voices. Stone holds *paths*. My grandmother taught me both trades, and everyone thought the second one was just so I'd have honest work to point at." She traced the mark with a fingertip. "A hearer who can't find her way through the mountains is a hearer who gets caught. So we carved. Every smuggler road, every shepherd's shortcut, every crack in the world the crown's maps never bothered with. Marked and remarked, year after year. There are more of my marks between here and Greyhollow than there are stars over Thornfell."

Osric studied the bird-footprint with the fierce concentration of the young. "And now the voices are gone, you just... use these."

"Now I use these," Wendell said. "Now I use my legs and my eyes and forty years of walking. Do you know what that makes me, Osric?"

He shook his head.

"An ordinary guide," she said, and found, to her own surprise, that saying it felt like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was carrying. "Come on. The combe narrows ahead, and the melt's rising. If we're not across the streambed by late afternoon, we camp wet, and Sister Maren will lecture us all night."

* * *

It was strange, learning the mountains again as a stranger among strangers.

All her life, Wendell had moved through Thornfell half-deaf to the world's surface noise, her attention sunk below it like a fisherman's line sunk through ice. Wind was a distraction. Birds were chatter. The groan of settling snow was nothing, less than nothing, a fly on the window while a congregation sang in the cellar. She had crossed the Red Scree in fog because a drowned quarryman's ghost remembered the fog lifting at dusk, and she had trusted the ghost over her own squinting eyes.

Now the surface was all she had, and she discovered it had been speaking to her all along, patiently, in a language she'd never bothered to learn.

Snow told her things. Old snow, wind-packed, squeaked underfoot and held a bootprint till morning; new snow, sugar-soft, swallowed prints and slid in slabs off steep faces. Rock told her things—the ring of it under her chisel-tip when she tapped for hollows, the dark lichen lines that meant water ran beneath in spring, the particular grey-green crust that said *this boulder has not moved in a thousand years; trust it*. Even the air told her things, if she stood still enough: the smell of pine meaning shelter within the hour, the mineral bite of an updraft from a cave mouth, the wet-wool weight of cloud coming over the shoulder of the mountain with evening behind it.

"You're reading it," Vane said one evening, watching Wendell study the firewood she'd gathered with the same attention she'd once given a skull. "The land. Like you used to read the—" She didn't finish. Nobody finished that sentence anymore.

"My grandmother called it the second sight," Wendell said. "The first sight is the dead. The second is everything else. She said most hearers starve the second sight because the first one shouts so loud." She fed a branch to the flames. "I'm having to learn my own inheritance all over again, at fifty-one years old, like a child with chalk."

"Does it feel like less?"

Wendell considered the question honestly, because Vane had earned honesty in a flooded vault, holding a broken woman upright while the dead poured past them like a river uncorked.

"It feels quieter," she said. "But quiet isn't empty. I keep waiting for it to be empty, and it keeps refusing." She looked up at the early stars pricking through the blue-black over the ridge. "There's a whole world talking, Serah. It turns out I spent my life listening to the basement and calling the house silent."

Vane laughed—a short, rusty sound, as though the mechanism hadn't been oiled in years—and Wendell realized it might be the first time she'd heard the woman laugh at all.

* * *

On the third day they came to the standing stones, and Wendell's craft failed her for the first time, and she nearly wept with relief when it did.

The stones stood in a saddle between two shoulders of the mountain—nine of them, taller than a rider, leaning like tired old men in a circle that predated Ashvale, predated the founding war, predated perhaps everything the crown kept records of. Smugglers called them the Nine Grandmothers. Wendell had passed them a hundred times, and every time, the dead had crowded close here, drawn to the stones like moths, whispering over each other in languages older than the kingdom. She had never once paused to ask why. She had marked the path, checked her chalk, and hurried on, the way you hurry past a door you don't want opened.

Now she stood before them and heard wind. Only wind, moving through the circle with a low hushed tone that was acoustics, nothing more, stone shaping air the way a bottle shapes breath.

"They're beautiful," said one of the widows, a stooped woman named Aldith who had buried a husband and two sons beneath Thornfell and walked with her grief like a third bundle on her back. "I never knew they were here. All those years in the Gate, and I never knew."

"Nobody knew," Wendell said. "That was rather the point of us. Hearers kept the old roads and the old places secret, and the crown kept *us* secret, and between us we built a country with two skins—one the maps show and one they don't." She walked the circle slowly, laying her palm flat against each stone in turn, feeling only cold rock and older-than-memory patience. "When the hunt burned the bone-shrines, this is what they were burning. Not superstition. The second skin. The memory underneath the memory."

Brannic shifted uncomfortably. He still wore his warrant-staff slung on his pack, though he hadn't touched it since the vault. "You're saying the crown feared rocks."

"The crown feared being *replaced*," Wendell said. "Rocks that remember are witnesses, and witnesses are inconvenient. Ask me how I know." She smiled, and it surprised her how easily it came. "We'll camp in the lee of the easternmost stone. It blocks the north wind, and the ground beneath drains clean. I know that because I can see the moss growing only on the south face, and because I slept here in a snowstorm when I was nineteen and woke up dry. No ghosts required."

That night, for the first time since the vault, she took out her carving knife and worked—not bone, but a scrap of birchwood Osric brought her, wanting to see the famous hands at their famous trade. The knife moved in the old patterns, and the wood gave up its shape chip by chip, and nothing spoke to her, and nothing needed to. A bird, she decided. Something small and quick-winged, for a boy who asked good questions. When she finished, near midnight, she turned it in the firelight and found it was good—good in the plain way of honest work, needing no voice inside it to justify its existence.

She slept better than she had in twenty years, and hated herself a little for it, and slept anyway.

* * *

The last day, the mountain ended.

It happened the way it always happened, with an abruptness that never stopped astonishing her: one step on grey stone and frost-killed grass, the next step looking down over the whole descending world—Greyhollow's smoke rising thin and straight from its chimneys, the river a brown ribbon untying itself from winter ice, the valley floor already green at the margins where the thaw reached first. Home. Or the place that had been home, before the town's constable had held her arms while Inquisitor Vane read the warrant, before her apprentice's voice had cracked on the witness stand of her own kitchen.

Behind her, the eleven strung out along the final ridge, blinking in the open light like creatures emerging from a den. Which, Wendell thought, was exactly what they were.

Vane came and stood beside her. Below, so far below, the roofs of Greyhollow looked small enough to cover with two hands.

"They'll have word already," Vane said. "Riders move faster than we do. They'll know the expedition failed—or succeeded, depending on whose account reaches them first. They'll know I'm alive, which is its own problem, and they'll know a bone-carver from Greyhollow broke the First King's Crown with a hammer, which is a problem for *you*." She was quiet a moment. "Some will call you a hero. The crown will call you a traitor. Both kinds will come to your door."

"Let them knock," Wendell said. "I've spent my whole life keeping secrets from that town. I find I've lost my appetite for it. Let them look at me and see exactly what I am."

"And what is that?"

Wendell watched the smoke rise. Somewhere down there was her workshop above the tannery lane, her tools, her unfinished commissions, the memorial carvings of forty years stacked on shelves like a library of the dead. Somewhere down there was Tally, her apprentice, seventeen years old and terrified, who had traded her teacher's name for her own skin and would have to live with that until she died, unless someone taught her how.

"A guide," Wendell said. "Just a guide. Come on—the light's good, and I want to be down the scree before it softens."

She led them down the last descent the old way: testing each hold, choosing each footing, marking the route with fresh chalk strokes beside the faded ones her grandmother had cut before she was born, adding her own small bird-footprints to a trail that now stretched unbroken across the whole spine of Thornfell. Behind her the survivors followed in her exact steps, trusting her not with their souls now but only with their ankles, their balance, their safe arrival—and she found that this trust sat easier than the other kind ever had.

At the valley floor, where the path met the river road, Osric caught her sleeve.

"Will you teach me?" he asked. "The chalk marks. The second sight. Somebody should know, shouldn't they? So it doesn't get lost again?"

Wendell looked at the boy—at his snow-chapped face and his stubborn chin—and thought of another apprentice, younger than this, waiting in Greyhollow with a betrayal in her mouth and a lesson coming that no one should have to learn twice.

"Somebody should," she said. "Ask me again in Greyhollow, when I've settled a debt."

Ahead, the first rooftops rose out of the riverside alders, and a figure was already standing in the road before the town gate—small, hooded, arms wrapped around herself against the spring wind, waiting as though she had been waiting for days.

Tally.

Wendell did not slow down.
