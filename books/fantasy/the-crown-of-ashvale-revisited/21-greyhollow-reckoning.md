## 21. Greyhollow Reckoning

The road down from Thornfell smelled of thaw and woodsmoke, and Wendell walked it the way a woman walks out of a long illness—carefully, testing each step, half-expecting the ground to give way beneath her into the dark she had left behind.

She could no longer hear the mountain. That was the strangest part. For thirty years Thornfell had murmured at the edge of her mind like a neighbor talking in his sleep, and now it was only stone. Only snowmelt running loud over granite. Only wind. She kept catching herself pausing at outcrops of rock, laying her palm flat against them the way she once laid her hands on fresh-carved bone, waiting for something to answer.

Nothing answered. The silence was so complete it had a texture, like wool packed in her ears, like snow fallen on snow. She was still deciding whether it was grief or freedom when Greyhollow's rooftops came into view below her, huddled in their bowl of valley like stones at the bottom of a cup.

*Home,* she thought, and then corrected herself: *the place that sold me.*

From this height the town looked unchanged—the crooked chimneys, the tannery's gray smoke, the shrine to the Drowned Mother leaning its usual drunken lean against the harbor wall. But she knew better now. She knew which windows had watched the crown riders come. She knew whose tongue had loosened under questioning, whose silence had been bought with a sack of coin or the simple animal relief of *someone else, take someone else.* She had walked out of Thornfell's dark carrying the last whisper as a promise, but she had also carried the memory of every face turned away as Vane's wagon rolled through the gate.

Serah Vane rode beside her, warrantless now, her inquisitor's badge buried somewhere on the Weeping Stair along with two of her soldiers. She had burned the warrant at dawn on their last morning underground, and the paper had gone up green and eager, and she had watched it with an expression Wendell still couldn't name—relief, maybe, or the particular exhaustion of a woman setting down a weight she'd mistaken for her own spine.

"You don't have to come into town," Wendell said. "They'll remember you."

"They should." Vane's voice was flat as slate. "I gave them every reason to fear me and not one reason to trust me. I'd rather they see what I am than wonder."

"And what are you?"

Vane considered the question honestly, which Wendell had learned was the rarest thing about her. "A woman who hunted children like you for eleven years because it was easier than asking who gave the orders." She nudged her horse forward. "Let's find out what I am next."

* * *

Greyhollow knew before they reached the gate. It always knew—smoke signals, a runner on the cliff path, the harbor bell rung twice for strangers. By the time Wendell passed under the timber arch, the street was lined, and the lining was silent in a way that had nothing to do with respect.

She heard her own name spoken without sound. She saw lips shape it: *Blackwood. Blackwood's back. She's alive.* A woman pulled her child behind her skirt. A man who had bought memorial carvings from her for fifteen years looked at his boots as though they had suddenly become fascinating. Old Marta Kess, who sold eels off the quay and had once paid Wendell in salt fish to carve her dead husband's name into a rib bone so she could hear him say *I love you* one more time—Marta looked at her with wet eyes and did not step forward.

Wendell stopped in the middle of the street and let them look. Let them see the thinness the mountain had carved into her, the white streak the thirteenth turn had left in her hair, the hands that still trembled sometimes when she wasn't holding a chisel to steady them.

"I'm not here for apologies," she said, and her voice carried further than she expected, the way voices carry in cold air. "I'm here to work. My shop is mine. My mother's grave is mine. What any of you owe me, we'll settle between us, one door at a time." She turned to Vane. "This is Serah Vane. She burned her warrant. If the crown comes asking after hearers again, she'll tell them the hunt was unjustified and the threat was never us. You can believe that or not. I've stopped needing you to believe things."

She walked on. Behind her the silence broke into a hundred small sounds—doors, breath, the creak of the harbor bell—and none of them spoke to her anymore, and none of them needed to.

* * *

Her workshop sat above the chandlery at the top of Rope Lane, exactly as she'd left it, except that the lock had been forced and badly mended and someone had nailed a strip of ash wood across the doorframe—a warding charm, crude and superstitious, meant to keep the dead out. Or keep her out. With Greyhollow it was hard to say which.

Inside, dust lay over everything like a second skin. Her tools hung where she'd hung them. The half-finished memorial for the drowned boy—Tam, little Tam Osgar, *tell my mam the water wasn't cold until the end*—still rested on the bench, his small face emerging from the whalebone with the patience of the unfinished. She picked it up and her chest did something complicated and painful.

She set it down again and got to work lighting the stove, sweeping, opening shutters onto a town that pretended very hard not to watch her. There was comfort in the labor. There always had been. Bone did not lie and wood did not flatter and stone asked nothing of her but patience. She had lost the voices; she had not lost the craft. The craft had never needed hearing. Only hands.

She was oiling the hinges of the good window when the knock came—soft, guilty, the knock of someone who hoped no one would answer.

Pell.

He stood on the landing looking like a boy who had grown ten years in four months, which he had. Fifteen when she took him in, all elbows and hunger and a gift for finding the grain in a piece of antler; sixteen now, hollow-eyed, his coat too big because he'd stopped eating properly sometime during the winter. He didn't come inside. He stood at the threshold like a dog that had been beaten at this door before.

"Wendell." Not *mistress*, not *teacher*. Just her name, said the way you'd say the name of a fire that had burned you. "I heard you were back. I—I came to say—"

"Come in," she said, "or say it on the stairs. But say it looking at me either way."

He came in. He stood by the stove with his cap wrung in both hands and told her the whole of it in a rush, the way confessions pour when they've been held too long: the riders in the square, Inquisitor Vane's voice like a blade being drawn, the questions getting closer and closer to the workshop above the chandlery. How he'd held out through the first hour. Through the second. How Vane had shown him the pyre-records from the last purge—hearers, burned, and among the names, hearers' apprentices, burned younger than he was. How she hadn't even threatened him. She'd just let him read.

"She didn't have to break me," Pell said. His voice cracked on it. "I broke myself. They asked if anyone in Greyhollow worked bone the old way, and I said—" He swallowed. "I said your name. And then I showed them the stair. And then I sat in the tavern and drank ale while they put you in the wagon, and I told myself you'd be dead by spring and at least it wouldn't be me."

The stove ticked. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor. Wendell found that she was not angry—not in the way she'd expected, the hot clean anger she'd rehearsed on the road south. What she felt instead was older and sadder, the feeling of looking at a wound she recognized, because she had worn its twin for thirty years. Fear doing the crown's work so the crown didn't have to dirty its hands. That was how the hunt really functioned. Not riders. Not pyres. Boys like Pell, breaking themselves in advance.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat, perched on the edge of the stool like a bird ready to fly.

"Do you know what I heard, in the vault under Thornfell?" she said. "I heard a generation of souls, pledged by a king who was afraid. Afraid of losing a war. So he spent other people to save himself, and then he built a crown to hold the debt, and then his heirs built a hunt to make sure no one ever listened closely enough to hear the debt being paid." She leaned forward. "You were afraid. I understand afraid. I have made my whole life out of afraid—it's why I hid, why I lied, why I carved in secret and spoke in whispers. Fear kept me alive. But understand what else it did, Pell. It kept every hearer in these mountains alone. Divided. Silent. And a hundred silent hearers couldn't stop one inquisitor, and couldn't stop a king, and couldn't save a single soul in that vault. Silence is what they want from us. It has always been what they want."

"So you're going to send me away." His eyes were dry now, braced for it. "Or worse. The town will say you should. They'll say it's justice."

"The town," Wendell said, "sold me for less reason than you had, and they'll get their reckoning separately. You're not the town. You're a frightened boy who made the worst choice of his life and then had the guts to walk up these stairs and say it to my face, knowing what I could do to you." She rose, went to the tool wall, and took down the smallest knife—the one she'd first put in his hand two winters ago, whalebone handle worn smooth. "Justice outside the law isn't exile. Exile is just the law wearing plainer clothes. Here's your justice: you'll sweep this floor every morning for a year. You'll grind pigment and fetch water and learn every joint and grain the slow way, and every time you pick up a carving you'll remember what your silence cost and what your voice can cost too. And when the next rider comes asking—you'll stand on these stairs and say my name again. Loudly. To their faces. That's the price. Can you pay it?"

Pell stared at the knife in her hand. Then at her. Something moved through his face—shame burning down to something harder underneath, something that might, given years, become courage.

"Yes," he said. "Mistress."

"Don't call me mistress. Call me teacher, if you must call me anything." She pressed the knife into his palm and closed his fingers over it. "Now. There's a memorial on that bench that's waited four months to be finished, and a mother across town who thinks her son's last words died with him. We're going to finish it today. And tomorrow you're going to carry it to her door yourself, and you're going to tell her what you heard him say, and you're going to watch her face when she hears it. That's the work. That's what it's *for*."

* * *

The doors of Greyhollow opened and closed for her unevenly, in the weeks that followed, like a tide that couldn't decide.

Some stayed shut. The harbormaster, whose signature was on the writ that had invited the crown riders in—the harbormaster would not meet her eye at the market and eventually stopped coming to market at all, sending his wife instead, who bought her eels elsewhere. The family in the blue house on Chapel Row drew their curtains when she passed. She did not force them. Doors had a right to their griefs and guilts, same as people.

But others opened.

Old Marta Kess came to the workshop on the third day with salt fish wrapped in cloth and stood in the doorway wringing her hands until Wendell took the fish and said, "You paid your debt to me years ago, Marta, in eels and kindness. Come in and sit down." Marta wept into her apron and stayed for tea and left lighter than she'd arrived.

The ferryman Brannock, who had hidden his own mother's hearing-bone in a loose plank rather than surrender it during the last purge, came with a bottle of plum spirit and no apology, because none was needed—they had both survived the same way, and survival needed no explaining between them.

And on the ninth day, a procession of the hesitant: the cooper's widow, the smith's youngest daughter, a tanner's boy with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Word had gone around, the way word does in small towns, that Blackwood was working again, that she was taking commissions, that the thing the crown had called witchcraft was being practiced openly on Rope Lane with the shutters wide. They came one at a time, then two, then in a quiet stream, carrying bones and tokens and locks of hair and questions they'd been afraid to ask for generations. *Can you—? Would you—? Is it true the hunt is ended?*

"It's ended," Wendell told each of them. "Not because the crown grew kind. Because we stopped being quiet."

Vane lodged at the inn and made herself useful in ways that surprised everyone including herself—organizing the town's defense against the next raid, drafting the testimony that would go to the capital under her seal, drilling the harbor watch with a soldier's impatience. Some townsfolk spat when she passed. She accepted it like weather. "Let them," she said when Wendell mentioned it. "I earned worse. Maybe in ten years I'll have earned something better."

On a bright raw morning a month after her return, Wendell stood at her bench finishing a commission—an old woman's femur, carved with her name and the names of her seven children, ordered by those same children, all of whom had come together, awkwardly, to pay. As she worked the last letter, Pell swept the floor beside her, and the light came through the open shutters, and below in the street the market roared its ordinary roar, and Wendell realized she was humming.

She hadn't hummed since before the mountain. Perhaps since childhood. There was nothing to sing along with anymore—no chorus of the dead under everything, no whisper threading through the whalebone—but her body remembered music anyway, and made its own.

"Penny for the tune," Pell said.

"No tune," she said. "Just noise. The living kind."

* * *

It was near dusk, the stove banked and Pell gone home, when the knock came again—two knocks, formal, unfamiliar. On her doorstep stood a girl of perhaps twelve, mud to the knees, breathing hard from the cliff path. Not a Greyhollow child. Her coat was cut in the northern style, Thornfell style, and clutched against her chest was a bundle wrapped in waxed linen that she held the way you hold something that has already been taken from you once.

"Mistress Blackwood?" the girl said. "My gran sent me. From Thornfell Gate. She said—she said you'd be the only one who'd understand." The girl's chin trembled, and she steadied it with visible effort, the way children do when they've been coached to be brave. "She said the digging crews opened the lower galleries last week, where the vault was. And there's a chamber nobody ever mapped. Full of bones. Hundreds of them, arranged in rows, facing a wall." The girl unwrapped the linen with shaking fingers and held out a single small bone, a finger-bone, polished smooth by centuries of handling. "Gran said the bones are all facing a carving on the wall. A carving of a woman with a hammer. And Gran said—" the girl swallowed— "she said the carving looks like the pictures of you. And mistress—there's writing under it. Fresh writing. Nobody knows who cut it. It says:

***She will hear us one last time.***"

Wendell took the bone in her palm. It was warm.
