## 22. The Last Whisper

The morning came in soft and grey over Greyhollow, the kind of light that made the harbor look like hammered pewter and turned every rooftop into a sleeping animal. Wendell Blackwood sat at her workbench with the window cracked to let in the smell of salt and woodsmoke, and she did not hear anything at all.

She had learned, in the months since Thornfell, that silence had a texture of its own.

It was not the silence of a sealed room or a held breath. It was not the silence of the deep galleries beneath the mountain, where the dead had pressed against every loose stone like water against a dam. It was an ordinary silence—the silence of gulls arguing over fish guts on the pier, of Marta Kell's loom clacking two doors down, of children running the gutters with sticks for swords. A living silence. She had spent most of her life mistaking it for emptiness. Now she knew it was simply the world, unaccompanied.

The memorial lay half-finished under her hands: a small piece of river birch, no longer than her forearm, for old Tam Ostler's wife, who had died in her sleep at the turn of winter. Tam had come to the shop in February with his cap wrung in his fists and asked—not pleaded, never pleaded, Greyhollow folk didn't plead—whether Wendell still did carvings for the dead who *stayed* dead.

"I do," she'd said. "That's most of them now."

"That's what I heard." He'd set three silver marks on the counter, more than the work was worth, and looked at her with eyes red-rimmed but steady. "I don't need words from her, mind. I know what she'd say. I just want something that says she was here."

So Wendell carved without listening, because there was nothing left to listen to, and found—to her continuing surprise—that the carving itself had become enough. The birch took shape slowly: a woman kneeling at a garden bed, trowel in hand, her face turned up toward a sun rendered as a simple spiral. Tam's wife had kept the best window-boxes on Rope Lane. Everyone said so. Everyone could say so now; there was no need for a bone-carver to ask the dead whether they'd been loved.

Her chisel whispered through the grain. Outside, the market was assembling itself in the square below—canvas awnings snapping open, carts rattling in from the valley farms with the first hard greens of spring. The thaw had come early this year, and with it the passes had opened, and with them the road south where Thornfell stood with its gates shut and its mines flooded and its dead, at last, nowhere.

Wendell set down the chisel and flexed her fingers. They ached in the cold mornings now, all four knuckles of her right hand, an ache she suspected would stay with her the rest of her life—a small toll collected by the thirteenth turn of the moon. The hammer blow had cost more than her hearing. It had gone into the bones themselves, some debt of force paid out over years instead of days. She minded it less than she'd expected. Pain was honest. Pain told you exactly what it wanted and then left you alone.

She picked up the chisel again and worked the spiral sun deeper, and that was when she felt it.

Not heard. *Felt*—a pressure behind the sternum, faint as a finger laid against glass, the way a very distant bell makes the air hum before any sound arrives. Wendell went absolutely still. Her breath fogged the window glass. Two doors down, Marta's loom kept its patient rhythm. In the square, a fishwife cursed a gull with real poetry. The ordinary world went on being ordinary, and yet—

*Wendell.*

One voice. One only. Thin as smoke drawn through a keyhole, and familiar in a way that made her eyes sting before she understood why.

*Wendell Blackwood.*

"Isolde," Wendell said aloud, to the empty shop, and her voice cracked on the name of the dead queen.

The echo of Isolde of Ashvale had walked out of the vault with the others—or so Wendell had believed. On the last night of the thirteenth turn, when the hammer came down and the crown broke like frost off a branch, the souls of Thornfell had sighed out of the mountain in a long exhale eight hundred years held. Wendell had felt them pass through her like a wind through wheat, thousands upon thousands, the pledged generation and everyone who came after, all of them going—she had assumed—to whatever lies past the edge of hearing, which for her was now the edge of everything.

But one had stayed. She remembered now, in the way you remember a dream only after the day has knocked it loose: a weight lingering at her shoulder as the others streamed away, a presence that had bent close, and a feeling rather than words—*wait, watch, I will show you*—and then nothing, months of nothing, until this grey morning in March with birch shavings curling at her feet.

*I promised you proof,* the voice said, and it was barely a voice at all, more the memory of one, the way a scar is the memory of a wound. *Listen. Not with your ears. Those are spent. Listen with what you built instead.*

Wendell looked down at her hands, at the half-finished memorial, at the spiral sun. Her whole life she had thought the gift lived in her ears—in the hearing—and that losing it had emptied her. But the gift had never been in the ears. The ears were only the door. What remained was the thing that had always opened the door: the part of her that listened. The patience. The attention. The willingness to sit with wood and bone and grief until they spoke.

And into that willingness, faint as starlight on water, Isolde spoke.

*They are resting, Wendell. All of them. I have counted.* A pause, and Wendell had the absurd, piercing sense of a queen counting souls the way a shepherd counts sheep at dusk, name by name, because someone ought to. *The pledged generation first—they were the angriest, and they went easiest, once the seal broke. They only ever wanted to be done. Then the miners, the hearers, my own household. My husband's line ended with me; I checked. No one is left to owe anyone anything.*

Wendell pressed her palm flat against the workbench. The wood was cool and dumb and beautiful. "Where are you?" she whispered. "Why did you stay?"

*Someone must be the last,* Isolde said, and there was dry humor in it, worn smooth by centuries but unmistakable—the same tone she'd used in the vault, offering Wendell a crown and a kingdom of the dead as though offering tea. *Every ledger needs a closing hand. And besides—I wished to see the spring. I was crowned in winter and buried in winter and I have been owed a spring for eight hundred years. Yours is adequate. The gulls are disgraceful.*

A laugh escaped Wendell, sudden and wet, startling a pigeon off the sill outside. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, leaving a smear of birch dust across her cheekbone, and did not care.

"The crown," she said. "When it broke. Did it hurt them? I've wondered every night since. Whether I freed them or just—unmade them."

The pause stretched long enough that she feared the voice had gone wherever voices go. Then:

*Child. When the seal broke, it was as if a hand that had been closed around us for eight hundred years opened. We were not unmade. We were released. There is a difference, and the difference is everything, and you gave it to us with a carving hammer and bleeding knuckles.* Another pause, softer. *You asked once what authority should feel like. Do you remember? You said it should feel like letting go. You were right. You have been right about the dead since the drowned boy in your workshop, and you will be right about them until they put you in the ground, and after that it will no longer be your concern.*

The drowned boy. Colm Teague, seven years old, pulled from the harbor the autumn before everything changed. His small voice through the birch: *Tell Mama the crab got away but I almost had it.* She had carved him a boat with a spiral sail and given his mother those words, and his mother had wept and thanked her, and within the year the hunt had begun and the world had decided that such comfort was sorcery and such comforters were plague-bringers.

It seemed a lifetime ago. It was, in a sense. The woman who had sat in that workshop fearing discovery was not the woman sitting here now. This one had walked into a mountain, refused a crown, broken a pact, forgotten her mother's face, and come home to a town that had sold her to the inquisitors—and had chosen, door by door, to forgive some of them and let the rest stay shut. This one had an apprentice named Pip who had betrayed her out of terror and whom she had not exiled, and who now swept her floor every morning and was learning, slowly, the ordinary craft of shaping grief into wood—with no voices in it at all, because Wendell taught him that the voices had never been necessary. Only the listening.

*One more thing,* Isolde said, and now the voice was thinning, fraying at the edges like cloth worn to translucence. *Vane. The inquisitor. Tell her—*

"She comes to supper Thursdays," Wendell said. "Tell her yourself, if you can manage it. She'll want to know you're at peace. She burned her warrant for you, in a manner of speaking. For all of you."

*Then tell her the account is closed. Signed and sealed. And tell her—* the voice wavered, gathered itself one last time, and Wendell leaned forward over the bench as though toward a dying friend's lips— *tell her that her sister waited for her. At the end. She was among the first through, and she waited, and they went together. I saw it. I am glad I stayed long enough to see it.*

Wendell bowed her head. Somewhere in the capital or the countryside, Serah Vane was living under a different name with her warrant burned and her faith remade, and Thursday next she would sit at this table and eat stew and pretend not to check whether Wendell's hands shook, and Wendell would give her this, the only inheritance the dead queen had left: that Maren Vane had not crossed alone.

*Goodbye, bone-carver,* Isolde said, and the words were nearly gone now, a warmth more than a sound, a hand lifting from a shoulder. *Keep the spring. You earned it. And when your own turn comes—many years from now, mind, I have seen it, you are insufferably durable—you will not hear us waiting. But we will be there. That is what rest means. It does not mean gone.*

The pressure behind Wendell's sternum eased, gentled, and was still.

The shop was silent. The ordinary, living, unaccompanied silence. Marta's loom. The gulls. A cart wheel squealing on cobbles. Somewhere below, Pip was arguing with a customer about the price of a luck-charm, his voice cracking in the middle the way a fifteen-year-old's voice did, and Wendell found she was smiling so widely her face ached.

She looked down at the memorial. The spiral sun gleamed where her chisel had polished it. She finished the last petal of the border in ten minutes' work, blew the dust clear, and rubbed the birch with linseed oil until it glowed like something alive—which, in the way that mattered, it was. A record. A witness. *Something that says she was here.* Tam would hang it above his door and touch it each morning, and it would never speak, and it would never need to.

Wendell cleaned her chisels, wrapped the memorial in linen, and stood. Her knees popped. Her knuckles ached. Through the window, the market square was in full riot: awnings and apples, a fiddler tuning up by the well, farmers' daughters selling early daffodils bundled in wet cloth, and everywhere the great indifferent roar of the living going about their business, needing nothing from her, asking nothing, leaving her gloriously alone in the middle of it all.

She pocketed her chisel—she carried it everywhere now, a habit from the mountain, a talisman that had actually worked—and lifted the wrapped memorial, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch.

For a moment she let herself imagine the voices returning: the harbor dead murmuring their small unfinished business, the mountain dead keening their ancient fear, the whole crowded choir of the world pressing at her ears. She let herself miss it, honestly and fully, the way you must miss a thing to prove you loved it.

Then she opened the door and stepped out into the noise of the living, which needed no hearing at all—only walking, and breathing, and the spring coming on—and Wendell Blackwood, bone-carver, last hearer of Thornfell, keeper of the last whisper, went down into the crowd carrying her silence like a lantern, and it lit the way just fine.
