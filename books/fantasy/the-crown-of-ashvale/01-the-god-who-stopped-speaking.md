## 1. The God Who Stopped Speaking

The shrine of Veyra smelled of cold ash and old pine, and I was being paid four silver marks a week to sit in it.

Four marks. Bren had negotiated up from three, which he considered a triumph of mercenary diplomacy, and which I considered proof that the gods punish ambition. A week before the long winter closed its fist around the valley, the elders of Hollowmere had decided their shrine — empty, cold, ignored for thirty years — needed guarding against *something*. They wouldn't say what. They'd paid in advance, in good coin, and when Bren asked what exactly we were guarding the shrine *from*, Elder Maren had looked at him with the particular blankness of a woman trying to remember a word that isn't there, and said, "From nothing, presumably. But guard it anyway."

So here I sat, on the eve of winter, guarding nothing from nothing, at four marks a week.

I'd been coming to this shrine since I was six years old, back when my mother still believed Veyra listened. Back then the altar-stone at the heart of it had been warm — or so everyone said. My mother swore she'd felt it once, a heat like a sleeping animal under her palm, the day Yara-that-was was named for the god's festival. By the time I was old enough to press my own hand to the stone, it was cold as river-bottom. Everyone's grandmother had a story about the year the altar went cold: *the winter of the silence*, they called it, though nobody could tell you what had been silenced, or why, only that one morning thirty years ago every prayer in Ashvale stopped landing, like arrows falling short of a wall.

Most people adjusted. People are good at adjusting; it's the one prayer the gods always answer. They kept lighting candles because candlelight is pretty and wax is cheap, and they stopped expecting anything back. The shrines became what they are now — places to get out of the rain, places to argue with your sister away from the house, places where a mercenary company could post a bored swordsman for coin.

But some of us didn't adjust. Some of us kept talking to the dark.

I'm not proud of it. Thirty years of praying to a god who doesn't answer is either faith or foolishness, and I've never been able to tell which side of that ledger my name falls on. My mother prayed until she died. I pray less now — a few words at the altar when I take a contract, the way other soldiers touch iron. Habit, mostly. Habit with a hole in it shaped like an answer.

The evening of the first snow, I sat on the shrine floor with my back against a pillar, whetstone in hand, working the edge of my sword the way you work a worry: over and over, without progress.

Outside, the light went the color of dirty pewter. First flakes came down fat and slow, unhurried, the way snow falls when it intends to stay until spring. The long winter wasn't weather, exactly — it was a season with a personality, a siege that lasted four months and killed the careless and the old and anyone who ran out of patience before they ran out of bread. Every year it came. Every year we counted our stores, patched our roofs, and hunkered down to outlast it. You didn't fight the long winter. You endured it, and if you were lucky, in spring you found most of yourself still there.

"Guarding nothing from nothing," I told the altar.

The altar, characteristically, said nothing back.

* * *

I want it noted that I did my job properly, even though the job was nonsense. I walked the perimeter twice at dusk. I checked the shutter-latches, all six of them, because Hollowmere elders believed wind was a thief. I built the fire up in the hearth and set water on to warm, because the contract said *maintain the shrine*, and maintaining a shrine apparently meant keeping a kettle hot for pilgrims who hadn't come in thirty years.

That's why I heard the door.

It wasn't opened. It was hit. Once, hard, the sound of a body arriving at wood faster than intention. Then a second impact, softer — a shoulder sliding down the planks.

I had my sword out before my brain caught up. That's the mercenary reflex for you: the body solves problems while the mind is still asking questions. I crossed the shrine floor low and fast, put my shoulder to the wall beside the door, counted two breaths, and pulled it open onto the snow and the dark.

He fell inward.

An old man, gray-bearded, dressed for travel that had gone wrong days ago — his coat was crusted with ice along the shoulders and his boots were wrapped in rags where the leather had given out. He was thin in the way that isn't poverty but erosion, the way stone gets thin when water works at it long enough. There was blood on his lips, black in the firelight, and his eyes were the worst part. Not wild. Not afraid. *Searching*, the way a man searches a room for something he knows used to be there.

"Pilgrim," I said, stupidly, and knelt, and got an arm behind his back. He weighed nothing. Cold came off him like he'd been carrying it inside.

"Shrine," he managed. His voice was ruined, gravel dragged over gravel. "Had to reach — the shrine. Before the — "

"Before the snow. All right. You reached it." I hauled him toward the hearth. Behind me the door banged shut in the wind, and I noticed — distantly, the way you notice things you'll understand later — that no snow had blown in with it. The storm outside was thick enough to chew, and the threshold was dry as a chapel floor.

I got him down by the fire. He seized at my wrist with a strength that surprised us both, and his eyes fixed on mine with terrible focus, the focus of a man spending his last coins.

"You're her," he said. "You're the one she — "

"I'm whoever you need, friend. Drink." I held the warming water to his mouth. He drank, coughed, drank again. Blood threaded through the water when he spat. Lungs, then. Or worse. I've seen enough dying men to know the pace of it, and his pace was fast.

"The road," I said. "Where from? There's nothing north of here but fog."

Something moved across his face — not pain, though pain was clearly available in abundance. Something more like embarrassment. Like a man caught doing something shameful.

"There's no road," he said. "There's no *north*. Don't you feel it? Don't any of you feel it?" And then he laughed, or tried to, and the laugh turned into wet coughing, and I held him upright while his body argued with itself.

"Easy. Easy. You're safe."

"No," he said, with such simple, flat certainty that it stopped my hands. "No one is safe. That was the whole point of — " He stopped. Swallowed. Looked at me again, and this time there was recognition in it, sharp and awful. "You pray to her. I can see it. It's all over you. You pray to her like she's going to answer."

My spine went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the door.

"Who told you that?"

"Thirty years," he whispered. "She's been quiet thirty years, and you people just — kept the candles lit. Kept the songs. Like children singing to a mother who's never coming home." His grip on my wrist tightened. Under my fingers his pulse was stuttering like a bird against glass. "She didn't leave you. You have to know that. She didn't leave. She was *taken* — no. No, she gave it. She gave it up. She spent herself on — "

"On what?"

His mouth worked. Blood came freely now, running from the corner of his lips down into the gray of his beard. "On you," he said. "On all of you. On the — " and then his face changed.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that night. His face changed. It's the only way to say it. The muscles around his mouth shifted as if someone else had taken hold of them from underneath the skin, the way a puppeteer's hand moves a jaw. His eyes stayed his own — frightened, human, drowning — but the voice that came out of him next was not gravel and ruin. It was low and clear and it resonated in the bones of the shrine the way the bell-tone resonates in the bronze, and I knew it the way you know a voice you've been talking to in the dark for twenty years without ever once hearing it.

It said:

"*Yara.*"

Not a stranger's name. Mine. Spoken the way my mother used to say it when she woke me before dawn, soft and certain, already sure I'd come.

Every hair on my body stood up. The fire flattened as if pressed by a thumb. The kettle stopped mid-rattle. And the altar-stone at the heart of the shrine — cold for thirty years, cold through every prayer my mother ever broke her heart on — made a sound.

A single crack, deep in the stone, like lake ice groaning at the end of winter. Like something turning over in a long sleep.

The old man's chest heaved. The voice came again through his ruined throat, patient, urgent, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

"*Yara Montrose. Listen. The winter comes early this year, and it does not mean to leave. Find the Door. Find the Door before the last light fails, find the Door, find — *" and then the voice broke apart, and it was just the old man again, coughing blood, his eyes rolling up white, his body arching.

I did what mercenaries do. I held him down so he wouldn't hurt himself, and I shouted at him to stay, and I looked around the empty shrine for help that had never once come from anywhere, least of all up.

He died between one convulsion and the next. Quietly, almost politely. One moment his heart was hammering under my palm; the next it was a fist knocking on a door no one would answer. His eyes stayed open. In the last of them, as the life drained out, I swear I saw relief.

For a long time I knelt there in the firelight with a dead stranger in my arms, and the snow ticked softly against the shutters, and the kettle finally boiled over and hissed on the coals, and none of it mattered, none of it registered, because the words were still hanging in the air like smoke.

*Find the Door.*

And beneath them, older, colder, spoken in my own name by a voice I had spent my whole life begging to hear:

*Yara.*

I laid him out straight, folded his hands on his chest the way you do, and stood there shaking. Then, because I am a professional and professionals fall back on procedure when the world cracks open, I went to check the altar.

The crack had split it clean across. And from inside the split — thin as breath, faint as the memory of warmth — heat was rising off the stone.

After thirty years, Veyra's altar was warm.

I put my hand on it. I couldn't not. The warmth was faint, failing, a coal buried deep in ash — but real. Alive. Flickering like a pulse that had been weak for a very long time and might, at any moment, stop altogether.

"Thirty years," I said aloud, to the stone, to the shrine, to the dead man, to whatever was left listening. "Thirty years of nothing, and now a dead man tells me to find a Door? What door? Where? What's coming?"

The warmth pulsed once under my palm — there and fading, there and fading, like a heartbeat running out.

Like a god, dying.

Outside, the snow kept falling, fat and slow and unhurried, and somewhere far off across the valley, I thought I heard — though I told myself later I imagined it — every bell in Hollowmere ring once, all together, with no one pulling the ropes.

I took my hand off the altar. I picked up my sword. And I started walking toward town, toward the company hall, toward Bren and the ledger and four silver marks a week, because whatever was happening, it was too big for one mercenary and a dead pilgrim, and the first thing I intended to do about it was wake my captain and tell him the impossible.

He was going to hate it.
