## 2. Ashes on the Ledger

The ledger smelled of tallow smoke and old lies.

I sat across from it in the company hall while Bren pretended to count a purse that I could see, from where I stood, held perhaps eleven silver and a quantity of copper that would embarrass a beggar. The Grey Company of Ashvale — forty sworn swords in spring, thirty-one by the first frost — had once been paid in gold by men who kissed our captain's ring. Now we guarded granaries for farmers who paid in turnips, and the turnips were soft.

"Say it," Bren said, without looking up.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You've been standing there breathing through your nose like a warhorse for two minutes. Say it."

I pulled out the chair opposite him and sat, which in the Grey Company's hall was a formal declaration of intent to argue. The chair groaned. So did I, a little. Thirty years old and my knees already kept their own ledger of grievances.

"The Marlowe contract," I said. "Page six."

"There is no page six problem."

"Bren. The Marlowes haven't paid since harvest. You wrote *promised* next to their name three times, and each time you wrote it smaller, like you were trying to hide it from yourself."

He turned the ledger around with one finger, slow, the way a man turns a wounded friend so the light doesn't hit the worst of it. "They'll pay."

"They'll pay in spring. If they have a spring. If we're all still eating." I tapped the column above his careful little script. "Look at the whole page. The Harrow mill, unpaid. The Veyra shrine tithe, unpaid — and that one's mine, so don't make that face. The road watch at Cold Ford, *paid in kind*, and the kind was a barrel of pickled eels nobody will eat. We are owed more promises than coin, Captain, and promises don't keep out the frost."

Bren closed the ledger. He did it gently, which was worse than slamming it. A slammed book means anger; a gently closed book means a man has run out of things to be angry at and settled into arithmetic.

"We have reserves," he said.

"We have a jar of reserve coin under your floorboard that holds enough to buy bread for the company for nine days. Ten if we water the soup, and Tam already waters the soup. Last week I caught him watering the *water*."

That got a twitch at the corner of his mouth, quickly strangled. Bren Halloway had a face built for gravestones and only occasionally betrayed him. "You've been counting my floorboards now?"

"Somebody has to do the accounts you won't finish." I leaned back. Outside the hall's narrow windows, the valley was doing its October trick — the birches going gold all at once, as if someone had set fire to the hillsides politely, from the top down. Pretty, if you didn't know what came after. In Ashvale everyone knew what came after. Winter here didn't arrive; it laid siege. "Thirty-one mouths. Nine days of bread. And the long winter coming early — you felt it this morning same as I did. The well water tasted of iron. That's a hard-frost sign. My grandmother swore by it, and my grandmother was right about everything except men."

"She sounds wise."

"She was a horror. I miss her daily." I let the silence sit a moment, because Bren respected silence the way other men respected shouting. Then: "There's work."

His eyes came up. Not fast. But they came up.

"No," he said.

"You don't even know what—"

"It's the stranger, isn't it. The one who's been asking at the taverns." He pushed back from the table, and the legs shrieked on stone. "Yara. No."

I hadn't actually been offered anything yet, but I'd drunk one cup of cider too close to a woman with ink-stained fingers and a way of looking at maps like they owed her money, and apparently my face had filed a report. "She approached me," I said. "Last night. Odile something-or-other. Foreign name. She wants an escort to the rim."

"To the *rim*."

"To the edge of the valley. Just to look. She says she's a cartographer."

Bren laughed. It was not a warm sound. It was the laugh of a man watching a child reach toward a stove. "A cartographer. Of what? There's nothing out there to map. There's nothing out there at all."

"That's rather her point."

"Her point is nonsense, and wanting to walk to the fog wall to prove it is how people stop being people." He came around the table and stood over me, arms folded, and I was reminded — not for the first time — that he'd been carrying me out of trouble since I was nineteen and had never once dropped me. It made arguing with him feel like biting a hand. "You know the law. You helped write half of it. Nobody goes past the boundary stones. Nobody *tries*. The elders don't post that rule because they're cruel, Yara. They post it because every generation somebody gets clever, and the clever ones go to the rim, and either they come back wrong or they don't come back at all. Remember Dannel?"

"Dannel was an idiot."

"Dannel was the best tracker this company ever produced, and he went to the rim on a dare, and when they found him three days later he was sitting in the fog like a man at prayer, and he couldn't remember his mother's face. Couldn't remember *my* face, and we'd shared a tent for six years." His voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and he hated that I'd heard it. "So no. Whatever she's paying, no."

I looked at the ledger instead of at him. Page six. All those small, shrinking words. *Promised. Promised. Promised.*

"How much is she paying?" he asked, which meant he already knew I'd asked.

"A lot."

"Yara."

"Enough to feed the company through winter, Bren. Enough to pay the Marlowes' debt ourselves and eat the loss. Enough to put real meat in the soup for the first time since—" I did the math and didn't like the answer. "Since before some of them enlisted. She pays half up front. Half on return."

"And if there's no return? What does she pay then?"

"Nothing. Which is why I'd want the front half in my hand before we took a single step."

He dragged a stool over and sat down heavily beside me, close, the way you sit with family when the news is bad. For a while neither of us said anything. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs, one of the younger recruits was practicing forms badly against a wooden post, over and over, the rhythm of someone trying to sweat away a fear.

"Tell me about her," Bren said finally.

"Odile. Dark-haired, maybe my age, talks like she's reading from documents she wrote herself and found fascinating. Hands ruined with ink. Carries a case of maps strapped to her back like a coffin she's fond of." I shrugged. "She showed me one. A map of the region — the real region, beyond us. Rivers, roads, towns, mountains. Very fine work. Better than anything drawn in Ashvale."

"And?"

"And there's a blank space in the middle of it." I traced the shape on the tabletop with one finger, though the map was upstairs in my room and I'd only seen it for a minute. "Right about where we're sitting. Not empty, exactly. Empty spaces have edges, hatching, little notes like *marshland* or *here be wolves*. This was smooth. Like the paper itself refused to hold ink there. She said she's tried eleven times to chart this valley. Eleven times, from eleven directions, and every time the map comes out blank, and every time she forgets she tried until she finds the blanks again in her own portfolio."

Bren was quiet for a long moment. "That's a madwoman's story."

"Yes."

"You believe her."

"I believe her hands shook when she said it," I said. "Madwomen lie beautifully. Liars don't shake."

He rubbed his jaw. I watched him do the thing he always did, which was weigh the company on one side of a scale and everything else on the other, and I knew — I have always known — which pan he'd load first. It's why I followed him. It's also, sometimes, why I wanted to shake him.

"If we take this job," he said slowly, "and the elders hear of it—"

"They won't hear of it until we're back."

"—and the elders hear of it, we lose the shrine contracts, the road watches, all of it. Every lawful wage we have left. They'll call us oath-breakers. They'll call us worse. There are families in this valley who still leave offerings at the boundary stones, Yara. Who genuinely believe the fog is holy. You want to walk a foreign woman with a haunted satchel up to the holiest forbidden thing in Ashvale and wave?"

"I want the company to eat," I said. "Everything else is decoration."

"That's not true and we both know it." He said it without cruelty, which made it land harder. "You've been keeping that cold altar alive for ten years out of your own purse. You think I don't notice? You think I don't notice you lighting candles for a god who hasn't spoken since before you were born, and then telling me coin is the only reason you'd walk to the edge of the world?" He shook his head. "You want to go because she told you something impossible about a place you've never managed to stop asking questions about. Same as Dannel. Same as every clever fool this valley grows."

I wanted to argue. The trouble was that he was right, and we both sat there letting it be true between us like a third person at the table.

Because he was right. I had grown up on stories with holes in them. Everyone in Ashvale had. Our grandparents spoke of the founding of the valley as if they'd all witnessed it personally, and the stories never agreed — founded by refugees, founded by pilgrims, founded by decree of gods whose names changed depending on which grandmother was talking. Our songs had verses missing. Our oldest graves bore names in a script nobody taught anymore. And Veyra — my god, the quiet one, the one whose shrine I'd guarded since I was twenty and whose altar had been cold as riverbed stone for thirty years — Veyra had stopped speaking the same year the oldest stories stopped making sense, and nobody in authority had ever once seemed troubled by this.

I'd spent my whole life in a country that wouldn't answer questions, and I'd told myself I was a practical woman. Mercenaries are practical. We count coin, we count arrows, we count the living and the dead. But there was a girl-shaped hole in all that practicality, and Odile's blank map had fit into it exactly.

"Half up front," Bren said at last.

I blinked. "What?"

"You heard me." He stood, went to the window, looked out at the burning hillsides like they'd insulted him. "If — *if* — we do this, it's done quiet. Four of us, no colors, no company marks. We tell the elders we've taken private escort work in the lowlands. We go to the rim, we let your cartographer look at her fog, we come home, we collect the second half, and we never speak of it again. And if the fog does to any of us what it did to Dannel—" his voice went flat, "—then I'll carry whatever's left of you home myself, and I'll never forgive you, and I'll say so at your funeral."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't." But the corner of his mouth had gone again, traitorously. "Four days. I'll give you four days to talk me out of it, and if you can't, we ride on the fifth."

"You'll talk yourself out of it by tomorrow night."

"Probably." He picked up the ledger, hesitated, and then — this is the part I keep coming back to, all these months later, when I count what that day cost — he tore page six out. Folded it. Put it in his coat pocket over his heart, where soldiers used to carry letters from home. "Debts we can't pay yet," he said, "don't belong in the book."

* * *

I should have gone straight to bed. Instead I did what I always do when a decision sits wrong in my chest: I walked to the shrine.

Veyra's shrine sat above the town on a shelf of grey rock, small enough that most pilgrims mistook it for a shepherd's hut. Inside: one altar, one brazier, one statue of a woman holding a lantern with no flame in it. Sculptors always gave Veyra the lantern. Nobody remembered why. The priests said she was the god of thresholds and safe passage, of lost travelers and honest endings, and for thirty years she had been the god of nothing at all, because a god who doesn't speak might as well be a story, and a story you keep paying rent on is either faith or folly, and I had never worked out which one I was committing.

I lit the candle I always lit. I put the copper I always put in the offering bowl — less than usual, this month; the ledger's arithmetic reached even here.

"Someone's coming to look at your wall," I told the dark statue. "The fog. The one at the edge of everything. I'm going to walk right up to it and I don't know if that's treason or pilgrimage, and you're the god of thresholds, so frankly this seems like your department, and you've got until the fifth day to object."

The flame bent in a draft from the door. That was all. That was always all.

But as I turned to go, my eye caught the altar stone — the old one beneath the cloth, the one the priests had covered a generation ago because it had *words on it*, worn almost smooth, in that script nobody taught. I'd read them a hundred times by touch alone, tracing them in the dark like a blind woman reading a letter from someone she loved.

*We were not always here,* the stone said. *Forgive us the hiding.*

I had always assumed it was a penitent's carving. Some ancient sinner, apologizing to the god.

Standing there in the cold, with a torn ledger page in my captain's coat and a stranger's blank map waiting upstairs, I felt the first thin blade of doubt slide in sideways: that maybe the apology ran the other direction.

Outside, far off toward the valley's rim, the fog on the mountains — the fog that never moved, that had stood in the same passes since my grandmother's grandmother — shifted. Just slightly. Just enough that I noticed.

Like something on the other side, turning over in its sleep.
