## 3. The Cartographer of Nowhere

I have been thrown out of better taverns than the Drowned Bell, but never in a town that didn't exist.

That was the thought I kept turning over as I sat in the corner with my back to the wall, watching the door and nursing a cup of something the barkeep called cider and which I called an insult to apples. Outside, the rain came down in the flat, patient way of a place that has never once been surprised by weather. Inside, mercenaries drank their wages and argued about whether the winter would be long or merely cruel.

The map on my table said this valley should not be here.

Not *shouldn't* — I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I've ever kept. There are plenty of places that shouldn't exist: cities built on floodplains, empires built on promises, my career built on a forged seal. But they exist anyway, and you draw them, and you move on. What I mean is that Ashvale did not appear on any chart I had ever seen, bought, stolen, or forged. And I have seen most of them. The Imperial Survey holds eleven thousand charts, and I spent four years copying them before they caught me altering one — in my defense, the original was wrong, and I remain right, and the fact that they exiled me for it tells you everything you need to know about the Empire's relationship with truth.

Eleven thousand charts. Rivers, ranges, coastlines, the little towns with names like hiccups. Somewhere in all that ink, a valley the size of a small country, ringed by mountains high enough to make its own weather, simply wasn't.

So naturally I came to see it.

Naturally, also, everyone I asked for directions looked at me as though I'd asked them to describe the inside of my own skull. "Ashvale," the last ferryman had repeated, frowning at the word like it was a coin he suspected of being clipped. Then he'd told me how to get here anyway, in detail, without noticing anything strange about the fact that he could do it. That was the part that kept me up at night, more than the missing maps. People knew the road to Ashvale. They just couldn't hold the name in their heads for longer than it took to give directions. I'd watched a merchant say it three times in one conversation and then, when I wrote it down, stare at my notebook as if I'd written something obscene.

A country erased from memory. I didn't have those words yet — those words came later, and cost far more than I'd budgeted. What I had then was a hypothesis and a habit: when the world lies to you, lie back, but take notes.

Which is why I was in the Drowned Bell, watching the door, waiting for the mercenary captain whose name I'd pried out of a drunk drover two days prior. Yara Montrose. The drover had spoken her name the way people speak about weather that has hurt them — respectfully, and from a distance.

She came in at dusk, shaking rain off a hooded cloak, and I knew her immediately, though we'd never met. Not because she matched any description — the drover's description had been mostly teeth and profanity — but because of how she moved through the room. She scanned it the way I scan a chart. Corners, exits, hands, weight distribution. A woman doing arithmetic on everyone present. I raised my cup to her, and watched her decide, visibly, that I was either harmless or interesting, and hadn't yet determined which.

She sat down across from me without being invited. Good. I hate negotiating standing up; it makes people feel tall.

"Odile Vasseur," I said. "You don't know the name."

"No."

"You will, or you won't, depending on how tonight goes." I slid the paper across the table. "You're Yara Montrose. You serve with the Grey Company under Captain Bren Aldous. Your company guards shrines, caravans, and occasionally the dignity of merchants who've been cheated by other merchants. Business is bad. Contracts are going unpaid. Winter's coming, and your ledger has more ash than coin in it."

Her expression didn't change, which told me she was good. Most people flinch when you recite their debts to them.

"Who told you that?"

"The ledger told me. Well — the ledger's absence told me. Your quartermaster buys ink in smaller quantities every season. I checked the stationer's records." I smiled. "Also the drover told me, but he was mostly the teeth and profanity."

For the first time something moved behind her eyes. Not amusement exactly. Recognition, maybe — the look of someone meeting another animal from the same strange forest. "You're a spy."

"I'm a cartographer."

"Those aren't different things where you're from?"

"They're not different things anywhere. People just pay spies better." I turned my notebook around so she could see the page. "But what I actually am, Captain Montrose, is lost. Professionally speaking. And I'd like to hire you to help me find out why."

* * *

Here is what I know about maps, and forgive me if I lecture, but I've earned the right through years of ink stains and one very undignified exile.

A map is a lie you agree to believe. Every projection distorts; every symbol stands in for something it isn't; every border is a fiction enforced by men with worse handwriting than mine. Cartographers know this. We are professional liars who have made peace with it, the way priests have made peace with silence, or soldiers with mud.

But there are honest lies and dishonest ones. An honest lie says: *the world is too big to show you whole, so here is a version of it.* A dishonest lie says nothing at all. It just leaves a space, clean and white, and lets you assume the world ran out there.

Ashvale was a dishonest lie. Eleven thousand charts, and not one of them even had the decency to put a sea monster in the gap.

Yara listened while I laid this out, arms crossed, rain still dripping off her cloak onto the tavern floor like the room itself was trying to wash her away. Around us the evening crowd thickened, and I lowered my voice accordingly. Some conversations you have loudly, to be overheard. This one I wanted buried alive.

"So the Empire doesn't have us on their maps," she said finally. "Empires forget places all the time. They forget them on purpose, usually after burning them."

"This isn't forgetting." I tapped the page. "Forgetting leaves scars. Old roads that go nowhere, songs with the names worn off, tax records with a hole in them. I've mapped forgotten places, Captain. Forgotten places *want* to be found again — they leave breadcrumbs. This—" I spread my hands. "This is different. This is a valley full of living people, with roads in and roads out, and no chart in the civilized world acknowledges it exists. Not hidden. *Unwritten.* Do you understand the difference? Hidden things can be stumbled upon. Unwritten things can't even be missed, because there's no blank space where they should be. The lie is seamless."

"And you noticed a seamless lie."

"I noticed a seam." I allowed myself the smile. "Seams are my profession. I was copying coastal surveys for the Imperial Archive — the real ones, the deep vaults, the charts they don't let surveyors see — and I found a stretch of mountain range drawn twice. Same peaks, same passes, drawn once on the western sheets and once on the eastern, with a gap between them exactly wide enough for a valley nobody mentions. Two maps, each pretending the other half of the truth belongs to someone else. It's the oldest trick in forgery, Captain. When you want to hide something big, you don't erase it. You split it, and hide each half inside something true."

She was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere behind us a dice game turned ugly and was smoothed over by the particular diplomacy of armed men.

"Why come to me?" she said at last. "If you wanted guides, the Company takes contracts at the hall like anyone."

"Because you're not anyone." I closed the notebook. "Because your god stopped answering prayers thirty years ago and you still guard his shrine, which means you're either a fool or a woman who knows what it feels like to be lied to by something bigger than you. Because your company is broke and hungry and therefore affordable. And because—" I hesitated, because the next part was true, and true things are expensive, "—because when I said the word *Ashvale* to that drover, he forgot it between sentences, and you haven't forgotten it once since I walked in. I've been saying it all night. Watching your face. The name sticks to you like it can't stick to anyone else. Which means whatever was done to this valley, Captain, it was done *to* you, not *by* you. And I trust victims more than I trust anyone else in this line of work."

Outside, the light went from grey to blue to nothing. She looked at me for a long time, and I let her, because I'd learned that the fastest way to be trusted is to be worth looking at.

"What's the job?" she said finally.

"Escort. Me, and my instruments, to the rim of the valley. The edge. Wherever your maps stop."

"Our maps don't stop."

"Exactly," I said. "That's the problem."

* * *

We negotiated terms over the insult-to-apples, and I'll grant her this: she drove a harder bargain than half the guild factors I've dealt with, and she did it without raising her voice, which is rarer still. Coin up front — half, because the Company's trust in strangers was apparently proportional to its empty purse. Provisions for three. And one condition she added herself, quiet, almost an afterthought, the way people add the things that matter most:

"We go up to the rim, we look at whatever you're looking for, and we come back down. We don't cross it. Whatever's past the fog stays past the fog."

"Fog?" I said. "There's fog at the rim?"

"There's fog at the rim." Something flickered across her face — not fear, exactly. Familiarity. The way you feel about a wall you grew up beside. "It doesn't move. Birds won't fly into it. My god-mark burns when I get close, and I'm told that's normal, which should tell you something about normal around here."

I wrote that down. Of course I wrote that down. A fog wall that birds refuse to cross, in a valley absent from eleven thousand charts, presided over by a god gone silent for thirty years. Any one of those facts alone would have been a footnote in someone else's survey. Together they were the kind of thing that got a cartographer exiled — or, in my case, exiled *again*, which at this point would hardly break the pattern.

"You think I'm mad," I said, as she stood to go.

"I think you're either mad or right," Yara said, pulling up her hood, "and I've met enough mad people to know they're usually a bit of both." She paused at the edge of the table. "One question. Why does it matter to you? The valley. You're not from here. Nobody's paying you. What do you get?"

And there it was — the question I'd been avoiding since the eastern archives, since the forged seal, since the day I'd stood on a hill in a country whose name I won't write down and realized that the map in my hands and the land under my feet were telling two different stories, and only one of them was lying.

"Because I was erased too," I said. "Smaller scale. Less dramatic. But I know what it looks like when powerful people decide a thing never happened. They took my name off the survey rolls. Took my credentials, my standing, my right to draw. And you know what the worst part was? Within a year, half my own colleagues couldn't remember working with me. Memory is a country, Captain Montrose, and whoever holds its borders decides who lives there." I shrugged, aiming for wry and probably landing somewhere near it. "I draw maps of places that don't exist. Someone has to. Otherwise the erasers win by default."

She studied me a moment longer. Then, quietly, almost against her will: "Veyra."

"Sorry?"

"My god. Veyra. You said nobody's paying me — that's not quite true. Thirty years of contracts guarding her shrines. She hasn't said a word since before I was born, and I keep taking the jobs anyway." Her mouth twisted. "Mad or right. Both."

Then she was gone into the rain, and I sat alone with my notebook and the dregs of the apple insult, and I did what I always do when the world shows me a seam: I started drawing.

The valley took shape under my pen in the lamplight — the shape of it as the drovers described it, as the ferryman's directions implied it, stitched together from the negative space of a hundred conversations. A long green trough between two white jaws of mountains. Roads running in like veins. And at the rim, all the way around, I drew the fog as a broken line, because that's the cartographer's symbol for *here be the edge of honesty.*

When I finished, I sat looking at it, and felt the feeling I live for and dread in equal measure — the click, deep in the chest, of a puzzle piece going in. Because I'd seen that shape before. Not this valley. The *shape*. In the deep vaults of the Imperial Survey, on a chart so old its vellum had gone the color of old teeth, there was a country drawn in faded ink with this exact outline, ringed by these exact mountains, and beneath it a name in a dead imperial hand:

*Ashvale. Fallen.*

Not founded. Fallen. And next to the name, in the margin, someone — some archivist, centuries ago, some predecessor of mine who had seen the same seam and been braver or stupider about it — had written a single line that I had copied out that night and carried with me ever since, folded against my heart like contraband:

*They did not fall. They were put away.*

I refolded the note, blew out the lamp, and went upstairs to pack.

Three days later, at first light, Yara Montrose met me at the north gate with a packed mule, a scowl, and a second figure beside her — a broad, grey-templed man with a captain's bearing and the expression of someone who has already lost an argument about being here.

"Captain Bren insisted," Yara said, in the tone of someone apologizing for weather. "He doesn't trust you."

"Sensible man," I said, and swung my pack onto my shoulder. "Neither do I. Let's go find out which of us the world is lying to."

The fog waited at the rim, patient as ink.
