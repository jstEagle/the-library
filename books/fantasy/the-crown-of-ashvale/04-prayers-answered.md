## 4. Prayers Answered

The trouble started with bread.

Not my bread — I'd been eating the same dense grey barley loaves for a week and they remained stubbornly unmiraculous. No, it was Marta Ellsen's bread, down in Lowfield, which had come out of her oven on the first morning of the frost already risen twice its size, warm as a sleeping child, and smelling — according to the six witnesses who swore oaths over it before anyone thought to eat it — of summer.

By noon there were forty people in her yard. By evening, the priest of Halven the Keeper had declared it a sign, and by the following dawn there were three hundred pilgrims on the Lowfield road, and someone had built an altar out of a fence post and a barrel head, and Marta was charging two coppers a loaf because she was a baker, not a saint, and saints don't have to feed three hundred mouths in a famine year.

I heard about all of this secondhand, from Bren, who heard it from his sister, who heard it from a drover who'd seen the crowd himself and turned back rather than get caught in anything that smelled like religion.

"Sign of what?" I asked.

"That's the question." Bren sat across the company table from me, sharpening a knife he did not need sharpened, which is what Bren does instead of thinking out loud. "Halven's been quiet thirty years. Same as Veyra. Same as all of them. Now suddenly his priest is handing out blessings that take."

"Take how?"

"Old Tam Wicker's bad hip. He prayed at the fence-post altar Tuesday. Walked home Wednesday without the stick." Bren set the knife down. "Tam's been milking that stick for a decade. Half the company chipped in for it when it was new."

I didn't laugh. I wanted to — the image of Tam Wicker abandoning his beloved cane like a jilted lover was genuinely funny — but something in Bren's face kept the laugh where it lived, down in my chest, cold.

"Miracles," I said. "After thirty years of nothing."

"After thirty years of nothing," he agreed.

"And Veyra?"

Bren looked at me the way you look at a friend whose dog has died and who keeps setting out food for it anyway. "You checked again this morning."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. But yes. Yes, I had checked again this morning, the way I'd checked every morning for fifteen years, since I was nine years old and my mother pressed my palm against the cold altar-stone in the shrine at Harrow's Bend and told me the god would know me now. The stone had been cold then. It was cold now. Cold as riverbed slate, cold as the inside of a promise nobody meant to keep.

Fifteen years of pressing my hand to a rock and calling it faith. Fifteen years of taking contracts in Veyra's name — guarding shrines, escorting pilgrims, standing vigil on the long nights — for a god who had stopped answering before my mother was born. Everyone said it was the same everywhere in Ashvale. Every god silent. Every altar cold. We were a valley of prayers that went up like smoke and came down like ash, and we'd made our peace with it, the way you make peace with weather.

And now Halven was curing hips and doubling loaves, and I couldn't decide whether I wanted to weep or burn something.

* * *

Over the next ten days, the valley went quietly mad.

In Millbrook, the shrine of Ossa of the Wells ran dry for a hundred years began flowing again — sweet water, they said, though it tasted faintly of iron, and the fish that came up with it were pale as drowned men and had too many eyes. In Harrow's Bend itself, old Father Anselm woke to find every candle in his church lit and burning without consuming themselves, and he took it as a blessing right up until the candles refused to go out, day after day, week after week, until he had to board up the windows so the light wouldn't keep the village awake, and people started calling them the Watching Lights, and stopped going into the church altogether.

At the crossroads near Tarn, a woman named Ilse Vay gave birth to a son she'd prayed for through eleven barren years. The boy was healthy, loud, perfect — except that he never cried at night. Never fussed, never whimpered. He lay in his cradle with his eyes open, watching the ceiling, and Ilse told the midwife it was like having a little landlord in the house.

Small miracles. Wrong miracles. That was the phrase Odile used later, but I got there on my own: each one answered a prayer truly asked, and each one answered it slightly off, like a stranger repeating your name back to you with one syllable wrong. Bread that rose too high. Water that flowed too sweet. A child who watched too well.

Hope curdles fast when you're hungry. The first week, people wept in the streets and burned tallow candles they couldn't afford. The second week, they started asking questions. Why now? Why these? And why — this question moved through the taverns and markets like a draft under a door — why were the answers always just a little bit wrong?

The company took contracts. Of course we did; the company takes contracts the way rivers take water, and coin was thin enough that winter was starting to look like a word other people used. Guarding processions. Escorting priests. Standing watch over the Millbrook well while pilgrims drank and argued about whether the many-eyed fish were omens or lunch.

I took the ones near shrines. Nobody assigned me to them; I just volunteered, quick and casual, the way you volunteer for things you can't explain. And at each one, between watching crowds and checking sightlines, I'd find myself pressing my palm flat against stone. Ossa's well-curb. Halven's fence-post altar, still standing in Marta Ellsen's yard, worn smooth now by ten thousand hands.

Warm. They were all warm. Not hot — not alive, exactly — but warm the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies. Something had passed through. Something was still passing through.

Veyra's altar-stone in Harrow's Bend stayed cold as a debt.

I told myself it meant nothing. Gods wake unevenly; maybe hers was a deep sleep; maybe silence was just her way. I'd told myself versions of that lie for fifteen years and I was very good at it, the way you get good at any trade you practice daily.

But lying to yourself is like carrying water in your hands. You can do it for a while. You just can't stop moving.

* * *

It was on the eleventh day that the dreams started, and I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I had left by then.

I dreamed of fog. Not the valley mist that rolls off the mountains every autumn, soft and ordinary and gone by midmorning. This was fog with edges. Fog that stood. In the dream I walked toward it along a road I knew — the north road, the one that runs toward the pass nobody uses — and the fog stood ahead of me like a wall stands, straight-sided, floor to sky, and it did not move when the wind moved, and the birds turned back at it, wheeling in tight frightened circles, and somewhere behind me a voice that I knew better than my own name said: *not yet.*

I woke with my hand pressed flat against the floorboards, as if I'd been reaching for something in my sleep. My god-mark — the small grey crescent behind my left ear that every sworn servant of Veyra carries, the mark that had been as inert as a scar my whole adult life — was burning.

Not stinging. Burning. A low, banked heat, like a coal wrapped in wool.

I sat up in the dark of the barracks and listened to the company breathe around me — Bren's slow rumble, the twins' matched snoring, young Piet talking to somebody in his sleep, some girl, probably, or possibly a horse — and I pressed my fingers to the mark and felt the heat pulse once, twice, like something knocking.

"Not yet," I said aloud, to no one. "What does that mean, *not yet?*"

Nothing answered. But the heat faded slowly, reluctantly, the way a hand lets go of yours.

I should have gone to Bren then. I tell myself that. I should have shaken him awake and said something true for once — *something is happening, something is happening to me* — because Bren was the only person in the world who'd have believed me without laughing, and even he would have needed a drink first.

Instead I dressed, and strapped on my sword out of habit, and walked through the frozen streets of Harrow's Bend to the shrine, because when the thing you've loved your whole life finally knocks, you don't ask questions through the door. You open it.

* * *

The shrine of Veyra in Harrow's Bend is a small stone building older than anyone can account for, tucked between a cooper's yard and a house that's been empty so long the ivy owns it. Inside: six pews, one lamp, one altar-stone, and usually me. The stone sits on a plinth at the front, waist-high, grey, uncarved. Most gods get statues. Veyra got a rock, and even that had been an afterthought, I think — the kind of thing a congregation does when they can't remember what their god looked like but feel rude leaving the space empty.

I'd been coming here since I was nine. I knew the smell of it — cold stone and old lamp oil and, underneath, something mineral and clean, like snowmelt. I knew the exact note the door hinge sang. I knew which pew wobbled.

That night, the shrine smelled different.

Underneath the familiar scents was something else — something green. Growing things. Wet earth and new leaves, in the dead of autumn, in a shuttered stone room. It hit me at the threshold and stopped me there with my hand on the doorframe, heart going like a drummer who'd been paid extra.

"Hello?" My voice sounded stupid and small. Fifteen years of one-sided conversations, and now that the silence might be breaking, all I had was *hello*, like a shopkeeper.

The lamp was lit. I hadn't lit it. It burned steady and low, and its light fell on the altar-stone, and the stone —

The stone was sweating.

That's the only word. Beads of moisture stood on its grey surface, catching the lamplight, and around its base, where the plinth met the floor, a single thread of moss had crept up overnight, vivid green, impossible, alive. I crossed the room in four steps and put my palm on the stone and it was — not warm. Warm is nothing. It was *warm like skin.* It was warm like the flank of a horse that knows you're coming. Under my hand I felt a rhythm, faint and slow and deep, and it took me an embarrassing amount of time to recognize it as a heartbeat, because I had spent fifteen years telling myself this stone was a rock, and rocks don't beat.

"Veyra." Her name came out cracked. I knelt, because kneeling was the grammar I knew, even if everything else had changed languages. "I'm here. I'm — I've always been here. Say something. Say anything. Say my name if you know it."

The moss grew while I knelt. I watched it happen — watched a green thread extend a finger's length across the plinth in the space of ten breaths, reaching toward my knee like a plant toward sun. The heartbeat under my palm quickened. The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft.

And then, from everywhere and nowhere — from the stone, from the walls, from inside my own skull where my own thoughts should have had exclusive rights — a voice. Not sound, exactly. More like remembering a sound. Like hearing words spoken long ago, in another life, by someone you loved.

*Yara.*

One word. My name, in a voice like cold water over stones, and it undid me completely. I put my forehead against the altar and wept like a child, ugly and graceless, fifteen years of vigil and doubt and stubborn unpaid devotion cracking open all at once. Somewhere in it I was aware of how ridiculous I was — a grown mercenary sobbing on a rock in an empty shrine at midnight — and I did not care even slightly.

When I could speak again, I said, "Where have you *been?*"

The warmth under my hand flickered. The heartbeat stuttered. And the voice came again, and this time it was wrong — strained, thinned, like a shout through a wall, like a singer running out of breath halfway up a hill:

*Listen. Listen to me. The others are waking and they are not — they are not what you remember. Do not pray to them. Do not answer them. If they call your name—*

The voice frayed. I felt it fray, the way you feel a rope go in your hands.

*—if they offer you anything, Yara, anything at all — the Door. Find the Door. Before winter—*

Gone.

The lamp guttered out. The heartbeat slowed, slowed, and settled back into that faint, far-off rhythm, like a sleeper turning over. The moss stopped growing. The green smell faded last, lingering in the stone like perfume in a room after someone leaves.

I knelt there a long time. Long enough for my knees to file formal complaints. Long enough for the barracks bell to mark the third hour.

Then I did what I always do when the world hands me something too large to hold: I broke it into pieces small enough to carry.

Piece one: my god was alive. She was weak, and frightened, and she spoke in fragments, but she was alive, and she knew my name, and whatever else happened, I would never press my hand to cold stone and call it faith again.

Piece two: she was afraid. Veyra — patient, silent, granite-calm Veyra, the god whose whole theology could be written on a coin — was afraid of the others. Afraid *for* me. *Do not answer them. If they offer you anything.*

Piece three: the Door. Whatever that was. *Find the Door, before winter.* And winter was coming early this year; everyone said so; the geese had flown south weeks ahead of schedule and the farmers were slaughtering pigs with grim efficiency, reading the sky like a ledger full of debts.

Piece four, the piece I turned over longest, the piece with teeth: the miracles were wrong. Small and wrong, every one, like gifts from someone who'd studied you from a distance and never actually met you. Bread and wells and watching children. Answers that fit the prayers the way a mask fits a face — closely, and hollowly, and hiding something behind them.

The old gods were awake. All of them, apparently, except mine. And they were awake in a valley that had forgotten them for thirty years, handing out wonders like a peddler handing out sweets to children whose parents had warned them about strangers.

They weren't blessing us.

They were looking for something. And every answered prayer, every warm altar, every pale fish and unblinking infant was a lantern held up, a knock on a door, a voice calling through a dark house: *where are you, where are you, where are you hiding.*

I stood. My knees cracked. Outside, the first hard snow of the season was falling — weeks early, fat flakes, falling straight down without wind, as if the sky itself were being careful.

On the altar-stone, in the dark, the moss had arranged itself while I wasn't looking. I saw it as I turned to go, and I stood very still for a moment, and then I committed it to memory the way you commit a face.

Three words, spelled in green thread across the grey plinth, small as a secret:

*the cartographer comes.*

I stood in the doorway of my god's house with snow gathering on my shoulders, and I laughed — one short, disbelieving bark that startled a roosting pigeon off the cooper's roof.

Because I knew exactly who that was. Because she'd arrived in Harrow's Bend two days ago with a case full of maps of places that don't exist, and she'd hired me that same afternoon, and I'd taken the contract for the coin and told myself it was for the coin.

*Before winter,* Veyra had said. *Find the Door.*

And my god, it seemed, had just told me where to start looking: with the stubborn foreign woman who drew maps of nowhere, and who had looked at me across a tavern table with eyes that said she suspected the world was lying, and who had no idea — none at all — that she was already part of the answer.

I pulled my collar up and walked out into the falling snow, toward the inn where Odile slept, moving fast, because winter was coming down early and wrong, and for the first time in my life, I knew something the darkness didn't want me to know.
