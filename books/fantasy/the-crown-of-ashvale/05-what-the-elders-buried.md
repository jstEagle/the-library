## 5. What the Elders Buried

There are three things a mercenary learns early: never count coin before the contract's signed, never drink before the wounded are stitched, and never open a door your captain told you not to open.

I'd broken the first two by my twentieth winter. The third took me thirty-one years and one bad night to get around to.

The sealed archive sat under the company hall like a stone in a boot — everyone knew it was there, nobody talked about it. Old Captain Maren had kept the key on a cord around her neck until the day she died, and then Halvard wore it, and when Halvard retired to a farm he was too sour to enjoy, it passed to me along with the ledgers, the debts, and the headache that comes with both. I'd worn the key for six years. I'd never once used it.

That's the thing about keys. They're heavier than they look. Not the iron — the *what if*.

* * *

It started with the ledger, because everything in my life starts with a ledger. Contracts going unpaid across half the valley — the mill at Corren's Ford, the shrine guard rotations, three separate escort jobs where the client had simply stopped coming to market. Coin thin as winter ice. And underneath all of it, the thing I hadn't said out loud to anyone: the stranger Odile had wanted escorted to the valley's edge, the job we'd turned down because it was forbidden, forbidden meaning *the elders said so*, and the elders saying so had been good enough for forty years of Bren Marchetti's professional life.

Except lately nothing was good enough. Yara came back from her shrine contract with a look on her face I'd only seen twice before — once when her mother died, once when Veyra's altar went cold and she realized she'd spent her girlhood praying at a hearth with no fire in it. A dying pilgrim had spoken through, or so the story went. Spoken Yara's name in a voice that wasn't his.

I'm a practical man. Practical men don't believe in dead men talking. But practical men also know when every account in the book stops balancing at once, and something has to be wrong with the arithmetic of the world itself.

So that night, after the hall emptied and the last of the younger ones staggered off to their bunks singing badly, I sat alone with the company ledgers and did what I do best: I looked for the oldest entry and followed it backward.

Company records went back a hundred and ten years. That was the official number. It was carved into the lintel over the archive door: *Founded Year One of the Valley, by the Nine Companions.* Every child in Ashvale learned it. The Nine Companions, first settlers, heroes who walked into an empty valley and built a country out of stubbornness and snow.

Here's what bothered me, sitting there with a candle guttering and my tea gone cold: our oldest ledger didn't start at Year One. It started at Year Forty. And the handwriting in the opening pages wasn't founding-handwriting, all careful and ceremonial. It was the hurried hand of someone copying from something older, something that was falling apart, something they were trying to save before it crumbled entirely.

You copy records when you're fleeing a fire. You don't copy them when you're founding anything. Founders write new books.

I sat with that for a long while. Then I got up, took the key off its cord, and went downstairs to a door I'd walked past four thousand times without seeing.

* * *

The lock was stiff but honest. It turned like it had been waiting.

Inside: dust, and the particular smell of paper left alone too long — dry, sweetish, faintly sad, like an attic full of old clothes that belonged to people who are dead now. One lamp. Two shelves of ledgers in the company hand, orderly, expected. And behind them, stacked flat and wrapped in oilcloth, boxes that didn't match anything else in Ashvale.

I pulled the first box out and knew immediately it was wrong. The wood was foreign — some pale grain I didn't recognize, no ash, no pine, none of the valley timber I could name blindfolded. The oilcloth was military issue, but not ours. Our quartermaster's stores used a coarse weave; this was fine, waxed, stamped with a mark I'd never seen: a circle broken by a vertical line, like an eye shut by a scar.

I unwrapped the top bundle and my hands were steadier than I deserved.

Muster rolls.

Now, muster rolls I know. I've written enough of them. Name, age, trade, oath-date, next of kin. This was a muster roll, but the format was older — columns ruled in faded red ink, entries in a script with flourishes nobody in Ashvale had used in living memory. And the header, in that same careful ceremonial hand:

*Muster of the Free Companies of Ashvale, Third Levies, in defense of the River Gate.*

Ashvale. My valley. Except the header called it something I'd never heard anyone call it. Not *the Valley*. Not *Ashvale Province*. It said *Free Companies*, which is a phrase for a country at war, and *River Gate*, which is a phrase for a city, and we have no river gate. We have a mill ford and a shrine road and fog at the edges that birds won't cross.

I read names. That's the part I keep coming back to, even now, even after everything. I read the names first, because names are the first thing a soldier looks for in any record — looking for his own, looking for his father's, looking for the ones he owes.

Marchetti was there.

Not once. Eleven times, across three pages. Marchettis who held a wall. Marchettis listed among the dead, with dates beside their names, and the dates were in a calendar I didn't know, counted from a year zero that wasn't our Year One.

Everyone in Ashvale knows their family came here with the Nine Companions, or shortly after, farmers and smiths and soldiers building something new. My grandfather told me that story with beer on his breath, pointing at the mountains: *We walked in, boy. Empty valley. Ours for the taking. Best luck our line ever had.*

My line hadn't walked in. My line had *mustered*. Had stood at a river gate in a country called Ashvale that already existed, already had companies and calendars and gates worth defending, and had died holding it.

Fell. The word arrived in my head quietly, the way bad news does, ahead of the messenger. Ashvale hadn't been founded. Ashvale had *fallen*, and someone had gathered up what was left and carried it here, into a valley that —

Into a valley that what? That appeared out of nowhere? Valleys don't appear. Countries don't get moved like furniture. Unless somebody very powerful wanted a country hidden, and had the power to hide it, and the price of hiding it was —

I put the roll down. Picked up another box. Kept going, because that's the other thing practical men do: when the floor gives way, we inventory what we're standing on.

* * *

Three hours. That's how long I stayed down there, and I'll tell you honestly that I don't remember most of it clearly — just flashes, the way you remember a battle. A trade census listing streets in a city called Vael Ashen, *lower terraces, tanneries relocated after the fires*. A tax ledger with a seal I later learned belonged to a crown that no longer existed anywhere but in that room. A letter, personal, unmailed, from a woman named Ilse Marchetti to her brother, saying *if the Gate holds till morning the children can be evacuated, tell Mother I did not run*.

Tell Mother I did not run.

I sat on the floor of a room my own key opened and read a dead woman's last letter to a family I apparently belonged to, in a country that had been erased so thoroughly that its own descendants sang founding songs about walking into an empty valley.

And here's the detail that finally made me put my head in my hands, the one that turned horror into something colder: the copies. Our oldest company ledger, Year Forty, the hurried hand — I went back up and compared. Whoever copied those records into our books had done it *deliberately*. Page by page. Someone had known. Someone in the first generation had looked at what was happening — whatever was happening, the forgetting, the fog, the silence of the gods — and had thought: *they will make us forget, so I will write it down and hide it where only the keeper of the keys will find it, and maybe someday, someone will be stupid or desperate enough to open the door.*

Forty years after the fall. Which meant for forty years, people still remembered. Grandparents who remembered raising grandchildren on lies. Not malicious lies — I have to believe that, I *have* to, or I start hating ghosts — but the lies you tell when the truth would get everyone killed. Or worse: the lies you tell when the truth has been taken out of your mouth before you can speak it, and all that's left in your head is the shape of a hole.

Maybe they hadn't lied at all. Maybe they'd woken up one morning certain their grandparents had walked into an empty valley, certain as sunrise, and the certainty had been installed in them like a roof beam. I don't know which is worse. I've turned it over every night since, and I still don't know.

* * *

I climbed out of the archive near dawn, locked the door, and stood in the empty hall feeling like a man who'd found out the house he grew up in was built on a grave.

Which, it turns out, is exactly what happened.

I did what I always do with unbearable information: I made tea, I checked the armory counts, I sharpened two blades that didn't need it. Routine is ballast. Then I sat by the cold hearth and had the argument I'd been avoiding since I closed that door.

Tell Yara.

Arguments for: she was the one the dying pilgrim spoke through. She'd served a silent god her whole life and the god had just started whispering again, and if anyone in this valley was owed the truth, it was the woman whose faith had been the realest thing about her. Also — and I want to be honest here, because honesty is the one luxury I allow myself in my own head — I didn't want to carry it alone anymore. That's the coward's version of the argument and I knew it, and I included it anyway, because a man who lies to himself about his motives will lie to anyone.

Arguments against: the elders forbade the archive for a reason, and the reason might be survival. If the loud gods — and something was waking up out there, shrines flaring, prayers answered wrong — if the gods had buried this country once, they might bury it again, and the shovel they'd use would look like a pale rider or a plague or simply forgetting, forgetting so complete that tomorrow I wouldn't remember writing this. Telling Yara might paint a target on her. On all of us.

But she already had a target on her. The dead man had said her *name*.

And there was one more thing, the thing that settled it. Odile. The cartographer who drew maps of places that don't exist, who'd hired Yara privately, who claimed Ashvale appeared on no chart in the world. I'd dismissed her as a crank with good boots. But a mapmaker who notices a missing country is exactly the kind of person who'd notice a missing *everything else* — and if the world outside had forgotten us, then somewhere out past the fog there were records, archives, answers. Odile might be mad. Or she might be the first honest stranger to look at Ashvale in a hundred and ten years.

Either way, Yara needed to hear what I'd found before she decided whether to trust her.

I banked the fire, shouldered my coat, and went to find her. She'd be at the shrine, or the training yard, or wherever the trouble was — Yara Montrose has a compass in her chest that points toward trouble, and it has never once needed recalibrating.

The snow was starting, early and fine, the kind that whispers rather than falls. Halfway across the yard I stopped, because the shrine bell was ringing — and it rang wrong, arrhythmic, urgent, the way bells ring when the ringer's hands are shaking.

Yara came out of the shrine at a run, white-faced, and the words she shouted across the snow were the last words I ever expected to hear from her mouth:

"Bren. The altar's warm."

She caught my arms, breath steaming, eyes bright with something between terror and hope. "Veyra spoke. Not through a dying man — through the *stone*. She said—" Yara's voice cracked. "She said the valley was hidden. She said we were never refugees. She said—"

"I know," I said.

She stared at me. "You *know*?"

"I spent the night in the archive," I said, "reading muster rolls from a country that fell." I watched the ground shift under her the way it had shifted under me. "Yara — your god didn't abandon us. She's been standing guard over the biggest lie in the world. And I think she's running out of strength to hold it."

Behind us, far off toward the valley's rim, the fog that never moves — the fog that has never moved in anyone's lifetime — began, very slowly, to creep inward.
