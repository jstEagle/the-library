## 6. The Edge of the Map

We left on the third morning after Odile's offer, which was two mornings after I stopped pretending I hadn't already decided to say yes.

Bren came with us because Bren always comes when he thinks I'm about to do something stupid, and because he'd spent the intervening days in the archive doing whatever it is a man does when he's found something he can't unfind. He didn't tell me what. He just packed more rope than the job required and said nothing at all, which from Bren is a sermon.

Odile arrived at the company gate before dawn wearing a coat that had been good twenty years ago and a smile that had never once been sincere. She carried three satchels, a brass tube full of rolled paper, and an instrument case she guarded like a sleeping child.

"You're late," she said to me.

"I'm on time."

"Then I was early. Same result." She looked me over the way she'd probably look over a coastline — measuring, cataloguing, deciding where I'd be useful and where I'd be in the way. "You're the mercenary?"

"I'm the mercenary."

"And you believe your valley appears on no map in the world."

I shrugged. "I've never seen a map that has it."

"That," said Odile, "is not the same thing, and the fact that you don't know it's not the same thing is exactly why I need you." She turned toward the eastern rim without waiting for agreement, because apparently that's how cartographers walk — like the ground owes them a road.

* * *

The valley of Ashvale runs long as a scar, cupped between ridgelines so steep the sun comes late and leaves early. Every child grows up knowing the shape of it: the river down the middle like a spine, the shrine-towns strung along it like beads, the rim roads climbing toward the passes that no one uses because there's nothing on the other side worth a week's walking. That's what we're taught. There's nothing on the other side. The world thins out into rock and weather and eventually, presumably, other people, but they're far and none of their business is ours.

It's a strange thing to be taught, if you think about it. We were taught the absence of neighbors the way we were taught our letters. Someone decided we needed to know it. Someone decided we needed to *not ask*.

I'd been to the rim twice before this trip. Once at fifteen, on a dare, with a boy named Corvin who kissed me behind a cairn and then lost his nerve at the fog line and made us both swear never to speak of it. Once at nineteen, tracking a poacher who claimed he'd found a way through, and who turned up three days later walking back down the valley with no memory of having gone up. Both times the fog had stood exactly where it stands: a wall, gray-white, utterly still, running along the high saddle like a seam someone had sewn shut and meant to keep shut.

Both times I'd thought: weather does that sometimes. Mountains hold clouds. It's natural.

I was thirty-one years old before anyone asked me whether it moved.

* * *

The climb took most of the day. Odile set a pace that suggested her legs had a grudge against her, and every hour or so she'd stop, unfold a little brass tripod, take some reading or other, mutter a number, and write it in a book small enough to lose in a pocket. By the fourth stop she was frowning. By the sixth she wasn't talking at all, which I gathered was bad, because she'd talked constantly until then — about map projections, about the imperial survey office that had expelled her, about how the word *hereafter* used to mean both "the future" and "the land beyond" and how languages always tell on themselves.

"What's wrong?" Bren finally asked, somewhere past the last shrine-town, where the pines started leaning east like men listening.

"The distances," she said. "By my reckoning we should have crossed the rim an hour ago. Either my maps are wrong—" she said this the way other people say *the sun rose in the west* "—or the valley is longer than it should be."

"Valleys can't be longer than they should be."

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "They can't. And yet."

She didn't explain further. I was starting to understand that Odile considered explanations a form of condescension, offered only to people who couldn't be trusted to arrive at things themselves. I hated it. I also noticed I kept arriving at the things.

* * *

We made camp below the saddle, in a hollow where the wind couldn't find us, and it was there that the birds told me what Odile's instruments wouldn't say outright.

You see birds near the rim. That part isn't a lie in the teaching. Choughs nest in the crags above the tree line, and ravens patrol the scree, and in autumn the whole sky gets busy with everything heading downhill before winter. What you never see — what I had never once seen in thirty-one years and two trips and a hundred stories from hunters — is a bird cross the fog.

That evening, a flock of choughs came over the ridge ahead of the dark, maybe forty of them, wheeling and calling, doing what choughs do, which is fly like the concept of joy got loose. They swept across the hollow above our heads, climbed, banked—

—and turned.

All of them. At the same instant. Forty birds executing one turn, smooth as water finding its level, away from the saddle, away from the fog wall, back toward the valley they'd come from. Not fleeing anything. Nothing chased them. They simply reached a line in the air I couldn't see and refused it, the way you refuse a smell, the way you flinch from a door that's suddenly open onto a room you were sure was walled up.

"They'll starve rather than cross, won't they," Odile said quietly. She was standing beside me, and she didn't sound like a woman making a discovery. She sounded like a woman confirming one.

"It's the wind," said Bren, behind us. "There's a shear off the saddle. Birds read it and turn back. Perfectly natural."

"Is there?" Odile asked.

"There's always a shear off a saddle," Bren said, with the flat certainty of a man holding onto a railing in rough water.

I looked at him. He was watching the birds go, and his hand was on his knife hilt the way it rests when he's afraid but won't say so. Bren had been up here before too. Everyone in Ashvale had been up here or knew someone who had, and everyone had a perfectly natural explanation filed away like a ration ticket, ready to produce on demand.

Nobody's explanation ever involved asking why the fog never, in living memory, lifted.

* * *

We went up at first light.

The last stretch was scree and old snow, the kind of slope where you climb with your hands and argue with gravity the whole way. My lungs burned. Odile climbed like a goat with a library, pausing only to take readings she increasingly didn't bother to record, and Bren brought up the rear with the rope coiled over his shoulder like a snake he'd tamed.

And then the slope crested, and the world ended.

I want to get this right, because I've tried to describe it since and keep failing, and failing matters now in a way it didn't use to.

The fog did not drift. Fog drifts; that's what fog *is*, moisture and laziness. This stood. It filled the saddle from scree to sky, wall to wall, a single seamless face of white-gray, still as poured stone. No curling at the edges. No thinning where it met the rocks. It didn't lap against the mountain; it *ended* against the mountain, cleanly, like paint meeting the frame of a picture. Behind us the morning sun was climbing red-gold over the valley we knew. Ahead, ten feet away, was a surface with no weather in it at all.

And it was close. That's the thing I keep failing to convey. From below, from the valley, from every story and every dare, the fog reads as distant — a haze on the horizon, weather happening somewhere else. Standing on the rim, I could have thrown a stone into it. I could have walked into it in four steps. The edge of the world was not far away. It had come as close as it possibly could without touching us, and it had been that close my entire life, hanging over every harvest festival and funeral procession, and I had called it *weather*.

"Well," said Odile, very softly, in the voice of a woman standing in a cathedral. "Hello, you."

She knelt and opened the instrument case.

* * *

What came out was a compass, or the corpse of one — a heavy brass disc, older than her, with a glass face gone cloudy and a needle that should have pointed north and instead lay dead still, sulking. She set it on a flat rock, waited, watched. Nothing. She tapped it. The needle shivered, woke, swung in a slow half-circle—

—and began to spin.

Not wildly. Smoothly, steadily, like a thing doing a job, around and around, patient as a millstone. Odile watched it with an expression I'd learn later to call her *working face*: no fear, no wonder, just appetite.

"North," she murmured, "is that way." She nodded back down-valley, south by any reckoning I knew. "Or the valley is north. Or north is inside the valley. Or—" she glanced up at me, and for the first time since I'd met her the wit in her face had a hairline crack in it, "—there is no north anymore, and the needle knows it, and it's telling us the only way it can."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning that whatever is beyond that wall isn't *elsewhere*, Yara. Elsewhere has directions. Whatever is past that fog has been *removed*. Taken out of the world's arithmetic. Your valley doesn't sit at the edge of the map." She stood, brushing grit from her knees, and looked at the wall with something close to respect. "Your valley sits in a hole cut out of it. And someone went to a great deal of trouble to cut it."

Bren said nothing. He'd walked to the fog line — closer than I wanted him, close enough to reach out and touch it — and he stood there with his fists at his sides, staring into all that nothing, and the look on his face was the look of a man reading a letter he'd been dreading for years.

"Bren," I said. "What did you find in the archive?"

He didn't answer. His breath was steaming in the cold and his eyes were fixed on the fog and his jaw worked once, twice, like a man chewing on something that bit back.

Then my arm caught fire.

* * *

I should explain the mark. Every sworn sword of Veyra carries one — pressed into the skin of the inner forearm at ordination, cold as riverbed stone, and cold it stays, always, forever, a little coin of chill under the skin that you stop noticing the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. Thirty years it had been cold. Thirty years it had been the most honest thing in my life: a god who kept her promises, even the promise of silence. Other gods' marks warmed when they listened, folk said. Ours stayed true. Ours stayed *faithful*.

It burned now. Not stung — *burned*, white-hot, as though someone had laid a coal against the bone, and I cried out and dropped to one knee and shoved my sleeve back, and there on my arm the mark had woken. It glowed. A dull gold, deep as embers, pulsing slow, like something breathing under my skin.

"Yara—" Bren was beside me instantly.

"It's fine. It's—" It was not fine. It felt like being *noticed*. Like standing outside a lit window on a winter night and feeling, suddenly, eyes. The heat climbed my arm and pooled in my chest and I pressed my palm flat against the frozen scree just to feel something ordinary.

Odile crouched next to me, and she wasn't looking at my arm. She was looking past me, at the fog wall, and her face had gone carefully, deliberately blank — the blankness of a woman hiding an expression, not lacking one.

"Your god," she said evenly, "is awake."

"She's been awake since the pilgrim died."

"No." Odile shook her head slowly. "Since the pilgrim died, she's been *speaking*. This is different. Look at the wall."

I looked.

The fog had moved.

Only a little. Only a hand's breadth, perhaps — the whole vast face of it swelling gently outward, toward us, the way a curtain stirs when someone in the next room leans against it. Toward the mark. Toward me. It hung there, ten feet away and now nine and a half, silent, deliberate, and I understood with a clarity that went through me like cold water that this was not weather, had never been weather, and that whatever held it in place had just felt me standing here the way you feel a splinter — small, foreign, and exactly where you left it.

The burning in my arm pulsed again. And underneath the pain, faint as a pulse heard through a wall, I felt something else. Not words. Not yet. A pressure, a leaning, a *wanting* — the sensation of a mouth about to open.

Veyra was trying to speak. Here, at the edge, where the veil was thinnest — she was spending thirty years of silence to say something, and the cost of it was written in fire down my arm.

"Get her pack," Odile said to Bren, crisp as an officer. "Water. And back her away from the line — slowly — no, wait." She hesitated, eyes flicking from my glowing arm to the stirring fog and back, weighing something fast and terrible. "No. Don't move her. If it wants her to hear something, I'd very much like to know what happens when it says it."

"That's my god you're talking about."

"That's a locked door talking through your arm," Odile said, "and I have spent eleven years looking for doors. Humor me."

The fog leaned closer. Eight feet. Seven. The cold coming off it wasn't winter-cold; it was older than winter, a cold like the inside of the earth, and my breath froze the moment it left me. The mark blazed. Somewhere beneath the pain, at the very bottom of the silence, something drew breath.

And Veyra — my god, my faithful, silent god, whose altar I had tended through thirty years of nothing — spoke her first word into my bones:

*Find.*

* * *

I don't know how long I knelt there. The sun cleared the ridge behind us and threw our shadows long and blue across the snow, three shadows and the wall, and the wall drank the light without giving any back. When the burning finally faded to warmth and the warmth to ache, Odile helped me up, and Bren put his cloak around my shoulders without a word, and for a while none of us said anything at all, because the valley behind us looked exactly the way it had always looked, and that was somehow the worst discovery of the day.

"We're going back down," Bren said at last. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't a suggestion. It was a man reasserting the existence of solid ground.

"Yes," I said. My voice came out scraped. "But not home. Not yet." I flexed my arm. Under the sleeve, the mark had gone cold again — faithful, silent — but I could still feel where it had burned, the way you feel a brand long after the iron lifts. One word, and it had cost her thirty years of hoarded voice to spend it. *Find.* Find what? Find whom? Find the way through?

Odile was already repacking her instruments, quick and precise, but I caught her watching the fog wall in the brass reflection of her compass lid, the way you watch a stranger on a night road.

"One word," she mused. "Thirty years of silence, and when she finally spends herself, it's one word. That's not desperation, Yara. That's *rationing*. She's saving her voice for something." Odile snapped the case shut and looked up at me, and behind the wit and the appetite there was something almost gentle. "Whatever your god is afraid of, I think it's already on its way. And I think we just heard the first minute of it."

Behind us, at our backs the entire climb down, the fog wall stood in the saddle — still, patient, and closer than it had been yesterday.

I checked. I'm a mercenary. I check.
