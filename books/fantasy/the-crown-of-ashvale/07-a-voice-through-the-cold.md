## 7. A Voice Through the Cold

The fog at the rim of Ashvale did not move.

I want to be clear about that, because everything else that day moved — the wind, the birds wheeling back from the wall of white like they'd struck glass, Odile's compass needle spinning in its brass housing like a drunk trying to find north in a tavern with no floor. Everything moved except the fog. It stood. It had the posture of a thing on duty.

"Three days," Bren said behind me. He'd said it twice already, and I'd ignored him both times, so naturally he said it again. "We've been out here three days, Yara. The horses are eating their own tack. I've seen men do that, but never horses."

"The horses are fine."

"The horses are *worried*." He crouched beside our little fire, which we'd built in a hollow of stone because there was no wood out here worth burning, only scrub and the black ribs of trees that had given up growing toward the fog centuries ago. "You don't sleep. You haven't slept since the shrine. You stand at that wall like it owes you money."

"It does," I said. "Everything does."

Odile looked up from her maps. She'd spread them across a flat rock weighted with four stones, and she'd been staring at them for an hour the way you stare at a letter that says one thing and means another. "My compass has never done this," she said. Not to me, exactly. To the fog. To the world in general. "I have surveyed coastlines that appear on no chart. I have mapped cities that empires pretend were never built. My compass has always found north eventually. Even lying north. Even *invented* north." She turned the instrument over in her long fingers. "Here it just spins. As if north itself is embarrassed to be associated with this place."

"That's Ashvale for you," I said. "Even the directions are ashamed."

Bren snorted. It was the kind of laugh you make when the alternative is screaming, and I recognized it because I'd been making it for three days myself.

Because here's what I hadn't told them. Here's what I'd been carrying under my ribs like a smuggled knife since the dying pilgrim spoke my name through lips that weren't his: the god-mark on my wrist had been burning since we reached the rim. Not the old ache, not the dull cold throb of thirty years of silence. Burning. A wire pulled taut under my skin, humming a note just below hearing, and every hour it hummed louder, and every hour I pretended otherwise, because I am forty-one years old and I have learned that when something inside you starts singing, the wise mercenary checks her blade first and her faith second.

"You should tell her," Bren said quietly, nodding at Odile. "Whatever you're not telling us."

"I'm not telling you lots of things. I'm a mercenary. Retention of information is most of the job."

"Yara."

I looked at the fog instead of him. Up close, the wall wasn't really white. It was the color of nothing — of a page before ink, of a room before candles, of all the things Veyra had never said to me arranged into a standing column about thirty feet high and, as far as anyone knew, infinitely deep. Birds hit it and turned. Rain fell into it and didn't come out the other side. Once, years ago, a company man named Aldric had thrown a rock at it on a dare, and the rock had come back. Nobody talked about that much.

"I dreamed of her again last night," I said finally. Because Bren was right, and because if I didn't say it aloud it would start saying itself. "Veyra. In the dream she was standing where the altar used to be, except the altar was whole, and she was — " I stopped. How do you describe your god's face after thirty years of imagining it? "She was crying. Gods aren't supposed to cry. That's sort of the entire business model."

Odile set down her compass. "And the mark?"

"Burns."

"Since when?"

"Since the pilgrim." Since a stranger died in a cold shrine with his eyes rolled white and his mouth full of my name, and I knelt there holding his hand while something vast and guilty poured through him like water through a cracked jug. "Since she started talking."

*Find the Door.* That's what the dead man had said, in a voice layered like river ice — thin over deep water. *Before the winter finishes falling. Please. Before it finishes falling.*

I hadn't told them that part either. The *please*. Gods don't say please. Gods command, gods promise, gods take their tithe and their incense and their fear. They do not beg through the mouths of dying strangers in shrines where the candles haven't lit in a generation.

"So we're agreed," Odile said, in the brisk tone of a woman reorganizing reality to fit her maps. "Your god is awake. Your god is frightened. And whatever she's frightened of, it's coming with the winter." She tapped the fog wall with one finger, and I swear the fog leaned away from her touch, just slightly, like a dog that knows it's been caught. "Then we're in the right place. This isn't weather, Captain Marchetti. Weather doesn't have edges."

* * *

It came at dusk, which is when everything in my life comes, because apparently even my god respects dramatic convention.

I was on watch. Bren was asleep sitting up, which is a skill he claims is military discipline and which I claim is laziness perfected. Odile was still working by lamplight, drawing the rim of Ashvale over and over, each attempt angrier than the last, as though she could shame the geography into admitting it existed.

The mark on my wrist went from burning to *singing*.

I was on my feet before I knew it, sword half-drawn, scanning the treeline for riders, for wolves, for anything with teeth. But the dark between the trees stayed empty, and the fire burned steady, and the only thing that had changed was the fog.

It had come closer.

Not crept — I would have heard creeping; the ground here was gravel and it announced everything. No, the wall had simply *arrived*, ten feet nearer than it had been at noon, close enough now that I could see it wasn't fog at all. Fog drifts. This hung in shapes. Faces, almost, if you squinted — the way clouds almost make faces, the way memory almost makes sense. And it was warm. I could feel warmth rolling off it like off a bread oven, which was wrong, so deeply wrong, because the rim of Ashvale had been cold for as long as there had been a rim, and the long winter was supposed to come *from* out there, not live inside the wall like a hearth-fire waiting to be lit.

"Yara." Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the fog and the fire and the marrow of my own bones, and it was hoarse, and it was small, and it was hers.

Thirty years. Thirty years of cold altars and unanswered prayers and a company of mercenaries keeping faith with a silent god because someone had to, because tradition is just stubbornness that outlived its reasons, because I had made a child's bargain with the dark — *if you ever speak, I'll listen, I'll listen better than anyone* — and I had kept my end for three decades without knowing if there was anyone on the other line.

And now she spoke, and the first thing I felt wasn't joy.

It was anger. White and total and shocking, rising up my throat like floodwater, and I stood there in the freezing dark shaking with rage at a wall of fog because the woman who raised me on stories of Veyra's mercy, the priestess who taught me my first prayer, the god I had defended in a hundred tavern arguments against drunks and cynics and my own captain — she had been *there the whole time*. Listening. Choosing not to answer. While people prayed themselves hollow. While children died asking. While I —

"Yara." A pause, and in the pause I heard it: guilt. Thick as tallow. "I know. I know what you're feeling. I felt it too, for a very long time, toward those who — " The voice frayed. Reassembled. "There isn't time. Winter is early this year. It will finish falling, and when it finishes, the Door will be buried past finding, and everyone in this valley will sleep until the world forgets there was ever anyone here to wake."

"Then talk fast," I said, and my voice came out flat and hard, a mercenary's voice, and I was grateful for it. "Because I've got questions, and I've had thirty years to draft them."

"*Yara — *"

"No. You don't get to do the gentle voice. You don't get to be sorry yet. Sorry is for afterward." I stepped closer to the fog, close enough that its warmth touched my face like breath. "Ashvale. What is it? The elders say the valley was founded by refugees fleeing the Godswars. The songs say Veyra led them here and blessed the ground. Every child learns it kneeling. So tell me, lady of silence — why does a blessed refuge need walls that birds won't cross?"

The fog shuddered. All of it, top to bottom, like a held breath released.

"Because it isn't a refuge," she said.

Somewhere behind me, Odile's lamp guttered. I didn't turn around. You don't turn around when the truth finally shows its face; you hold its gaze or it takes the knife out of your hand.

"Ashvale was a country," Veyra said. "Before it was a valley. Before the fog. We called it Ashvale then too — ash for the fields burned in the first wars, vale for the mourning. It had cities, Yara. Harbors. Kings who were merely kings, and priests who were merely liars, and a hundred thousand people who sang the songs you sing now, except theirs were new and theirs were true." The voice dropped, and the warmth of the fog turned feverish. "When the Godswars ended, the surviving gods held a reckoning. There had been a crime. At the founding of the world. Something done that could not be undone and could not be *witnessed*, and the ones who had witnessed it — "

She stopped.

"The people of Ashvale," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "The witnesses."

"They saw what was done at the world's founding. And the others wanted them silenced. Permanently. All of them. Every soul who remembered." A sound came out of the fog that I took a moment to recognize as a laugh, because it was the worst laugh I'd ever heard, dry as old bone. "And I — I had loved them. Do you understand? Out of all the mortal peoples, I had loved *them*, stubborn and singing and unimpressed by divinity. So I offered a compromise. Not death. Erasure. Hide the country. Fold it out of the world. Let the other gods wipe it from every map, every memory outside the veil — let mothers in distant kingdoms forget children who marched to Ashvale and never returned. And in exchange, the people lived. Hidden, but alive."

"And the cost," I said. I already knew. Oh, I already knew, and knowing didn't stop the words from tasting like ash. "The cost was your voice."

"The hiding required a keeper. Someone to hold the veil from inside, to pour their own self into the walls of it, year upon year, century upon century. A god's silence, freely given, is the strongest seal there is." The fog pressed closer, warm and terrible. "I told them I would do it. I told them I would give up my voice, my presence, my answers — everything but the barest thread of watching. And I told myself it was mercy. I told myself thirty years was nothing, to a god. I told myself your prayers were enough, that being *heard* was enough, even unheard — "

"It wasn't," I said.

"No," she said simply. "It wasn't."

Behind me I heard Bren sit up. Heard Odile whisper something in a language I didn't know, probably a curse, possibly a prayer, possibly a map notation. I didn't turn. Turning would mean letting my face do what it wanted to do, and my face wanted to do something I wasn't ready to survive in front of witnesses.

"My whole life," I said. "Every cold candle. Every unanswered funeral rite. Old Marta praying her son home from the border skirmishes, and he came home in a box, and she prayed anyway, and you were *right there*, behind the wall, listening, choosing — " My voice cracked and I hated it and I kept going, because some things, once started, have to finish. "We built everything on you. The company's oath. The shrine calendar. My *name*, Veyra. My mother named me for one of your feast days. And it was all — it was the wallpaper of a prison. We decorated our cage and called it grace."

"Yes."

"Just — yes? That's it?"

"What would you have me say?" For the first time, the god sounded tired. Truly tired, in the way only immortals can manage, the fatigue of geological ages compressed into a whisper. "That I regret it? I regret it the way a mother regrets the scar she left carrying her child from a fire. I would do it again. That is the worst part, Yara. Given the same choice, I would seal the veil and swallow my voice and let you hate me, because the alternative was a hundred thousand graves and the others would have dug them *singing*."

The anger in me hit a wall of its own. Because that was the unforgivable thing, wasn't it — not that she'd lied, but that she was right. That I'd have done the same. That somewhere under the fury, the part of me that had loved her since I was six years old was already, traitorously, starting to understand.

"The winter," I said instead. Business. Business was safe. "What does the winter have to do with any of this?"

"The veil was meant to be temporary. A generation, perhaps two, until the others' attention moved on and I could negotiate a return. But gods are not good at forgetting, and worse at forgiving. The loud ones — Solenne of the answered prayers, the Twins, the rest of that gilded chorus — they never intended to let Ashvale back into the world. The erasure was supposed to become permanent. And now they've woken, and they've noticed the seal weakening, and they've begun to *help* it along." The fog dimmed, if lightlessness could dim. "The long winter is not weather, Yara. It is a lid. When it finishes falling, the valley freezes past thawing, the people sleep past waking, and in a few centuries even the songs will be gone, and Ashvale will be nothing — not hidden, not punished. Just *gone*. A hole in the world where a country used to be."

"And the Door?"

A pause. Long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer.

"There is a way out of the veil. One. Made at the sealing, in case — in case mercy changed its mind. It stands where the chapel stands, beneath the chapel, in the place where the walls remember. It needs names to open. True names, the names of the erased country, and those were taken from us and locked away beyond the fog, in the archive of the loud gods." The voice dropped to something raw. "Find it. Open it. Lead them out. Before the winter finishes falling, Yara, I am begging you, and I have not begged since the world was young — "

"Why me?"

"Because you kept faith with silence for thirty years. Because you know what it costs to be unheard and you chose to stay anyway. Because — " and here the great voice of my god, the voice I'd imagined leading armies and judging kings, went small as a girl's — "because you're the only one left who prays to me, Yara. Everyone else stopped hoping. You never did. Even angry, even now. I can feel it. It burns."

I looked down at my wrist, at the mark searing under the skin, and understood at last that the burning had never been pain.

It had been a hand, reaching through thirty years of wall, holding on.

"Odile," I said, without turning around. "How quickly can you forge imperial surveyor credentials?"

A beat of silence. Then, in a tone of profound professional offense: "*Forge?* Captain, I'll have you know my credentials are entirely genuine. The empire just doesn't know it granted them yet."

"Bren?"

He was already on his feet, checking his blade, wearing the expression he wore before battles he expected to lose. "I've got a bad feeling," he said. "Which historically means we're about to do something historic."

Behind us, the fog wall breathed once, warm and vast and grieving, and my god's voice faded with it like a tide going out:

"Hurry. The loud ones have hunters in the snow. And they wear the faces of answered prayers."

* * *

We broke camp in the dark, because I couldn't sleep and neither, it turned out, could anyone else, and because the mark on my wrist had begun pulling — actually pulling, a gentle insistent tug toward the valley's heart, toward the buried chapel Sefa's grandmother's songs used to whisper about.

Odile walked beside me, map case strapped tight, compass dead in her pocket. After a mile she said, "You're going to forgive her."

"I'm going to *find a door*."

"Mm." She watched the road ahead, where the first flakes of the wrong winter were beginning to fall — fat, slow, warm-touched flakes, snow with a heartbeat in it. "For what it's worth, my family was erased too. Different empire, same arithmetic. I've spent my life drawing places that powerful people insisted didn't exist." She smiled, thin and sharp as a cartographer's blade. "So when we find this archive of theirs, Captain, I'd consider it a personal favor if we burned it down thoroughly."

Above us, the wrong snow thickened, and somewhere far off in the white, something began to sing in voices we almost recognized.

We walked faster.
