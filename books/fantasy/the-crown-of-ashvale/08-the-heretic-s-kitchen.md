## 8. The Heretic's Kitchen

I have slept in a great many questionable places in my career as a professional liar with a compass. A smuggler's hold that smelled of pickled eel. A border inn where the mattress was stuffed with straw and something that moved. Once, memorably, a ditch.

None of them prepared me for Sefa's kitchen, which was the warmest room I had ever entered and the most illegal.

The house sat low against the hillside above the mill road, half-swallowed by ivy the color of old copper, with a chimney that smoked thin and sideways as though it had learned to be discreet. The door was opened before we knocked — by an old woman who looked us over the way a fishmonger looks over a catch, deciding which of us was fresh enough to sell.

"You'll be the ones the fog spat back," she said. "Come in before someone sees you standing there looking like a map nobody wants."

Yara opened her mouth — to explain, presumably, or to threaten, she has a gift for both — and Sefa waved her silent with a hand like a bundle of kindling.

"I know why you're here. Everyone who comes here is here for the same reason, even when they don't know it yet." She stepped aside. "Wipe your boots. The floor's older than your gods and I won't have you tracking the century across it."

* * *

The kitchen was a cathedral that had decided, at some point in its long life, to stop pretending.

Herbs hung from every beam in braided bundles — some I recognized from imperial apothecaries, some I did not recognize at all and strongly suspected no licensed apothecary would admit to knowing. Dried flowers I couldn't name were pressed between the panes of the window glass, so that the late afternoon light came through them stained red and gold, like the room was remembering its own sunset. And on the mantelpiece, where any respectable household in Ashvale kept a little iron figure of Veyra the Silent — cold, cheap, mass-produced piety — Sefa kept instead a row of small clay lamps shaped like sleeping birds, each one painted with a different mark.

Marks I had seen before. In my own work. On maps so old the empires that drew them had rotted off the page.

"Where did you get those?" I asked, before my manners caught up with me.

Sefa followed my eyes to the mantel and her face did something complicated — pleasure crossed with grief crossed with the expression of a woman deciding how much truth a stranger could carry without straining.

"My grandmother made them," she said. "And hers made them before her. You'd know that, wouldn't you, cartographer girl? You've got ink under your nails from three different countries." She squinted at me. "Two of them don't exist anymore. Or never did. Depends which end of the eraser you're standing on."

Bren, who had spent the entire walk from the rim wearing the expression of a man being slowly digested by his own conscience, set down our packs and asked the practical question: "How did you know we were coming?"

"The birds told me," said Sefa, entirely seriously, and put a kettle on.

I want to note, for the record, that I am a woman of rigorous method. I triangulate. I verify. I once refused a commission because the client's measurements were off by a quarter-league and I found it morally offensive. But there was something about the way this small round old woman moved through her impossible kitchen — unhurried, certain, humming — that made rigor feel like a luxury for people whose world had edges.

* * *

She fed us first. Of course she fed us first; I suspect she'd have fed us first if the pale riders themselves had been scratching at the door. There was barley soup with roots I couldn't identify, dark bread with seeds that popped on the tongue, honey that tasted faintly of smoke. Yara ate the way soldiers eat — fast, efficient, watching the door between spoonfuls. Bren ate the way guilty men eat, mechanically, staring into his bowl as though the answers to chapter five through nine of his personal crisis might be written at the bottom.

I ate the way I always eat in strange houses: slowly, cataloguing everything, because a meal tells you more about a place than a census ever could. The bread was leavened with a starter older than the hearth. The salt came from somewhere inland — there was no sea-salt bite to it. The honey-smoke taste meant they burned a particular wood near the hives, and that wood didn't grow in Ashvale. It grew, according to three dead cartographers and one very drunk surveyor I once bought drinks for, in a country called Vessa, two hundred leagues east of anywhere Ashvale had ever officially been.

Which meant either Sefa traded with ghosts, or her family had carried that recipe out of a place that no longer appeared on charts.

"You're doing the thing," Yara murmured beside me.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you eat soup like it's a crime scene."

"It *is* a crime scene," I said. "Everything here is evidence. That's rather the point of this whole country, isn't it?"

Across the table, Sefa snorted into her cup. "I like her," she told Yara. "You should keep her."

"We haven't discussed keeping anyone," Yara said, in the tone of a woman who had already, privately, discussed keeping everyone and lost the argument with herself.

* * *

It was after the dishes were done — Bren washing, because he's the sort of man who cannot sit still in someone else's kitchen while a woman older than his grandmother stands — that Sefa began to sing.

She didn't announce it. She was banking the fire, kneeling at the hearth with her back to us, and the song just came out of her the way warmth comes off stone: low, unhurried, in a language that wasn't the valley dialect. The vowels were rounder. Older. And underneath the melody ran a counting rhythm, one-two-three-four-five, over and over, like a child's game or a marching cadence or—

"A ledger," I said aloud, before I could stop myself. "It's a ledger. She's singing numbers."

Sefa turned her head and looked at me over her shoulder, firelight catching the deep lines of her face. "Numbers of what?"

"Houses. Fields. Ships." My heart had started doing something unprofessional. "Censuses get set to rhythm so clerks can memorize them. It's an old trick — the Empire used it for grain tallies before paper was cheap. If your song counts *anything*, then somewhere behind it is a list of—"

"Names," Sefa said.

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, wind worried the eaves.

"The song has names in it," she went on, rising stiffly, brushing ash from her skirts. "Forty-one verses and every verse is a family. The Millward line. The Harrow-daughters. The folk of the Nine Terraces, whoever they were — that verse even my grandmother only half remembered. We sang it at births and at burials and at threshing-time, and none of us knew what we were singing. That's the trick of it, see." She tapped her temple. "They took the country out of the books and out of the stones and out of the priests' mouths. But you can't take a song out of a people's blood. They buried Ashvale so deep even the gravediggers forgot the grave. But the songs stayed. Songs are patient."

Yara had gone very still. I've watched her sit motionless through ambushes and blizzards and one memorable bar fight, but this was a different stillness — the stillness of a woman listening to a sound she's been hearing all her life and finally understanding what it was.

"My mother hummed that," she said quietly. "She didn't know the words. Just the tune. She said her mother said it was a lullaby."

"It is a lullaby," Sefa said. "It's also a muster roll. It's also a grave marker. Old things don't bother being only one thing." She lowered herself into her chair by the fire and looked at the three of us — the soldier, the sinner, and the surveyor — with an expression I can only describe as inventory. "So. The god spoke to you at the rim. I can see it sitting on you like weather. Ask your questions, girl. I'm old; I fall asleep early."

* * *

What followed was the strangest interrogation I have ever participated in, conducted mostly by Yara, punctuated by Bren asking careful logistical questions and me interrupting whenever Sefa said something that mapped onto something I'd read in a forbidden archive, which was often enough that eventually she threw a crust of bread at me.

Yes, there had been rites, once — hearth-rites, threshold-rites, songs sung backwards at midwinter to "call the year home." Yes, they were banned; the elders' council had outlawed them within living memory, gently, with apologies, the way you outlaw something everyone secretly still does. No, Sefa had never stopped. "A ban is just a memory with a stick," she said. "I kept the memories and hid the sticks."

She showed us, eventually. That was the part that undid me.

She pulled back the rag rug before the hearth — the rug I had been standing on all evening — and beneath it was a trapdoor, and beneath the trapdoor was not a cellar but a shallow cavity lined with waxcloth, and in it lay the banned country itself: carved tokens for each hearth-god of old Ashvale, their names scratched in a script I recognized from exactly one fragment in the Imperial Restricted Collection, the one catalogued as *provenance unknown*. Bundles of dried herbs for rites of naming. A child's toy — a wooden ox with wheels — painted with symbols that matched the sleeping-bird lamps on her mantel.

"They come to search, sometimes," Sefa said, replacing the boards, tamping down the rug with her foot. "The council men, when the elders get nervous. They find a kitchen. A clean kitchen, with a good rug." She smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who has outlived several institutions and expects to outlive several more. "The gods took everything else. They left us our floors. Arrogance, that is. They never think to look down."

"Why keep it?" Bren asked. His voice was rough. "If remembering is dangerous — and you clearly know it is — why keep all this?"

Sefa looked at him for a long moment.

"Because forgetting is worse than dying," she said. "Dying, somebody mourns you. Forgetting, nobody even knows there's a hole." She poked the fire up. "My husband forgot. Not by choice — after our daughter died, he prayed and prayed at that cold little altar for the pain to lift, and one morning he woke up easy. Couldn't tell you our daughter's name. Smiled at me like a stranger and asked when I'd moved in." Her voice didn't waver, which somehow made it worse. "That's what the silence does when it sits in a person too long. So yes, boy. I remember. On purpose. Even the parts that hurt. *Especially* those."

Nobody said anything for a while. The fire talked to itself. Yara reached over — I saw her do it, almost surprised at herself — and put her hand briefly on the old woman's knee. Sefa covered it with her own, patted twice, and returned to the fire as if nothing had happened, which was, I suspect, exactly how she preferred mercy to be delivered: quickly, without commentary, like good stitches.

* * *

Later — much later, with Bren asleep in the loft and Yara taking first watch at the window out of pure habit despite Sefa's insistence that no rider would come tonight — the old woman caught me alone by the fire.

"You knew the counting-song's shape before I finished the first verse," she said. "And you knew my grandmother's script. And when I mentioned the Nine Terraces, your hand went to your satchel like it was bleeding." She poured two cups of something amber and medicinal and pushed one at me. "So talk, cartographer. Why does a woman from outside care whether Ashvale remembers itself?"

I considered lying to her. Genuinely considered it; it's my native language and I speak it fluently. But there's a category of person before whom lying feels less like strategy and more like blasphemy, and apparently I'd wandered into her kitchen and found one.

"Because I've spent eleven years drawing maps of places that don't exist," I said. "Places the charts refuse. Islands that appear in sailors' logs and vanish from atlases. Roads that lead to towns no tax collector has ever visited. Every empire I've worked for calls them errors. I started collecting the errors." I turned my cup. "Do you know what I found, when I laid them all side by side? The errors aren't scattered. They cluster. Around gaps. As if someone tore pages out of the world's atlas and the remaining pages curl toward the tear."

"And Ashvale is a tear."

"Ashvale is *the* tear. The largest one. The one all the others bend around." I looked up at her. "I came here because your valley shouldn't exist, and I needed to know who had done the erasing, and whether it could be undone. I expected a curse. A war. Some emperor's dirty secret." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I did not expect the gods themselves to be holding the erasers. And I certainly didn't expect to end up drinking elderberry brandy with a heretic who keeps a nation under her doormat."

Sefa drank, considering.

"You remind me of my granddaughter," she said at last. "She died before she could walk, gods forgive the saying. Too curious for her own safety, my Tam used to say of her, as if curiosity were a disease and not the only cure." She set down her cup. "Listen to me now, girl, because I'm going to say the thing I say to everyone who carries true memory, and I only ever say it once."

The firelight moved on her face. Outside, the winter wind had begun to sing against the shutters in a register I was beginning to understand this country heard differently than I did.

"The loud ones are awake now. You felt them at the rim — the answered prayers, the wrong miracles, all that noise like bells in a burning town. They are hunting, and here is the thing your soldier and your mercenary may not fully grasp yet, because they were born inside the veil and think the fog is weather." She leaned forward. "*Remembering is how they find you.* Memory has a weight to it, a heat, like a body under blankets in snow. Every true name spoken aloud, every old rite performed, every verse of that song sung past the fourth one — it shines. To them, it shines like a signal fire. That's why the elders banned the rites, child, and it wasn't only cowardice. A quiet people is a hidden people. My kitchen has stayed safe for sixty years because I remember in whispers."

"But we can't whisper forever," I said. "The winter's coming down wrong. Whatever they're planning—"

"No," she agreed. "We can't. Which means soon, very soon, someone will have to stand up in this valley and sing the forty-one verses at the top of their lungs, and let every pale horror in creation come running." She smiled again, and this time there was nothing comfortable in it at all. "When that day comes, cartographer, I hope you've finished your map. Because the moment we start truly remembering — all of us, loudly, everywhere — this kitchen stops being a hiding place and becomes a target. And so does everyone who's slept under my roof."

She rose, joints crackling like the fire, and paused at the doorway to the back room.

"Sleep well," said Sefa. "You've got maybe three days of it left."

* * *

I did sleep, eventually — the brandy helped — and woke before dawn to a sound I couldn't immediately place: voices, low and rhythmic, from the main room.

Yara was awake. Of course she was. She was sitting cross-legged by the hearth with the rug rolled back and the trapdoor open, the clay bird-lamps arranged in front of her, and she was singing. Badly. Haltingly, verse by verse, while Sefa corrected her the way a fencing master corrects a grip — patiently, relentlessly, over and over.

Counting houses. Counting fields. Counting names.

Yara Montrose, mercenary of a silent god, learning the muster roll of a dead country one family at a time, her voice cracking on the fifth verse and steadying by the ninth.

I sat down beside her without a word, listened until I could match the melody, and joined in on the tenth.

Above us, faint through the rafters, the first pale light of a winter morning touched the shutters. And far off down the mill road — so distant I told myself I'd imagined it, though neither Yara nor Sefa stopped singing — a bell began to ring.
