## 9. Hunters in White

I've been in the mercenary trade for twenty-two years, and I have a rule about omens: ignore them until they cost you money. Thunder on a clear day? Bad for morale, good for drinking. A crow following you for three days? Crows are stupid and hungry, same as everyone.

But when Sefa stopped singing mid-verse and went to her window with a face like a woman reading a death warrant, I felt my rule bend.

"What is it?" Yara asked from the hearth, where she'd been teaching Odile's hands how to hold a whetstone without looking like a butcher's apprentice.

"The song came back," Sefa said. "It shouldn't. I buried it under the floorboards so it couldn't be heard."

Odile looked up from the stone. "Songs can't be heard from underground?"

"Mine can." Sefa didn't turn around. "That was rather the point of burying it where I did. Under my own floor, in my own house, wrapped in enough of my own silence that anything listening would have to already know exactly where to look." She finally turned, and her eyes were dry and very old. "Someone just knew where to look."

I want to say I understood immediately what she meant. I want to tell you I felt the cold coming down off the rim before anyone else, that I smelled the wrongness on the wind like an old campaigner should. The truth is dumber: I was thinking about soup. We had turnips, we had a ham bone, and I'd been promised soup if I split the wood, which I had, badly, because splitting wood is not the same skill as breaking skulls no matter what the recruiting posters imply.

The knock came while the soup was still heating.

Nobody knocks in Ashvale. That's the thing people outside never understand about us — or about what we were, before all this — nobody knocks because there's nowhere worth knocking from. You walk into a friend's house, or you shout from the road, or you mind your own business. A knock at Sefa's door at dusk was as alien as snow falling upward.

Yara put down the whetstone. Odile put down the map she'd been annotating with little furious pencil strokes. Sefa blew out the candle nearest the window, which told me everything, because you only do that when you don't want to be seen from outside, and Sefa had spent sixty years making sure nothing outside could see her.

I went to the door because that's my job. Captain of the Grey Ledger company, first through doors, last out of fires. I put my eye to the gap in the shutters.

There were three of them on horseback in the lane, and I will describe them as honestly as I can, though honesty has been getting more expensive by the day.

They wore white. Not armor-white, not linen-white — white the way fog is white, white the way the wall at the valley's rim is white, and I recognized it now, that particular shade of nothing. Their horses stood too still. Horses shift, stamp, toss their heads; these three stood like furniture that had been taught to breathe.

And their faces.

Here is where I lose people, so let me go slowly. They had faces. Good faces. Familiar faces. The one in front had the face of old Tam Wicker, who'd died two winters back, except rendered better than Tam ever managed alive — every line smoothed, every blemish forgiven, a portrait of Tam as someone's loving memory of him. The second wore the face of a girl I didn't know, maybe sixteen, with a smile so kind it made my skin crawl. The third —

The third wore the face of a prayer answered.

I don't know another way to say it. You know those small miracles that started happening all over the valley, week six, week seven? The well in Harrow's Ford running sweet again. The fevered child in Lowmark waking up whole. The lame miller walking. People had come back from their shrines glowing, saying *the gods hear us again*, and I'd watched hope spread through this country like fire through dry grass, and I'd hated every minute of it, because I'd read the muster rolls in the archive and I knew what the gods had done here, even if I hadn't yet worked up the nerve to tell Yara.

The third rider had the face of that child from Lowmark. Grown older, serene, lit from within. A miracle wearing its own answer like a mask.

"Bren," Yara said behind me, low. "How many?"

"Three."

"Weapons?"

"That's the strange part. No."

"They're confident," Odile murmured. She'd come to stand at my shoulder, and her breath fogged the shutter crack. "Or they don't need them."

Sefa had moved to the hearth. She wasn't looking at the door. She was looking at the floorboards, at the place beneath them where she kept the banned rites — the dried herbs, the hand-copied litanies, the little clay figures of gods whose names hadn't been spoken aloud in Ashvale since before my grandmother was born. Sixty years of careful, criminal devotion, packed into a box the size of a bread loaf.

"No," Yara said, seeing it too. "No, Sefa, we fight—"

"You'll do nothing of the sort." Sefa's voice was steady in a way I've only ever heard from women who've already done their crying, decades ago, in private, where it wouldn't feed anybody. "You three are the ones carrying true memory. The songs, the names, the maps. I'm carrying trinkets. There's a difference, and it's measured in whether the world gets to keep existing."

"They'll kill you."

"They might." She smiled, and it was Tam Wicker's smile, almost — the real kind, worn at the edges. "Child, I'm seventy-three years old. I've been waiting for them since I was younger than you. The only tragedy would be dying before I finished my soup, and mercifully, it smells nearly ready."

* * *

Let me tell you what happened at the door, because I got it mostly right and I want credit for the parts I got right, since I'm about to confess the parts I got wrong.

I opened it. Standing in a doorway with your hand near your axe and your weight on your back foot is the oldest negotiation technique in the world, and I've used it against tax collectors, bandit kings, and one memorable bear. The lead rider — Tam-that-wasn't-Tam — looked at me with dead Tam's warm brown eyes, and spoke.

Its voice was every voice at once, the way a choir is one sound. "We seek the rememberers."

"Never heard of them," I said. "Nice horses."

"You carry the ledger-names." It tilted its head, and the gesture was wrong, fluid, like water deciding to be curious. "Muster rolls. A country that fell. You read them by candlelight in a room that smelled of dust and dishonesty."

So they knew about the archive. About me. My stomach did something professional soldiers train it not to do, which is feel things.

"I read a lot," I said. "Mostly debt collection notices. Very dull stuff. You'd hate it."

Behind it, the child-faced one smiled wider. Too wide. Teeth like a hymn. And I heard — I swear on the cold altar of Veyra, whom I'd never prayed to and was starting to reconsider — I heard singing underneath its silence. The same song Sefa kept under her floorboards. Being played back.

They weren't hunting the song. They were *wearing* it, the way a hunter wears a doe-hide.

"Sefa," Yara said quietly, from behind me, "get away from the hearth."

Too late for that. Sefa had already pulled the box from under the floor, already set it on the coals, and the old papers caught with a smell like burnt sugar and myrrh, and she fed it piece by piece, unhurried, a woman banking her fire for the last time. The clay figures she cracked one by one between her palms and dropped into the flames.

"Sixty years," she said to the fire, conversationally, while three impossible riders watched her through the doorway with faces borrowed from the beloved dead. "Sixty years keeping the rites alive in a kitchen. Kneading prayers into bread dough because paper gets found. Teaching children the songs as lullabies so they'd never know they were learning heresy. And do you know what I learned, in sixty years?"

None of us asked. The riders didn't either, but I think they wanted to.

"That remembering isn't a treasure," she said. "It's a debt. Somebody has to hold it, and pay the interest, until the ones who owe it come home." She looked over her shoulder at Yara, and Odile, and me, and her eyes were bright as banked coals. "You're the payment arriving late. Go and make it count."

Then she stepped outside.

I moved to stop her — I'll always believe I would have, if Yara hadn't caught my arm, and if some animal part of me hadn't understood that stopping her meant dying beside her for no purchase whatsoever — and Sefa walked down her own garden path toward the pale riders like a hostess greeting late guests.

The lead rider dismounted. It took Tam's face and bent it into something like reverence, and the choir-voice said words I couldn't fully parse, syllables that slid off the ear, but I caught the shape of it: *you kept the flame; the flame is recalled.*

"I know," Sefa said. "I've known since the fall. That's rather why I kept it burning." She reached up — tiny old woman, reaching up — and touched its face. "You wear him kindly. Thank you for that. He deserved kindness."

And then the white took her. Not blood, not violence — the riders simply closed around her like fog closing around a lamp, and for one moment I saw her silhouette inside the brightness, standing straight, arms folded, entirely unafraid, and then there was no silhouette, and the fog thinned, and the lane was empty except for three horses that turned in unison and walked away without disturbing the dust.

* * *

We burned Sefa properly the next morning, or as properly as you can burn a woman whose body declined to be left behind. There was ash on her kitchen floor shaped, briefly, like a kneeling figure. Odile swept it into an urn before the wind could take it, and neither Yara nor I said anything about the fact that the ash was warm, hours later, and smelled faintly of her soup.

I dug the grave because digging is what I'm for. Yara stood watch, though against what, none of us could say — the riders had come and gone like weather. Odile paced the garden, cataloguing.

"They took her memory with them," she said. "That's what that was. Not an execution. A repossession."

"Don't," Yara said.

"It matters. If they collect the rememberers instead of killing them, then Sefa might still exist somewhere — in their archive, in their—" 

"*Don't.*" And Yara's voice cracked on it, and I set down the shovel, because I've buried enough friends to know when the person standing next to you needs the work more than the words.

Here's what I got wrong at the door, since I promised honesty: I thought we had time. I thought the riders were a probe, a first finger testing a wound, and that we'd have weeks — a season, at least — to move the refugees, fortify the chapel, argue our councils. Twenty-two years of soldiering teaches you that enemies arrive on schedules. Armies march. Supply lines creak. Even assassins need sleep.

These didn't. These were prayers with legs, and prayers travel at the speed of belief, and half the valley believed in the loud gods again.

By noon the next day, the signs were everywhere. A shrine in Millbrook gutted, its keeper gone, white ash on the threshold. Old Harl the ferryman missing, his boat adrift. And in the market at Crossroads, a white-robed figure standing perfectly still amid the stalls, wearing the face of a woman everyone swore they'd seen healed at the spring shrine — smiling at shoppers, being smiled at, collecting love the way a net collects fish, while underneath the smile, faint as a tune through a wall, ran the stolen song.

"They're mapping us," Odile said that night, back at Sefa's kitchen, which felt obscene and necessary both, because where else would we go. She had her maps spread across the table where Sefa used to knead prayers into dough, and she was marking sightings with pins. "Every place they appear is a place where someone remembered something true. Millbrook — the keeper sang the planting hymns. The ferryman — Harl used to hum the old crossing-song. They're not searching randomly. They're triangulating." She looked up, and for once the cartographer's confidence had a crack in it. "And every sighting is closer to the chapel. Closer to the song under the floorboards. Except—" her finger stopped on the map, on a pin placed at Sefa's garden, "—they already have this one. So why haven't they come here?"

The answer arrived at midnight, and it arrived as smoke.

Not Sefa's cottage — the barn at the edge of the village, the one where we'd stashed the company's winter grain and, more importantly, the ledgers. My ledgers. Contracts, debts, the Grey Ledger's entire institutional memory, going up like a festival bonfire while three pale shapes stood in the burning yard, not moving, not fleeing, simply *present*, letting the light show them to everyone who came running.

The village came running. Half of them saw the riders and dropped to their knees, because to them the riders were answered prayers given flesh, beautiful, holy. The other half saw the riders and saw Sefa's empty grave, and understood.

That was the night the company broke. I stood in the firelight watching men I'd bled beside for years look at me across a widening gulf. Deklan — fifteen years, saved my life at the ford, godfather to half my godchildren — wouldn't meet my eye. Little Maret, who I'd taught to hold a shield, whispered to a cluster of others, and the word I caught was *bargain*. Give the loud gods what they want, kneel, be spared. Winter's coming, Bren. The crops are dying. The fog is creeping inward. Praying costs nothing.

Praying costs nothing. That's what the ledger says when you've never had to pay it.

Yara found me at the burn site at dawn, kicking ash off the iron strongbox that had held exactly nothing worth stealing, because the ledgers had been the point. "Bren. Talk to me."

"They offered terms," I said. "Deklan says a rider spoke to him directly. In his mother's voice, Yara. His dead mother's voice, telling him he could go home safe if he brought them the rememberers." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Twenty-two years I've paid these people honestly. Coin for blood, blood for coin. Clean arithmetic. And now the enemy pays in dead mothers' voices, and half my company thinks that's a fair rate."

"And the other half?"

"The other half is standing here."

She put her hand on the strongbox, on the ash, and I watched her decide something. "Then we move tonight. Everyone who stands here — to the chapel, under the hill, before the song leads them anywhere worse. And Bren—" she hesitated, and Yara Montrose does not hesitate, which frightened me more than the riders had. "There's something I haven't told you. Something Veyra said at the rim. About what's buried under the chapel. About why."

I looked at her, this woman I'd followed out of loyalty before I ever followed her out of faith, and I thought about Sefa stepping into the white with her arms folded, paying interest on a sixty-year debt.

"Then tell me on the way," I said, and picked up my axe, and called the roll of everyone we had left.

It was a short roll. It was, God help me, enough.
