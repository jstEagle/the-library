## 10. The Song Under the Floorboards

Sefa's song had eleven verses, and she refused to sing more than three at a time.

"It's not stubbornness," she said, ladling out barley soup that tasted mostly of hope and turnip. "It's the shape of it. The song wants to be sung in pieces, in order, in places. Sing verse four in the wrong place and it's just noise. Sing it in the right place—" she tapped her temple with two fingers, "—and it's a key."

"Keys open things," Odile said. She was sitting cross-legged on the floor with three days' worth of maps spread around her like a fortune-teller's deck, none of which agreed with each other. "Things that are locked are things that were hidden on purpose."

"Everything about this valley was hidden on purpose," I said.

"Then we're all keys, aren't we? Or locks." Odile grinned at me over the rim of her cup. "I haven't decided which is worse."

We'd been in Sefa's kitchen for six days since the riders came through the pass. Six days of thin soup and thinner conversation, Bren sleeping with his sword unsheathed by the door, the refugees from the eastern farms packed into every corner like winter itself had chased them in. Which, in a way, it had. The fog wall had crept another mile inward while we hid. The birds had stopped flying over the valley entirely. And somewhere above us, the loud gods went on answering prayers with their wrong small miracles — milk that never soured but also never nourished, wells that sang at night in voices no one recognized.

Veyra's altar stayed cold. My god-mark stayed quiet. I'd stopped talking to it sometime around the third day, because talking to something silent starts to feel less like faith and more like an argument you're losing.

But the song. The song was doing something.

* * *

Here's what I knew about Sefa's kitchen before all this: nothing. Here's what I knew now: it was built over something older, and the old thing hummed.

You could feel it when the fire burned low. A vibration under the floorboards, faint as a second heartbeat, and whenever Sefa sang one of her three-verse fragments, the humming changed. Matched her. Followed.

"The house remembers the song," Sefa said, the fourth night, watching my face as I felt it happen. "Or the song remembers the house. I told you. My grandmother taught me the fragments and made me swear never to sing them whole. She said the gods listened for it."

"And your grandmother learned it where?"

"From hers. Who learned it from hers." Sefa stirred the soup with the patience of someone who has outlived everyone who ever argued with her. "Back and back until you reach women who weren't hiding it. Women who sang it in the open, in a country with a different name."

"Ashvale," Bren said from the doorway. He'd been saying it more often lately, testing the word like a loose tooth. "The real one. The one that fell."

"The one they buried," Sefa said. "Yes."

Odile looked up from her maps. "And the song goes somewhere."

The old woman smiled, and there was something in it that made the hair on my arms stand up — not menace, exactly. Recognition. Like she'd been waiting sixty years for someone to ask the right question, and now that it had been asked she found the answer both wonderful and terrible.

"The song goes down," she said.

* * *

We sang the verses in order over two nights, moving through the house as the song directed — or so it seemed. Verse four by the eastern window, where the fog-light came in gray. Verses five and six at the hearth. Verse seven in the pantry, of all ridiculous places, with Sefa's jars of pickled onions watching us like a jury. Each time, the humming under the floors grew louder, and each time it moved. Southward. Downward.

"It's a map," Odile breathed, on the second night, standing in the pantry with her arms wrapped around herself. "It's not a song about a place. It *is* a place. It's directions set to music so that no written copy would survive — so that only people who were trusted enough to be taught could ever follow it." She laughed, high and slightly unhinged. "Whoever built this made the map unstealable. Do you understand what that means to a cartographer? It's beautiful. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever heard."

"It means the gods couldn't burn it," Bren said quietly. "Even if they tried."

"They tried," Sefa said. "My family has the scars to prove it. Why do you think we kept the rites in the kitchen? Nobody searches a kitchen for heresy. They look for altars, for shrines, for books." She patted her flour-dusted table almost fondly. "They never think to look under the bread."

Verse eight was sung at midnight, at the threshold of the cellar door. And when the last note faded, the humming below us did not fade with it. It waited.

"Well," I said, into the silence. "I suppose we're going down."

"You suppose correctly," said Odile.

Bren lit a lamp.

* * *

The cellar was a cellar for about ten feet, and then it wasn't.

The back wall — stone, damp, ordinary — swung inward on hinges so well-made and so long-unused that they screamed like a soul being flayed, but they held. Beyond it, stairs cut from living rock descended into dark, and the air that rose up to meet us smelled of dust and cold iron and something else, something green, impossibly, as if spring had been sealed down there and forgotten.

Nobody spoke. There are moments when even Odile runs out of words, though I want it recorded that this lasted perhaps four seconds before she whispered, "*Pre-fall construction,*" in the reverent tones other people reserve for lovers.

Bren went first, because Bren always goes first; he'd have gone first into the mouth of the loud gods themselves if it meant the rest of us walked behind him. I went second, lamp raised, hand on my knife out of thirty years of habit rather than any belief it would help. Behind me came Odile, then Sefa — who should not have been making that descent at her age, and who informed us of this fact twice, cheerfully, both times.

The stairs went down farther than they had any right to. I counted ninety steps and stopped counting after a hundred and forty. The walls changed as we descended: rough-hewn, then dressed stone, then carved, and the carvings began to appear — figures, processions, tall shapes whose faces had been deliberately smoothed blank. Not broken. Smoothed. Someone had taken the time to erase faces that were already underground.

"They didn't want even the stone to remember them," I said.

"Or they wanted the stone to remember *only that much*," Odile murmured, running her fingers along a blank face. "A silhouette. Enough to know someone stood here. Not enough to say who."

The stairs ended at a door. Of course they did. Everything in my life had become doors lately — the Door at the edge of the fog, the Door in Veyra's fragmentary pleading, and now a smaller one, wooden and iron-banded and utterly unremarkable, except that it was buried a hundred feet beneath a heretic's kitchen in a country that officially did not exist.

There were no handles. No locks. Just a recess at eye height, cupped like two hands folded together in prayer.

Sefa looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked at me.

"This part isn't in the song," she said. "The song ends at the stairs. Whatever's past this door, my grandmothers never saw it. They kept the way and trusted someone else to keep the destination." She stepped back. "This is yours, mercenary. Whatever your god was to you — this is where she'll be."

I put my hands in the recess.

The stone was warm.

Not sun-warm. Blood-warm. And beneath my palms I felt it again — the humming, but close now, resolved into something with rhythm and weight, and underneath the rhythm, faint as breath on the far side of a wall, a voice.

Not speaking. Not yet. Just — present. The way you know someone is standing behind you without hearing a footfall.

"Oh," I said. My voice cracked in the middle of it, which I chose to blame on the dust. "She's here. She's been *here.*"

I pushed, and the door opened.

* * *

The chapel was round, and vast, and it should not have fit beneath the hill above it. I've learned not to write that sentence in reports anymore, because it invites questions, but the fact remains: the room was bigger than the ground containing it, the way a memory is bigger than the moment it came from. Pillars rose into darkness overhead. The lamp-light fell short of anything, and yet everything was visible, dimly, the way things are visible in dreams.

And the walls remembered.

That's the only honest way to say it. The murals weren't painted. They were *kept* — held in the stone the way Sefa held her songs, in pieces, in order, in places. As our lamplight moved, the images moved with it, brightening where we looked, dimming where we didn't, shy as animals.

We saw a valley. Green, gold-lit, terraced fields climbing hillsides that no longer existed anywhere in Ashvale. We saw a city — a real city, towers and bridges and markets, larger than anything our valley town had grown into in three hundred years. We saw crowds. Harvests. Weddings. Ordinary, enormous life.

Then the murals turned, as we walked the circle, and the story turned with them.

Fire on the horizon. Columns of figures fleeing. And above it all, towering, shapes that I knew in my bones though no mural had ever shown me their faces — the gods, the loud ones, the ones currently answering prayers across the valley with their milk-that-never-sours miracles. They were depicted as they must have been at the world's founding: vast, radiant, and holding something. In panel after panel they carried it away — a light, a flame, a crowned figure rendered small against their scale — and the fleeing people reached after it, and the gods did not look back.

"What did they take?" Bren asked. His voice came out flat, the way it does when he's keeping himself level by force.

"I don't know," I said. "But everyone in these paintings is reaching for it."

The next panels showed the hiding. And this — this is the part I will carry until I die.

A figure knelt at the edge of the fleeing crowd. Taller than the people, robed in gray, and her face was not smoothed away like the gods' faces on the stairway. Her face was left bare, and it was drawn with such tenderness that whoever cut those lines must have loved her, and it was *my* face. Not mine-mine. But the set of the jaw, the scar over the brow, the way the head bowed forward under a weight — the same face that stares back at me from my reflection on bad mornings, older and wearier.

Around the kneeling figure, the mural showed the great working: a veil drawn down over the valley like a curtain over a stage, fog rising to swallow the hills, roads unraveling into grass, names lifting off gravestones like smoke. And the kneeling figure was doing it. Her hands were raised, and the threads of the veil ran from her fingers, and she was pulling the whole country into silence — and the artist had carved her expression, in loving detail, as grief. Not triumph. Grief, and guilt, and a resolve so absolute it looked like pain.

Beneath her knees, in characters older than any alphabet I knew but legible anyway, the way some things are legible anyway, ran a line of text. Odile translated it aloud in a whisper, stumbling twice:

"*Here stands the one who chose silence, who veiled the beloved country so that its people might live, and who gave her voice as the price of the veil.*"

Nobody said anything for a while. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us, muffled by a hundred feet of rock and six hundred years of lies, the wind moved over Sefa's kitchen garden.

"She hid us," I finally managed. "Veyra hid us. Not the loud gods — *her.* All those years I sat at that cold altar thinking my god had abandoned us, abandoned *me*, and she was—" I gestured at the wall, at the bowed head, at the veil threading from those carved hands. "She was spending herself. Buying us with her own voice. Thirty years of silence wasn't neglect. It was *payment.*"

"That's why the other shrines flare and hers stays dark," Odile said softly. "The loud gods woke and started performing. She can't. She paid everything she had to make this hiding hold." Her eyes were wet, and she didn't pretend otherwise. "Yara. She didn't stop speaking to you. She *can't speak at all.*"

"Then the dying pilgrim—"

"Was her spending the last coin she had," Bren said. "One name, through a stranger's mouth. Yours." He put his hand on my shoulder, brief and heavy, the way he does instead of sentences. "She broke her own silence for you. That's not nothing, Yara. That's everything."

I stood in front of the mural a long time. Around us the walls kept their vigil — harvests and funerals, weddings and fires, the whole buried life of a country that had fallen and been forbidden to be remembered. At the far side of the circle, past the veiling, the murals showed one final image: the kneeling figure again, but risen now, standing before an enormous door — the Door, the true one, rendered with a precision that made my mark ache against my forearm — and beside it, smaller, a second shape. Unfinished. The stone there was raw, the carving abandoned or withheld.

Waiting for something. Or someone.

"There's more," I said. "The song had verses Sefa wouldn't sing yet. And the walls aren't finished telling it either."

"No," said a voice.

Every blade in the chapel cleared leather at once, which tells you something about how the last month had gone.

The voice had come from everywhere and nowhere, the way it had come through the dying pilgrim's mouth — except this time it wasn't borrowed. This time it filled the stone itself, the murals brightening with each word, the carved figure at the center of the veiling seeming to draw breath.

This time it was her. All of her, whatever remained.

*"Yara,"* said Veyra, in the voice of the walls themselves, and the warmth of it and the grief of it hit me together like weather. *"You found the chapel. Then you deserve the truth — all of it, not the piece I could buy with a dying man's last breath. Come to the unfinished wall. What I hid, I hid to protect something. And you must decide, before the winter finishes falling, whether it should have been protected at all."*

The lamp-flame bent toward the far side of the circle, toward the raw stone and the empty space beside the carved Door, as if pointing.

Odile looked at me. Bren looked at me. Even Sefa, ancient and unafraid, looked at me.

"Mercenary," Sefa said gently, "your god is asking you to walk."

So I picked up the lamp, and I walked.
