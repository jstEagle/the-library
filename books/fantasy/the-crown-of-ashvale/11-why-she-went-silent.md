## 11. Why She Went Silent

The chapel did not want us to leave.

I don't mean it locked the doors. I mean the walls kept *talking*. The murals we'd uncovered by candlelight — the Door, the veiled country, the figure with her mouth sewn shut in gold thread — they seemed to shift when I wasn't looking directly at them, the way a face changes when you stop watching it. Odile had already sketched everything twice and declared both attempts "unreliable," which for Odile was roughly equivalent to screaming.

Bren stood by the entrance with his arms crossed, doing his best impression of a man who was not rattled. He'd been holding that pose since midnight. It was wearing thin around the eyes.

"So," he said. "We're just going to stand here until your god decides to finish the sentence."

"She's not my god."

"You've got her mark burned into your forearm, Yara. You've prayed to her every morning since you were nine. I've watched you do it. You do it like other people smoke."

I looked at my arm. Under the bandage, the god-mark still throbbed — a slow, hot pulse, like a bruise learning to speak. Thirty years of cold altar, thirty years of one-sided prayer, and now the silence had weight to it. Now it felt less like absence and more like someone standing very close behind a door, breathing.

"I do it out of habit," I said.

"Sure."

"Habit and spite."

"That's more like it."

The candles guttered — all of them at once, though there was no draft — and the temperature dropped the way it does before snowfall, except we were underground, under a floor Sefa's grandmother had bricked over to hide a country's memory from its own grandchildren. In the mural, the sewn-mouthed figure turned her head. Just slightly. Just enough.

Then Veyra spoke, and she did not use a dying stranger this time. She used the whole room.

*Yara.*

It came up through the flagstones, through the soles of my boots, into my teeth. Not a voice exactly. More like remembering something you never learned.

"I'm here," I said, and hated how fast I said it. How eager. Thirty years of being ignored and I came running the moment she whistled. Bren gave me a look that said he'd noticed too, and had the decency to say nothing about it.

*I cannot show you all of it,* the room said. *Some of what I know, the others can still feel me knowing. If I think it plainly, they will come. So I will give it to you the way it was given to me. In pieces. In the order I could bear it.*

"Wonderful," Odile muttered, uncapping her ink. "A god who redacts herself." But her hand was shaking, and she wrote anyway, because that was Odile: fear worn as competence, terror transcribed in a neat cartographer's hand.

*Ask me,* Veyra said. *You have earned your questions. You have earned them for thirty years of speaking into my silence, and I am sorry — I am sorry — that I let you do it alone.*

I had rehearsed questions my whole life. Why did you go quiet? What did I do wrong? Was it something the valley did? And now that I finally had an audience, every rehearsed question evaporated, and what came out instead was:

"Did you ever hear any of them? My prayers?"

The pause went on long enough that I understood the answer before it arrived.

*Every one.*

The floor of my stomach opened. Thirty years. Every dawn prayer mumbled over a cold altar while the other mercenaries slept. Every desperate one — when Bren took the spear meant for me at Kellin's Ford, when my mother coughed blood into her winter blanket, when I was nineteen and stupid and begging anyone who'd listen for a sign that my life was going somewhere. She heard all of it. She lay there listening like a woman buried alive, hearing footsteps overhead.

"Why didn't you answer? Even once. Even a *coin on the ground*, even a dream—"

*Because answering is a door,* Veyra said, *and doors open from both sides. If I had spoken to you — truly spoken, even a whisper — the others would have felt the warmth of it. They would have followed the sound to Ashvale. To you.* A pause, and then, quieter, in a register I can only describe as ashamed: *And I could not let them find you. That is the whole of my silence, Yara. It was never abandonment. It was hiding you behind my own tongue.*

Bren shifted. "Find us why?" he asked. "What are the loud gods looking for?"

*Witnesses.*

The word dropped into the chapel like a stone into a well. We waited for the splash.

"The founding," I said slowly. "You told the pilgrim — you told him the world was founded on something. On a crime."

*On the first crime. Before there were nations there were names, and before there were names there was the making, and the making required a price that the six agreed upon together and then agreed, together, never to name.* The murals moved as she spoke — I saw it clearly now, couldn't unsee it. Figures kneeling in a ring. Something in the center of the ring that the artist had painted as a wound in the world, a place where the paint simply refused to be anything. *They swore an oath of forgetting, each god over their own portion of creation. What was spent to make the world would be remembered by no one. Not by mortals. Not by history. Not even, they hoped, by themselves.*

"And it worked?" Odile asked. Her pen hadn't stopped moving. "Nobody remembers?"

*Nearly no one. Memory is stubborn. It pools in low places — in songs, in kitchens, in old women who refuse to stop singing. And it pooled here.* The candle flames bent toward the mural, toward the kneeling figures, toward the wound. *This valley was not always a valley. This was the place where the price was paid. The center of the ring. The people who lived here were the ones who carried the cost — and the ones who saw it done.*

"They saw the gods make the world," Bren said flatly.

*They saw the gods pay for it. There is a difference, and the difference is why the six wanted them dead.*

The cold in the chapel deepened. Somewhere above us, I heard wind move across the buried hillside, and it sounded like a held breath.

*When the making was finished, the six went out to tidy the world. And tidying, to gods, means removing what does not fit. The witnesses did not fit. They remembered. Their children remembered, because memory ran in the water here, in the soil, in the songs mothers sang. So the six came back with white faces and clean hands and began to kill a country.*

"How many?" I asked. I don't know why I asked. I knew I'd regret knowing.

*All of them,* Veyra said. *That was the plan. All of them, down to the last child who might hum the wrong tune.*

Odile set down her pen. I think her hand had finally refused the work.

"And you—" I started, and had to start again. "You were one of the six."

*I was the youngest. I was the one they sent to Ashvale, because I was the one whose portion of the making these people had witnessed most closely, and they thought — they reasoned, the way gods reason, which is to say without once imagining themselves wrong — that I would be glad of the task.* The murals shifted again. The sewn-mouthed figure stood apart from the others now, facing them, and even rendered in ancient pigment I could see the set of her shoulders. I knew those shoulders. I'd seen them in my own reflection every time I decided not to walk away from a contract that smelled wrong. *I went to kill a country and I found a people. Farmers. Smiths. A woman who fed me bread at her own table without knowing what I was, because hospitality here was older than the crime, older than the gods, older than the price. They fed the executioner, Yara. They fed her and asked nothing but her company.*

The mark on my arm burned. I pressed my palm against it and kept my voice level, mostly.

"So you hid them instead."

*I hid them. I took the country — the whole living shape of it, its name, its position in the world, its reflection in every map and memory beyond its borders — and I folded it away. I made Ashvale a pocket the world's eye slid past. And because a hiding of that size requires a seal, and because the only seal the six would believe was one of their own oaths, I sealed it with myself. I swore the same oath they had sworn at the founding. Silence. Forgetting. I gave up my voice so that theirs would be kept. I erased my own name from heaven so that Ashvale could keep its name, even if no one outside could hear it.*

"For thirty years," Bren said.

*For three hundred,* Veyra said. *Thirty years is only how long it has been since the seal began to fail. Since the others woke and began to feel the loose thread of a country they believed they had unmade. Everything since — the answered prayers, the pale riders, the winter coming early and wrong — that is them, pulling the thread. Looking for the hole. Looking for the witnesses they failed to kill, and for the sister who betrayed them.*

The chapel was silent except for the candles. I stood there in the wreckage of my entire life and tried to assemble something like a thought.

Here is what I had believed, since I was old enough to believe anything: that Veyra had gone silent because the world had grown too wicked or too noisy or too far away; that faith meant tending a cold altar anyway; that devotion was endurance without reward. I had built a whole self on top of that belief the way a house is built on a foundation stone. Mercenary work, the long winters, the contracts unpaid, the prayers into the dark — all of it propped up by the idea that silence was a test, and that someday, if I endured well enough, the silence would break and mean something.

It had broken. It meant something. Just not anything I had imagined. My god wasn't distant. She was *buried alive next to us*, holding her mouth shut with both hands for three centuries so that a country of farmers and smiths wouldn't be murdered for remembering. And the whole time, the other five — the loud ones, the ones whose shrines were suddenly flaring with miracles across the valley — were the ones who'd swung the blades in the first place.

My faith didn't shatter. I want to be honest about that, because everyone expects shattering, glass everywhere, dramatic. It was worse than that. It *turned over*, like soil, like a grave, and what had been buried underneath came up into the light, and I recognized it, and I could never put it back the way it was.

"You let us worship you," I said. My voice came out strange and calm. "You let a whole valley build shrines and light candles and tithe to a god who couldn't answer because answering would get us killed. You watched us starve in bad winters. You watched my mother die praying to you."

*Yes.*

"You could have told someone. Left a message. Drawn a picture in the frost. Anything."

*A message is a sound, and sounds travel. I told myself, every year, that I would find a way. And every year I found instead another reason the risk was too great, and I want you to understand that some of those reasons were true and some of them were cowardice wearing truth's coat, and after enough centuries I could no longer tell which was which.* The candles flared, steadied. *I am not asking you to forgive me. I am telling you the shape of the prison, because you are standing inside it, and the door is closing faster now.*

"The Door," Odile said immediately. "The one in the mural. The one the loud gods are hunting for. What is it?"

*Not a thing,* Veyra said. *A choice. When I folded Ashvale away, I could not destroy what I hid — the country's true name, its memory, its claim on the world — because destroying it would have killed every soul carrying it. So I sealed it into a threshold and set it where no one would look: at the center of the wound itself. The Door stands in the oldest place in this valley, and it opens onto what was taken. And the six cannot rest while it exists, because as long as it exists, the crime has a witness that cannot be killed.*

"So they'll dig," Bren said. "They'll tear the valley apart."

*They will not need to dig. The winter is their instrument. When the long winter finishes falling, the fog will close, the cold will finish, and everything sealed inside will sleep past all waking. They are not searching for the Door anymore, Yara. They are waiting for the season to bury it for them. That is why the cold comes early. That is why it comes wrong. They are not punishing you.* A pause, and the shame in it was bottomless. *They are filing you away.*

I thought of the ledger with its unpaid lines. Of the fog wall at the rim, standing like a wall built by intent. Of Sefa burning her relics in the kitchen while the riders' hoofbeats faded, staying behind so the memory could run. Of refugees who'd soon be flooding toward a buried chapel because nowhere else in the world would hold them.

"Well," I said. "Then we open it first."

*Yara—*

"No. Don't." I held up a hand to a room, which is a stupid gesture, and I made it anyway. "You don't get to confess beautifully and then hesitate. You hid us for three hundred years. Fine. Terrible, necessary, fine. But the season is their weapon, which means the season is a clock, which means we're not hiding anymore. Hiding is over. It ended the day your seal cracked and the prayers started getting answered wrong. So tell me how the Door opens."

Silence. Real silence, the ordinary kind, the kind that belongs to the living.

Then, softly, in the voice of a woman who has been holding her breath for three hundred years:

*Names. The Door answers to the true names of the erased — the country's name, spoken whole, by someone the country remembers. But I did not keep them, Yara. I could not. Keeping them would have been a warmth the others could feel. I scattered them beyond the fog, into the keeping of the six themselves, into their archive, where stolen things go to be forgotten safely.* The candles bent low. *The names you need are in the enemy's house.*

Odile and I looked at each other across the candlelight. She was already reaching for her satchel — for the forged imperial credentials she wore like a second skin, the surveyor's seals she'd used to talk her way into places no exile should have walked. She caught me looking and lifted her chin.

"Don't," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're about to say something noble and suicidal, and I'm telling you now, I've already done the arithmetic. Nobody else can pass the archive's scrutiny. Nobody else can draw a map of a place from a single fragment of its name." She slung the satchel over her shoulder. "So whatever speech you're loading, save it. I'll need the energy for lying to gods."

Above us, muffled by earth and brick and three hundred years of deliberate forgetting, the wind changed. It came down through the hillside with an edge to it that had no business existing in autumn, and every candle in the chapel leaned the same direction at once, like grass before a storm, like people turning to watch something arrive.

Veyra spoke one last time, and her voice had gone small.

*The winter has begun.*

* * *

We climbed out of the chapel into a world gone white at the edges. Frost on the bracken in the middle of harvest month. Breath hanging in clouds. And far off, past the tree line, past the rim, the fog wall had moved — I was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of a door you left shut standing open. Closer. Maybe half a mile closer. Maybe more.

Bren squinted at it, then at Odile, then at me.

"How long do we have?"

Odile consulted some internal ledger of her own. "If the cold keeps this pace? Weeks. Fewer if the gods hurry it."

"Then we move tonight." I pulled my collar up against the bite in the air and found that my hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the chapel, and I was grateful, and I was frightened of how grateful. "Sefa's song led us here. There'll be more verses. There's always more verses — old women don't waste lyrics. We find every scrap of name she remembers, and then Odile walks into the mountain, and the rest of us make sure there's still a valley for her to walk back to."

"And you?" Bren asked. "What's your part in this arithmetic?"

I looked down at my arm, at the mark that had burned for thirty years and finally, finally had something to say.

"I'm going to pray," I said. "Out loud. For the first time in my life, somebody's actually listening — and I intend to give her a piece of my mind the whole way to the Door."
