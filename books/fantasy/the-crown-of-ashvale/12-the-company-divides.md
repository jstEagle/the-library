## 12. The Company Divides

I have held councils in worse weather. I once argued contract terms in a hailstorm on the Kettering flats, and another time negotiated a prisoner exchange while both sides' archers had the draw on each other. But I have never held a council where half the people in the circle were deciding whether to sell the other half to gods.

The snow came down soft that morning, which felt like a cruelty. If the sky had been doing something violent — sleet, thunder, one of those wrong-colored sunrises Odile kept muttering about — at least it would have matched the mood. Instead it was gentle. It gathered on shoulders and hoods and the flat of spearheads, and it made everything look clean, and nothing was.

We met in the yard behind the chapel, because the chapel's undercroft was full of refugees and the common room was full of fear, and fear travels faster in a warm room. Forty-one of my company stood in a rough ring. That number mattered to me. I count my people the way a miser counts coin: constantly, obsessively, with a sick feeling whenever the total changes. We'd been fifty-three when the riders first came out of the fog wearing borrowed faces. Twelve gone, between the ambush at Sefa's road and the scattering after her kitchen burned. Twelve names I carried in a ledger inside my head, next to the ledger of debts the company owed and couldn't pay.

Yara stood across from me. She always did, these days. Not against me — never quite against me — but across, the way two people stand when they're holding opposite ends of something heavy and neither trusts the other not to drop it.

"Say your piece," she said to the circle. "All of you. Nobody leaves this yard thinking their side wasn't heard."

That was Yara all over. Fair to a fault, even now, even with the fog creeping inward and the crops dying black in the fields and a god's confession still sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal. I'd have been tempted to just decide. That's the difference between us. She wants people to choose rightly. I mostly want them to choose quickly.

Dace spoke first, because Dace always speaks first; the man has never met a silence he didn't take personally.

"I'll say what everyone's thinking," he said, stamping his boots. "The loud ones are answering prayers again. Real answers. Marta's boy had a fever last week — you all know — and she prayed at the shrine of Callen-by-the-Road, and by morning the fever broke. Broke *clean*. You want to tell me that's a trick?"

"It might be," Yara said quietly.

"And it might not! And here's my point." Dace spread his hands, snow gathering in his palms like an offering. "These gods are offering terms. The riders said so, plain as tax collectors. Lay down the memory-business, hand over anyone carrying true remembering, and the rest of us get through winter alive. That's not a trap, Bren. That's a *contract*. We're mercenaries. Contracts are the one language we speak."

He looked around the circle for agreement and found some. I watched it move through faces like wind through barley — Hesk nodding, old Tamra crossing her arms but not arguing, young Piet staring at his feet with the particular misery of a man who agrees with everyone and pleases no one, including himself.

"That's not a contract," I said. "Contracts have two parties who intend to honor them. That's bait."

"You don't know that."

"I know that Sefa burned seventy years of relics rather than let the riders take them, and stayed behind so we could run. I know what they did to her." I didn't describe it. Everyone had heard enough by then, secondhand, from the two scouts who'd doubled back far enough to see the smoke and not far enough to see anything worth seeing. "You don't offer honest terms and then do that."

"Sefa was *carrying*," Dace said. "She was one of the rememberers. I'm not saying abandon them. I'm saying the math is ugly and somebody's got to do it. Hand over the ones who carry true memory — Yara, the cartographer, whoever else has songs stuck in their heads — and the rest of the company walks into spring. Fifty-some lives for three or four. Any client in this valley would take that trade and sleep well."

The yard went very quiet. Snow ticked down. Somewhere beyond the wall, a refugee child was singing one of Sefa's songs without knowing what it was, which is either the saddest thing I've heard or the most hopeful, and I still haven't decided.

Yara didn't flinch. I've seen her take a crossbow bolt in the shoulder with less expression than she wore just then, standing in the snow while a man she'd bled beside for six years suggested handing her to monsters as a line item.

"Dace," she said, "do you think I haven't done that math?"

"I think you're too close to it to do it honestly."

"I think," she said, "that the moment we hand over one person to buy the safety of the rest, we stop being a company and start being a queue. And queues get shorter. Ask yourself who's next after me. The person who hums the wrong song? The person whose grandmother told stories? The loud gods aren't hungry for a fixed number, Dace. They're hungry, full stop. Feed them and they stay hungry."

"She's right," Tamra said, surprising everyone including, by her face, herself. "My mother used to sing a song about a river with no name. Just a nonsense tune. Last night I caught myself wondering if it counted as remembering. Wondering if I should turn myself in for *humming*." She spat. "That's what their terms do to you. They make you police your own head. I buried a husband under a god who went silent, and I'll not kneel to gods who wake up hungry."

"So we fight," Dace said. "Forty-one of us, against things that wear the faces of answered prayers. Against winter that comes early and wrong. Against *fog*, Bren. You've seen the rim. You want to fight weather?"

"No," I said. "I want to open a door."

* * *

Here is what nobody outside the inner circle knew yet, and what I'd spent a sleepless night deciding whether to say aloud: Odile thought she could work with the name-fragment she'd stolen from the archive. Not finish it — she was clear about that, in her infuriating precise way, drawing little diagrams in the frost on the window until I wanted to throw a mitten at her — but use it. Find the Door's lock. Start turning it. And every hour mattered, because the archive plans she'd glimpsed made one thing certain: the loud gods intended to let the long winter finish falling and bury Ashvale under ice and forgetting forever. This wasn't persecution with an end date. It was an execution, scheduled.

Telling forty-one frightened armed people that our survival plan depended on a foreigner's chalk diagrams and a scrap of stolen god-names felt, I'll admit, like a gamble. But Yara had asked for honesty in that yard, and I've learned — slowly, expensively, the way I learn everything — that the alternative to honesty in a mercenary company is mutiny with better manners.

So I told them. All of it. The Door, the fragment, the clock.

The reaction split the yard like an axe.

Piet was the first to come over to our side, which surprised me; he'd struck me as a bargainer. "They killed Sefa," he said simply, as if that settled it, and maybe for him it did. Hesk went the other way, taking four others with him, muttering about children and widows and how heroes die warm-blooded while cowards live to raise the orphans. Tamra planted herself beside Yara and started quietly recruiting, the way old sergeants do, with eye contact and small nods, no speeches needed.

And Marta — Marta of the fever-broken boy, whose gratitude to the loud gods was the freshest and most human thing in that yard — stood between the factions with tears freezing on her cheeks and said, "I can't choose. Do you understand? My son is alive because one of them listened. If I stand with you and he dies of the next fever, I did that. If I stand with them and you all burn, I did that too." Her voice cracked. "Somebody decide for me. Please. Somebody just *decide*."

Everyone looked at me.

That's the joke of it, really. Yara's the one with the god-mark burning on her arm, the one the dead pilgrim named, the one Veyra chose. But when the company needs someone to carry the weight of a decision, they look at the fat old sergeant with the bad knee. Maybe because Yara carries too much already. Maybe because they know I'll actually decide, whereas she'll deliberate beautifully and then decide the same way anyway.

I looked at Dace. Six years. We'd pulled each other out of the marsh at Corren's Ford, split a bottle of terrible plum brandy on the anniversary of it every year since, taught each other's card tricks and cheated each other at them. He stood there in the snow with his jaw set, and I could see he thought he was being the brave one. The realist. The man willing to be hated to save lives. I knew that look because I've worn it.

"Dace," I said. "Come with us."

"Bren—"

"Not to fight. Not to die. Just — don't hand anyone over. Stand apart if you have to. Take your faction to the east barns and sit out the winter neutral, and if we fail, you bargain with whatever's left. I'll even put it in writing. Company seal." I held his eyes. "But I'm not splitting this company by giving it halves. I'm asking you to give me time."

"And if the time runs out?"

"Then you'll have lost nothing but a winter's comfort, and I'll have lost—" I stopped. Started again. "Don't make me do the math on you, Dace. I refuse."

Something moved behind his face. Not agreement. Grief, maybe, wearing agreement's coat. He looked at Yara, at Tamra, at the smoke rising off the chapel roof where refugees were warming soup over a fire built from Sefa's broken furniture, because Yara had refused to let anything of hers go to waste and also refused to let it be sacred and unused.

"East barns," Dace said finally. "Until thaw. Neutral. But Bren — if the riders come for the chapel, my people will not bleed for it. Swear that's understood."

"They won't be asked to," Yara said.

He nodded once, turned, and walked, and five followed him, and the sound of their boots in the snow going away from us was the worst sound of the winter so far, and the winter had already taken Sefa.

Thirty-five left. I recounted. Thirty-five.

* * *

Afterward, Yara found me in the chapel doorway, watching the snow bury Dace's footprints. She handed me a cup of something hot that tasted like pine needles and stubbornness — Sefa's recipe, or close enough, the refugees had gotten it from her before the end — and we drank in silence for a while, the way old soldiers drink, which is to say: together, and about nothing, which means about everything.

"He'll keep his word," Yara said.

"He'll try. That's different." I blew on the cup. "You know what the ugly part is? He's not wrong about the math. Fifty for three or four *is* the trade any sensible client takes. I've brokered trades like it. Ugly ones. I stood there today and chose the other side of a ledger I've worked my whole life, and I couldn't tell you exactly why, except that Sefa stayed behind humming, and Marta's boy is alive, and somewhere past that fog there's a door, and I'd rather die trying to open a door than live having closed one."

Yara smiled, small and tired. "That's the most romantic thing you've ever said."

"Don't spread it around. I have a reputation."

"You have heartburn and a ledger."

"Both earned." I drained the pine-needle swill and set the cup down. "There's one more thing. The council bought us time, not safety. Riders hit Sefa's because someone talked. Dace's word is good, but words travel, and there are thirty-five of us and some of them cried in their sleep last night where others could hear. If the pale ones come again, they'll come soon, and they'll come hard, and Odile says she needs — what did she say — 'days I may not have.'"

Yara looked out at the falling snow, at the fog-wall far off on the rim, faintly visible even from here now, a gray bruise on the horizon that hadn't been there a month ago. Her hand drifted to her arm, where the mark lived under the sleeve.

"Then we don't wait for her to be ready," she said. "We get ready anyway. Tonight. Bren — wake the thirty-five who stayed. All of them. If the company divides, let it divide along the doorframe, not in the dark."

I went to do it. And as I crossed the yard, I passed the spot where Dace had stood, and the snow had already filled his footprints, smooth and white, as if no one had ever chosen to walk away at all.

Which is a lie snow tells, and a lie the loud gods would love us all to believe: that leaving leaves no trace, that choosing doesn't happen, that nothing was ever decided here.

I stepped on the smooth patch, deliberately, and went to wake my people.
