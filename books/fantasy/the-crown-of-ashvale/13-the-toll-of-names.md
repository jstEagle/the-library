## 13. The Toll of Names

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a room full of people who have just heard a terrible plan and are trying to decide whose job it is to say so. I know that silence intimately. I have caused it in at least four cities and one monastery.

This time, it was my own terrible plan hanging in the air of Sefa's kitchen, and the silence had a texture to it — flour dust and woodsmoke and the faintly judgmental ticking of an egg timer Sefa refused to explain.

"Let me see if I've understood you," Bren said, in the tone of a man checking his own footing on thin ice. "You want to walk out past the fog wall. Alone. Into the territory of gods who are currently hunting everyone in this valley for the crime of remembering things. Using papers you forged."

"Forged is such an ugly word," I said. "*Provenanced* is better. The credentials have excellent provenance."

"They're drawn on a napkin."

"The napkin is *vellum*, Captain, and the seal is better than the real Imperial Survey Office's, because I made mine symmetrical."

Yara had her arms crossed and was doing the thing where she looked at me like I was a map with a deliberate error in it — which, to be fair, is what most people look like if you stare long enough. "Odile. Why yours? Why not send someone who can fight?"

"Because no one can fight their way into the Archive of Small Gods," I said. "Believe me, I've read about it. It's not a fortress, it's a *library* that learned to be a fortress. You don't breach it. You get *invited*. And invitations go to surveyors, tax assessors, census-takers — people with clipboards and stamps and the moral authority of bureaucracy." I tapped the leather tube at my hip. "I spent eleven years forging imperial credentials before I ever set foot in Ashvale. It's the only honest work I've ever done, using them for something true."

"And what's true?"

"That they're keeping the names." I let myself feel it for a second, the thing under all the wit, the cold stone of it. "Every name of this country. Every street in every town that fell. The Door wants a toll, Yara — you told us yourself, Veyra said it plain: *the Door answers only to what it lost.* We can't pay that toll from inside here. Everything inside has been... sanded down. Sefa's songs are fragments. The chapel walls are pictures. But names — true names, written names, catalogued by clerks who never imagined anyone would come asking — those are still sitting in the loud gods' mountain, filed under *Confiscated*, waiting for someone with the right stamp."

The egg timer ticked. Sefa, at her hearth, stirred something that smelled like rosemary and defiance and didn't look up.

"I don't like it," Yara said finally.

"You don't have to like it. You have to hold the chapel when I'm gone."

"That's not what I meant," she said quietly, and I discovered that the worst part of being known by people is that they can say things quietly and they still land like shouted ones.

"Well," I said, standing, brushing off my skirts with more briskness than the moment deserved, "fortunately for us both, I'm extremely difficult to kill. Ask anyone. Ask the Inquisitorial College of Vell Maros. Actually, don't ask them; they're sore about it."

*

I packed light, which for me means two changes of clothes, three sets of forged documents, a compass that lies, a compass that tells the truth, ink, six nibs, a knife I mostly use for sharpening pencils, and a pencil. Bren watched me lay it all out on Sefa's table with the expression of a man inventorying a coffin.

"You could take the lying compass," he offered. "Leave the honest one. Might be kinder."

"The honest compass stays because the honest compass is the whole reason I'm alive." I wrapped it in oilcloth. It had been spinning uselessly since the fog wall, true north drowned somewhere beyond it, but it pointed at *something* out there — a stubborn, needle-drunk insistence — and I had decided, without telling anyone, to trust it. A cartographer who trusts nothing ends up drawing beautiful maps of nowhere. I'd done that already. I had the portfolio to prove it.

Bren lingered. Big man, careful hands, a face built for scowling that kept betraying him by looking worried instead. "When I was breaking into the company archive," he said, "there was a ledger. Muster rolls. And in the margin of the oldest page, someone had written names in a different hand — corrections, additions. Small writing. Precise."

"Go on."

"It looked like your writing, Odile."

I kept folding the oilcloth. Fold, crease, fold. "Lots of precise hands in the world."

"In a sealed archive. Under the floor of a mercenary company in a country that officially doesn't exist. In handwriting I've watched you make maps with for three weeks." He paused. "Were you here before? Before any of this?"

And there it was — the question I'd been carrying like a stone in my boot since the day I crossed the fog and felt, absurdly, *recognized*. Since I'd stood at the rim and my honest compass had stopped spinning and pointed *inward* like a homesick dog.

"No," I said. "I'd remember."

"Would you?" he asked gently, and left me alone with my packing.

That night I took out my oldest folio — the one I never showed anyone, the one with the maps of nowhere — and looked at the earliest drawing in it. A valley. Ridgelines I had never surveyed. A river with a bend exactly where Ashvale's river bent. I had drawn it at nineteen, in a rented room in a city eight hundred leagues away, and told myself it was practice. Imagination. A cartographer's dream of a place that fit together too well to be false.

Underneath it, in my own younger hand, a caption: *Ashvale.*

I hadn't remembered writing it either.

*

The fog wall did not part for me. That would have been convenient, and the world had stopped doing convenient roughly around the time the old gods woke up. Instead I walked into it the way you walk into cold water — fast, before the body can file a complaint — and the fog closed over me like a held breath.

Inside the gray, there was no path and also somehow always a path. My boots found ground. My honest compass spun, then steadied, then spun again. Time went soft. I counted my steps because counting was a thing I could do, and around four hundred I began to hear voices in the fog — not words, exactly. More like the memory of words. Someone calling a child home. Someone haggling over fish. A woman singing a lullaby in a language I did not know and then, horribly, halfway through, did.

"*Not listening,*" I told myself, and walked faster.

The fog thinned the way a fever breaks — all at once, and into a world that was colder than the one I'd left.

Ashvale's rim had been late autumn, gold and rust and woodsmoke. Beyond the fog it was deep winter, hard and blue, snow to my knees, and above me — this was the part that stopped my breath and made me stand there like a fool until my toes went numb — above me were mountains I knew. Not recognized. *Knew.* The way you know your own signature. The Sereth Range, the spine of the Empire, except that on every imperial chart ever printed, these peaks carried different names, given by different conquerors, three hundred years apart.

They had renamed the mountains. They had renamed everything. And underneath all the renaming, the bones stayed put, patient as bones, waiting for someone to count them properly.

"Right," I said to the snow, because saying things aloud keeps the madness at a manageable volume. "Survey begins."

*

The Archive of Small Gods sat in the shoulder of the third peak, and I will give the loud gods this much: they have taste. White stone, terraced against the mountainside, stairways wide enough for processions, and everywhere — everywhere — writing. Carved prayers, tiled verses, banners with scripture in gold thread. A building that believed in itself so thoroughly it made you want to argue with it just to feel real.

The gate wardens wore white robes and faces I couldn't quite fix in memory. I looked at the first one and thought, *middle-aged, kind eyes, a scar on the chin,* and then looked again and the scar was on the other cheek and the kindness had moved somewhere else, like furniture rearranged while I blinked. Pale riders' cousins, maybe. Or pale riders in their day clothes.

"Credential," it said. Its voice sounded like many voices agreeing quickly.

I handed over the tube. Inside: letters of commission from the Imperial Survey Office of Vell Maros, stamped, signed, countersigned, aged with tea and smoke and one very careful tear repaired with matching paper. Eleven years of craft. My hands did not shake. I had trained them not to, back when shaking hands got people hanged.

The warden unrolled the commission. Read it. Looked at me. And here is the moment I want noted in whatever record survives of my life: I did not look away. Fear is a currency; spend it visibly and you'll be bled dry. So I gave the warden the flat, faintly bored stare of a career bureaucrat who has been kept waiting in worse places than this.

"The Empire does not survey this region," the warden said.

"The Empire believes it does," I said. "There's a discrepancy on the master charts. A gap. His Majesty's cartographers do not tolerate gaps. Gaps invite questions, and questions invite *other* powers making claims." I leaned in, conspiratorially, the way I'd watched actual surveyors do in customs houses. "You wouldn't want another power making claims on your god's mountain, would you?"

Something moved behind the warden's unfixed face. Calculation, or its imitation. "State your purpose."

"Reconciliation of boundaries. I'll require access to the pre-Restoration territorial records. Land grants, parish registers, cadastral rolls." I named the shelves I wanted the way a gambler names cards. "I'm authorized to copy. Not to remove."

A pause. Snow ticked down between us.

"The Archive is open to accredited servants of the Crown," the warden said at last, and stepped aside, and my heart performed a maneuver I chose to interpret as professional pride rather than terror.

*

They gave me a desk.

That was the worst part, honestly. Not the wards humming along the ceilings like trapped wasps, not the clerks with their too-smooth faces, but the *desk* — oak, lamplit, with a brass lamp I could adjust and a cushioned chair, because the loud gods run a courteous tyranny and courtesy is how tyranny gets you to stay. There was even a little bell to ring for tea. I rang it once, out of spite, and drank the tea, and it was excellent, and I hated how much I enjoyed it.

Then I asked for the pre-Restoration rolls, and a smooth-faced clerk wheeled them out, and I forgot the tea entirely.

Eleven crates. I had hoped for two.

The labels were in the high ecclesiastic script, but the contents — oh, the contents were pure mortal clerkship, blessedly, beautifully mundane. Parish registers from towns whose names I'd only met as Sefa's half-sung fragments. Land grants. Tax assessments. And names — column after column after column of names, the living and the taxed and the baptized and the buried, a whole country's worth of people reduced to entries, which is normally the tragedy of archives and was, tonight, the miracle of them.

Because the Door wanted a toll of true names, and here they were. All of them. Filed.

I worked through the night and into the next day and lied fluently whenever checked on — *cross-referencing boundary disputes, verifying tithing districts* — and copied names onto survey paper in my neat cartographer's hand, hundreds of them, a thousand, hiding each sheet inside legitimate-looking charts. My fingers cramped. My eyes burned. Somewhere past dawn on the second day I opened crate nine and found the muster rolls.

Ashvale's army. The country that fell. Every soldier listed by name, company, and — my pen stopped dead — *by birth-village.* Villages I knew. Villages whose ruins Yara and I had camped in on the road to the chapel, arguing about rations.

One entry near the top of the oldest roll had been annotated in a different ink, older, careful:

*M. Marchetti, sergeant, of Hollowmere. Fallen at the Sundering. Survived by daughter, infant.*

Marchetti. My name. The name I chose for myself at fifteen, off a gravestone in a border town, because orphans need surnames and stolen ones travel lighter than invented ones.

Except apparently it wasn't stolen. Apparently I'd walked across half the world, forged my way into the gods' own mountain, and requested — by bureaucratic instinct, by some compass in the blood — the exact shelf where my own family was filed.

I sat very still for a long time. The lamp hummed. Somewhere above, the wards droned.

"Hello, Grandmother," I whispered to the roll, to the woman who had survived the Sundering long enough to bear a child who bore a child who drew maps of a valley she'd never seen. "I'm the surveyor. I've come to reconcile the discrepancy."

Then I rolled up my sleeves and kept copying, faster now, because I understood at last that this wasn't espionage anymore. It was genealogy. It was grief with a clipboard. And I intended to be thorough.

*

It was crate eleven that ruined everything.

I'd saved it for last because its label was different — not *Territorial Records* but a single word in the high script: *Disposition.* And because I am who I am, a woman who reads the fine print on other people's doom, I saved the worst for last the way you save the last bite of something you suspect will make you sick.

It wasn't names. It was minutes. Council minutes. Dated within the last year — the year the gods woke.

I read them twice to be sure my tired eyes weren't composing fiction. They weren't.

The loud gods had debated what to do about Ashvale now that the veil was fraying. Two factions. One argued for restoration on their terms — reopen the country, reinstall worship, harvest the faith of a captive population as before. But the other faction's proposal was shorter, and written in a firmer hand, and it said simply:

*Let the winter finish falling. Let the fog close inward. What cannot be remembered cannot be resurrected. Seal the valley beneath the ice and strike even the fragments from mortal song. The crime remains hidden. The cost is acceptable.*

Cost. As though a valley of living people were a line item.

As though Yara — who held a shrine for thirty years for a god who couldn't answer — were a rounding error.

I copied the resolution verbatim, both proposals, the vote tally, and one final line appended in fresh ink, dated *three days ago*: *Winter accelerates. Fog advances on schedule. Archive sealing commences at month's end.*

Month's end. I had weeks. Perhaps less — the fog outside my window had visibly crept down the mountainside since I arrived.

My hands were steady. That surprised me. Underneath the steadiness, something was screaming in a very small, very controlled voice, and I let it scream, because fear worn as competence is still competence if the work gets done.

I finished copying the last name I needed — the last of the muster rolls, the last village register, the toll paid in full — and slid everything into the false bottom of my chart case, beneath eleven years of impeccable forgery.

Then I picked up the little brass bell, considered it, and rang for tea one final time.

While the smooth-faced clerk poured, I smiled at him with everything I had — warmth, gratitude, the whole performance — and asked, lightly, idly, the question that had been scratching at me since crate nine:

"One small matter for my report. The Disposition resolution — 'the cost is acceptable.' Whose cost, precisely, did the council have in mind? I'll need it for the summary."

The clerk's face did the furniture-rearranging thing. When it settled, he was smiling too.

"Yours," he said pleasantly. "All of yours."
