## 14. The Archive of Small Gods

The trick to walking into a lion's den, I have found, is to dress like you own the den.

My forged credentials as an imperial surveyor said I was Odile Vasseur-Quenel, Third Assistant to the Office of Cartographic Reconciliation, empowered to inspect and certify all records pertaining to territorial memory. The seal was good — Sefa had a friend who had a friend who had once been hanged for forgery in three provinces, which by my reckoning made him the best forger alive, because you don't get hanged in three provinces for being mediocre. The ink was right. The paper was right. The little wax stamp even smelled faintly of incense, because apparently the gods' bureaucracy liked its documents consecrated.

Everything about me was right except the part that mattered, which was that I was about to steal from gods.

"Third Assistant," Yara had said the night before, squinting at my papers by candlelight. "Not second?"

"Seconds get noticed," I told her. "Assistants are furniture. You want to be furniture with access."

Bren had laughed, then stopped laughing when he remembered what we were doing, then laughed again anyway, because Bren is constitutionally incapable of staying grim longer than a bowl of soup lasts. "You're going to walk into the mountain where the loud gods keep their names," he said, "and your plan is to be furniture."

"My plan," I said, "is to be *boring*. Boring is invisible. Gods are exactly like emperors that way — they can't see anything duller than themselves."

Yara didn't laugh. She took my hands, which was unlike her, and held them too hard, which was very like her, and said, "If the fog wall starts moving before you're out—"

"I'll be out." I said it lightly. I am good at saying things lightly; it's most of what exile taught me.

She didn't believe me. That was fine. I didn't quite believe me either.

* * *

The mountain was called something like *Vhal* or *Vhale* or possibly nothing at all, since names slide off holy places the way rain slides off oiled wool. It stood beyond the fog wall, two days' hard ride from the rim of Ashvale through country I knew only from charts so old they predated the erasure — which meant they showed roads that no longer existed and towns that had never been built, and I navigated by them anyway, because a map doesn't need to be true to be useful. It just needs to be *insistent*.

The road up to the gates was lined with statues. Not statues of the loud gods — those came later, enormous and smug inside the walls — but of small ones. Little gods. Household gods, river gods, the god of the third hour after dawn, the god of lost buttons. Every one of them had been defaced, not broken: their faces smoothed over like thumbprints pressed into clay, their names chiseled away and the stone left rough where the letters used to be. Somebody had wanted these gods *forgotten*, not destroyed. Destroyed things leave ruins, and ruins make people ask questions.

I counted forty-one statues on the way up. By statue thirty I understood what I was looking at, and by statue forty-one I was doing arithmetic I didn't want to finish. A country erased from every chart needs somewhere to put the pieces it cuts away. The gods hadn't thrown Ashvale's stolen names into the sea.

They'd filed them.

The gates were bronze, thirty feet high, worked with scenes of the founding — the surviving gods dividing the world between them like heirs reading a will, each holding a corner of a cloth that covered something beneath. The sculptor had been careful to leave the covered thing vague. A hill? A body? A door? I stood there a long moment, letting the wind bite my face until I looked properly cold and travel-worn rather than properly terrified, and then I knocked.

Knocking on a god's front gate is a specific kind of absurdity, and I recommend it to anyone who wants to feel briefly immortal. The sound went on and on, rolling down the mountain behind me like I'd struck a bell the size of the sky.

A door opened within the doors — a human-sized door set into the divine one, which tells you everything about how gods actually run their affairs. The person who answered was a clerk. Grey robe, grey face, ink-stained fingers, eyes that had the particular flatness of someone who has catalogued ten thousand miracles and found nine thousand nine hundred of them repetitive.

"Business," he said. Not a question. A category.

I produced my credentials. "Odile Vasseur-Quenel, Third Assistant, Office of Cartographic Reconciliation. I'm here to certify the archive's territorial holdings against the imperial register." I let a beat pass. "There's a discrepancy."

There is always a discrepancy. This is the first law of bureaucracy, and it works on gods precisely because gods invented it. The clerk's flat eyes flicked to my seal, smelled the incense, found it orthodox enough, and stepped aside.

"Third gallery," he said. "Don't touch anything that hums."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the house of gods: it smells like a library.

I had expected thunder, or myrrh, or the ozone stink of immanent divinity. Instead: dust, lamp oil, vellum, and the faint vinegary tang of old ink. Corridors of shelves rising past the reach of lamplight. Clerks gliding between them with the unhurried gait of people whose workday is eternity. Somewhere far off, a bell tolled a schedule no one hurried to meet.

The small gods were everywhere, and they were *shelved*.

That's the only word for it. Alcove after alcove held them — not statues this time but vessels, jars, lamps, bells, little stone figures with their faces intact because here, in the deep archive, forgetting wasn't necessary. Nothing gets forgotten in a place no one visits. Each alcove bore a label in a script I half recognized from Sefa's songs: *the god of the threshold at dusk. the god of spilled milk, merciful. the god of names spoken wrong on purpose, in love.*

One alcove was dark. Its label read: *VEYRA. THE ONE WHO CHOSE SILENCE.* The vessel inside was a plain clay lamp, unlit, cold, ordinary — and I swear to you, standing in that corridor with my forged papers sweating against my ribs, I felt watched by it more keenly than I had felt watched by anything in my life. Not hostile. *Hopeful.* Like a dog that has been waiting by a door for thirty years and has not yet learned to stop.

I touched the shelf edge — one finger, quick, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal — and whispered, "Hold on. She's coming for you."

The lamp did nothing. But the cold around it shifted, very slightly, like breath drawn in.

Then a bell rang somewhere above, and I remembered that hopeful dogs die waiting if nobody moves, and I went to find the territorial registry.

* * *

The registry occupied a rotunda at the heart of the mountain, ringed with drawers — thousands of them, cedar and iron, labeled in that same sliding script. An index table stood in the center with a ledger chained to it. Two clerks dozed at their desks. The gods, I noted, were conspicuously absent from their own filing system. Divinity, it turns out, delegates.

*Ashvale* would not be under *Ashvale*. Erased countries don't get honest labels. I thought about how I'd file something I was ashamed of, if I were a god with eternity and no supervision, and pulled the index ledger toward me.

Under **H**, for *Hidden*: nothing. Under **S**, for *Sealed*: a drawer number, 4,411, crossed out and renumbered 9,002. Under **W**, for *Witnesses*—

I stopped breathing.

*Witnesses (the matter of). See also: the crime at the founding. Access: principal gods only. Disposition: pending final winter.*

Pending *final winter*. I copied the number — 9,002 — onto my cuff in pencil, because a surveyor might annotate her sleeve, and walked as slowly as furniture toward drawer 9,002, and my heart was doing the thing hearts do when the body knows danger before the mind consents to it.

The drawer was heavy. Inside lay scrolls wrapped in oilcloth, dozens of them, each tagged with a name in a hand that trembled slightly, as if the writer had been crying while working. I unwrapped one.

It was a name. Just a name — a true name, the kind Sefa sang fragments of, the kind that made the chapel walls remember. *Maren of the Terraces, who kept bees on the roof of the world and fed the first swarm sugar-water from her wedding cup.* Below the name, in smaller script: *witness. retained per protocol. do not release.*

Protocol. They had protocols for keeping witnesses. Witnesses to what — the crime at the founding, whatever it was, whatever Veyra had hidden a whole country to protect them from seeing punished. These weren't prisoners. These were *loose ends*, filed alphabetically by the sound of their suffering.

I unwrapped another. And another. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd assumed they wouldn't be. *Tomasin Vale, who argued with a god about flood rights and won, and was therefore dangerous.* *Ilse of the Ford, midwife, attended the birth of the godling Erevan and saw what the child was.* *The Choir of Ashvale Cathedral, forty-one voices, present at the singing-down of the sun's first name.*

Forty-one voices. Forty-one defaced statues on the road up the mountain. I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding and it finished itself, ugly and complete: they hadn't just hidden Ashvale. They had harvested it. Every person who had seen too much, loved too loudly, witnessed the founding crime with open eyes — pulled out of the country like splinters, named, wrapped, and shelved. The valley below the fog wall wasn't the wound. It was the bandage. The wound was in here, in cedar drawers, kept fresh forever.

And then I found the folder that wasn't names.

* * *

It sat at the back of the drawer, thicker than the rest, bound in grey leather with no title — because the title was presumably something like *THE PLAN WE CANNOT WRITE DOWN*, and gods, despite everything, have a sense of shame. Or self-preservation. With gods the two are difficult to distinguish.

I should tell you honestly: I hesitated. There is a line every thief knows, the line between stealing a thing and learning a thing, and learning is the one you can't give back. Once I read what was in that folder, I would carry it out of this mountain in my head, and heads are not oilcloth. Heads cannot be re-shelved.

I opened it.

The first pages were ledgers — temperatures, snowfall depths, dates. The long winter, tracked year by year since the erasure, measured with the loving precision of a gardener watching a crop come in. Someone had annotated the margins in red ink. *Slower than hoped.* *Adjusting.* *The fog advances 40 rods this decade; acceptable.* And then, on a page dated eleven years ago, a single sentence in a different hand, larger, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*Let it finish. When the last field dies and the last fire goes out, the valley will bury itself, and the witnesses will freeze quiet in their drawers, and no Door will matter because there will be no one left to knock.*

They weren't hiding Ashvale anymore. Hiding had been the old plan, Veyra's plan, mercy wearing the mask of theft. This was the new plan, written in some god's impatient hand: *wait*. Let the winter do what armies couldn't. A country that no longer exists cannot be missed; a people who freeze to death leave no witnesses to free. The fog wall wasn't a prison boundary.

It was a lid settling over a grave, at the pace of glaciers, on purpose.

I stood in the rotunda with the folder open in my hands and heard — or imagined I heard, though I no longer trust that distinction in holy places — the wind outside begin to change. Deeper. Colder. The sound of a season arriving ahead of its appointment.

There was a date at the bottom of the final page. Not a day and month; winters like this don't keep calendars. Just a condition, written plainly, the way executioners write plainly:

*Upon the death of the last hearth-fire in Ashvale, the matter concludes.*

The last hearth-fire. I thought of Sefa's kitchen, the banned rites, the bread she'd baked us with her crooked hands. I thought of the chapel refugees, of Yara's campfire temper, of Bren's terrible jokes told over a cookpot. Somewhere in that buried valley, right now, fires were burning. Counting down.

How many fires does it take to keep a country alive? Nobody knows. Nobody has ever needed to know, because no one has ever run the experiment in reverse. But the gods had run it forward, patiently, for a generation, and their margin notes said *acceptable*.

I put the folder back exactly as I'd found it. I rewrapped the name-scrolls. I closed drawer 9,002 and stood a moment with my palm flat on the cedar, apologizing to everyone inside it.

Then I took one scroll.

Just one. The tag read: *MAREN OF THE TERRACES.* I chose it because it was nearest, because thieves take the nearest thing when the clock starts, and because — this is the true reason, and I'll own it — the fragment of her story I could read fit in a single glance, and a woman who keeps bees deserves better than a drawer.

Down my sleeve it went, into the lining of my coat, where surveyors are known to carry spare pencils and worse habits.

* * *

I made it four corridors before the mountain noticed.

I want to be clear that I did nothing wrong, procedurally. I walked at furniture speed. I nodded at clerks. I even paused to admire a display case of consecrated rulers, because boredom is camouflage and I committed to the bit. But the lamp in VEYRA's dark alcove — the cold one, the hopeful one — had felt my touch earlier, and something of that touch had traveled, the way a shout travels through a wire fence, and when I passed the alcove again on my way out the whole corridor was *colder*, and the clerks were standing still, all of them, faces turned toward the dark vessel like compass needles.

"The silence moved," one said.

"It hasn't moved in thirty years," said another.

"Something touched it."

I did not run. Running is a confession. I adjusted my satchel, frowned at a shelf label as if it had misspelled something, and continued toward the human-sized door in the divine one at a pace I can only describe as aggressively unhurried.

Behind me, the cold followed. Not fast. Patient. The way winter follows a traveler — never gaining, never losing, absolutely certain.

The clerk at the gate looked up from his desk. His flat eyes went past me, down the corridor, to the frost blooming along the shelves in a thin advancing line, delicate as lace, precise as a signature. Then his eyes came back to me, and for the first time they were not flat.

"You," he said, "have disturbed an archived god."

"Third Assistants disturb things professionally," I said, and produced my credentials again, and his hand closed on my wrist instead of the papers, and his grip was cold as drawer-cedar, and somewhere above us a bell began ringing a schedule that people *did* hurry to meet.

What happened next happened quickly and I will report it without pride: I bit him. A clerk of the gods, bitten on the ink-stained hand by a forged surveyor from a country that doesn't exist. He shouted. I ran. Camouflage abandoned, dignity abandoned, the entire doctrine of boring discarded in favor of the oldest cartographer's truth, which is that the fastest route between two points is whatever lets you survive to draw the map.

Frost chased me down the mountain road. Past the forty-one faceless statues, past the gates I'd flattered myself I owned, out into the white country beyond the fog wall, where the snow was falling wrong — too early, too dry, too *deliberate*, flakes landing in rows like a ledger being ruled.

I rode two days without sleeping, and the winter rode beside me the whole way, and neither of us spoke.

* * *

The fog wall was thinner when I reached it. I could see light through it — real light, lantern-light, the orange flicker of hearth-fires on the far side — and I understood with a clarity that felt like falling that every one of those flames was now a number in a god's margin, counting down toward zero.

I found them at the rim: Yara pacing like a caged animal, Bren sharpening a knife that was already sharp, both of them turning at the same instant when my horse crashed through the frozen bracken. Yara's face did something complicated when she saw mine — relief first, then the swift recalculation of a woman reading weather.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm punctual for disasters," I said, swinging down. My legs nearly failed me; I let the horse take my weight a moment longer than dignity allowed. "Yara. Listen. I got a name — a true one, Maren of the Terraces, enough to start Odile's Great Work of Lockpicking the Divine. That part went beautifully."

"And the other part," Yara said. Not a question. She'd already seen it in my face. People who grew up serving a silent god learn to read silences fluently.

"They're not going to fight us," I said. "They're not going to hunt us, not really, not as a strategy. The pale riders, the patrols — that's theater. Their actual plan is simpler and much worse." I pulled my coat tighter against a cold that was coming through the fog wall now, seeping through like ink through cloth. "They're going to wait for winter to do it for them. The long winter finishes falling, the last hearth in Ashvale goes out, and the matter — their word, *the matter* — concludes. Everyone in that valley freezes quietly, and the witnesses in the drawers stay frozen, and no one is ever left to open any Door."

The silence that followed had weight. Behind us, the fog wall breathed. Through it, faintly, I could see the lights of the valley — hundreds of them, thousands, a constellation of small stubborn fires.

Bren spoke first, voice stripped of every joke he'd ever told. "How long?"

"I don't know. The winter's ahead of schedule. The fog's closing faster than their own margins predicted, which means either their plan is failing or accelerating, and I genuinely cannot tell which is worse." I reached into my coat lining and drew out the scroll, Maren's name, warm somehow against all that cold. "But I know this: every fire we light buys time. And every fire we lose—"

The fog wall shuddered. All along the rim, the mist drew inward, smooth as a sheet being pulled, and where it had stood a hundred feet out it now stood eighty, and through the gap I heard — distant, tiny, unmistakable — a church bell in the valley below ringing the alarm for an early frost.

Yara took the scroll from my hand.

"Then we stop waiting," she said, "and we go get the rest of the names."
