## 15. Winter Finishes Falling

The first frost came on the ninth day of Harvestmonth, which any farmer in Ashvale could have told you was a crime. Frost belongs to the eleventh week, after the last of the barley is in and the turnips have had their final insult of sun. On the ninth day it should still be warm enough to sweat through your shirt while you work. Instead I woke in the chapel loft to a silence that had teeth, and when I breathed out over the edge of the sleeping platform, my breath hung in the air like a small ghost that couldn't find its way home.

"Odile," I said. "Your maps are wrong."

She didn't even open her eyes. "My maps are always wrong. That's what makes them useful." A pause. Then her eyes did open, sharp as a compass needle, because she'd done the same arithmetic I had. "That's not right," she said quietly. "It's too early. By weeks."

Below us, the buried chapel smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and the particular sourness of too many people learning to share. We'd been here eleven days since Sefa burned her relics for us. Eleven days of refugees trickling down the goat track from Hollowmere and the river villages, each one carrying less than the one before, each one asking the same question — *is it true, is there something under the valley, do you know what's happening* — and me giving the only honest answer I had: *no*.

I'd led soldiers. I'd guarded caravans through bandit country and sat three nights in a shrine waiting for a god who wouldn't speak. None of it had prepared me for being asked questions by a miller holding his daughter's hand and having nothing but *no* to give him.

I got up. The floorboards were cold enough to hurt through my boots, which was also wrong, and I was starting to keep a list.

* * *

By midday we could see the frost damage from the chapel's hidden entrance — the slit in the hillside where Sefa used to hang her washing, back when she had washing to hang. The terraced fields below us, the ones that fed half the western villages, wore a rime of white that should have been six weeks away. And it wasn't sitting on the plants the way honest frost does. Honest frost lies on top; you knock it off and the plant underneath is fine, sulking maybe, but fine. This frost had gone *into* things. The barley stalks stood black at the joints, like something had reached up out of the soil and pinched them.

"They're dead," Bren said beside me. He said it the way he said everything now, flat and careful, a man rationing his own voice. "All of it. Look at the pattern — it came up the valley from the rim."

I looked. He was right, and I hated him for it. The blackening ran in a wedge, widest at the fog wall far to the north and narrowing south toward us, like a spill spreading across a tablecloth. Like the winter outside wasn't waiting for the season anymore. It was walking in.

"The fog's moving too," Odile said. She'd climbed onto the rocks above the entrance with her spyglass, wrapped in two cloaks and a blanket she refused to admit was a blanket. "Yesterday the wall stood at the old survey line. Today—" she lowered the glass, and for once there was no wryness anywhere in her face, "—today it stands at the burnt oak. That's four miles. In one day."

Four miles a day. Ashvale was maybe ninety miles corner to corner. I did the sum the way you do sums you don't want to do, quickly and then again slowly, hoping for a different answer both times.

"Twenty-two days," I said.

"Less, if it speeds up." Odile folded the spyglass with more force than the instrument deserved. "And it will speed up. Walls don't creep. They're pushed."

Bren looked at me. I looked at the dead fields. Somewhere behind us, inside the hill, a child started crying and was hushed by someone who wasn't its mother, because its mother hadn't made it off the river road, and the someone who wasn't its mother had been crying herself an hour ago and had decided, apparently, to stop.

"That's the third family today," Bren said. "Word's spreading faster than the cold now."

"Then we're going to need more of everything," I said, "and I haven't the faintest idea where any of it comes from."

"Well," said Odile, swinging down from the rocks, "on the bright side, nobody will ever accuse us of overpromising."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about leading people: it looks exactly like not leading them, except you're the one who has to decide things, and deciding things is just choosing which mistake to apologize for later.

The chapel had been built to hold a congregation of perhaps sixty. By the fifth night of the frost we were past two hundred. People slept in shifts in the nave, packed on straw and Sefa's ancient stores of sacking, breathing steam into air that grew warmer and fouler by the hour, which was the single mercy of the situation: whatever else the deep cold was doing outside, it hadn't found the door yet.

Sefa's kitchen hearth — the real heart of the place, the room where banned rites had been kept warm for forty years like coals under ash — became everything at once: infirmary, cookhouse, council chamber, and on the worst nights, a place where people who couldn't sleep sat in a ring around the fire and let the light convince them, briefly, that the world was still shaped normally.

It was there I learned the refugees' names, mostly against my will, mostly because they insisted.

There was Tammas Reeve, the miller, whose hands shook until you put something in them — a hammer, a ladle, a crying child — and then went perfectly steady, because some men are only built to work. There was his daughter Liss, seven years old, who had appointed herself Odile's assistant and followed the cartographer everywhere demanding to know what the little symbols meant, and Odile, who claimed to dislike children with the fervency of someone who'd never actually met one, had begun teaching her map-letters in the corners of spare parchment.

There were the Kettleworth brothers, drovers, enormous and gentle, who took over the water hauling without being asked because hauling was what their bodies were for. There was Widow Corrag, who remembered more of Sefa's songs than Sefa did and sang them in a voice like a gate hinge, and the young priest-of-nothing named Ellery who'd been defrocked by a god who no longer existed to defrock him, and who spent his days listening — really listening — to people, which turned out to be a skill, like carpentry.

And there was Maren. Sixteen, alone, walked out of the northern villages with frostbitten fingers wrapped in rags and a stare like a drawn bow. She'd seen the fog wall eat her family's steading. She didn't talk about it. She talked about nothing, in fact, except one thing, said once, flatly, the way you'd report the weather: "It wasn't fog. It had faces in it. My da said don't look, so I didn't, but I heard it. It sounded like prayers."

Nobody asked her anything after that. But everyone watched her, the way you watch a wound that hasn't decided whether to close.

"You can't feed two hundred people on hope and sacking," Bren told me on the sixth night, in the low doorway between the kitchen and the nave, both of us speaking quietly because quiet was contagious in the best way left to us. "We've got maybe nine days of stores if we stretch, twelve if we eat like soldiers instead of saints, and the gardens outside are already black."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've been standing in that kitchen handing out bread like a woman who has a plan, and I've known you twenty years, Yara, and I know what your plans look like, and this isn't one."

He was right, which was annoying. I looked past him at the nave, at the banked fires and the sleeping shapes, at Liss asleep against Tammas's chest with a scrap of parchment covered in Odile's map-letters clutched in her fist like a talisman.

"What would you do?" I asked. "Honestly."

"Honestly?" Bren rubbed his jaw. "I'd pick leaders. Not one — a dozen. Put the Kettleworths on stores, put Corrag and Sefa on the sick, put Ellery on the grieving, because he's better at it than any of us and it frees the rest of us. And you—" he pointed at me, and his finger was steady even though his voice wasn't quite, "you stop trying to be everyone's mother and go be the thing you actually are. We're going to need a mercenary captain before this is done. The cold isn't the worst thing coming. You know that. Maren knows it. Whatever's in that fog answered prayers once, and I don't think it stopped."

I wanted to argue. I was very good at arguing; it was practically my trade. But the truth was I'd been awake three nights running doing arithmetic of a different kind — how many able-bodied adults, how many weapons, how many days — and every sum came out the same shape: *not enough*, with a margin of error measured in corpses.

"All right," I said.

"'All right' what?"

"All right, Captain." His eyebrows went up. "You held a company together for fifteen years on bad pay and worse gods. Take the stores, take the watches, take the work crews. Organize them. I'll organize the other thing."

"Which is?"

"The part with faces in it."

He nodded slowly, and something passed between us that we didn't say out loud, because saying it would have made it a promise and promises were expensive that winter. Then he went to do it, because Bren had never in his life met a problem that couldn't be improved by a rota.

* * *

The seventh night, the fog crested the hills above Hollowmere.

We saw it from the high ground behind the chapel, a couple dozen of us who shouldn't have been outside and went anyway, because you cannot ask people not to look at the end of the world. It came over the ridgeline not like weather but like a tide, silent and total, pouring down the near slopes and swallowing the village lights below one by one. No wind carried it. It moved against the wind, if anything, flowing uphill in places, and where it touched the last farms the windows went dark all at once, not flickering out but *choosing* to be dark.

And it glowed. Faintly, from within — a pale luminescence like moonlight through linen, and shapes moved in it. I didn't look long. Maren had been right. Looking felt like being read.

"It's slowing," Odile said. She had the spyglass up and her lips moving through numbers. "It took Hollowmere in minutes but it's thickening as it goes — eating the valley takes effort, or interest. At this rate we have ten days. Maybe eight."

"Eight days," said Tammas Reeve behind me. He'd come up without a torch, a big shadow among shadows, and his voice was the level voice of a man who had decided to be calm the way you decide to carry a heavy load — deliberately, knowing it costs. "Eight days, and then what? We die in a hole in a hill?"

"No," I said.

It came out before I'd decided to say it, which is how most of my decisions worked that winter. Two hundred people downstairs, and a man asking *and then what*, and every instinct I had — the mercenary instinct, the shrinedaughter instinct, the oldest-Yara instinct, the one that had taken a contract from a silent god at seventeen because somebody had to stand somewhere — all of it answered at once.

"We leave before it closes," I said. "The Door opens out of Ashvale entirely. Out of the fog, out of the winter, out from under the gods who built the cage. Veyra said find it before the winter finishes falling, and the winter is finishing falling, so we find it, and we go, and we take everyone who can walk."

Silence on the hilltop. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"The Door's a story," Tammas said, but gently, the way you correct a child. "A song the old woman sings."

"The old woman's songs have been right so far." I turned and looked at him, and then at the others — the Kettleworths, Corrag, Ellery, a scatter of faces I knew by name now whether I'd wanted to or not. "Look, I won't dress it up. I don't know where the Door is. I don't know if it opens. I know the walls remember it, and the walls are under our feet, and I know that staying is dying on a schedule set by something with faces in its fog. So tomorrow we dig. All of us. Every hand that can hold a tool goes into those undercrofts, and we follow the murals, and we find the thing my god died hiding."

"And if it doesn't open?" Ellery asked softly.

"Then we'll have died digging toward daylight instead of sitting in the dark," I said, "and I've read enough company ledgers to know that's the better entry."

Someone laughed — a short, startled bark, Corrag I think — and it broke something loose in the group, some knot of terror that had been holding everyone rigid. Tammas put his huge hand on my shoulder, briefly, like setting a seal on a document.

"Tomorrow, then," he said.

I walked back down the hill ahead of them and thought: *that's it, then. That's how it happens. Nobody crowns you. One night you're just a woman standing in the cold lying convincingly about a plan, and the next morning two hundred people are following you into a hole in the ground.*

Bren fell into step beside me, reading my face the way he always had. "You okay?"

"No," I said. "But neither is anyone else, and I'm the best liar available."

"That's leadership," he said gravely. "I'll put it on the rota."

* * *

On the eighth morning, we dug.

I wish I could tell you it was heroic. Mostly it was dust and blisters and the Kettleworth brothers cheerfully moving stones that had no business being movable, and Odile crouched in the undercroft with Liss solemnly holding a candle, tracing the mural lines with chalk and muttering geometry at the walls like a woman negotiating with them. The murals ran deeper than we'd mapped — past the chapel proper, down a corridor of painted stone where the figures grew older and stranger, and at the end of it, half-buried in rubble from some ancient collapse, a carved archway that was either the Door itself or the Door's front porch.

Odile knelt before it for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone strange and reverent and annoyed all at once, which is a combination only Odile can manage.

"There's a lock," she said. "A name-lock. It wants words. True names — the ones the loud gods stole, the ones in their archive." She looked back at me, candlelight jumping in her eyes, and I watched her arrive at the same sum I had. "Yara. I know where those names are. I've been inside that mountain. I have credentials that'll pass, forged well enough that I almost believe them myself."

"No," I said.

"It's four days' hard travel to the rim, and the fog's closing from every direction at once—"

"*No.*"

"Yara." She stood, and brushed the dust off her knees with the brisk precision she used for feelings. "Every day we spend digging, that wall walks another four miles. Someone has to go get the key, and I am the only person alive who can walk into that archive and not be a stranger there. You're the leader. Leaders don't get to protect everybody. They get to choose who risks what. Choose."

Behind her, Liss held the candle and watched us with wide eyes, too young to understand and old enough, in the way of children that winter, to understand everything.

The fog glowed beyond the hills. The archway waited under our feet, hungry for names. And somewhere in the dark of the mountain archive, in drawers labeled by gods who had erased a whole country to hide their crime, lay the true name of home.

"Three days," I said finally, because I couldn't make myself say *no* twice. "If you're not back in three days, I'm coming in after you, and I fight dirty."

"Wonderful," said Odile, shouldering her pack. "Try not to start a war before breakfast."

She kissed Liss on the forehead, which surprised everyone including Odile, gave Bren her spare maps, and walked up out of the chapel into the white and gathering dark, alone, with a lie for credentials and the truth in her bag.

That night the frost reached the chapel's threshold and stopped, as if considering. And on the ninth morning, we woke to find the first pale rider's tracks in the snow outside — hoofprints that began in the middle of an untouched field, heading toward us, and did not come from anywhere at all.
