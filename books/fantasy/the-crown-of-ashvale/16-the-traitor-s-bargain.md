## 16. The Traitor's Bargain

I've broken bread with killers, liars, and at least one man who sold his own mother's house out from under her while she was still living in it. I thought I'd seen every shape a betrayal could take.

Then I saw one wearing Doss Marren's face.

* * *

It started with the snow going quiet.

You'd think snow couldn't be loud, but you'd be wrong. The long winter had a sound to it — a low grinding whisper, like the world being filed down — and for three days before everything went wrong, that sound stopped. The cold just sat there. Listening.

Yara noticed it first. She always notices things first and then pretends she didn't so the rest of us can pretend we're useful. "The riders have stopped circling," she said, standing in the chapel doorway with her breath hanging around her like a shroud. "They're not hunting anymore. They're waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For someone to tell them where we are."

I laughed. It was the wrong laugh, the kind that comes out of you when your body knows something your head hasn't caught up to yet. Nobody in the chapel knew where we were except the company. And the company was family. Thirty years of shared rations and shared graves. You don't sell family.

You don't. That's the whole point of the word.

* * *

The council happened in the undercroft, by candlelight, because candles felt safer than the hearth upstairs where Sefa's relics used to burn. Forty of us crammed into a room built for wine casks, and the air was thick enough to chew.

The factions had already formed themselves, the way they do — not with speeches but with seating. Yara's people sat near the stairs. The others clustered around Harl Vetch, who was our best tracker and our worst man, in roughly equal measure.

"We can't win," Harl said. Not cruelly. That was the trick of him; he never sounded cruel. He sounded reasonable, which is far more dangerous. "Two hundred riders in white, and they don't die properly when you kill them. We've got forty fighters, sixty refugees, and a god who spent thirty years not returning our calls."

"She returned them eventually," someone said.

"And look how that's going." Harl spread his hands. "I'm not saying surrender. I'm saying there's a middle road. The riders offered terms at the ford last week. Safe passage out of the valley for anyone who lays down arms. Passage *through* the fog, which is more than any of us could buy with all the coin in Ashvale."

"They offered *you* terms?" Yara's voice was very quiet. Quiet-Yara was worse than loud-Yara. Loud-Yara shouted at you. Quiet-Yara started digging your grave in her head.

"A herald came. Spoke fair enough."

"What did it cost?"

Harl looked at her, and then — this is the part I keep replaying, the part that should have warned me — he looked at me. Just for a heartbeat. Just long enough.

"Nothing yet," he said.

* * *

Doss found me that night on the wall walk above the chapel door. He brought two cups of the barley tea that passes for comfort in the deep winter, and handed me one, and we stood there watching the fog wall creep another few yards closer, the way it did now every night, patient as debt.

Thirty years I'd known Doss Marren. He'd been the first to swear into the company after me, back when Captain Aldric still ran it and Veyra's altar was already cold and none of us knew why we kept guarding shrines to a god who wouldn't speak. Doss had pulled an arrow out of my shoulder with his teeth gritted against my screaming. I'd carried him three miles on my back when his leg broke on the Kelder scree. His daughter, Liss, had learned her letters sitting on my knee, tracing the ledger marks.

"You're going to do something stupid," Doss said.

"That's usually your department."

"I mean it, Bren." He wasn't smiling. Doss was always smiling; it was his whole architecture. A face without a smile on it was a stranger wearing his skin. "Harl's taking terms. Tonight. He's got eleven with him, maybe twelve if Tamsin's husband comes."

The tea turned to lead in my stomach. "Taking terms where."

"You know where." He wouldn't look at me. "The riders need the chapel's location confirmed. Where the Door-work is happening. Where the girl with the maps is working. In exchange — passage. Real passage. Out through the fog, past the winter, down to the lowlands where the sun still behaves."

"Doss." My voice came out strange. Flat. Like someone else's. "There is no out. That's the entire point of everything we've learned since autumn. There is no out. There's only the Door, and the Door belongs to all of us or none of us."

"The Door's a story Yara tells because she talked to a god once." He said it gently. That was the worst part. He said it the way you'd correct a child about the tooth-fairy. "Bren. I love her too. I've followed her into worse holes than this. But she's asking us to die in a hole in the ground for people we met six weeks ago, against soldiers who don't stay dead, for a country that — be honest with yourself — doesn't exist. Never did. Our grandfathers made it up, or somebody did, and we've been paying rent on a lie ever since."

"And the lowlands will take you? Pale riders' promises?"

"They showed Harl the way through. Fog parts for them like a curtain. Eleven of us walk out alive, Bren. Eleven. Versus forty of us frozen or ridden down by spring." He finally turned to me, and his eyes were wet, and I hated him for it, because tears meant he meant it, and meaning it was so much worse than malice. "Liss is down there with the refugees. She's nine years old. What am I supposed to tell her when the riders come through the doors? That Daddy believed in a dead god's apology?"

"You're supposed to tell her you stayed."

"And if staying kills her?" His voice cracked down the middle. "If staying kills everyone because some cartographer and some mercenary decided they knew better than the gods themselves? They *buried* this place for a reason, Bren. Maybe the reason was mercy. Maybe the reason was shame. But maybe — maybe — the reason was that whatever's behind that Door shouldn't come out. Did you ever think of that? Everyone's so busy hating the loud gods that nobody asks whether the cage was built around something dangerous instead of something precious."

I didn't have an answer. That's the truth of it. I stood there on the wall walk with my oldest friend and I didn't have an answer, because he was wrong and right at the same time, the way only people you love can be.

"Come with us," Doss said softly. "That's why I'm telling you. Harl wanted to just go. I made him give me the night. Come with us, Bren. Bring Liss's blanket-roll up from the refugee row yourself. We'll walk out through the fog together and let the rest of them keep their chapel and their Door and their god. Nobody has to bleed."

And there it was. The bargain, laid out plain. Not a knife at my throat — a door held open, with everyone I loved on both sides of it.

"No," I said.

"Bren—"

"No." Louder. "You want to know what I figured out in that archive, Doss? Under the floor, in the sealed room? I figured out that our grandparents didn't lie to us because they were wicked. They lied because they were *scared*, and being scared is no excuse, but it's human. And now Harl's scared, and you're scared, and you're both about to do the same thing our grandparents did — trade other people's lives for your own safety and call it prudence." I set down the tea. My hands were shaking. "I won't stop you. That's the rotten heart of it, isn't it? I can't stop you. Twelve armed men walking out of a chapel full of civilians — what am I going to do, fight you? Warn the riders myself? No. Go. Take your terms. But know what you're buying, because I've read the ledgers, and pale riders don't honor contracts. They honor usefulness. And the moment you're done being useful—"

"We'll take our chances." He drained his own cup and set it beside mine, upside down, the old field signal for *no more*. Thirty years of habit, right up to the end. "I'm sorry, Bren. If there'd been another road—"

"There's always another road. That's what roads are."

He walked away down the wall walk, and I watched him go, and I did not call after him, and to this day I don't know whether that was resolve or cowardice. Some nights it's one. Most nights it's the other.

* * *

I told Yara at dawn.

She listened without moving, which I should have expected, and then she asked the question I dreaded: "Did you try to stop them?"

"I tried to change his mind."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I said. "I didn't try to stop them."

She looked at me for a long time. Outside, the fog had crept to within a stone's throw of the chapel yard, and the light coming through it was the color of old bone. "Why not?"

Because twelve of mine against forty of ours would have been a massacre. Because half the refugees would have sided with Harl if it came to blades. Because I loved him. I opened my mouth to say all of it and what came out was simpler and truer:

"Because you can't arrest a man for wanting to go home. Even when home's a lie. Especially then."

Yara nodded slowly, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not forgiveness exactly, and not agreement. Recognition. "Then we'd better make sure the real home is worth staying for," she said, and went to wake Odile, because the name-fragment work couldn't wait, and neither could anything else, anymore.

* * *

They left at dusk. Twelve figures in company grays, moving north along the tree line toward the ford where the herald had promised the fog would open. I watched from the tower with a spyglass I didn't need, because you can see a betrayal fine from any distance.

What I hadn't counted on was how fast the riders moved once they had what they wanted.

The fog didn't part for Harl's twelve. It swallowed them — and then it *moved*, the whole gray wall of it, sliding south like a tide with intent, and out of it came the white shapes, dozens of them, riding not toward the ford but straight across the valley floor toward the chapel, and ahead of them, running, stumbling, falling, one figure in company gray with his arms waving, trying to turn them, trying to shout —

Doss.

I was down the tower stairs before I'd decided to move, shouting the alarm, and the chapel erupted into motion — Yara buckling her sword, Odile clutching the name-fragment to her chest like a coal she couldn't drop, refugees grabbing children, my mercenaries forming lines with the grim economy of people who've done this too many times.

At the gate, gasping, bleeding from the mouth, Doss fell into my arms.

"They took the others," he choked. "Took them into the fog. Harl — Harl gave them the location and they took *him* anyway, Bren, they took everyone, it was never a bargain, it was a *tally*—" He coughed red onto my coat. "I ran. Gods forgive me, I ran, and they let me run, because—" his hand closed on my collar, and his eyes were the most frightened I'd ever seen them, and Doss Marren had once held a severed artery closed with two fingers for four hours without flinching, "—because they wanted a messenger. They wanted me to come back and tell you."

"Tell me what?"

Behind him, at the edge of the yard, the fog had stopped. All of it, the whole creeping wall, standing still as a held breath. And out of that stillness rose a single voice, layered like a choir singing through ice:

**"SURRENDER THE MAP-MAKER AND THE NAMED ONE, AND THE REST MAY KEEP THEIR CHAPEL UNTIL SPRING."**

Doss sagged against me. "I'm sorry," he whispered. "Gods, Bren. I'm so sorry. I thought I was saving her."

I held my oldest friend while the enemy's offer hung over the yard, and inside the chapel I could hear Odile's stylus scratching against stone, faster now, racing a clock none of us could see.

Spring, they'd given us. Looking at the fog, at the riders massing silent behind it, at the sky going the wrong color for dusk —

I didn't believe we'd get the week.
