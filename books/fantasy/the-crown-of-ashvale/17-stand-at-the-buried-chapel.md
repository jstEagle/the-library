## 17. Stand at the Buried Chapel

The morning of the assault, I woke with Veyra's mark burning like a coal pressed to my wrist and knew, before anyone shouted, that today was the day.

That's the thing about a god who spent thirty years silent and then started talking again. You learn her moods fast. And this one — this slow, steady ache, like a tooth that's decided it's done pretending — meant *they're coming.*

I dressed in the dark. The chapel's undercroft was full of sleeping refugees, forty-odd souls packed between the old stone pews and the walls that remembered, breathing in shifts, children tucked between strangers because warmth was rationed now like everything else. Somewhere among them Odile was already awake. She never slept much the last week. She'd sit cross-legged on the floor with her maps and her stolen name-fragment spread out around her like a surgeon's tools, muttering syllables under her breath, testing them against the Door.

The Door was at the back of the chapel, behind the collapsed altar, a seam in the world that looked like nothing at all unless you knew to look. Which, I suppose, is the whole history of Ashvale in one sentence.

"Yara." Odile's voice came soft from the shadows. She didn't look up from her work. "It's close, isn't it."

"It's close."

"Then I need until midday. Maybe past it." Now she looked up, and in the grey light her eyes had the flat calm she wore like armor. I'd seen it crack exactly twice. "If they break through before—"

"They won't." I said it before she could argue. "Go back to work. That's your wall. This is mine."

She held my gaze a moment longer than was comfortable, the way she did when she was deciding whether to trust you with something true. Then she nodded once and turned back to her fragments of stolen language, and I went up the stairs to find Bren.

* * *

Bren was on the chapel roof, which wasn't a roof anymore so much as a suggestion of one, counting things the way he counted everything — arrows, blades, bodies able to stand. He'd gone grey at the temples since Sefa died. Not gradually. All at once, like a candle snuffed by wind.

"Tell me," he said, without turning around.

"Riders. Before noon. More than us."

"That's not news, Yara. That's every contract we've ever taken." He turned, and there it was: the arithmetic on his face, the same look he wore over the ledger in chapter — well. Over the ledger, always. "We've got eleven swords who'll stand. Twelve if you count Marta, and she shouldn't, she took a rider's lance through the shoulder at the ford. We've got maybe thirty bows worth trusting and a stockade that would embarrass a turnip farmer. Against them—" He stopped. Shook his head. "Against them we've got fog and faith, and only one of those is ours."

"The fog's theirs too," I said. "It always was. It's just finally working for us instead of against us."

He almost smiled. Almost. "And the refugees?"

"What about them?"

"There's a hollow, west side, past the spring. Deep enough to hide the little ones. If we put everyone who can't fight down there—"

"Bren." I climbed up beside him and looked east, where the fog stood against the sky like a tide deciding. "You want to know what I keep thinking about? Sefa. Last thing she ever said to me, shoving that song into my hands like it was a hot loaf. She said, *girl, some things are worth being burned for.* She stayed behind in that kitchen so the rest of us could run. So no. Nobody gets hidden away while the rest of us bleed. We stand together or we don't stand at all. That's the whole sermon."

"You're a terrible priest," Bren said.

"I'm worse than that. I'm an optimist."

* * *

We spent the morning making ready, and I will tell you honestly what war-preparation looks like when you're losing: it looks like people being kind to each other.

Marta insisted on standing anyway, arm bound in a sling of boiled leather, and when I told her to get below she looked at me with the particular stubbornness of someone half my age and twice as certain. "You said together," she said. "You don't get to unsay it when it's inconvenient."

Gods help me, I let her stand.

Old Tomas, who hadn't held anything sharper than a pruning hook in twenty years, sharpened the pruning hook. A woman named Ilse whose name I learned that morning — that's how it goes, you learn their names the day you might have to die for them — braided her daughter's hair and told her it was a game, hide-and-seek with the whole valley. The child believed her. Children believe what they must.

And through all of it, Odile worked. I checked on her twice. Both times she was surrounded by scraps of parchment covered in looping script that hurt to look at directly, like trying to read text reflected in moving water. Both times she waved me off without looking up. The second time she said, "Yara. When this works — when the Door opens — what do you think is on the other side?"

"Home," I said. "The real one. The one they stole."

"And if it's full of ghosts?"

I didn't have an answer for that yet. I still don't, fully. But I said, "Then we introduce ourselves politely and ask them to move over," and she laughed — actually laughed, short and startled, like the sound surprised her own mouth — and went back to work.

* * *

They came at noon exactly, because of course they did. Pale riders have no imagination. Why would they? They wear borrowed faces; imagination is the first thing to go.

They came out of the fog wall like teeth out of a jaw — not the dozen I'd braced for but closer to thirty, white horses, whiter men, faces I knew from shrine-crowds and answered prayers. One of them wore the face of the healer from Millbrook whose lame daughter had walked again in the first week of miracles. Another wore the face of old Father Aldous, dead ten years, smiling his dead smile. They arranged themselves on the ridge above the chapel with the unhurried confidence of wolves who've read the ending already.

A horn sounded once, long and low, and the sound went through my chest like a hook.

"Company!" Bren's voice carried down the slope, steady as bedrock. "You know the terms! Nothing gets past us to that door! Nothing!"

Eleven swords. Thirty bows. Forty souls behind us who met most of us weeks ago. Marta with her ruined shoulder. Tomas with his pruning hook. Ilse with her hands shaking and her feet planted.

And me, with a god-mark burning on my wrist and a lifetime of guarding things that turned out to be lies, finally standing in front of something true.

"Yara." Veyra spoke inside my skull, faint, like a voice through a wall. *Little shadow. I cannot fight for you. My strength is spent holding the Door shut from their side of knowing. What you do next, you do alone.*

"You've been telling me that my whole life," I muttered. "Get in line."

The riders came down.

* * *

I won't give you glory, because there wasn't any. War at this size isn't glory; it's arithmetic performed in blood, and mostly it's waiting.

The first wave hit our barricade of overturned pews and frozen earth and broke like water on rock — because rocks are patient and water isn't, and because Tomas, gods rest him, put his pruning hook through the eye of the first rider off his horse with the same unhurried skill he used on apple trees. Then the rider's neighbor ran Tomas through, and the second wave was already coming, and there was no time to grieve. There wouldn't be time to grieve for anybody until it was over, and grief was going to be a very crowded room.

I killed three riders in the first hour and I remember none of their faces except that each one wore somebody's face, somebody loved, somebody prayed to, and every time my blade went in I heard a voice — the real one underneath the borrowed skin — make a sound like a bell cracking.

That's how you kill them, if you're wondering. Not the body. The *wearing.* You have to mean it. You have to look into a dead man's smile and say *you are not him* and act on it with everything you have. Faith, again. It turns out I had more of it than I thought. It just never belonged to the gods. It belonged to us.

Bren fought beside me the whole day like he'd been born attached to my left side. At some point — hour two, hour three, I couldn't say — a rider's blade opened his cheek to the bone and he didn't even blink, just turned and took the man's horse out from under him and finished it on the ground. "Ledger's balanced!" he bellowed at me over the din, grinning through the blood, and I understood it was a joke, our oldest joke, and I laughed, actually laughed, in the middle of all of it, because what else do you do.

By late afternoon the line had bent but not broken. We'd lost four. Marta had taken a cut across the thigh and was fighting sitting down, which should have looked ridiculous and instead looked like the bravest thing I've ever seen. The refugees who could carry stones were carrying stones. Ilse had put an arrow through a rider's throat at forty paces and was crying while she nocked the next one, and nobody told her to stop crying, because crying and killing turn out to be skills that pair fine.

And through it all, from behind the broken altar, Odile's voice rose and fell — syllables in a dead language, the name-fragment unfolding itself into the lock of the world, and every rider on that field knew it, and every charge they made bent toward the chapel doors like iron filings toward a magnet, and every time we stepped into their path.

* * *

Dusk. The worst hour. The light dies and courage follows it down.

There were nine riders left, and they knew it, and they stopped pretending to be men. The borrowed faces slid off them like wax off a candle — I watched the healer's face melt into something older, greyer, a shape like hunger given posture — and the remaining nine came on foot, together, in silence, and the silence was worse than any horn.

"We can't hold them," Bren said. Not a complaint. An assessment. He was leaning on his sword and bleeding from somewhere under his mail and doing the arithmetic one last time. "Nine of them. Four of us still standing straight. Yara—"

"I know."

"If they reach the altar—"

"I *know*, Bren."

Veyra stirred in my skull, faint as a dying fire. *Little shadow. Let me spend what's left.*

"No," I said aloud. "You hold the Door. That was the bargain. That was always the bargain." And then, because apparently I'd become the kind of person who says these things: "Everyone behind me. Everyone. Marta, take the left with Bren. Ilse — arrows until your fingers quit, then pick up a rock. I'll take the center."

"You'll be flanked," Bren said.

"I'm always flanked. It's practically my signature."

He huffed something that wanted to be a laugh. Around me, the survivors of a mercenary company that had guarded empty shrines for coin moved into position to guard a miracle for free, and I felt — I don't know how else to say it — I felt *rich.* Poor as winter and rich as a queen, both at once.

The nine came on.

What happened next took maybe two minutes and lasted approximately forever. I remember pieces. Marta screaming as she drove her sword through a rider's knee. Bren taking a spear meant for me and turning it aside with his bare forearm, the crunch of it, the way he kept fighting one-handed without comment, as though pain were a tax he'd simply budgeted for. Ilse's last arrow. My own blade breaking — snapping clean at the midpoint — and me finishing the fight with the stub of it and a dagger and, at the end, my forehead, which is a technique no one teaches you and everyone discovers eventually.

When it was over, I was kneeling in the trampled snow with a broken sword and blood that was mostly not mine, and the last rider was ash dispersing on the wind, and the silence after battle settled over the chapel yard like snowfall.

Behind us, Odile's voice stopped.

For one terrible heartbeat I thought: *too late, we bled for nothing, the lock stayed shut—*

And then the world behind the broken altar lit up like dawn coming from underground, and the Door began to open.

* * *

I got up. That was the whole of it, really — the sum of the company, the sum of me. We got up.

Bren leaned on me and I leaned on him and we stumbled toward the light together, past Marta laughing weakly through her pain, past Ilse gathering her daughter out of the hollow, past Tomas laid out under a cloak with his pruning hook still in his hand, and I made myself a promise about grief: later. There would be time. Grief could wait its turn; it always could, it always would, and it would get its due.

At the threshold, Odile stood silhouetted in a light that had no business existing in Ashvale in winter, and she turned to me with tears cutting clean lines through the ash on her face, and she said:

"Yara. It's open. And there's someone here to see you."

Beyond her, through the opening Door, I saw sunlight. Green hills. A country whole and unburied, wearing its own face at last.

And walking toward me out of that sunlight, alive, breathing, smiling — a face I knew from murals, from songs, from thirty years of prayers thrown at a cold altar —

Someone who had no right to exist.

Someone the gods had sworn was dust.

My knees nearly went. Bren caught my elbow.

"Who—" I managed.

Odile's voice shook for the first time since I'd known her.

"That's the keeper of Ashvale, Yara. That's who they buried. And she says—" Odile swallowed. "She says the Door only opens one way, and someone has to stay on the far side of it, and she thinks it should be you."

The light waited. The country waited. And somewhere beneath my ribs, Veyra went very quiet, the way one does when the truth is finally standing in the doorway.
