## 21. What the World Remembers

The thaw came in nine days.

I know because I counted them, the way you count anything when you don't trust it — like a purse that's been light all winter and suddenly jingles. Nine days from ice on the chapel well to mud to green shoots coming up through ground that should have slept until midsummer. Old Marta stood in her field with her hands in the dirt and wept, and I couldn't tell if they were glad tears or frightened ones, and neither could she, and I think that was the truest thing anyone said that spring.

The world outside had noticed us by then. That's what Odile called it — *noticed* — as though Ashvale were a woman at a market who'd caught someone staring. The fog wall was gone. Had been gone since the night Yara unmade it, since the Door dissolved into ordinary stone and the gods' grip on our memories slipped like wet rope. And in its place: roads. Actual roads, worn by cart wheels, running down out of the passes toward a country that had never heard of us because we had never existed to be heard of.

First through the pass was a wool merchant named Corvin Hask, who rode into the valley square on a mule too small for him and asked, in all sincerity, where he might find the famous shrine of Veyra the Veiled, because pilgrims on his side of the mountains had been dreaming of it.

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, cracked sound, like a door hinge remembering how to move — and said, "You're standing in it."

He wasn't, quite. The shrine was rubble now, mostly. But the altar-stone was in the square, planted upright in fresh earth like a grave marker or a sapling, depending on your mood, and there were already candles at its base, and flowers, and one very confused offering of a smoked fish that nobody would claim.

That was the spring, more or less. Everything arriving at once, and none of it fitting.

* * *

The company had been forty-two strong before the riders came. We buried eleven of them behind the chapel, in ground that was frozen so hard we had to burn fires on the graves all night just to soften the earth enough to dig. I remember Kess doing the arithmetic of it with her boot toe in the snow — how many fires, how many hours, how much wood — because that was how she grieved, in numbers, because numbers didn't look back at you.

Eleven. And Sefa, who wasn't company but was ours anyway, burned in her own kitchen with her banned rites and her relics and her songs, buying us the hour we needed. There was nothing left to bury of Sefa except an iron spoon, which Odile keeps on her map table to this day and refuses to explain to visitors.

So: thirty-one of us, give or take the wounded, come spring. And no contracts. Because the god we'd served had faded into a warmth in the stones, and the valley we'd guarded had stopped being a country and become a question, and every mercenary company in the world knows what happens to soldiers without a war.

They'd started leaving in ones and twos even before the thaw. Daven went first, south over the pass with his shield strapped to a pack mule, saying he'd find work with some lordling who still paid in real coin and didn't ask him to die for abstractions. I couldn't blame him. I've never blamed any of them. You can't hold loyalty against a man whose loyalty has nowhere to stand.

But some stayed. Enough stayed. And on a grey morning in early spring, with mud sucking at everyone's boots, I stood up on the mounting block in the yard of what used to be the company hall and made the speech I'd been drafting in my head for weeks.

"Right," I said. "Listen up, because I'm only saying this once and half of you are hungover."

Kess snorted from the front row. Good. If Kess was laughing, the rest would follow.

"The Grey Company is done," I said. "Veyra kept our ledgers, in her way. She answered the muster. She was the reason half of you signed on — a god who pays her soldiers in purpose instead of coin, which sounds noble until you try to buy bread with it." A few grim laughs. "She's gone. Not dead — don't let anyone tell you she's dead — but gone the way a voice is gone after the singer leaves the room. What's left is us. Just us."

I let that sit. Mud dripped off the eaves. Somewhere a rooster was making an idiot of itself, which felt appropriate.

"So here's what I propose. We keep the name — not the Grey Company, the name doesn't fit anymore, we're not grey, we're whatever color you get when you dig a country out from under thirty years of forgetting. New name. New oath. Or no oath, maybe, because oaths are how gods get their hooks in." I held up my hand before anyone could object. "Here's the work, plain: there's a Door in that hillside. It doesn't open anymore — Yara saw to that — but it's still the seam. The place where we were cut out of the world and sewn back in. People will come to it. Pilgrims, scholars, fools, thieves, and worse than thieves, because now that the loud gods have lost their archive, some of them will want to know exactly what got loose when it fell. Somebody has to stand between this valley and everything that wants something from it. Not for a god. For the people sleeping behind us."

Silence. Then old Tammas, who'd been with the company longer than me and had opinions older than both of us combined, spat thoughtfully into the mud and said, "Sworn to no god. Sworn to what, then? Each other?"

"To the threshold," I said. "That's the whole oath. The line stays held."

"And pay?" asked a young spearman I only knew as Ferro's boy.

"Pay comes from the valley council, once they stop arguing about whether they need us," I said. "Which will take all summer, knowing them. Until then, we eat what we've got and I'll sell my armor piece by piece before anyone goes hungry." I looked around the yard. "Who's in?"

You want to know the truth of that moment? Half of them stepped forward immediately, and the other half looked at their boots, and then Kess — bless her cold mercenary heart — said loudly, "Well, someone has to keep Bren from writing the roster himself, he alphabetizes," and the rest came forward laughing, and that was that. Thirty-one became twenty-six became, by summer, thirty-four, because word travels and there are always people looking for a company that means what it says.

We called ourselves the Threshold Guard. Odile drew our badge: a doorway, open, with nothing on either side of it. When I asked her why nothing, she said, "Because that's the point, isn't it? What matters is that it stands open." Cartographers. They think in symbols the way normal people think in sentences.

* * *

Not everyone wanted us, of course. That was the other lesson of spring.

There's a family up in the high terraces — the Ellerys, farmers going back four generations as far as anyone can prove, which is now not very far — and old Widow Ellery came down to the square in the second week of the thaw and stood in front of the altar-stone with her arms crossed like a woman guarding a gate that no longer existed.

"You've let the world in," she said. Not to me particularly; to all of us, to the whole muddy square. "Thirty years the gods kept us safe and quiet, and now there's merchants on the road and strangers asking questions and my grandson talks about leaving. *Leaving.* He's never left. Nobody's ever left. That was the whole—" she stopped, jaw working, and I watched her decide not to cry in public. "That was the whole point of this place."

"It was a prison, mistress," I said, as gently as I could, which is not very.

"It was *home,*" she said, "and home is allowed to be small," and she turned and walked back up the terraces, and her grandson did leave, in June, with the wool merchant's caravan, and sent back a letter in autumn that made her cry in private, which I know because she told Yara, and Yara told me, and I've decided it's not gossip if three people carry it.

You can't argue with that grief. I've tried, in the quiet hours, arguing both sides like a man playing chess against himself, and both sides win. The veil was a cage and the veil was a shelter and both things were true, and now the cage door is open and some people are standing in the wreckage of the bars wondering what they're supposed to do with all this sky. The loud gods took their memories and gave them safety. Yara gave them back their memories and took away the safety. Nobody asked the valley which it preferred, which is the oldest crime there is, and we committed it with the best intentions, which somehow makes it heavier, not lighter.

What you can do is stand in the square and listen. So that's what we do, part of the time. The Threshold Guard keeps the peace at the market days when the outside traders come, and half the job is breaking up fights and the other half is standing next to frightened old women while they shout at the sky, and I'm honestly not sure which half matters more.

* * *

Odile came back in late spring with maps.

Of course she did. She'd been gone two months, over the pass and into the wide world with a satchel of charts and that iron spoon wrapped in oilcloth like a relic, which I suppose it is. She arrived at the company hall at dusk, muddy to the hip, dropped her pack on my clean table, and said, "Your valley is on the maps now. Officially. Imperial Survey, third revision, ink not yet dry."

"How does it look?"

"Small," she said. "Wrong scale. They've put the mountains in the wrong place and spelled Ashvale with two s's." She sat down heavily and pulled off her boots with a groan that I felt in my own feet by sympathy. "I corrected it. Obviously. I may have also added a mountain range that doesn't exist, out of spite, in the northeast. Give it fifty years and someone will build a monastery there."

"That's fraud, Odile."

"That's *cartography,* Bren. All maps are arguments. I'm just honest about mine." She flexed her fingers, and I saw the new scar across the back of her right hand, thin and white, and didn't ask, because she hadn't offered, and that was the arrangement we'd always had. "The survey office wants to send a delegation. Proper one, with instruments and a chaplain. I told them the road was bad and the locals were hostile and I'd handle it myself for a modest fee."

"You lied to an imperial institution."

"I *negotiated* with an imperial institution. There's a difference and it's measured in fees." She leaned back and looked at the ceiling, and for a moment the wit dropped off her face like a mask slipping, and underneath she just looked tired, tired in the way you get when you've walked through the world that erased you and found it full of people who never knew you were missing. "They have songs, Bren. Out there. Old ones. Fragments — a harvest verse here, a lullaby there — things that leaked under the veil before it went up, or bled through after. Nobody knows what they're from. They think they're just old. And I stood in a tavern in Marrowford listening to a drunk fiddler play a tune Sefa used to hum in her kitchen, and he didn't know, and I couldn't tell him, and—"

She stopped. Wiped her eyes briskly with the heel of her hand, the way you'd swat a fly.

"Well," she said. "Anyway. The world remembers more than it knows it remembers. That's worth mapping too, someday. Songs. Where they survived and where they didn't. A different kind of atlas." She looked at me sideways. "Want to fund an expedition?"

"I want to fund dinner," I said. "Expedition after."

That's Odile. Grief goes in the drawer with the instruments, labeled, to be taken out later in private. I've learned not to open other people's drawers.

* * *

Yara I saw less, that spring, and understood why.

She lived near the square now, in a room above the chandler's, close to the altar-stone. Every morning she was down there before dawn, sweeping the ground around it, setting the candles straight, talking — I heard her once, from across the square, low and continuous, the way you talk to someone who can't answer but might be listening anyway. Whether Veyra heard, whether there was anything left of Veyra to hear, nobody knows. Odile says the god faded. Yara says fading isn't dying. I say I've stood watch over enough dying men to know that the last thing to go is the hearing, and if there's any mercy stitched into the fabric of the world, that holds for gods too.

In public she was the valley's reluctant hero, which sat on her like armor that didn't fit. The council wanted to give her things. Strangers wanted to touch her sleeve. Children followed her around the square chanting the Door-song that somebody had already composed, because the world moves fast now and memory is a marketplace. She bore it the way she bore everything, with that flat stare that made grown men find reasons to be elsewhere, and then she'd come by the hall in the evenings, sit on our bench without asking, eat whatever was in the pot, and argue tactics with Kess until midnight, and that was when she was actually resting — though she'd deny it with violence if you said so.

One evening in early summer she sat down across from me while I was doing the roster — yes, I alphabetize, and Kess can suffer — and said, without preamble:

"I'm going to walk the rim. All of it. Where the wall used to be."

"Why?"

"Because somebody should see it with their own eyes. All the way around. Know what's there now the fog's gone." She turned her cup on the table. "And because she walked it. When she set it up. Thirty years ago, alone, sealing us in. Someone should walk it back the other way. Un-sealing."

I looked at her. She looked at the cup.

"Take company?" I asked.

"No." And then, quieter, the flat stare lifting just enough to show the person underneath: "Ask me again in autumn. Maybe the answer's different."

I wrote her name at the top of the roster anyway. Leave of absence, indefinite. Some entries you keep even when the soldier's gone, because the roster is a kind of memory too, and we'd all had enough of memory being taken away by powers that claimed to know better.

* * *

Summer came on like a rumor confirmed. The traders multiplied. The council argued. The fields — Yara's old fields, the ones she'd worked as a girl before the mercenary life took her — came in heavy, almost showy, as though the land itself were trying to make amends. And the Door stayed shut, a slab of seamless stone in the hillside above the chapel ruins, warm to the touch and utterly silent, and every day somebody climbed up to check on it, and every day it was the same, and every day that sameness felt like a gift.

We trained the new recruits in the yard. We buried Tammas in July, peacefully, in his sleep, and gave him a Threshold Guard funeral with no prayers at all — just the muster roll read aloud, every name, his last, and Kess singing the marching song without the words, just the tune, which somehow hurt worse and healed better. We hired on a pair of brothers from over the pass who'd heard we were the only company in the mountains that had ever fought pale riders and won, and they were disappointed to learn the riders were ash now, and then relieved, and then good soldiers. We fixed the roof twice.

Grief settled into scar tissue, the way it does. You stop checking the wound every morning. You catch yourself laughing at something stupid and feel guilty for half a breath, and then you don't. Sefa's spoon sits on Odile's map table. Eleven names head every muster roll, read aloud on the first of each month, and the new recruits learn them before they learn anything else, because that's the whole oath, really, when you strip it down: the line holds, and we remember on our own terms, and no god, loud or silent, gets to edit us again.

It was a good summer. I want that written down somewhere, by someone who was there: after everything, it was a good summer, and we earned it.

Then, on the last day of harvest, a rider came up the valley road at a pace that meant trouble, wearing the colors of the imperial survey office, carrying dispatches sealed with the double-s of a spelling nobody in Ashvale uses.

And the seal was broken. Opened before it ever reached us. Which meant someone between Marrowford and here had read it — someone who knew what it concerned, and cared enough to risk imperial wrath to find out.

I broke the wax myself, in the hall, with Odile and Kess looking over my shoulders, and read it twice, and then handed it to Odile without a word, because my hands had gone strange.

The empire, it seemed, had received a petition. From within Ashvale. Signed by a name I knew — a name from the yard, from the bad winter, from the council of the divided company — requesting formal recognition, imperial protection, and, quote, *the removal of the armed band styling itself the Threshold Guard, who hold the valley's sole landmark by force and answer to no lawful authority.*

Kess read it over Odile's shoulder and said, very quietly, "That's Daven's hand."

It was. I'd seen it on a hundred contract forms. My oldest friend in the company, the first one to leave, had learned to write a finer fist somewhere in the south, and the first thing he'd written with it was a knife pointed at everything we'd built.

Odile set the letter down on the map table, next to Sefa's spoon, and smiled the smile she wore when negotiating with institutions, which is to say the smile of a woman measuring the distance to the nearest exit.

"Well," she said. "Someone's decided the Door needs a landlord after all. The only question left is whether it's going to be us — or the empire, with Daven holding the pen."

Outside, the harvest bells were ringing, and for the first time all year, they sounded to me like a warning.
