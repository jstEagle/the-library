## 22. The Crown of Ashvale

The crown was made in three days by a jeweler who had never made anything but wedding rings, and it showed. The band was too thick, the points uneven, and one of the seven stones — river amber, because Ashvale had no gold and never had — sat crooked as a drunk on a stool. It was, all things considered, the most honest object anyone had ever tried to put on my head.

They brought it to me in the town square on the first morning of true spring, when the frost had finally let go of the eaves and the gutters ran loud enough to talk over. The elders came in their good coats, the ones they'd saved for funerals and tax day, and old Maren Hallow carried the crown on a cushion that I recognized, with a lurch, as the altar cloth from Veyra's shrine. Someone had washed it. Someone had thought that was appropriate.

"Yara Montrose," Maren said, in the voice she probably used for calling children out of rivers. "Ashvale stands in the world again. The world knows our name. Somebody has to speak for us when the outside comes asking." She held out the crooked, beautiful, ridiculous thing. "We ask you to be our queen."

I looked at the crown. I looked at the square behind her, where half the town had gathered to watch, and where Bren stood with his arms crossed wearing the particular expression of a man watching someone else step on a rake he'd already stepped on twice.

"No," I said.

Maren blinked. "You haven't even—"

"It's a lovely crown," I said. "Truly. The crooked stone is my favorite part. But no."

"You refused the gods' order," said Tam Vetch, who had spent thirty years as the valley's most committed grumbler and had found new purpose as its most committed patriot. "You dragged two gods through a hole in the sky and made them look at what they did. You held a door against riders out of nightmare with forty mercenaries and a kitchen's worth of grandmothers. If anyone's earned a throne—"

"That's exactly why not," I said. "Everything you just listed, I did *against* authority. All of it. Every single thing I'm being thanked for was me telling someone powerful to go to hell. And now you want to make me the someone powerful." I spread my hands. "You'd be building the next prison and asking me to hold the keys. I've seen how that story goes. I was raised inside the last one."

The square went quiet in the way squares do when everyone is deciding whether to be offended. I let the silence sit. Then I knelt — not to Maren, not to the crown, but down to the cobbles themselves, and pressed my palm flat against the stone.

"But I'll tell you what I'll do instead," I said. "Because you're right about one thing. Somebody has to speak for us. Just not somebody wearing a crown. Somebody standing *here*, where everyone can see them, where they can't hide behind a throne or a title or a wall of fog. So bring me the altar-stone."

* * *

Veyra's altar-stone had spent the winter in the ruined shrine above the terraces, cold as it had been cold for thirty years before any of this started. We'd gone back to it in the first thaw, Odile and Bren and I, mostly because I couldn't stand the thought of it sitting alone up there in a roofless room full of bird droppings and old wax. I'd expected to feel something when I touched it. Grief, maybe. Or that terrible hollow gratitude I'd been carrying since she faded.

What I felt was nothing, which is its own kind of answer. The stone was just stone. Whatever had lived in it had gone where gods go when they're finally allowed to rest, and it hadn't left so much as a chill behind.

But stone remembers weight, if nothing else. Thirty years of pilgrims pressing their palms to it. A generation of prayers that went nowhere and were offered anyway, which is either the saddest thing I know or the most stubbornly beautiful, and I have never fully settled the question.

It took six men and a sledge to move it down into the square. We set it upright in the center, where the market stalls could be arranged around it and children would inevitably use it for climbing, and I stood back and looked at it and thought: yes. This. Not a crown. A rock. Something you can lean against.

Maren watched me work with her jaw set. "A memorial to a god," she said. "After everything. After she hid us. After she let us be forgotten."

"She didn't let us be forgotten," I said. "She made sure we'd forget, so we wouldn't grieve ourselves to death over a country that fell. That's not the same crime. And then she spent thirty years silent in a box because speaking would have cracked the wall, and the first thing she did with her voice was beg a stranger to save us." I wiped stone dust off my hands. "She lied to us to keep us alive, and then she died paying for it. I can think of worse epitaphs. Most kings can't manage half that."

"And you?" Maren asked, softer than I expected. "What do we call you, if not queen?"

"Yara," I said. "Just — ask me things. Sometimes I'll even answer."

* * *

Odile arrived while the mortar around the stone's base was still wet, which meant she arrived criticizing it.

"Your plumb line's off," she said, squatting beside me with her sleeves rolled and ink permanently stained into the creases of her fingers. "Half a degree east. In a hundred years your memorial will be leaning like a tired soldier and everyone will blame the masons."

"The masons *are* the problem," I agreed. "Terrible crew. One of them keeps talking back."

"I've heard she's insufferable." She sat down on the cobbles next to me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and for a while we just watched the square fill. Market day. The first real one. Traders from beyond the fog — actual traders, from actual elsewhere, with accents like broken pottery and goods nobody in Ashvale had seen in living memory — haggled in the shadow of the altar-stone while local farmers eyed their oranges with open suspicion. Two children were already climbing the stone, exactly as predicted. Their mother told them to get down. They did not get down.

"Have you decided?" I asked. Odile had a ship's berth waiting in Corvess and had been conspicuously not mentioning it for two weeks.

"I've decided my map is wrong," she said.

"Which map? You've drawn forty."

"The big one. The whole-world one." She pulled a folded sheet from her coat — the master chart, the one she'd carried through the fog wall and the archive and the siege, the one with a blank space in the middle labeled, in her neat hand, *ASHVALE — POSITION DISPUTED BY REALITY*. She smoothed it out between us. "I drew the valley back in. Right after the veil fell. And it's wrong."

"It looks right to me."

"The shape's right. The position's right. But look." She tapped the borders, where she'd drawn the mountains fading into the lowlands beyond. "I drew hard edges. Because that's what I knew — a country that ended where the fog began, a line you could put on paper. But that's not what it is anymore. There's no edge now. Roads run through. People come and go. Ashvale isn't a secret anymore; it's just a place." She took out her pen, crossed out *POSITION DISPUTED BY REALITY*, and wrote underneath: *HERE, LIKE EVERYWHERE ELSE.*

"There," she said. "That's better cartography. Terrible poetry, but better cartography."

"You're staying," I said.

"I'm staying *for now*," she corrected, with the precise dignity of a woman who had already unpacked everything she owned. "There's an entire continent that has never been properly surveyed past the Thornmarks, and I intend to fix that, and I intend to hire local guides, and the only local guide I trust is stubborn, cheap, and owes me her life several times over."

"I don't owe you my life. We're even. You counted."

"Then I'll invent a debt. I'm very good with records." She rolled up the map and stood, offering me her hand. "Come on. Bren's been pacing outside the meetinghouse for an hour pretending he isn't waiting for us. He gets worse every year he ages. By fifty he'll be entirely composed of pacing."

* * *

Bren had the company ledger open on the meetinghouse table, and I knew before I sat down what I'd see, because I'd helped him write it: the old page struck through, every unpaid contract marked VOID in his blocky hand, and beneath it a fresh heading in fresher ink.

**THE ASHVALE THRESHOLD COMPANY — CHARTER (DRAFT 9)**

"Draft nine," I said. "What happened to eight?"

"Had a clause letting us swear service to whoever held the Door. Odile pointed out that's just a god with extra steps." He turned the page toward me. "Read it. Both of you. Tear it apart. That's what this meeting is for."

I read. *We swear service to no god, no crown, and no border.* Good start. *Our charge is the threshold: the road, the Door, and all who pass through in peace.* Better. *We take payment in coin, goods, or honest gratitude, and refuse payment in worship.* I snorted. "That last one's going to cost us contracts."

"Then it costs us contracts," Bren said. "I buried eleven people this winter because some of our own thought a god's favor was worth more than a neighbor's life. I will happily starve before I price faith into a contract again."

Odile read in silence, then uncapped her pen. "Clause four. 'All members equal in council.' You'll regret that the first time the whole company votes to do something stupid."

"They'll regret it," Bren said. "That's how learning works. I'm not doing crowns either, Odile. Yara's made it contagious."

"Somebody should write that down," I said. "*The Contagion of Refusing Crowns.* Could sell copies beyond the fog. The outside world loves a cautionary tale about power; they've got so much of it."

Bren almost smiled. It was a narrow thing, worn thin by a winter of graves, but it was there. He signed the draft. Odile signed. I signed last, under the clause refusing worship, and felt something settle in my chest that I hadn't felt since the shrine went cold thirty years ago — not faith, exactly. Faith had been believing someone was listening. This was quieter. This was just believing we'd show up tomorrow, for each other, without anyone making us.

"We'll need recruits," Bren said. "Half the valley thinks we're heroes. The other half thinks we're trouble. Both halves make fine soldiers."

"Interview them yourself," I said. "I've got one more job first."

* * *

I went up to the shrine at dusk, alone, the way I had gone up a thousand times across a thousand years of winters that weren't actually that many years but felt like it. The roofless room. The cold hearth. The empty spot where the altar-stone used to sit, outlined in clean dust.

I'd brought nothing. No offering, no prayer. I'd given those up, or they'd been given up for me, and I wasn't sure the difference mattered anymore.

But I sat down on the ledge where I'd slept during that first contract, the one with the dying pilgrim, and I talked anyway. Not praying. Just talking. To the air, to the stone-shaped absence, to whatever was left of her wherever gods go when they're done.

"You'd hate the crown," I said. "You always said power was just a bigger kind of silence — that the more people had to listen to you, the less you could hear them. I think about that a lot lately. Mostly when Maren Hallow looks at me like I've disappointed her by having a spine." I pulled my knees up. "The stone's in the square now. Kids climb it. A trader from Corvess asked me yesterday whose grave it was, and I said, everybody's and nobody's, and he looked at me like I was mad, and honestly? Fair."

The wind moved through the roofless room. It didn't say anything. It was just wind. I'd learned the difference the hard way, and I wouldn't trade that knowledge for anything, including a god.

"Odile's redrawing the maps. Bren's rebuilding the company. Sefa's songs are being sung in the open again — badly, mostly, people remember fragments, but they're singing. That was the whole point, wasn't it? Not restoration. Not the old country back from the dead. Just... the living, keeping what's theirs." I rested my chin on my knees. "I'm not going to say thank you. You hated that. But I'm not going to say goodbye either, because you're not gone, exactly. You're just somewhere the prayers don't reach. And I know a little something about countries like that."

I stayed until the stars came out — real stars, unhidden, the same ones shining on Corvess and the Thornmarks and every place that had forgotten us and was now, slowly, clumsily, remembering. Then I climbed down through the terraces to the square, where the lamps were lit and the altar-stone stood leaning half a degree east, exactly as promised, and Odile was waiting on the meetinghouse steps with a lantern and a fresh sheet of paper.

"Well?" she said. "The road south needs surveying before the passes clear. Bren wants to leave in a week. Are you coming or not?"

I looked back once at the stone, at the lamplight on the crooked amber, at the town moving around it like water around a rock — not worshipping it, not fearing it, just living alongside it, which is the only monument anything deserves.

"Yeah," I said, and took the pen she handed me, and drew the first line of the road myself. "Let's go see what the world remembers."
