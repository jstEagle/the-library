## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The fishwife came in just before dusk, when the light through the cellar grate had gone the color of weak tea and Ivo was already thinking about closing.

He knew her type before she'd said a word. She stood at the top of the stairs a long moment, one hand on the rail, the other clutching a basket to her chest like a shield, looking down into his shop the way people looked down wells—half hoping, half afraid of what might look back. The shop didn't help. It was a low room of stone and shadow, shelves crammed with the harmless debris of a careful life: river stones polished smooth, knotted cords in seven colors, dried lavender bundled with red thread, little clay birds that were only little clay birds. Nothing here that would interest an inquisitor. Nothing here that could hang him.

"Are you open?" she called down. Her voice had the particular hollowness of someone who'd been crying recently and expected to again.

"Always," Ivo lied, because he wasn't, and because *always* sounded like a man with nothing to hide.

She came down the stairs sideways, the way big women did in small spaces, and filled most of the room when she arrived. Up close he could see the details: salt-cracked hands, a wedding ring worn thin as wire, eyes red-rimmed and darting to the door behind her every few breaths. A fishwife from the Tidewalk, by the smell of her. Brine and tar and something sweeter underneath—fear sweat, which Ivo had learned to distinguish from work sweat years ago. They smelled different. Fear had a sharpness to it, like vinegar.

"You're the charm-man," she said. Not a question. "Marta on Candle Lane says you're the charm-man."

"Marta on Candle Lane," Ivo said mildly, "says a great many things."

"I need a luck-knot." She set her basket on his counter with a thump that rattled his clay birds. "For my husband's boat. The *Gull's Mercy*. He goes out tomorrow with the tide and—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter. "Three boats went out last week. Two came back."

Ivo kept his face still. This was rule one, the oldest rule, the one he'd learned before he'd learned anything else worth knowing: *your face is a door you keep shut.* "And you think a knot will bring it back?"

"No." She wiped her nose with the back of her wrist. "I think a knot will make me feel like I did something. And I'll pay for that feeling, master, if you've got it to sell."

There it was. The honest version, which almost nobody gave him. Most customers wanted to believe; this woman knew better and wanted it anyway. Ivo found, as he often did, that he liked her for it.

"Luck-knots are three coppers," he said. "But I'll tell you what you're buying, because I don't sell lies. You're buying cord and a pattern and five minutes of my time. What it does after that is between you and whatever's listening."

"Whatever's listening," she repeated. Her voice dropped. "You mean the old listening. The sea-listening."

"I mean whatever's listening."

She glanced at the door again. Down here, with the grate going dark above them, the word *inquisitor* didn't need saying. It lived in Caerlum the way damp lived in stone—in everything, under everything, needing no name. Five years since the Edict of Ash closed the temples of the old craft and put the white-cloaked patrols on the streets. Five years since magic became the one crime that couldn't be bribed away, because the Crown had decided, all at once and forever, that the working of charms was treason against the throne itself. Nobody quite knew why. Nobody asked twice.

Ivo turned to his shelf of cords and let his hands do the thinking while his mouth kept the customer talking. That was rule two: *keep them talking about themselves.* People watching their own story unfold never watched your hands.

"Husband's name?" he asked.

"Tomas. Tomas Wren."

"And the boat's named for what? Someone's mercy?"

"His mother's. She drowned when he was nine." The fishwife laughed, short and bitter. "Family tradition, drowning. That's why I want the knot."

Ivo selected a length of grey-blue cord, the color of deep water, and began to work it between his fingers. And here was the part no one ever saw, the part that made his heart do its small guilty dance every single time, even after five years and some thousands of knots: he didn't just tie the knot. He couldn't help but tie the knot. His fingers moved through the pattern and beneath the pattern, and where a common charmer would cross the strands left-over-right, he crossed them left-under-right-and-through, a variation that existed in no book because books got their authors burned, and the cord warmed faintly against his skin—a warmth no one else in the room could feel—and something settled into the fibers like a seed pressed into soil.

A hedge-charm. A nothing-charm. Enough to tip a wave, to snag a net, to turn a capsize into a soaking. The kind of working so small that even the Crown's instruments, if they ever dangled one over his counter, would read it as coincidence or craftsmanship.

That was the whole architecture of his life, built grain by grain across five years: magic so small it hid inside ordinary skill. Sleight of hand as camouflage. He was not a wizard hiding among merchants. He was a mouse living inside the wall of a house, eating crumbs so tiny the cat never bothered to wake.

"There," he said, and laid the finished knot on the counter. To any eye it was a handsome piece of sailor's craft, the kind sold in a hundred stalls along the Tidewalk. "Grey-blue for deep water. Seven crossings for the seven winds. Tell Tomas to hang it off the mast, not the prow—the prow takes the punishment first."

The fishwife stared at it. Then at him. Then, slowly, she reached into her bodice and drew out a coin purse, and from the coin purse—not the coppers on top but the coins beneath, the ones people kept for real emergencies—a silver mark.

"That's too much," Ivo said.

"It's exactly enough." She pressed it into his palm and closed his fingers over it with both of her salt-rough hands, and for a moment she held his hand shut, and looked at him, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "Two boats came back, master. Out of three. My sister's man was on the one that didn't. If there's anything in the world still listening—if there's anything left in this city that remembers how to ask it kindly—then spend my silver well. And if there isn't"—her grip tightened—"then you take the silver anyway, for the feeling. Understand?"

"I understand," Ivo said, and meant it, and hated how much he meant it.

She gathered her basket and climbed the stairs, and paused at the top, silhouetted against the last grey light. "Marta said you were the real one," she said. "She said the others sell knots, and you sell knots that know they're knots." Then she was gone, and the door clicked, and Ivo stood alone in his cellar with a silver mark warming in his fist and the taste of that sentence sitting on his tongue like a stone.

*Knots that know they're knots.*

Rule three, he thought, setting the mark in the floorboard cache beneath the counter: *never let them say it out loud.* Marta on Candle Lane had been saying it out loud for two years now, sending him the frightened ones, the desperate ones, the ones who needed more than cord. Every referral was a thread, and threads could be followed, and somewhere up there in the lamplit streets a man in a white cloak with a patient voice was very good at following threads.

* * *

After dark, Ivo allowed himself the luxury of a candle and the necessity of counting.

The cache held eleven marks, forty-some coppers, and a cracked garnet he'd never been able to sell. Eleven marks. In a good month he cleared six. Rent on the cellar was two. Food, if he ate the way he ate—sparingly, standing up, without pleasure—was one. Which meant that if the patrols swept the Tidewalk quarter next, as they swept some quarter of the city every season, he had perhaps four months of running money.

Four months. He'd run on less. He'd run on nothing once, the night he'd left Maren's cottage with blistered feet and a head full of her last words chasing him down the mountain road like wolves. *You'll be back,* she'd said. Or maybe she'd said *you'll be sorry.* Five years later he still hadn't decided which was worse.

He snuffed the candle rather than watch himself count again. Counting was a trap. Counting was how a man turned his whole life into arithmetic—so many coins, so many days, so many knots tied and never once, not one single time in five years, a spell worked openly, loudly, joyfully, the way he'd worked them at Maren's hearth when he was nineteen and stupid and the world was wide.

He'd been good at it. That was the joke nobody else got. All this hiding, all this smallness, and underneath it he was *good*—good the way Maren said his grandmother had been good, back when the ravensworn name still meant something, though Maren had only ever said that once, drunk on elderwine, and refused to repeat it, and Ivo had spent five years pretending he hadn't heard.

A knock at the street door above.

Ivo went absolutely still. It was full dark now, past the hour when honest customers knocked, and honest customers didn't knock at the street door anyway—they came down the alley steps to the cellar entrance, because everyone who came to him came quietly. The knock came again. Three slow raps. Patient. Unhurried. The knock of someone who knew the door would open eventually and was enjoying the wait.

Rule four: *never answer after dark.*

He stood in the black of his shop and listened. Above him, boots shifted on the cobblestones—heavy boots, official boots, the kind with iron heel-plates that rang a certain way. He'd learned that sound in his first year, from a window in a garret on Bellows Street, watching two white cloaks walk a weeping chandler out of his own shop. The chandler had been selling love-philtres. Real ones. The crowd had watched in silence, because that was rule five, the rule the whole city lived by: *see nothing, say nothing, be nothing.*

The boots waited. Then, unhurried, they moved on—past his door, down the alley, fading toward the harbor road. A patrol walking its rounds. Maybe. Or a patrol noting doors, making lists, feeding names to the quiet machinery of the inquisition, which worked the way rot worked: slowly, invisibly, and always inward.

Ivo let out a breath he'd been holding for thirty seconds and discovered his hands were shaking. He sat down on his stool in the dark and pressed his palms flat against his thighs until they stopped.

This was the ache he never said aloud, the one that lived under his ribs like a swallowed coal: not fear of dying. Fear of the shape of his life. He was twenty-six years old and he had built himself into a keyhole of a man—thin enough to slip through the world unnoticed, shaped exactly to the lock, holding nothing, opening nothing. Every day the same. Knots and coppers and the counting and the listening for boots. He had a gift that could ease a fishwife's terror, warm a cold room, find water in a drought, and he spent it in thimblefuls, smuggled inside handicraft, deniable, invisible, safe.

Safe. He turned the word over. It tasted like the cellar smelled: stone and dust and long dark.

Somewhere out there, Sera the courier was probably riding through the rain with her satchel of sealed letters, and Tam the archivist was probably drunk in some waystation, and Bryn—whoever Bryn was, wherever Bryn was—probably had no idea yet that her life was about to change. He didn't know any of their names yet. That was the mercy of tonight: he still didn't know.

What he knew was this: the door above was shut, the candle was out, the silver was counted, and tomorrow the fishwife's husband would sail with a knot that knew it was a knot, and Ivo Ravensworth—though no one had called him that in five years, though he flinched even now hearing it in his own head—would open his shop and sell trinkets to the frightened and keep his face a shut door and his magic smaller than a whisper.

The boots did not come back. The city settled into its nighttime hush, that vast held breath of a hundred thousand people agreeing together to see nothing.

Ivo lay down on his cot behind the shelf curtain and stared at the ceiling stones, and just before sleep took him, he thought—not for the first time, but with unusual clarity—that a man could live like this for fifty years. Safe. Invisible. A keyhole. And that someday, when they finally came for him anyway, for some knot that knew too much, some Marta who talked, some thread followed home—he would lie on a scaffold and realize the inquisitors hadn't taken his life from him at all.

They'd only collected what he'd already given up.

He slept badly, as always. And in the morning, when he pulled the bolt on the cellar door and found the alley empty and grey and ordinary, he felt the familiar wash of relief, and beneath it the familiar grief, and told himself—as always—that today would be the same as yesterday, and the day after the same as today, all the way down the long grey corridor of his careful life.

The courier would reach his door at closing time.

He didn't know that yet. But the day had already begun turning toward it, the way a tide turns—silently, and far offshore, and with all the weight of the sea behind it.
