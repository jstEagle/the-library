## 2. The Courier at the Door

The bell above the cellar door had a crack in it, so it rang flat and sour, like everything else in Caerlum. Ivo had learned to love that sound anyway. It meant a customer. It also meant he had time to hide whatever shouldn't be seen.

He was counting out the day's coppers when the door burst open without a knock and a woman came through it sideways, one hand pressed to her ribs, the other already reaching for him like he was a rope thrown into deep water.

He knew before she spoke. You learned things after five years of selling luck-knots to frightened people. You learned how fear moved in a body — the fishwife's flinch at every boot on the stair, the dockhand who wouldn't meet your eye when he bought a charm against drowning. Fear was small and hunched. This woman wasn't afraid. She was *past* it, somewhere beyond, where the body just did what it did because the mind had stopped giving orders.

She was maybe thirty, lean as a whipstock, dressed in courier's grey gone dark with blood from collar to hip. Her eyes found his and held them with a ferocity that made him step back into his own counter.

"Door," she said. "Lock it."

"I'm closed—"

"Lock it or we're both dead."

His hands moved before his brain finished arguing. That was the thing about panic; it was faster than caution. The bolt slid home. He turned back and she was already folding down onto the floor beside his workbench, her back against the leg of it, leaving a smear of red on the wood that he would later stare at and hate himself for not cleaning sooner.

"You're Ravensworth," she said. "Ivo Ravensworth."

Nobody called him that. Nobody *knew* that. In Caerlum he was Ivo the charm-seller, Ivo of the cellar shop off Tanner's Row, a man whose name appeared on no lease, no guild roll, no tax ledger. He paid his rent in coin left under a loose brick and existed everywhere else like breath on glass.

"You've got the wrong cellar," he said. "There's no Ravensworth here."

"There is." She laughed, and the laugh cost her something — she bent forward over the wound in her side and breathed through her teeth until it passed. "I checked. Three days. I checked you three ways before I came. You're the ravensworn heir, whether you like the word or not."

*Heir.* The word landed in his chest like a dropped stone. His mother had used it once, only once, drunk on elderwine the winter before she died, tracing a shape on the table that might have been a bird and might have been nothing. He'd spent five years making sure no one else ever said it aloud.

"Listen," he said, crouching down to her level because standing over her felt wrong, "whatever you were told, whoever sent you — I sell trinkets. Knotted thread. Dried lavender. Things that work because people believe they work. There's no magic in this shop and there's no bloodline either. You need a physicker. There's one two streets over, old woman, doesn't ask questions—"

"I can't walk two streets." She said it simply, the way you'd say *the sun has set*. Then she pulled something from inside her coat, and the sight of it stopped his mouth.

A map. Folded small, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with wax the color of dried liver. And across the wax, half-soaked away but unmistakable, blood — hers, still wet, still warm enough that he felt it through the cloth when she pressed the whole bundle into his hands and closed his fingers over it with both of hers.

Her hands were freezing. That detail would come back to him later, in the bad hours: how cold her fingers were, and how strong.

"The king is dead," she whispered.

Ivo looked at the door. Looked at the window slit near the ceiling, where evening light came down thin and grey. Somewhere above, a cart rattled past on Tanner's Row, ordinary as bread.

"The king is very much alive," he said carefully. "I saw the procession at Midwinter. Everyone did."

"The thing in the palace isn't the king." Her grip tightened on his hands, hard enough to hurt. "Hasn't been for years. Listen to me. I don't have long and you're going to waste it arguing. Find the ravensworn. The map shows the waystones. Unbind—" A cough took her, wet and terrible, and when she wiped her mouth her palm came away black in the candlelight. "Unbind what the binders bound. Your family's work. Your family's debt."

"My family sold charms," Ivo said. "Badly."

Something like amusement crossed her face, there and gone, a candleflame in wind. "So did mine, once. Sera," she added, as if introducing herself at a market stall. "Sera Vane. Courier, third rank. Remember the name. Someone should."

"Sera, please—"

"Corvane will come." The name dropped between them like ice into water. Ivo felt his whole body go still, the way small animals go still. Everyone in Caerlum knew Corvane the way sailors know a storm's name. First Inquisitor. The Crown's own grey shadow. Men had been taken from their beds for less than what this woman had just done — bled in a charm-seller's cellar, speaking treason with her last breath.

"When they find me," Sera said, "they'll find you. They'll pull this cellar apart board by board. So burn me. Burn everything. The map is the only thing worth more than your life, and I'm sorry — I am truly sorry — for handing it to you." Her eyes were losing focus now, sliding toward the ceiling, toward somewhere past it. "I ran out of choices. You were the last name on my list. The only name."

"How many names were on it?"

"One," she said. "It was a short list. Ravensworn are rare these days. Wonder why."

And then, because the world is cruel about timing, the bell above the street door — the hidden one, the trapdoor entrance he used for deliveries — gave its cracked flat ring.

Both of them froze.

Footsteps on the stairs outside. Not hurrying. The particular unhurried tread of someone who has all night and knows exactly what they're looking for.

"*Back room,*" Ivo breathed. "Under the floor. There's a space behind the cistern—"

She shook her head. Just once. Small and final. "Too late for that. Too late for me either way — look at me, charm-seller. I'm a message, not a passenger. Get the map somewhere they'll never think to look. Not on you. Not here. *Not on you.*"

The footsteps reached the landing.

Ivo did the fastest thinking of his life, which is to say he did almost none. He shoved the map up inside his shirt, against his skin, feeling the wax seal press cold between his shoulder blades. Then he dragged Sera behind the workbench, out of the sightline of the door, and grabbed the first thing his hand found — a rag — and pressed it uselessly against her wound.

"Hold this," he whispered. "Just hold it. Be quiet. Please."

She looked at him with those fading eyes and, absurdly, winked.

The door opened.

* * *

The man in the doorway wore the grey-and-black of the Inquisition, but not the full regalia — no mask, no silver chain of office. A junior, then, or a watcher. Young, clean-shaven, with the patient face of a man who enjoyed waiting more than finding.

"Evening," the inquisitor said. His gaze went around the cellar slowly, shelf by shelf, the way a tongue goes around a missing tooth. "Charm-seller. This is the one on Tanner's Row? Below the tannery?"

"That's right." Ivo kept his voice bored. Boredom was armor. He'd learned that early — fear invited questions, and questions were how they dug. "Though if you're here about the licensing levy, I pay quarterly, and Master Hobb collects it himself."

"No levy tonight." The man stepped down into the cellar, boots loud on the stone. "We're looking for a runner. Grey coat. Came off the river road an hour ago, bleeding. Seen her?"

"A bleeding woman?" Ivo shook his head. "No. Wish I could help. Though if she's hurt badly, the physicker on Cordwain Street—"

"We know where the physickers are." The inquisitor's eyes had found the workbench. Found the smear of blood on its leg. Ivo watched him see it and felt the floor tilt gently beneath his feet, the way a boat tilts.

"Tanner's Row," Ivo said. "Half the shops down here have blood on something. Butchers upstream, tannery downstream. It gets everywhere. You get used to it."

"Mm." The inquisitor crouched, touched the smear with one finger, rubbed it between finger and thumb. Behind the bench, Ivo heard — or imagined he heard — Sera's breath catch. He began talking, quickly, filling the air the way you fill a silence in a tavern before a fight starts.

"Actually, since you're here — do you have any idea when the new edict comes down? About charm-licensing? My customers keep asking and I keep having to guess, and guessing is bad for business—"

"What edict?"

"The one the criers mentioned Tuesday. Or maybe they didn't, maybe I misheard, it was crowded—" He let the sentence die, shrugged, scratched his jaw. Let the confusion be real. It wasn't hard. Half his life was performance; the other half was inventory.

The inquisitor straightened up. For a long moment he just looked at Ivo, and Ivo looked back with the open, slightly dim expression of a man with nothing in his head but thread and lavender. Inside, his heart was going like a drum in a parade.

"Tuesday's edict was about grain tariffs," the inquisitor said.

"Was it? Well. There you go." Ivo smiled apologetically. "Told you I misheard."

Another pause. The inquisitor's gaze drifted toward the back of the cellar, toward the shadows behind the cistern, and Ivo's stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept going.

"Candle's guttering," Ivo said. "Let me get you a fresh one for the walk."

"I didn't say I was walking anywhere yet."

"No, but it's dark out, and the watch on Tanner's Row is useless after sundown. Robbers. Well. One robber, really, but he's committed."

That got the ghost of a twitch at the corner of the man's mouth. Gods, thought Ivo, they're human. That's the worst part. They're human and they go home to dinners.

"Lock up when you close," the inquisitor said finally. "If a bleeding woman comes to your door, you'll send for the watch. Immediately. Won't you?"

"On my mother's grave."

Which was a lie twice over, because his mother had no grave, and he would feed a bleeding woman to the river before he fed her to the watch.

The inquisitor climbed the stairs. The door shut. The footsteps faded up toward the street, unhurried, patient, and then there was only the sound of Ivo's own breathing and, behind the workbench, Sera's — thinner now, with a whistle in it like wind through a broken shutter.

He went to her. She was smiling faintly, which frightened him more than anything else that night.

"He believed you," she whispered.

"For about a minute. Maybe two. They always come back." He pressed the rag harder against her side and she hissed. "Listen to me. Can you stand? If I get you to the river—"

"Ivo." First time she'd used his name. It sounded strange in her mouth, familiar, like she'd been saying it to herself for days on the road. "Stop. Please stop. I want to say this while I still have words." Her hand found his wrist. Cold fingers, iron grip. "The map. The seal. Break the seal only when you're somewhere safe, and only alone, and only — only if you mean to do something about it. If you just want to live, throw it in the river unopened and forget my face. That's allowed. That's the smart move. I'd have done it."

"Why give it to me, then?"

"Because the smart move is how we got here." Her voice was fraying at the edges now, words coming slower, spaces growing between them. "Everyone taking the smart move. Five years of everyone being careful. And the kingdom just... gets quieter. People forget things. Names. Songs. Whole villages. You'll see. On the map. You'll see."

"Sera—"

"Find the ravensworn," she said. "Tell them — tell them Sera carried it. Tell them she didn't drop it."

Her grip loosened. Tightened once, reflexively, like a hand closing on a rope. Loosened again.

After that, the cellar was very quiet. The candle burned. Somewhere above, the tannery's dogs started up at something and settled again. Ivo knelt on the cold stone with a dead woman's blood drying on his hands and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that his life was over.

Not his body. His body would probably survive; bodies were good at that. But the careful, invisible, five-years-in-the-building architecture of his survival — the false name, the harmless stock, the rent under the brick, the smile that promised nothing — all of it had a corpse in it now. All of it was evidence.

* * *

He hid her the way you do anything shameful: fast, and badly, and while hating yourself.

There was a space behind the coal bin, deep enough for a curled body, old enough that he'd never needed it before and had told himself for years it was just a cellar quirk. He laid her in it as gently as he could, folded her coat under her head, and closed his eyes for a moment because looking at her face was unbearable and looking away from it was worse.

"I'm sorry," he told her. "This isn't — I'll fix this. I'll do it properly. Tonight."

She didn't answer. Dead women are patient that way.

Then he worked. He scrubbed the blood off the workbench until the wood went pale, and then scrubbed the pail, and then stood in the middle of his shop holding the bloody rag and realized there was nowhere in the world he could put it that would matter. He burned it in the brazier with a handful of lavender to cover the smell, and the smoke rose up through the vent slit and out into the free air of Caerlum, carrying the evidence of a murder that hadn't happened yet.

Because that was the arithmetic, wasn't it? He ran it three times and it came out the same every time. The inquisitor had seen the blood. Would report it, or wouldn't — but Corvane's people reported everything, that was the whole point of them. They'd come back. Tomorrow, maybe. Next day. With dogs, with warders, with the quiet instruments they used on charm-sellers who lied about edicts. And they'd find her, and then they'd find him, and the question they'd ask — the only question that mattered — was *what did she give you?*

He could run. The thought arrived wearing its best clothes: slip out tonight, take the river road south, be nobody in some other city by week's end. He'd done it before. Running was the one skill he'd truly mastered; he had a talent for it the way other men had a talent for music.

But running meant leaving the body to be found in his cellar, tied to his name. And running meant the map either came with him — a brand on his back, *kill this man and read what he carries* — or stayed behind, and he couldn't make himself put it down. He tried. He actually walked to the door with it in his hand, stood there in the dark with his cloak on, and his arm would not lift. Five years of staying alive by wanting nothing, and it turned out he wanted *this*, whatever it was, whatever it cost.

He sat back down on the floor next to the coal bin instead, in the dark, and listened to the building settle around him.

*Burn me,* she'd said. *Burn everything.*

Caerlum had rules about fires. Charm-sellers especially didn't burn things at midnight; smoke drew eyes, and eyes drew questions. But there was an old lime kiln below the tannery, disused since the tanners switched to ash-curing, with a flue that vented into the cliff face. His landlord's man used it for rendering waste. If a fire was going to happen, it would happen there, and it would happen to look like exactly what it was supposed to look like: a charm-seller clearing out spoiled stock. Bad season. Mold in the dried herbs. Happens every autumn.

He was already planning the fire when he understood what he was doing, and had to sit with that for a while — the fact that hiding a dead courier and burning her body had become, in the space of four hours, a logistics problem. That he was good at this. That some part of him, the part Maren had trained and then despaired of, had woken up the moment Sera hit the floor and started solving.

*You were the last name on my list,* she'd said. *The only name.*

"Ravensworn," he said aloud, to the dark, and the word sat in the cellar like a third person.

He got up. He fetched the shovel and the lamp oil and the sack of spoiled stock he'd been meaning to throw out anyway, and he went to the coal bin, and he knelt, and he said, "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry," the whole time, like a prayer with no god attached.

Then he stopped. Because his hand, reaching for her shoulder, had brushed the front of her coat — and there was a pocket he hadn't searched. Stupid. Reckless. The kind of oversight that got men hanged. He'd been too busy being horrified to be thorough.

Inside the pocket: a courier's token, bone-white, stamped with the Vane mark — proof of who she was, for whoever came after. And a scrap of paper, folded eight times, soft as cloth from handling.

He unfolded it by candlelight with hands that would not quite hold steady.

Three lines, in a quick slanted hand that wasn't hers — written by someone else, passed to her, carried against her heart:

*If I fall, the ravensworn boy keeps it. Don't let him bury it with me. He'll want to. He's spent his whole life learning how to disappear. Make him stop.*

Below that, unsigned, a single drawn shape: a bird. A raven, wings spread, rendered in four strokes of ink by someone who had drawn it many times before.

Ivo sat down on the floor of his shop, in the wreckage of his careful life, and laughed — one short, cracked sound with no humor in it at all. Whoever had written that note had known him. Had known him the way Maren knew him, the way you know a thing you've given up on and loved anyway.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a watch bell began to ring. Not alarm-rhythm. Change-of-shift. Midnight in Caerlum, and the city turned over in its sleep, and somewhere in it a junior inquisitor was writing his evening report: *blood found, charm-seller questioned, subject cooperative, recommend follow-up.*

Follow-up. Tomorrow. Perhaps at dawn.

Ivo folded the note into his shirt beside the map, picked up the shovel, and went to build a fire.
