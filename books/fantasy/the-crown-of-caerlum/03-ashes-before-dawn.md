## 3. Ashes Before Dawn

The body was heavier than I expected.

That was the first thing I learned about death, in the small hours of that terrible night: the dead do not cooperate. Sera—the courier, I never even learned her name until later—slumped against my cellar wall like a sack of wet grain, and when I took her under the arms to drag her toward the stair, her head lolled against my chest and her blood smeared across my shirt in a long dark comma, like a sentence someone had started and abandoned.

I had rules. Five years of rules. *Never work after dark. Never sell to anyone who asks twice. Never let them see your hands shake.* Rule six, unwritten but understood: *if trouble finds you anyway, be gone before it finishes knocking.*

Instead I was dragging a dead woman up my stairs at moonset, with a bloodstained map sewn into the lining of my coat, whispering to myself like a madman.

*Not here. Not here. Not here.*

My shop occupied the ground floor of a crooked house on Tanner's Lane, three doors down from a tannery whose stench covered a multitude of sins, mine included. Behind it, past a yard where I kept nothing but broken crates and honest weeds, stood an old lime kiln from the days when this street actually tanned things—a squat stone throat of a structure, cold for thirty years.

Cold, but not broken.

I won't pretend I thought it through. Thought is too orderly a word for what happened between midnight and dawn. What happened was this: I looked at Sera's face—gray now, young still, a smudge of freckles across a nose that had been broken once and set slightly wrong—and I understood that if the inquisitors found her here, they would not ask how she died. They would ask why she came to *me*. And then they would peel my life open like an oyster, and everything I'd built around the soft part of myself would come apart on their table.

So: the kiln.

I laid her inside as gently as I could, which felt obscene, because gentleness was wasted now. I folded her hands. I don't know why. Her fingers were cold and stiffening and folding them accomplished nothing except to make me feel, briefly, like a man who had done one decent thing on the worst night of his life.

Then I went back inside and made the other decision, the one that hurt worse.

* * *

A hedge-witch's stock is a biography. Mine said: *coward.* Luck-knots braided from ordinary hemp, charmed just enough to warm the wrist. Dried feverfew that was only dried feverfew. Tin charms shaped like keys, sold to people who needed to believe a door would open for them. Everything calibrated to be deniable. Everything designed so that if an inquisitor held it to the light, he'd see nothing but craft and superstition.

I piled all of it into the yard. The knots, the tins, the little glass bottles of colored water I sold as "blessed dew" at two coppers each because grief makes people generous. I doused the pile with lamp oil and stood there holding the flask, and I want to be honest: some part of me was relieved. Five years of hiding, and here was permission to burn it all. The coward in me has always been good at finding silver linings.

I struck the flint and the fire went up with a sound like a held breath released.

And then I sat on my back step, coat over my shoulders against the cold, and watched five years of careful invisibility curl into the dark sky, and I took out the map.

*Burn it too,* every instinct said. Every rule I owned, screaming in chorus. *It's evidence. It's a death warrant with a wax seal. Whatever ravensworn means—and you know, some buried part of you knows—it's not your problem. Let the kingdom rot. You've only ever saved yourself. Be consistent.*

The map was good work. Oiled linen, wax-sealed, the kind of thing that survives rivers and rain and careless pockets. In candlelight it showed the western reaches of the kingdom: Caerlum on the coast, then the Weald, then places I'd only heard named in tavern songs. Vessany with its hundred bells. The capital, marked not with the usual crown symbol but with something else—a circle, drawn hollow. An empty ring.

An empty crown.

Sera's blood had soaked one corner, and where it had dried, the linen had stiffened into a small dark wing shape. I turned it over. Nothing on the reverse. Just linen, and the ghost of her blood, and the word she'd spent her last breath on.

*Ravensworn.*

Ravens. My name. Coincidence—I told myself it was coincidence, the way you tell yourself the floorboards creaking upstairs are only the house settling. Ravensworth. Ravensworn. A dying woman's delirium, grabbing at whatever name her failing mind could reach.

But my hands knew better. My hands, which had been steady through the whole grim business of the kiln, were shaking now, and they shook the way they shook when magic moved through them—not with fear exactly, though fear was in there, but with recognition. Like a tuning fork hearing its own note from across a room.

I should have burned it. I had the fire right there. All I had to do was stand up, take four steps, and let go.

I sat there until the sky went gray, and the map stayed in my hands, and the fire burned down to orange eyes in the kiln's throat, and I did not burn it.

I've spent a long time since trying to explain that choice, even to myself. The best I can manage is this: Sera died pressing it into my hands. She could have thrown it in a gutter. She could have swallowed it. She chose me, out of everyone in Caerlum, out of every door on every lane—and maybe she chose wrong, but I couldn't bring myself to finish the job her killers started. Burning it felt less like safety and more like helping them.

So I folded it small, and I hid it in the one place no inquisitor would think to search: sewn into the hem of my coat, beneath a patch I'd mended badly on purpose months ago, because bad mending invites no second look.

Then the sun came up, and with it, boots on Tanner's Lane.

* * *

They came at first light, which told me they'd worked fast, which told me Sera had been missed before she ever reached my door.

There were four of them, but only one mattered. The other three wore the gray-and-scarlet of the Crown's inquisition and stood in my shop doorway radiating institutional patience. The fourth stood in front of my counter and looked around my little den of harmless trinkets with the expression of a man reading a letter he already knows by heart.

He was tall and narrow, somewhere past fifty, with gray hair cut short and a face like a well-kept ledger—everything recorded, nothing revealed. His coat was plain black, no insignia, which in Caerlum meant more than any insignia could. Only very confident men dressed plainly.

"You're the charm-seller," he said. Not a question.

"I'm a trinket-seller." I kept my voice bored. Boredom is armor; fear is a door left open. "Hemp knots and tin keys. If you're looking for real charms, you've wasted a walk."

"Have I?" He smiled. It reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Corvane," he said, and offered his hand.

Inquisitors didn't offer hands. Inquisitors offered warrants and irons. I took his hand anyway, because refusing seemed more dangerous than touching him, and his grip was dry and cool and brief, a clerk's handshake.

"Ivo Ravensworth."

"Aye."

"May I?" He gestured at my stock—or what remained of it, the few pieces I hadn't burned, the ones too worthless to bother with. He picked up a tin key and turned it in the light. "Charming workmanship."

"It's tin."

"It is." He set it down with exaggerated care, aligning it with the others, and I understood suddenly that this was a man who noticed alignment. Who noticed everything. Who had probably already counted the gaps in my shelves and drawn conclusions from them. "You had a fire last night, Master Ravensworth."

"My neighbor's chimney throws sparks. Old Flitwick. You can ask him."

"I did." Still smiling. "He says your chimney throws sparks. He says you burn things often. He says—" Corvane tilted his head, consulting some inner document, "—he says you're a quiet fellow, keeps to himself, sells luck to fishwives. He used the word 'luck' four times. Fishwives rarely use that word about trinkets. They say 'charms,' or they say nothing at all and buy in secret."

"Fishwives say what fishwives say."

"They do." He wandered my shop the way a man wanders a garden he owns. "You know what interests me, Master Ravensworth? Not the fire. Fires happen. Not the trinkets—though I confess I find the economics puzzling. A man with your rents, selling two-copper charms? Either you're very bad at business or very good at something else." He stopped at the counter and looked at me directly for the first time, and his eyes were pale gray and absolutely without malice, which is the most frightening thing an inquisitor's eyes can be. "What interests me is a courier."

"Couriers come through Caerlum daily."

"This one didn't arrive daily. This one arrived bleeding. Stabbed on Coil Street around the ninth bell, walked—crawled, likely—somewhere south and west, and vanished. Blood drops on Tanner's Lane." He said it mildly, like reporting weather. "They end at your door, Master Ravensworth."

"Lot of blood on Tanner's Lane. Tannery next door slaughters—"

"Sheep," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "Which bleed indoors. These drops were moving south, spaced like a walking man—or woman. And they stop at your threshold." He paused. "Where did you sleep last night?"

"In my bed."

"Alone?"

"That's a personal question for an inquisitor."

"It's a professional one." He wasn't smiling anymore, but the courtesy stayed, wrapped around him like a coat. "Here is what troubles me. We searched the lane this morning. No body. No dragged marks leading away. A wounded courier does not walk off and vanish, and yet she vanished, precisely at your door, precisely on the night your yard smells of smoke and burnt hemp." He spread his hands—an apology, almost. "You see my difficulty. Every fact I have points at you, and every fact you give me points away, and a man whose answers are smoother than his facts is a man I cannot stop thinking about."

I gave him the look I'd practiced in cracked mirrors for five years: the affronted small tradesman, half confused, half offended, too dull to be worth the trouble. "If a dying woman crawled to my door, I'd have called the watch. I'm not a fool. Dead couriers in your cellar are bad for trade."

"No," Corvane said softly. "You're not a fool. That's what troubles me most." He straightened his cuffs, precise as everything else about him. "I'm going to tell you something, Master Ravensworth, because I find honesty gets better results than threats, and because I suspect you already know it. The Crown sees everything. Every lane, every ledger, every lie. The king's peace is not a metaphor—it is a web, and every citizen of Caerlum is caught in it, and the only variable is whether the web notices you early or late." He moved to the door, then paused. "You seem like a man who values his quiet. I hope you keep it. Quiet men live long lives here."

"And loud ones?"

"Loud ones get audited." The smile returned, thin as paper. "We'll speak again, I'm sure. I find that conversations with you would be educational."

They left. All four of them, unhurried, boots ringing on the cobbles, and I stood in my gutted shop among the ash-smell and the surviving junk, and I waited until their footsteps faded before I let my knees inform me how afraid I'd been.

* * *

Here's the thing about lying: it's a craft, same as knot-work, and I was good at it because I'd had practice being someone else my whole life. The hedge-witch's apprentice learns masks the way other children learn prayers. Maren used to say I lied like breathing, and she didn't mean it as praise.

But Corvane hadn't believed me. That was the discovery sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone. Men like Corvane don't need to believe you—they file you. Somewhere in the Crown's vast archive of citizens, there was now a card with my name on it, and the card said *watch*, and watch was a slow word. Watch could take months. Watch could take years. Watch meant every purchase, every visitor, every odd hour noted by neighbors paid in coppers to notice.

I had perhaps a day. Maybe two, if Corvane liked his puzzles unsolved.

I climbed to my room above the shop and pulled up a floorboard and took out the things that mattered, which fit in a satchel smaller than a bread loaf. My mother's ring, which I'd never worn. Forty crowns in mixed coin. A knife Bryn—no, I didn't know Bryn yet; a knife, that's all. And the map, which I unrolled on the bare boards and read again by daylight, as if daylight might reveal it as the ordinary thing I desperately wanted it to be.

It didn't. In daylight it was worse. In daylight I could see the fine stitching along one edge, the kind of work a courier does to reinforce a document she expects to carry through hell, and I could see that the hollow circle at the capital had been traced and retraced so many times the wax seal beside it had nearly worn through. Sera had touched that mark over and over. Praying over it, maybe. Or cursing it.

*Find the ravensworn,* she'd whispered, and died on my floor, and I had burned her and lied to her killer's face and kept her map, and all three of those acts were now braided together into something I couldn't undo, a knot with no loose end.

I thought about running. Of course I did. South to the free ports, west over the mountains, some city where nobody knew the word Ravensworth. I had the coin. I had the talent for vanishing—it was the one art I'd truly mastered.

But running was the whole shape of my life, and looking at that map, I understood—with the flat, cold clarity that comes after a sleepless night—that Corvane's web stretched further than any road I knew, and that a man who runs leaves tracks, and that sooner or later every track ends at a door with an inquisitor standing outside it, offering his hand.

Besides. Some traitor part of me, the part that had channeled magic into a luck-knot for a frightened fishwife just yesterday, the part that had felt the map hum against my palms like a struck bell—that part wanted to know what *ravensworn* meant. Wanted it the way a starving man wants bread and hates himself for wanting it.

I sewed the map deeper into my coat hem. I put on the coat. I banked the kiln's ashes so they'd look like what they were supposed to look like—a trinket-seller clearing old stock—and I stepped out onto Tanner's Lane into the morning, carrying a dead woman's errand against my ankle and a living man's lie on my tongue.

Behind me, smoke still rose faintly from my scorched yard, carrying the smell of burnt hemp and tin and five years of careful, cowardly, invisible life.

Ahead, at the corner of the lane, a girl with a sword-callused hand and her sister's eyes was watching my door.

She didn't know yet that I'd seen her. But I had spent five years learning to notice the people who noticed me, and she had been there since dawn, and she was not blinking, and I knew—with the same certainty that had kept me alive this long—that whoever she was, she had come for answers about Sera.

And I was the only answer left.
