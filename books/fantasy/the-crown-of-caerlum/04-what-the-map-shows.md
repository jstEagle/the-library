## 4. What the Map Shows

I did not sleep.

That was the plan, at least—the not-sleeping part. The rest of it, the sitting in my cellar with a dead woman's map and a single candle stub and every rule I'd built my life on stacked in a corner like furniture waiting to be sold, that had not been the plan. The plan had been: burn the courier's satchel with everything else, sweep the ash before dawn, open the shop at the usual hour, sell three luck-knots and a fever-charm to a nervous grandmother, and never think about Sera again except in the small hours when thinking was allowed.

But I kept the map.

I told myself it was practical. Evidence, if Corvane came back. Something to surrender if it came to that—*here, take it, I found it on her, I never opened it.* A lie with an object attached is always more convincing than a lie alone. That was hedge-witch arithmetic, one of the first lessons Maren ever taught me, back when her voice still had warmth in it: *a charm works better if you give them something to hold.*

So I kept the map because it made a better lie.

That's what I told myself, sitting cross-legged on the floor of my cellar at the hour when even the rats go quiet, turning the thing over in my hands for the hundredth time.

It was good vellum, older than it looked. Wax-sealed once—the broken seal still clung to one corner, pressed with a signet I didn't recognize: a tower under a crescent moon. The ink had gone brown as old blood, which was appropriate, since there was actual blood on it too, Sera's, smeared across the lower third where she'd clutched it against the wound in her side. It showed the kingdom. Not all of it—not the tidy cartographer's version with its compass rose and its border of sea-serpents—but a rough hand-drawn sweep from Caerlum on the coast down through the Weald, past Vessany with its bells, into the hill country, ending somewhere near the capital without quite reaching it. The line simply stopped, as if whoever drew it had run out of certainty before they ran out of paper.

There were marks along the route. Small ones. A triangle here, a circle there, a symbol like a bird's foot scratched so faintly I'd needed to hold it to the candle three times before I was sure it wasn't a stain.

And nothing else. No names. No *unbind*, no ravensworn, no instructions. Just geography and symbols and a dead woman's blood.

I'd checked. Believe me, I'd checked. I'd held it over the candle until the heat stung my knuckles. I'd tilted it against the light looking for watermarks. I'd run my thumb along every crease feeling for a second layer of vellum, a hidden pocket, anything. Tam—whoever Tam turned out to be, if the name meant anything more than a dying woman's last word—was welcome to his archives. There was nothing in the map but a road and a riddle.

Which meant the message wasn't in the map.

The thought arrived sometime after midnight, the way the worst thoughts do: quietly, already fully formed, waiting for me like something that had been sitting in the dark of my cellar all along.

*The message isn't in the map. The message is in what the map is made of.*

Because I knew about hidden writing. Of course I did. Every hedge-witch knows about hidden writing—it's the first trick you learn after the luck-knots, because half your customers are people who need to say things they can't be caught saying. Milk-writing revealed by ash. Charcoal-and-tallow that only shows against a warm stone. Maren had taught me a dozen ways to hide words from eyes, and she'd taught me the thirteenth way too, the one she'd made me swear on salt and iron never to use.

Words written in the working itself. Script laid into the fabric of a thing while it was being made, woven through the vellum or the wood or the silver the way a weaver lays a pattern into cloth that looks plain until you hold it up to the right light. Except the right light wasn't light at all.

To see it, you had to push a thread of magic through the object and let the script catch on it, the way dust catches on a sunbeam. And to do that—to channel, even a hair's breadth, even for a heartbeat—I would have to break the only vow that mattered.

Five years. Five years of sleight of hand and misdirection, of selling knots tied tight by fingers and nothing else, of letting people believe in me just enough to buy and not enough to look twice. Five years since I'd walked out of Maren's cottage with frost still on my boots and sworn that whatever lived in my blood could starve there. I'd been careful the way other men are pious. I'd been careful the way drowning men are quiet.

And now there was a dead woman on my conscience and an inquisitor who'd smiled at me too politely and a word—*ravensworn*—that I couldn't stop hearing in her voice no matter how loud I made my own thoughts.

I sat with the map on my knees for a long time.

Here is what nobody tells you about vows: they don't get weaker with age. They calcify. They become part of the architecture, load-bearing walls, and you forget they were ever choices. You tell yourself you don't miss it. You build a whole life in the shape of the absence and call the shape contentment. I had a shop. I had regulars. I had Mrs. Tallow who bought a luck-knot every month because her husband drowned and she liked the ritual of it, and I had the fishwife from yesterday, and I had my rules, and I had my invisibility, and I had told myself for five years that this was enough, this was safety, this was the whole of what I wanted.

But I hadn't slept. And in the dark, with the candle guttering, I could admit the thing I only ever admitted in the dark: it hadn't been enough for years. It had been *bearable*. Those are different words, and the distance between them is exactly the width of a cellar.

Sera had died on my floor. She'd spent her last breath choosing me—a stranger, a trinket-seller, the least important man in Caerlum—and pressing this map into my hands as if I were someone. *Find the ravensworn.* As if those were words that meant something. As if I were someone who could answer them.

I looked at the map. The candle looked at me. Somewhere overhead, the city turned in its sleep, and a patrol's boots crossed the street above my grate—three sets, unhurried, the rhythm of men who owned the night—and passed on.

"All right," I said aloud, to no one, in the smallest voice I owned. "Just to look. Just to see."

Liar, said the part of me that sounded like Maren.

I know, I told her.

*

You don't forget how. That's the cruel joke of it. You can starve a gift for five years and the moment you reach for it, it's there, eager, like a dog that waited by the door the whole time and never once stopped believing you'd come back.

I cupped my left hand over the map, palm down, fingers spread the way Maren had drilled into me—*ground yourself first, boy, or the ground will do it for you*—and I pressed my fingertips to the cold vellum, and I breathed out, slow, the way you breathe when you're trying to be smaller than you are.

Then I let the door open. Just a crack. Just a thread.

It came up through me like warmth returning to a frozen hand—that first sting of blood rushing back, sweet and awful at once. A thin bright filament of it, no thicker than a spider's silk, running down my arm, through my palm, out through my skin and into the map.

The vellum warmed under my fingers.

And the script woke up.

It rose through the surface like fish rising to feed—faint at first, brown-gold, shimmering, then sharper, lines of a fine cramped hand threading between the drawn roads and around the symbols, everywhere and nowhere, visible only where my magic touched. I snatched my hand back and the script sank away, the map going plain and innocent as a sleeping face. My heart was slamming. The candle flame had bent toward me, though there was no draft.

I put my hand back down. The script rose again, and this time I made myself read.

It was a list. That was the first shock of it—not a letter, not a warning, but a catalogue, written in the same hand that had drawn the roads, each entry anchored to one of the little symbols. I traced the first mark, the triangle near the coast below Caerlum, and read:

*Waystone the First — beneath the split oak, Wealdmarch. Sealed. Sleeping. Do not wake it alone.*

My mouth went dry. I moved to the circle further south, near Vessany.

*Waystone the Second — under the bell-founders' yard, Vessany. Sealed. Cracked. It remembers what was done.*

Under the bell-founders' yard. I'd been to Vessany once, years ago, before I'd learned how thoroughly a man could disappear. Bells day and night. I'd never thought to wonder what was underneath them.

The third symbol—the bird's-foot, so faint—sat at the end of the unfinished road, at the edge of the capital.

*Waystone the Third — beneath the palace itself. Opened. Fed. Guarded by the thing that wears him.*

*The thing that wears him.*

Sera's whisper came back to me across the hours and the ash: *the king is dead.* I had taken it for the delirium of a dying woman, or a traitor's rumor, or madness. But here it was in ink older than her wound, written by a steadier hand: the king was a garment. Something wore him. Something guarded the third stone the way a spider guards the center of its web.

I read on. The entries grew stranger, more personal, the writer's composure fraying as the list went down the page. Notes in the margins, angled sideways, hurried:

*The stones are anchors. What they anchor, I will not write here. If you are reading this, you have already felt it—the hollowing. The forgetting. It moves outward from the palace like weather.*

*They called it binding once. They called it mercy. Look what mercy became.*

*I have carried this map for eleven years. I am tired. Whoever finds it: the seal is mine, the fault is mine, the road is yours now. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.*

And then, at the bottom of the page, beneath everything, larger than the rest, pressed deep into the vellum as though the writer had leaned their whole weight onto the pen—one word, repeated, the letters slightly different each time as if written on different nights, in different moods, over years:

*Unbind. Unbind. Unbind.*

The candle went out.

Not guttered—went out, straight down, as if pinched by invisible fingers, and the cellar dropped into blackness so complete I could feel it pressing on my eyes. The warmth of the magic was still in my arm, humming, and in the dark I could see the map anyway—could see the script glowing faintly gold where my fingers rested, the last threads of my own power lighting it from within like a lantern made of paper and guilt.

I sat in the dark and read the word again. And again.

*Unbind.*

*

Here is what I should have done, and I want it on the record, whoever's record this turns out to be, that I knew it even as I failed to do it: I should have fed the map to the candle and watched it burn, and swept the ash, and opened my shop, and spent the rest of my life being a man who sells luck-knots to frightened women and never once wonders what happened to the kingdom.

Instead I relit the candle with a snap of my fingers—no flint, no match, just a spark pulled out of the air like picking a berry—and hated myself for how good it felt, and kept reading.

Because there was more. Under the list of stones, in the margin beside the second entry, the writer had added something in a different ink, newer, the letters shakier:

*The stones answer to a bloodline. The binders' blood. Ravensworn. I searched for years. The archives say extinct. The archives are wrong—they are always wrong about the things that matter. There is a family. There was a family. They scattered, they hid, they changed their names the way you'd change a coat. If any of them live, the stones will know them. The stones will sing to them. God help them, because no one else will.*

Ravensworn.

I stared at the word until it stopped looking like a word.

My mother used to hum a song when she thought I wasn't listening. Low, in a language I never learned, with a word in the refrain that sounded like *raven* but wasn't, quite—something older, rounder, worn smooth by generations of mouths. When I asked her about it once she stopped humming for a month. My father had a ring he never wore, a plain dark band he kept in a box under the floorboards, and when he drank—which was rarely, but memorably—he would take it out and turn it in the lamplight and say nothing, absolutely nothing, with an expression I have spent thirty years failing to describe.

And our name. Ravensworth. I'd always assumed it was a coincidence, some clerk's whim two generations back, the way smiths are all named Smith. Names are cheap. Names are coats.

*They changed their names the way you'd change a coat.*

The candle flame stood perfectly still. My hand, still resting on the map, had begun to tremble, and the script trembled with it, the golden letters swimming, and I realized distantly that the warmth in my arm hadn't faded. The thread was still open. I'd been channeling for minutes—longer than I'd allowed myself in five years—and the gift was pouring through me like water finding an old channel, greedy, glad, filling every dry place I'd dug in myself, and the terrible thing, the thing I'll never say out loud, is that it felt like coming home to a house I'd burned down on purpose.

I closed my fist and shut the door on it. The light died from the script. The dark rushed back in, ordinary this time, honest.

I sat there shaking, in a cellar that smelled of ash and tallow, holding a dead woman's map.

Wonder had come first—I won't pretend otherwise. For a few shining minutes I'd been a boy again, the boy who'd found his gift at nine years old and thought it was the most beautiful thing in the world, before Maren taught him what it cost and the world taught him what it earned. Wonder, real wonder, the kind that makes your chest ache.

But wonder curdles. Wonder always curdles, if you leave it sitting long enough, because underneath every marvelous thing is a bill, and bills come due. The wonder lasted until I understood what the map was actually saying. Not *here is a mystery*. Not *here is an adventure*.

Here is a job. Here is a debt. Here is your name, Ivo Ravensworth, written in a dead stranger's hand in a script only your blood can read, and here is a road, and here is a word, and here is the certain knowledge that a woman bled out on your floor so that this page could reach the one person in the kingdom whose great-grandparents helped forge the thing that is eating it.

She hadn't chosen me at random. Dying women don't have the luxury of random. She'd known—or guessed, or been told—and she'd crawled to my door because the map had led her there, or fate had, or the stones themselves, singing, as the writer said they would, to whatever ravensworn blood still walked the world.

*God help them, because no one else will.*

"No," I said to the empty cellar. My voice cracked on it. "No. Find someone else. Find another branch. Find anyone."

The map lay in my lap, plain and silent, offering no one else.

Above me, the city was waking. Carts on the cobbles, gulls fighting over the harbor refuse, the fishwives crying their morning prices—all the vast indifferent machinery of a place that did not know its king was dead, or that its memories were being eaten like a moth-eaten coat, or that a man in a cellar had just been handed the bill for a crime committed before he was born. Somewhere in that machinery, Corvane was waking too. Polite, patient Corvane, with his unsettling courtesy and his questions arranged like knives on a tray. He'd be asking about a courier today. He'd find the ash pit eventually; men like him always find the ash. And when he did, he'd ask me again, gently, whether I'd taken anything from the body, and I would lie, because lying was the one craft I'd truly mastered—

—and the map would be under my floorboards, and the word *unbind* would be under my skin, and neither of those things was going anywhere.

I wrapped the map in oilcloth, the way you wrap a wound. I pried up the loose flagstone behind the drying racks—the one place in Caerlum I'd never shown another living soul—and put it down in the dark earth, and covered it, and sat back on my heels.

The past was calling me by name. I could still hear it, faintly, through the oilcloth and the flagstone and the five years of careful silence: a song my mother hummed, a ring my father turned in lamplight, a word pressed deep into old vellum by a tired hand.

*Unbind.*

I got up, splashed water on my face, and went upstairs to open the shop.

I was unshuttering the window when I saw the girl across the street—dark-haired, travel-worn, standing in the shadow of the chandler's awning with her arms folded and her eyes fixed on my door with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it myself for five years.

She wasn't shopping. She was hunting.

And she was watching the shop of the man her sister had died in front of.
