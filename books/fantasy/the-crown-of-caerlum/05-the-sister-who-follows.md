## 5. The Sister Who Follows

Bryn had been watching the charm-seller for three days.

She'd learned patience the hard way—on the herring boats out of Saltmere, where you could stand in one position for six hours with a knife in your hand and your arm wouldn't shake until you let it. Patience was just pain you refused to acknowledge. She was very good at it now.

The charm-seller's name was Ivo Ravensworth. She knew that because names were the first thing she collected about anyone she meant to hurt. He kept a cellar shop off Cordwainer Lane with a crooked sign and a door that stuck, and he sold luck-knots and fever-charms and little braided things that fishermen's wives bought because fear needed somewhere to go. He smiled at his customers like he meant it. He touched every charm before handing it over, thumb pressed flat to the center, quick as a blessing, quicker than a blessing—and Bryn had watched him do it forty times before she understood what she was seeing.

Magic. Small, sly, deniable magic, in a city where they burned people for less.

That wasn't why she was going to kill him. Plenty of hedge-witches in Caerlum; half of them harmless, all of them careful. She was going to kill him because Sera had died three streets from his shop with a hole in her side and blood frozen black on her lips, and because the last person to see her alive—the last name in her sister's ledger, written in that cramped courier's hand Bryn had known since they were girls sharing a slate—was *I. Ravensworth, cellar, Cordwainer Ln.*

Sera had been fast. Sera had been careful. Sera had survived nine years running the Crown's roads with contraband letters sewn into her coat, and she hadn't died of bad luck. She'd died of someone. And when Bryn had turned over her sister's body in the morgue-cold back room of the Saltgate chapel, she'd found two things: a torn scrap of wax seal, red, stamped with a raven, and a smear of something on Sera's palm that smelled faintly of dried herbs and candle smoke. Charm-seller's smell. She'd stood in enough of those shops since to know it everywhere.

Three days of watching. He closed at dusk. He counted his coin twice. He never used the front door after dark—he went out through the coal chute into the alley behind, because a man who hides his gift learns to hide everything, and Bryn recognized the habit the way you recognize a limp.

Tonight she waited in the alley behind the coal chute, in the gap between a tanner's fence and a stack of rain-rotted crates, with her hood up and her knife loose in its sheath and her heart doing something slow and terrible that wasn't beating so much as counting down.

* * *

He came out at dusk exactly, pulling the chute-cover down soft behind him, and Bryn gave him four steps—enough that he'd be away from the cover, not enough that he'd be near the street—before she moved.

She'd planned it clean: blade to the throat from behind, arm locked, question him, get answers, then decide. That was the plan. The plan lasted until she saw the back of his neck, pale and ordinary above his collar, and thought *Sera's neck looked like that*, and then she wasn't asking questions anymore.

Her knife came around toward his kidney instead of resting at his throat, and that single change ruined everything, because he moved.

Not dodged—there was no room to dodge. He *dropped*, sudden and low, like a puppet with cut strings, and her blade rang off the iron chute-cover instead of sliding between his ribs. He rolled. He was clumsy getting up, all elbows, nothing like a fighter, but he got a crate between them and put his back to the tanner's fence and there was a look on his face that Bryn would remember later: not fear exactly. Recognition. Like a man hearing a knock he'd been dreading for years.

"Wait," he said. "Wait—I know what this looks like—"

"You don't know anything about what this looks like." Her voice came out lower than she intended, scraped raw. "You know what it *is*, though."

"I didn't—" He swallowed. His eyes flicked to the street, to the chute, calculating exits, and she let him see her shift to block the alley mouth so he'd stop wasting both their time. "Who sent you? Was it Corvane? Because if the inquisition sent a woman with a boat-knife, they're getting cheap."

"Nobody sent me." She drew the second knife. "I sent myself."

Something moved behind his eyes. He was quick, she'd give him that—not with his body but with his reading of people, the way he took in her stance, her grip, the salt-scars on her knuckles, and arrived somewhere he didn't like.

"You're a courier's kin," he said quietly. "You've got her shoulders."

And that—that—was what broke the last of Bryn's discipline. Later she would understand he'd said it to stall, to humanize himself, a charm-seller's trick with words instead of knots. In the moment it landed like a hand on a fresh burn.

She came at him.

He was faster than he had any right to be, which she'd learn later was Maren's training living in his hands whether he wanted it or not, but he wasn't a fighter and the alley was narrow and grief made her sloppy and strong in equal measure. Her first slash he caught on a crate-lid wrenched up off the rot-pile; the lid split like kindling. Her knee found his thigh and he went sideways into the fence, and she had the knife at his collarbone, actually touching skin, when his hand shot out—not for his own defense, but for the stack of crates beside them, and pulled.

They came down together in an avalanche of rotten wood and old straw. She lost her grip. He scrambled, she grabbed his ankle, he kicked free and gained the alley mouth and turned with—of all the stupid things—a fish-gutting knife from his belt, held wrong, point too high.

"Stay back," he said. His hands were shaking. Hers weren't. Hers were perfectly steady, and somehow that was worse.

"You killed my sister."

"No." Flat. Immediate. No hesitation, no wounded innocence, just the word laid down like a stone. "I didn't. And you don't believe I did, or you'd have done it already instead of talking."

Bryn circled left. He mirrored without seeming to notice he was doing it. "Then explain the ledger."

"What ledger?"

"Sera's delivery book. Last entry: *I. Ravensworth.* You're the only Ivo on Cordwainer Lane. She came here. Three hours later she was dead in a gutter with her throat full of blood." Her voice cracked on *gutter* and she hated it, hated him for hearing it. "So either you killed her, or you're the last thing she trusted before whoever did kill her found her. And I've spent three days deciding which, and I'm tired of deciding."

"She came here," Ivo said. "Gods help me, yes, she came here." He wasn't holding the gutting knife like a weapon anymore; it hung at his side, forgotten. "It was closing time. She was bleeding when she got here—already bleeding, do you understand? Someone had opened her up before she ever reached my door. She fell through it herself. I never even heard her coming, and I hear everyone coming, it's how I've stayed alive in this city—"

"Liar."

"Ask the map," he said.

Bryn stopped circling.

The word sat in the alley between them. *Map.* Sera had carried maps the way priests carried scripture—rolled, sealed, memorized and burned when she could. If there was a map, it was cargo. Cargo meant a run. A run meant someone had paid her, and payment meant a trail, and a trail meant—

"Show me," Bryn said.

* * *

He didn't want to take her inside. She could see it in every line of him, the way a man looks at floodwater climbing his doorstep. Five years of careful invisible life, and here was a stranger with two knives and dead sister's eyes asking to be let in.

"Do you think I care about your secrets?" Bryn said. "I'll find them anyway. This way is faster."

"It's not the secrets." He pushed open the stuck door with his shoulder. "It's that once you see it, you're in it. There's no walking out of this one. I've tried telling myself otherwise for three days and it doesn't take."

The cellar shop was small and smelled of beeswax and dried yarrow, shelves of little charms ranked like a congregation, a cold brazier, a chair with a dark stain on the floor beneath it that Bryn's eye went to at once and stayed on.

"That's hers," she said.

"Some of it. I—" He stopped. Started again, and this time he didn't perform anything; the charm-seller's smoothness was gone, worn through. "I couldn't save her. I'm not a healer. I sell luck to frightened people and I tell myself it's nothing, it's sleight of hand, and then a woman bleeds out on my floor and I learn exactly what all my nothing adds up to." He crouched at a chest beneath the counter, hesitated with his hand on the lid. "She pressed it into my hands. Sealed. She said—" His voice did something strange. "She said *the king is dead*. Then she said *find the ravensworn*. Then she died. Those were her last words and I've been turning them over like stones ever since and none of them make sense except that she chose me, out of everyone in Caerlum, and I don't know why."

*Ravensworn.* The word snagged on something in Bryn, some story from childhood, a nurse's tale—but Sera's face was in front of her, gray and still, and she shoved the story down.

"Open it."

He lifted the lid and unwrapped oilcloth, and there it was: a map, folded small, sealed with red wax. Even half-crushed, even bloodstained along one edge, Bryn knew the seal the way you know your own name written in someone else's hand. Sera had carved that stamp herself at fourteen, from a cork, sitting on the end of their mother's bed, because a courier needed a mark and Sera had always loved ravens—they went where they pleased and answered to no one.

Bryn reached for it and her hands finally shook.

She turned the map over twice. Pressed her thumb to the seal, though there was no need; she'd have known it blind, by the slightly lopsided beak, by the nick in the wing where the cork had crumbled. Nobody forged that. Nobody alive could have. It was a private thing, a sister-thing, a thing Sera had laughed about—*if you ever get a letter with an ugly raven on it, Bryn, it means I love you and I'm in trouble.*

Both, then. Both at once.

"When," Bryn said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "When did she give you this."

"Dusk. Four nights ago."

Four nights ago. Bryn had spent those four nights sharpening knives and composing questions and rehearsing, in the worst hours, what it would feel like to put steel in the man who'd killed her sister. Four nights hating a man who had, by his own account, held her sister while she died, and hidden the body—she made him say it, made him describe the burning, and listened with her jaw tight and her stomach turning, not because she doubted him anymore but because she needed to know what Sera's last hours had cost someone else too. Misery wanted company. That was another thing the boats taught you.

"Why hide her?" Bryn asked. "Why not go to the inquisitors, tell them a courier died in your shop? Clean hands. They might even have believed you."

"Because they'd have asked what she was carrying." He wrapped the map again, slowly, and Bryn noticed he didn't offer it to her and she noticed she didn't ask. "Because whatever killed her will come looking for it, and the inquisitors answer to the Crown, and if the Crown is what she was running from—" He stopped. Laughed, short and unhappy. "You see the shape of it. I've spent five years being nobody. Being nobody works. And now I'm holding the one object in Caerlum that everybody dangerous wants, and I can't give it back to her, and I can't burn it, and I don't even know why I can't burn it except that she died pressing it into my hands and it felt like—" He searched. "Like being handed something. Not a package. A *task*."

"The ravensworn," Bryn said. "She said find the ravensworn. What does that mean?"

"I don't know." But he said it a half-beat too late, and his eyes went, briefly, involuntarily, to his own hand—to the back of it, the wrist, as if checking for a mark—and Bryn had spent her whole life reading people across water and weather, and she filed that away the way she filed everything: sharp side down.

Outside, boots on cobblestones. Two pairs, unhurried, the particular unhurried rhythm of men walking a beat they owned. Patrol. Ivo went still in a way that was practiced, total, a lamp turned down to a coal. Bryn watched him do it and understood suddenly, completely, the shape of his life: five years of this. Five years of listening for boots.

The boots passed. Neither of them breathed until they had.

"So," Bryn said. She sheathed her knives—one, then the other, deliberately, letting him watch each motion, a courtesy in her language. "Here's where we are. My sister sealed that map and spent her blood delivering it to you. I don't know yet if that makes you the luckiest man in Caerlum or the deadest. But you're going to open it, and you're going to find out what *ravensworn* means, and I'm going to be there when you do. Not because I trust you." She stepped close enough that he had to tilt his head back to keep her eyes. "Because if anyone comes for that map, they come through me first. And because if it turns out you're lying to me, Ivo Ravensworth, I won't need an alley to finish this."

"And if I'm not lying?"

"Then you're the last person my sister trusted," Bryn said, "and that buys you more than trust ever could."

He looked at her for a long moment. Something in his face shifted—not softened, exactly. Recalibrated. A man alone for five years, discovering that company could arrive as a knife at your throat and still be company.

"There's a festival at week's end," he said. "Gates stay open past dark. If we're leaving Caerlum—if we're following wherever this leads—we leave then, in the crowd, or not at all."

"*We*," Bryn repeated.

"You said you'd be there when I open it." He set the map on the counter between them, red seal up, ugly raven staring at the ceiling. "Fair warning. I looked at it for three days and it showed me nothing. Whatever's in it, it doesn't open for eyes alone."

Bryn looked at the seal. Her sister's lopsided raven. *I love you and I'm in trouble.*

"Then we'd better find out what it opens *for*," she said.
