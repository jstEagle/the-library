## 6. Terms of Uneasy Company

**BRYN**

The knife stayed at his throat because it was easier than the questions.

Bryn had spent the whole walk from the alley rehearsing what she'd do with him—drag him to the Watch-house, drag him to the harbor, drag him somewhere dark and make him say Sera's name until the words stopped sounding like lies. But the map changed things. The map, with its wax seal bitten by her sister's teeth the way Sera always bit seals when she was nervous, sitting there in this stranger's shaking hands like an accusation.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat. He was good at sitting, she'd give him that—the way a man is good at something he's practiced for years. He folded himself onto the stool behind his little counter of trinkets like he could make himself smaller by agreeing quickly.

"You knew her." Not a question. She'd seen it in the alley, in the way he flinched at Sera's name before she'd even said it.

"I didn't kill her," Ivo said. "I want that on the record before anything else."

"There's no record. There's just you and me and a dead woman's map."

He swallowed. The knife followed the swallow. "She came into my shop at closing. Bleeding. She pressed the map into my hands and said—" His voice caught, and Bryn watched the catch happen and hated herself for believing it might be real. "She said *the king is dead, find the ravensworn.* Then she died. On my floor."

"And you burned her."

Something moved through his face—guilt, yes, but under it something else, something almost like shame at being caught being practical. "I burned her so the inquisitors wouldn't find her and then find me and then find anyone who'd ever bought a charm from me. Including you, if you'd come asking after your sister. It wasn't mercy. It was math."

"Math." Bryn laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You did arithmetic on my sister's corpse."

"Yes." He didn't look away. That surprised her. Men usually looked away. "And I'd do it again. Because the alternative was Corvane's people tracing her here, and Corvane doesn't do arithmetic. He does erasure."

The name landed in the room like cold water. Bryn knew it. Everyone in the courier trade knew it—inquisitors didn't chase couriers, they chased *routes*, and Sera had run the Vessany line twice a season for three years. If Corvane had Sera, Corvane had the line, and everyone who'd ever carried along it.

"She was running something," Bryn said quietly. "Before she died. She sent me a letter a month ago. Four words. *If I go quiet—*"

"—and nothing after?"

"*And nothing after.*" Bryn lowered the knife at last, mostly because her arm was tired and partly because killing him now would mean never learning what Sera had been carrying. "So here's how this goes, charm-seller. You're going to take that map wherever it wants to go. And I'm coming with you. Not as your friend. As your jailer. Every mile, every meal, every night—you don't piss without me knowing where. When I decide you killed her, I'll kill you. Until then, you stay alive because she trusted you with her last breath, and I want to know why."

"That's insane," he said.

"It's grief. Close enough."

He looked at her for a long moment. She watched him do the thing he clearly did best—calculate. Weighing the madwoman with the knife against whatever was waiting for him if he ran alone. She watched him arrive at the only answer there was.

"When do we leave?" he said.

**IVO**

They left during the Festival of Lanterns, because Bryn said the gates would be crowded and drunk, and because Ivo's whole life was built on the principle that crowds were cover.

He spent the last afternoon in his shop destroying evidence. Not burning it this time—he'd learned that lesson—but unmaking it, unpicking the luck-knots back into plain cord, scraping the sigils off river-stones until they were just stones. Five years of careful work, dissolved in an afternoon. It should have hurt more than it did. Mostly it felt like taking off a coat he'd outgrown.

Bryn sat on his stairs and watched him do it, sharpening a knife that was already sharp.

"You're quiet," he said, to fill the space.

"You're chatty for a man packing to run."

"I talk when I'm frightened. It's a flaw."

"Noted." The whetstone whispered. "What are you frightened of? Besides me."

He considered lying, decided against it—she had a way of smelling lies, or maybe she just smelled everything, all the time, like a dog that had been kicked too often. "That the map is what it looks like. That your sister died carrying something that matters. Because if it matters, then I can't go back to not mattering, and I've spent five years getting very good at not mattering."

The whetstone stopped. "That's the saddest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"It gets worse. Keep me alive long enough and you'll hear sadder."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. It wasn't quite a smile. It was the place where a smile would go, cleared and leveled, like a lot where a house burned down. But Ivo found himself absurdly pleased to have put something there.

"Gates at moonrise," she said. "Wear something dull. Don't charm anyone. Don't *be* charming. And if I say run, you run, and if I say drop, you drop, and if I say die, you'd better be convincing."

"Do you give that speech to all your prisoners?"

"You're the first." She stood, sheathing the knife. "Most people aren't worth the speech."

* * *

The Festival of Lanterns turned Caerlum into a river of light. Thousands of paper lanterns went drifting up off the rooftops toward the dark, each one carrying a wish nobody believed in anymore, and the streets below ran thick with wine and song and the particular lawlessness of a city that had been told, once a year, it was allowed to be happy.

Ivo moved through it in a borrowed fishmonger's coat, Bryn half a step behind him with her hand near her knife and her eyes doing constant work. They were two more bodies in the crowd. That was the whole trick. Be nobody, among ten thousand nobodies.

The Northgate checkpoint had doubled guards for the festival, but doubled guards at a festival were bored guards, and bored guards wanted to be anywhere else. A sergeant with a waxed mustache was waving through families and stopping young men traveling alone, which meant Ivo needed to stop being a young man traveling alone.

"Lantern-sellers," Bryn announced brightly, before Ivo had agreed to any such thing. She thrust a bundle of cheap lanterns into his arms—she'd bought them, gods knew when, gods knew with whose coin—and looped her arm through his. "Last batch of the night, love. Mind the wet paint."

The sergeant looked at them. Looked at the lanterns. Looked at Bryn, who smiled at him with the specific warmth of a woman who has decided you are harmless, and Ivo watched a trained professional decide, in real time, that these two tired vendors were beneath notice.

"Festival rate's doubled," the sergeant said.

"Robbery," Bryn said fondly, and paid it, and pulled Ivo through the gate before he'd finished counting coins.

They walked. The city noise fell away behind them in slow layers—first the music, then the shouting, then the bells, until there was only the road and the dark and the lanterns still rising over the walls behind them like slow orange stars.

Ivo let out a breath he felt he'd been holding for five years.

"You did well," Bryn said.

"I did nothing. You did everything."

"Yes. Remember that." But she said it lightly, and there it was again, that cleared lot where a smile used to be.

They made camp in a stand of pines off the Vessany road, no fire, sharing a blanket because the night was cold and neither of them mentioned it. Ivo lay staring up at the black lattice of branches and listened to Bryn breathing beside him, awake, watchful, her knife-hand resting on her chest like a promise.

"Bryn."

"Don't talk."

"Sera bit her wax seals. When she was nervous. You knew that."

A long silence. Then: "Since we were children. She thought it made them look official."

"The seal on the map was bitten." He said it gently, the way you set down something fragile. "I checked. Twice. It's hers. Whatever else I am, I'm the man she chose. I'm sorry it was me instead of you."

In the dark, her breathing changed—caught, resumed, steadied by force. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough as gravel.

"If you're lying about that seal, I'll take a very long time."

"I know," Ivo said. "Sleep. I'll take first watch."

"You don't know how to take a watch."

"How hard can it be? Stay awake. Look at things."

A sound escaped her—half laugh, half sob, smothered immediately, as if it had escaped custody. "Gods help me," she murmured. "I'm traveling with an idiot."

But she slept. Eventually. And Ivo kept the watch, looking at things, holding the map against his chest like a coal he couldn't put down and couldn't drop, while somewhere far behind them the lanterns of Caerlum rose and rose and went out, one by one, over a kingdom that was forgetting itself.

Somewhere ahead, in the dark past the pines, a wolf began to sing.
