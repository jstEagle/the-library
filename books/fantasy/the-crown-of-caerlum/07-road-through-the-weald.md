## 7. Road Through the Weald

The Weald began where the road stopped pretending to be a road.

For two days out of Caerlum they had followed the old pilgrim track through farmland gone feral—orchards swallowed by elder, hedgerows grown into walls, farmhouses standing open to the sky with their doors swinging like the owners had stepped out mid-supper and simply never come back. Ivo had expected that. What he hadn't expected was how quiet it was. No dogs. No crows. Even the wind moved through the trees like it was trying not to be heard.

"Nobody lives here anymore," Bryn said, for the third time since dawn.

"People live everywhere," Ivo said. "They just stop remembering why."

She gave him a look over her shoulder—the look she'd been giving him for two days, the one that said *I haven't decided yet whether to kill you*. She carried it well. She carried everything well: the long knife at her hip, her sister's satchel across her back, a water skin she refilled without ever offering it to him first. He was learning her habits the way he'd once learned the habits of inquisitors' patrols—not because he wanted to, but because his life depended on reading people accurately.

Bryn's habits were simple. She walked ahead. She slept light. She checked the map—Ivo's map now, though she treated it as hers by inheritance of blood—every morning and every night, tracing Sera's route with one finger as if the finger might tell her something the ink couldn't.

And she watched him. Always him. Especially when he did anything with his hands.

He'd been careful. Five years of careful, ground into habit so deep it had become personality. On the road there was no shop counter to hide behind, no sleight of hand dressed up as street performance, no crowd to lose himself in. Just open country and a woman who had already decided magic had killed her sister, or would have, if Sera hadn't died of a knife wound instead. Bryn hadn't said any of this aloud. She didn't need to. It lived in the set of her jaw every time Ivo flexed his fingers.

So he kept his hands still. He built their fire with flint and steel like an honest man. He found water by looking for the plants that grew near it, the way any traveler could. When the rain came on the second evening he rigged a shelter from oiled canvas and rope knots, all of it plain craft, nothing more, and if the knots held better than knots had any right to hold—if his fingers moved over them with a fluency that wasn't entirely learned—that was nobody's business but his own.

"You're doing it again," Bryn said that night, watching him tie off the last knot.

"Doing what?"

"That thing where you almost do something and then don't." She sat against a fallen trunk, knife across her knees, sharpening it with slow, even strokes. "Like a man who's given up drinking walking past a tavern with his eyes shut."

Ivo sat back on his heels. The firelight made her face half-shadow, and in the shadowed half she looked very much like her sister. That was the worst part. Every time she turned her head he saw Sera dying on his cellar floor, and he could see that every time *she* looked at him, she saw it too.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

"Liar," she said, without heat. It wasn't an accusation anymore. It was starting to sound like a nickname.

* * *

The wolves came on the third night, in the deep fold of the Weald where the track dipped between two ridges and the trees closed overhead like a roof.

Ivo heard them before he saw them—or rather, he heard the absence of them, the way the night went suddenly organized, every small sound ceasing at once the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters. He stopped mid-stride. Ahead of him Bryn took three more steps before she registered that he'd stopped, and then she heard it too: the soft displacement of bodies moving through undergrowth, more than one, flanking.

"How many?" she breathed.

"Four. Maybe five." His mouth had gone dry. "They're cutting us off from the ridge."

"They don't usually come at people. Not unless—" She stopped. He knew how the sentence ended. *Not unless they're starving, and nothing out here is eating anything.* The Weald had been hollowed the same way the village had been hollowed, the same way everything the tithe-collectors touched was hollowed. Whatever hunger lived in these woods, it wasn't natural hunger.

The first one came out of the dark on their left, low to the ground, and it was wrong in a way Ivo's hindbrain understood before his eyes finished reporting. Too thin. Its coat hung off its ribs like a wet sack, and its eyes caught the last grey light with a flat shine that had nothing behind it—no wariness, no calculation, none of the ancient negotiation that existed between wolves and men. It looked at them the way floodwater looks at a door.

"Bryn," Ivo said quietly. "Don't run."

"Don't tell me what to—"

The second wolf came out on the right, and the third behind them, closing the dip, and Bryn's knife was out and her back was finding his back, which was either trust or arithmetic, and there was no time to work out which.

They were going to die here. He understood that with a strange clarity, the way he'd understood it in the cellar when Sera bled out—some situations are simply past the reach of cleverness. A knife against five starving things that felt no fear. Flint and steel against animals that no longer feared fire. Everything he had was craft, and craft was not going to be enough.

Everything he had was craft, except the thing that wasn't.

His hands were shaking. Not from fear—from the other thing, the old thing, the current that lived under his skin like a river under ice, the thing Maren had spent three years teaching him to shape and he'd spent five years teaching himself to bury. He could feel it rising now on its own, answering the animal emergency of his body, pressing up against the seal he'd kept on it the way water presses at a dam. All he had to do was let go. All he had to do was break, in front of the one person in the world whose opinion could actually hurt him, the rule that had kept him alive and invisible and alone for five years.

*They'll trace it,* Maren had warned him, once, in another life. *Magic leaves a wake, boy. Every working is a shout into the dark, and there are ears in Caerlum that never sleep.*

Corvane's ears. Corvane, who had stood in Ivo's burned shop with his courteous voice and his patient eyes, certain that the courier had come somewhere, told *someone*, left *something*. If Ivo shouted into the dark now, the dark would answer.

And if he didn't, the wolves would answer first.

The lead wolf lunged.

There was no decision in it. Later, trying to reconstruct the moment, Ivo could find no moment of choice at all—only his hands coming up, only the words leaving him in the old tongue Maren had beaten into him, only the cold clean feeling of the current running out through his palms for the first time in five years, like a breath held so long it had become the whole of him finally released.

Light bloomed between his fingers. Not firelight—colder than that, whiter, the color of moonrise—and it hit the wolves not as a blow but as a *word*, a single syllable of command older than their blood: *away*. The lead wolf skidded in the leaf-mold as if it had struck glass. The others froze mid-circle. And Ivo, gasping, shaking, poured everything he had into the oldest hedge-witch working there was, the one Maren had taught him first, the one meant for exactly this: *the turning aside*.

*Go,* he said, in the language that wasn't a language. *This is not your road. Go home.*

The wolves turned. All of them, together, like a thought changing direction. They flowed back into the dark between the trees, unhurried, unharmed, and the night closed behind them, and the Weald was quiet again in the ordinary way, the way of wind and nothing else.

Ivo's knees gave out. He sat down hard in the leaf-mold, hands still raised, still faintly glowing at the fingertips like banked coals, and stared at them.

Five years. Five years of flint and steel and sleight of hand and luck-knots sold as luck-knots when they were luck, actually, just hidden inside craft like a pill inside honey. Five years of being a man who *almost* did things. And now it was done, undone, broken open in a ditch in the Weald in front of the one witness he couldn't afford.

He didn't look up at Bryn. He couldn't. He kept his eyes on his own hands and waited for the blade, or the word, or the walk-away into the dark—all of which he deserved, all of which he'd earned by lying to her since the alley, since before the alley, since the day he'd taken her sister's map and run.

"Well," Bryn said.

He flinched.

"That's the most useful thing I've seen you do," she said.

He looked up despite himself. She was standing over him with her knife still drawn, breathing hard, and her face in the dimness was—unreadable was the wrong word. It was readable. He just didn't believe what he read on it. There was no disgust in it. There was no fear. There was something he'd stopped expecting from anyone years ago, something he'd forgotten the shape of: she was looking at him the way you look at a tool that turns out to be real.

"You—" His voice cracked; he cleared his throat. "You saw."

"I have eyes."

"It's outlawed. In Caerlum, it's—they hang people for less, they—"

"We're not in Caerlum." She sheathed the knife and crouched down in front of him, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the firelight reflected in her eyes. "We're in the Weald, and we were about to be eaten, and you did whatever that was, and we're not. So." She shrugged, one shoulder, the way she did when emotion was trying to get past her and she wouldn't let it. "Thank you."

Ivo stared at her. "That's it? Thank you?"

"What did you want? A lecture?" Something moved across her face—grief, maybe, surfacing and being pushed back down, the way it always was. "My sister ran messages through half this kingdom for people like you. Hedge-witches, charm-sellers, the ones the inquisitors grind up for existing. She never once came home and called any of them monsters. She called them *careful*. She used to say careful people were the bravest ones she knew, because they were frightened all the time and did the work anyway." Her jaw tightened. "So no. No lecture. You hid a thing to stay alive. Everyone I've ever loved has done worse for less reason."

The words went through him somewhere unprotected. He looked away, back at his hands, which had finally stopped glowing, and found that his eyes were burning, which was absurd, which was exhaustion, which he refused to examine.

"I swore off it," he said, mostly to the dark. "After I left my teacher. I swore I'd never use it again. Not once. Not even a warming-cantrip in winter. I've been—there's a hole in me shaped like it, and I've been walking around the hole for five years telling myself the hole was the point."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight the hole was going to get us killed."

Bryn nodded slowly, as if he'd said something reasonable, as if five years of self-erasure were a reasonable thing to discuss beside a dead fire in a haunted wood. Then she stood, and held out her hand.

He looked at it.

"Fire," she said. "It's freezing, and apparently you can do it without flint. Show me."

And that was the strangest part—not the wolves, not the light, not even the breaking of the vow. It was this: sitting in the wet leaves while Bryn Kell held out her hand and waited, without judgment, without fear, for him to make fire appear in it. As if it were simple. As if it were allowed. As if he were a man with a skill rather than a man with a crime.

He took her hand. Her grip was calloused and strong and utterly steady, and he raised his other palm toward the dead fire-ring, and spoke the small warm word Maren had taught him second, and flame rose up out of the char like a flower opening—easy, gentle, obedient, the way it had always been before he'd learned to be afraid of it.

Bryn watched it burn for a long moment.

"Sera would have liked you," she said finally, and then, before he could answer, before the grief could land on either of them: "Sleep. I'll take first watch. And Ivo?"

He paused, halfway to his bedroll.

"Do it again next time. Don't wait until we're dying." She settled against the fallen trunk, knife across her knees, eyes on the dark. "Whatever you were saving it for—it's not worth more than staying alive."

He lay down with the fire warming his face and the current still humming faintly under his skin, unfamiliar and familiar at once, like a room he'd grown up in and locked for years and was now standing inside again. It should have felt like ruin. It felt, instead, terrifyingly, like relief.

He was still turning that over when sleep took him—and he never learned what woke Bryn in the grey hour before dawn, or why, when he opened his eyes, she was standing at the edge of the camp staring down the track they'd come by, knife drawn, at a single rider sitting motionless in the mist where no rider should be.
