## 8. The Village That Forgot Itself

The village had no name on any sign.

That was the first wrong thing. Ivo noticed it from the crest of the hill, where the road bent down through barley fields gone half-wild, and there should have been a post with a carved board—every village in the Weald had one, usually rotted, often illegible, but *there*. He'd spent five years memorizing the small furniture of ordinary places precisely so he could tell, at a glance, what was normal and what was a trap. A missing name-sign was not a trap. It was something else.

"Sign's gone," Bryn said beside him. She'd seen it too. Of course she had. She missed nothing; it was her whole personality, as far as Ivo could tell—grief with excellent eyesight.

"Fell?" Tam offered from behind them, wheezing slightly on the downhill grade. The archivist walked like a man carrying invisible books. "Weather. Rot. Villages replace them."

"When?" Bryn said. "Show me the pile of fresh-cut posts. Show me the carpenter's shavings."

Tam had no answer for that, so he shut his mouth, which Ivo was learning was Tam's second-best quality after his memory.

They came down into the village at late afternoon, when the light went long and gold and made even poor places look kind. This one should have looked kind: thatched roofs, a crooked well, geese arguing in the lane, smoke from cookfires smelling of onions and river fish. Everything present. Everything correct.

Except nobody looked at them.

That was the second wrong thing. In every village Ivo had ever passed through—and he'd passed through dozens in five years of careful invisibility—strangers were an event. Children trailed them. Dogs announced them. Old women priced their boots from doorways and decided within seconds whether they were beggars, soldiers, or taxmen. Being unseen was his craft, but being *unnoticed by an entire settlement* was different. It felt like walking through a room where everyone had died politely upright.

A woman stood at the well with a bucket. She watched them approach with mild gray eyes and then, as they drew level, her gaze slid off them like rain off waxed canvas.

"Good day," Ivo tried.

She turned to him. She smiled—a pleasant, vacant, perfectly shaped smile. "Good day," she agreed, and went back to her bucket.

Bryn's hand had drifted to her knife. Ivo shook his head once. *Not yet.*

He tried the baker next, or the man he assumed was the baker, given the flour to his elbows. "Fine village," Ivo said, pitching his voice friendly, harmless, the charm-seller's voice. "What do you call it?"

The man considered this with genuine effort, brow furrowing like a student facing an unfair examination. "It's..." He looked around—at the houses, the well, the geese—as if the answer might be written somewhere. "It's ours," he said finally, satisfied.

"That's not a name."

"It's what we have." Still smiling. Still pleasant. And underneath the smile, when Ivo leaned close enough, something flickered—the briefest contraction, the way a face moves just before crying.

* * *

They took a room at the inn because there was nothing else to take, and because leaving at dusk would look worse than staying. The innkeeper charged them without knowing how much things cost. Ivo watched her count coins three times, mouthing numbers, losing her place, starting again. When she finally arrived at a figure, relief crossed her face like sun through cloud.

"You've had collectors here," Tam said quietly, once they'd climbed to the room. He sat on the bed with his satchel open, already writing, because writing was how Tam held onto things. His handwriting had gotten worse since the Weald road; Ivo pretended not to notice.

"The tithe wagons," Bryn said. She was at the window, watching the street. "Sera mentioned them. Crown-tithe collectors, riding circuit twice a year. Everyone pays."

"Everyone pays *taxes*," Tam said. "Tithes are different. Tithes are owed to the Crown directly, in person, and the collectors are inquisitors wearing softer robes." He tapped his pen against the page. "When did they come?"

Ivo went down and asked. Or tried to. The innkeeper had to think about it the way the baker had thought about his village's name—with visible labor, reaching for something that wasn't where she'd left it.

"A season ago?" she guessed. "Maybe two. Men in gray. Very polite. They set up tables in the square and everyone came and..." She stopped. Her pleasant face worked for a moment. "Everyone came," she repeated, as though the rest of the sentence had been sold.

"And they took?"

"Tithes." She smiled her shaped smile. "Everyone pays."

* * *

Night fell, and the village grew stranger by candlelight.

At supper, Ivo counted fourteen villagers in the common room, and not one of them spoke to another by name. They said *friend*, and *neighbor*, and *you there*, and once—chillingly—a woman called her own child *little one* with the careful genericness of someone who has lost a word and is covering for it. The child didn't seem to mind. The child, Ivo realized with a cold sliding feeling, probably didn't know its own name either.

An old man by the fire was trying to sing. It was clearly a song he had known all his life—you could see the shape of it in him, the way his body swayed to a rhythm his mouth couldn't find. He got four notes in, lost them, laughed at himself, and started over. Four notes. Lost. Laughter. Again. Nobody joined in. Nobody remembered the words either, but nobody seemed troubled, and that was the worst part. Grief requires knowing what you've lost.

"They don't hurt," Tam murmured. He'd barely touched his food. "That's what no one understands about it. Memory-loss isn't pain. It's *smoothness*. It's a stone worn round. They're content. They're—" He stopped, jaw tight.

"You've seen it before," Bryn said.

"I catalogued reports. Eleven villages in the northern reaches, six years back. I wrote a memorandum. My superiors filed it under *agricultural irregularities*." His laugh had no humor in it at all. "Then I kept asking questions, and then I was exiled, and now I'm sitting in one of my footnotes eating stew."

Ivo ate his stew. It was good stew. That felt obscene.

He was tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix. Three days on the road, wolves two nights past, magic used openly for the first time in years and the strange raw ache that came after, like a muscle he'd forgotten he owned. He wanted his cellar shop. He wanted the small safe rituals: bolt the door, bank the fire, count the day's coins, know exactly who he was and exactly who he pretended to be.

Instead he was in a nameless village full of smooth-faced strangers, guarding a dead courier's map, running from an inquisitor who spoke to him like an uncle.

*I am Ivo Ravensworth,* he thought, the way he did every night, a small prayer against erasure. *I sell charms. I have no family. I came from—*

The thought snagged.

It was nothing. It was less than nothing—a half-second hitch, like a stair that isn't where your foot expects it. But Ivo had built his entire life on noticing hitches, on reading the tiny wrongnesses other people stepped over. He reached for the thought again, carefully, the way you reach for a splinter.

*I came from...*

Maren's cottage. The hedge-road. The smell of feverfew and smoke. He had those. He had those images fine. But somewhere behind them, further back, there should have been more—there should have been a before-Maren, and when he reached for it his mind presented him with a smooth grey surface, polite and blank, like the innkeeper counting coins.

"Ivo." Bryn's voice. "You've gone white."

"Fine," he said. "Tired."

She studied him with those grief-sharpened eyes, and he knew she didn't believe him, and he knew she was filing it away the way she filed everything, and he found—to his genuine surprise—that he didn't entirely mind being seen. That was new. That was dangerous. He put it away with the rest of the things he refused to examine.

* * *

He woke past midnight to the sound of the old man singing again.

Four notes. Lost. Silence. Then, faintly, weeping—not the old man; someone upstairs, or outside, or inside the walls of the whole village, it was impossible to say. Ivo lay in the dark and did the exercise Tam had taught him on the road, the archivist's trick for a failing memory: recite, verify, record.

"My name is Ivo Ravensworth. I was born—" 

Born where?

The grey surface again. Smooth as the well-water. He sat up too fast and the room tilted.

*I have a mother.* Yes—he could see hands, kneading bread, flour to the wrist, a chapped knuckle. He could not see her face. He could not remember her voice. He could remember Maren telling him to scrub the cauldron, could remember it in perfect sensory detail, and his mother's face was a locked room, and had it always been? Had he simply never looked? People forgot faces; that was normal; that was—

That was what the baker would say. *People forget things. It's what we have.*

He lit the candle with fingers that weren't quite steady and took out the map.

Under candlelight it was inert as ever—wax seal, bloodstain, faded ink showing roads he'd half-memorized. But he didn't need to unseal anything tonight. He just needed to hold it and feel the pull, faint and constant, like a current under ice. The map wanted to go somewhere. The map had been going somewhere for years, waiting for someone with the blood to read it, Sera had died getting it partway there, and now it pulled at him across a dark table in a village that had traded its name for nothing it could remember receiving.

And beneath the pull, beneath his fear, he felt the other thing. The thing he'd been refusing to feel since the hilltop.

Something was drinking here.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. There were no black robes, no chanting, no obvious evil. There was just a village being gently, thoroughly hollowed—a tithe paid in the only coin the Crown truly valued—and the drinker was patient, and vast, and very far away, and it had left a straw in this place and a straw in eleven others and it was, right now, at the edge of his own mind, tasting.

*You're imagining it,* he told himself. *Fear does this. Tam's stories got into you.*

But he thought of his mother's face and the surface came up smooth, and he thought of his mother's face *again*, harder, angry now, and for one terrible half-second he saw it—clear, young, laughing at something he couldn't hear—and then it went, like a fish sounding, and left only water.

He put his head in his hands over the map and breathed until the shaking stopped.

When he looked up, Bryn was standing in the doorway with her knife drawn and her eyes on the window, and Tam was behind her holding a candle, and neither of them had slept either, and none of them said anything at all for a long moment.

"We leave at dawn," Bryn said finally.

"Yes," Ivo said.

"And Ivo." Her voice dropped low, rough. "In the morning, ask the child its name. Before we go. I want to watch its face when it tries to answer."

"Why?"

"Because if it can't," Bryn said, "then whatever took this village is still hungry. And if it's still hungry, it will follow us. And if it follows us—" She stopped. She looked at the map on the table, at the bloodstain that used to be her sister, and finished in a whisper:

"Then we need to get wherever it's going before it finishes its meal. Because I don't think it stops at villages. And I don't think it stops at memories."

Outside, faintly, the old man began the song again. Four notes. Lost. Silence.

And then—for the first time—a fifth, hanging alone in the dark like a hand reaching up out of deep water, before the silence swallowed everything.
