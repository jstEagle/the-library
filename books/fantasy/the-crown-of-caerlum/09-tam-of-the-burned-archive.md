## 9. Tam of the Burned Archive

The trouble with knowing everything was that it made forgetting impossible.

Tam Vessic sat in the corner booth of the Drowned Bell, the waystation town's only tavern worth the name, and did what he had done every night for six years: he drank until the catalogue in his head went quiet. It never worked. The catalogue didn't care about ale or cheap wine or even the good plum brandy they'd had here once, three years ago, before the trade road shifted and took all the good things with it.

Item four thousand one hundred and twelve: *Royal Signet of Aldous III, gold, garnet, lost at the ford of Merrow, year 612.* He could see it. He could see every item, every page, every marginal note in his own cramped hand, because a mind trained for thirty years to hold an archive does not release its holdings just because the archive has been burned and its keeper thrown into the street.

He raised two fingers toward the taproom girl. She'd stopped asking how many. That was the one kindness left in his life, and she probably didn't know she was giving it.

The Drowned Bell smelled of wet wool, tallow smoke, and the river mud that never fully dried out of anything this close to the water. Waystation towns were all the same: a bridge, a shrine nobody tended, a tavern, and people passing through who wanted to forget where they'd been. Tam had made a profession of remembering, and the world's punishment was to strand him in the one place built entirely for forgetting.

He was on his fourth cup when the door opened and the cold came in wearing two strangers.

* * *

He noticed them the way he noticed everything now — cataloguing, helplessly. A woman, mid-twenties, dark hair cut short and practical, moving like a soldier though her clothes said fisher-kin from the coast. A knife at her belt worn high, for drawing seated. Grief on her the way mud is on a boot: dried in, ground deep, part of the structure now.

And behind her, a man who moved wrong for the room. Not badly. Carefully. He stood with his shoulders drawn in and his eyes doing constant arithmetic — exits, faces, hands near weapons. Tam had seen that arithmetic before, on scholars caught with forbidden folios, on couriers who knew too much. It was the posture of a man being hunted, or a man who had been hunted so long the hunt had become his skeleton.

They took a table near the wall, backs to it, facing the door. Amateurs would have taken the hearth seat. These two weren't amateurs; they were just tired enough to be slow.

The woman spoke to the serving girl briefly. The man kept watching the room. And then — this was the interesting part, item four thousand nine hundred and something, filed under *patterns I cannot stop seeing* — the man glanced at the woman's pack, and Tam saw the edge of something wrapped in oilcloth, and he saw the man look away from it quickly, the way you look away from a wound.

A map. Or something shaped like one. Sealed. Bloodstained, if Tam's eyes still worked, and they always worked, that was the curse of them.

He should have gone back to his drink. Six years of exile had taught him the survival value of blindness. But knowledge was a hunger, and hunger doesn't care about survival, and so Tam Vessic picked up his cup, weaved across the taproom with the practiced drunkenness that was half real and half costume, and dropped into the bench across from them without asking.

"Buy you both a drink," he said. "You've got the look of folk carrying news."

"We're not carrying anything," the woman said.

"Everyone's carrying something." He gestured at the oilcloth corner peeking from her pack. "That, for instance."

The knife was out and under his chin before the taproom girl finished pouring. Fast. Very fast. Tam didn't flinch, partly from courage and mostly because his reflexes had pickled years ago.

"Bryn," the man said quietly. "Don't."

"He looked."

"I know he looked." The man studied Tam with those careful eyes, and Tam watched him do the arithmetic again, and this time the sum apparently came out *harmless*, because the man added, "He's drunk."

"I'm exactly drunk enough," Tam said, "to say true things. You want to be careful around men like me. Sober liars are common. Drunk truth-tellers are rare as hen's teeth, and twice as dangerous."

Bryn — the name settled into his catalogue automatically, *subject: Bryn, sister presumably of the courier Sera, grief-pattern consistent* — narrowed her eyes. "What did you call me?"

"Nothing. Yet." He set down his cup. His hands were steady. They were always steady when it mattered; it was only between matters that they shook. "But I'll make you a bargain. Let me buy your next round, tell me which road you came in by, and I'll tell you something worth more than wine."

"We don't—"

"The king is dead," Tam said.

* * *

The silence afterward had texture. Tam had spent thirty years reading rooms the way other men read documents, and this silence read like a confession.

Bryn's knife hadn't moved. The man — Ivo, he'd learn later, but for now *the careful one, subject unidentified* — had gone very still, the stillness of a man listening for footsteps above a floorboard he knows is loose.

"You're mad," Bryn said finally.

"Quite possibly. Exile does that. But madness and accuracy aren't mutually exclusive. Ask any royal archivist." He smiled, and felt how bad the smile must look. "Which I was. Thirty-one years. Senior Keeper of the Restricted Stacks, before they burned them."

"The Restricted Stacks burned?" The careful one leaned forward despite himself. Curiosity — there it was, the same hunger Tam recognized in his own chest. "When?"

"Six years ago. Officially: accidental fire, unsalvageable losses, tragic." Tam turned his cup slowly on the scarred wood. "Unofficially: the Lord Inquisitor Corvane himself gave the order, after I asked one question too many about an accession record."

"What record?" the man asked.

"The coronation of King Aldous the Fourth." Tam let the name sit. "The current king. Beloved of the church. Voice of continuity. The man whose face is on every tithe notice from here to the capital." He drank. "There is no coronation record. No oath register entry. No anointing inventory. For a king crowned nineteen years ago, in the most documented ceremony in Caerlum's history, there is *nothing*. Do you understand? We recorded the number of candles. We recorded the weight of the crown. There is no record of the man."

"People lose paperwork," Bryn said, but her voice had changed. He'd hooked something in her.

"Not us. Not then. I was there. I signed the intake ledger myself, the week before, for the regalia transport." He tapped the table once, twice. "So I pulled the older records. And I found the last verified sighting of King Aldous the Third — alive, well, hunting party at Caerlum Wood — dated twenty-two years back. Then nothing. No death record. No funeral. No interment. A king of Caerlum does not simply stop existing in the paper, friend. Kings die in triplicate."

"Then what did you find?"

"I found," Tam said, "a single requisition order. One line. *Transport of the Hollow Crown to the private chapel, by night, no escort listed.* Signed with the privy seal. Dated the same season Aldous the Third vanished from the record. And beneath it, in a different hand, someone had written a question — *what is it hollow of?* — and that question had been struck through so hard it tore the page."

He looked up. Both of them had forgotten their drinks. The careful one's face had gone pale in the tallow light, and Tam thought: *he knows something. He came in here already carrying a piece of this.*

"So I asked my question," Tam continued. "At a senior conclave. Nicely. With citations." He laughed, short and bitter. "Within the month, my wing of the archive burned. Within two, I was standing in the snow outside the gates with a decree naming me a spreader of sedition against the Crown's holy person. Which is amusing, because sedition requires saying the king is illegitimate. I never said that. All I ever said was that the *paperwork* was illegitimate. Apparently that's worse."

"Why?" The word came out of the careful man low and urgent. "Why would the Crown burn its own records over a gap?"

"Because the gap isn't a gap." Tam leaned in, and for the first time in six years the catalogue in his head wasn't torment; it was a weapon, and he aimed it. "Because I kept copies. Because before they exiled me, I spent one long night in the surviving fragment stacks, and I found references — old ones, pre-dynastic — to the Hollow Crown. Binding-work. Pre-Founding binding-work, the kind the church pretends never existed. Every reference agrees on three things." He held up fingers. "One: it was made to hold something. Two: it feeds on memory and magic to sustain the holding. Three—" his voice dropped, "—it wears whoever it holds until there's nothing left inside the shape."

"That's superstition," Bryn said. But her hand had drifted to the oilcloth in her pack without her seeming to notice.

"It's catalogue entries, girl. Cross-referenced from eleven sources, three of them royal scribes' private journals." He straightened. "I'm not telling you the Crown eats kings because a wise woman told me under a full moon. I'm telling you because I *filed* it. Item numbers and all. And then I watched the most powerful institution in this kingdom decide that the existence of those items was more dangerous than the truth of them. Institutions don't burn libraries over superstition. They burn libraries over evidence."

The taproom noise washed around them — dice games, a fiddler tuning, rain starting against the shutters. The careful man stared at the table as though reading something written there.

"The courier," he said at last, very quietly. "She said the king was already dead. Before she died herself."

"And I'm telling you she was right, and that it happened years ago, and whatever wears him now has been collecting taxes, appointing bishops, and signing treaties ever since." Tam drained his cup and signaled for another, because the brand of what he'd just said required it. "The Hollow Crown didn't steal a throne. It inherited one. Legally. Quietly. With excellent paperwork — except for the one ledger I noticed, because noticing ledgers was the only thing I was ever good at."

Bryn's knife was back in its sheath; he couldn't say when that had happened. "If this is true, why are you sitting in a ditch-town drinking instead of shouting it from rooftops?"

"Because I shouted it once. From the finest rooftop in the kingdom." He gestured at himself — the stained coat, the swollen nose, the hands that shook between matters. "This is what happens to the man who shouts. Now I keep the archive where they can't burn it." He tapped his temple. "Four thousand nine hundred and sixty-three items. Every one of them memorized. They can exile the archivist. They can't exile the catalogue."

The careful man looked at him for a long moment, and Tam watched him arrive at a decision the way you watch weather turn.

"You said you found references. To the unbinding. Is there a rite? A method? Anything?"

Tam paused with the fresh cup halfway to his mouth.

Now, here was the thing. Here was the thing he had not said aloud in six years, the thing he kept locked in the deepest shelf of himself, because saying it would mean it was real, and as long as it stayed unsaid he could go on drinking in ditches pretending he was merely a ruined man rather than a cowardly one.

"There are fragments," he said carefully. "Rites have requirements. Requirements have... conditions. Bloodlines. Locations." He met the man's eyes. "Why do you ask? What's on that map you're both pretending isn't in her pack?"

Neither of them answered. But Bryn's jaw tightened, and the careful one — Ivo, the world would tell him soon enough — reached out and drew the oilcloth bundle from her pack and laid it on the table between them, unwrapped, and Tam looked down at a bloodstained map covered in hidden script that seemed to shift when he blinked, and every hair on his arms stood up, because he had seen script like that exactly once before, in a single surviving fragment, catalogued under item three hundred and forty-four, *Waystone inscriptions, ravensworn hand, provenance unknown.*

"Where," Tam whispered, "did you get this?"

"A dying woman," Ivo said. "Who told me to find the ravensworn."

The tavern went on around them — dice, fiddle, rain. Tam sat very still while six years of exile rearranged themselves inside his skull into a single unbearable shape: not a random ruin, not a drunkard's purgatory, but a waiting room. He had been keeping the catalogue warm all this time for a reason. The archive survived in his head because somewhere, someday, someone would need it.

Someone had walked through the door.

"There's a revealing charm," he heard himself say. "Simple one. Any hedge-witch knows it. It shows bloodline the way ink shows watermark." His hands had begun to shake again, but not from the drink. "It would take a minute. Less."

"Do it," said Bryn, before Ivo could speak, her eyes flat and hard with a grief that needed someone to blame and had run out of better candidates. "I want to know who we've been traveling with."

Ivo looked at the map. At the word Tam could almost read glowing under the stains — *unbind* — and then at the door, at the rain, at the whole dark road behind him, like a man measuring the distance back to a life he was about to lose.

"All right," he said. "Show me."
