## 10. Ravensworn

Tam had spent eleven years learning how to fail properly.

It was a discipline, like any other. The trick was to fail in private, at the bottom of a bottle, in a rented room above a tannery that stank so thoroughly of rendered fat that even his own memories had trouble finding him there. He'd gotten good at it. He'd gotten so good that when three strangers walked into the Drowned Kettle that evening — a sharp-eyed woman with a sword she wore like an argument, a nervous young man with ink-stained fingers who kept his hood up, and himself, dragged along because apparently he'd volunteered — he felt the old professional instinct stir in him like a worm under a stone.

*You're out of practice*, he told himself. *Eleven years of cataloguing nothing but your own regrets.*

But the map was real. He'd touched it in the alley behind Ivo's cellar shop, before Bryn had decided they were leaving Caerlum that night rather than risk the gates in daylight, and he'd felt the wax seal hum against his thumb like a struck bell. Old binding-work. Archive-grade. The kind of thing he used to catalogue in the deep vaults beneath the palace, back when he'd still been allowed to have opinions about what belonged to the kingdom and what belonged to the Crown.

So here he was, three days down the Weald road, sitting in the back room of a waystation tavern with a candle, a borrowed table, and two people who trusted him exactly as far as they could throw him — which, in Bryn's case, would be quite far, and probably through a wall.

"Show me your hand," he said to Ivo.

Ivo looked up from the map he'd been studying for the hundredth time. "Why?"

"Because I asked nicely, and because if you don't, I'll explain in front of Bryn why a man who sells luck-knots has calluses on his fingertips in the exact pattern of someone who channels raw magic without a focus." Tam laced his own fingers together on the table, trying to look like a scholar instead of a drunk, which was mostly a matter of posture and lying to yourself first. "I'm being discreet. Discretion is expensive. Take the discount."

Bryn, sharpening her knife by the window, didn't look up. "Do it," she said. "If he's going to lie to us, I want to know now."

"I haven't lied to either of you," Ivo said.

"You told me your name was Merrick for two days," Bryn said.

"That was caution, not lying."

"It's both." She tested the blade's edge against her thumb, satisfied. "Show him your hand or I'll hold it still for you."

Ivo exhaled through his nose — the little theatrical sigh of a man performing reluctance — and set his hand palm-up on the table. Tam had seen that performance before, in witnesses, in petitioners, in junior archivists trying to hide a misfiled folio. The sigh was the tell. People who had nothing to hide just showed you their hands.

Tam drew the charm.

He hadn't worked one in over a decade, and his fingers remembered the shape of it the way his mouth remembered the taste of cheap brandy: with shame, and with terrible fluency. A revealing charm was archive work — third-circle diagnostic, the kind they ran on new acquisitions to check for tampering, forgery, blood-ink, curse-residue. He'd run ten thousand of them. He'd once run four hundred in a single week during the Vessany bequest audit, until his hands shook and Master Ellery told him to go sleep it off, boy, the vaults aren't going anywhere.

The vaults, as it happened, had gone somewhere. They'd burned. But that wasn't tonight's failure.

He pricked Ivo's fingertip with his knife-point — "Sorry, standard procedure" — and let one bead of blood well up on the pad of the finger. Then he laid the charm over it, a folded scrap of parchment covered in cramped notation, and whispered the activation word, and waited for the blood to do what blood does under a revealing charm: glow faintly amber, settle, and show its ordinary human signature. Red-gold, unremarkable, common as dirt.

The blood turned silver.

Not glowed silver. *Turned.* The bead on Ivo's finger went pale and bright as moonlit water, and then it moved — crawled, actually, up out of the fingerprint's whorls and into the air a half-inch, where it hung and spread into a shape Tam knew better than he knew his own handwriting.

A raven. Wings folded. Head bowed. The mark of the ravensworn binders.

Tam stopped breathing.

There were perhaps nine documents in the world that bore that sigil, and he had catalogued six of them personally, and every one of them had been sealed under triple ward in the deepest tier of the royal archive with a restriction tag reading, in his own neat hand: *Lineage extinct. Verify before access. Do not duplicate.*

Extinct. He had written the word himself. He had believed it, because the alternative — that the last family capable of unbinding the Hollow Crown was still walking around selling trinkets in outlawed-magic Caerlum — was too absurd for a man of records to entertain.

And yet.

"Well," Tam said, and his voice came out steadier than he deserved. "That's inconvenient."

Ivo snatched his hand back. The silver raven collapsed into ordinary blood, and he pressed his finger against his sleeve as if he could scrub the light off it. "What did you do?"

"What did *I*—" Tam laughed, a short ugly sound. "Son, I just read your mail. Your blood wrote back."

"What is it?" Bryn had crossed the room in three strides, knife still in hand, though she held it low. She was looking at Ivo differently now. Not softer. Sharper. Recalculating. "What did it show?"

"A sigil." Tam kept his eyes on Ivo because watching Bryn watch Ivo was not going to help anyone. "An old one. Very old. Older than the kingdom, near enough. It belongs to a bloodline called the ravensworn — binders, oath-smiths, the family that—"

"Don't." Ivo was on his feet, chair legs shrieking against the floorboards. The hood had fallen back and Tam saw his face fully for the first time that night, and it wasn't fear on it. It was something worse. It was recognition, arriving somewhere he didn't want it. "Don't say it."

"—that made the Crown," Tam finished, because a scholar's worst habit is completing the citation.

The silence afterward had weight. Somewhere below them, the tavern roared at a dice game; a chair broke; somebody sang three bars of something filthy and was shushed. In the small room, nobody moved except the candle flame, leaning in the draft from the window like it wanted to leave too.

"My mother," Ivo said quietly, "never spoke of any bloodline."

"No," Tam agreed. "She wouldn't have."

"You don't know anything about my mother."

"I know that if she was ravensworn, keeping you ignorant was the single most loving thing she ever did." Tam spread his hands, palms out, the gesture he'd used on frightened donors and hostile auditors alike: *I am only showing you the ledger.* "Think about it. Your whole life, you've hidden what you can do. You taught yourself rules — never channel where anyone can see, never use more than sleight-of-hand, never, never be noticed. Where do you think those rules came from? Children don't invent that kind of fear. They inherit it, along with their eyes and their knuckles."

"She was a hedge-wife," Ivo said. "She stitched sails in Harrowmouth."

"And I was a royal archivist with a pension and a title," Tam said, "and neither of those facts survived contact with the truth. Sit down, son. Please. Because what I'm about to tell you doesn't get easier standing up, and I've only got the courage for one telling."

Ivo didn't sit. But he stopped backing toward the door, and after a moment Bryn pulled the second chair out and stood beside it, arms crossed, in the universal language of *sit down or I'll make this worse*. Ivo sat.

Tam took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve — an affectation, they were nearly clean, but it gave his hands something honest to do — and put them back on.

"The Hollow Crown," he began, "was not always hollow. That's the part the histories get wrong, or rather, the part they were made to get wrong. Three hundred years ago there was no crown on the throne of this kingdom. There was a tyrant — Aurelic the Undying, they called him, though 'undying' was flattery; he simply refused to die and had the resources to make it stick. War-magic. Blood-tithe. The works. And a company of binders was assembled to end him, because you can't kill a man who won't stay killed, but you can *bind* him. Cage him. Seal the door and swallow the key."

"Binders," Bryn said. "Like your sigil."

"Like his sigil." Tam nodded at Ivo. "The ravensworn were the greatest of them. Oath-binding was their craft — not charms, not hexes, but *agreements*. They bound things with promises so heavy the things couldn't wriggle out of them. When the company forged the Crown to cage Aurelic, it was a ravensworn who wrote the central binding. His name was Corvath Ravensworn, and the record of what it cost him runs to forty pages, and I have read all forty, and I will spare you thirty-nine of them."

"What happened to them?" Ivo asked. His voice had gone flat. Careful. The voice of a man holding a door shut from the inside. "The ravensworn."

"They served. For generations, they maintained the Crown's bindings — renewals, inspections, repairs. Hereditary duty. And then, roughly ninety years ago, the line thinned. Fewer children. Fewer talents. By fifty years ago there was one heir left, a woman named Maren Ravensworn, serving as court binder—"

The sound Ivo made wasn't a word. It was smaller than a word, punched out of him, and his hand went flat against the table as if the room had tilted.

"Maren," he said.

"You know the name."

"I know a *Maren*." His eyes had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere years away. "Hedge-witch. Up past the Weald. She—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, angrier. "She took me as an apprentice when I was fourteen. Five years I studied with her. She never said. Five years of lessons and scolding and hauling her damn herbs and she never once—"

"Would you have stayed, if she'd told you?" Tam asked gently. "If she'd said, *boy, your blood is the key to the cage that eats kings, and one day we may need to turn it — would you have learned your lessons better, or would you have run sooner?*"

Ivo didn't answer. Which was an answer.

"She hid you," Tam said. "Sent you into the world with no name but your mother's married one, no history, no craft but what she could teach you sideways. And then the Crown found the archive records, or the archive found the Crown's attention — the order of events is disputed, and I would know, because I'm the dispute." He smiled without any humor in it. "The ravensworn registry pages were ordered destroyed fifteen years ago. I filed the destruction order myself. I also, quietly, filed a false copy in the restricted stacks and kept the originals. That is item one on a very long list of reasons I am no longer employed by the Crown."

"So she's alive?" Ivo's voice cracked on it. "Maren. My—"

"I don't know. Fifty years ago she'd have been young. Thirty, perhaps, when she took you in — the arithmetic works, if binders age slow, and they do. Whether she's still breathing is beyond my ledger." Tam leaned forward. "But here is what matters, and then I'll let you hate me in peace. The Crown eats memory. You've seen the village; you know. It eats *magic* too — pulls it out of the land like water out of a sponge. And the binding at its heart was written in ravensworn blood. Which means the only force in this kingdom that can speak to that binding in its own language—" he tapped the table, once, "—is currently sitting in a waystation tavern wishing I'd choke."

"I'm not a binder." Ivo's voice was shaking now, and anger and terror were braided through it so tightly Tam couldn't tell which was load-bearing. "I sell luck-knots. I hide in cellars. I have spent five years getting *small*, do you understand me? Small enough that nothing finds me. And you want me to be — what, the last of some ancient line? Walk up to the throne and say *hello, I'm the key*? My mother stitched sails. My father was a fisherman who drowned when I was six. That's my lineage. That's all of it. You're wrong."

"I'm never wrong about catalogues," Tam said. "It's my only virtue. The sigil doesn't lie, son. Revealing charms can't be fooled by feeling — believe me, people tried, for centuries, to forge that mark. Silver raven, wings folded, head bowed. It's your blood. It's been your blood since before you had a name for it."

"Then my blood is wrong!"

The shout rang off the rafters. Downstairs, the noise stuttered — someone hushed someone — and resumed. Ivo stood there breathing hard, and Tam watched five years of careful invisibility tear straight down the middle, and understood, with the particular misery of a man who reads everything, exactly what was happening: it wasn't the danger Ivo was refusing. It was the grief. To accept the bloodline was to accept that everyone who'd kept him safe had lied to him, and that the safety itself had been the lie, and that the small careful life he'd built — the shop, the rules, the not-being-seen — had never once actually protected him from what he was.

Bryn crouched beside his chair. Tam expected a threat. Instead she said, in a low voice, "My sister died saying *find the ravensworn*. I thought she meant a person worth finding. Someone noble." She tilted her head at Ivo. "You're not noble. You're a coward with good hands. But Sera bled out on a cellar floor betting on you, so either she was a fool, or she saw something in you that you're too busy hiding to see yourself. I haven't decided which yet. But I'm deciding."

"Bryn—"

"Don't." She stood. "Don't apologize, don't explain, don't do the sad face. Just decide whether you're the man she bought with her life or not. Take your time. We've got a long road."

She went to the window and took up her watch, and gave him her back, which was its own strange mercy.

Ivo sat with his head in his hands for a long time. Tam busied himself repacking the charm, folding the parchment along its worn creases, giving the man his privacy the only way scholars know how: by pretending to work.

Finally Ivo lifted his head. His eyes were red-rimmed and dry, and something in them had changed — not resolve, nothing so clean, but a kind of surrender to motion, like a man stepping off a ledge because staying on it had become unbearable.

"The map," he said. "You said it's binding-work. Archive-grade. Can you read it? Properly? All of it?"

"Some of it. Given time. Given—" Tam hesitated, and hated himself, and said it anyway, because the road ahead was long and lies between companions are heavier than truths. "Given access to reference materials I no longer possess, most of which are in the church archives at Vessany, which is four hundred miles in the wrong direction from everywhere you want to be, and crawling with the Crown's people."

Ivo stared at him. Then he laughed — one broken note, half hysteria — and dragged a hand down his face.

"Of course it is. Of course. Why would it be anywhere easy." He looked at the map spread on the table, at the hidden script only he could wake, at whatever future was coiling inside it. "Vessany," he said. "Fine. Fine. We go to Vessany."

"Ah," said Tam, "there is one other thing."

Ivo closed his eyes. "There's always one other thing with you."

"There is indeed, it's called thoroughness, and it's why I'm alive." Tam reached into his coat and produced a folded page — not the charm, something older, soft as cloth at the creases, guarded by eleven years of a drunk man's paranoia. "Before the archive burned, I removed certain documents. One of them concerns the ravensworn registry. Specifically, it concerns the *last entry* in it, dated fifty years ago, written in Maren Ravensworn's own hand."

He laid the page on the table between them, and watched Ivo look at it without touching it, the way one looks at a snake or a letter bearing bad news.

"What does it say?" Ivo whispered.

Tam had read the entry so many times he could recite it in the dark, and had, often, in the bad years, like a prayer he hated. He recited it now:

"*The line ends with me. Let it. What we built cannot be unmade by the likes of us — only carried, or inherited. If any raven remains after me, let them live blind and free. Blindness is mercy. Sight is the debt.*"

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

Below them, faint through the floorboards, a horse hammered into the waystation yard at a gallop — hard, urgent, wrong for this hour — and Tam heard the rider's voice crack out across the mud, high and official, asking questions of the stablehand, and the word that drifted up through the boards made his stomach drop through the floor and into the Weald itself.

"*Inquisition.*"

Bryn was already moving. Ivo's hand closed over the registry page, crumpling it, his mother's words crushed in his fist like something he could keep or kill — and Tam, gathering the map with hands that had finally, properly, begun to shake, thought: *Sight is the debt. And here comes the collector.*
