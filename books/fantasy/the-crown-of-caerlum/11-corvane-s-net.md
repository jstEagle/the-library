## 11. Corvane's Net

The report came to Corvane at the fourth bell of the morning, delivered by a boy with chilblains on his ears and a courier's satchel that smelled of wet leather. Corvane read it standing at his window in the Bastion of the Vigilant Flame, watching the fog burn off the Caerlum docks, and by the time he reached the bottom of the page the fog had burned off entirely and the city lay open below him like a body on a table.

Three travelers. One a woman with a sword and river-country vowels. One an old man who paid for rooms with archivist's script — a script that had been struck from the rolls five years ago. And one a charm-seller from the Saltrow cellars named Ivo Ravensworth, whose shop had burned three nights after a courier died in it.

The name did not mean anything to Corvane. Not yet. He said it once aloud, quietly, the way a man tests a lock, and then he put on his grey coat and went down to the records hall.

* * *

The Vigilant Flame kept its records better than the Crown kept its kingdom. Corvane had always taken a certain pride in that. The inquisition was younger than the throne, older than most of its own doctrines, and its founding purpose was simple enough to carve above a door: *the king is eternal; therefore nothing which threatens him may be permitted to sleep.* Everything else — the patrols, the tithe-inspections, the pyres for hedge-magicians, the quiet disappearances — everything else was housekeeping.

He found what he wanted in the ninth drawer of the genealogical cabinet, under a folder so thin it was nearly a rumor. *Ravensworth.* A merchant family, minor, extinguished. A mother recorded as deceased twenty-two years past. A son recorded as — and here Corvane paused — no son recorded at all. The family line simply stopped, ink trailing off into nothing, as if the clerk had been interrupted.

Clerks were never interrupted. Clerks were thorough to the point of madness. Which meant someone had made the line stop, or the line had made itself stop, and either way a name that should have been written down had spent two decades unwritten.

Corvane closed the drawer and stood for a moment with his hand flat on the wood, feeling the cool grain of it, letting the shape of the thing assemble itself in his mind the way he let all shapes assemble: patiently, without forcing, the way smoke fills a room.

A courier dies whispering about ravensworn. A shop burns. An unwritten name walks out of Caerlum with a dead woman's sister and a disgraced archivist. And somewhere ahead of them, at the end of whatever road they thought they were walking alone, the Hollow Crown sat on the king's brow and dreamed its long slow dream.

He did not believe the courier's words — *the king is dead* — because believing them would be like believing the sun was dead. The king was not a man. The king was the axis the world turned on, sustained these hundred years by the Crown of Caerlum, holy and self-renewing, and the fact that no one had seen His Majesty eat a meal in living memory was not horror but mercy: a man so given over to his office that even his body belonged to the realm now. Corvane had knelt before the king once, eleven years ago, at his investiture into the inquisition. The king's hand had rested on his head. It had been cold, but so is marble, and marble is beautiful, and no one calls a cathedral dead.

But the courier had died saying something, and things that die saying something are worth following.

* * *

They had gone east. Of course they had gone east. Everything in this kingdom's rotten old story went east eventually, back toward the Weald and the waystones and the first sins, and Corvane had read enough of the sealed archives to know the geography of guilt the way other men knew the backs of their hands.

He took six brothers and rode light. No banners. The inquisition worked best when it looked like weather — distant, inevitable, arriving without anyone having agreed to it.

His second, Brother Aldous, asked him on the second day whether they should expect resistance.

"From a charm-peddler, a drunk, and a grieving woman?" Corvane said. "Expect confusion. Expect them to run badly. Grief makes people stupid, and stupidity is a gift."

"And if they're not stupid?"

Corvane considered the question honestly, because he tried to consider everything honestly; dishonesty was for people with something to hide, and he had nothing. "Then we learn something," he said. "Either way the Flame is served."

Aldous nodded and dropped back, satisfied, and Corvane rode on through the dripping green of the Weald road thinking about the courier. Sera Vael. He'd pulled her file before leaving. Tithe-runner, twelve years' service, unremarkable in every column except the last one, where some supervisor had noted, in the bored shorthand of a man filling out forms: *asks questions.* Two words. In the inquisition's grammar those two words were a whole sentence, and the sentence ended with her face-down in a charm-seller's cellar with her blood going tacky on the floor.

She had known something. She had run west to give it away instead of east to give it to her masters, which told Corvane she'd understood exactly who her masters were, and had chosen anyway. That was the part that nagged at him. Not treason — treason was common as rain. But choice. Deliberate, informed, fatal choice. People only made choices like that when the alternative frightened them more than death, and Corvane could not imagine anything frightening more than death, because death was merely the end of a servant and the Crown was eternal, and eternity swallowed endings whole.

Unless.

He let the *unless* sit there, unopened, the way you leave a door shut that you are not yet ready to walk through.

* * *

They found the village on the third day, or rather they found the absence of one.

It sat in a fold of hills where the map promised a market town of four hundred souls, and there were indeed four hundred souls there, more or less, milling in the lanes and tending fields and answering doors. But when Brother Pell asked a woman her name, she smiled at him with a pleasant blankness and said, "I'm sure I had one." When Aldous asked the miller how long the Crown's collectors had been coming through, the miller said, "Coming through where?" and then, helpfully, "What's a collector?"

Corvane stood in the market square for a long while with his gauntlets tucked under his arm, listening to the sound of the place. It sounded like any market square. Dogs, children, a hammer on a wheel-rim somewhere. But underneath it, the way you hear water under ice, there was a hollowness. The children remembered their names. Everyone over forty did not. Whatever had come through had skimmed the cream of memory off the top of the town and left the rest standing, blinking, polite, and empty.

"Tithe-collectors," Pell said. "Ours?"

"No," Corvane said. "Ours take grain and coin and lists of magicians. Ours don't take names." He turned in a slow circle. The church of the Flame at the square's edge had a fresh coat of whitewash, applied recently, generously — the kind of generosity that arrived after something terrible to make the ledger look clean. "Something else has been collecting here."

"You think it's them. The travelers."

"I think," Corvane said, "that a dying woman gave a charm-seller a map, and that maps lead places, and that whatever led *here* leads onward too." He remounted. "We ride faster."

That night he wrote in his journal, by candle, in the small exact hand he used for everything: *Village of Marleford (if that is its name) emptied of memory by agency unknown. Pattern consistent with pre-consolidation accounts of waystone decay. Recommend further study. Do not recommend further study.* He looked at that last contradiction for a moment. Then he left both lines in. A record that lied by omission was worse than no record at all. That was the one doctrine he held above the rest, the one he'd have carved above his own door if he'd been allowed a door of his own: *write what is true, even when it is disloyal.*

Especially then. Loyalty without truth was just obedience wearing a nicer coat.

* * *

They caught them on the fifth day, at a crossing of the River Liss where the old bridge had half-fallen and the ferry was a rope and a flat barge, and Corvane knew afterward that he had made his first mistake of the affair, which was assuming that a man who had survived five years invisible in Caerlum would panic when cornered.

Ivo Ravensworth did not panic.

Corvane saw him first across the water — a lean, ordinary man in a peddler's coat, doing the ordinary work of hauling the ferry rope hand over hand, with a tall dark-haired woman behind him holding the barge steady and a grey-bearded old man sitting amid their packs like luggage. Three figures. Unremarkable. The kind of people you forget while looking at them.

Then the charm-seller looked up and met his eyes across forty yards of brown water, and Corvane felt the look go through him like a fish-spear. Not fear. Recognition. The man had marked him, weighed him, filed him, and begun calculating — all in the space of a heartbeat — and there was nothing ordinary left anywhere in his face.

"Ravensworth!" Corvane called, and made no move for his weapon, because the first tool of the inquisition was courtesy and the courtesy was not false. "I've come to talk. Nothing more. Bring the ferry across."

The old man said something. The woman drew her sword, casually, the way other people scratched an itch. And Ravensworth called back in a clear carrying voice: "Talk about what?"

"About a dead courier. About a map. About your mother's name, which isn't in any book where it ought to be." He let that last one land and watched it land. The rope-hauling stopped.

"There's nothing to talk about," Ravensworth said.

"There's everything to talk about. You're carrying something that belongs to the Crown, and the Crown—" and here Corvane paused, because he wanted the words exactly right, and exactness mattered to him the way prayer matters to the devout— "the Crown is patient. Give it back and walk home. I'll write in my report that you cooperated. Cooperation is remembered kindly. It's practically a pardon."

He meant it. That was the thing his brothers never quite understood about him and his enemies never believed: he meant it. If the charm-seller surrendered the map and the truth and himself, Corvane would have written the kindest report in the Bastion's files, because the goal was never suffering. The goal was order. The king eternal, the realm continuous, the story uninterrupted. Pain was just occasionally the price of the continuity, and he paid prices without flinching, but he never confused paying with enjoying.

Ravensworth looked at him for a long moment. Then the woman said something short and vicious that carried anyway — "*He's lying*" — and Corvane thought: *no, actually, I'm not, that's the tragedy of me,* and then the charm-seller did something impossible.

He let go of the rope, spread his hands over the river, and the river answered.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. There was no flash, no thunder, none of the theater the pyre-preachers described. The current simply changed its mind. The barge swung downstream as though a giant had tilted the world two degrees, and the water between the bank and the far shore went suddenly, violently fast, white-capped, roaring, a flood where a ford had been, and Corvane's horse screamed and balked and threw him sideways in the saddle, and when he got his seat back the barge was thirty yards downstream and moving like a leaf in spring melt.

"Magician," Pell breathed, and there was lust in it, the ugly excitement of a man who has been given permission.

"Magician," Corvane agreed, and found, to his own surprise, that the word tasted different in his mouth than it had yesterday. He had burned hedge-witches. He had signed the orders with a steady hand. But he had never watched one *work* — never seen magic done cleanly, usefully, almost gently, a river bent aside to save three lives from six crossbows. The doctrine said all magic outside the Flame was corruption. The doctrine had not accounted for the current changing its mind in front of him.

He crushed the observation the way you crush a spark in dry grass. Later. Examine later. Doctrine was load-bearing; you did not pull stones out of a wall to inspect them while standing inside the building.

"Aldous. Downstream. They'll beach within the mile — the current will spit them out at the weir. Take four brothers and be waiting." He was already swinging down, checking girth, voice level. "Pell, with me along the near bank. We'll keep pace and keep talking. Pressure from two sides makes people reasonable."

"And if they're not reasonable?"

"Then the Flame provides," Corvane said, and heard how the sentence fit his mouth like an old glove, and hated, briefly and privately, how easy it still was to say.

* * *

They lost them at the weir, and losing them taught Corvane more than catching them would have.

The barge grounded a mile down on the shingle below the old fish-weirs, exactly as he'd predicted, and Aldous was waiting with four crossbows, and it should have ended there in ten tidy minutes. Instead the old man — the drunk, the disgrace, the luggage — stood up in the shallows and began to *talk*, fast and strange, words tumbling like a scholar reciting under torture, and the words were old. Old-structure grammar, ley-forms, the cadence of pre-consolidation binding-verse, and where they fell, the ground listened. The reeds thickened. The shingle shifted. Aldous's men waded into mud that had been firm footing a breath before, and the crossbow bolts flew wide as if the air itself had developed opinions, and by the time Corvane rounded the bend at a gallop the three of them were already up the far slope and into the trees, and the trees were closing behind them like a door.

He reined in at the weir and sat looking at the churned mud, the scattered bolts, the deep prints of running feet already filling with water.

"Binding-work," Pell said, spitting. "Filthy old craft. We'll track them. Dogs—"

"Dogs won't hold a trail laid by a man who can argue with the ground." Corvane dismounted and crouched and pressed his palm flat against a print. Still warm, somehow, or seeming so. He thought about the river bending. He thought about the village with no names. He thought about the thin file, the missing line, the word that the dying courier had supposedly whispered — *ravensworn* — a word he had since found in exactly one place, a sealed folio in the Bastion's deepest shelf, catalogued under a heading so old it predated the inquisition itself: *On the Makers of the Crown.*

He had read it by candle at the third bell. It was eight pages. Six were burned deliberately, edges charred in a pattern too neat to be accident. The two remaining pages spoke of a bloodline sworn to ravens, binders by inheritance, and the final legible sentence read: *of the making and the unmaking, the same hands, God forgive them both.*

Corvane knelt at the river's edge a moment longer than he needed to.

Here was the difficulty, and he faced it squarely, because he faced everything squarely: the doctrine said the king was eternal, and the evidence — the hollow village, the wrong-smiling reports from the capital, the courier's dying words, the six burned pages — the evidence kept suggesting, politely, persistently, that eternity might be a thing being *worn* rather than lived. And Corvane was a man built around one belief the way a pearl is built around grit. Remove the grit and there was no pearl. There was just a soft animal that had burned people for nothing.

So the grit stayed. But he permitted himself this: he would follow the charm-seller all the way to the end of the map, wherever it led, and he would look at whatever was at the end of it with his own eyes, and then — only then — he would decide what was true. That was not doubt. He was careful to name it correctly, even alone, even kneeling in river mud. It was diligence. The Flame rewarded diligence.

"Brother Corvane?" Pell said. "Your orders?"

Corvane stood. Upstream, the water ran smooth again over the weir, innocent as glass, and somewhere beyond the tree line three fugitives were running east with a dead woman's map and an old man's forbidden verses, toward the oldest stones in the kingdom.

"We send to Caerlum for the waystone surveys," he said. "All of them. Sealed folios, my seal only." He mounted. "And then we ride east, Brothers. They're not fleeing toward safety."

"How do you know?"

"Because nobody runs *toward* the Weald," Corvane said, "unless they know something the rest of us have forgotten." He gathered the reins, and the grey coat of the inquisition moved off through the green country like weather coming on, patient, inevitable, and — though it did not yet know it itself — beginning, very slightly, to doubt.
