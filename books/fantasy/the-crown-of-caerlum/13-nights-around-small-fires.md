## 13. Nights Around Small Fires

### I.

Bryn woke him by kicking the sole of his boot.

"Up," she said. "You fight like a man apologizing for it."

The fire had burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones. Above them the Weald leaned close, black branches stitched against a sky still holding the last of the stars. Ivo sat up stiffly and found his hands aching from yesterday's walking, and beyond that nothing — no reason at all to be standing in wet grass before dawn except that Bryn had decided it.

"I've survived five years in Caerlum without learning to fight," he said.

"You've survived by running and hiding and being too small to notice." She tossed him a stick — not even a proper practice blade, just a branch with the bark rubbed smooth. "Corvane doesn't chase small men anymore, Ivo. He chases you specifically. So we're going to fix the way you move, because right now you move like a rabbit deciding which direction to die in."

He took the stick anyway. That was the thing about Bryn he was slowly, unwillingly learning: arguing with her took longer than obeying her.

"Feet apart," she said. "Weight on the balls. You're a shopkeeper, yes? Then stand like something worth stealing."

He stood. She circled him once, slow, her own practice knife held loose at her hip, and he felt the particular embarrassment of being looked at thoroughly — the way he'd never let anyone look at him, not in Caerlum, not ever.

"Sera taught me," Bryn said, without preamble. "Our father wanted sons. He got two daughters and drank about it, so Sera learned everything first and then beat it into me in the yard behind the tannery until I stopped crying." She flicked her wrist; the knife spun once. "She said fear is fine. Fear is fuel. But flinching gets you killed, because a flinch tells your enemy where you'll be before you know it yourself."

"And what do I do?"

"You flinch constantly. Every time I step toward you, your whole body votes to be somewhere else." She stepped toward him.

He flinched. The stick came up in a guard he'd invented on the spot, all wrong, elbows out like a man shielding his face from thrown slops.

"There," she said. "Again."

They went again. And again. The sky went from black to iron to a thin bruised gold at the treeline, and Bryn drove him back across the campsite again and again, patient as tide, correcting his grip with the flat of her hand, kicking his feet wider when they narrowed. She never struck him hard. She didn't need to. Every touch landed like a lesson: *here, and here, and here, you are open, you are open, you are always open.*

"Stop," she finally said, when Ivo was bent double with his hands on his knees, sweat dripping off his nose into the grass. "Good enough for today."

"That wasn't good enough for anything."

"No." She crouched across from him, forearms on her knees, and for a moment her face in the firelight was younger and rawer than he'd ever seen it. "But it's honest. Yesterday you fought like someone who expected me to stop. Today you started expecting me not to." She stood and rolled her shoulders. "That's the whole secret. Everything after that is just years of practice, and we don't have years."

Ivo straightened, gasping. "Why are you teaching me this?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere in the Weald a wood pigeon called the dawn in, one dull note at a time.

"Because Sera pressed that map into your hands instead of mine," Bryn said. "Because she chose you, and I've decided to find out if she was stupid or right." She turned away toward the cold kettle. "And because if Corvane catches us, I can't carry you. You have to walk."

### II.

Tam lectured at lunch, because Tam lectured at every meal, because silence made him itch like wool.

"The ley-lines," he said, gesturing with a fork speared with half a sausage, "are not lines. That's the first thing everyone gets wrong, including most archivists, including me for eleven humiliating years. They're seams."

Ivo chewed and waited. Bryn didn't bother pretending to listen, but she'd stopped leaving when Tam started talking, which Ivo had noticed and suspected Tam had too.

"A seam," Tam repeated, delighted with himself, "like where two pieces of cloth were joined. The world was made — or grown, or argued into existence depending on which cosmogony you prefer, and I've read all forty-one — and the making left joins. Places where the material of things runs thinner and older magic pools underneath like water under a floorboard. The waystones sit on the seams. So did the old shrines, before the church rebranded them as wells of sainted blood, which is nonsense, but profitable nonsense." He waved the fork. "Your map isn't following roads, Ivo. It's following stitches."

"And the Crown eats along those seams," Ivo said.

Tam's delight dimmed to something more careful. "Yes. That's the horror of it, done elegantly. Whoever built the Crown anchored it to the seams so its binding would hold everywhere at once. But a seam runs both ways. Whatever drains down one end arrives at the other. The village we passed — the forgetting — that wasn't the Crown reaching out deliberately. It was leakage. Runoff." He set the fork down. "Which means every mile we walk toward the capital, we're walking upstream through water that's getting dirtier."

Bryn snorted. "Cheerful."

"I'm an archivist. Cheerfulness is above my station." Tam pulled his satchel into his lap and began, as he did every day, rearranging papers that were already perfectly arranged. "But there's a practical point. If you can feel the seams — and you can, I watched you go grey-faced at the first waystone while Bryn and I felt nothing but wind — then you can feel the Crown's attention moving along them. Like feeling a spider on the far side of a wall."

Ivo looked at his hands. Since the waystone, since his grandmother's memory had poured through him like cold water, he'd been aware of something he couldn't name: a faint tension under the ground, northward, constant, like a wire drawn tight between his ribs and somewhere very far away.

"It hums," he admitted. "Quietly. All the time now. I thought it was my imagination."

"It's not." Tam said it gently, which was rare for him. "It's the other thing nobody tells you about seams. Once you've touched one, you belong to the garment a little." He caught himself, winced. "That sounded better in the archive. What I mean is — you're not imagining it, and you're not mad, and it doesn't necessarily mean doom. It might just mean you're listening."

Bryn, unexpectedly: "Can he learn to listen *better*?"

Tam blinked at her. "Actually, yes. Tonight, maybe. If we camp near the stream — streams like seams — I could show him a focusing exercise from the Third Archive. Pre-Exclusion material. Very illegal. Very useful." He smiled crookedly. "Most of my favorite knowledge has exactly those two qualities."

### III.

That night, while Tam walked Ivo through breathing exercises by the stream — *don't reach for the hum, make room for it, it comes to a quiet room faster than a noisy one* — Bryn sat apart at the fire and pretended not to watch either of them.

She was sharpening her knives, which she did every night, which was mostly true and partly a way of having her hands busy so her mind could go where it went every night lately: to Sera.

Not to the death. She'd worn the death smooth already, handled it so many times it had stopped cutting. It was the smaller things that ambushed her now. The way Sera whistled through her teeth when she read. The specific insult she used for bad horses. How she'd written letters home from every courier run and signed them with a little drawing of a lark instead of her name, because she said names could be traced and drawings couldn't.

The amulet hung at Bryn's throat. Sera's — silver, cheap, bought at a market stall, shaped like a lark with its wings folded. The chain had snapped during their escape from Corvane's net outside the waystation town, and Bryn had stuffed it in her pocket rather than leave it, and it had sat there broken for days because she couldn't fix it and wouldn't ask anyone to, because asking anyone to mend your dead sister's amulet meant saying its purpose out loud.

She heard footsteps and knew them before she looked up. Ivo walked differently now than he had a week ago. Less apology in it.

"Tam's asleep?" she asked.

"Face-down in his notes. Mid-sentence." Ivo hesitated, then sat across the fire from her. In his palm he held the broken amulet. Her stomach dropped — he must have seen it fall during the fight, or picked it from her pack, and she opened her mouth to be angry —

"I'm not going to keep it," he said quickly. "I want to fix it. If you'll let me."

"How did you even—"

"Bryn. You sleep with your fist closed around it. You think I haven't noticed?"

She had nothing to say to that. She uncurled her fingers around it now, reluctantly, and held it out over the firelight. The lark lay in her palm with one wing bent wrong and the chain snapped at the clasp, and looking at it hurt worse than she'd braced for.

"It's nothing," she said. "Market junk. Sera won it dicing off a sailor."

"You told me she carried it on every run."

"So?"

"So market junk doesn't get carried on every run." Ivo turned it over carefully with one finger. "This is a luck-piece. Cheap ones work as well as expensive ones — the charm's in the maker's intent, not the metal. Someone put real wanting into this." He glanced up. "Whoever sold it to her believed in luck. Or she asked them to, and they did it as a kindness."

Bryn's throat was tight. "Fix it or don't," she managed. "Stop narrating."

So he worked. She watched despite herself. He didn't use tools at first — just held the lark in both hands, eyes half-closed, lips moving slightly, and she felt something in the air change the way the air changes before rain. When he opened his eyes they had a faint shine to them, gone as soon as she noticed. He straightened the wing with a needle from his kit, murmuring, and the metal gave like warm wax and then hardened cool. The chain he mended link by link, each one glowing briefly at the join.

Magic. Outlawed, burned-at-the-stake magic, performed openly ten feet away from her, and her hands stayed calm on her knees.

Five years ago, she thought, he would have been dragged to the pyres for this. Five years ago, she would have reported him herself.

"Here." He held it out.

She took it. The lark sat whole in her palm, wings folded, lighter than she remembered. Warm, though the night was cold. "It feels different."

"Repaired charms usually do. A mended thing carries the mending in it." He poked the fire. "Sera's intent is still in there. That part I didn't touch. I wouldn't."

Bryn closed her fingers over it. For a moment she couldn't speak at all.

"My mother had one like it," she said finally, to the fire, because it was easier than his face. "Before she died. Sera gave her half her rations for a month to buy it. She never told me that. I found the receipt in her things." She swallowed. "She was always doing that. Spending herself quietly on people who didn't know."

"I know the type," Ivo said softly.

She looked up. He was watching the fire, and there was something in his profile she hadn't seen before — not the hunted shopkeeper, not the reluctant heir Tam kept calling ravensworn. Just a person, tired, sitting with her in the dark, keeping her company in the only way that mattered, which was simply staying.

"If you die on this road," Bryn said, "I'm going to be extremely annoyed."

"Noted. I'll try to survive out of politeness."

She almost smiled. Almost. But it was close, and it counted.

* * *

### IV.

Later, when the others slept, Ivo sat alone by the dying fire and did the breathing exercise Tam had taught him, making room, making room.

The hum came. Northward, taut as a wire, the Crown's attention somewhere at the far end of it — and beneath that, fainter, the seams themselves, running through the world like veins under skin. He could feel the second waystone ahead of them now, days distant, waiting. He could feel, though he would not say this to Tam yet, something else: a third point past it, deep under the capital, bright and terrible and aware.

Aware of him. It had been aware of him since the first stone. He understood that the way you understand a hand resting on the back of your neck.

*I see you,* the pressure said, without words. *Little ravensworn. Come home.*

Ivo opened his eyes. Across the ashes, Bryn slept with her fist curled around a mended lark. Behind him, Tam snored atop his beloved notes. Two people who, a month ago, had been strangers — one hunting him, one drunk — and who were now, impossibly, the shape his life had bent around.

*Come home,* the Crown whispered along the wire.

"I'm already there," Ivo said to the dark, very quietly.

Far to the north, along the seam, something ancient paused — and listened harder.
