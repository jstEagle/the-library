## 14. The City of Bells

Vessany heard them coming three miles out.

Not the travelers themselves—Bryn had seen to that, wrapping the cart wheels in wool and oilcloth until they rolled near-silent—but the bells. They rang from every tower in the city, all day, all night, a brazen tide of sound that rolled across the lowlands like weather. Tam had tried to explain it twice on the road: something about resonance, about how the Crown's church tuned its bells to frequencies that soothed the faithful, opened them, made them *receptive*. Ivo had understood exactly one thing from the lecture, and it was this: in Vessany, everyone was listening whether they meant to or not.

"Three thousand pilgrims pass through those gates every week," Tam said now, standing up in the cart to look. He'd washed, somewhere outside Corvane's reach—washed, shaved, and dug a clean black coat out of his pack that Ivo had never seen before, archivist's formal wear, the kind worn by men with access to things worth stealing. On the road he'd been a shambling drunk. In the coat he looked like what he was: a dangerous man who had once known too much. "Pilgrims are anonymous by design. The church wants them flowing in and out like water. It's the best cover we'll ever get."

"And if they check us?" Bryn sat at the cart's edge, boots swinging, whittling a stick into nothing. She hadn't stopped whittling since the bridge. Her arm was healed enough to use, thanks to Maren's salves, but she moved it carefully, like a debt being counted.

"They won't," Tam said. "They'll ask where you're pilgrimaging *from*. Have an answer ready."

"I'm going to the Wells of Contrition," Bryn said flatly. "To wash away my sins."

"That's actually perfect."

"I know." She didn't smile. She never smiled anymore, not really—not since Sera's name had passed between them and turned everything heavy. But there was a new steadiness in her, Ivo thought. Grief had burned off whatever softness she'd had left, and what remained was hard and useful, like forged iron.

They joined the road two miles from the gates, letting the cart drift into the stream of foot traffic—farmers with produce, penitents walking barefoot, whole families hauling children and chickens toward the sound. Ivo kept his hood up and his eyes down and felt his old rules reasserting themselves one by one, like joints cracking back into place. *Be small. Be forgettable. Give them a face they'll lose by supper.*

The gate guards were not inquisitors. That was the first mercy. They were city watchmen in the church's livery—grey and gold, the colors of the Hollow Crown—and they waved people through in batches, asking only the questions Tam had predicted. Where from. What sin. Which well.

Ivo gave his answers in a voice pitched low and bored, and the guard's eyes slid off him like rain off wax. Five years of practice, and for the first time it felt less like cowardice and more like craft.

Then they were inside, and the sound closed over them.

* * *

Vessany was built vertically. The city climbed a hill in terraces, and every terrace held towers, and every tower held bells—bronze, iron, some pale metal Tam couldn't identify—and between the towers the streets ran narrow and shadowed, paved in stone worn glassy by centuries of knees. Incense drifted from a hundred doorways. Pilgrims crawled the central stair on their bellies. Somewhere above, unseen, a choir sang a note that held and held and never seemed to need breath.

"It never stops," Ivo said. His teeth ached faintly. "The ringing."

"The Bells of Attendance," Tam said. "There are nine hundred and forty-four of them. Each one named for a soul the Crown has 'gathered.'" He said the last word the way a man spits out gristle. "The church teaches that when you die, your bell rings for a day, and then you're folded into the harmony. Eternal chorus. Very comforting."

"You don't believe it."

"I catalogued the foundry records." Tam's jaw worked. "Nine hundred and forty-four bells, and the casting ledgers go back four hundred years, and do you know what I noticed? The number never changes. Bells crack. Bells get recast. And every single time, somehow, the total stays the same. As though the city itself refuses to count higher." He looked up at the towers. "Things that refuse to be counted are usually hiding something."

They took rooms at a pilgrim hospice called the Drowned Lamb—cheap, crowded, anonymous, smelling of tallow and wet wool. The innkeep asked no names, only coin, and gave them a room with three pallets and a window that looked out onto a sheer drop and, far below, the white sprawl of the cathedral close.

That was the thing Ivo kept returning to, lying awake that first night under the bells: the cathedral. Not the spires, not the gold. The *size* of it. The Crown's church in Caerlum was a single chapel tucked behind the magistrate's hall, staffed by two bored clerics who mostly handled death registrations. Here, the faith had a body. Here, the faith had *teeth*—he could see the barracks adjoining the close, the drill yard, the long grey building marked with the eye-and-crown that meant inquisitors.

Corvane had come from a place like this. Maybe from this exact place. Lying in the dark with Bryn's breathing slow and even across the room, Ivo tried to imagine the inquisitor young, robed, kneeling under these same bells, learning that the world was a hymn and someone had to keep the tune. It was easier to hate a man than to understand him. Understanding felt dangerous, like opening a door you couldn't close.

He slept badly, and dreamed of bells tolling his own name.

* * *

The archive of the Cathedral of the Nine Hundred and Forty-Four occupied the third terrace of the close: a long, low, unassuming building that looked, deliberately, like a granary. Tam stood across the street from it at dawn, disguised in pilgrim grey, and stared at it the way other men stare at lovers or enemies.

"Sub-levels," he murmured. "Everything important is below ground. The reading room up top is for show—genealogies, saints' lives, tithe schedules. The restricted vaults run down six levels. Pre-Unification texts. Binding theory. Everything the church decided was too dangerous for the faithful to know existed." He rubbed his hands together, and Ivo saw that they were shaking. "I was exiled before I got past level two."

"How do we get past level one?" Bryn asked.

"We don't break in." Tam turned, and there was something in his face Ivo hadn't seen before—a kind of terrible eagerness, a man walking willingly toward the thing that ruined him. "Breaking in is what got me exiled. We're invited."

"Invited."

"The archive accepts visiting scholars. Petitioners. You present credentials—or the appearance of them—you state a field of inquiry, and they assign you a reading carrel and a supervising canon. If your inquiry is orthodox, they'll give you anything that isn't actively heretical." He smiled thinly. "And here is our advantage, friends: binding magic is not heretical. It's *foundational*. The Crown's whole theology rests on the idea that the king binds the kingdom to himself in love. There are shelves of approved texts on sympathetic bonds, on oath-magic, on the transfer of burdens. The church has been accidentally preserving the exact scholarship I need, because they think it flatters them."

"So we walk in the front door," Ivo said slowly, "and ask to read books about the thing we intend to destroy."

"Ask correctly," Tam corrected. "Ask as believers. Ask as scholars seeking to deepen devotion through understanding. The archive's guardians are trained to catch doubt. They are not trained to catch grief dressed as piety." He looked at Ivo. "You're the believer. You've spent five years pretending to be harmless; pretending to be devout is a promotion. I'll be your mentor—Archivist Odo of the Caerlum chapterhouse, retired, come to complete my life's work on the theology of sacred bonds. And Bryn—"

"I'm the servant," Bryn said. "Nobody looks at servants."

"Bryn—"

"She's right," Ivo said quietly. "It's the safest role."

Bryn's jaw tightened, but she nodded once, and Ivo caught the flicker in her eyes—the part of her that hated being invisible, that had spent her whole life beside a sister who chose shadows, and hated the choosing. But she said only: "Fine. Servant. But if it goes wrong, I'm not waiting to be saved."

"No one's saving anyone," Tam said. "We're scholars."

* * *

The petition went smoother than any of them dared hope, which was how Ivo learned to distrust smooth things.

Canon Eligius received them in a chamber above the reading room: a dry, precise man with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a hawk that has read many documents and believed most of them. He listened to Archivist Odo's credentials—Ivo's heart hammering the entire time, certain the forgery would crack apart in the man's hands—and then listened to Tam describe their inquiry in a voice of genuine scholarly hunger: the theology of sacrificial binding, the doctrine of the king's loving burden, the question of whether a bond freely given differs in nature from one inherited.

"A subtle question," Canon Eligius said. "Perhaps too subtle for the common faithful. But the archive exists for subtlety." He made a note. "You will have carrel nine. Your servant will wait in the outer gallery. Brother Anselm will supervise your reading. Certain folios require his presence at the turning of pages."

"Of course," Tam said smoothly.

"One more matter." The canon's quill paused. "Your timing is providential, Archivist. His Radiance the King arrives within the week. The Procession of the Burden passes through the close on the Feast of the Gathered. Every petitioner is expected to witness. It is considered..." he searched for the word, "...*instructive*."

"Then we will be instructed," Tam said, bowing.

But Ivo saw the canon's eyes linger on him a half-second too long—hawk's eyes, counting—and felt the old cold crawl up his spine. *Be forgettable*, he told himself. *You've done it ten thousand times.* The canon looked away. Probably nothing. Probably.

Probably.

* * *

Three days of research followed, each one a held breath.

Carrel nine was a desk in a vaulted gallery lit by clerestory windows, and Brother Anselm turned out to be an ancient, half-deaf monk whose supervision consisted of dozing in a corner chair while Tam read and copied and whispered findings through his teeth. The approved texts gave them pieces: the rite's structure confirmed what Tam's hidden pages described—three anchors, sequential opening, a willing bloodline at the center. A commentary from the reign of King Aldous the Second referred, in one marginal note, to "the ravensworn office, now happily extinct"—and Ivo had to leave the gallery for twenty minutes after reading that, standing in a courtyard with his hands flat against cold stone, breathing.

Bryn waited in the outer gallery with the other servants, and came back each evening with reports delivered flat and quiet: the layout of the close, the rotation of the greycoats, which doors were warded and which merely watched, and—most importantly—that the sub-level stairs were guarded by pairs of inquisitors who changed shifts at dawn and dusk, and that the shift change left ninety seconds of thin coverage.

"Ninety seconds," Ivo said. "For six levels."

"I didn't say we'd use it," Bryn said. "I said it existed. Knowing a thing and using a thing are different sins."

On the fourth evening she came back different—walked fast, face pale, and pressed something into Ivo's hand under the table at the Drowned Lamb. A printed broadsheet, the cheap kind sold to pilgrims by the bundle.

The king was coming early.

Tomorrow. The Procession of the Burden, moved up by royal decree, and the whole city ordered to line the processional way, every soul required to attend, every soul required to *see*.

"He's real, then," Bryn said. "The puppet. Tomorrow we see him with our own eyes."

Ivo turned the broadsheet over. The woodcut portrait showed a handsome man, middle-aged, serene. Beneath it, in pious capitals: BEHOLD YOUR KING, WHO CARRIES YOU ALL.

*Who carries you all.* Ivo thought of the village that had forgotten its own name, of the hollow pulling at his own memories, of the word scratched in hidden script: *unbind*. He thought of the courier dying on his floor with the map already soaked through, and the whisper: *the king is dead.*

"Tam," he called softly across the room. "How much do we trust the ninety seconds?"

Tam looked up from his notes, and Ivo watched him arrive at the same answer, and watched the color leave his face.

* * *

The Feast of the Gathered dawned clear and merciless.

They stood among ten thousand pilgrims on the processional way, packed shoulder to shoulder, and the bells rang so loud that Ivo felt them in his sternum, in his fillings, in the backs of his eyes. Incense smoke rolled down the terraces in silver rivers. Greycoats lined the route every ten feet. Above them all, on balconies and rooftops, the clergy of the close stood in ranks, faces lifted, mouths moving in silent anticipation.

"He's coming," someone breathed, near Ivo's ear, and the crowd surged forward a half-inch as though pulled by a string.

The procession entered the close below the fourth terrace, and Ivo saw it wrong before he understood why it was wrong.

It was the silence.

Ten thousand people roaring, bells crashing, choirs pealing—and around the king's palanquin, a bubble of dead air. Sound refused him. The noise bent around his little island of stillness like water around a stone, and the nearer the palanquin came, the wider the silence grew, until Ivo could hear his own heart and Bryn's breath and nothing else.

The palanquin was gold and white, borne by twelve bearers who did not sweat. And on it sat the king.

King Osric the Third. Ivo had seen his face on coins and warrants and chapel walls his whole life. This was that face—aged now, silver-bearded, dignified—and it was smiling.

It was the smile that was wrong.

It moved. That was the horror of it. It moved *correctly*, all the muscles doing what muscles do, curving at the right moments as the crowd cheered, warming, widening, gracious—and none of it arrived anywhere. No light reached the eyes. The eyes were busy elsewhere. Ivo knew that look; he had seen it in the mirror during the worst years, the look of a person whose attention has left the room while the body stays behind to be polite.

And the king's hands. The hands lay open on the armrests, palms up, in the posture of a man receiving—except they never adjusted, never shifted, never curled with living restlessness. For the length of a full minute Ivo watched those hands and they did not move at all. Nothing alive holds that still.

Beside him, Bryn had gone rigid. "Ivo," she said, very quietly, in the dead-air hush. "His shadow."

Ivo looked. The sun stood high and white above the close, throwing crisp shadows from every balcony rail, every bearer, every greycoat spear. The king cast a shadow too—it lay across the palanquin's gold floor, thin and dark.

It was facing the wrong way.

The crowd began to kneel, wave after wave, like wind through wheat. Tam grabbed Ivo's sleeve and pulled, but Ivo couldn't move yet, because at that moment the king's head turned—smoothly, gracefully, without any of the small effortful corrections a living neck makes—and the wrong eyes swept across the crowd and *stopped*.

On Ivo.

One heartbeat. Two. The smile widened a fraction, warm as a hearth, empty as a grave, and Ivo felt something touch the edges of his mind the way Maren's wards used to feel when he came home late: a testing pressure, patient and vast, an intelligence the size of a winter checking a door latch.

*There you are,* the pressure said, without words. *Ravensworn.*

The bells crashed. The crowd roared. The moment broke, and the king's gaze moved on, and the procession rolled forward through its bubble of silence, and Ivo was kneeling in the dust with Bryn's fist knotted in his collar and Tam's grip bruising his arm, and all three of them were saying nothing because there were greycoats everywhere and ten thousand witnesses and nowhere in the world to say it aloud.

That night, in the dark of the Drowned Lamb, Bryn spoke first.

"It saw you."

"Yes."

"Not the king. The *thing*. It looked at you like—" she stopped, started again. "Like it had been looking for you."

"It has been," Tam said from his pallet. His voice was flat with exhaustion and fear. "Since Caerlum, maybe longer. Since the courier died, certainly. And now it knows your face, Ivo, and it has ten thousand greycoats and nine hundred and forty-four bells, and we still need six levels of its archive."

Silence, except the bells, which never stopped, never rested, never slept.

"So we go tonight," Bryn said. "Ninety seconds. Shift change at dusk tomorrow—we take it tonight, take it now, before it decides what it found." She was already sitting up, reaching for her boots. "Because I don't know what that thing does next, but I know what I do. And I'm done waiting in buildings that can hear me thinking."

Ivo lay staring at the ceiling, feeling the ghost of that vast attention still resting against his mind like a hand on a door.

Below them, six levels down, in the dark under the granary-plain walls, the answers were waiting.

So, probably, were the inquisitors.

He got up, and reached for his boots.
