## 15. What Tam Stole

The waystation town of Marrowgate had one decent inn, and Tam had been drinking its worst wine for six hours before the girl with the knife and the charm-seller with the guilty face finally dragged him upstairs to their room.

They thought they were interrogating him. That was the funny part. Bryn sat him on the edge of the bed with her arms crossed and her boots planted like she owned the floorboards, and Ivo stood by the shuttered window looking like a man who wanted to be anywhere else in the world, including the gallows. And Tam sat there with his third cup of vinegar pretending to be drunker than he was, because a man who seemed drunk was a man people underestimated, and underestimation was the only armor he'd had left since the day they burned his office.

"Start from the beginning," Bryn said. "The archive. What did you do?"

"I told you," Tam said. "I catalogued regalia. I asked questions about the Hollow Crown. I was exiled for it."

"You told us *part* of it," she said. "You told it the way a man tells a story he's rehearsed until it stopped hurting. I've watched my sister lie that exact way." Her jaw tightened on the word *sister* and released again. "The part you left out is the reason your hands shake when you think nobody's looking."

Tam looked down at his hands. They were shaking. He hadn't noticed.

He set the cup down on the nightstand, carefully, because if he set it down carelessly he'd knock it over, and then he would have to watch wine spread across a cheap bedspread and pretend that was the thing making his eyes sting.

"All right," he said.

* * *

"Here's what I told you," he began. "Here's the version I've been telling for nine years, to innkeepers and border guards and anyone who asked why a royal archivist smelled of cheap spirits and failure. I was Senior Cataloguer of Regalia. Do you know what that means? It means when something was carried into the vaults beneath the palace — a sword a king died holding, a ring that outlived three dynasties, a crown — it passed through my hands first. I measured it. Weighed it. Wrote its entry. Recorded what it was made of and what it was said to do and which chroniclers were full of shit about it. Nine hundred years of objects, and me at the end of the line, writing them into memory so the kingdom wouldn't forget them."

"The Crown came through your hands," Ivo said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"No." Tam almost laughed. "That's the thing. The Hollow Crown never came through my hands. Nobody's hands. It sits — or sat, or whatever it does — in the deepest vault, behind wards keyed to blood, and no archivist touches it. What came through my hands was its *paperwork*. Its provenance. Fourteen centuries of entries, each one written by the cataloguer before me, each one copied from the one before. And because I was the kind of insufferable boy who read footnotes, I read all fourteen centuries of it. Twice."

Bryn shifted her weight. "And?"

"And there's a gap." He held up a finger. "Not a missing page. Missing pages are common; vellum gets reused, fires happen, chancellors steal things to sell. No — this was worse. This was a *pattern*. Every fifty to seventy years, somewhere in the kingdom, there's a season of forgetting. A village that loses its founding date. A guild whose charter songs come back wrong. A noble house that can't remember why it hates another noble house, only that the hatred is old and must be honored. Chroniclers always note these things in passing, the way you'd note weather. And every time, within a year or two, someone updates the Crown's entry."

"Updates it how?" Ivo asked.

"That's the beautiful part. The horrifying part. The same sentence, in different hands, across four hundred years: *"Restored to full function. Tribute accepted. The realm endures."* Restored to full function. As though the Crown were a bridge that needed mending. As though the forgetting were maintenance." Tam's mouth tasted of the wine now, sour and metallic. "I cross-referenced. Sixty-one incidents of mass forgetting in the historical record. Fifty-eight of them followed by that sentence. The other three were years the court archivist position sat empty. Plague years. War years. Years when nobody was left to write the sentence down."

The room was quiet. Downstairs someone laughed too loudly, and the sound came up through the floorboards like water through a cracked hull.

"So I asked questions," Tam said. "That much was true. I took my findings to the Keeper of Records, who was a decent man named Aldous Vey, and Aldous went pale and told me to burn my notes and never speak of it, and I — being twenty-six and certain of my own importance — went over his head. To the Lord Chamberlain. Who smiled at me. Do you know how frightening a smile can be, when you realize it's the smile of a man deciding whether you're a problem or an opportunity?" He rubbed his eyes. "I was neither. I was a footnote. Two weeks later I was summoned before the Chancery Tribunal on charges of falsifying records and spreading sedition against the Crown's honor. My findings were declared fabrications. My access was revoked. My name was struck from the rolls. And I was put on a cart and taken to the city gate and told that if I ever spoke the words *Hollow Crown* inside Caerlum's walls again, I'd lose more than my post."

"But that's still not all of it," Bryn said. She hadn't moved. Her eyes were the color of a winter sea, and they didn't blink enough.

"No," Tam said. "That's not all of it. Because here's what I didn't tell the tribunal, either. Here's what got me exiled, really, underneath the official charge." He made himself look at her, then at Ivo, because this next part deserved witnesses. "Before Aldous told me to burn my notes, before the tribunal, before any of it — I stole."

* * *

"I stole pages," Tam said. "Eleven of them."

He stood up, because sitting still while saying this felt like lying, and walked to the narrow window and put his hand flat against the shutter as though checking whether the world outside was still there.

"They weren't the most important pages in the archive. That's what I tell myself at three in the morning — that I chose well, that I chose the load-bearing evidence. The original binding rite's marginalia, from the hand of the first Head Cataloguer, a woman named Ossiline Vael. Three pages of her private observations on what the Crown actually did when it was worn. Not the official entry — the official entry says *ensures continuity of just rule* — but her own notes, unguarded, written for herself. Then two pages of correspondence between her successor and the Court Physician of the era, discussing tremors in the king's hand and gaps in his recall after the coronation, and the physician's conclusion, in his own words, that *the artifact feeds upon the bearer's remembrance as a leech feeds upon blood, and I know not where the feeding ends.* Then the tribute ledgers. Four pages. Village names, dates, quantities — not coin, mind you, the tributes weren't coin. They were recorded as *remembrances received*. Names of the dead. Founding dates. Wedding days. Whole seasons of a town's collective past, itemized like grain."

"And the last two?" Ivo asked. His voice had gone very careful, very soft, the voice of a man standing near a wounded animal.

"The last two are why I'm going to die badly someday," Tam said. "They're instructions. A rite fragment. Partial, damaged, half the notation eaten by damp — but it's the counter-rite. The unbinding. Written in the margin of the tribute ledger by someone who signed themselves only with a raven drawn in ink." He turned around. "Someone knew. Four hundred years ago, someone in that archive knew exactly what the Crown was, and wrote down how to break it, and hid the knowledge inside the bureaucracy itself, where it would sit in the dark until someone like me found it."

"Why hide it instead of using it?" Bryn demanded.

"Because a rite needs a ravensworn," Tam said simply. "And the ravensworn were already gone. Extinct, the histories said. Dead out, or scattered, or renamed. Whoever drew that raven knew the knowledge might have to wait generations for a hand that could use it. So they buried it deep and trusted paper to outlast persecution." He smiled, and it hurt his face. "Paper usually does. That's the whole religion of archivists, if we have one. Ink is patient. People aren't."

"So you stole the pages," Ivo said. "And then?"

"And then Aldous told me to burn my notes. And I understood — truly understood, standing in his office watching a good man terrify himself for my sake — that the tribunal wasn't coming for my conclusions. It was coming for the pages. Someone above the Lord Chamberlain wanted eleven sheets of vellum removed from the world, and everyone else involved was just the broom sweeping toward them." He let out a breath. "So the night before the tribunal, I went back into the archive. Last time my seal worked. I took the eleven pages out of their boxes, and I burned eleven *other* pages in their place — tax schedules, boring as sin, same dimensions, aged the same way — and I walked out of the royal archives of Caerlum carrying four hundred years of buried truth under my arm like a stolen loaf of bread."

"And the tribunal found the fakes," Bryn said slowly, working it through.

"The tribunal found the fakes, declared them seditious forgeries planted by enemies of the realm, and burned them in the courtyard with great ceremony. Which means—" He spread his hands. "Officially, the real pages were destroyed nine years ago. There is no record anywhere that they exist. Except in one place."

* * *

Bryn saw it before Ivo did. He watched it land on her — the way she looked at his temples, at his eyes, and her hand drifted unconsciously toward her knife.

"What did you do with them?" she asked. "Nine years on the road. Inns. Border searches. Inquisitors pulling travelers apart at checkpoints. You couldn't carry eleven pages of high treason on your body for nine years."

"No," Tam agreed. "I couldn't. So I did the only clever thing I've ever done in my life, and I've regretted it every day since, and I'd do it again tomorrow, and that contradiction is basically the shape of my soul."

He sat back down on the bed, because his legs had opinions about standing.

"There's a discipline. Old hedge-craft, older than the colleges — Maren-blood magic, the country witches used to call it, before the purges. A memory-charm. Not the parlor trick kind, not *forget-this-conversation*. The deep kind. The kind that takes something held in the mind and moves it somewhere the mind doesn't go. Somewhere even a truth-spell skates over, because there's nothing there to find — no memory of hiding anything, no memory of the pages, no memory of stealing them. The hiding itself is hidden." He looked up at Ivo. "You were raised by a hedge-witch. You know what I'm describing."

Ivo had gone very still. "A vault," he said. "In your own head."

"A vault with no door," Tam said. "I found a woman in the Weald — the last practitioner I could find who remembered the full working, and she charged me everything I had and a favor I haven't paid yet, and she moved the eleven pages into me. Into my memory. Not as words I could recite — that was the genius and the cruelty of it. As *structure*. Buried below the level where I can reach. I cannot read them. I cannot describe them. If you asked me right now what page four says, I genuinely could not tell you, and no spell short of the extraction itself could make me tell you, because the knowing isn't *in* me the way knowing lives in a mind. It's stored the way a seed stores a tree."

"And the cost," Ivo said. Not a question either. This one, Tam thought, was a man who had grown up around magic recognizing the shape of a price before he heard it named.

"The cost," Tam said, "is that the vault isn't free. It rents space. Space in a living memory." He tapped his temple. "Every year, a little more of mine goes to keeping the pages intact. At first it was small things. I lost the name of my first tutor. Then my mother's face — I have her hands, her voice, the smell of her kitchen, but the face is gone, filed away somewhere below the waterline to make room for Ossiline Vael's marginalia. Then bigger rooms started going. I don't remember my thirtieth birthday, or the year of the flood, or the name of the woman I nearly married. She wrote me letters for two years. I kept them because I knew they mattered, and I couldn't remember why." His laugh came out cracked. "I keep a catalogue of my own losses now. It's a habit of the trade. Page after page. *Lost: the taste of honeycakes. Lost: the layout of my childhood street. Lost: what my father sounded like when he laughed.*"

"How much is left?" Bryn asked bluntly.

"Less every season." He met her stare, because she'd earned directness. "Lately the losses have accelerated. The woman in the Weald warned me the vault erodes — that a living mind resents being used as storage, and pushes back harder as it ages. She said I'd know when it was failing, because I'd start losing things adjacent to the pages. The context around them. And in the last year I've lost the name of the river I grew up on, the order of the months in the old calendar, and—" his voice caught, and he hated it— "and Aldous. Aldous Vey, the Keeper who tried to protect me. I remember that he existed. I remember that he was decent. I cannot remember his face anymore, and I worked beside that man for four years."

Silence. Downstairs the laughter had stopped; the inn was settling into its creaking midnight bones.

"So the clock," Ivo said finally, "isn't just the kingdom forgetting. It's you."

"It's both, and worse than both, and they're the same clock." Tam leaned forward, elbows on knees, and gave them the plain truth without varnish because they'd earned that too. "When the vault fails — and it is failing — those pages won't gently surface. The working tears loose. Everything stored comes up at once, unsorted, and takes whatever it wants of the surrounding mind with it on the way. The witch told me I might survive it as an idiot. She said it kindly, the way you'd tell a man his house might survive a fire as a foundation. Either the rite gets extracted properly, soon, by someone who understands the craft — or I lose it all, pages and self together, and four hundred years of buried truth dies in a ditch with me."

"Then we extract them," Bryn said immediately. "Now. Tonight. Find whoever—"

"You can't. Not here, not fast. The extraction needs the same practitioner-level craft that placed them, and the woman who placed them is eight years dead — I went back to thank her once and found her cottage green-grown. There may be others. There may not. That's the honest state of my knowledge, and I apologize for how little it comforts anyone." He rubbed his face with both hands. "There's one other road. The raven-marginalia describes the unbinding rite as *threefold*: three waystones opened in sequence, and at the throne, a willing ravensworn. If the map you're carrying leads to those stones — and Sera died getting it to a ravensworn heir, so I'd stake what's left of my memory that it does — then somewhere along that road there may be a stone, a shrine, a leftover piece of the binders' craft strong enough to open the vault properly. The binders built the Crown's counter into their own works. It stands to reason they built a key."

Ivo was staring at the floor. When he looked up, his face had the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. "You're telling me your life depends on finishing our quest."

"I'm telling you," Tam said, "that for nine years I've been a coward carrying a library. I stole the proof, I hid it, and then I drank myself stupid in border towns telling rehearsed stories about questions I asked, because confessing to the theft meant confessing to the vault, and the vault meant the price, and the price meant admitting I was dying by inches for eleven pieces of paper." He straightened his spine, which creaked. "Well. Now it's confessed. Now you know what I am: not a disgraced archivist. A thief. A dying one. And the single most dangerous object in this kingdom between the Crown and the vault under the palace."

Bryn uncrossed her arms for the first time all night. It was, Tam thought, the closest thing to absolution she offered anyone.

"Then you'd better write down everything you still remember," she said. "While you still remember it. Starting tonight. Names, dates, the rite fragments you *can* reach, your catalogue of losses — all of it. If the vault opens on its own terms or not at all, then the rest of you goes in the book too."

Tam blinked at her. Of all the mercies he'd imagined tonight — pity, horror, a knife — he hadn't imagined being handed a purpose.

"Ink is patient," he said softly.

"Then be patient with it," she said. "And Tam." She paused at the door, hand on the latch. "Your mother's kitchen. The honeycakes. Write those down too. They count."

She left. Ivo lingered, and for a moment the charm-seller looked at him with something that wasn't pity — something closer to recognition, one keeper of hidden burdens acknowledging another.

"The woman in the Weald," Ivo said quietly. "The one who placed the charm. What was her name?"

"Maren," Tam said. "Why?"

Ivo's face did something complicated and closed, like a shutter drawn halfway. "No reason," he said. "Get some sleep. We leave at dawn."

Dawn. Toward the first waystone, and the clock ticking behind Tam's eyes, patient as ink, hungry as the Crown.

That night he filled nine pages in a hand that shook, and dreamed of a kitchen he could no longer picture, smelling of bread he could no longer taste.
