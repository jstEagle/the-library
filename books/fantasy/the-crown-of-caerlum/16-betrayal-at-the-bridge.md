## 16. Betrayal at the Bridge

The river at Marlow's Crossing was the color of old pewter, and it smelled like the inside of a fish barrel, and I loved it anyway because water meant distance and distance meant we might actually live.

"Three coppers for the ferryman," Tam said, squinting at the toll board nailed to the bridge's near post. "That's robbery."

"That's a toll," Bryn said.

"That's *robbery with a sign*."

We'd been on the road nine days since Vessany, and somewhere in those nine days Tam had stopped being a stranger and started being an irritant, which I understood was his way of becoming a friend. He catalogued everything—the tolls, the birds, the number of times Bryn rolled her eyes per mile—and I'd begun to suspect that if he ever stopped talking, we should all be very worried.

Bryn walked ahead of us toward the bridge, her bow slung across her back and her hand nowhere near it, which was how she looked when she was relaxed, which was to say she still looked like she could kill everyone within thirty feet but had decided against it for now. The amulet of Sera's hung at her throat, mended where I'd soldered the crack, and she touched it sometimes without seeming to know she was doing it.

I hung back, as usual. Nine days of travel hadn't changed my habits so much as rearranged them. I still counted exits. I still sat facing doors. But somewhere between the wolves in the Weald and the village that had forgotten its own name, I'd stopped walking ten paces behind them and started walking five, and then three, and then beside them, and I hadn't noticed myself doing it until Tam pointed it out with the tactless delight he brought to all his observations.

"You're part of a group now," he'd said. "Isn't that interesting."

"It isn't."

"It is from where I'm standing."

From where I was standing, at the edge of Marlow's Crossing with the river running fast below, it was terrifying. Groups got people killed. Groups drew eyes. For five years I had been one man with a cellar full of harmless trinkets and a face nobody remembered twice, and that invisibility had kept me breathing. Now there were three of us, and one of us was a woman who'd sworn vengeance over her sister's body, and another was an exiled archivist whose name was probably still in inquisitorial files, and I was—I was whatever I was turning out to be. The ravensworn heir. The last of a bloodline of binders. A man with hidden script crawling up his arm whenever he channeled too much.

A man the inquisitor Corvane wanted very badly.

"Ferryman's waving us over," Bryn called back. "Come on. I want to be across before dark."

The bridge itself was old, older than the town, built of grey stone with a humped back and a chapel niche in the middle where some long-dead mason had carved a saint whose face the weather had eaten away. Carts crossed it in both directions—grain going south, salt coming north—and a knot of travelers waited by the ferry landing below, arguing about fares.

Tam paid the toll with coins he counted out twice, because he counted everything twice, and we stepped onto the bridge.

I felt it before I saw anything. That was new, too—that sense, like a toothache in the air, when binding magic was close. I'd felt it at the first waystone, deep and humming under the earth. This was different. Thinner. Wronger. Like hearing a song played on an instrument it wasn't written for.

I stopped.

"What?" Bryn said, half a stride ahead.

"I don't—" My mouth had gone dry. "Something's off."

"Everything's off. We've been off since Caerlum." She turned back to me, exasperated, and in the moment her eyes left the far end of the bridge, the far end of the bridge stopped being empty.

They came out of the chapel niche first. Two men in the dun-colored cloaks of local levy, crossbows already raised, and behind them, unhurried, hands folded behind his back like a man strolling through a garden, came Corvane.

He wore no armor. He never did. Just the grey coat of the inquisition with its silver thread at the collar, and his face wearing that expression I'd come to dread more than any weapon: courteous, patient, almost kind. The look of a man who has already won and is sorry about the inconvenience.

"Ivo Ravensworth," he said, and my name in his mouth sounded like a key turning in a lock.

The travelers on the bridge scattered—not running, just flowing away with the practiced ease of people who knew what inquisitors meant and wanted no part of it. Within seconds the three of us stood alone on the stone, forty feet from Corvane, twenty from the crossbows, with the pewter river churning below.

"Walk backward," Bryn said quietly. "Slow. Back the way we came."

"They'll shoot," Tam whispered.

"They'll shoot if we run. Walking's not running yet."

Corvane took two steps forward. Behind him, more figures emerged from the toll house—a third crossbow, and a woman in a ferryman's oilskin who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes. I looked at her, and then at the toll board, and then at the way she stood apart from the other locals, and I understood.

"The informant," I said.

Corvane smiled slightly. "Mistress Odile. Yes. A helpful woman. She remembered three travelers asking after the road south, and one of them"—his gaze settled on me with terrible gentleness—"asking whether the bridges were watched. You see how it works, Master Ravensworth? People remember what they're afraid of. Fear is the finest informant the Crown employs. It costs nothing and it never sleeps."

"You could have taken me in Caerlum," I said. My voice sounded steadier than I felt. "You've had weeks."

"I could have. I chose not to." He took another step. "Do you know why? Because I wanted to understand you first. I read everything the archive had on the ravensworn line—which, incidentally, was nearly nothing, someone was thorough about burning it—and I spoke to hedge-witches and charm-sellers and one very frightened fishwife who bought a luck-knot from you in the spring." Something flickered across his face, there and gone. "You gave her back her luck, you know. After the tithe collectors hollowed her street. She remembers her mother's name again. She doesn't know why. She thinks it was prayer."

Tam made a small sound beside me. Bryn's hand had drifted, casual as breathing, to the knife at her belt.

"So here is what happens now," Corvane continued. "You come with me. Your companions walk free—I give you my word on it, and my word holds; ask anyone in the inquisition, they hate me for it. You and I will go to the capital together, quietly, and you will stand before the king, and you will tell him what your blood tells you when you touch the waystones. That is all. No chains. No cell. A conversation."

"And if I say no?"

"Then this becomes complicated, and I would genuinely prefer it didn't." He spread his hands. "I have read the same histories you have, Master Ravensworth, or the fragments that survive. I know what your ancestors bound into the Crown. I know what it cost. And I believe—with my whole heart, which is the only thing I own—that the thing it binds must stay bound, because the alternative is the tyrant-king rising again out of whatever grave your grandmothers poured him into. You are not a criminal to me. You are a lock that has learned to walk. And locks belong on their doors."

"The king is dead," Tam burst out. "You must have seen it—you've stood in audience chambers, you've seen him up close—"

"I have knelt before His Majesty four hundred times," Corvane said, and for just a moment something moved behind his certainty, some hairline fracture, "and every time, the kingdom stood. Every time, the harvests came in and the borders held and the memory-tithe stayed small enough to bear. Whatever wears the crown, it *works*. Show me your alternative working, archivist, and we can discuss theology."

Bryn moved.

I saw it start—a shift of weight, a breath drawn—and I saw the crossbowmen track her, and I did the only thing I could think of, the thing five years of hiding had trained out of me and nine days of these people had trained back in.

I threw my hand out and pulled the magic up through my feet like drawing water from a poisoned well.

The ward went up as a shimmer, barely visible, a skin of force across the bridge between us and the crossbows—and it held for exactly as long as it took me to realize I'd never cast anything that large in my life. The first bolt hit it and shattered into splinters of light. The second punched through like the ward was wet paper.

And Bryn was already moving, already shoving past my ward-line, knife out, closing the distance to the nearest crossbowman—

"No!" I shouted.

The second bolt was loosed before she reached him. Not aimed at her. Aimed at me—at the man standing exposed with his hand raised and magic bleeding off his fingers, the prize, the lock that had learned to walk.

She saw it. I will swear until I die that she saw it, that in that half-second she did the arithmetic faster than I did and didn't even slow down.

She turned her back to the bolt and put herself between it and me.

It took her high in the chest, below the collarbone, punching through and out, and the sound she made wasn't a scream—it was just air leaving her, a grunt, almost annoyed, like the world had interrupted her mid-sentence.

Time went strange. I remember it in pieces. Tam screaming. The crossbowman fumbling to reload. Corvane shouting *hold, hold, I want him alive*, and meaning it, meaning it so sincerely that it bought us the three seconds we needed. Bryn on her knees on the stone, looking down at the blood spreading across her shirt with an expression of genuine offense, as though her body had betrayed her worse than any informant.

And I remember moving. Not deciding—moving. I caught her before her head hit the bridge, and the world narrowed to her weight in my arms and the blood soaking hot through my sleeves and her face, gray already, furious at being gray.

"Bridge," she said. "Get off the—"

The third bolt cracked off the stone where we'd been kneeling. Tam grabbed my collar and hauled, and I hauled Bryn, and somehow we were moving, stumbling backward off the bridge's shoulder toward the ferry landing, bolts snapping past us, Corvane's voice cracking out behind us—not with rage but with something worse, urgency, *don't let him reach the water, don't lose him*—

The ferry. The ferryman had fled, but the boat was tied at the landing, and Tam cut the rope with a letter-opener he'd somehow never surrendered to anyone, and we went into the current sideways, all of us, the boat slamming off a piling, the river taking us like it had been waiting all day for exactly this.

* * *

The river carried us fast. Marlow's Crossing fell behind in the dusk, its bridge shrinking to a grey hump with small figures on it, and then trees swallowed the bank and there was only the water, the oar Tam wrestled with, and Bryn bleeding in the bottom of the boat.

I knelt over her and pressed my hands to the wound and pretended that pressure was enough.

The bolt had gone in below her left collarbone and out beneath her shoulder blade. Exit wounds bled less, Tam said—said quickly, in his lecturing voice, the voice he used when facts were the only rope keeping him from panic. Pressure. Keep her warm. Keep her awake.

"Bryn. Hey. Look at me."

"I'm looking at you." Her voice was thin but level. "You look terrible."

"You just—" My hands were shaking. I pressed harder and she hissed. "You jumped in front of a crossbow bolt. For me. Are you insane? I was supposed to—the whole point was they wanted *me*—"

"Yes." She coughed, winced. "That was the arithmetic."

"That's not—that's not *arithmetic*, that's—"

"Ivo." She caught my wrist. Her grip was weaker than it should have been and that fact went through me like cold water. "Stop talking. Listen. If I fade out, keep me awake. Talk. You're good at brooding silently, do the opposite."

Tam rowed. The banks slid past, dark trees, an occasional lit window that none of us dared approach. Inquisitors had reach in towns. Informants everywhere. Mistress Odile's face floated back to me—the woman who wouldn't meet our eyes, who'd sold three strangers for whatever the Crown paid or threatened, and I couldn't even hate her properly, because I'd spent five years being the man who never met anyone's eyes either. Fear was the finest informant. Corvane was right about that, and being right made him worse.

"Bryn." I tore a strip from my coat hem and packed it against the exit wound. "Stay with me."

"Where else would I go?" A ghost of her grin. Then it faded, and her eyes found mine, and there was something in them I'd never seen there before—something unarmored. "Sera died alone in a stranger's cellar," she said. "Alone, in the dark, holding a map. I used to think about that every night. How afraid she must have been. How no one—" Her breath hitched. "Don't let me die like that. That's all. Not alone in the dark."

"You're not dying."

"Promise the other part, then."

"You're not alone in the dark. I'm right here. Tam's here. You're the most annoyingly accompanied dying woman on this entire river."

That got a laugh out of her, which turned into a cough, which scared me badly, because there was red at the corner of her mouth.

"Blood in the throat," Tam said quietly from the stern. He didn't look back. His knuckles were white on the oar. "Means it may have clipped the lung. Or not. I'm an archivist, not a surgeon. I catalogued a medical text once. Chapter seven was wounds. I remember chapter seven."

"Then recite chapter seven," I said.

So he did. In his dry, pedantic, relentless voice, rowing and reciting, arrow wounds and their treatment, poultices and packing and the signs of a lung filling, while the river ran black under a sky going to stars, and Bryn's hand stayed wrapped around my wrist with a grip that kept loosening and that I kept pressing tighter whenever it slipped.

Somewhere in the middle of it, without deciding to, I did the thing I'd been refusing to do since the wolves. I let the magic rise—not into a ward, not into anything useful, just let it fill my hands where they pressed against her wound, warm, the small green warmth Maren had taught me when I was fourteen and she'd said *healing is just the body remembering itself, you're only reminding it*. I had no skill for it. I had abandoned the lessons. But I poured what I had into her anyway, all of it, until my vision spotted and my arms shook, and the bleeding slowed.

Not stopped. Slowed.

"It's not enough," I said. "Tam, it's not enough. She needs a real healer, and every healer in every town is a risk, and—"

"Maren," Tam said.

The name landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"She's two days' ride north of here," Tam went on, still rowing, still reciting. "You told us. The night around the fire, when Bryn asked why you really ran from her. Hedge-witch. Healer. The best you ever saw." He finally looked back at me, and his eyes behind their smudged spectacles were steady and merciless. "You said she could pull a fever out of a dying man like drawing a splinter. You said it like a man describing a wound of his own."

"I can't go back there."

"You can't go anywhere else." He nodded down at Bryn, whose eyes had closed, whose grip on my wrist had gone slack. "Chapter seven, final passage: *when the remedy available is unbearable, the wound decides, not the pride.* I may be paraphrasing. I was drunk when I catalogued it."

Bryn's fingers twitched against mine. I bent close.

"Still here," she murmured. "Told you. Where else would I go."

Her lips were gray. The boat rocked. Ahead, the river bent south toward the lowlands and the capital and Corvane's net, and north, somewhere past the dark hills, a cottage I had run from five years ago with a slammed door and words I'd never taken back.

I had spent my whole life building rules so I'd never owe anyone anything, so no one could be taken from me because I'd never let myself have them. Nine days. Nine days and a shared fire and a mended amulet, and the whole careful architecture was gone, burned through like my ward on the bridge, and underneath it was this: the simple, unbearable truth that I could not imagine the world without her in it, this sharp, grieving, infuriating woman who had taken a bolt meant for a stranger and made him not a stranger anymore.

"Hold on," I told her, and my voice broke on it, and I didn't care. "Hold on, Bryn. I know someone who can save you. And this time I'm not running from her."

Tam shipped the oar and turned the bow north, toward the current that would fight us every stroke of the way, and began to row.
