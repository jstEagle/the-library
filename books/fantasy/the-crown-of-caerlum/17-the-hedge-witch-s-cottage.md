## 17. The Hedge-Witch's Cottage

Bryn stopped bleeding twice on the road north.

The first time was an accident of luck—a tanner's scrap of cloth Ivo stuffed into the wound below her collarbone, pressed there by Tam's shaking hands while Ivo held the stolen riverboat steady against the current. The second time was not luck at all. It was magic, and it nearly killed him to do it.

He'd sworn off the deep workings years ago. Small charms, sleight of hand, a warmth in the fingertips—those he allowed himself, the way a starving man allows himself crumbs. But clotting a crossbow wound is not a crumb. He sat in the bottom of the boat with Bryn's head in his lap and poured himself into her the way you pour water into a cracked jug: knowing most of it would spill, knowing the spilling would cost him. His nose bled. His vision went white at the edges. When it was done, Bryn's breathing had steadied and Ivo could not feel his hands.

"She needs more than that," Tam said quietly. "That's a stopgap. The bolt head is still in there—I can feel it when she breathes wrong. And there's something on the metal. A residue. Inquisitor work."

"Corvane's bolts are blessed," Ivo said. "Not blessed-blessed. Treated. They keep wounds open."

Tam looked at him over his spectacles, and for once the archivist did not lecture. "Then you know where we have to go."

Ivo looked north, where the Weald folded into hills, and said nothing, because saying it would make it real.

* * *

Maren's cottage sat in a clearing that did not want to be found. Five years ago, Ivo had walked away from it and told himself the path would forget him. Paths near Maren's house always forgot; she paid them in riddles and old songs. He'd counted on that. He'd built five years of survival on the assumption that no one who knew him before Caerlum would ever find him again.

Now he stood at the treeline with Bryn unconscious across his shoulders and Tam wheezing behind him, and the clearing opened up like a mouth.

"You knew how to find it," Tam said.

"I didn't." Ivo shifted Bryn's weight. "It found me. Or it remembered me. Same thing, out here."

The cottage was smaller than he remembered, or he was larger. Turf roof, wattle walls gone silver with weather, herbs hanging under the eaves in bundles so old they'd turned to dust held together by habit. Smoke rose from the chimney, thin and gray. She was home. Of course she was home. Maren had lived in that cottage longer than some kingdoms lasted, and would live in it long after this one finished dying.

Ivo set his shoulder against the door and pushed it open without knocking, because knocking was a courtesy between equals and he had never once been Maren's equal.

The smell hit him first—dried yarrow, tallow, the iron tang of a working recently done—and then the heat of the hearth, and then Maren herself, standing at her table with a mortar in one hand, looking at him the way you look at a stain that has walked back into your house.

She was older. That was all. Her hair had gone from iron-gray to white, and her hands were thinner, but her eyes were exactly as he'd left them: brown, flat, patient as stones in a river. Eyes that had watched a thousand apprentices fail and remembered every one.

"You're bleeding on my floor," she said.

"It's not my blood."

"No," she said. "Yours never ran freely enough. Put her down."

* * *

He laid Bryn on the table where the mortar had been, and Maren swept the herbs aside with her forearm and put two fingers into the wound without ceremony or apology. Bryn came halfway awake, made a sound like tearing canvas, and Maren pressed her back down with one palm flat on her sternum.

"Hold her," Maren said, and it was not a request.

Ivo held her. Bryn's hand found his wrist and gripped it hard enough to grind bone, and her eyes opened, unfocused, finding his face.

"Ivo," she said. "Don't—" Whatever she meant to forbid him, she never said. Her eyes rolled back and she was gone again, and Ivo stood there holding the hand of a woman who had put a knife to his throat three weeks ago and now trusted him with her whole weight, and he thought: *this is what running costs. This is the bill.*

Maren worked in silence. He remembered that about her—the silence. Other hedge-witches muttered and sang and made a theater of it, because theater inspired confidence in the frightened. Maren worked like a woman gutting fish. She drew the bolt head with a hooked sliver of bone and a word he didn't catch—or refused to catch—and set it on a dish, where it smoked faintly and smelled of cold iron and something sweeter underneath, something like rotting fruit.

"Treated," Maren said. "Church work. Who shot her?"

"Inquisitors."

"Mm." She packed the wound with moss and a paste that steamed gently, then stitched it with thread she pulled from her own sleeve hem. "They'll come here, eventually. Nothing keeps them out but distance and disinterest, and I have run short of both."

"We'll be gone by dawn."

"You'll be gone when she can stand," Maren said. "Not before. Sit down, Ivo. You look like a scarecrow the crows got tired of."

There was a stool by the hearth. He had sat on it a thousand times as a boy—no, not a boy, he corrected himself, as a young man of nineteen who had thought he knew everything—and sitting on it now was like stepping into cold water. Everything was exactly where it had been. The kettle hook. The chipped blue cup he'd once knocked off the shelf and mended so badly she'd kept it anyway, as a lesson. The third chair, the one that had belonged to the apprentice before him, whose name Ivo had never learned because Maren never spoke of failures and he had been too young and too pleased with himself to ask.

Tam stood in the doorway, dripping, clutching his satchel like a shield.

"And you're the archivist," Maren said, without turning around.

Tam started. "You know me?"

"I know the shape of you. Burned men carry a particular smoke." She finally turned and looked at him, top to bottom, the way she used to look at a sick animal brought to her door. "Sit by the fire. Don't touch anything. If you touch anything, I'll know, and you won't enjoy how I know."

Tam sat. Tam touched nothing.

* * *

Bryn slept on the table, breathing in long, careful pulls. Maren covered her with a blanket that had been mended so many times it was more knot than wool, and then she stood over her for a moment, watching, and Ivo saw something move through her face that he almost recognized. Tenderness, maybe. Or its expensive cousin, grief.

"She fought," Maren said. "You can tell, with bodies. Some people take a wound like they were waiting for permission. This one argued with it the whole way here."

"That's Bryn."

"Mm." Maren turned. "Tea?"

"I—yes. Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." She filled the dented kettle and hung it. "Tell me why you're here. Not the wound. The wound is obvious. Tell me the rest."

So he told her. All of it, in the flat voice he'd learned for inquisitors—the courier dying in his shop, the map, the waystones, the king who was already dead and walking anyway, the Crown eating the kingdom's memory one village at a time, Corvane, the bridge, the bolt. He left out almost nothing, which surprised him. He had planned to leave out everything. But the cottage had a quality he'd forgotten: it loosened the tongue. Not a charm—he'd have felt a charm. Just the accumulated weight of a place where he had once, briefly, been honest.

When he finished, Maren poured the tea. Three cups. She gave one to Tam, one to Ivo, and set the third on the table beside Bryn's sleeping hand, for whenever she woke.

"The ravensworn," she said.

"You knew." His voice came out strange. "Tam says the bloodline was hunted out generations ago. He says it's extinct. But you looked at me the day I arrived on your doorstep and you—you tested me, didn't you? The fever charm. The one that should have failed. You knew what I was and you never said a word. Five years, Maren. Five years I thought I was nobody."

"You were nobody," Maren said. "That was rather the point."

The words landed like a slap, and he let them, because he deserved the sting if not the truth behind it.

"Why?" he said. "Why hide it? If I'd known—if I'd understood what I could do—"

"What could you have done?" She sat down across from him, cup untouched, hands folded. "At nineteen. With your talent and your temper. What exactly would you have done with the knowledge that you were the last of a line of binders, hunted by a church that burns children for lesser blood?"

"I could have—"

"Run faster?" she said. "You already ran. From me. From a cottage in the woods where no one would ever find you. You ran from safety because I asked too much of you, and now you sit in my kitchen and tell me you'd have stood and fought an empire if only I'd given you a banner to carry." She shook her head slowly. "No, Ivo. I hid it because hiding it was the only mercy available. And I failed anyway, because you were never going to stay hidden. People like you don't stay hidden. They just take longer to surface."

"I ran because you lied to me," he said, and heard his own voice crack on it, five years late. "Every day. You taught me charms and called them tricks. You taught me binding and called it knots. You let me think I was ordinary, and I built my whole life on being ordinary, and it turns out even that was a lie you fed me. Do you know what that does to a person? Finding out the ground was never real?"

"Yes," Maren said. "I know precisely what it does. Because my own mistress did it to me, and her mistress did it to her, all the way back to the first ravensworn who decided that a child raised in ignorance survives longer than a child raised in fear." She lifted her cup at last and drank. "We are a lineage of liars, Ivo. It's our second inheritance. The first is the binding. I chose which one to give you."

"And I chose neither," Ivo said. "I threw both back in your face and took myself to Caerlum."

"Yes," she said. "And look what you did there. Five years invisible. Five years selling luck-knots to fishwives and pretending the magic in your fingers was sleight of hand." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. "Your knots were terrible, by the way. Tam showed me the map's wrapping cord. You tied a ravensworn keeper's hitch and didn't even know it. Any binder alive would have known you on sight."

Ivo stared at the cord still coiled at his belt, the one that had held Sera's map for a month of roads, and felt the ground shift again—but differently this time. Not the ground lying to him. The ground revealing how much of it had been him all along.

* * *

Bryn woke near midnight, feverish, asking for water and then for Sera and then, seeing Ivo's face above her, for nothing at all. Maren changed the dressing, fed her willow-bark tea laced with something bitter, and pronounced that the treated edge of the wound had surrendered. "She'll live. She'll hate the scar. Good. Scars argue with vanity."

Tam, meanwhile, had discovered Maren's bookshelf and was suffering visibly from the instruction not to touch it. Ivo found him standing a respectful arm's length away, reading spines, making small wounded noises.

"There are folios here," Tam whispered, "that were lost in the Archive fire. I catalogued their ashes. Their *ashes*, Ivo. And they're on a shelf in a witch's cottage between a jar of newts and a book on—on goat husbandry."

"Goat husbandry is chapter twelve of the same folio," Maren said from across the room, not looking up. "Practical knowledge keeps theory honest. Touch nothing."

Ivo left them to their silent standoff and went outside.

The night was clear and cold, stars sharp as nailheads. He walked to the well at the clearing's edge—the same well, the same crooked post—and stood there doing nothing, which was its own kind of prayer. Behind him the cottage glowed amber at the windows. Inside it were a woman he'd betrayed, a woman he'd failed to save, and a man he'd known three weeks and already could not imagine the road without. He thought about Sera dying on his cellar floor with the map under his hands, whispering *find the ravensworn*, and how he had spent a month believing those words pointed at someone else. Someone worthier. Some hero of the old blood.

There was no one else. There had never been anyone else.

Maren came out without a cloak, because Maren had never in her life acknowledged weather. She stood beside him at the well and they watched each other's breath fog.

"You're going to the capital," she said.

"The third waystone is under the palace. Tam's pages say the rite needs all three, opened in order, and a ravensworn at the throne."

"And the rite requires the bearer to take the binding into themselves." She said it flatly, factually, the way she'd once told him that the fever charm would hurt and that complaining wouldn't shorten it. "Or pass it on. Those are the only two doors. You've read Tam's pages?"

"Some. Not all."

"Read all of them." She reached into her sleeve and produced something small, wrapped in waxed linen. "Before you go. Two things."

She unwrapped the first: a charm the size of a thumbnail, carved from antler, threaded on a leather cord. It was old work. Older than her. He could feel it humming against his palm even before she spoke over it.

"A truth-charm," she said. "Wear it under your shirt. It does one thing: when someone speaks to you with intent to deceive, it warms. It won't tell you *what* the lie is. It won't protect you from your own lies—that would require a charm the size of a cathedral. But in a court full of hollow men and a church full of sincere ones, knowing when you're being lied to may be the difference between arriving at that throne and becoming part of the furniture."

"And the second thing?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something she had never done in all the years he'd lived under her roof. She reached up and took his face in both her thin, cool hands, the way you hold something you are checking for cracks.

"When you get there," she said, "and the Crown offers you whatever you want most—and it will, it is very good at wanting, better than you are—I need you to remember one thing. It will offer to give you back everything that was eaten. Every memory, every name, every village's history. And it can. That's the horror of it. The offer will be *true*." Her grip tightened. "But a kingdom carried by one man's sacrifice is just another binding, Ivo. A kinder cage is still a cage. Your grandmother's generation learned that too late, and it cost them everything, and it left you holding the bill."

"I know," he said. "I think I've known since the second stone."

"Knowing isn't enough. It will show you Bryn safe. It will show you Tam whole. It will show you yourself, loved, unafraid, home." Her voice dropped. "It will show you this cottage with the fire lit and me in it, and you forgiven, and none of it will be false except the price. That's how it wins. Not with lies. With truths you can't afford."

Ivo's throat closed. He put his hands over hers, over his own face, and stood there in the cold with the woman he'd abandoned holding him like a son, and neither of them said the word *forgive*, because it was too large for the space between them and too small for what was actually happening.

"Come inside," Maren said finally, letting go. "It's freezing, and your archivist is about to explode from not touching my shelves, and your fighter will wake at dawn hungry enough to eat the goat husbandry book." She paused at the door, silhouetted against the light. "Ivo. The truth-charm. Wear it always. And when it warms in the presence of the king—"

She stopped.

"When it warms in the presence of the king," she said again, slower, "it will mean the thing wearing him is lying. But listen carefully, boy, because this is the part no page will tell you: sometimes it doesn't lie. Sometimes, when it speaks of sorrow, of hunger, of what it lost—it tells the truth. And that truth will be worse than any lie it could have told you. That's how you'll know it can be pitied. And pity, Ivo, is the last weapon it will leave you."

She went inside. Ivo stood a moment longer under the nailhead stars, the antler charm cooling against his sternum, and thought about pity as a weapon, and about what kind of monster leaves its enemies armed with grief.

From the cottage, faintly, he heard Bryn's voice—awake now, rough, demanding—and Tam's answering laugh, startled out of him like a bird from cover. The sound of people waiting for him. Not needing. Waiting.

He went in out of the cold.
