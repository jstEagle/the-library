## 18. Maren's Warning

The girl in the bed would live. That was the first thing Maren let herself feel, because everything else could wait and grief could not.

Bryn slept the deep, drugged sleep of someone whose body had finally been given permission to stop fighting. The crossbow bolt had gone through her shoulder and out the back, missing the lung by a finger's width—Maren had felt that finger's width with her own hands, had pressed her palm flat against the wound and whispered the old words her own mistress had taught her, the ones that asked the body to remember it wanted to live. The body had listened. They usually did, if you caught them early enough and if you meant what you said.

Ivo stood in the doorway of the sickroom like a man afraid the floor might be pulled out from under him. He'd been standing there for an hour. Maybe two. He hadn't sat since they'd arrived, hadn't eaten the bread she'd left on the table, hadn't taken off his mud-caked boots even though she'd told him twice that the cottage floor was older than his grandmother and didn't care about his dirt.

"You should sleep," Maren said, not turning from the window. Outside, the herb garden was going to seed the way it did every autumn, the feverfew gone leggy, the comfrey sprawling. Five years of autumns he had missed. She had counted them without meaning to, the way you count the gaps in a fence.

"I'm fine."

"You're not. You look like something a crow dragged out of a ditch." She turned then and looked at him properly, and the looking hurt more than she'd prepared for. He was twenty-six now. When he'd run, he'd still had boyhood on him—the softness at the jaw, the way he flinched before he decided things. The flinch was still there. Everything soft had been burned off and replaced with a kind of careful blankness she recognized, because she'd worn it herself for thirty years before he came to her door.

"Sit down, Ivo."

He sat. That surprised her a little; the old Ivo would have argued just to prove he could.

She poured the tea she'd been keeping warm—chamomile and something bitter for the shock—and put it in his hands and made him hold it until his fingers stopped shaking around the cup. Then she sat across from him, in her own chair, the one with the worn arms, and she looked at the fire instead of at him, because some things were easier to say to a fire.

"You want to know why I never told you," she said.

His cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Don't bother denying it. You've been circling the question since you walked in, the way you circle everything. You ask about Bryn's fever, about my wards, about whether the road south is watched. And every question has this other question hiding inside it like a worm in an apple." She took a breath. "Why didn't you tell me what I am."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse went about its small business, indifferent to kingdoms.

"Yes," Ivo said quietly.

"Then I'll tell you. But you'll hear all of it, or none of it. No walking out halfway through. No deciding halfway through that you already know better." She met his eyes. "You were always quick to decide you knew better. It's your worst inheritance."

"My inheritance," he repeated. "That's a careful word for it."

"It's the only word for it." She leaned back. "Your mother brought you to me when you were four years old. Do you remember her at all?"

"Bits. Her hands. She hummed when she worked."

"She hummed when she was frightened, is what she did. It was a spell, actually—a small one, a self-soothing charm, the kind hedge-witches teach their daughters so they don't scream in front of the wrong people. Your mother was better at hiding than anyone I ever knew. Better than me. Better than you." Maren wrapped her hands around her own cup. "She came up my path in the rain with you on her hip and frostbite on her heels, and she said, *Take him and never tell him what our blood is worth.* Those were almost her exact words. What our blood is *worth*. Not what it is. Worth. Like a price tag on a thing in a shop window."

"What blood?" Ivo asked, though Maren could see by his face that he already knew—that Tam had shown him, that the revealing charm had lit him up like a lantern, that he'd spent however many days since then carrying the word *ravensworn* around inside himself like a stone he couldn't spit out.

"You know what blood."

"I want to hear you say it."

"Ravensworn." She said it plainly, no ceremony, because ceremony would have been a lie and she had promised herself honesty tonight even if it killed them both. "Binders. The line that helped make the Hollow Crown. Thought extinct for three hundred years, except it wasn't, because your mother's mother's mother kept moving, kept marrying out, kept having daughters and telling them nothing, until the name wore off entirely and only the blood stayed. Until there was a woman with frostbitten heels carrying the last of it up my path in the rain."

"And you hid it. From me. For twenty years."

"For twenty-two years," Maren said. "And I'd do it again."

He set the cup down hard enough that tea slopped over the rim. "You don't get to say that. You don't get to sit there and say you'd do it again when—" His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hating it, hating that five years away hadn't fixed the crack. "When I spent my whole life thinking I was nobody. Thinking the magic was just—something wrong with me, something I had to bury. I built my entire life around being nobody, Maren. I had *rules*. Don't channel. Don't stand near the ley-stones. Don't let anyone see your hands shake when magic passes close. I thought I was a coward. I thought the fear was mine, some flaw in me, and it turns out I was raised inside a secret I was never allowed to see."

"Yes," Maren said. "You were."

"That's it? That's all you have? *Yes, you were?*"

"What do you want me to say, Ivo? That I'm sorry?" She stood, because sitting still had become impossible. "I am sorry. I have been sorry since the day you ran, and sorry for five years before that, every time you asked me why we never celebrated anything, why we moved the wards twice a year, why I taught you to hide before I taught you to heal. You think I didn't watch you build those rules? I handed you the stones myself. Every rule I gave you was a wall between you and the truth, and I built them knowing exactly what I was doing, and I would build them again, and I will not apologize for the building. I will apologize for how much it cost you. Those are different things."

"How are they different?"

"Because the alternative was you dying at nineteen," Maren said, "or worse. Because the alternative was the Crown finding you."

The word landed in the room like cold water. Ivo had gone very still.

"The Crown doesn't find people," he said. "The Crown sits on a head in the capital and eats memories. That's what Tam says. That's what the map says. It doesn't send out hunters for—"

"It doesn't need to send hunters." Maren crouched at the hearth and fed a split log to the fire, mostly to give her hands something to do. "It *listens*. That's the part nobody understands who hasn't felt it. The binding your ancestors wove—it isn't a cage, exactly. It's more like a net thrown over the whole kingdom, and every thread of that net is anchored somewhere, and one kind of anchor is ravensworn blood. Living ravensworn blood. You are a knot in that net, Ivo. You always have been. When you channel, when you work magic openly, the thread tightens, and the thing wearing the king feels the tug the way a fisherman feels a fish. Your mother understood that. Her mother understood it. That's why they buried the name. That's why your mother ran until her heels froze. Not because she was ashamed of what you were. Because she loved you enough to make you small."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used for lying, and Maren recognized it with a fresh twist of guilt: she had taught him that voice too.

"So the trinkets. The luck-knots. The little charms I sold in Caerlum for five years. Sleight of hand, misdirection, never channeling—"

"Were real discipline," she said. "Harder than any training I gave you. You held a dam shut with your bare will for five years in the middle of the most warded city in the kingdom. Don't you dare call that nothing. But yes. If you'd ever slipped—if you'd ever truly opened yourself, even once, even for a kindness—the tug would have gone down the thread, and something in the palace would have turned its head. And then Corvane, or whatever Corvane was before Corvane, would have come for you. Not to kill you. Killing a ravensworn wastes the blood. They'd have come to bring you to the throne. To be *used*."

"Used how?"

Maren looked at him. Really looked, the way she used to when he was small and had broken something and was deciding whether to lie to her.

"You know how," she said gently. "Tam found it in the pages, didn't he. Or the waystones showed you. The unbinding rite needs a willing ravensworn at the throne. But that's only half of it, and Tam's pages wouldn't have the half that matters, because the half that matters was never written down. It was passed mouth to ear, binder to binder, and your family broke the chain on purpose." She sat back down across from him. "So I'll finish the chain, right now, tonight, because you're going to walk into that vault whether I bless it or curse it, and you deserve to know what you're carrying."

"Tell me."

"The Crown cannot simply be shattered. Your ancestors tried—there's a whole schism in the memory at the second stone, you've seen it, the ones who wanted to break it against the ones who wanted to keep it. Breaking it releases everything it holds at once. Every memory it has eaten, every scrap of magic it has swallowed, all of it coming apart in a single moment. Do you know what that does to a kingdom?"

"No."

"Neither does anyone alive. That's the point. The binders who argued for destruction were guessing. The ones who argued for control were also guessing. They chose control, and their choice ate them, and the Crown learned from their choice. It learned that a bearer can be consumed slowly, that a kingdom can be milked instead of slaughtered. It has had three hundred years to refine the technique." She folded her hands so they wouldn't shake. "Unbinding it safely means drawing the binding *out* of the Crown and into a living vessel. A ravensworn vessel. The binding doesn't die. It transfers. Someone has to wear it—not the crown of gold, but the crown of weight. All of it. The hunger, the hungering-back, the constant pull of a kingdom's worth of stolen memory pressing against the inside of your skull, forever, or until your body gives out, which historically takes years. Decades, if you're strong. Your great-grandmother's brother carried a fragment of a failed binding for eleven years before he walked into the sea."

Ivo's face had gone white. "There has to be another way."

"There is one other way." Maren made herself say it steadily. "Pass it on. The binding can be handed to another ravensworn, willingly taken, willingly given. Which means the last ravensworn in the world would hand a lifetime of torment to the only other person who shares his blood. Except there is no other person, Ivo. You're the end of the line. So the choice is simpler than it is cruel: carry it yourself, or shatter the Crown and gamble every soul in the kingdom on a guess three hundred years old."

The fire cracked. In the sickroom, Bryn murmured in her sleep, and both of them turned toward the sound like compass needles, and Maren saw—Ivo probably didn't, but Maren saw—how completely the girl had already become his reason.

"Why are you telling me this now?" he asked finally. "If you hid it for twenty-two years to protect me. Why hand me the truth the night before I need it?"

"Because hiding it stopped working the day Sera bled out on your cellar floor." Maren's voice roughened. "Because protection was possible when you were small and the Crown was sleeping and your mother was alive to run with you. None of those things are true anymore. Protection now would mean locking you in this cottage, and I've seen what happens to people I try to lock in cottages." She gestured at him, at all of him, at the five years of running written into his shoulders. "They leave. And they come back wounded, dragging girls with crossbow bolts through them, asking questions I should have answered when their hands were still small enough to hold."

"Maren—"

"Let me finish. I've practiced this speech for five years and I'm going to get through it." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at them. "I hid your blood because I loved you. I want that said plainly, so you never get to rewrite it into cruelty. I was wrong about the shape of it—love that makes someone small isn't love, it's fear wearing love's coat—but I wasn't wrong about the loving. Your mother trusted me with the only precious thing she had left, and I kept it safe the only way I knew how, which was badly, the way everyone keeps things safe. Badly. With walls and silence and rules. And now you're grown and the Crown is awake and the only thing I have left to give you is the truth, which is worth less than a wall and worth more than everything, depending on the day."

Ivo crossed the room. She thought for a moment he was going to embrace her, and braced for it, and was almost disappointed when he didn't—he only knelt beside her chair, the way he used to kneel beside her when he was learning to draw wards, close enough that she could see the gray starting at his temples that hadn't been there five years ago.

"The truth-charm," he said. "You said you had one. For the vault. For when the Crown speaks."

"I have it." She reached into her collar and drew out the cord she'd worn for thirty years without once using it: a small gray stone, river-smoothed, threaded with silver wire gone black with age. "My mistress made it. Her mistress before her. It does one thing: when a voice tries to rewrite what's true inside your head, the stone remembers the original. It won't stop the Crown from showing you lies. It will just—hold the door. Give you something solid to stand on while the flood comes through." She pressed it into his hand and closed his fingers over it. "It has never been tested. My mistress died without needing it. I hope it works. I hope you never find out."

"What did it cost you," Ivo said, "keeping this? Thirty years, waiting for someone to need it."

Maren laughed despite herself—a short, wet sound. "Everything. Nothing. The usual price." She covered his closed fist with both her hands. "Listen to me now, because this is the last warning I get to give. When you're in that vault, and the Crown offers you something—anything—it will offer you the thing you want most. Not the thing you say you want. The thing under that. It will dress the wound, not the wish. And you will want to say yes, and the wanting will feel like wisdom, like clarity, like finally coming home. That feeling is the trap. The moment it feels like home, run."

"And if I can't run?"

"Then you remember you were never alone in that vault." She squeezed his fist hard enough to hurt. "That's the whole lesson, Ivo. The one I failed to teach you, the one your ancestors died forgetting. Whatever the binding asks of you, the answer was never *carry it alone*. There were three of you at the second stone and there will be three of you at the throne, and if you're too proud or too frightened to take their hands, then everything your mother froze her heels for dies in that room with you."

He bowed his head over her hands. For a moment he was four years old again, humming his mother's frightened song without knowing it was a spell, and Maren's heart broke along the old seam, cleanly, almost gratefully.

From the sickroom, Bryn cried out—a thin, hurting sound—and then went quiet again.

Ivo was on his feet instantly. Maren rose more slowly, and as she passed the window she glanced out at the dark garden, at the frost coming down over the feverfew, and felt—faint, far off, like a bell rung in a drowned church—the net tighten.

Somewhere north, a knot had pulled taut. Something had noticed the ravensworn blood moving toward it.

"They know you're coming," she said quietly.

Ivo stopped in the doorway. "What?"

"The thread. I felt it just now." She turned to face him, and made her voice steady, because he needed one steady thing tonight and she had decided long ago it would be her. "Rest while she rests. Leave before dawn. And Ivo—when you reach the capital, don't go in through the gates. Go under. There are ways beneath Caerlum older than the city, and I marked them on the back of your map while you slept."

He stared at her. "You've had the map?"

"I've had five years to learn to read backwards," Maren said. "Now go sit with your girl. We've said everything honest we can afford tonight."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat by the fire with the dregs of the tea and listened to the house breathe, and after a while she heard Ivo's low voice through the sickroom door, talking to the sleeping Bryn in fragments—apologies, maybe, or plans, or just the sound of a man practicing not being alone.

Maren took her knife and went out into the garden and cut a sprig of rue, the bitter herb, the herb of regret and grace, and laid it on the windowsill where the frost could touch it.

"For what it's worth," she told the dark, to no one, to the net tightening over the world, "he turned out better than either of us deserved."

Far to the north, faint as a heartbeat, something answered.
