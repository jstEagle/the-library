## 19. The Second Waystone

Bryn had been stabbed before. Twice in the ribs by a dockhand in Saltmere who'd objected to her winning at cards, once through the shoulder by a man whose name she'd never learned and never needed to. She knew the arithmetic of wounds: how much blood was too much, which pains meant torn muscle and which meant something worse, how long a body could keep going on stubbornness alone.

The crossbow bolt had gone in below her collarbone, angled down and back, missing the lung by what Maren said—grudgingly, like a woman admitting a debt she didn't want to owe—was the width of two fingers.

Two fingers. Bryn thought about that a lot during the days she spent flat on her back in the hedge-witch's cottage, staring at a ceiling of smoke-blackened beams while fever came and went like a tide. Two fingers between her and nothing. Two fingers between Ivo Ravensworth standing over her body with her sister's death still unavenged, or Ivo kneeling beside it with his hands pressed to the wound and his face gone the color of tallow.

He'd carried her. That was the part that sat wrong in her chest, worse than the wound itself. She remembered fragments—the riverbank, the reeds, his arms under her knees and shoulders, Tam stumbling ahead with a lantern he kept shielding against the rain as if the light were something they could be arrested for. She weighed maybe nine stone soaked. He was not a big man. He'd carried her four miles anyway, and when she'd half-woken somewhere along the way she'd heard him talking to her, low and steady, nonsense really—*stay with me, Sera's sister, you don't get to do this, she gave you to me*—except it wasn't nonsense, because Sera *had*, hadn't she? Pressed a map into a stranger's hands because some instinct older than sense told her this cowardly little charm-seller was the one thing in Caerlum that might matter.

Bryn had spent five years learning to hate efficiently. Hate was a craft, like knife-work. You honed it, you kept it clean, you didn't let it rust into grief. For five years her hatred of the Crown had been a straight line: the Crown's men killed Sera, therefore break the Crown. Simple. Load-bearing.

Now there was Ivo, lying across the line like a fallen beam.

* * *

On the sixth day she got out of bed.

Maren found her in the kitchen, grey-faced, one hand braced on the table, trying to lace a boot with an arm that wouldn't lift past her breastbone. The old woman didn't scold. She just took the boot out of Bryn's hands, set it on the table, and poured tea.

"You'll tear the stitching."

"Then stitch it again."

"I will," Maren said, "and I'll charge you for it, and you'll pay, because you're not stupid." She pushed the cup across. "Sit. Drink. Then we'll discuss what your body can do versus what your anger wants it to do. They are rarely the same animal."

Bryn drank. The tea was bitter with willowbark and something else, something that made the ache behind her eyes recede like a tide going out. Through the cottage's small window she could see Ivo in the yard, splitting wood. His swings were terrible—all shoulders, no hips—and every few strokes he stopped and flexed his hand like it pained him. It probably did. She'd seen what the binding-residue did to him even this early, the way his fingers twitched when he thought no one watched, the faint frost-rimed look his breath took on warm days.

He was already carrying it. A splinter of whatever the full Crown would have been, lodged in him like shrapnel, and he'd taken it knowing, and he'd told no one but them, and then he'd stood in Maren's kitchen two nights ago and said, in a voice like a man reading his own eulogy, exactly what the unbinding would cost.

*I take it into myself. All of it. The binding, the hunger, the weight. It doesn't die. It moves.*

And Bryn had lain awake after that, doing arithmetic of a different kind.

Sera was dead. Nothing on any road, under any stone, at the end of any quest would un-kill her. Bryn had known that from the first night with a blade at Ivo's throat, known it and refused to know it, because vengeance was a shape you could hold in your hand when everything else had been taken away. But now she lay in a witch's cottage with a hole in her chest and a choice in front of her, and the choice was this:

She could spend Sera's map on Sera's revenge. Or she could spend it on what Sera had actually died for.

The courier hadn't died saying *avenge me*. Bryn had read the inquisitor reports, bribed, stolen, begged for them over five years. Sera's last words, passed cell to cell before Corvane's people silenced everyone who'd heard them, were *the king is dead—find the ravensworn*. Not *kill them*. Not *make them pay*. *Find.* Her sister had died pointing forward.

Bryn set down the cup. Her hand shook, and she let it.

"I'm leaving tomorrow," she said.

Maren looked at her over the steam, and for a moment the old woman's face did something complicated—not approval, not quite, but recognition. One runner recognizing another.

"No," Maren said. "In three days. Your body needs—" She stopped herself, exhaled through her nose. "Three days. And you'll carry these." She rose, went to the shelf above the hearth, and came back with a small clay pot sealed with wax and a bundle of dried herbs tied in red thread. "Marrowsalve. Change the dressing nightly. And this is feverfew and something of my own devising; chew it when the pain climbs past thinking. It will make you stupid and pleasant for an hour. Try to enjoy it. It's the only time in your life anyone will describe you that way."

Despite everything, Bryn almost smiled.

"And tell my apprentice," Maren added, turning back to the hearth, her voice going flatter, "that the truth-charm has three uses, not four. If he tries to stretch it, it will take the memory of whoever he's using it on instead. Make sure he counts."

* * *

They left at dawn on the ninth day, three of them and a mule Tam had won at dice from a carter who either hadn't known the animal was lame or had known very well indeed. The road bent south and east, away from the river, up into hill country where the Weald's old forest thinned into moorland and the king's roads gave way to drovers' tracks nobody had maintained since before the tithe-collectors started coming.

Bryn walked. Riding made the wound scream, so she walked, and the walking hurt in a rhythmic, manageable way that she found she preferred to rest. Rest left room for thinking. Walking gave the thinking a place to go—into the legs, into the ground, mile by mile, burned off like fuel.

Ivo hovered. That was the word for it. He kept drifting to her side of the column, slowing when she slowed, and twice he opened his mouth to say something about her pace and twice closed it again when he caught her expression. On the second morning he finally risked it anyway.

"We could wait in the next town. A week. Let you—"

"Ivo." She didn't stop walking. "If you say *let you heal* one more time, I'm going to show you where the bolt went in, from the inside."

He shut up. But he didn't drift back to the front of the column, and after a while she realized why: he wasn't hovering out of worry. He was positioning himself between her and the treeline. Between her and anything that might come out of it.

She decided not to mention that she'd noticed. Some things you let a man have.

Tam, meanwhile, talked. Tam always talked—it was how he breathed, Bryn suspected—but on the moorland he talked about the waystones, and for once she listened, because the knowledge was ammunition and she'd learned from Sera that ammunition was the only inheritance worth keeping.

"The network predates the kingdom," Tam said, walking backward in front of the mule so he could lecture properly, which was a habit that would get him killed someday and Bryn privately hoped she'd be there to see it. "Older than Caerlum, older than the ravensworn, possibly older than writing. Ley-lines aren't metaphors—they're seams, places where the world's own magic pools close to the surface. The first binders didn't make the lines. They made *valves*. Waystones to open and close them, to draw power up or push it back down. Thirteen major stones, by my count, though the archive only ever confirmed nine." He held up fingers. "One near Harrow Fen—that's the one Ivo woke. One here, in the hills ahead. The third is the problem. The third is under the palace."

"And opening them does what, exactly?" Bryn asked. "For the rite?"

"Opens the veins. Lets the binding in Ivo be drawn out and dispersed instead of pooling in one body." Tam's cheerfulness flickered, just for a step or two. "In theory. The theory is several hundred years old and its principal authors are dead."

"They're not all dead," Bryn said. "Their memories live in him now. Every stone he touches, he gets more of them." She glanced at Ivo. "Don't you."

He was quiet long enough that the mule's breathing seemed loud.

"Yes," he said finally. "It's not like remembering. It's like... being remembered. Someone else's afternoon happens inside my head and for a moment I can't find the edges of myself." He rubbed his right hand, the one that twitched. "My grandmother. I've seen her now, three times. Maren says the stones choose which memories to give, and the stones want me to understand what we built. What my family built."

"Then let's hope your family built something worth understanding," Bryn said, and immediately wished she'd bitten it back, because his face did the thing it did—flinched inward, apologized for existing—and she was tired of making him do that. "That wasn't fair," she added grudgingly. "You didn't build it."

"No," Ivo said. "But I'm the one holding the bill."

* * *

They found the second waystone on the evening of the third day, in a hollow between two hills where the moor grass grew wrong—too green, too thick, in a perfect circle forty feet across, like something had been feeding there for centuries.

The stone itself barely broke the turf. A shoulder of dark rock, waist-high, carved over completely: spirals within spirals, and around the rim a band of script that none of them could read until Ivo knelt and laid his palm flat against it and the letters lit up under his skin like embers under ash.

Bryn felt it before she saw anything happen. A hum, deep, below hearing—the kind of pressure you feel in your back teeth before a storm. The hair on her forearms lifted. The mule screamed once, a raw sound, and bolted twenty yards before Tam caught its halter and hung on, swearing in three dead languages.

"Ivo," she said carefully. "Whatever you're about to do. Do it fast."

"I don't think I'm doing anything," he said. His voice had gone strange, layered, like two speakers slightly out of phase. "It's—opening. It wants to—"

The world folded.

Later, Bryn would try to explain to herself what she witnessed, and the best she ever managed was this: it was like watching a man drown in air. Ivo stayed kneeling, both hands on the stone now, and his eyes rolled white, and his mouth moved—not screaming, worse, *conversing*—and around him the circle of grass shimmered with heat-haze that had no heat, and shadows pooled and shifted, and Bryn, who had sworn off magic the day the inquisitors burned the ferryman of Lowside alive in front of his own children, found herself stepping into the circle anyway, because he looked alone in there, alone in a way that made her chest hurt worse than the bolt had.

She couldn't touch him. When she reached for his shoulder her hand met resistance, soft and total, like pushing into deep water. So she did the only other thing she could think of. She knelt facing him, close enough that her knees touched his, and she talked.

"Ivo. Ravensworth. Charm-seller. Terrible liar, worse woodcutter." His mouth kept moving. "You carried me four miles through the rain and you talk to unconscious women like it's normal. You count your coins twice and give the extra to beggars when you think no one sees. Sera picked you. Out of everyone in that whole rotten city, she picked you, and my sister was never wrong about people. Never once." The haze thickened. His breathing went ragged. "So whatever they're showing you in there—you come back and tell us. Because we're not leaving you in it. Do you hear me? Nobody carries this alone. Not even you. *Especially* not you."

His eyes snapped open. They were his own eyes, brown and startled, and he pitched forward into her, and she took his weight against her bad shoulder and didn't cry out, mostly.

* * *

He told them by the fire, that night, wrapped in a blanket with his hands around a tin cup he never drank from.

"There were more of them than I thought," he began. "The binders. I'd been seeing my grandmother, and before her her mother, and I thought—I don't know. That it was a family thing. A small story." He laughed, without humor. "There were hundreds. An order. They called themselves the Kithren—*those who knot*. And the stone showed me the year they finished the Crown. Showed me the argument afterward."

Tam leaned forward so far he nearly fell into the fire. "The schism. The archive had fragments—a purge, a falling-out, records expunged—"

"It wasn't a purge. It was a vote." Ivo stared into the flames. "The tyrant was bound. The war was over. And half the Kithren stood in the vault where they'd forged it and said: destroy it now. Melt it down. Unmake the binding while our hands still remember how. They said a tool that eats kings would eat whoever held it next. They said power like that doesn't lie dormant; it *waits*."

"And the other half?" Bryn asked, though she already knew. She'd known since the first waystone, since the shape of the grief in his grandmother's memory.

"My line." Ivo's voice went flat. "My grandmother's mother cast the deciding voice. She argued the kingdom would need it again someday—that a cage built for tyrants shouldn't be broken while the world still bred them. Control, not destruction. Keep it, guard it, pass it down." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "They voted. Forty-one to thirty-nine. Two votes. The whole catastrophe of the last three hundred years turned on *two votes*, and my ancestor cast one of them, and then they wrote the dissenters out of the record and called their side traitors, and the Crown sat in its vault getting hungry."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere on the dark moor the mule stamped.

"So it wasn't malice," Tam said softly, in the tone of a man rearranging furniture in a room that had just burned down. "It was prudence. Reasonable, defensible, catastrophic prudence."

"It was fear," Ivo said. "They were afraid to let go of the one weapon that had saved them. And the ones who wanted to destroy it—the stone showed me their faces, Tam, they weren't fanatics, they were *tired*, they were the ones who'd done the actual forging and knew what it cost—they were outvoted by people who wanted to feel safe." He set down the untouched cup. "I used to think my family's sin was creating the Crown. It's worse than that. Our sin is that when we had the chance to end it, we chose to keep it. We chose the leash over the knife because the leash felt safer in our hand."

Bryn watched him across the fire—the runaway apprentice who'd spent five years selling luck-knots to frightened fishwives, carrying a three-hundred-year-old vote in his head—and felt something shift in her chest, some load-bearing wall coming down.

Because she recognized it. Gods help her, she recognized it exactly.

Five years ago, when the news of Sera's arrest reached her, she'd had a choice too. She'd had friends in the river-guilds, contacts in the smuggler warrens, a dozen paths that led toward *resistance*—slow, collective, the long work of many hands. Instead she'd chosen the leash. Her own private leash: the hunt, the blade, the single straight line from her grief to someone's throat. Vengeance was control. Vengeance was the feeling of safety you got from believing one death could balance another. And it had felt prudent. It had felt like the responsible choice, the clear-eyed one, while everyone around her preached patience and numbers and she'd sneered at them for cowards.

Her sister had died pointing forward, and Bryn had spent five years pointing a knife.

"Ivo." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. Everyone looked at her. She pushed through it. "When this is done—when the Crown is unbound and your part in it is finished—I'm not going to kill Corvane."

Tam blinked. Ivo just looked at her, waiting.

"He deserves it. I've wanted it since the ferry docks. But I've been thinking, lying on that witch's floor with a hole in me, about what Sera would want, and what she'd want isn't a corpse. She'd want the thing that made him *possible* gone. The inquisition, the tithe-carts, the whole machine that turns frightened people into informants and couriers into corpses." She fed a stick into the fire, deliberately, watching it catch. "Corvane is a symptom. I've been hunting symptoms my whole life. I'm done. I want the disease."

"That's not vengeance," Tam said quietly.

"No," Bryn agreed. "It's better bookkeeping."

Ivo made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. When she glanced at him, he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't name—something between gratitude and grief, as if she'd handed him something heavy and precious and he didn't know where to put it down.

"What?" she snapped.

"Nothing. It's just—" He hesitated, then seemed to decide honesty was cheaper than evasion. "My grandmother said something like that. In the memory. At the vote. Before she cast it." He looked into the fire. "*A leash only works while someone holds it,*" he quoted. "*And holders die. Break the cage while breaking it is still ours to choose.*"

"She was one of the thirty-nine, then," Bryn said.

"No." Ivo's jaw tightened. "She was one of the forty-one. She cast the deciding vote for keeping it. And she spent the rest of her life regretting it, and the stone made sure I felt every year of it." He looked up, and the firelight made his face flicker, young and old at once. "The Kithren split over this exact question—revenge or remedy, leash or knife—and my blood chose wrong, and I've inherited the choosing. That's what the waystones keep showing me. Not glory. The bill."

Bryn nodded slowly. Then she reached over, took the untouched cup out of his hands, drank the cold tea herself, and handed it back.

"Then we pay it," she said. "Together. All three of us. And the mule."

"The mule is lame," Tam protested.

"The mule is a symbol," Bryn said, and this time Ivo did laugh, short and startled, and Tam looked between them like a man watching a door he'd long since given up on slowly swing open.

* * *

She took the middle watch, as always. The moor was silver-grey under a thin moon, wind combing the grass in long slow strokes, and Bryn walked the perimeter of their small camp with her sword loose in its sheath and her wound aching in time with her heartbeat, and she let herself do the thing she never let herself do: she talked to Sera.

Not aloud. Just in the old way, the way sisters talk across distance, the way you talk to the dead when you've finally stopped demanding answers from them.

*I found him,* she thought. *Your ravensworn. He's not what I expected. He's a coward who keeps choosing courage anyway, which I'm told is the better kind. He carries things. He carried me. I think you knew that about him before he did.*

The wind moved the grass. No answer, because there was never an answer, and for the first time in five years that was bearable.

*We're going to finish it,* she told her sister. *Not for you. For what you pointed at.*

Behind her, in the camp, Ivo murmured in his sleep—fragments, ancestral syllables, the dead speaking through the living—and Tam slept sitting up against the mule's flank with his catalogue clutched to his chest like a child's doll, and the second waystone hummed its low note in the hollow behind them, patient as a held breath.

Bryn paused at the edge of the firelight and looked east, toward the capital, toward the palace and the thing wearing the king, and her hand rested on her sword not the way a killer's does but the way a warden's does.

That was when she saw it: far off on the southern horizon, a point of light moving against the stars. Too steady for a lantern. Too fast for a rider.

Torches. Many torches, traveling in formation, on a road that led where they were going.

Someone was ahead of them. Or behind them, closing.

Bryn went back to camp and put her boot against Ivo's ribs, gently, the way you wake a man you've decided not to kill.
