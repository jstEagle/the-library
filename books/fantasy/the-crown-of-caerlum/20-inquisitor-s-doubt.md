## 20. Inquisitor's Doubt

The informant's name was Oskit Vell, and he wept before Corvane had finished locking the door.

They were in a hired room above a dyer's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, the kind of place Corvane used when he needed privacy without officialdom. No cells. No clerks. No record. The room smelled of lye and old urine, and the single window let in a gray light that made everything look like it had already been washed and wrung out.

Oskit sat on the stool with his hands between his knees, a small man with a leather-seller's soft hands and a face built for apology. He had sold them out—the ravensworn boy, the woman with the knife, the archivist—for thirty crowns and a promise of consideration. Now the promise was coming to collect him.

"Master Inquisitor," he said, "I never saw their faces clearly. It was dark, and I'd been drinking—"

"You saw enough to describe the woman's scar." Corvane did not sit. He stood by the window with his hands folded behind his back, watching the street below, where a dyer's apprentice dragged bolts of cloth through the mud. "You saw enough to name the tavern where they drank. You saw enough to earn your thirty crowns. Don't insult us both by shrinking now."

"I'm sorry." The words came out wet and immediate. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

"Sorry is not what I want from you." Corvane turned. "What I want is everything you remember about the one called Ivo. Not what you told my subordinates—they got the summary. I want the texture of it. How he moved. What his hands did when he talked. What he looked like when you told him you knew what he was."

Oskit blinked up at him. "He didn't... I mean, I never told him anything. I just watched. That was the arrangement. Watch and report."

"And?"

"And nothing. He sold charms. Little ones. Luck-knots, fever-charms, that sort of thing. Harmless stuff, or near enough. He was careful—always careful—but he wasn't *strange*, if you follow me. He laughed at jokes. He haggled. Once he gave a girl a charm for free because her mother was sick and she couldn't pay." Oskit's voice dropped. "I reported that too. The generosity. They said to keep watching."

Corvane said nothing.

"He seemed like a man trying very hard to be small," Oskit went on, as if filling silence were the only way he knew to survive a room. "You know the type. Head down. Never first through a door. But when he thought no one was looking—" He stopped himself, then, with the fatal honesty of frightened men, kept going. "When he thought no one was looking, he looked *tired*. Like a man carrying something heavy up a long stair."

"Did you ever see him use magic? Real magic?"

"No. Only the trinkets. But there was a night—a market night, last autumn—when a cart nearly ran down a child, and I swear on my mother's grave the cart *stopped*. Just stopped, like it had hit glass. And Ivo was standing right there with his hand half-raised, and then he put it down quick, like a man catching himself reaching for bread he hadn't paid for." Oskit wiped his nose. "I reported it. Nothing came of it. I don't know why nothing came of it."

*Because I buried it,* Corvane thought. *I read that report and filed it under insufficient evidence, because a man who stops carts to save children does not fit the shape of the thing we hunt.*

He had been telling himself for five years that the work was simple. Magic was a wound in the world; the Crown had closed that wound; the inquisition kept it closed. Heretics were not people, exactly—they were leaks, and you patched leaks. It was clean. It had to be clean, because if it was not clean then every door he had kicked in, every family he had broken apart, every man he had watched weep in a room smelling of lye—all of it was something other than holy duty.

"The woman," he said aloud. "Sera's sister. Bryn. What do you know of her?"

"Only what everyone knows. She came asking after her sister's last route. Fierce as a banked fire, that one. When she found the charm-seller, she didn't kill him, though I heard she had the blade at his throat." Oskit hesitated. "There's talk in the low taverns. Talk that the courier who died—the sister—wasn't killed by the charm-seller at all. Talk that she was running from something bigger. Something about the king."

The room went very quiet.

"Say that again," Corvane said, "carefully."

"Just tavern talk." Oskit's hands were shaking now, visibly. "Drunk men say the king is dead, drunk men say the moon is cheese. But this one kept coming back, and the men saying it weren't drunk. Dockworkers. Men who'd seen the royal barge pass close. They said the king doesn't blink right. Doesn't breathe right. One of them swore he saw the king stand motionless for two hours at a window, and when he finally moved, it was all at once, like a puppet whose strings got pulled." Oskit swallowed. "That's all. Tavern talk. I only mention it because—because you asked for texture."

Corvane crossed the room in three strides and took Oskit by the collar, lifting him half off the stool. It was not rage, or not only rage. It was the reflex of a man who has heard a floorboard creak over an abyss.

"You will never repeat those words again," he said quietly. "Not to me, not to your wife, not into your cup. The king is the Crown's living vessel. To speak otherwise is the gravest heresy there is. Do you understand the gravity of what you've said?"

"Yes," Oskit whispered. "Yes, master. Forgive me."

Corvane released him. His own heart was hammering, which shamed him. He was Corvane of the Ninth Chair; he had stared down hedge-witches whose eyes leaked smoke. A dockworker's drunken story should have been nothing.

But he remembered, now, the audience he had attended two years ago. A formality—fifty inquisitors kneeling while the king blessed the order's new charter. Corvane had knelt among them, head bowed, and the king had walked the line of them, and when the king paused before Corvane, Corvane had looked up into the royal face, as protocol permitted, for exactly three seconds.

He had felt *watched*. Not by the king. By something behind the king, the way a lit window makes you feel watched from a dark street. He had filed the feeling away as awe. That was what you did with such feelings. You named them awe, and you knelt lower.

"Where are they now?" he asked Oskit. "The trio. Best guess."

"They took a boat downriver after the bridge. Toward Vessany, maybe. Or past it." Oskit wrung his hands. "Master—inquisitor—if they're heading where I think they're heading, you should know the daughter—"

"What daughter?"

"My daughter." Oskit's face crumpled. "Liss. She's nine. Since the tithe-collectors came through our parish last spring, she's been... losing things. Small things first. Then bigger. Last month she forgot my face for a whole day. The physicians say it's a wasting of the mind. But the mothers in the parish, they say the collectors marked our street, and since then it's like the street itself is being *eaten*." He looked up, and there was something worse than fear in his face—there was hope, aimed at Corvane like a weapon. "You serve the Crown. The Crown protects. Can the Crown give me back my daughter?"

And Corvane, who had answers for heretics and kings and burning questions of doctrine, found he had nothing at all to say to a father in a room that smelled of lye.

"I'll look into it," he said, and hated how the words sounded. Like a charm-seller's promise. Like a luck-knot.

He left Oskit weeping softly on the stool and descended to the street, where the gray light had thickened toward evening.

* * *

He could have sent a subordinate to the palace. That was the proper channel: report the intelligence, request an audience through the Office of Royal Observance, wait six weeks for a clerk to deny it.

Corvane went himself, that same night, because the creaking floorboard would not stop creaking.

The Palace of Caerlum rose above the city like a held breath, its spires lit with cold white lamps. He had entered it perhaps forty times in his career, always through the Inquisitors' Door, always with papers, always feeling the building's weight settle on him like a vestment. Tonight the guards waved him through without reading the papers. Everyone waved Corvane through. That was the trouble with being believed: no one ever looked at you twice.

He was escorted through corridors he knew, past tapestries depicting the Binding of the Tyrant—the great founding myth, the wizard-king shackled by his own crown—and delivered to the antechamber of the Royal Presence at an hour when no sane petitioner would be received.

But the king received petitioners at all hours now. Everyone knew that. The king slept little. The king worked ceaselessly. The king's devotion to the realm was legendary.

A chamberlain with a waxy complexion announced him, and the doors opened, and Corvane knelt on the marble as he had knelt fifty times before.

"Rise, Corvane of the Ninth Chair."

The voice was the king's voice. Warm, measured, a little tired—the voice of a man in his fifties who had carried a kingdom for twenty years. Corvane rose and performed the triple bow and lifted his eyes as protocol allowed.

King Aldous IV sat by a window overlooking the dark city. A candle burned beside him, though the moonlight was plenty. Papers lay on the table before him, and his hand rested upon them, and—

And he was not moving.

Corvane had prepared questions. He had prepared a dozen graceful approaches to the subject of the fugitives, the ravensworn bloodline, the waystones. All of it evaporated, because the king was not moving, and the stillness was wrong in a way that scrambled something deep in Corvane's brain. Living things were never still like that. Statues were still like that. Corpses were still like that. The king sat with his hand on his papers and his eyes open and fixed on the middle distance, and he did not blink, and his chest did not appear to rise.

Then, all at once, he moved.

It was exactly as the dockworker had described: not a resumption but a *resumption*, motion arriving whole, like a puppet whose handler had returned from fetching wine. The king turned his head, focused on Corvane, and smiled.

"Inquisitor." The smile reached the mouth and stopped there, like water reaching a dam. "You come at a strange hour. Good. Strange hours are when truth walks."

"Your Majesty." Corvane bowed again, buying time, steadying his voice. "I come regarding the fugitives. The ravensworn heir and his companions. They were sighted—"

"I know where they are." The king said it mildly, the way a man says *I know what weather we're having*. "They opened the second stone. I felt it open. Like a tooth coming loose."

Corvane's spine went cold. The second waystone had been opened four days ago, in the Weald, hundreds of miles away. No courier could have brought word. No spy could have—

Unless the Crown itself had ways of knowing. Which doctrine said it did. Which doctrine said the king and kingdom were one flesh, the Crown the nerve that bound them.

"Your Majesty feels the waystones," Corvane said carefully. "This is consistent with the teachings. The Crown is the kingdom's—"

"Do not recite to me." Still mild. Still smiling. "You came here tonight because a leather-seller wept in a rented room and told you the king doesn't blink right. You wanted to see for yourself. So look."

The words landed like ice water. Corvane had told no one about Oskit. Had written nothing down. The interview existed only in his memory and in a locked room above a dyer's shop.

"How—" he began.

"*Look,*" the king said, and the word came out layered, doubled, as though two throats spoke a half-breath apart.

And Corvane looked. Really looked, for the first time in twenty years of devotion.

The king's eyes were brown. Everyone knew that; portraits hung in every courthouse. But in the candlelight, in the moment Corvane truly looked, the brown thinned like tea over-steeped, and beneath it there was a color that was not a color at all—a depth without bottom, a darkness that was not absence but *appetite*. And in that darkness something turned its attention onto him.

There was no other word for it. Attention. A vast, patient, curious attention, the attention of a thing that had been watching for a very long time and had just now decided to be noticed. It flicked through the royal eyes the way a lamp flame flickers behind horn panes, and Corvane felt it move across his face, cataloguing him, weighing him, finding him—

Finding him *doubting.*

The pressure of it was physical. Corvane had faced witches and warlocks and once, in the marsh country, a thing that wore a drowned man's skin. None of it had felt like this. This did not threaten him. This *considered* him, the way a merchant considers a coin, checking for clipping, checking for counterfeit.

"You doubt," the thing behind the king's eyes said, using the king's mouth with terrible gentleness. "That is not treason, Corvane. Doubt is the mind's immune response. I have watched yours grow for two years, since the day you knelt and felt my gaze and called it awe." The king tilted his head, and the gesture was almost fond. "Do you know what I am doing, while you sleep? While your order burns hedge-witches and hunts runaway boys? I am holding this kingdom together. Every memory I take, I keep. Every wonder I swallow, I preserve. The village that forgot itself—it is not destroyed. It is *archived*. Safe. Perfect. Nothing lost, nothing decayed, nothing grieved. I am not the kingdom's destroyer, inquisitor. I am its most devoted servant."

"Your Majesty speaks of consuming memories as preservation," Corvane managed. His knees ached; he realized he had sunk partway down without meaning to. "The Church teaches the Crown is the king's wisdom made eternal—"

"The Church teaches what I permit it to teach." A pause, and then, softer, almost sorrowful: "You may go, Corvane. Pursue your fugitives. You will find that you cannot catch them until I decide you can. And when you stand before them at last, ask yourself what they offer that I do not. They offer you a kingdom that forgets and grieves and heals. I offer you a kingdom that is kept." The smile again, reaching the mouth and stopping. "Weigh them honestly. You are honest. It is your best quality and it will be your ruin."

The attention withdrew. The brown flooded back into the eyes. King Aldous IV blinked—twice, three times, rapidly, like a man surfacing—and looked down at his papers with a small frown, as though unsure how long he had been sitting there.

"Inquisitor?" the king said. His voice was only a voice now, human, faintly confused. "Forgive me. I... drifted. What was it you came to say?"

And that—that was the worst of it. Worse than the doubled voice, worse than the appetite behind the eyes. Because for one clear moment Corvane had seen the man underneath, surfacing, drowning, *asking*, and the man did not know what had been speaking through his mouth. The man did not know what he had promised, or threatened, or confessed.

"Nothing of urgency, Your Majesty," Corvane heard himself say. "Routine matters. The fugitives progress. We will have them soon."

"Good. Good." The king rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, a tired, ordinary, human gesture. "It's late. These reports blur. Sometimes I think I've read the same page for hours." He smiled at Corvane, and this time the smile reached all the way, and it was so painfully, ordinarily kind that Corvane had to look away. "Keep the realm safe, Corvane. It's all any of us can do."

Corvane bowed and walked backward to the doors, as protocol demanded, and did not turn his back on the king until the doors closed between them.

* * *

He walked the whole way back across the city. He did not remember deciding to walk. The streets were emptying, lamplighters working their rounds, and Corvane moved through them like a man whose house had burned down while he was out—everything he owned technically intact, nothing of it livable.

Twenty years. Twenty years of the clean, simple work. Magic was a wound; the Crown had closed it; heretics were leaks. He had said the words at a hundred burnings. He had held the hands of condemned witches who cursed him, and the hands of some who thanked him, and he had gone home each night certain that the ledger balanced.

*A kingdom that is kept.*

The thing had not denied eating the village. It had boasted of it. It had called grief a defect and forgetting a mercy, and it had spoken of the king—of the *man*—the way a farmer speaks of a field lying fallow. And the man himself surfaced for one moment, blinking, lost, asking what he'd been about to say, and Corvane understood with total clarity that King Aldous IV had been dead for years, and that Corvane had knelt before his corpse a hundred times and called it holiness.

He stopped on the Bridge of Nine Arches, midway across, and gripped the cold stone rail.

Every doctrine held. That was the horror. Nothing the thing had said contradicted the Church's teaching—it had simply shown him what the teachings meant when you followed them all the way down. The Crown preserved. The Crown unified. The Crown consumed, yes, but consumption in service of unity was sanctified; the catechism said so in eleven places. He had read those passages aloud. He had taught them to novices.

The enemy was not a liar. That was what broke him. If the Crown had lied, he could have fought it the way he fought everything, with evidence and fire. But it had told him the truth with a monster's sincerity, and the truth was that it believed itself good, and it had looked at Corvane's doubt and called it an immune response, and it had been *right*.

Below him the river moved, black and indifferent, carrying the reflections of lamps toward the sea. Somewhere downriver—Vessany, the Weald, the road—three fugitives were moving toward the third stone, toward the throne, toward whatever the ravensworn bloodline had spent five generations pretending wasn't theirs to fix.

*Ask yourself what they offer that I do not,* the thing had said.

He knew what they offered. They offered a kingdom that could hurt. That could lose things and mourn them. That could change. He had watched it in fragments for years without naming it: the fishwife who bought a luck-knot because hope was illegal; the archivist exiled for asking; the courier who died rather than surrender a map. All of them criminals by the letter of the law, and all of them, he saw now, fighting for the right to *forget badly and remember painfully and remain human*.

His faith was not cracking. That was too gentle a phrase. Cracking implied the structure might be saved. What Corvane felt, gripping the bridge rail in the dark, was the specific vertigo of a man discovering the foundation stone was hollow—and had been hollow all along, and that he had built his life on the sound it made when he knocked on it.

He thought of Oskit's daughter, Liss, nine years old, forgetting her father's face. *Can the Crown give me back my daughter?*

No. The Crown had taken her. The Crown was taking her still, street by street, parish by parish, and calling it love.

Corvane pushed off from the rail and walked on, and his steps, without his quite deciding it, turned not toward the Inquisitors' Hall but toward the river docks—toward the downstream gates, the smugglers' stairs, the roads that led to Vessany.

He did not yet know what he would do when he caught them. Arrest them, perhaps. Question them. Or kneel, as he had knelt a hundred times before, and ask the ravensworn heir to teach him the words for what he had seen behind the king's eyes.

Behind him, the palace spires burned cold and white against the sky, and in the highest window a light still burned, where a dead man sat unmoving over papers he could not read, wearing a crown that loved him the way a trap loves the foot it holds.
