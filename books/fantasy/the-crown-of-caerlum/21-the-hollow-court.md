## 21. The Hollow Court

The capital had a smell, and the smell was wrong.

Ivo noticed it first from the smuggler's cart as they rolled through the outer gate under a tarp of dyed wool. Caerlum had smelled of fish and tar and fear. Vessany of incense and wet stone. But the capital—Aldermere, seat of the Crown for nine hundred years—smelled of nothing at all. Clean air, scrubbed streets, braziers burning sweet resin on every corner to mask whatever lay beneath. A city that had been perfumed like a corpse.

"Everyone's smiling," Bryn muttered beside him, her voice low under the tarp. "Why is everyone smiling?"

Ivo peered through a gap in the wool. The street beyond was busy, prosperous, orderly. Bakers handed bread across counters with wide, pleasant faces. Children played a game with a knotted rope, laughing at intervals so regular it might have been a clock striking. A woman waved to a neighbor, held the wave exactly three seconds, then turned away mid-gesture as if someone had cut her string.

"I've seen this before," he said quietly. "In the village that forgot itself. But there it was absence. This is—" He searched for the word.

"Performance," Tam said from the front of the cart, where he crouched beside the driver, a gray-faced smuggler named Osk who took their coin and asked nothing. "The archive records called it *the smooth season*. After the tithe collectors pass through a region, everything runs smoothly for a while. Too smoothly. Fewer quarrels. Fewer questions. Fewer children born." He didn't look back at them. "Then the forgetting sets in."

The cart lurched through a checkpoint. Ivo held his breath. A guard in Crown livery—black and silver, the raven-and-crown sigil polished bright—leaned in, glanced at the wool, and waved them through with a smile that reached his eyes and stopped there, like light hitting glass.

They were let out in the lower city, in a yard behind a dye-house that Osk's people owned. The plan was simple, because simple plans were the only ones that survived contact with Corvane: they would spend two days in the lower city, moving among the crowds, learning the rhythms of the palace district. Tam needed to reach the royal archive eventually—but not yet, not until they understood what moved through these streets. And Ivo needed to see the king.

He'd told himself it was strategy. He'd told himself a lot of things since Sera died in his cellar with a map going red in his hands.

* * *

Bryn found the lodging: a room above a cookshop, one bed and two pallets, paid for with coin earned honestly by mending nets on the docks while Ivo sold charms—harmless ones, luck-knots and traveler's blessings, the old trade, though here he did it with his hands only, no magic, because the city hummed with wards like a struck bell. He could feel them when he closed his eyes. Threads of binding magic woven through every gate and courthouse, all of it flowing one direction. Inward. Toward the palace hill.

"It's eating," he said the first night, lying awake on his pallet. "You can feel it. The whole city is a funnel."

Tam sat up in the dark. "Now you understand why I stole the pages." His voice had the flat carefulness of a man handling broken glass. "Nine years I catalogued requisitions. Grain tithes doubled. Ink requisitions tripled—the chancery copying the same documents over and over because the originals kept... degrading. Not fading. *Degrading.* Words changing. Dates shifting. I pulled the same census three times in one year and the population dropped by eleven thousand between copies, and nobody noticed, because the clerks who would have noticed had forgotten they'd ever counted." He laughed, short and terrible. "I brought my findings to my superior. He thanked me warmly. Two days later I couldn't find my own report. Neither could he. Neither could anyone. It had never existed. I checked my own notes—I'd written *I am afraid* in the margin, and by then I couldn't remember being afraid, or why, or what the words referred to. So I stole the pages that mattered and hid them somewhere even I couldn't easily reach, and I got drunk, and I stayed drunk for six years."

Bryn, sharpening her knife by feel in the dark, said nothing. She'd been quieter since the second waystone. Since she'd watched Ivo's grandmother choose control over destruction, and had recognized, maybe, something of her own arithmetic—the ledger where vengeance lived and never balanced.

"The court," she said now. "Tomorrow. You said we could see the audience from the square."

"You can see everything from the square," Tam said. "That's the design of it. The Hollow Court wants witnesses. A king who is seen ruling needs do nothing else."

* * *

The Palace of Aldermere rose over the lower city like a white cliff, terraced and colonnaded, its highest tower crowned with gold that caught the morning sun and threw it back in a hard flat glare. Below it, the great square spread wide enough for ten thousand people, ringed by arcades, and the crowd that morning was festive—banners, roasted chestnuts, a fiddler playing something jaunty that everyone hummed along to without appearing to enjoy it.

They stood at the edge of the arcade, three faces among hundreds. Bryn wore a dyer's apron, her hair bound and stained at the temples with indigo. Tam had shaved his beard down to stubble and looked, Ivo thought, like every other failed scholar in every city in the world. Ivo himself wore a porter's cap pulled low and carried a delivery crate he had no intention of delivering.

The bells began at midmorning.

Not the city bells—the palace ones, high and silver-toned, and at their sound the crowd turned as one body toward the terrace, and the fiddler stopped mid-phrase, and the chestnut seller set down his pan, and ten thousand faces lifted with an expression Ivo could only call *readiness*. Not joy. Not reverence. Readiness, like dogs hearing a whistle.

"They come every day," murmured a woman next to him, to no one, to everyone. "Every day the king shows himself. Every day we are blessed."

"Do you remember when it started?" Ivo asked carefully.

She smiled at him with perfect warmth. "It has always been thus," she said, and her eyes, for just a moment, went empty as wells, and then filled again, and she went on smiling.

On the terrace, doors opened.

The king came out slowly, and Ivo's stomach dropped.

He had expected a puppet in the crude sense—something jerking, something obviously wrong. What he saw instead was worse. King Aldous V walked with perfect grace, dressed in white and silver, and he waved, and the wave was right, and the smile was right, and every individual motion was flawless the way a painting of a man is flawless. Nothing moved that should not move. Nothing moved *except* what should. Between gestures there were pauses—a half-second too long, a stillness like a held breath—and in those pauses the king's face went utterly blank, a mask waiting for the next instruction, and then the smile bloomed again like a wound reopening.

"He's dead," Bryn breathed. "Ivo. Look at his eyes. There's no one home."

"There's something home," Ivo said. His hands had gone cold inside his gloves. The map, hidden against his ribs, felt warm. "Something's looking out through him. Watch—it's watching *us*, or trying to. It looks at crowds the way you'd look at a meal."

The audience proceeded. Petitioners climbed the marble stair one by one, kneeling, presenting scrolls. A merchant asking relief from tariffs. A widow petitioning for her son's pardon. A guildmaster with a complaint about river rights. And the king received each one with the same gentle smile, spoke the same measured words—Ivo was close enough, barely, to catch fragments—and granted each petition with a gesture of the hand that was always, always slightly wrong. Too slow coming up. Too fast coming down. Like a marionette whose puppeteer was very skilled but very far away, working through deep water.

The widow with the pardon petition knelt a long time. When the king raised his hand in mercy, she wept with gratitude, kissed the marble, and descended—and halfway down the steps she stopped, frowned at her own hands, and asked the guard beside her, quite loudly, why she was crying. The guard smiled and said, "Because you are blessed," and she nodded and smiled too, and finished descending, and Ivo watched her walk away into the crowd already forgetting, the pardon itself dissolving behind her eyes like sugar in rain.

"She won't remember she has a son in prison by tomorrow," Tam said hollowly. "But the prison record will show the pardon. The paperwork will be perfect. That's how it works. The kingdom keeps its records; the people lose their reasons."

A servant crossed the terrace behind the throne bearing a tray of wine, and midway across he simply stopped. Stood. The tray trembled in his hands. His lips moved—counting, maybe, or praying—and then his face cleared and he continued walking as if the gap had never happened, except that he was now walking toward the wall instead of the dais, and a chamberlain gently, courteously, turned him by the shoulder and aimed him the other way, the way you'd redirect a cart with a broken wheel.

Nobody laughed. Nobody stared. Ten thousand people watched the king hold audience, and none of them saw anything strange, because seeing strangeness had been eaten out of them along with everything else.

Except—

Ivo felt it before he understood it: a pressure at the back of his skull, a slow turning, like a lighthouse beam sweeping toward him. On the terrace, the king's head began to rotate. Not toward the petitioner. Not toward the chancellor standing at his elbow. Toward the crowd. Toward the arcade. Toward *him*.

The blank face swept past a hundred rows of subjects and stopped.

Across the entire breadth of the square, across ten thousand heads, the dead eyes of King Aldous V found Ivo Ravensworth, porter, holder of an undelivered crate, last heir of the ravensworn line—and the smile widened. Just slightly. Just enough. An expression of recognition, of appetite, of terrible patience, worn on a face that belonged to a man twenty years dead.

*I see you,* the smile said. *I have always known you would come.*

Ivo's knees nearly buckled. Beside him Bryn's hand clamped his arm hard enough to bruise. "Don't run," she whispered. "Whatever happens, don't run. Walk."

He walked. One step. Another. The gaze held him the whole way, a hook behind his sternum, dragging. At the edge of the arcade he made himself turn, made himself bow—a porter's bob, nothing—and when he straightened, the king had turned back to his petitioners, granting mercies, wearing his smile, and the moment was sealed away as if it had never happened.

But Ivo's hands shook for the rest of the day.

* * *

They didn't speak until the cookshop, until the door was barred and the candle lit and Bryn had checked the window twice.

"It knows I'm here," Ivo said. "It knew before I did, maybe. The waystones, the map—maybe it's been calling me the whole time. Maybe Sera—" He stopped. Started again. "Maybe Sera wasn't sent by the resistance. Maybe the Crown *let* her run. Bait on a hook."

"Does it matter?" Bryn said. Her voice was rough, but steady. "If it wants you to come, fine. We're coming. On our terms, through the third waystone, with the rite Tam remembers. Let it watch. Let it open the door for us." She leaned forward, firelight catching the scar on her jaw. "It made one mistake, Ivo. It thinks you're alone. Everything it showed us today—every hollow face in that square—is what alone looks like. One thing wearing ten thousand people, each of them carrying nothing for anybody else. That's its whole trick. That's all it is."

Tam looked up from his notes, ink-stained fingers trembling slightly. "There's something else," he said. "Something I saw today that I need to write down before—" He pressed his palm flat against the page, steadying himself. "The chancellor. The man at the king's elbow in the silver chain. I catalogued his predecessor nine years ago. Same face. Same chain. Same *hands*. Either the chancellery has remarkable succession practices, or the Crown doesn't bother replacing its puppets' handlers anymore, because it's stopped needing them to be alive either."

Silence in the little room. Outside, the sweet-resin braziers hissed, perfuming the dark.

"So we go down," Bryn said finally. "Undercroft, vault, throne. Three waystones, one ravensworn, one willing sacrifice—that was the rite. Except we changed the ending, didn't we? Together, not alone." She looked at Ivo, and then, unexpectedly, at Tam, and something passed between the three of them that had no name yet. "We go down together."

Ivo nodded. But sleep wouldn't come, and near midnight he rose and stood at the shuttered window, listening to the palace bells toll a hour that no longer existed, and below in the street a night-watchman passed with his lantern, singing the hours softly to himself—and Ivo realized, with a cold that started in his spine and spread outward, that the watchman was singing yesterday's hours. All of them. Over and over. The same night, forever, patrolled by a man who no longer remembered there had ever been any other.

And on the palace hill, high above, one golden-lit window burned steadily in the topmost tower, and Ivo had the sudden, unshakable certainty that behind that window, on a cushion of velvet, the Hollow Crown sat awake, patient, and hungry—and that tomorrow, when they moved toward the archive, its attention would already be waiting for them there.
