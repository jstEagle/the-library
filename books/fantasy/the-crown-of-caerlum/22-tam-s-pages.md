## 22. Tam's Pages

The memory-charm had been failing for three years, and Tam had known it the way a man knows a tooth is going bad—by the ache that came and went, by the sweet wrong taste of forgetting.

He sat in the back room of the waystation inn with a candle, a basin of water, a knife, and two people who had no idea what he was about to do to himself. Bryn leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed. Ivo sat across from him, hands flat on the table, watching with the particular stillness of someone who had already guessed.

"You should know," Tam said, "that I have done this exactly once before, on a pig, under supervision, and the pig did not enjoy it."

"Reassuring," said Bryn.

"The pig survived."

"And you?"

"I intend to find out." He rolled up his sleeve. On the inside of his forearm, half-hidden beneath thirty years of ink stains and old scars, was the charm: a small sigil drawn in his own blood, sealed with wax, keyed to his heartbeat. It looked like nothing. It had cost him everything.

Ivo's eyes fixed on it. "That's why they exiled you. Not the questions."

"No one exiles a man for asking questions, Ivo. Questions are cheap. Everyone in the archive asked questions." Tam turned his arm over, studying the sigil the way he'd studied it every night for years, looking for the rot. "They exile you for *removing pages*. And I didn't just remove them from the catalogue. I removed them from existence. Every copy, every index entry, every marginal note in Master Aldric's hand. I burned eleven years of my own work to make sure no one could reconstruct what I'd taken." He smiled, and knew it was a bad smile. "Then I put the only surviving record somewhere the Crown would never think to look. Somewhere they'd already decided was empty."

"Your head," Ivo said quietly.

"My head. Specifically—" he tapped his temple above his left ear— "here. A memory-charm doesn't store paper, obviously. It stores the *memory* of the paper. Every word, every diagram, every correction, encoded as recollection. As far as any divination could tell, I was simply a man who remembered being an archivist and remembered nothing about the Hollow Crown's consumption mechanics. A gap. And gaps are invisible if you build them honestly." He paused. "The problem with gaps is that they don't stay built."

Bryn pushed off the doorframe. "You're saying it's coming apart."

"I'm saying that for the past year, I've been finding fragments on my tongue. Words I shouldn't know. Last month I woke up reciting the tensile properties of crown-gold alloy in my sleep. Two weeks ago I drew a ley-line convergence map on the frosted window of a tavern without realizing I'd done it until the innkeeper asked me why I was defacing her glass." He laughed, briefly, horribly. "The charm is dissolving, and when it goes, everything comes back at once. All of it. Eleven years of forbidden knowledge detonating inside a skull that has spent three years learning how to be empty. Do you know what that does to a mind? I've seen it. We called it *shattering the shelf*. The man who tried it before me—we found him in the reading room, alive, breathing, smiling at the wall. He never spoke again. His mind was full, you understand. Perfectly, catastrophically full."

"So we wait for it to happen naturally?" Ivo said. "Let it break you?"

"If we wait, I lose everything anyway—the shock will scatter it. If I extract it now, deliberately, slowly, I might keep most of it and most of myself." He picked up the knife. "Might is the operative word. I want that recorded. When this goes wrong, I want someone to remember that I said *might*."

"Tam." Ivo's voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was frightened and hiding it. "What does the extraction actually involve?"

"Blood, water, and honesty." Tam set the basin between them. "The charm is keyed to my blood because blood can't be tortured out of you—it has to be given. I open the sigil, I feed it, and then I talk. The charm releases what I speak. Piece by piece. Like drawing thread from a spindle." He met Ivo's eyes. "And here is the part where you both earn your keep. If I start speaking nonsense—if I stop answering direct questions—you pull me back. Say my name. Say something true and small. The name of a street. What I ate yesterday. The charm holds onto the present if the present is loud enough. You are the anchor rope. Do not let go of the rope."

Bryn crouched beside the table so she was level with him. Up close, Tam could see Sera in her face—not in the features but in the set of the jaw, the refusal to blink first. "How much do you lose?" she asked. "Of yourself. Honestly."

"Honestly? I don't know. That's the price of hiding things inside a person instead of a vault. Vaults don't bleed." He held out his arm to her. "Hold this still. Ivo, the water. And whatever happens—whatever I say in there—remember that some of what I hid, I hid because it was dangerous, not because it was false."

He opened the sigil.

* * *

Pain first. Of course pain first; the charm had been sewn into his pain like a pearl into flesh, and opening it was unsewing. Tam felt the knife-tip catch the wax seal and heard himself make a sound he hadn't made since childhood, high and involuntary, and then Bryn's hand clamped his arm flat and Ivo's voice said, low and steady, "*Tam.* The inn. The waystation. You had mutton. You complained about the mutton."

"Mutton," Tam repeated, and the word was a rope in his fist, and he gripped it.

The water in the basin began to move. Not ripple—*move*, turning slow circles, and in its surface images surfaced like drowned things rising: parchment, ink, the green lamp of the royal archive's restricted stacks. The charm was releasing. He could feel it leaving him in threads, each spoken word pulling a strand loose, and with each strand came the memory whole and terrible.

"Speak," Ivo said. "Tell us."

So Tam told it.

"The Hollow Crown was forged in the reign of the last true dynasty, before the ravensworn were purged from record. Its purpose was never coronation. It was a binding instrument—a cage for power too large to hold any other way. But a cage must be anchored, and the anchor they chose was a living mind. The first bearer. The Crown does not rule through the king. The Crown *is* the king, or rather, it wears him, and it feeds. And what it feeds on—" his voice cracked, and he steadied it against the rope of the ordinary— "what it feeds on is memory. Not metaphorically. Literally. Memory is the substrate of magic in this kingdom; every hedge-charm, every blessing, every song that keeps a hearth-luck warm is memory given form. The Crown eats the raw material. Slowly. So slowly no one noticed the famine while it was happening. Tithe collectors who take more than grain. Villages that forget their names. A kingdom going quietly hollow from the edges inward, and everyone blaming age, or war, or God."

The basin-water had gone dark as ink. In it, he watched his own younger hands carry pages through lamplit corridors, watch himself burn copies in a brazier while Master Aldric stood guard at the door.

"How far along is it?" Ivo asked. His face was grey.

"That was page forty-one of sixty-three. The consumption rate doubles roughly every decade. At current rates, within twenty years the outer provinces will be functionally mindless. Within fifty, Caerlum itself. Within eighty—" Tam swallowed. "Page fifty-two. The rite. This is the part worth dying for, so listen."

He closed his eyes and let the memory come up clean.

"The unbinding requires three waystones opened in sequence—north, center, south—the same stones the original binders used to anchor the Crown's cage. Each stone, once opened, loosens one third of the binding. They must be opened in order and within one turning of the moon of each other, or the binding reseals and hardens, and after that no power under heaven opens it again. The map you carry marks all three. The third lies beneath the palace itself, under the throne room, where they buried it so no one would ever find it."

"And then?" Bryn said. "You open three rocks and the Crown pops off like a cork?"

"No." Tam opened his eyes. "And then a ravensworn must stand at the throne and take the binding into their own body. That was always the design. The binders built the escape into the cage: the blood that made it can unmake it, but unmaking means *holding*. Every thread of the binding, transferred from the Crown to living flesh. One person. Alone. That was the flaw in them, you see—that was the sin the whole thing grew from. They couldn't imagine sharing the burden, so they wrote a burden that could only be carried alone." He laughed, and it hurt. "I catalogued that sentence once. *The bearer shall bear alone.* I thought it was poetry. It's an instruction."

The room had gone very quiet. Tam became aware, distantly, that the candle flame had stretched tall and thin, and that the water in the basin was climbing the sides slightly, clinging, as though the words themselves had weight.

Ivo was staring at the basin. At him. Tam watched the young man do the arithmetic—he was quick, quicker than he let anyone see—and watched him arrive at the sum.

"One person," Ivo said. "A willing ravensworn."

"There is exactly one ravensworn left," Tam said gently. "And yes. Willing matters. The rite fails with an unwilling bearer—it was designed that way too, a mercy, or a cruelty, depending on how you look at it. The Crown cannot simply seize him. He has to choose it."

"Then he chooses nothing," Bryn said flatly. "We find another way."

"There is no other way. I removed the other ways myself. I checked. Twice." Tam felt suddenly, enormously tired, the tiredness of a man whose skull had been a locked room for three years and who had just watched the door come off its hinges. "Ivo. Look at me."

Ivo looked at him. He looked, Tam thought, like a man hearing his own death sentence read in a foreign language he unfortunately spoke fluently.

"It may not kill you," Tam said. "The original bearer survived the binding—for a while. Years. It consumes, but slowly, and a strong will slows it further. And there is—" he hesitated, because the next fragment was still surfacing, half-dissolved, and he reached for it carefully, the way you reach for a fish in dark water— "there is a line. Page fifty-eight. Marginal note, Aldric's hand, quoting something older. *The burden borne alone devours; the burden borne together dilutes.* It was dismissed as commentary. Heresy. But the original binder's own journal says something similar, and I hid that page too, and I can't—I can't quite reach it yet, it's still deep, it's—"

His tongue went thick. The room tilted. Somewhere very far away, the candle was a white star and Bryn was shouting his name and Ivo's hand was on his shoulder saying *the mutton, Tam, the mutton was terrible, tell me about the mutton*—

"—underseasoned," Tam heard himself say, from a great distance. "Underseasoned and cold in the middle. The innkeeper's mother cooked it. She's been dead nine years, which explains a great deal."

The star that was the candle shrank back to a flame. The room reassembled itself around him: the table, the basin now full of black water, Bryn's grip bruising his arm, Ivo's face inches away, pale and fierce.

"Stay with us," Ivo said.

"For the moment." Tam flexed his fingers. They obeyed. He counted his memories the way a miser counts coin after a robbery: name, street, sister's face, the smell of the archive, the taste of the mutton. All present. Most present. Something small was missing—he'd know what later, probably, and probably it wouldn't matter—and something enormous had been gained.

He looked down at the basin. The black water was settling, and in it, faint as breath on glass, diagrams were still visible: the three waystones in their sequence, lines of force running between them like the ribs of a great beast, and at the apex, where the lines converged, a crude drawing of a throne with a single figure standing before it.

Alone, the drawing said, in his own handwriting from another life.

Except—Tam squinted, leaned closer, and felt his heart turn over. The ink had shifted. Charms did that sometimes, in the moment of release; the memory settling into its new container, smudging, rearranging. The figure at the throne was no longer alone. Fainter, cruder, drawn as if by a different and more desperate hand, two more figures flanked it. One holding its right hand. One holding its left.

And beneath, in letters that had not been there a moment ago, in handwriting Tam did not recognize—older, sharper, a woman's hand:

*we should have carried it together*

He stared at it for a long time. Then he looked up at Ivo, and at Bryn, and said the thing he had crossed a kingdom and burned a career to be able to say:

"I know how we win. And I know why we might not have to lose you doing it. But we need the third waystone, and the third waystone is under the palace—which means we need to get into the palace." He wiped the black water from the basin's rim with his thumb, almost tenderly. "And Ivo, I am so sorry to tell you this, but I believe the Crown already knows we're coming. Because the charm wasn't the only thing listening tonight. Did neither of you feel that? Halfway through page fifty-two, something turned over in its sleep and paid attention." He nodded toward the shuttered window, toward the city beyond, toward the towers where the bells hung silent and the king sat smiling at nothing.

"It knows a ravensworn exists. And it has been hungry for a very long time."
