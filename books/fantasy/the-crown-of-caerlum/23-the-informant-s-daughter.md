## 23. The Informant's Daughter

She was waiting outside the smuggler's cellar on Saltcote Lane when I came up for air after three days underground.

I nearly walked straight past her. That's the thing about the capital—you learn to walk past everyone. The beggar with the weeping sores, the woman who talks to the bells, the child selling nails from a broken crate. Caerlum's underworld had swallowed me whole for three days, and I'd already learned its first lesson: look at nothing, owe nothing, stop for no one.

But she stepped into my path, and I knew her.

Not by name. By face. She'd been standing at the rail of the river bridge the night Corvane cornered us—the woman in the fish-oil cloak who pointed, who called out *there, that's them, that's the ones*, while a crossbow bolt meant for my back went through Bryn instead. I'd seen her face for half a second in the torchlight as we fled downriver, and I'd carried it since. Not with hatred, exactly. With something worse. With understanding.

I would have sold her out too, five years ago. Maybe last month.

"You," she said.

"Me," I said. My hand found the knife under my coat without asking permission. Three days of hiding will do that.

"I need—" Her voice cracked and she pressed her fist against her mouth like a child holding back sickness. Around us, Saltcote Lane went about its business: a cart of eels, two dockhands arguing over wages, a bell tolling somewhere in the upper city, slow and wrong, the way all the bells had sounded since we came back. "I need the ravensworn."

The word landed like ice down my collar. Nobody was supposed to know that word yet. Tam was still alive—wounded, fevered, but alive—and he'd told exactly one person outside our little company what I was, and that person was me.

"Wrong man," I said. "Never heard of him."

"Don't." She grabbed my sleeve. I could have pulled away. I didn't, and hated myself for the hesitation. "I know what you are. Everyone in the low city is whispering now—the nameless charm-seller, the one who fixed the Alderman's memory. My name's Odile Vass. I sold you to the inquisitors at the bridge. I'll not pretend otherwise. I took Crown silver because my girl was hungry and I'm a coward, and I've regretted it every hour since I watched them carry that red-haired woman away bleeding."

"She lived," I said. "Your aim was worse than your conscience."

Something flickered across her face—relief, or maybe just exhaustion finding nowhere else to go. "Then I owe you a life I can't repay. So I'll pay with this instead." She glanced up and down the lane, then leaned close. Her breath smelled of cheap gin and fear. "Corvane has your faces on every gatepost by tomorrow morning. He has your old shop sealed. He has men asking after a lame archivist and a red-haired swordswoman. And he knows about the smugglers on Saltcote—not from me, from the harbormaster's books—but they'll be taken within the week unless you're gone before then."

"And why tell me?"

"Because I need you to do something first." Her chin trembled once, and she stilled it with visible effort, the way people do when they've been holding themselves together for a long time and can't afford to stop. "My daughter. Marta. She's nine."

I waited.

"The tithe-collectors came to Ropewalk Court in the spring. Before the bridge, before any of it. They go door to door in the poor quarters now—I thought it was census work, I signed their book myself, God forgive me—and they stood in my kitchen and asked Marta questions. What's your name, little one. Where were you born. What did your father do before he drowned." Odile's voice went flat, reciting. "She answered every one. And then she looked up at me and said, *Mama, why did I say those things? Those aren't mine.*"

*The village that forgot itself,* I thought. The hollowed-out place on the road where an old man had smiled at us with empty eyes and asked if we'd come to tell him who he was.

"How long?" I asked.

"Since spring. It comes and goes. Some days she's entirely herself. Some days she looks at me like I'm a stranger, and she cries, and she doesn't know why she's crying, and neither do I." Odile's composure finally tore, just a seam. "Last week she forgot my face for a full hour. She sat across the table from me eating bread and called me 'goodwife.' Ivo Ravensworth—" she used the name like a blade, like proof, "—I have nothing left to lose but her, and I'm losing her anyway. Whatever you are. Whatever it costs. Fix her."

The lane seemed very quiet around us. The eel-cart had passed. Somewhere above, gulls argued over the rooftops.

Every rule I'd built my life on arranged themselves in a neat row inside my head, like soldiers presenting arms. *Never work openly. Never work twice in the same place. Never let them see the shape of what you are.* Five years of rules. Five years of small sleights and hidden hands, luck-knots disguised as knots, wards disguised as embroidery, everything done sideways and slant so no one ever saw the actual current move.

And here was a woman who'd betrayed me, asking me to break all of them at once, in the middle of the capital, with Corvane's net drawing tight around us by the hour.

"No," I said.

She didn't flinch. "I expected that. Ask your price, then. Silver? I have none. Information? Take it—it's yours already, I gave it free. My body, my labor, my silence—"

"It's not the price." I turned to go. "It's that I don't know if I can. What the tithe-takers take doesn't come back on its own. In the village, it never came back at all. I've never tried to give back what the Crown has eaten, and if I try and fail in front of witnesses, I won't just kill myself, I'll burn the people sheltering me. There are eleven people asleep in that cellar, Odile. Children among them."

"Then learn," she said.

I stopped.

"My mother taught me letters," Odile said, quieter now. "Before the plague took her. She used to say the worst sin isn't failing—it's deciding in advance that you'll fail so you don't have to try." A bitter almost-laugh. "She also said never trust a man who flinches from his own hands, so make of that what you will, charm-seller."

She pressed a scrap of paper into my palm—an address in Ropewalk Court—and walked away down Saltcote Lane with her back straight and her shoulders shaking, and I stood there in the cold holding a piece of paper that weighed approximately as much as my entire former life.

* * *

Bryn voted to throw the paper in the fire.

"She sold us," she said. She was sitting on a cask near the cellar's single lamp, cleaning her sword with the grim thoroughness she brought to everything, including conversation. Her wound had closed; the scar across her ribs was still angry-red, and she moved like someone renegotiating terms with her own body. "She put a bolt in me. And you want to walk into her house and light a beacon over our heads."

"She gave us warning about the gates and the harbormaster."

"After she profited off us once already. Guilt's cheap, Ivo. It costs nothing to warn a man you've already robbed."

Tam coughed from his corner—his corner now, permanently, stacked with the smugglers' spare blankets and a clay brazier that did more good than all of Maren's tonics, though I'd never say so. He'd aged ten years since the bridge, or maybe the bridge had only made visible what the archive-burning and exile and drink had already done. His hands shook unless he was holding a book. He was holding a book now, upside down, which I chose not to mention.

"The interesting question," Tam rasped, "isn't whether she deserves help. Morality is a luxury of the unwatched. The question is what happens if you succeed." He looked up at me, eyes bright with fever and something sharper. "If you restore what the Crown consumed—even once, even in a child—that tells us something vital. It means the consumption isn't destruction. It means the memories still exist *somewhere*, held in the binding, retrievable. Which means the rite might restore everything. Every village. Every mind."

"That's a reason for the rite," Bryn said. "Not a reason to gamble on a traitor's kid."

"They're the same reason," Tam said. "We've been assuming the unbinding releases what was eaten back to its owners. But we've never tested it. If Ivo works this restoration and fails, we lose little. If he succeeds—" he spread his shaking hands, "—we learn the shape of the miracle before we stake the kingdom on it."

"And if he succeeds," Bryn said flatly, "and someone sees him do it, Corvane learns exactly what we can do, and every ward, every trap, every defense between us and the vault gets rebuilt overnight to counter it."

Silence. The lamp guttered. Down the passage, the smugglers' muffled voices rose and fell—old Dree arguing about tide-tables, someone laughing, the ordinary music of people who trusted each other enough to sleep.

That was the part I couldn't get past, lying awake afterward on my bedroll. Eleven people overhead. Children. All of them safe right now because Ivo the invisible charm-man had spent years building debts and favors and small kindnesses through the underworld, and the bill was coming due regardless—Odile had said so. Corvane would find Saltcote Lane whether I lit a beacon or not. The noose was tightening either way.

The only variable was Marta Vass, nine years old, calling a stranger *goodwife* across her own kitchen table.

I got up in the dark and took the paper out of my boot, where I'd put it without deciding to, and read the address again by feel.

"Bryn," I whispered across the cellar.

"I heard you get up. I hear everything. It's exhausting." A pause. "You're going."

"I'm going."

She sighed the sigh of a woman whose sister had also been impossible, which was the closest thing to a blessing I was going to get. "Then I'm coming. And if this is a trap, I'm killing her properly this time, none of your mercy."

"That's the spirit," I said.

* * *

Ropewalk Court was a wound of a place: six tenements leaning together for warmth around a dirt yard strung with washing lines, the kind of neighborhood the capital pretended didn't exist whenever processions came through. We went separately, Bryn trailing me by thirty paces with her hood up and her sword wrapped in sacking like a bundle of tools, and I kept my head down and my hands in my pockets and felt the old familiar itch between my shoulder blades—the feeling of being watched that in this city was usually accurate.

Odile opened the door before I knocked. She'd been watching the yard.

Inside was poverty arranged with stubborn dignity: scrubbed floorboards, a mended curtain, a shelf of chipped cups displayed like treasure. A smell of onions and tallow. And at the table by the window, a girl.

Marta Vass had her mother's dark hair and a thin, serious face. She was drawing in charcoal on the back of a discarded playbill—a ship, drawn with surprising skill, rigging and all. She looked up when I entered, and my chest tightened, because her eyes did the thing I'd seen in the forgotten village. Not emptiness. Something worse: a person looking out from behind frosted glass, present and reaching and unable quite to touch the world.

"Marta," Odile said gently, "this is—Ivo. He's a friend. He's going to help you remember."

The girl studied me with terrible politeness. "Am I supposed to know you?"

"No," I said, and knelt by the table so we were eye level. Up close I could see the wrongness humming in her, faint as heat-shimmer. The Crown's leavings. "Do you want to play a game?"

Her mouth quirked—the ghost of a nine-year-old's skepticism. "Mama says that about medicine."

"This is like medicine. But I'm worse at it." I set my hands palm-up on the table. "Put your hands on mine. If anything feels bad, pull away, and we stop. Agreed?"

She looked at her mother. Odile nodded, once, her knuckles white against the doorframe.

Marta laid her small hands in mine. They were warm and ink-stained and slightly sticky, and some tired animal part of my brain registered: *this is the most dangerous thing you have ever done*, and another part, older and quieter, registered only: *she trusts you.*

I let my walls down.

Five years of discipline came apart like wet paper. I hadn't worked openly since I'd run from Maren's cottage—hadn't let the current rise through me unwrapped, unnamed, visible to anyone with the sense to see. Now I breathed out, and reached along the thread of Marta's forgetting, and followed it down.

Down was the right word. Her memories weren't gone. They were *tied off*. Each one—a face, a name, a grief, her father's drowning, her mother's laugh—was knotted at its root with a binding sigil I recognized with a sick lurch, because I'd seen its ancestor written in fire behind my eyelids at the waystones. The Crown's mark. The ravensworn mark, corrupted, coarsened, mass-produced. Someone had taken my bloodline's oldest craft and stamped it onto children by the hundred.

*I can untie this,* I thought, and then, honestly: *I think I was born knowing how.*

I began with the smallest knot—a memory of a dog, brown and ridiculous, gone a year now. I loosened the sigil the way Maren had taught me to loosen a snarled thread: never pulling, always easing, giving the knot room to remember it was wool and not wire. The sigil resisted. Then, with a sensation like a held breath releasing, it unraveled—

—and the dog came home.

Marta gasped. Her fingers clenched mine hard enough to hurt. "*Barley!*" she cried, and then her face crumpled and tears spilled freely. "He ran into the road, Mama, I forgot he ran into the road, I thought I'd dreamed him—"

"Keep going," Odile whispered. Whether to me or to her daughter, I couldn't tell.

I went deeper. Knot after knot, sigil after sigil, easing each one open while sweat ran down my spine and the lamp flames in the room bent toward me like listeners. This was the danger I hadn't told Bryn about: working restoration wasn't subtle. I was pulling memory-matter out of the Crown's grip, and the Crown noticed things being taken. At the third waystone I'd felt its attention like weather on the horizon. Now, prying loose its smallest holdings one by one, I felt that attention turn—slow, vast, curious, hungry—like something enormous shifting in its sleep beneath the palace.

*Let it look,* I thought wildly. *Let it see what a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice can do.*

The big knots were harder. Her father's death had been bound three times over, layered sigils, as though the tithe-takers had known it was load-bearing. When it finally came free, Marta sobbed aloud—grief arriving all at once after months of leaking away—and I understood, with a clarity that frightened me, that this was the Crown's true cruelty. Not erasure. *Slow leak.* Taking grief in installments so the bereaved never got to finish mourning, never got to arrive anywhere, just thinned forever.

The last knot was the deepest: her own name.

I hesitated over it. Names are anchors; tampering with one wrong could unravel her entire self. But the sigil here was the same coarse stamp as the others, mass-produced, careless. Whoever had designed the tithe-ritual had relied on volume, not finesse.

They'd never met a ravensworn.

I eased the final knot open, and Marta Vass looked at me with clear, unfrosted eyes and said, "You're the charm man. From Saltcote. Mama talks about you when she thinks I'm asleep." Then, frowning at my expression: "Why are you crying?"

"Onions," said Odile, who was crying too, and crossed the room and gathered her daughter up, and for a long moment the two of them were just a knot of arms and dark hair and shaking breaths, and I stayed kneeling at the table feeling emptied out and scoured clean, like a cup after wine.

Bryn appeared in the doorway. I hadn't heard her come in. She looked at the mother and child, then at me kneeling there with lamplight bending around me and the residue of working still shimmering faintly off my skin like heat off summer cobbles.

"You're glowing," she said. "Slightly. Like bad cheese."

"Thank you."

"We need to leave. Now."

"Why n—" I started, and then I heard it.

Bells. Not the slow, wrong tolling that had become the capital's background murmur. Fast bells. Alarm bells, from the direction of the Processional Bridge, and beneath them, thin and rising, the whistles of the inquisitorial watch—the pattern Corvane's patrols used to ring a district shut, gate by gate, street by street.

Odile lifted her head from her daughter's shoulder. Her face had gone grey.

"They'll have felt it," she said. "Everyone with the sight for it will have felt it. A whole child's worth of memory, torn out of the Crown's keeping, all at once, in *this quarter*—" She was already moving, snuffing lamps, dragging a trunk from the wall. "There's a coal-cellar tunnel under the ropewalk, comes up by the tannery. Take it. Take her."

I stared at her. "Take her where?"

"With you. Please." Odile gripped my wrists, and her eyes were fierce and ruined and absolutely certain. "The tithe-takers will come back for her now—they always re-take what's stolen, I've seen it happen to the Ferre family's boy. She's not safe in this city, and I'm not safe to her anymore, because I signed their book, and someday she'll remember that I did." Her voice broke and hardened in the same breath. "You broke her chains, ravensworn. Don't leave her standing next to them."

From the streets beyond the court, the whistles were converging. Somewhere far off, at the center of the web, I felt—or imagined I felt—a vast and patient attention settle on Ropewalk Court like a hand on a map.

Bryn caught my eye across the room. She didn't argue. She just said, quietly, "Decide."

I looked down at Marta, who was clutching her charcoal drawing of a ship and looking between the adults with the sharp, scared intelligence of a child who understands that the world is rearranging itself around her.

"Do you want to see a real smuggler's cellar?" I asked her.

She wiped her nose with her sleeve. "Is it under the ground?"

"Very far under the ground."

"Okay," said Marta Vass, and took my hand.

Behind us, alarm bells swallowed the quarter whole.
