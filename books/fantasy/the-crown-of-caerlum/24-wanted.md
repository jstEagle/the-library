## 24. Wanted

*Corvane*

The warrants went up before dawn, pasted to every gatehouse and guildhall in the capital by clerks who did not ask questions. Corvane had written the descriptions himself, and he had been careful with them—careful in a way that troubled him.

*Ravensworth, Ivo. Charm-seller. Do not approach alone.*

*Bryn of Sallowmere. Armed. Lethal.*

*Tam Ostrewe, former Second Archivist. Consider him dangerous despite appearance.*

He had left off the word *heretic*. It would have made the hunt simpler, galvanized the patrols, given every zealot with a cudgel license to kill. But heresy was a charge you laid against someone wrong, and Corvane was no longer certain what wrong looked like. He had seen the king's eyes flicker—that sideways sliding, like a man reading something over your shoulder—and he had spent three nights since telling himself it was exhaustion, illness, anything but what Tam's stolen pages claimed.

The pages were gone now. The archivist had bled them out of his own skull somewhere between Vessany and the capital, and Corvane's informants said the three of them had gone to ground in the lower city like rats into a wall.

He stood before the warrant board in Inquisitors' Square while the morning crowd eddied around him, and he watched a fishmonger read Ivo Ravensworth's name aloud, slowly, sounding out the letters.

"Charm-seller," the fishmonger said. "The one on Tanner's Row? He fixed my wife's rheumatism charm for nothing once."

"Report him if you see him," Corvane said.

The fishmonger looked at him—a long, flat look—and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer.

Corvane walked back through the square feeling the city close around the warrant like water around a dropped stone. Five years he had served the Crown. Five years of believing that order was a holy thing, that the king's stillness was sanctity, that magic outlawed was magic safely dead. And now the first crack had opened, and through it he could hear something moving underneath Caerlum that he had never let himself hear before: the sound of ten thousand people who had quietly decided, long ago, that some laws were worth breaking.

They had all known about the charm-sellers. Every inquisitor knew. They had simply agreed not to see.

Corvane stopped at the mouth of an alley where a boy sold roasted chestnuts, and bought a paper cone he didn't want, because the boy's stall sat across from a smugglers' door he'd been watching for two days.

"Busy street," the boy said.

"Very."

"Lot of folk coming and going from that cellar there. Odd hours." The boy shrugged. "Not that anyone asked me."

"Has anyone asked you?"

"Not yet." The chestnut boy met his eyes with the frank courage of the very young and very poor. "But my mum says the ravensworn man cured the Ferrier girl's fits last winter. So if you're asking me to ask around, I'll ask slow."

Corvane paid him double for the chestnuts and walked away, and the terrible thing—the thing he would carry into the undercroft weeks later like a splinter—was that he wasn't sure he wanted the boy to ask fast.

*Ivo*

The smugglers' door was behind a tannery on Ropewalk Lane, down a stair that smelled of wet stone and old brine, and Ivo knew it because five years ago he had charmed a luck-knot for a woman named Odile Voss who ran cargo that the harbor taxes preferred not to meet.

She had paid him in silver and a warning: *If the inquisitors ever come asking, you never charmed me.* It had stung at the time. Now it was the only reason he was alive.

"You've got nerve," Odile said, "coming here with your face on every post in the city." She was broader than he remembered, gray coming into her black hair, and she hadn't stopped glaring at him since he'd come down the stairs with Bryn bleeding-adjacent at his side and Tam swaying with fever behind them. "I could sell you to the Crown and buy a new ship."

"You won't," Ivo said.

"Give me one reason."

"The Ferrier girl. Her fits. You brought me to her at two in the morning because no physician would touch her and you were afraid she'd die." He held her glare. "You didn't do it for money. You did it because she was somebody's daughter and you couldn't stand it. That woman is still in there, Odile. I'm looking at her."

Odile Voss stared at him for a long moment, and then she swore with the fluency of a dockworker and turned to her people. "Clear the back room. Get the archivist horizontal before he falls down. And someone get word to the Saltgate cell—we move cargo tonight, and the cargo walks on two legs."

Bryn caught Ivo's sleeve as the room emptied. "You just *asked* her," she said. "No charm, no trick. You just told her the truth and she believed you."

"I've found people mostly want an excuse to do the decent thing. They're waiting for permission."

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard."

"It's also why we're not in irons."

She almost smiled. It was the closest she'd come in days, and Ivo felt it land somewhere under his ribs and stay there.

The back room was a storeroom with pallets among the crates, and it became their world. Tam slept twenty hours and woke raving about cataloguing systems; Bryn drilled knife-forms in the narrow space until Odile threatened to charge her for the floorboards; Ivo mended charms by candlelight—not for coin now, but for the smugglers themselves, because word traveled fast below the waterline that the ravensworn was under Voss protection and half the crews came by with things broken or aching or cursed.

A helmsman with salt-scars brought a compass that spun wrong near the palace district. A cook brought her mother's ring, which whispered at night. A boy of maybe fourteen brought a wooden bird his dead father had carved, and asked, very quietly, if Ivo could make it sing again.

Ivo held the bird a long time. There was no enchantment in it. There never had been. But he breathed on it anyway, the way his mother used to breathe on scraped knees, and told the boy: "It sings when you remember him right. That's the whole trick. That's always been the whole trick."

The boy nodded solemnly and took the bird home, and Ivo sat in the dark afterward feeling something he had no name for. For five years he had built a life out of small transactions—coin for charm, charm for silence—and told himself it was safety. Sitting in a smugglers' cellar with a price on his head, doing magic for free for people whose names he was learning, he understood for the first time that the hiding had been the lonely part. Not the danger. The hiding.

"They're saying things about you upstairs," Bryn said that night, settling onto the crate beside his pallet. "The dock folk. That you're the ravensworn come back. That you're going to fix the king."

"I'm going to try."

"They don't say *try*. They say *fix*, like it's weather." She was quiet a moment. "Sera would have liked this. All these people deciding, all at once, to be brave in small ways." Her voice roughened. "She died carrying a map so strangers could finish something. I used to think that was foolish. Dying for a cause you wouldn't live to see."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's the only kind of dying that means anything." She lay back on her own pallet. "Sleep, Ivo. Tomorrow Odile moves us closer to the palace, and after that I stop being able to protect you from what you have to do."

* * *

*Tam*

Tam Ostrewe had spent thirty years believing that knowledge was a thing you kept—filed, guarded, locked against fire and fool alike. Lying on a pallet in a smugglers' cellar with holes in his memory where his stolen pages used to be, he was learning the opposite lesson at the speed of a falling man.

What survived was what he gave away.

He tested it each morning. He would recite, from memory, everything he could reconstruct of the Hollow Crown's history to Bryn and Ivo and whoever else Odile's people drifted in to listen—and each day the recitation came easier, the gaps filling not with recovered pages but with the act of telling. Memory, he was discovering, was less like a vault and more like a muscle. Or a fire. Hoarded, it guttered. Shared, it spread.

"The binders' guilds weren't secret," he told a semicircle of smugglers one evening, gesturing with a cup of Odile's terrible wine. "That's what nobody remembers anymore, because remembering is precisely what the Crown eats. Two hundred years ago every village had its binder. Your grandmother's grandmother probably wore a bound charm at her wedding. Magic wasn't outlawed because it was dangerous. It was outlawed because it was *distributed*, and power hates company."

One of the helmsmen raised a hand like a schoolboy. "Then why'd the ravensworn help build the thing?"

"Ah." Tam drank. "Because they were trying to cage a tyrant, and they succeeded, and then they made the oldest mistake in the catalogue: they kept the key instead of melting the lock. Control feels like wisdom right up until the moment it becomes the cage." He looked at Ivo across the room. "Your line has been paying interest on that mistake for two centuries. You're the last payment due."

"Cheerful as ever," Ivo said.

"I'm a dying man with a failing archive in his skull. Cheerfulness is for people with futures." But Tam smiled when he said it, and the smugglers laughed, and later, when the room had emptied, Bryn sat beside him and said, without preamble:

"When you die, I'll remember it. All of it. Whatever you tell me between now and then—I'll carry it."

Tam looked at her for a long moment. His eyes were wet. "You know," he said, "in thirty years of archiving, no one ever offered to be my shelf."

*Corvane*

The lower city swallowed his patrols whole.

Corvane sent six men into Ropewalk Lane on the third night and got back four, none of whom could describe the door they'd failed to find. He sent informants and bought silence. He doubled the bounty and watched the posters defaced overnight—Ravensworth's face given a kindly mustache, the word WANTED amended to WANTED: FOR FIXING THINGS—in handwriting too varied to trace.

He stood before a defaced warrant in Tanner's Row at dawn, alone, and understood that he was not hunting three fugitives. He was hunting a city, and the city was winning.

His lieutenant, a young man named Pell with a true believer's clean face, found him there. "We could burn the tannery district," Pell said. "Flush them out."

"With whose authority?"

"Yours. You're First Inquisitor. The Crown's voice in Caerlum."

Corvane looked at him—at the certainty, the hunger to be ordered—and felt the crack in his own faith widen another hair. Once, the words *the Crown's voice* would have steadied him like a hand on the shoulder. Now he heard them and thought: *which Crown? The king? Or the thing wearing him?*

"No burning," he said. "We do this cleanly or we prove every word they've said about us."

Pell's face flickered—disappointment, quickly mastered. "And if cleanliness fails, sir?"

"Then I'll go down myself." Corvane touched the warrant, the mustached face, the amended word. *Fixing things.* "There's a door on Ropewalk Lane that doesn't exist. I'm going to knock on it."

*Ivo*

Odile moved them on the fifth night, through the belly of a grain barge, up a smugglers' canal into the Warrens—the rookery of leaning houses hard against the palace district's outer wall, close enough that from certain rooftops you could see the light in the royal tower, burning steady and wrong, night after night, like a lamp no one ever trimmed.

Their new host was a fence named Quill who owed Ivo for a hearing-charm that had saved him from a customs ambush two years back, and Quill's debt was paid in full within a day: safe rooms, food, and a rooftop map of the palace approaches drawn from twenty years of watching.

"The third waystone's under them," Quill said, tapping the palace on his map with a cracked fingernail. "Everyone below the waterline knows it. Old stories—there's a door in the undercroft the royals sealed two hundred years ago, and the smugglers' grandmothers' grandmothers used to say the stones hum when you pass above them. My own gran swore she felt it through her boots on the Processional Bridge." He looked up. "You're really going under there."

"That's the plan."

"Plan." Quill snorted. "My gran also said the ravensworn would come back someday, and I thought that was a story too." He rolled up the map and pressed it into Ivo's hands. "For what it's worth—half the Warrens would walk in behind you if you asked. Don't ask. Dead heroes are bad for business. But know it's there."

That night Ivo climbed to the roof alone. Below him the capital sprawled, dark except for the watchfires and the tower lamp, and he could feel it even from here—a faint pull at the edges of his mind, like a tide going out, the Crown feeding at its leisure on the sleeping city. Somewhere under that light was a vault, and a stone, and a throne, and whatever remained of a man who had once been a king.

Bryn came up beside him without a sound. She carried her crossbow now with a fresh scar along her forearm where the bolt had kissed her, and she stood shoulder to shoulder with him in the cold and said nothing for a long time.

"Pell was spotted asking after Quill's door," she said finally. "Corvane's closing. We have days. Maybe less."

"Then we go soon."

"There's something else." She hesitated, and Bryn hesitating was more frightening than any patrol. "The boy with the chestnuts. The one who asks slow. He found Quill's runner tonight. Didn't give up the address—led them a chase instead. They caught him at the canal." Her jaw tightened. "He's alive. Odile's people pulled him out. But Corvane's men marked him, and next time they won't be slow."

Ivo closed his eyes. A boy who had never met him, who knew him only as a name on a warrant and a rumor of kindness, had run from inquisitors on his behalf and been caught for it.

"He believes in you," Bryn said. "Half this city does. That's the weapon Corvane can't counter, and it's the weight you can't put down." She turned to face him fully. "So. Third stone, under the palace, behind wards keyed to royal blood, with the First Inquisitor knocking on our door. Tell me the plan, ravensworn. And tell me true—because whatever you say next, I'm walking in beside you."

Ivo opened his eyes. The tower lamp burned steady and wrong above the sleeping city, and beneath his feet, faint as a heartbeat through stone, he could almost feel the waystone humming in the dark, waiting two hundred years for a hand like his.

"We open the door Quill drew us," he said. "And we go down together."
