## 25. The Third Waystone

The undercroft beneath the palace smelled of cold stone and older secrets.

Ivo had imagined the third waystone in a hundred shapes on the road here—buried in a field, sunk in a riverbed, hidden behind a wall of the palace kitchens where no one but rats and cooks would ever look. He had not imagined it at the center of everything: directly below the throne room, three levels down, at the exact point where every ley-line Tam had ever mapped converged like threads pulled tight to a single knot.

"They built the palace on top of it," Tam said, his voice thin in the dark. He was leaning against the corridor wall, one hand pressed to the bandage at his side where the vault's last trap had caught him. The blood had soaked through twice already. "Not near it. On it. Every foundation stone laid according to the old geometry. They didn't want to hide the waystone, Ivo. They wanted to *sit* on it."

"Keep it down," Bryn murmured. She was ahead of him in the passage, knife drawn even though they hadn't seen a living soul since Corvane's access codes had opened the last gate an hour ago. "If it hums, the whole palace will hear it."

"It won't hum," Ivo said. "The other two didn't."

"The other two weren't under the throne of the thing that wants you dead."

He couldn't argue with that. He followed her down.

The passage narrowed as it descended, the masonry changing around them—newer stone giving way to older, older giving way to something that wasn't quite stone at all, a grey material shot through with veins of faint silver that pulsed when Ivo looked away and went still when he looked directly at them. His grandmother's memory stirred in him, unbidden: *veinstone*, they'd called it, grown not quarried, coaxed up from the deep places by binders who understood that the land itself could be taught to hold a shape.

His ancestors had taught the land to hold this shape. This whole palace was a lid.

And beneath the lid—

The chamber opened without warning, the corridor simply ending, and Ivo stepped out into a space so vast his candlelight couldn't find its walls. The floor was veinstone too, polished smooth by centuries, and in its center rose the waystone.

It was taller than the others. Taller than a man, taller than two men, a single pillar of black rock flecked with silver, and unlike the first two stones—which had slept until touched—this one was already awake. Ivo felt it before he saw it, a pressure against his mind like a hand pressed flat against a door, patient, immense, waiting for him to open it from the inside.

"There it is," Tam breathed. He slid down the wall to sitting, too weak to stand and past pretending otherwise. "Third marker. The keystone. Everything else is just—doors. That's the hinge."

Bryn looked back at Ivo. In the candlelight her face was all sharp planes and exhaustion, and underneath both, something gentler she would have denied with a blade if accused. "Say it again," she said. "What happens when you touch it."

"I see what they saw." He'd said it before, at the second stone, at Maren's cottage, on the road through the Weald in the small hours when the fire burned low and none of them could sleep. But she wanted it again. She wanted it the way a soldier wants the plan repeated before the gate opens. "Their memories. The binders'. What they made, why they made it, what went wrong." He paused. "What I have to do."

"And after?"

"After, we go up." He nodded at the ceiling, at the thousands of tons of palace above them, at the throne room somewhere overhead where a dead man sat wearing a crown that ate kingdoms. "After, I end it."

Tam coughed—a wet, bad sound—and began, quietly, to recite. Not the rite; the catalogue. It was what he did now when the pain got loud, working through his life's inventory item by item, shelf by shelf, as if naming things could keep them from being lost. *Accession regalia, pre-Crown period, forty-one items. Correspondence of the northern binders, partial, water-damaged.* Ivo had stopped asking him to rest. Some men were held together by their work, and Tam was held together by his, and there wasn't time left to build him anything else.

Ivo walked to the stone.

Up close, the black rock wasn't rock at all. It was dense with script, thousands upon thousands of characters cut so fine they read as texture, and he recognized the hand of them now—the ravensworn hand, the same angular letters that had crawled across Sera's map, across the first waystone in the hills, across the second in the drowned chapel. His family's writing. His family's work.

*We built this,* he thought. *All of it. The Crown, the stones, the cage. We built the thing that killed the king.*

His palm hovered over the surface. The pressure against his mind increased—not pushing, never pushing, just present, the way Maren used to stand in the doorway of the cottage when he was late home, saying nothing, letting her presence do the work of words.

"Bryn," he said.

"I'm here." She was. Right at his shoulder, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her, hear her breathing. "I'll be here the whole time. Same as before."

"And if it takes me—if I don't come back out of it—"

"Then I drag your body out and sit on Corvane until he tells me how to fix you." A beat. "He'd hate that. It'd be very satisfying."

Despite everything, Ivo laughed. It came out ragged, but it came out real, and he thought: *this is what I ran from, all those years. Not Maren. Not the magic. This—being known well enough to be joked about while walking into the dark.*

He touched the stone.

* * *

The world folded.

Not darkness—he'd expected darkness—but light, unbearable, total, the light of a day five hundred years gone, and Ivo was standing in it, or someone was standing in it and calling herself by his blood.

She was old. That surprised him. In the memories the first two stones had given him, the binders had been young, fierce, certain—his grandmother hammering the first draft of the Crown's binding in a forge of green fire, his great-uncles arguing geometry over a table of veinstone. But the woman whose eyes he wore now was bent and grey, her hands spotted, her ravensworn blood worn thin with age, and she was climbing. Down. Through passages Ivo recognized—the same ones he'd walked an hour ago, though then they'd been new-cut and bright, lit by lamps that burned without flame.

Behind her, distantly, the palace screamed.

No—not the palace. The king. The true king, the one the Crown had been made to imprison, except the prison had failed, or succeeded too well, or done something no one had planned, because the tyrant was dead and the Crown remained, and a hungry thing with nothing left to eat does not starve. It finds a new meal.

*It took him today,* the old woman thought, and her thoughts were Ivo's thoughts, grief moving through him like floodwater through a broken levee. *It took Aldric today. My king. My friend. I held his hand while it happened and he looked at me and he knew, he knew what we'd made, and he forgave me, which is worse than any curse.*

She reached the chamber. The waystone stood before her, young then, silver-bright, and she fell against it and began to cut.

The script came off her fingers like light. Ivo felt the cost of it in his own body, in the vision: each character carved from her own years, her own strength, her own name. She was sealing the stone—sealing the whole system, the three stones and the Crown between them, locking the rite of unbinding behind blood and sacrifice so that only a ravensworn could ever undo what the ravensworn had done. Her final act. Her penance.

Her granddaughter knelt beside her. Young. Fierce. Certain. The same face from the forge-memory, the same hands.

"Grandmother, stop. You're killing yourself. Let me finish it—I'm stronger, I can carry the cutting—"

"No."

"Then let me help. Cut it together. Half and half. We can—"

"*No.*"

The word cracked like a whip, and Ivo flinched inside the memory, feeling everything behind it: the terror, the guilt, the terrible arithmetic of a woman who had helped make a god-devouring machine and believed, with her whole breaking heart, that the debt was hers alone to pay.

"You don't touch it," the old binder said, softer now, taking her granddaughter's face in her ruined hands. "None of you touch it. This is mine. I helped forge it, and I will seal it, and my children and their children will guard the knowledge and never, never bear the burden. Do you understand? One bearer. One line. One debt. That is the only mercy in any of this—that it can be contained in a single life instead of spread across everyone."

"Grandmother—"

"Promise me."

And the granddaughter—furious, grieving, loving her too much to refuse a dying woman anything—promised.

And that promise became law. And that law became the ravensworn inheritance: one bearer per generation, one life spent holding the knowledge, the duty, the slow poison of proximity to the Crown. Ivo's mother, who never spoke of it. His mother's mother, who died tired. All the way down the long thin line of his blood, one person at a time, each carrying it alone because a dying woman's guilt had been mistaken for wisdom.

The old binder finished the last character. The stone sealed. She sagged against it, and Ivo felt her strength go out of her like heat leaving iron, and in her last moments she looked up—at her granddaughter, at the ceiling, at the throne room far above where a dead man's body sat wearing a live man's crown—and Ivo felt the thought rise in her, the truest thing she'd ever known, arriving too late to be anything but regret:

*We should have carried it together. Not alone. Oh, you fools—we should have carried it together.*

The memory closed like a book.

* * *

Ivo came back to himself on his knees, gasping, Bryn's arm already around his chest, holding him upright.

"Ivo. Ivo. Look at me. How many fingers."

"Three." His voice was gravel. "Bryn. I know why it's failing."

She went still. Even Tam's recitation stopped.

"The binding," he said. "The whole system—it was designed wrong. Not the magic. The magic holds fine. The *design*. One bearer, one generation, one life carrying the whole weight alone. Five hundred years of that. The Crown learned it from us." He heard the words come out of him and felt them land like stones dropped into still water. "That's why it eats alone. That's why it hoards. That's why it wears the king instead of sharing the burden with the court, the kingdom, anyone. We taught it that power is something one person carries and everyone else protects. We taught it loneliness as a principle."

Tam made a sound. Ivo turned. The archivist had both hands over his mouth, and above them his eyes were wet and wide with the particular agony of a scholar who has just seen his life's work rearranged into its true shape.

"The schism," Tam whispered. "At the second stone. The binders who wanted to destroy the Crown outright—they argued the control faction was arrogant. Prideful. *Isolated.* And the control faction won, and wrote the histories, and everyone assumed the destroyers were the radicals." He laughed, a broken little sound. "They were right. Gods forgive us, the radicals were right. Not about destruction—about *sharing*. The Crown was never meant to be borne by one. No burden that size was ever meant—"

"Tam." Bryn's voice, gentle and sharp at once. "Breathe."

But Ivo was already standing. His legs shook. His head rang with a dead woman's regret. And underneath all of it, quiet and enormous, a door was opening in his mind that he suspected would never fully close again.

"That's the key," he said. "That's what the rite needs. Not just a ravensworn at the throne. Not just the three stones. Tam—the pages, the rite you memorized—look at it again. The part everyone reads as ritual language. *One to bind, one to hold, one to witness.* We always thought it meant three offices. Three roles."

"Yes," Tam said slowly.

"It doesn't. It means three people. *Together.* The rite was written for a single bearer because my ancestor demanded it be—but the underlying structure, the actual mechanism, it wants distribution. It wants the weight split. That's why every lone attempt fails. That's why the Crown consumes its bearers. It's a shared burden running on a solo engine, and the engine keeps burning out, and the Crown keeps absorbing the wreckage." He pressed his palms against his eyes. "My grandmother saw it. At the very end. *We should have carried it together.* She died knowing the answer and sealed it away anyway, because her guilt was louder than her wisdom."

Silence, in the vast dark chamber, under the weight of the palace and the throne and the starving thing above them.

"So," Bryn said at last. She crouched in front of him, forcing him to meet her eyes. "When we go up there. When you do this. You're not doing it alone."

"I don't know if it works if—"

"You're not doing it alone." No blade in it this time. Just bedrock. "Sera died alone in your shop because she was trying to carry something by herself—she wouldn't even stop to find me, the stubborn—because that's what this kingdom teaches. Carry it alone or die. Well." She stood, and pulled him up with her, and her grip was strong enough that arguing would have required effort he didn't have. "I'm done with it. Tam?"

Tam pushed himself up the wall, white-faced, bleeding, smiling like a man who has finally finished a proof he's worked on for twenty years. "I've catalogued every failure of solitary power in five centuries of royal records," he said. "I would consider it professionally negligent to let you repeat one."

Above them, faintly, through three levels of stone and ceremony and lies, a bell began to ring.

Not a festival bell. Not a hour bell. The alarm pattern—Corvane's alarm pattern, the one they'd agreed on, three strikes and silence, meaning *they know, they're coming, run or finish it.*

Bryn drew her knife. Tam straightened as much as his wound allowed. Ivo looked up at the ceiling, at the throne room almost directly overhead, and felt the waystone humming against his back like a heart that had waited five hundred years for someone to listen.

"They've found the empty vault," he said. "The Crown knows we're here."

"Then we go up," Bryn said.

"Together," Ivo said—and for the first time since Sera died in his arms, the word didn't feel like a risk.

It felt like the answer.
