## 26. Corvane's Choice

Corvane had followed the ravensworn boy across half a kingdom, and in all that time he had learned one thing above the rest: the boy lied beautifully.

He lied with his shoulders, his hands, the small apologetic tilt of his head. He lied the way other men breathed. Corvane had watched him do it to innkeeps and gate-guards and a tithe-collector in Vessany who had asked one question too many. Each time, the lie was small, warm, harmless-seeming—a charm of misdirection woven so finely that even Corvane, who had been trained since childhood to feel the texture of falsehood, had to concentrate to find its edge.

It was, Corvane admitted privately, the only impressive thing about him.

The boy was not what the warrants described. The warrants said *heretic, binder, enemy of the Crown*, and Corvane had written those words himself, had believed them the way he believed the sunrise. But the man he had tracked through the Weald and into the capital's underworld was tired, careful, and—this was the part that troubled Corvane most—kind. He had seen it in the smugglers' quarter, when the boy had knelt in a gutter and worked magic openly over a stranger's daughter while inquisitors walked the street forty paces away. Compassion was not the behavior of heretics. Heretics were supposed to be hungry for something.

Corvane sat alone in the scriptorium of the Inquisitors' Hall, past midnight, with the informant's confession spread before him and no idea what to do with it.

* * *

The informant's name was Doss Merrow, and he had sold the trio at the river bridge for thirty crowns and a pardon. Corvane had interrogated him twice. The first time had been procedure: names, faces, routes, the location of their safehouse in the tanners' warren. Doss had given all of it freely, weeping, grateful, eager to be useful. It was the second interrogation that had unmade Corvane's week.

He had gone back because of a discrepancy. Doss claimed he'd sold them out of fear—that the ravensworn boy had threatened his daughter. But every witness in the tanners' warren told the same story: the boy had saved the girl. Had spent an hour kneeling in filth, weaving light through her hair, restoring memories the tithe had eaten, while the whole street watched and did nothing to stop him. A wanted man, working outlawed magic in the open, to give a child back her mother's face.

So Corvane had returned to Doss's cell and asked him, gently, why he had lied about the threat.

And Doss had looked up at him with hollow eyes and said: "Inquisitor, do you remember your mother?"

Corvane had answered without thinking. "Of course."

"Her name?"

"Elenna." Then, slower: "Why does that matter?"

Doss had smiled, and it was the worst thing Corvane had ever seen on a living face. "I asked my daughter that question last month. She looked at me like I'd spoken in tongues. The tithe collectors came through our district two seasons ago, inquisitor. They took a census. They measured our houses. And ever since, pieces of us have been going missing. My wife forgot how to make bread. My neighbor forgot his son's name and grieved for a child he still had. And now my daughter is forgetting me, and the Crown tells us this is prosperity." He had leaned forward, chains clinking. "You wear the king's badge. Tell me—when did you last see the king? Not his procession. Him. His eyes."

Corvane had ended the interrogation there. He had signed the release order himself—an irregularity he would answer for later—and walked out into the rain, and stood in the courtyard of the Hall for a long time, letting the water run down his collar, asking himself a question he had forbidden himself to ask for twenty years:

*What if the faith is the lie?*

* * *

He had been nine years old when the king's procession came through his village, and he remembered it with the perfect clarity of a founding memory. A thin figure in gold, riding slowly, one hand raised in blessing. The drought had broken three days later. Corvane's father had wept and enrolled his son in the Church's service before the season turned. *The king keeps us,* his father had said, every night, over supper, like a prayer. *The king remembers us so we don't have to carry it ourselves.*

That was the doctrine, distilled. The Crown bore the kingdom's memory so the kingdom could be free. Every tithe collected, every census taken, every charm confiscated—it was all stewardship. Burden-bearing. The king carried everything so that farmers could farm and smiths could smith and children need never know grief they couldn't survive.

Corvane had built his entire life inside that sentence.

And now a smuggler's confidant, a disgraced archivist's scattered pages, and a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice were pulling threads from it, and the garment was starting to look, in the midnight dark of the scriptorium, very much like it had been woven by something starving.

Because here was the arithmetic Corvane could not escape. He kept the Hall's own records. He knew the tithe schedules. Twenty years ago, the memory-tithes had been gathered once a generation, ceremonially, from volunteers. Ten years ago, quarterly. Now monthly, district by district, and the districts were going quiet—not peaceful, *quiet*, the quiet of rooms where someone has stopped speaking. He had filed those reports himself. He had called the fading *contentment*.

He had called it contentment because the alternative was unthinkable, and Corvane was beginning to understand that he had spent his whole life making things unthinkable so he would never have to think them.

* * *

He found them on the fourth night, or perhaps they let themselves be found—he was no longer certain which, and the uncertainty itself felt like a symptom.

They were sheltering in the undercroft of a deconsecrated chapel in the bell-founders' quarter, a place the Hall had written off years ago. Corvane went alone. No escort, no warrant party, no crossbows. Just his badge in his pocket instead of on his chest, which was either humility or cowardice, and he genuinely could not tell the difference anymore.

The archivist saw him first. Tam—the burned man, the exile—came up off his cot with a knife already in his fist, and Corvane noted distantly that the man's hands shook badly and his eyes were worse, flickering toward sounds that weren't there. The memory-charm eating him alive, if the rumors were true. A man being consumed from the inside by his own conscience.

"Easy," Corvane said, and raised both palms. "I'm not armed."

"You're always armed," the woman said. Bryn. She stepped out of the shadow of the collapsed stairwell with a blade low and ready, and her eyes were the coldest thing in the room. "You just prefer the kind you don't have to hold."

"I deserve that."

"You deserve a lot more than that." But she didn't come closer, and she didn't call for the boy, and Corvane understood he was being weighed.

Then Ivo Ravensworth came down the steps.

Up close, without the distance of pursuit, the boy looked younger than his file and older than his years. There were shadows under his eyes like bruises, and his hands—Corvane noticed the hands immediately, trained to notice—had a fine tremor that hadn't been there at the bridge. Whatever he was carrying, it was getting heavier.

"Inquisitor Corvane," Ivo said quietly. "Come to finish it?"

"No." And here it was, the moment his whole life narrowed to. Corvane took his badge from his pocket, looked at it—the crowned sun, the sigil he had kissed at his ordination—and set it on the crate between them. "I've come to ask you a question, and I need you to answer it as honestly as you've ever done anything in your life."

Bryn laughed, short and hard. "He wants honesty. From a liar."

"I know exactly what he is," Corvane said, not looking away from Ivo. "I've read his file. I wrote half of it. I also watched him spend an hour giving a child back her mother's face while my colleagues patrolled the street. So yes. From him. Because everyone else in my life has stopped being able to answer questions, and I need one person left in the world who can."

Silence. Somewhere above, a bell foundry tapped out the night shift, slow as a heartbeat.

"What's your question?" Ivo said.

Corvane made himself say it. Made himself stand in front of the heretic and the vengeance-sworn woman and ask the question that had been eating him since a chained man smiled at him in a cell.

"Can the king be saved?"

* * *

The silence after it went on long enough that Corvane heard his own pulse.

"The man wearing the crown," Ivo said at last, carefully. "Or the thing that's wearing him?"

"They're the same thing. That's doctrine."

"That's the lie." Ivo crossed the room slowly and sat on the crate's edge, close enough that Corvane could see the exhaustion carved into him. "Your doctrine says the king carries the kingdom's memory. That's true, in the way a tumor carries blood. The Hollow Crown isn't preserving anything, inquisitor. It's *eating*. It ate the real king years ago—I don't know how many; Tam would know. What's on the throne now wears his face and moves his mouth, and behind the eyes there's nothing but hunger dressed up as duty. And it doesn't stop with him. Every tithe, every census, every memory your Hall collects in his name—it feeds. The villages going quiet aren't content. They're being *consumed*."

"I've seen the reports," Corvane said. His voice came out flat. "I've filed them."

"Then you've known longer than I have."

"I called it contentment." The words tasted like ash. "Twenty years of reports. I called the emptying of the eastern districts *contentment*."

Tam spoke from his corner, hoarse. "The archive had a term for it. Before I was exiled. *Graduated attrition.* The Crown's own clerks coined it. They knew what the tithes were doing. They wrote it down in ledger-hand and locked it away, and the ones who read it too closely—" he touched his own temple, where the skin had gone grey—"were burned out or burned."

Corvane looked at the ruined archivist and felt something in his chest shift, some load-bearing wall developing a crack. "You hid pages."

"I hid pages inside my own head, and they're killing me, and I'll give them to you if you want them, though they may be the last coherent thing I ever do." Tam's laugh was dry as old vellum. "That's the price of knowing, inquisitor. You've started paying it already. I can see it."

"Then tell me the rest," Corvane said. "All of it. If I'm going to lose my faith, I'll do it with full documentation."

And they gave it to him. Bryn told him about the village that had forgotten its own name, about children who couldn't recognize their mothers. Tam told him about the binders, the original forging, the schism—how the Crown had been made to cage a tyrant and had learned instead to wear kings. And Ivo told him about the waystones, and the memories that poured out of them, and the first ravensworn binder's dying regret, and when he reached the end of it—*we should have carried it together, not alone*—Corvane found he had sat down without noticing, on a crate, in a deconsecrated chapel, like a man whose legs had finally confessed what his pride wouldn't.

"There's a rite," Ivo finished quietly. "Three waystones opened in sequence, and a willing ravensworn at the throne. I'm the last one. I can unbind it. The king dies free—or whatever's left of him does—but the kingdom gets its memory back. All of it. Everything the Crown has eaten comes home."

"And the cost?" Corvane asked, because he was an inquisitor, and inquisitors knew that every rite had a cost line item, and honest people put it in writing.

Ivo met his eyes. "Me. Mostly."

* * *

Corvane walked the foundry quarter until dawn.

He did not decide anything. That was the strange part. He had expected decision to feel like a door closing, a clean pivot, the crisp certainty that had carried him through twenty years of service. Instead he felt like a man standing in a flooded field, watching the water level drop and reveal that the ground he'd farmed his whole life was not ground at all but the roof of something vast and hollow underneath.

His father's voice: *the king keeps us.*

Doss Merrow's voice: *tell me—when did you last see the king?*

And beneath both, older than either, the memory he had never let himself examine: himself at nineteen, newly ordained, brought into the presence chamber to be blessed. The king's hand on his head. The king's smile. And the single instant—one heartbeat, no more—when the smile had *stopped*, mid-curve, like a clock skipping a tick, and the eyes above it had fixed on Corvane with an attention that felt less like regard and more like appetite. Then the smile resumed, seamless, and everyone knelt, and Corvane had knelt too, and had spent twenty years telling himself he had imagined it.

He had not imagined it.

He thought about the ordination oath. *To guard the king's peace, and the king's person, unto death.* He had always assumed the second clause meant from assassins. Standing in the grey light outside the foundry walls, Corvane understood, with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own sentence correctly for the first time, that the oath had never specified *from whom*.

The king's person needed guarding from the thing wearing it.

At first light he went back to the chapel. They were awake—none of them seemed to sleep properly anymore—and Bryn watched him come down the stairs with her hand near her knife, and Tam watched him with the flickering attention of a man losing his own library room by room, and Ivo simply watched him, waiting, the way you wait on a verdict.

"I can get you into the palace," Corvane said. "Not the front ways. The undercroft access codes are keyed to senior inquisitors—mine will work until tomorrow night, when the rotation changes. After that, you're on your own." He paused. "There are wards keyed to royal blood below. Tam will know. And there are men down there—my brothers—who will kill you on sight, and I will not be able to stop all of them."

"Why?" Bryn said flatly. "Why turn? Give me one reason I shouldn't assume this is a trap."

Corvane considered the question honestly, because she deserved that.

"Because I kissed that badge at nineteen," he said, "and I meant it. Every word. Guard the king's person unto death." He looked at Ivo. "I'm keeping the oath. That's all this is. The rest of it—the Hall, the doctrine, the thing on the throne—they're the ones who broke faith first. I'm just the last one still holding my end of it."

Nobody spoke. Then Ivo extended his hand across the crate, palm open, and Corvane looked at it for a long moment—at the tremor in it, at the weight the boy was already carrying, at the burden he intended to take onto himself alone because apparently that was the ravensworn inheritance too—and took it.

"One condition," Corvane said. "When this is done—if any of us is left—you write down what happened. All of it. Names, dates, the villages, the rite. Full documentation. The Hall survives on records; it can die by them too."

"He'll write it," Bryn said. "I'll make sure."

"Good." Corvane retrieved his badge from the crate and, deliberately, in front of all three of them, snapped the pin and dropped the crowned sun into the foundry dust between his feet. It made almost no sound. Twenty years, and it sounded like almost nothing.

"The rotation changes tomorrow night," he said. "We go tonight."
