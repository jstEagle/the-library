## 27. Into the Undercroft

The access codes came at midnight, delivered the way Corvane did everything now—without apology and without warmth.

He met them in the smugglers' cellar beneath the Saltgate tannery, still wearing his inquisitor's gray, which should have been a death sentence on its own. The warrant with Ivo's face was pasted to every gatehouse in the capital. Corvane had walked past three of them to get here.

"Four wards between the servants' stair and the vault," he said, laying a strip of parchment on the barrelhead. "Three are keyed to royal blood. The fourth is keyed to me."

"You're going to walk us into the heart of the palace you've spent your life defending," Bryn said. It wasn't a question. She sat with her boots up, a whetstone moving along her knife in slow, even strokes, as if this were any other night.

"I'm going to stand at the door while you do it," Corvane said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes." He looked up, and Ivo saw again what he'd seen at the third waystone—the man's faith not broken but bent, like a bow drawn past its limit. "I can still unmake this choice. Remember that when you're down there. Whatever you find, whatever it offers you—remember that I chose to open the door."

* * *

They went in through the laundry courts before dawn, four figures among fifty servants hauling water, because the Crown's attention, Tam had explained for the hundredth time, was a searchlight and not a lantern—it swept where it expected trouble and ignored the predictable.

"Predictable is our whole religion now," Bryn muttered, hunching under a basket of linens.

Ivo had spent five years being predictable. He knew the craft of invisibility the way Tam knew catalogues: exhaustively, obsessively, out of terror. But walking through the palace gates with a stolen livery pin and Corvane's seal pressed flat against his palm felt different from hiding in his cellar shop. Hiding had been small. This was a held breath the length of a courtyard.

The servants' stair descended farther than any stair had a right to. Torches burned in brackets every ten steps, and none of them guttered, and that was wrong—Ivo felt it in his teeth, the way you feel a note held too long. The flame wasn't burning. It was *being made to burn*, fed by something below.

"The wards begin at the bottom," Corvane said quietly. He had insisted on coming this far. Beyond this point, he said, his orders were to report an intrusion, and he wanted the distance between himself and them to be honest distance. "The first one will taste your blood whether you bleed or not. It reads intent. Walk slowly. Want nothing."

"That's the hardest thing anyone's asked of me all year," Bryn said.

The stair ended in a landing where the air changed texture—thicker, older, like stepping from a house into a cave that had been furnished by someone who hated caves. The corridor beyond was black marble veined with white, and the veins moved when Ivo wasn't looking directly at them.

Corvane stopped. "Here." He drew his belt knife, hesitated—one heartbeat, two—and then opened his own thumb and pressed it to a recessed plate of pale stone.

The stone drank. Light ran outward from the touch like frost forming in reverse, tracing lines across floor and ceiling until they resolved into words no living language used anymore. Tam made a sound low in his throat.

"First-binding era script," he breathed. "*By the blood that binds, the door that binds.* They built the wards out of the same grammar as the Crown itself. Everything down here is one sentence, Ivo. The palace, the vault, the king. One very long sentence with no full stop."

"And we're going to be the period," Bryn said.

They passed the first ward without stopping. Ivo felt it pass over him like cold water—a searching pressure, patient and total, reading the shape of his wanting. He thought about nothing. He thought about Maren's kitchen. He thought about tea going cold. The pressure withdrew, satisfied or indifferent.

The second ward was not so easily fooled.

* * *

It stood across the corridor at waist height: a line of silver light so thin it looked like a scratch on the air, humming at a frequency Ivo felt behind his eyes. Royal blood, Corvane had said. Keyed to the line of Caerlum itself.

"We don't have royal blood," Bryn whispered.

"No," Tam said. His face had gone gray. "But we have ravensworn blood, and the binders were granted ward-rights in perpetuity—they wrote themselves into the security, back when they trusted their own creation. Which means—" He swallowed. "Which means the ward will recognize Ivo. And it will also announce him. Every ward between here and the vault will know a ravensworn has entered, and it will tell the Crown."

Silence. The silver line hummed.

"So it's a bell," Bryn said. "We're ringing a bell the whole way down."

"It's worse than a bell," Tam said. "It's a confession. Once we cross, the Crown knows exactly what we are and exactly where we're going. There is no more stealth after this step. There is only speed."

Ivo looked at the line of light. Five years ago he would have turned around. Three months ago, on the road through the Weald, watching wolves circle in the dark, he had already turned around once in his life too many.

"Then we run," he said, and stepped through.

The line parted around him like water around a keel. Light bloomed—not silver now but deep raven-black shot with gold—and somewhere far above them, so far it might have been imagination, Ivo felt something vast and old turn its attention downward. The temperature dropped. The torches along the corridor burned black for a single instant, all of them together, then resumed their false cheerfulness.

"It knows," Tam whispered.

"Run," said Bryn.

* * *

They ran.

The undercroft unfolded beneath the palace in tiers, each older than the last, architecture arguing with itself across centuries—foundation stones from the first city, then vaulted halls in the style of the tyrant-kings, then narrow passages cut in panic during wars whose names Tam recited as they passed their murals. The wards came faster now. Corvane's codes opened the third; the fourth tried to kill them.

It was a corridor of mirrors—or near-mirrors, dark glass panels that showed not reflections but intentions. Ivo saw himself in them, but each panel showed a different version: the boy who fled Maren's cottage, the shopkeeper counting coins with the shutters barred, the man at Sera's side doing nothing while she bled. He caught himself reaching toward the glass and Bryn hauled him back by the collar.

"They show you your failures," she shouted over the rising hum. "Keep running!"

Tam was slower. Tam had been slower since Vessany, since the extraction of his hidden pages had left something scraped raw inside him, and the altitude of his knowledge couldn't compensate forever. At the fifth ward he stumbled against the wall and his palm came away red—he'd been gripping a decorative spike of iron hard enough to open it, and the ward drank the blood greedily and let them pass, and Ivo understood with a sick lurch that some part of the undercroft *wanted* them bleeding.

"It's feeding," he gasped. "The wards feed on blood. That's what they're for. Binding magic runs on—"

"On sacrifice. Yes." Tam wiped his hand on his coat without looking at it. "The founders were very clear in the marginalia. Very proud of it, actually. 'The kingdom's safety is written in its blood.' I catalogued the phrase when I was twenty-three and thought it was poetry." His laugh was terrible. "I was twenty-three and I thought everything was poetry."

Bryn took Tam's arm and half-carried him. Ivo watched her do it and felt the strange gravity of the last weeks compress into something simple: these people. These specific, impossible people, bleeding and swearing and running beside him into the dark. He had built a life around needing no one, and now he could not imagine the next corridor without them in it.

The thought frightened him more than the wards.

* * *

They reached the antechamber as the false dawn must have been graying the city above—a round hall floored in the same moving-veined marble, domed, silent, dominated by a single door.

It was tall enough for a giant and made of a metal Ivo had no name for, dark and faintly warm-looking, and carved across its whole surface in relief: a crown. Not the Hollow Crown itself—an image of it, rendered enormous, its points branching like a tree, like roots, like veins. The carving was worn smooth at the center, at the place where a brow would sit beneath it, as if countless hands had touched it in worship.

Or as if it had been touched from the inside.

"The vault," Corvane said. He had followed them after all. Ivo didn't ask how he'd gotten past the fourth ward; the man's thumb was wrapped in fresh linen, and his expression said he'd stopped keeping accounts of his own betrayals. "Beyond that door is the binding seat. The old kings kept the regalia there. The current—" He stopped. Started again. "It is there."

"How do we open it?" Bryn had her knife out, which was useless and steadying, and everyone pretended not to notice.

"You don't," said a voice from the dome above. "It opens."

Light gathered in the center of the ceiling—not torchlight, not day, but a slow condensation, like dew forming on darkness. Ivo's skin prickled. Every ward they'd crossed, every drop of blood spilled on the marble below, all of it had been payment, he realized. Toll collected in advance.

And now the door was being unlocked from the other side.

"Get behind the pillars," Tam rasped. "If it comes out, don't look at its—"

The door swung inward without sound.

Cold air rolled out, carrying a smell Ivo knew from his worst years with Maren—the smell of deep earth and old copper, of things preserved. Beyond the threshold: a descending gallery lit by that same black-burning torchlight, and at its end, impossibly far down, a glint of gold.

The Crown was not in a case. It was not guarded.

It was waiting.

"Three waystones opened," Tam whispered, clutching his catalogue against his chest like a shield. "Two done. A willing ravensworn at the throne. That's the rite. Everything we need is down those stairs—and so is everything that wants to stop us."

From the depths of the gallery, soft as a father waking a child, came a voice that was not a voice at all, arriving directly behind Ivo's eyes:

***You brought friends this time. How wise.***

*I hope,* it added, almost gently, ***they survive learning what you'll have to become.***
