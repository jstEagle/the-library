## 31. Siege of the Vault

**Corvane**

He heard them coming before he saw them—the measured tread of boots on undercroft stone, forty men moving in the cadence of the inquisition's drill. Corvane knew that cadence. He had drilled it into half of them himself.

"They're early," Tam said, pressing a shaking hand against the wall of the third waystone's chamber. His fingers came away red. The extraction of his hidden pages had cost him more than he'd admitted. "The wards should have bought us another hour."

"The wards were keyed to royal blood," Corvane said. "Someone gave them mine."

Bryn was already at the door, ear pressed to the iron, crossbow level. "How many?"

"Forty," Corvane said. "Perhaps fifty."

"And how many will you kill?" she asked. She didn't look at him when she said it. That was worse than if she had.

Corvane didn't answer. He stood in the vault antechamber with Ivo Ravensworth at his shoulder and felt the whole architecture of his life groan like a ship taking water. Thirty years in the white-and-grey. He had sworn the oaths at nineteen with his father's hand on his head—*the Crown is the kingdom, the kingdom is eternal*—and he had believed it the way you believe in stone, without ever thinking to ask what the stone was made of.

Then he had looked into the king's eyes and seen something looking back that was not a king.

"They'll offer terms first," he said instead. "Voss commands them. He'll want me alive."

"You're sure?" Ivo asked.

"I trained him." Corvane almost smiled. "He'll want me alive so he can be the one who reports my death."

The first blow struck the outer door—a deep, resonant boom that shivered dust from the ceiling. Old magic in the stones moaned in response, the palace itself protesting the violation of its own foundations.

Ivo turned to the waystone they had opened, its light still guttering in the floor like a drowned star. "How long do you need to finish the rite?"

"I don't know." Ivo's voice was steady, but Corvane had learned to read the tremor underneath it. "An hour. Less, if the Crown doesn't fight."

"It will fight," Tam said. He slid down the wall until he was sitting, his ledger open across his knees, writing even now—even now, the archivist, cataloguing his own death. "It has been fighting for three hundred years. It is very good at it."

Another boom. The door held, but the hinges screamed.

"Bryn," Corvane said. "With me. We hold the antechamber. Ivo—stone. Tam—"

"I know where I'm needed." Tam smiled, grey-lipped. "I've been needed here for twenty years. It's only now anyone's come to collect."

Corvane wanted to say something to that. He found he had nothing. He drew his sword instead—the inquisition's blade, sun-steel, blessed by the very church whose king was a hollow thing—and walked toward the door with Bryn falling into step beside him as naturally as if they had fought together all their lives.

They hadn't. They had tried to kill each other twice. But war makes strange kinships faster than peace ever could.

The door burst inward on the fourth blow.

* * *

**Bryn**

They came through the smoke in white-and-grey, shields locked, and Bryn put her first bolt through the eye-slit of the second man before her mind caught up with her hands.

She had fought soldiers before. These weren't soldiers. They moved like a single animal, forty bodies sharing one purpose, and the purpose was to reach the chamber behind her and stop whatever Ivo was doing in there. Behind her, through the broken doorway, she could hear him chanting in a language that made her teeth ache, and beneath the chanting, faintly, something else—a sound like a great bell rung underwater.

"Hold the line!" Voss's voice, from the rear. "Take the traitor alive! The others—"

She stopped listening. Crossbow up, bolt, drop, reload behind the pillar. Corvane fought beside her like a man possessed—not wildly; precisely, terribly, each cut placed where it would end a fight fastest. He had taught these men. He knew every opening they had because he had built them.

"Inquisitor!" one of them shouted, grief and fury in equal measure. "Inquisitor, what are you doing?"

Corvane cut him down anyway. Bryn saw his face as he did it. There was no pleasure in it, no rage. Just a man paying a bill he had known was coming.

A shield wall formed at the breach, six men deep, and began to grind forward. Bryn's quiver was half gone. Her arms burned. A blade found her forearm, opened it to the bone, and she killed its owner with the crossbow's butt and kept moving because stopping meant dying.

"We can't hold this," she shouted to Corvane.

"We don't have to hold it." He was bleeding from a cut above his eye, blood sheeting into his vision, and he wiped it away with the back of his sword-hand like an inconvenience. "We have to make it expensive. Every minute they spend on us is a minute the Crown spends screaming."

She glanced back once—Ivo in the stone-light, arms raised, hair lifting as though in a wind that wasn't there—and understood. This was the shape of it. The scholar bled knowledge, the witch bled memory, and she and Corvane bled time.

A grenade of some kind went off near the far pillar—white fire, ward-breaker, the inquisition's own arsenal turned on its own halls. The blast threw her across the antechamber. She hit the wall, tasted copper, and came up on one knee with her vision swimming.

Through the smoke: Voss, advancing past his fallen men, sword drawn, face pale with something worse than anger.

"Corvane." His voice cracked across the room. "Brother. Stand down. Whatever they've told you, whatever lies the ravensworn has fed you—it can be forgiven. The Crown forgives. The Crown *always* forgives."

And Corvane, standing over the bodies of men he had eaten with and prayed with and bled beside for thirty years, said:

"The Crown isn't the king, Voss. I've seen its face. It's a hunger wearing our faith like a coat." He raised his sun-steel blade. "I'm sorry. I should have seen it decades ago."

Voss charged, and Bryn shot him through the shoulder, and then there was no more talking, only the terrible arithmetic of steel.

* * *

**Tam**

Tam had died before, in a manner of speaking. He had died the day they burned his archive and struck his name from the rolls, and everything since had been a man walking around in the aftermath. So when the bolt took him—low, through the body, a stray shot from a panicked loyalist who would never know what he'd done—he felt less fear than recognition.

*Ah,* he thought. *There it is.*

He had crawled, or been dragged—he couldn't remember which—to the base of the waystone, where the light fell strongest, and he had his ledger open across his thighs because his hands refused to stop. Twenty years of exile, and the habit of record outlived the man.

Around him the battle roared and faded, roared and faded, like weather. He watched Bryn take a wound and keep fighting. He watched Corvane turn his blade against his own order with the efficiency of a man dismantling a machine he no longer believed in. He watched Ivo, deep in the rite now, sweat pouring down a face lit gold and shadow, and thought: *he looks like his grandmother did, in the illumination on folio 114. The binder at her work. We never knew her name. Perhaps we will again.*

A loyalist broke through—young, barely bearded, sword shaking—and stood over Tam with no idea what to do with him.

"The books," Tam said. His voice came out wet and thin. "In the archive below the chapel at Vessany. Third shelf, false back. Everything I stole. Everything I remembered. It's yours now, boy. Read it."

The young man stared at him, sword trembling.

"Read it," Tam said again, "and then decide who you serve."

The boy fled. Tam supposed that counted as a victory. He wrote it down.

The pain arrived properly then, and with it a strange clarity, the way pain sometimes clears a room. He thought about Sera, whom he had never met, who had carried the map through a city full of knives because someone had to. He thought about pages burned, names forgotten, villages that had lost themselves. He thought about the catalogue—the great work, the life's work, the record of everything the Crown had eaten—and realized with a calm that surprised him that it would not survive him unless it lived in other people now.

That had always been the point. He had simply been too proud, too long, to see that a book is not paper. A book is what remains when people choose to remember together.

Footsteps. Bryn, kneeling over him, pressing hands against the wound, shouting for something—for help, for Maren's charms, for a mercy none of them carried.

"Don't waste it on me," he told her. "There's a flask in my left coat pocket. Give it to Ivo when the rite falters. It will falter. Binding-magic always asks for more than the binder has."

"You're going to be fine," Bryn said, in the voice people use when they are lying to the dying.

"Naturally." Tam smiled. His vision was going soft at the edges, candlelight through vellum. "But write it down anyway. All of it. Memory survives us if we share it. See that it shares."

The bell-under-water sound was growing louder now. Or perhaps that was just the last of him, ringing.

* * *

**Ivo**

He felt every death in the antechamber like a plucked string.

That was the rite's cruelty—or its honesty. To unbind the Crown, he had opened himself to the binding-web that laced the kingdom, and the web carried everything: the waystones' hum, the Crown's vast slow hunger, and now, close and bright and unbearable, the lives of the people holding the door for him. Bryn's fury. Corvane's grief. Tam—

Tam's light was guttering. Ivo felt it go down like a lamp in a window he had been watching all night.

*Not yet.* He gripped the flask of Maren's truth-charm in his left hand and drove the rite deeper with his right. *Hold. Hold.*

The Crown rose to meet him.

It did not attack. That was the horror of it. It simply showed him things—Caerlum in golden age, streets singing, magic legal and loved—and threaded through the vision, soft as a lover's whisper: *this is what I remember. This is what I can give back. Stop this, little ravensworn, and I will restore every stolen name. Even your friend's. Even the dead archivist's. I am generous. I have always been generous.*

"Liar," Ivo said through his teeth.

*I am also patient,* it said, and the vision became the antechamber—Bryn dead, Corvane broken, the vault sealed for another century, another ravensworn born too late. *Choose.*

The outer doors of the rite-chamber boomed. Once. Twice. The wards were failing. Through the widening crack in his concentration Ivo saw white-and-grey figures pouring in, saw Bryn and Corvane fall back toward him, bleeding, backs to the stone, and understood with terrible simplicity that the choice the Crown offered was no choice at all, because both roads led through the same door.

The door broke.

And Corvane stepped into the gap—not the inquisitor, not anymore, but a man in a shattered white coat with a borrowed cause and nothing left to lose—raised his sun-steel sword, and planted himself between the Crown's servants and the last ravensworn.

"For the kingdom," Corvane said, and his voice did not shake at all. "The real one."

The siege crashed down upon them like a wave upon a rock, and somewhere beneath the noise, beneath the pain, beneath the Crown's velvet voice, Ivo felt the third waystone answer—felt the old binding shift, and loosen, and begin, at last, to come apart.

One stone left to open. One throne to reach.

And Tam's ledger, lying open in spreading red, waiting for someone to pick it up and carry it forward.
