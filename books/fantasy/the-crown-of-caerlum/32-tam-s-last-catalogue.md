## 32. Tam's Last Catalogue

Tam had spent his whole life believing that knowledge was the thing that saved you. He had believed it at nine, when he'd copied out his first ledger in a hand so careful the master archivists had laughed and said he wrote like a monk. He had believed it at twenty, when he'd been given keys to the deep stacks of the Royal Archive and told, *no one under forty sees these shelves, boy*. He had believed it at thirty-one, standing before the tribunal with ink still on his fingers, listening to them strip him of his title while he tried to explain — patiently, reasonably, as if reason had ever once stopped a blade — that the Crown was eating the kingdom's memory and someone ought to write it down.

He believed it now, lying on cold stone with Corvane's loyalists hammering at the vault door above, bleeding out from a wound he could feel with every heartbeat like a bell being rung inside his chest.

Knowledge hadn't saved him. But by all the drowned gods, it was going to be *useful* one last time.

"Told you," he said, and coughed, and was annoyed to find blood on his lips. "Told you both. Back on the road. I said archives burn and people forget and the only library that can't be looted is—" another cough, worse, wet— "is the one you carry in your skull."

"Stop talking," Bryn said.

She was kneeling over him, hands pressed hard against his stomach where the crossbow-adjacent shrapnel of the ward-trap had torn through him, and her hands were red to the wrist, and she was pressing anyway, because Bryn did not stop doing things just because they were hopeless. She pressed like she fought: with total commitment and no interest whatsoever in the odds.

"It's bad," Tam observed.

"It's *fine*."

"It's very bad. I've catalogued wounds. Occupational hazard of a misspent life. This is a category-four." He tried to smile. His face didn't quite cooperate. "You should know I'm using my last coherent minutes to be insufferable. It's what I would have wanted."

Above them, the vault door boomed. Dust sifted down through the light of Ivo's charm-lamps, drifting gold and slow, like the archive in autumn. Tam had always loved dust in lamplight. It had been the first beautiful thing he ever noticed, back in the reading room at seven years old, watching motes turn above the desks and thinking: *all of this was someone's knowing, once. Every book. Every page. Someone knew this, and now it sits here, waiting for the next knower.*

He had spent forty years being the next knower. It had cost him everything except the two people currently kneeling beside him, and one dead man upstairs choosing sides against his own brothers-in-arms.

"Ivo," Tam said.

Ivo crouched down. Up close, the man looked exactly like what he was: a trinket-seller who had never learned to stop flinching. Except for the eyes. The eyes had changed somewhere between the second waystone and the vault door. They were old now. Ravensworn old.

"The rite," Tam said. "The pages I pulled out of my own head — you have most of it. Three waystones opened in sequence. A willing ravensworn at the throne. But there's a final requirement. The part I kept back because—" he laughed, which hurt, everything hurt, fine, good, pain meant alive— "because I'm a coward, and because I hoped I'd misread it."

"Then tell us you misread it," Ivo said quietly.

"I didn't." Tam closed his eyes. Reciting was easy. Reciting was home. He let the words come up out of the place where he'd buried them five years ago, wrapped in a memory-charm around a stolen truth, and he spoke them clearly, in the cadence of the old catalogue entries, because form mattered, order mattered, and if he was going to die he was going to die *accurately*:

*"Item the last. The binding cannot be destroyed, only transferred or dispersed. A single bearer who takes it whole will hold it until death — and upon death, it passes to the nearest mind unguarded, and begins again. Therefore: the rite requires not one ravensworn but three hands. One to open. One to anchor. One to witness. The burden divided thrice is diluted; shared, it becomes survivable. Alone, no bearer has ever endured it. Alone, every bearer has become it."*

Silence. Even the pounding above seemed to pause, as if the door itself were listening.

"That's why they all went mad," Ivo said. His voice was very steady, which Tam recognized as the voice of a man holding something enormous very carefully. "The binders. My grandmother's line. They each tried to carry it alone."

"They were taught to. Pride dressed up as duty." Tam opened his eyes again. The lamps swam. He made himself focus on Ivo's face, because faces were easier than the spreading grey at the edges of his vision. "You'll need Bryn and Corvane. Literally need them. Their hands on yours at the throne. Don't be noble about it. Nobility is how the Crown wins. It *wants* a hero. Heroes are just bearers who refuse help."

"He's going to argue," Bryn said. "Look at his face. He's going to argue."

"I'm not going to argue," Ivo said.

"You're composing the argument right now."

"I'm — fine. Yes. Maybe." Ivo dragged a hand down his face. "Later. After we get out of this corridor."

*After.* Tam filed the word away with fondness. There was no after for him, and they all three knew it, and none of them had said it yet, and he decided — with the small, clear authority of a dying man — that he would be the one to say it, because euphemism was just bad cataloguing.

"Right," he said. "Let's discuss the estate."

"Don't," Bryn said.

"My estate consists of one satchel, forty years of notes, a forged letter of transit I never used, and a truly criminal quantity of guilt. The satchel goes to Ivo. The guilt—" he coughed— "the guilt I'm taking with me. Consider it my final act of selfishness."

The door boomed again. Closer, somehow. Or maybe that was just his heart, stuttering its way toward silence.

"There's something else," Tam said. "The important thing. Listen, because I won't say it twice; I don't have the breath budget." He gripped Ivo's sleeve. Ink-stained fingers on cheap wool. Forty years of scholarship ending in a cellar, holding onto a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice. The archive would have wept. Good thing the archive burned. "My catalogue. Not the notes — anyone can copy notes. The *catalogue*. Every entry I ever made, indexed, cross-referenced, memorized. Fourteen thousand entries. The names of every village that tithe-collectors emptied. The dates the ley-lines dimmed. The true regnal year of a king who died six years ago. I put it all in my head because paper burns, and I told myself: *someone will need this someday, and I will be there to give it.*"

"You're giving it now," Ivo said.

"I'm giving it now." Tam smiled, and this time his face cooperated, mostly. "But here's the trick, and pay attention, because it's the only clever thing I ever did. Memory isn't a possession. That's the lie the Crown runs on — that remembering is hoarding, that a mind is a vault. It isn't. It's a *hand*. You remember by giving it away. By telling it. By writing it. By saying your sister's name out loud in a tavern so a stranger carries it home to a village that forgot her." His grip tightened. "So take it. All fourteen thousand entries. Take them and *use* them, and when you use them, say where they came from. Say: *these are Tam's*. Not for glory. For continuity. A fact without a source is an orphan. I don't want orphans."

"How?" Ivo asked. "How do I take it?"

"The same way I hid the pages. A transfer-charm. Maren's truth-charm — look at it, really look, it's built on the same lattice. Reverse the flow. I speak; you receive. It will hurt you. Less than it's hurting me, which is the entire principle of the rite, so consider it a lesson delivered in advance." He was shaking now. He noted it, distantly, the way he noted everything: *subject trembling, cause blood loss, prognosis poor.* "Bryn. Your amulet-hand. Give me your hand."

She gave it. Her face was wrecked and furious and she held his hand like she was holding a door shut against the sea.

"Sera's seal," Tam said gently. "On the map. I saw it that first night and I thought: whoever carried that seal loved precision. A clean stamp. Even pressure. She cared about things being *right*." He squeezed Bryn's fingers. "Your sister died getting the map to the right person. That's not chance. That's craft. Grieve her properly, later, with wine and swearing. But know that she succeeded. Archivists respect nothing more than a job completed."

Bryn made a sound that wasn't a word and wasn't a sob but was, Tam thought, cataloguing even now, *grief, category: love, prognosis: survivable.*

"Now," Tam said. "Ivo. Take my hand. And listen, because I'm going to start, and once I start I can't stop, and I have perhaps enough voice left for the index and the critical entries. The rest will come across as impression — you'll dream them for weeks, waking with facts in your mouth like stones. Write them down as they come. All of them. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Say it properly. Witnesses present. Bryn, witness this."

"Witnessed," Bryn said, thick-voiced.

"I, Ivo Ravensworth," Tam prompted, "promise to preserve the catalogue of Tam of the Burned Archive—"

"I, Ivo Ravensworth, promise to preserve the catalogue of Tam of the Burned Archive. Every entry. With attribution."

"With attribution," Tam echoed, satisfied. "Good. Then hold still. This is going to feel like drowning in a library. Try to enjoy it. Most people never get the chance."

He found the lattice of Maren's charm in his mind — he'd studied it that night by the fire, of course he had, he studied everything, it was the only vice he'd ever had — and reversed it, and opened himself the way a book opens, spine cracking, pages fanning, and began to pour.

It came out of him like light through water. The names first: *Alderholt, taken in spring; Marrow's Ford, taken at harvest; the ferry-villages of the Vessany delta, all eleven, taken in a single season.* Then the dates, the ley-lines, the regnal reckoning, the true names of the first binders — *Maren-of-the-lines, no relation, ha; Aldous Ravensworn, who regretted; the nine who signed and the four who refused* — and beneath it all, the great slow sediment of everything else, fourteen thousand entries compressing into a river, and Tam felt himself growing lighter with each one, which was either the charm working or the dying, and he found he genuinely did not care which.

Because this was the thing. This was the thing he'd known since he was seven years old watching dust turn in lamplight: knowledge doesn't live in books. Books are just bodies. Knowledge lives in the passing-on. In the hand opening. And he had been so afraid, all these years, that he'd die with it all locked inside him, unread, uncited, orphaned —

— and instead he was dying in a cellar beneath a usurping palace, handing his life's work to a trinket-seller with ravens' blood, while a woman who fought like a landslide held his other hand, and somewhere above them an inquisitor was buying them time with a sword.

There were worse shelves to end up on.

"—entry thirteen thousand nine hundred and twelve," he heard himself recite, far away now, his voice a thread, "the Hollow Crown does not hate. Note for future researchers: it is not malicious. It is *hungry*, and it has confused hunger with love for so long that it no longer knows the difference. Recommend compassion in all dealings. Recommend also extreme caution. These recommendations are not contradictory—"

His voice failed.

The river stopped.

For a moment there was only the boom of the door, and Bryn's breathing, ragged, and Ivo's face above him, wet-eyed, full of borrowed facts already settling into their new shelves.

"Tam," Ivo said.

"Mm." He couldn't see well anymore. Just lamplight, and dust turning gold, and two shapes leaning over him like the covers of a book closing. "Almost done. Last entry. Unnumbered. Final."

"Tell me."

"Tell me *write it down*," Tam corrected, smiling. "That's the entry. That's the whole catalogue, boiled down. Everything I know, everything any of us know — write it down. Say it aloud. Teach it to someone annoying and young. Memory survives us if we share it. That's the entire secret. That's the rite underneath the rite." His hand found Ivo's sleeve one more time, or thought it did. "You were going to run, at the end. I watched you want to. Don't. Stay. Be *found*. It's the only entry that matters—"

The lamplight turned.

The dust hung, golden, unmoving, the way it hangs in the deep stacks when no one is breathing.

And then, distantly, through stone and iron and the fading hum of a transferred life, Tam of the Burned Archive heard — or imagined he heard, and he would have wanted the distinction noted, *source uncertain, verify before citing* — the vault door above give way, and Ivo Ravensworth stand up, and say in a voice like winter arriving:

"Bryn. Corvane. Take my hands. We finish this together."

* * *

They laid him in the archive's ruined reading room, on a table, with a lamp lit at his head, because Bryn said he'd have wanted proper lighting and Ivo said he'd have wanted a citation and they compromised on both. Corvane stood guard at the door and did not pray, having recently lost the habit, but stood straight, which was its own kind of prayer.

Above them, the palace shook with the sound of the Crown beginning, at long last, to be afraid.
