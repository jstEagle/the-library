## 33. Unbinding

The throne room of Caerlum had not been swept in years.

Ivo noticed that first—before the dark, before the cold, before the thing on the dais turned its ruined eyes toward him. Dust lay in the gutters between the flagstones like grey snow. Cobwebs hung from the banners of dead kings, thick as fishing nets. A kingdom was forgetting itself from the top down, and the first thing to go, apparently, was the broom.

"Charming place," Bryn said at his shoulder. Her voice was steady. Her hand on her knife was not.

"It used to be beautiful," Corvane said behind them. He sounded like a man describing a wound. "The glass alone. There were ten thousand panes once, each one a different saint. They took them down for the war." He paused. "Which war, I couldn't tell you anymore."

That was the horror of it, Ivo thought. Even Corvane—the truest believer the Crown had ever made—was starting to lose the thread of his own certainties. The vault doors stood open behind them because Corvane's access codes still worked, but the codes were all he had left. His faith had cracked in the undercroft like river ice, and now they were all standing on it, listening to it groan.

Three waystones hummed in their packs. Tam's pages lay folded against Ivo's chest, inked with a rite that required exactly what Tam had died to name: three stones opened in sequence, and a willing ravensworn at the throne.

Willing. Tam had underlined it twice.

The king sat on the throne like a coat hung on a hook.

That was the only way Ivo could describe it, and he hated how accurate it felt. The body was a man's body—tall, once broad, dressed in robes that might have been magnificent before the moths got at them. But nothing inside it moved the way a man moved. The head tilted too far when it tracked them. The fingers drummed against the armrest in a pattern that never varied, never tired, never quite matched any rhythm a living hand would choose. And the face—

The face was smiling. It had been smiling when they entered. It would be smiling when they left, or died, or succeeded. The smile belonged to no one.

"Ivo Ravensworth," the king said.

The voice came out layered, like two speakers an inch apart saying the same word slightly out of time. The room swallowed it. Somewhere above them, through forty feet of stone, bells were ringing the evening hour, and Ivo clung to that sound the way a drowning man clings to driftwood. Bells meant the city was still alive up there. Bells meant people.

"You know my name," Ivo said.

"We know many names." The drumming fingers stopped. "We have eaten most of them."

Bryn shifted beside him. "Talk less," she murmured. "Do more."

She was right. Every minute they stood here was a minute the Crown spent studying them—and Ivo had no doubt it was studying them, the way a spider studies the exact point where the web begins to tremble. He unslung his pack and drew out the waystones one by one: the first from the weald, moss-stained and warm as a sleeping bird; the second from beneath the shrine ruins, still carrying the scorch of Corvane's soldiers' torches; the third pried from its socket under the palace an hour ago, so heavy with old magic that his arms ached from carrying it.

He set them in a triangle before the dais, as the rite demanded. The moment the third stone touched the floor, all three began to sing.

Not sound, exactly. It was deeper than sound—a vibration in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the wet dark of the inner ear. The dust on the flagstones shivered into little dancing rings around each stone. Above them, the bells stumbled mid-peal, then found the same rhythm, as if the whole city were being tuned to a single chord.

The king rose from the throne.

It moved wrong. Of course it did. Joints bending in orders no spine had agreed to, robes hanging off a frame that seemed briefly unsure of its own dimensions. But it did not attack. It simply stood there, at the top of seven steps, looking down at Ivo with those ruined eyes, and waited.

*It wants me to come to it,* Ivo realized. *It can't leave the throne. That's why it wears a king—to be carried.*

"The rite," Corvane said quietly. He had drawn his sword—not pointed at anyone, just held, the way a man holds a rail in a storm. "Where does it begin?"

"With me." Ivo pulled off his gloves. His hands were shaking; he let them. Maren had taught him long ago that shaking hands channeled cleaner than clenched ones, that fear was just another current if you stopped pretending you weren't afraid. Five years of hiding that lesson, and here it was, coming home. "You two stand at the stones. When I start, the power will try to run—it'll look for anywhere to go but into me. You hold the circuit. Whatever you see, whatever you hear—" he met Bryn's eyes, then Corvane's, "—don't let go."

"And what will we see?" Corvane asked.

Ivo thought about the waystone memories. His grandmother's hands in molten gold. The schism. *We should have carried it together, not alone.* "Me," he said. "All of me. The parts I'd rather you didn't."

Bryn snorted. "I've seen you eat a boiled sheep's eye on a dare. How bad can it be?"

He almost laughed. That was the gift of her, he realized—she could put a joke in the mouth of the abyss and make the abyss blink first. He touched her shoulder, brief and hard, and then walked toward the steps.

The Crown watched him climb.

Up close, the king smelled of cedar and rot, incense burned over something older. Up close, the smile had texture—Ivo could see the strain in the lips, the actual physical effort of a dead man's mouth held in a shape it hadn't chosen. And the eyes—

The eyes were sorry.

That was the worst discovery yet. In the vault, touching the Crown's edge, Ivo had met its consciousness and found it starving and sorrowful, convinced it was saving the kingdom by eating it. He had pitied it then. He pitied it now, looking into those eyes, and he knew the pity was a trap. The Crown had worn kings and queens and saints. It knew precisely how to be pitied.

"You are the last," the king said. The doubled voice softened, or seemed to. "The last of the hands that made us. We have waited so long for you, Ivo Ravensworth. We kept your kingdom safe while you hid in cellars selling luck to fishwives."

"I know what you think you did."

"We know what we did." Something flickered across the ruined face—pride, maybe, or hunger wearing pride's clothes. "Every memory returned to a grieving mother. Every name given back to the forgotten. We are the only reason this kingdom remembers itself at all."

"And every memory you keep is a memory someone lost." Ivo stopped on the sixth step, close enough to touch the dead king's chest, and made himself say the rest. "You don't preserve anything. You just move it from the people who earned it into a box that will never use it. That's not keeping. That's theft with good manners."

For a moment the smile stayed perfectly fixed.

Then the king's head cocked, birdlike, and the temperature of the room dropped ten degrees, and the Crown stopped pretending to be a man.

"Then take it from us," it said, and every banner in the hall snapped taut though there was no wind. "Take our burden, heir of binders. Open your hands."

Ivo opened his hands.

* * *

The rite began with a word Tam had transcribed phonetically from a language dead a thousand years—a word that tasted, when Ivo spoke it, of iron filings and his grandmother's kitchen. The waystones flared. Light ran between them in a triangle of white fire, and the circuit reached for him like a tide reaching for a swimmer, and he let it in.

The binding poured into him.

There was no other word for it. It was vast beyond measuring, ancient beyond counting, and it came into his body the way winter comes into a house—through every gap at once, patient, total, indifferent to what it froze. He felt it settle into his bones and begin to *weigh*. This was the burden Maren had named in her cottage, the cost spoken aloud: a ravensworn who took the Crown's binding took it into flesh, forever, or until he passed it on.

*I'm not passing it on,* he told the weight. *And I'm not wearing your crown either. I'm unwinding you, stitch by stitch, and you're going to give back everything you stole.*

The Crown answered by showing him his mother's face.

Not a memory he'd chosen. The real one—the last morning, the kitchen, the smell of burnt porridge, her saying *you'll be careful, won't you* in the tone that meant she already knew he wouldn't. He hadn't thought of that morning in fifteen years. He had buried it so deep he'd half convinced himself it never happened, and now it bloomed in him entire, unbearable, while the binding flowed through his chest and the white fire sang.

*This is the price,* the Crown whispered through the memory. *Everything you carry, we carry. Everything you buried, we dig up. Do you truly want it all, little heir?*

"Yes," Ivo said, through his mother's voice.

The Crown dug deeper.

Maren came next. Not the cottage reunion—worse. The night he left. He had rebuilt that night over five years into something almost noble: a young man striking out for freedom, escaping a mistress who asked too much. The Crown handed him the truth instead, in full color. Maren sitting at the table after he'd gone, his packed bag still by the door, her face not angry but *grieved*, saying to the empty room, "He could have been ready in another year. One more year and he'd have been ready." And Ivo, watching his own memory from outside himself, understood for the first time that she hadn't failed him. He had failed the year.

His knees buckled. The fire wavered.

"Hold!" Bryn's voice, raw, from somewhere below. She had both hands flat on the first waystone and the light was trying to tear free of her, whipping around her wrists like a live thing, and she was holding anyway, teeth bared, swearing steadily in harbor dialect. On the far side Corvane knelt at the third stone with his sword driven into the floorboards beside him for balance, and his lips were moving—prayer, Ivo realized dimly. The old prayers to the eternal king. Praying to the very thing they were killing, because prayer was the only shape his courage knew.

Ivo dragged himself upright. The binding surged, hungry for the cracks in him.

Sera came.

The cellar shop. Closing time. The door banging open and the courier stumbling in with blood sheeting down her side, pressing the map into his hands with fingers already cold. *The king is dead—find the ravensworn.* The weight of her against his shoulder as he lowered her to the floor. The terrible small silence after her breathing stopped. The way he had hidden her body among his own stock like she was inventory, because panic was the only language he spoke fluently, because five years of invisibility had taught him that the safest response to a human being in need was to make them disappear.

He had never forgiven himself for that. He had built the guilt a small dark room in his mind and locked it, and the Crown picked the lock in a heartbeat and threw the door wide.

*You let her die alone,* it said, gentle as a confessor. *You burned her rather than bury her. You ran from her sister with a blade at your throat. This is who you are, Ivo Ravensworth. A man who hides. You hid from us for thirty years. Why should we give the kingdom back to a coward?*

The fire guttered. The triangle of stones flickered like candles in wind. Somewhere far away Bryn screamed his name, and the sound arrived muffled, underwater, because the past was closing over him like water and every year he had hidden was rising up at once—the cellar, the alley, the false bottom in his pack, the fake laugh, the sleight of hand, the thousand small surrenders of a life built entirely around not being seen—

*Yes,* the Crown murmured, and its voice was almost tender. *Stay down there. Stay in the dark where you're comfortable. We will hold the kingdom's memory for you. We have been so patient. We can wait another thirty years.*

And Ivo, drowning in his own worst hours, felt the old reflex rise—the one that had kept him alive for five years, the one that said *hide, run, survive, be no one*—

—and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, he heard Tam's voice.

*Memory survives us if we share it.*

Tam was dead. Tam had died reciting a catalogue so his friends could finish what he started, and his last act on earth had been the opposite of hiding. And Sera—Sera had run bleeding through half the capital to reach a stranger's cellar, because dying men don't hide things, they *give* them. And Bryn was holding a waystone that was burning her alive and not letting go. And Corvane was praying to the enemy with tears on his face and not letting go.

They were all sharing it. All of it. The grief, the guilt, the weight.

Ivo opened his eyes inside the flood of memory and looked at his own worst self—the man in the cellar hiding a dying woman's body—and said, out loud, in the middle of the Crown's battlefield:

"You're right. That's who I was."

The memories faltered.

"But I'm not doing this alone anymore." He planted his feet on the seventh step, directly before the dead king, and seized the binding with both hands—metaphorically, actually, it hardly mattered which, because at this depth the distinction had dissolved. "You want to show me my past? Fine. Look at it closely. Look at what comes *after*."

He pushed.

He shoved five years of shame back down the Crown's own throat and kept pushing—Maren's cottage, Bryn's blade at his throat turning into her hand in his, the wolves on the weald road, the village that forgot itself, the small fires, the amulet mended stitch by stitch, the bridge where Bryn took a bolt meant for him, the letters signed by no one, every moment since Sera died in which he had stopped running—and the binding shrieked, a sound like a continent of hinges, because the Crown had spent centuries feeding on loneliness and it had never once been fed the cure.

The white fire roared back up to full strength. The triangle blazed. The dead king's smile finally, finally broke—not into anger, but into something naked and terrified, the expression of a thing that has just discovered it can end.

"What are you doing?" the Crown demanded, and for the first time the doubled voice was only one voice, cracking, singular, *afraid*. "What are you—stop. STOP—"

"No," Ivo said.

He pulled the first thread loose.

It felt like pulling a tooth the size of a cathedral. The binding tore along a seam laid down a thousand years ago by his grandmother's generation, and the whole throne room lurched—dust leaping from the floor, banners ripping free of their rods, the bells overhead ringing all at once in a chaos of joy or alarm, impossible to tell. Through the high windows, far across the sleeping city, lights were kindling in windows where no one remembered why they lived there. Memory, returning like weather.

The Crown convulsed. The king's body jerked against invisible strings, and the ruined eyes fixed on Ivo with something new burning in them—not sorrow, not hunger.

Hatred. Pure, ancient, cornered hatred.

"Clever," it hissed. "Clever little heir. But you have unwound one thread of ten thousand, and your friends cannot hold what comes next. No one can hold what comes next. You will drown, Ivo Ravensworth. You will drown in everything we ever ate, and your last breath will be a memory that isn't yours, and we will still be here—"

The second thread came loose.

The world went white. And somewhere in the whiteness, Ivo felt Bryn's hand find his—small, scarred, gripping hard enough to hurt—and heard her voice cut through the roar like a knife through canvas:

"I've got you. Now *pull*."
