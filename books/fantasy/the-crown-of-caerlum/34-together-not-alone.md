## 34. Together, Not Alone

**Ivo**

The Crown fought him the way a drowning man fights his rescuer.

He knelt on the dais steps with the broken king's hand in both of his, and he reached into the thing wearing Edran IV the way Maren had taught him to reach into a fevered body—gently, finding the heat first, following it to its source. The binding rose to meet him like black water rising in a well.

*Take it,* the Crown whispered. *You already carry so much. What is one more weight?*

It showed him things. It was good at showing him things—it had eaten ten thousand memories and knew exactly which shapes they made when you fed them back. Maren's face the morning he left, her door closing without a word. Sera's blood soaking through his shirt where she'd leaned against him and died. Every cellar night alone, every market day spent smiling at strangers who would have burned him if they'd known what he was. Five years of not being seen, offered back to him as if being seen had been the wound all along.

"Stop," Ivo said through his teeth. "I know what I am."

But knowing wasn't enough. The rite Tam had recited with his last breath required three stones opened, and they were open—Bryn had held the second, Corvane the third, and the waystones' power ran through the undercroft now like veins of light in the dark stone—but above all it required a ravensworn willing to take the binding in. Willing. He was willing. That was supposed to be enough.

It wasn't.

The binding poured into him and kept pouring, and there was more of it than any single body could hold. His grandmother had known that. The first binder's dying regret came up through the third waystone's memory like a bubble through tar: *we should have carried it together, not alone.* Ivo had thought he understood those words. He hadn't. Understanding them meant feeling them, and feeling them meant this—the binding filling him past fullness, past pain, past the place where a person ends, and still coming, an ocean funneling into a cup.

His bones hummed. His teeth tasted of iron and old snow. Somewhere very far away, Bryn was shouting his name.

*I can make it stop,* the Crown said, almost tender. *Let go, and I will carry us both. You need only wear me.*

"No," Ivo gasped—and then, because honesty was all he had left, "I can't hold it. I can't hold it alone."

*No one ever has,* said the Crown, and for one terrible instant it sounded less like a monster than like a child who had been told this from the beginning and never believed.

Then someone took his hand.

* * *

**Bryn**

She didn't think about it. Thinking was for people with time.

She'd watched Ivo kneel down into the light pouring off the waystones and start to come apart—not bleeding, nothing so simple; he was coming apart the way a rope frays, strand by strand, and the strands were going somewhere she couldn't follow. She had buried one sister already. She had stood at a deathbed once before and done nothing, because there had been nothing to do.

There was something to do now.

She crossed the dais in four strides, dropped to her knees beside him, and took his right hand in both of hers. His skin was cold as well-water and shaking hard enough to rattle.

"I've got you," she said, which was a lie, because she didn't have anything, she was just a woman with a sword and a grudge she'd set down somewhere around Vessany and never picked back up. But her hands remembered other work. They remembered holding Sera's when Sera was small and feverish, when their mother worked nights, when the only medicine was a sister's grip and a story told until dawn. *Hold on and don't let go.* She gripped Ivo's hand the way she'd gripped Sera's, and she said the only true thing available: "Whatever it's taking from you, it'll have to come through me first."

The binding noticed her.

It came through her like a river through a reed—fast, cold, scouring. She felt it pull at her memories and braced for the hollowing she'd seen in that forgotten village, but it didn't take. It *passed*. The grief for Sera went through her and came out the other side lighter, the way a stone worn smooth by water is still a stone but no longer cuts. For half a heartbeat she was inside something vast and starving and old, and it looked at her, and she looked back.

"You don't get her," Bryn told it. "You don't get any of them."

The pressure eased. Not gone—never gone—but shared now, split between two bodies, and Ivo sucked in a breath like a man surfacing, and his eyes found hers, and they were his own eyes again.

"It's not enough," he rasped. "Two isn't enough. There has to be—"

"There's me," said Corvane.

* * *

**Corvane**

Corvane had spent thirty years learning that faith meant standing firm. Knees bent, head bowed, unwavering before the altar of the eternal king. He had flogged men for wavering. He had signed warrants for worse.

Now he crossed the dais of the Hollow Court, past the throne where the king he had worshipped sat slack and dying, and knelt on the other side of a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice, and took his hand, and wavered—no. He corrected himself even as he did it. This was not wavering. This was the first time in thirty years his faith had pointed at something true.

"I do not forgive you," he said to Ivo, gripping the cold hand firmly, formally, as if sealing a document. "For the lies. For the running. None of it. Understand that."

"Noted," Ivo managed.

"But I have seen the king." Corvane's voice cracked, and he let it crack; there was no one left in this room to perform for. "I have looked into those eyes and seen nothing looking back. And if the Crown is not the king—if it only ever wore the king—then everything I did in its name was done for a hungry thing wearing a dead man's face. So." He tightened his grip. "Take my hand or don't, but I am not letting go."

The binding hit him like a wave hitting a seawall built by an honest mason—strong, and tested, and holding.

Through Corvane it moved differently than through Bryn. Where her grief passed through scoured smooth, his conviction passed through *burned clean*. He felt every warrant he'd signed, every family dragged into the questioning rooms, every village tithe-collector he'd escorted into places like the one that forgot its own name—and the binding tried to show him these things as shame, as weight, and instead he held each memory up and looked at it squarely and said, *yes, mine, I did that, and now I undo it.* The binding had no purchase on a man who refused to look away from himself. It slid off him like rain off oiled leather.

Three hands now. Three bodies. The ocean still poured, but it poured into a basin wide enough to hold it.

"It's working," Ivo breathed. "Keep—keep holding—"

The Crown screamed.

* * *

**Ivo**

It was not a sound. Sound would have been merciful. It was a tearing in the fabric of the undercroft itself, the scream of an ancient thing discovering, after centuries of eating, what it was to be eaten.

Because that was what they were doing. He understood it suddenly, wholly, the knowledge arriving through the shared channel of their joined hands: the unbinding rite was not destruction. It was digestion in reverse. Everything the Crown had taken—the kingdom's memories, its magic, its names, its songs, whole villages' worth of selves—it was giving back. Pouring out of itself through three mortal vessels and returning to the world it had starved.

And it was afraid. Gods, it was afraid. Ivo felt its fear as clearly as he'd felt its sorrow in the vault, and pity rose in him unwanted, enormous, awful.

"I know," he said to it, aloud, though Bryn and Corvane couldn't hear what he heard. "I know you thought you were saving us. You took everything so we wouldn't lose everything. You kept the kingdom in a jar so it couldn't break." His voice shook. "But a jarred kingdom is already broken. We were never yours to keep."

*Alone,* the Crown moaned. *You would have been alone.*

"We're not," Ivo said. "That's the whole point. That was always the whole point."

The binding surged out through the joined circuit of their hands—one line through Bryn's iron grip, one through Corvane's white-knuckled formal hold—and the Crown's edges began to fray. He saw it now with the sight his bloodline gave him, the sight his ancestors had used to forge this prison: the artifact was not gold at all, had never been gold. It was a knot. A binding-knot of immense age and immense hunger, tied around a dead king's skull, tied by binders who had loved their country too much to share the burden of saving it.

A knot could be untied.

"Tam," Ivo whispered. "Tell me how."

And Tam—who was lying two floors above them with a spear-wound in his chest and a lifetime of stolen pages in his head—answered anyway, because of course he did, because the man had memorized his own catalogue against exactly this hour, and some part of Ivo's frayed, flooded mind was still touching the waystone network, and the network still carried Tam's voice the way it carried everything:

*"Counterwise. From the crown-point, left over right, as the raven flies. Gently, boy. It was tied with love. Untie it with love or don't untie it at all."*

"With love," Ivo repeated. His laugh came out wet and broken. "Right."

He closed his eyes. He stopped fighting the memories flooding through him and started *thanking* them. Maren's closed door—he thanked it, because it had led him here, to this dais, to these hands in his. Sera's blood on his shirt—he thanked her, courier, stranger, sister-of-Bryn, for pressing a map into a coward's hands and trusting him with the end of the world. Five years of hiding—he thanked every invisible day, because invisibility had taught him exactly how much a person needs to be seen, and now he could give that seeing back to a whole kingdom.

Left over right. As the raven flies.

The first loop of the knot loosened.

Light came out of it—actual light, warm and golden and impossibly familiar, the color of every hearth-fire in every home the Crown had ever hollowed. It spilled across the dais, across the slumping king, across Bryn's astonished face and Corvane's tear-tracked one, and where it touched, things returned. Ivo felt them going out like birds released: a fishwife's luck-knot charm, given freely in a shop that smelled of tallow; a village remembering the name of its well; a hundred thousand mornings, birthdays, grudges, lullabies, streaming home to the places they'd been torn from.

The Crown's scream softened. Changed. Became something smaller, older, almost relieved.

*Together,* it whispered, in a voice like the last coals of a fire. *Oh. That is how it was meant—*

And it cracked.

Not shattered—cracked, the way ice cracks on a spring river, a long bright seam splitting it pole to pole, and through the seam the light poured faster and faster, and the thing inside it unfolded like a fist finally opening after years clenched.

* * *

**Bryn**

Later—hours later, though none of them would remember the hours clearly—she would try to describe the moment to herself and fail. Words like *explosion* and *collapse* were wrong. It was quieter than that. It was a knot coming undone, which is to say it was the sound of a long-held breath finally let out.

The Crown broke into pieces no larger than coins, and the pieces broke into dust, and the dust became light, and the light went everywhere at once, up through the vault ceiling, out through the palace stones, into the air of a city that had been forgetting itself for a decade.

The king breathed.

Just once—a real breath, deep and ragged, the breath of a man surfacing from a drowning that lasted years—and his eyes cleared. For a moment they were ordinary eyes, tired and human and frightened, and they found Ivo kneeling there with his hands still held by a swordswoman and an inquisitor, and the king's mouth moved.

"...free?" he whispered. Or maybe *thank you*. Maybe both. The words tangled together the way words do at the end.

Then the breath went out of him and did not come back, and Edran IV, last of his line, lay still on his own throne with his face at peace for the first time since anyone could remember.

Bryn realized she was crying. She realized she was still gripping Ivo's hand, and that his grip had gone slack, and that he'd toppled sideways into her shoulder, unconscious, breathing, alive. On the king's other side, Corvane knelt with his head bowed—not praying, she thought, or not only praying. Grieving. A man mourning the god he'd just helped kill, and grateful for it, and unable to say either thing aloud.

She understood that completely.

"Hold on," she told Ivo, shifting his weight, getting an arm around him. He weighed less than she expected. Runaways usually did. "You don't get to die after all that. I forbid it. I'm your jailer, remember? You're still my prisoner until I hear the truth about Sera, and you don't escape me twice."

He didn't answer, but his breathing was steady, and under her ear where his pulse beat against her arm it was slow and strong and *shared*, somehow—she could feel it, faintly, doubled and tripled, echoing in her own chest and, glancing up, she saw Corvane pressing his own wrist as if he felt it too.

One binding. Three bearers. Diluted down to something a person could live with.

"Look," she said softly, because Corvane was staring at the empty air above the throne, where the last of the light was going. "Look at it go."

Far above them, muffled by feet of stone and vaulted ceilings, the bells of the capital began to ring.

* * *

**Corvane**

They were ringing wrong, and then they weren't.

Corvane knew the capital's bells the way he knew his own heartbeat—which carillon struck the hours, which tolled executions, which had rung the coronation of Edran IV twenty-two years ago, a day he remembered with a clarity that had always frightened him, as if his own mind suspected it was keeping evidence.

The bells rang the coronation peal now. No one was commanding them. No ringer stood in any tower—the city below was in the streets, he could hear it, a rising tide of voices, people waking in doorways and market stalls and calling out names they'd misplaced, children's names, mothers' names, the names of streets and saints and ships. Memory was falling on Caerlum like rain after drought, and somewhere in that flood the bell-ringers—or the memory of bell-ringers, or simply the bells themselves, freed—had remembered the old peals, and the sound rolled down through the palace stones into the undercroft like a blessing delivered late.

Corvane wept without shame. He had earned the right to one unguarded moment, and he spent it here, kneeling between an unconscious hedge-witch and a mercenary who was humming along with the bells through her tears, badly, off-key, with her whole heart.

When it passed, he rose. His knees protested. Thirty years of service, and they chose now to complain.

"The city will come to the palace," he said. His voice came out rough. "Some to celebrate. Some to loot. Some with questions no one can answer yet." He looked down at Ivo, pale and breathing in Bryn's arms. "Including the council. Including whoever inherits. The king left no heir, and there are men in that court who will see a vacancy and start sharpening knives tonight."

"So we tell them the truth," Bryn said.

"The truth is that a wanted fugitive dissolved the monarchy with help from an inquisitor and a corpse." Corvane straightened, and found, to his mild surprise, that he was smiling. It felt strange on his face, like a coat borrowed from a younger man. "The truth will cause a riot."

"A riot might be an improvement," Bryn said.

Above them the bells thundered on, and beneath them—Ivo stirred, groaning, one eye cracking open, and the faint tripled pulse Corvane carried in his own chest stuttered and steadied, and steadied, and held.

"He's waking," Corvane said, and crouched, because whatever came next—the council, the crowds, the knives, the impossible accounting of a kingdom given back its own name—came next together.
