## 37. What Bryn Keeps

The amulet had been in Bryn's pocket for eight months.

She knew the exact count because she'd started counting the day Sera died—not the hours, she'd given up on those, but the days, marked in charcoal on the inside of her left boot cuff where no one could see them and no one could take them from her. Two hundred and forty-one. She'd counted through the fight in the alley where she'd put a knife to Ivo Ravensworth's throat and meant it. She'd counted through the weald and the village that forgot itself, through Vessany's bells and the bridge where a crossbow bolt meant for Ivo had torn through her instead. She'd counted feverish nights at Maren's cottage, counting being the only thing that kept her anchored while the poison worked itself out of her blood and the hedge-witch's poultices worked themselves in.

And she'd counted through the vault beneath the palace, flat on her back on cold stone with Corvane's hand clamped over her wound and Ivo screaming somewhere ahead of her, screaming like a man being unmade, while she held the waystone's power in her palms and felt it burn her down to something new.

Two hundred and forty-one days. And today, finally, she was going to put the amulet in the ground.

The hill above Caerlum's eastern wall was called the Gull's Rest, though no gulls rested there—it was too far inland, too high, all wind and coarse grass and the bones of an old watchtower nobody had bothered to rebuild. From its crown you could see the whole city: the gray slate roofs, the harbor cranes standing up like the ribs of some drowned thing, the palace on its spur with the scaffolding already climbing its flanks where masons were pulling down the sealed doors of the undercroft. The bells of the temple district rang wrong-footed this morning—no schedule anymore, just whoever felt like pulling the ropes—and the sound drifted up ragged and human and alive.

Bryn climbed it with a shovel over her shoulder and the amulet in her fist.

It was Sera's. Had been since they were girls, when their mother bought two of them off a peddler's cart for a copper apiece—cheap tin worked into the shape of a gull in flight, the paint long worn away. Sera had worn hers every day of her life. Bryn had lost hers in a river when she was nine and had cried about it for a week, and Sera, who never gave anything up without a fight, had pressed hers into Bryn's hand and said, *We'll share it. You wear it Mondays, I'll wear it the rest.*

Bryn had never given it back. That was the joke between them, the standing debt: *you owe me a Monday, Bryn, you owe me a Monday* — shouted across the yard, thrown with bread crusts, laughed about until the year their mother died and the laughing stopped and Sera took the amulet back without asking and Bryn let her, because grief makes thieves of everyone.

Then Sera ran messages for a living and wore it under her shirt against her heart, and then one night she staggered into a charm-seller's cellar with her blood soaking through her coat and pressed a map into a stranger's hands and died whispering about dead kings, and the amulet came home to Bryn in a leather pouch along with everything else the inquisitors hadn't confiscated, and Bryn had carried it every day since without once opening the pouch.

That wasn't quite true. She'd opened it. The first night after the alley, alone in her room with her knuckles split and her pride worse, she'd poured the contents out and found the little tin gull bent almost double, its wing snapped, one eye of cheap glass missing. Whatever had happened to Sera in her last minutes—the running, the falling, the desperate crawl—Ivo said the courier had been struck by something before she ever reached his door. The amulet had taken part of it. It had probably saved nothing. Tin doesn't stop blades. But it had been against her sister's heart when her sister's heart stopped, and so Bryn had closed the pouch and carried it, and carried it, and carried it, because putting it down felt like the one betrayal she couldn't survive.

She reached the top of the hill and stood a while, letting the wind have at her.

Her hair was longer than it had been eight months ago. There was a scar on her collarbone where the bolt had gone in, white and ridged, that ached when the weather turned. Her hands had healed crooked—the middle finger of her right hand sat at a slight angle now, Maren's doing, good enough to hold a knife but never again to thread a needle—and she'd caught herself, more than once these past weeks, turning that crooked hand over in the light and thinking not *I'm ruined* but *I paid.* Everyone had paid. Tam had paid the whole price. Some mornings she woke up reaching for the sound of his voice droning on about ley-lines and cataloguing systems, and the absence of it hit her like cold water, and she lay there and let it, because he deserved to be missed loudly even if there was no one left to hear her do it.

They'd buried him in the archive.

Not a grave—the building wasn't finished, half of it still scaffolding and the smell of fresh-cut timber—but a stone in the foundation wall of the reading room, cut with his name and the dates and one line that Corvane had insisted on and the council had, after some argument, allowed:

*He wrote it down, and so we remember.*

Bryn had put her hand on that stone exactly once, the day they set it, and had not gone back, because going back would become a habit and habits are how you start living inside a loss instead of beside it. Today was different. Today she had somewhere else to be first.

She picked her spot near the footings of the old watchtower, where the grass grew thin and the wind came off the sea clean, and she drove the shovel in.

The ground was harder than it looked. Of course it was. Nothing about this had ever been easy, not the dying and not the burying either. She worked in silence, sweat cooling on her neck, the city spread below her going about its strange new business—market stalls reopening, a fish-carter arguing cheerfully with his mule, two children chasing each other through the square where, eight months ago, inquisitors had posted warrants with her face inked on them. Someone had pasted a playbill over the spot where her wanted notice had hung. It advertised a comedy. Bryn had laughed until she cried when she saw it, and hadn't examined too closely which of the two she was doing.

When the hole was as deep as her forearm, she stopped and knelt and took out the pouch.

She opened it slowly this time. Let herself look.

The gull lay in her palm, bent wing and missing eye, tin gone dull with years and wear and one final violence. She turned it over. On the back, scratched so faint you'd miss it unless you knew to look, was a mark Sera had made with a pin when they were eleven: two strokes and a curve. Their private sign. *Still flying,* it meant. They'd used it on notes passed under doors, on fogged window glass, on the last letter Sera had ever sent her, which Bryn still had folded in a box under her bed and could recite word for word without looking at it.

*Off to Caerlum. Work's work. Still flying. —S.*

She'd thought, for months, that burying the amulet would feel like losing Sera twice. That was why she hadn't done it. Every plan she'd made since the alley—follow the map, kill the man who killed her sister, burn the Crown out of the world—every single one had really just been a structure built around that fear: keep moving, keep carrying, and the moment of putting her down never has to come.

But things had changed, and Bryn had changed with them, and what she understood now, kneeling on the windy hill with the tin gull in her crooked hand, was that she had been wrong about what the burial meant. It wasn't losing Sera twice. It was finally letting her arrive somewhere. Eight months of running, of vengeance, of war in cellars and palaces—and Sera had spent all of it in a pouch, in transit, a message that never got delivered. Couriers hated that above all things. Undelivered mail. A job unfinished.

Bryn almost smiled. Almost.

"Job's finished," she said aloud, to the wind. "You can put it down."

She wrapped the amulet in a scrap of clean linen—her own shirt, torn, because Sera would have mocked any ceremony fancier than that—and laid it in the hole. Then, because it felt right, she added the charcoal stick from her boot cuff, the one that had kept the count. Two hundred and forty-one days was enough. Let someone else count forward from here.

She filled the hole. Tamped it flat with the back of the shovel. And then she sat down on the cold ground beside it, elbows on knees, and stayed a long time, saying nothing, because there was nothing left that needed saying. The grief didn't lift—that wasn't how grief worked, she'd learned; it just changed shape, became less a fire you fought and more a scar you lived inside of—but for the first time it sat quiet in her chest, settled, like something sleeping rather than something screaming.

After a while she stood, shouldered the shovel, and walked down toward the city.

* * *

The new archive rose where the old royal library had stood before the inquisition burned it—or rather, where the burned shell had stood until three months ago, when the council finally approved the plans and the masons came with their scaffolding and their timber and their endless cheerful noise. Tam had drawn the first sketches of it himself, drunk, on the back of a tavern bill, the night he told them what he'd hidden inside his own memory. *A place where questions don't get people exiled,* he'd said. *Where the answer to 'who says?' is 'it's written down, go look.'* He'd called it a catalogue of everything the Crown ever ate. He'd called it, once, when the wine was deep, *my apology.*

Corvane had taken up the work like a man doing penance, which, as far as Bryn could tell, was exactly what it was.

She found him on the second floor, sleeves rolled, directing the shelving of the recovered volumes—what the inquisition had seized rather than burned, hauled out of government warehouses now that warehouses could be opened without warrants. He'd changed too. The immaculate gray coat was gone; he dressed like a working clerk now, plain wool, ink stains to the elbow. But the courtesy remained, that unsettling, precise courtesy, though it aimed differently these days. He bowed slightly when he saw her.

"Mistress Kess. You've come about the position."

"I've come to look at it."

"Of course." He gestured, and led her through the half-built rooms: the reading hall with its long tables, the map room where the original bloodstained chart—the one that had started everything—already hung behind glass, the narrow stair down to the vaults where the restricted materials would live under triple lock. He talked as he walked, low and thorough, the way he'd once talked while arresting people, and Bryn listened with her arms crossed and let him finish.

"The post is warden," he said. "Not librarian. Librarians tend books. The warden tends the *conditions* under which books can be tended." He paused at a window overlooking the reading hall. "Access for anyone who asks. No question refused. No record of who asked what. And—" his jaw tightened, the only crack in the clerk's calm, "—no arm of any government enters these rooms without the warden's leave. Including mine. *Especially* mine."

"You're trusting a stranger with that?"

"I'm trusting you specifically." He turned to face her fully. "You broke into the palace vault with a crossbow wound in your chest. You held a waystone's power bare-handed. And when the council offered you a captaincy, you told them, in my hearing, exactly what you thought of institutions run by men with titles." Something dry moved at the corner of his mouth. "You are precisely the sort of person who should guard against my kind."

"And if I catch you coming in here some night with a lantern and a warrant?"

"Then you'll put a knife in me," Corvane said mildly, "and history will record that the system worked."

Bryn snorted despite herself. She looked out over the reading hall—the dust motes turning in the light from the unfinished skylights, the shelves waiting empty and patient as held breath—and felt the thing she'd been circling for weeks finally settle into place. She'd told herself she'd go back to the coast after this. Back to fishing villages and ferry work, small jobs, small rooms, nowhere that required her to stay. Staying was a debt. Staying was a Monday she couldn't repay.

But Sera had run messages her whole life because she believed letters should reach people. And this place—this whole enormous building—was nothing but letters. Thousands of them. Hundreds of thousands. Every memory the Crown had eaten and Tam had smuggled out in his own skull, every page the inquisition had hoarded, every name and date and recipe and song the kingdom had nearly lost—all of it waiting to be delivered.

Someone had to carry it the last mile.

"I'll take it," Bryn said. "The warden's post."

Something in Corvane's shoulders eased—a small motion, quickly mastered, but she saw it. "The council will want formalities."

"The council can send them to me in writing. I'll file them." She tested the words, found she liked the weight of them. "First rule of the archive: everything gets written down. Even me."

"A wise rule." He hesitated, then, quieter: "He would have been pleased. Tam."

"Don't." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She breathed through it. "Don't tell me what he'd have been. Tell me what the pay is."

Corvane told her the pay, which was modest, and the lodging, which was a room above the map room with a window facing east—facing the Gull's Rest, though neither of them remarked on it—and the terms, which Bryn amended twice and signed once. When it was done, he shook her hand with both of his, careful of the crooked fingers, and said, "Thank you," and she pretended not to hear it, and they both allowed the pretense.

* * *

She ran into Ivo three days later, and it went the way she'd known it would go, which is to say it barely qualified as running into him at all.

He was setting up his stall in the market square—the same corner, the same folding table, the same faded awning—except everything about it was different now, because the charms on the cloth were real. Little knots that actually held luck in them. Salt sachets that genuinely kept the damp out of a fisherman's joints. A tray of memory-charms, simple ones, Maren's design, for people whose recollections had come back patchy and needed help stitching themselves whole. Business was brisk. Nobody hid anymore, or almost nobody, and the ones who did hid for reasons that had nothing to do with inquisitors.

Bryn watched from the edge of the crowd for a while, arms crossed, doing what she always did, which was watching before entering.

He'd filled out some. Slept better, or at least slept. The constant flick of his eyes toward every uniform in sight was fading—she still caught it, the old survival habit, but it surfaced slower now and sank faster. His hands moved over the goods with the ease of a man who'd stopped apologizing for them. Once, a child held up a luck-knot and asked him how it worked, and instead of deflecting with patter, Ivo crouched down and actually explained—simply, honestly, showing her the loop of the binding—and the girl's mother stood nearby not frightened, not angry, just listening, and something in Bryn's chest pulled tight at the sight of it.

The runaway apprentice, teaching magic to children in broad daylight in the middle of Caerlum. Five years of hiding, ended at a market stall.

She stepped forward, and he saw her, and she watched the recognition move through him—not surprise, exactly. More like confirmation. As if some part of him had known she'd find her way to this square eventually, the way water finds the low ground.

"Bryn."

"Ivo."

Neither of them rushed. That was the thing people didn't understand about her and him—they'd started with a knife at a throat and a lie about a murder, and somehow out of that wreckage they'd built something slower and stranger and sturdier than most people managed in easy friendship. They didn't need to perform. The silence between them was load-bearing.

"New stock," he said, nodding at the trays. "Maren sent a crate. Half of it I don't dare sell yet—one of these wards could flatten a city block if I botch the handling."

"Better not botch it, then."

"That's roughly my plan." He studied her a moment. "You've decided, then. About the archive."

News traveled fast in a city rebuilding itself; she shouldn't have been surprised he'd heard. "I signed yesterday."

"Warden Kess." He tried the title out, and nodded slowly. "Tam would have—"

"If you say 'approved,' I'm buying nothing and telling everyone your luck-knots are hollow."

His mouth quirked. "Hollow," he agreed solemnly. "Terrible craftsmanship. Don't shop here." Then the humor settled into something quieter. "It suits you. Guarding the truth. Making sure nobody gets to decide what people are allowed to remember." He glanced down at his hands, at the faint silver scarring along the fingers where the binding's residue lived, permanent now, a weight he carried and shared. "Somebody has to stand at the door."

"Somebody does." She looked at the trays, at the little tin gulls he'd started making—she'd seen them around the city lately, sold cheap, worn by dockworkers and schoolchildren, and she had never asked him why and he had never offered, and that was fine, because some things didn't need explaining to be understood. She picked one up. Turned it over. On the back, two strokes and a curve.

She set it down gently. "How much for the gull?"

"For you?" He shook his head. "Paid in full. A long time ago."

"It's bad business to give away stock."

"It's terrible business," he agreed. "I'm a terrible businessman. Ask anyone."

She took the gull anyway, and he let her, and they stood a moment in the market noise—the calls of sellers, the arguments over prices, a fiddler tuning up badly two stalls down—and Bryn realized she was waiting for the goodbye. The part where one of them said something about roads and letters and seeing each other, the ceremonial wrapping-up that people did, the words that always sounded like endings even when they weren't.

Ivo wasn't offering any.

He was just standing there, half-smiling, arranging his trays, entirely unbothered, as if the conversation weren't ending because it wasn't over—as if there were no door closing here at all, just two people who would both, tomorrow and the day after and the day after that, be living in the same city, walking the same streets, separated by a quarter mile of cobbles and completely within reach.

Because it wasn't a goodbye. That was the thing. Goodbyes were for departures, and neither of them was going anywhere.

"I'm opening the archive next month," she said. "Day after the equinox. There'll be a reading of Tam's catalogue—Corvane's organized it. Public. First floor, noon."

"I'll be there."

"You hate crowds."

"I'll be there anyway." He glanced up, and his eyes were steady and warm and utterly unhurried. "Front row, probably. I'll bring flowers. Tam liked flowers, didn't he? Or did he just lecture about them?"

"He lectured about them. Extensively. Latin names."

"Then I'll bring flowers with Latin names and mispronounce every one of them in his honor."

She huffed—almost a laugh—and pocketed the tin gull, and turned to go, and got four steps away before she stopped. Not because she'd forgotten anything. Because she wanted to say one thing properly, once, without ceremony, the way you hand someone the last item on a list.

"Sera trusted you," she said, without turning all the way around. "Out of everyone in this city, bleeding out in your cellar, she picked your door. I spent eight months trying to understand that. I think I finally do." Now she turned. "She wasn't delivering a map. She was delivering *you*. To us. To this." She shrugged, awkward with sincerity, always had been. "Best delivery she ever made."

Ivo didn't say anything for a moment. His throat worked once.

"Tell her," he managed finally, voice rough, "that I owe her a Monday."

"She knows," Bryn said. "She's known for months."

And she walked away across the square, into the crowd, past the fiddler who'd finally found his tune, and she did not look back, and he did not call after her, because it wasn't a goodbye.

It was a Tuesday. And Tuesdays, she'd decided, were hers.

* * *

On the evening before the equinox, Bryn climbed the Gull's Rest one more time.

The grave was green now, grass grown thick over the tamped earth, indistinguishable from the hillside around it except that she knew, and would always know, exactly where it was. She sat down beside it in the last of the light with a bottle of cheap coastal wine—Sera's brand, awful stuff, purchased specially—and drank two cups' worth and poured the third slowly onto the grass.

Below, the city lit itself lamp by lamp. Somewhere in the tangle of streets, a charm-seller was closing his shutters for the night. Somewhere across town, an ex-inquisitor was locking up a building full of rescued truths. Tomorrow the doors of the archive would open and anyone who walked in could ask anything, and the answers would be written down, and the writing would survive them, because that was the whole point, that was the lesson Tam had paid everything to teach: *memory survives us if we share it.*

Bryn touched the tin gull at her throat—new one, unbent, bright-eyed, bought fair and worn daily—and looked out at the lights.

"Still flying," she said.

And below her, all across Caerlum, the lamps kept burning, and the city that had learned to remember went on remembering, and tomorrow there would be work—shelves to fill, questions to answer, a door to stand at—and Bryn found, settling into the wind and the wine and the wide dark coming on, that she was not bracing for it.

She was ready for it.

She stayed until full dark, because she could, because staying was the whole point. Then she shouldered the empty bottle, took the path down the hill two steps at a time, and went home—to the map room, the east-facing window, the door with her name freshly painted on it.

Warden Kess. Keeper of the door.

She'd leave the lamp burning in the window all night. Just in case anyone came asking.
