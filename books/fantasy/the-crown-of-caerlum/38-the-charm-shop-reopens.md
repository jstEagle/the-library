## 38. The Charm Shop Reopens

The sign took me three tries.

Not the carving—I could carve a luck-knot in my sleep, and had, for years, into bits of driftwood and bone and whatever else the tide of Caerlum's back alleys washed up to my door. The carving was easy. It was the words that fought me.

*RAVENSWORTH CHARMS*, the first attempt said, and I burned it in the stove because it sounded like an apology.

*CHARMS & CURES*, the second said, and I burned that too because it sounded like everyone else, and after everything, being like everyone else felt like its own kind of lie.

The third one I carved slow, sitting on the step of my own cellar door in full morning light, where anyone walking down Gull Street could see me doing it. That was the part that made my hands shake—not the knife, not the wood, but the *being seen*. Five years of practice at invisibility, five years of hunched shoulders and half-lowered eyes and charms sold as "just trinkets, m'lord, nothing in them but pretty work," and now the council's new writ was nailed to the post outside the fish market declaring magic licensed, registered, taxed—*legal*—and I still flinched when a shadow crossed my doorway.

The third sign said: **IVO RAVENSWORTH — CHARMWRIGHT**.

My name. Out loud, in public, on wood.

I hung it above the cellar steps with Bryn's help. She held the ladder and offered commentary.

"Crooked."

"It's not crooked."

"It's crooked left. Everything you do leans left. You lean left when you walk. You leaned left out of Maren's cottage like a man escaping a fire."

"I *was* escaping a fire," I said, tightening the last nail. "Metaphorically."

"You're leaning left right now."

I stepped down and looked up. She was right. It was crooked. I left it that way. Some things you have to let be crooked or you'll never hang them at all.

* * *

The first week, almost nobody came.

That wasn't surprising. Caerlum was learning to breathe again the way a man learns to breathe after a rib has been reset—carefully, wincing, not quite trusting that the pain won't come back. The writ was two months old. The inquisition's towers stood empty, their windows dark, and Corvane had spent those two months doing something I still didn't entirely understand: dismantling, piece by piece, publicly, the machine he'd given his life to. He read the names of the condemned aloud in the old assize hall until his voice gave out, and then he started reading them again the next morning. He said someone had to remember who they'd been before the city forgot what forgetting cost.

But old fear doesn't empty out of a city just because the writ says so. Fear soaks into stone. People walked past my cellar door and glanced at the sign and glanced away, quick, the way you glance at a dog that might bite.

Except for the fishwife.

Marda—her name was Marda; I'd known her for years without ever letting myself know her name, which tells you most of what you need to know about how I lived—came down the steps on the second morning, wringing her apron, and stood in the middle of my shop like a woman who'd wandered into the wrong funeral.

"You're the knot man," she said.

"I'm the charmwright," I said. "Now."

She looked at me a long moment. Then she put a bundle on my counter and unwrapped it, and there inside was a luck-knot I'd sold her four years ago, frayed, salt-stiffened, tied wrong because I'd been afraid to tie it right.

"My husband went down with the *Kestrel's Pride*," she said. "Three winters back. And I paid you for this thing and you told me it was nothing, just string, just a pretty bit of nothing, and I wore it anyway because I needed to believe in something and you were cheaper than the temple." Her jaw worked. "And then the tithe-men came through the harbor quarter last autumn and my boy forgot my face. Forgot it. Looked at me like I was a stranger off the street. And when the memories came back—" her voice cracked and she pushed through it, the way Bryn pushes through things, head down, fists closed— "when they came back, he remembered the knot. Remembered me tying it round his neck when he was small. Said it was the one thing the hollow feeling couldn't get at, because it wasn't a memory, it was a *thing*. A made thing. With somebody's hands in it."

I didn't say anything. My throat had closed up.

"So." She shoved the frayed knot across the counter. "Tie me a proper one. And don't tell me it's nothing. I'll know you're lying, and I've had enough of lying things in this city to last me."

So I tied her a proper one. Real binding-work, the kind Maren taught me at sixteen, the kind I'd been doing in miniature and denial for five years—a knot that held, that gathered the loose stray warmth of a person's intention and kept it, the way a jar keeps summer. I worked in silence while she watched, and when I set it in her palm she closed her fingers over it and cried without any noise at all, which is the way strong people cry, and then she paid me double and told me I was charging too little, and left.

After that, word traveled the way it always does in harbor quarters: faster than ships, carried by women.

* * *

By the end of the month I had a waiting list.

It should have been joyous. Some days it was. There was a morning when a girl of maybe nine came in with her mother and asked, with the blunt courage of children, if I was the one who broke the king's crown, and when I said I'd had help, she informed me gravely that her grandmother remembered her own wedding day now, all of it, even the part with the goose, and that the goose part was the best part, and did I want to hear about the goose. I said I did. I heard about the goose. It took eleven minutes and it was the best eleven minutes of my week.

But other days—

Other days the weight sat on me. The residue. That's what Tam would have called it, if Tam were here to catalogue it: *residual binding-load, bearer-class, permanent.* The Crown was broken, cracked down to its black heart by three pairs of hands instead of one, and what remained of it lived distributed between me and Bryn and Corvane like a debt split three ways—still heavy, but survivable, which had been the whole point. In me it sat behind the sternum, a faint cold hum, a sense of something vast turned down to an ember. On bad days I could feel the shape of everything it had eaten, every name and wedding-day and goose-story it had swallowed in its long starving centuries, and I understood, finally, why it had done what it did. Not cruelty. Hunger mistaken for love. Preservation mistaken for mercy.

On bad days I hummed too, faintly, like a struck bell nobody meant to ring.

Bryn noticed before I admitted it. She came by most evenings, ostensibly to inspect my security arrangements, actually to eat my food, and one night she looked at me across the table and said, "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The quiet thing. The going-away-inside thing. You did it on the road after the bridge, when I had a bolt in me and you thought I was dying, and I'm telling you now the way I wish someone had told you then: stop it. Whatever's eating you, say it out loud or feed it supper, but don't sit there letting it chew."

So I told her about the humming. And she reached across the table, took my wrist—the scarred one, the one with the waystone mark—and pressed two fingers to it like taking a pulse, and frowned, and said, "Huh. It's quieter than mine," and that was all, and somehow that was exactly enough. We were three people sharing a wound. You learn to compare dressings.

Corvane came less often—he was a busy man, unbuilding an institution—but he came, and he drank my tea standing up, and he never once apologized for hunting us across half the kingdom, and I never once asked him to, and we got along fine.

Tam was the hole in all of it. Every evening, setting out cups, I caught myself counting heads and coming up one short. Every time I learned something new about binding-work, some trick of the old stones or the ley-roads, I thought: *Tam would want this written down,* and reached for a page that no longer needed filling. He'd left me his catalogue—all forty-one volumes of it, the life's work of a man who believed memory survives us if we share it—and I'd promised him I'd keep it, and I was keeping it, but keeping a dead friend's books is not the same as hearing him complain about your handwriting.

I missed him the way you miss a tooth. Not constantly. Just whenever anything touched the gap.

* * *

Maren came on a Tuesday.

I knew it was Tuesday because Tuesdays were slow, and because the bell over my door rang at midmorning with a particular hesitation—one push, a pause, a second push—belonging to someone deciding whether to enter. I looked up from a warding-stitch I was working into a fisherman's net, and there she was in my doorway, gray-haired now, straight-backed as ever, wearing the same travel cloak she'd worn the day I ran from her cottage five years ago.

Five years ago. Which meant, if I was honest, that it had really been six since I'd started running, and eight since I'd stopped listening, and the whole arithmetic of my cowardice laid itself out in my chest like a ledger.

"Your sign's crooked," Maren said.

"I know."

"Hm." She came down the steps. She looked around the shop the way she used to look around my work when I was fifteen—thoroughly, missing nothing, saying nothing yet—and I sat very still under it and let her look, because I owed her that much and more.

The shop, for the record, was not much. One room below street level, stone-floored, smelling of beeswax and sea-salt and the cedar shavings I never swept up promptly enough. Shelves of finished work: knots and wards and truth-charms and the small weather-bells that had become my best sellers among the fishing families. A workbench under the window, such as the window was. A kettle on a brazier. Two chairs, because somewhere in the last year I had become a person who owned two chairs.

Maren touched a weather-bell, gently, the way she touched everything—like asking permission. She picked up a truth-charm and turned it over. She examined the warding-net on my bench, and her eyebrows did something complicated.

"Who taught you the anchor-loop?" she said. "In the net."

"You did."

"No, I didn't. I showed you the theory once and you botched it for a month and then sulked." She looked at me. "Someone fixed your sulking."

"Bryn," I admitted. "She doesn't abide sulking. She says it's a form of hiding."

"She sounds wise."

"She sounds terrifying. It comes to nearly the same thing."

Something moved at the corner of Maren's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of a smile. She set the charm down and folded her hands, and I recognized the posture—this was the stance she took before hard lessons, and I braced for the reckoning I'd been rehearsing against for five years, all the speeches I'd composed on roads and in cellars, *I'm sorry, I was young, you were hard, I was scared, you never told me—*

"Tea," she said instead. "If you're offering."

I put the kettle on. My hands were steadier than I expected.

* * *

We drank the first cup in silence. It was good silence, or as close to good as our silences had ever managed—the silence of two people circling a fire they'd both been burned by.

"You knew," I said finally. "About the bloodline. About what I was. All along."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Would it have changed what you did?" she asked. "At eighteen. Would you have stayed, if I'd said *you are the last of the ravensworn, the binders of the Crown, and one day something will come looking for you?*"

I thought about my eighteen-year-old self. The boy who'd flinched from a raised voice, who'd hidden his gift from his own teacher, who'd run from a cottage over nothing worse than an argument and a lifetime of feeling handled.

"No," I said. "I'd have run faster."

"There it is." She turned her cup. "You want a confession from me, Ivo, and you'll have it, but take it whole: I hid your lineage because I loved you, and I was hard on you because I loved you, and both of those things were mistakes, and neither of them was the mistake you think they were. The mistake was that I taught you to hide before I taught you to stand. I made you careful when you needed to be brave. I did it to keep you alive—you were all I had, after your mother—but keeping a child alive and teaching a child to live are different crafts, and I only knew the first one." She met my eyes. "You ran because I built you a door and showed you where it was. That's my sin. Carry it a while; it's heavy, and I'm tired."

I sat with that. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor; the kettle ticked as it cooled; the ember behind my sternum hummed its low cold note, and for once I didn't mind it, because Maren had just handed me half her own weight to balance against it.

"And my sin?" I said. "Since we're trading."

"You tell me."

"I ran," I said. "And I told myself for five years that running was survival. And then a dying woman put a map in my hands and I *kept it*—against every instinct, against every rule I had—and I told myself that was survival too, that I had no choice. But that's not true either, is it. I kept it because some starved thing in me wanted to be called by name. Wanted the past to reach out and grab me by the collar so I wouldn't have to choose to turn around." I laughed, short and graceless. "Even the grand parts of my story were me refusing to choose. Even saving the kingdom was me getting dragged."

"Mm," said Maren, in the tone that meant *continue.*

"But here—" I gestured at the shop, the shelves, the sign hanging crooked over the door. "Here I chose. Nobody dragged me. The council sent letters asking if I'd license the premises and I sat with the letter for three days and then I signed it, awake, in daylight, with my whole name. That's the first thing I've ever done that I can't explain as flight." I looked at her. "So that's my confession. I'm not sorry I ran. I'm sorry it took me so long to stop."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Then she set down her cup, rose, walked around the counter, and did something she had never once done in all the years I'd been her apprentice.

She hugged me.

It was brief and awkward and smelled of woodsmoke and bitter herbs, and she thumped my back once, hard, the way you'd strike a bell to hear whether it was true, and then she stepped back and cleared her throat and said, "Your tea's gone cold," and that was the whole of it. Forgiveness neither of us asked for nor refused. Just a bell, struck. True.

* * *

She stayed until evening. We talked shop—real talk, craft-talk, the conversation I'd been starving for without knowing it: which woods hold binding best (ash, but yew holds *grief*, which has its uses), how to teach a truth-charm not to bruise the speaker, whether the new licensing examiners knew anything or were just clerks with stamps (the latter, we agreed, and someone ought to fix it). She corrected my anchor-loop. She approved, grudgingly, of my prices. When Bryn arrived at dusk and found a strange woman drinking the last of the good tea, the two of them measured each other for approximately four seconds, and then Maren said, "You're the one who fixed his sulking," and Bryn said, "You're the one who caused it," and Maren said, "Also true," and that was settled forever.

At the door, pausing on the bottom step, Maren looked back up at me.

"Your mother would have liked this," she said. "The shop. The crooked sign. The company." A pause. "She ran too, you know. That's the part I never told you. Ravensworn run—it's in the blood alongside the binding. But they come back. Every one of them, sooner or later, comes back and builds something." She touched the doorframe, briefly, like a blessing. "Welcome home, Ivo."

Then she was gone up the steps into the dusk, straight-backed, and I stood in my lit doorway watching until the street took her.

* * *

Bryn found me later, still at the bench, not working, just sitting with my hands flat on the wood.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"No," I said. "This is the other thing. The one where I stay."

She considered that, then pulled up the second chair—the guest chair, the chair that had hosted Corvane and Tam's ghost and a nine-year-old goose enthusiast—and sat, and put her boots up on my counter, which I allowed because I had learned which battles were worth losing.

"Post came while you were brooding," she said, nodding at the shelf by the door, where a small stack waited. "Harbor runner brought it. Looks like more of the same."

More of the same. For three weeks now, letters had been trickling in—first one or two, then a steady flow—from villages and towns across the kingdom, places I'd never been, addressed to *the charmwright of Gull Street* or *the ravensworn, if he lives* or, once, memorably, *to the man who broke the crown, care of anyone who knows him.* People whose memories had come back like weather after the breaking, writing to say thank you to a name they didn't have. I answered each one by hand. I signed none of them. It felt right, somehow—that the ravensworn, who had spent centuries binding things in secret, should end in gratitude received anonymously.

But tonight, when I sorted the stack, the topmost letter stopped my hand.

It wasn't the handwriting, though the handwriting was careful, clerk-trained, faintly familiar. It was the seal—or rather, the place where a seal had been, scraped away, leaving only a ghost of red wax and, pressed beneath it in the paper, the faint impression of a device I knew.

A tower. An open book. The mark of the royal archive.

Tam's mark. Tam's office. Tam's seal, which had died with Tam on the undercroft floor.

"Bryn," I said quietly. "Look at this."

She leaned over. Read the direction on the front. And went very still, because the letter was addressed not to *the charmwright* nor to *the ravensworn* but in a precise, clerk-trained hand:

*Ivo Ravensworth — Charmwright, Gull Street, Caerlum.*

Someone who knew my name. Someone with a dead man's seal. Someone who wanted, very specifically, to be found by me.

I broke the ghost of wax, and unfolded the page, and began to read.
