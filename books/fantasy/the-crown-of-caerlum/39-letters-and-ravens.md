## 39. Letters and Ravens

The first letter came in autumn, carried by a girl of perhaps ten who knocked on the charm shop's door with the solemnity of a child entrusted with something holy.

"I'm to give this to the ravensworn," she said. "That's what my gran said. Find the ravensworn and give it over."

Ivo took the envelope—cheap paper, sealed with a dab of pitch instead of wax—and felt his stomach drop through the floorboards. He'd been back in Caerlum eight months. Eight months of selling luck-knots openly, of hanging a sign that read *CHARMS, HONESTLY MADE* in letters he'd painted himself, of sleeping without one ear cocked for boots on the stairs. Eight months of learning how to be visible on purpose, which turned out to be harder than invisibility ever was.

He'd thought he was done being found.

"Your gran," he said carefully, "what did she tell you about the ravensworn?"

"That they fixed it." The girl shrugged, the way children shrug at miracles that happened before their time. "The forgetting. One day she remembered her mother's face, and she cried all morning, and then she made me promise."

Ivo crouched to her level. "And did your gran say what I look like?"

"She said you'd know." The girl studied him with frank brown eyes. "Do you know?"

He did. That was the trouble. He knew exactly what he looked like: a man with ink-stained fingers and a faint permanent weight behind his sternum, like a stone worn smooth by a river that never stopped running. A man whose name was on no letter, because he had asked Bryn—warden of the new archive now, keeper of Tam's catalogue—to make sure it never would be.

"The person you're looking for," he said, "isn't here right now. But I'll see it delivered. That's a promise."

She went away satisfied. He sat down at his workbench with the envelope in both hands and didn't open it for an hour.

When he finally did, the handwriting was shaky, the kind produced by hands that had spent a season not remembering how to hold a pen. *To the one who gave us back our dead,* it began. *My wife has been gone eleven years and I forgot her voice before she died. Last week I woke up hearing it. I don't know what this is worth to you, but I've enclosed everything I have.*

A copper bit fell out of the fold. Just one.

Ivo sat with it a long time. Then he dipped his pen and wrote back—not to thank her for the coin, but to ask her wife's name, and what the voice had been singing when she heard it again. He signed nothing. He sealed nothing with any mark that could be traced. When the girl came back the next week for her gran's answer, he handed it over wrapped in plain paper and said, "From a friend of the ravensworn."

It became a habit the way rivers become canyons: slowly, then all at once.

* * *

By midwinter there were forty letters. By spring, so many that Maren's old delivery raven—a mean-tempered bird named Sorrel that Ivo had inherited along with the habit of feeding him—was making three trips a week from the waystation roosts, dragging a satchel nearly as heavy as himself.

The letters came from everywhere. From the village that had forgotten itself—the one where he and Bryn had first understood what the Crown was eating—an old miller wrote that his daughter's name was Liss and that he'd spent four days saying it aloud to make sure it stayed. From Vessany, a bell-ringer described standing in the tower when the memories returned "like weather," as Ivo had once described it to Corvane, and how the whole city had stopped to weep in the streets without knowing why. From a farm in the Weald, a woman thanked him for saving her from wolves she had no memory of being saved from; someone had told her the story secondhand, and she wanted the ravensworn to know the wolves' pack still avoided her land.

Some letters were angry. Those were harder. A man from the capital wrote nine pages about the years the Crown had stolen from him—years he'd spent hollow-eyed in the lower city while his children grew up strangers—and demanded to know why the ravensworn hadn't come sooner. There was no honest answer to that. Ivo wrote back anyway. *You're right,* he said. *We should have. I'm sorry. Tell me about your children.* The reply, months later, was two lines: the children's names, and *they know them now.*

Tam would have catalogued every letter. Sometimes Ivo caught himself arranging them by region, by date, by tone, and had to stop, because the impulse felt too much like borrowing a dead man's spine. But he did keep them—all of them—in a cedar chest under his bed, and once a month he copied out three or four into a ledger, in Tam's old notation style, because memory survives us if we share it. That was the last instruction. Ivo intended to follow it until his hands gave out.

Bryn came by on the evenings the roosts were quiet. She never knocked anymore; she just let herself in, hung her cloak on the peg that had somehow become hers, and took over whichever chair was best positioned to watch the door. Warden of the archive suited her the way a blade suits a hand. She'd rebuilt Tam's burned collection from scraps and testimony, and she ran it with the same ferocity she'd once aimed at Ivo's throat in an alley.

"You answered the angry one from Harrowgate," she said one night, reading over his shoulder without asking.

"He deserved an answer."

"You apologized to him."

"He was owed one."

"You apologize like a man paying off debts he didn't incur." She dropped into her chair. "The Crown ate those years, not you."

"Some of them I hid through myself." He set down the pen. "Five years of hiding, Bryn. Before the Crown ever touched most of those people, I was already doing its work for free."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Maren says the same thing about herself, you know. Nearly word for word. You two deserve each other."

"We're getting better."

"You are." And because Bryn did not say soft things often, the smallness of it landed hard. She nodded at the letter pile. "Next one. Read me the sad ones. I'll read you the funny ones. Fair trade."

There were funny ones. A boy in the Weald remembered, suddenly and mid-meal, that he had been betrothed as a child to a girl three villages over, and wrote asking whether the ravensworn could undo that too. A fishwife—Ivo recognized the name, the same frightened woman from his first chapter of this life, the luck-knot buyer—wrote that she remembered buying charms from a nervous young man with clever hands, and that she'd always suspected, and that she hoped he was eating properly. Ivo laughed until his ribs hurt, and Bryn demanded to know what was funny, and he read it aloud, and she snorted and said, "She's right, you look terrible," and stole his bread.

This was the shape of his life now. Letters in, letters out, bread stolen, chair occupied, raven fed. An inheritance transformed—not the bloodline's old inheritance of binding and control, but a new one, written by hand, signed by no one, owned by everyone.

* * *

The difficult letters were the ones that asked him to be more than a correspondent.

A widow in Caerlum's dock district wrote that her son had been an inquisitor under the old order, one of Corvane's brothers-in-arms, cut down in the vault siege. She didn't blame the ravensworn—that much she said plainly—but she wanted to know if her son's name would be remembered, or if the new council would sweep the whole inquisition into shame and silence. Ivo brought that one to Corvane personally.

Corvane had changed less than Ivo expected and more than he admitted. He still wore grey, though the inquisitor's sigil was gone from the collar. He still spoke with that unsettling courtesy, though it had softened into something almost like warmth, the way frost softens toward morning. He was dismantling the institution he'd served, record by record, trial by trial, and he did it with the thoroughness of a man doing penance he considered merely administrative.

"Edric Vole," Corvane said, reading the name. "Yes. I remember him. He held the eastern stair." He was quiet a moment. "His name will be in the archive. Not in the shamed list and not in the honored one. In the true one. That's the only list worth making."

"Will the council agree?"

"The council agrees to whatever frightens them least." Corvane's mouth twitched. "Which is why they offered you the throne again last week, and why you refused again, and why they're frightened of you anyway. Power that doesn't want itself terrifies powerful men."

"I don't want it."

"I know." Corvane met his eyes. "That's the strange part, Ravensworth. I spent my life serving a thing that consumed a kingdom because it believed it was saving it. And here you are, refusing actual power, and actually saving things. It has taken me two years to understand the difference, and I am still not certain I could explain it to my former brothers."

"It's the difference between carrying alone and carrying together."

"Yes." Corvane looked out the shop window, at the street where children played a game involving chalk circles and, inevitably, magic of the harmless sort. "I write letters too, these days. To the families. Every inquisitor who died in the siege, every one we wronged before it. I sign mine."

Ivo blinked. "You do?"

"Someone must be accountable. You carry the binding; let me carry the names." He rose, straightened a coat that needed no straightening. "Besides, if we both signed nothing, who would the angry letters reach?"

After he left, Ivo sat with the widow's letter a long while, then wrote back: *Your son held the eastern stair. He is remembered by name, by the man who fought beside him, and now by me. That is not nothing. None of it is nothing.*

* * *

On the anniversary of the breaking—the second one—he climbed the walls with Bryn and Corvane, as they now did each year, and stood in the wind feeling the faint hum of the shared binding, diluted across three bodies, survivable, permanent. Below, Caerlum glowed with lamps. Somewhere in the city, a hundred letters were being written by people who would never learn his name, and a hundred answers were waiting in his cedar chest, unsigned, unowned, given freely.

"Another year," Bryn said.

"Another year," Corvane agreed.

Ivo said nothing. He was watching a raven cross the darkening sky toward the waystation roosts, satchel-empty now, going home. Tomorrow there would be more letters. Griefs he couldn't fix, gratitude he didn't deserve, questions only a ravensworn could answer and only a nameless man should. He would sit at his bench with ink-stained fingers and answer every one by hand, and sign nothing, and that would be enough. More than enough. It would be the whole of the inheritance, passed on one envelope at a time.

But that night, when he returned to the shop, Sorrel was waiting on the sill—wet, furious, and early—with a single letter in his beak that bore no seal, no postmark, and no place of origin. Only a single word pressed into the paper in a hand Ivo did not recognize:

*Ravensworn.*
