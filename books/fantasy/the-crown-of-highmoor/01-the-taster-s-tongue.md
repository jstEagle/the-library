## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The bell above the kitchen door rang at half past four, and Wren Quintana was awake before the second chime, as they had been every morning for nine years.

Not because they slept lightly—though they did—but because a food-taster who overslept was a food-taster whose replacement was already being discussed in hushed tones by the Chamberlain's clerks. The crown did not forgive lateness. The crown barely forgave existence. It simply permitted it, the way a wall permits ivy: so long as the ivy stays flat against the stone.

Wren dressed in the dark without lighting a candle. They knew their room the way they knew their own tongue—with an intimacy that required no light. Three steps to the washstand. Two to the peg where the grey livery hung. The tunic was plain wool, deliberately unadorned; the only mark of office was a thin silver chain beneath the collar, holding a small spoon that never touched food except for the king's. They braided their hair back tight and plain, the way one wrapped a parcel one did not wish noticed, and went down into the sleeping dark of the palace's underbelly.

The kitchens of Highmoor Palace were a city unto themselves at this hour—or rather, a country. The great hearths were banked coals breathing slow orange. Somewhere a scullery boy snored against the flour sacks he'd been meant to guard. The smell of yesterday clung to everything: tallow, ash, dried thyme, the ghost of roasting meat soaked so deep into the stone that no amount of scrubbing would ever lift it. Wren moved through it all like water through reeds—present, unnoticed, essential.

"Quintana." Old Marta the pastry-mistress didn't look up from her dough, but her hands paused, which was her version of a greeting. "You're early."

"I'm on time."

"You're early." She resumed kneading. That was all. In nine years, that exchange had perhaps varied by three words total, and Wren found a strange, cold comfort in its constancy. There were people in the palace—the gardeners, the farrier, the woman who gutted fish down by the wet gate—who would nod to Wren in passing, who might even offer a word about weather or a grandchild's fever. But none of them would notice, truly, if Wren vanished between one dawn and the next. They'd say *the taster? Quiet one. Always quiet.* And then someone else would be fitted for the grey tunic, and the bread would rise, and the king would eat, and the machinery of Highmoor would grind on with one more small part replaced.

Wren had made peace with this. Or rather, they had made something that lived in the same house as peace and wore its clothes, and no one had yet looked closely enough to see the difference.

* * *

By six, the breakfast service began its assembly, and with it, the ritual.

It happened in the antechamber off the royal apartments—a small, cold room with a stone table scrubbed white and two guards at the door who never spoke to Wren and never looked away from them either. The dishes came up one at a time, each carried by a footman who set it down and withdrew, and each time, Wren took up the silver spoon and tasted.

Honeyed porridge first, because the king liked it warm and the warmth could carry things. Wren let it sit on the tongue and counted—one breath, two, three—reading it the way a sailor reads a sky. Sweetness first, then the toasted-oat depth beneath, then the faint bitter edge of the honey itself, wild honey from the southern hives, which always ran a little green. Normal. They swallowed a full spoonful, waited the count again, and nodded.

Then smoked river trout, folded in an herb butter. Then small beer. Then the winter apples baked with cinnamon, which the cooks had begun making when the king's digestion turned sour last autumn and which he now ate as though they had always been his favorite. Dish by dish, spoonful by spoonful, Wren built the morning's ledger in their head: *safe, safe, safe.*

People imagined poison-tasting was drama—a sudden collapse, a gasp, guards rushing in. In truth it was arithmetic. Nine years of measured doses had done what nine years of doses do: the crown had fed Wren poisons in increments since they were nineteen years old, small enough to survive, steady enough to build a body that shrugged at things that killed lesser men. Arsenic in the wine cellars' dust. Hemlock extract, once, in a training exercise Wren was not told was a training exercise until after they'd vomited blood for a day and been given double rations for a month. Their blood was a museum of small murders. Their tongue was an instrument tuned over nearly a decade to catch the wrongness in a dish the way a musician catches a sour note—not knowing why it's wrong, only that the whole chord shudders.

And so: safe, safe, safe—and then, at the seventh dish, the spiced pear conserve, Wren paused.

Not poisoned. Nothing so crude. But the spice balance was different—clove forward where it should have been ginger, a half-measure too much of pepper—and Wren held the taste on their tongue and thought: *Cook changed the recipe. Cook is nervous. Cook heard something.*

That was the other half of the work, the half no one wrote into the job description. A taster who only caught poison caught poison once, if lucky. A taster who paid attention caught the tremor in a kitchen, the new face among the porters, the day the water cart came late and everyone pretended it hadn't. Fear had a flavor. So did ambition. So did the particular stillness of a household where someone had been paid to be careless. Wren had survived nine years not by being immune but by being *attentive*, and attention, they had learned, was the loneliest form of vigilance there was. You saw everything. You mentioned nothing. You became a person made entirely of noticing, and there is no lonelier kind of person than that.

They flagged the conserve anyway—not as suspicion, merely as *altered*, and the steward sent it back, and somewhere in the kitchens a cook was quietly relieved of the morning's pride, and the machinery ground on.

* * *

The king ate at seven. Wren never watched him eat. They stood at the prescribed distance—ten paces, near the tapestry of the First Landing, eyes lowered, present as furniture—and listened to the small wet sounds of royal appetite while the chamberlain read out the day's petitions. This morning it was grain tariffs from the river provinces, a dispute over mill rights, and a long complaint from some border lord about sheep going missing in the high pastures below the Thornfells.

At the word *Thornfells*, Wren's heartbeat did something traitorous.

They kept their eyes down. They kept their breathing even. Inside, behind the careful blank face, something old and hungry lifted its head.

Everyone knew the Thornfells—the mountains north of the marches, the ones that broke the horizon like broken teeth, wrapped half the year in cloud. Everyone knew you didn't go there. The maps said *HERE BE STONES AND OLD WEATHER*, which was mapmaker's humor, and also true. What fewer people knew—what almost no one at court knew—was that the mountains were not empty. They were threaded. Old smuggler roads ran through them like veins through marble: paths cut by generations of people who needed to move goods, or people, or secrets past the king's tolls and the king's soldiers.

Wren knew those roads. Not from books. From their mother—who had died when Wren was eleven, leaving behind a border village childhood, a handful of lullabies in a language nobody at court recognized, and a mind that had memorized every switchback, every hidden ford, every cave-with-a-second-exit along forty leagues of the range. Wren had redrawn them from memory, years ago, on scraps of paper they kept folded inside the lining of their boot: not one map but many, disguised as mending patterns, inked in the coded shorthand smugglers used, legible to no one alive except Wren and possibly three dead men.

They had never told anyone. They had never needed them. The roads existed in Wren's keeping the way a candle exists unlit—held, not spent. Some nights Wren lay awake tracing the paths in their head, walking them the way other people walked in dreams, and told themselves this was not longing. It was inventory. A professional habit. You memorized escape routes the way you memorized antidotes.

You told yourself that often enough, and sometimes, in the hour before dawn, you almost believed it.

* * *

Service ended at half past eight. Wren returned the silver spoon to its case, signed the ledger with the steward's clerk—a signature so small and cramped it was nearly a smudge, which was deliberate—and collected the day's ration chit. Back through the corridors, down past the laundry court where steam rose in grey ghosts, out to the narrow strip of yard behind the servants' quarters where the palace's working people took what air they could.

Wren stood there with their chit and their untouched breakfast roll—it was policy that tasters ate separately, another small severance, as though the work itself made their food suspect—and watched the storm coming in over the eastern towers. It had been building since midnight, a bruise-colored weight pressing down on the city, and now the wind arrived ahead of it, smelling of iron and cold rain. The first drops hit the cobbles like thrown gravel.

Around them the yard emptied fast. Servants scattered for cover, calling jokes and curses, and Wren stayed, because rain was one of the few things in the world that asked nothing of them. Rain fell on kings and tasters alike and did not check the ledger first.

They were thinking, distantly, of supper service, of whether the king's mood would hold through the council meeting—storm days made him cruel, and cruel kings ordered rich meals, and rich meals meant longer hours at the table—when they heard the sound.

Not thunder. Not quite. Underneath the storm's growl there was a thinner sound, high and ragged, coming from the direction of the outer kitchen gate. A sound Wren's body recognized several seconds before their mind consent to: a child crying, or keening, in a cadence that was not weeping at all but something older, rhythmic, almost sung.

The yard was empty now. The rain was thickening into sheets. Wren stood alone at the edge of it, listening, and felt the folded papers shift inside their boot lining as though the roads themselves had stirred.

They should have gone inside. Every instinct honed over nine years said *go inside, be invisible, let the guards deal with whatever it is.* That was the whole architecture of survival: see everything, touch nothing, tell no one.

But the sound came again, closer now, threading through the rain, and beneath the strangeness of it Wren caught something that stopped their breath—a turn of melody, a rising fall of syllables, that they had not heard since they were small enough to be rocked to sleep. Their mother's voice, thirty years drowned, singing against the dark.

Wren looked toward the outer kitchen gate, where the mud was already running in brown rivers toward the drain, and saw, huddled against the doorpost, small and shaking and watching Wren with eyes too old for its muddy face—

a child.

And the child, in a language dead these three hundred years, spoken soft as a prayer and clear as a blade through the rain, called out a single word.

Wren understood it.

That was the trouble. That was the beginning of everything.

They understood it.
