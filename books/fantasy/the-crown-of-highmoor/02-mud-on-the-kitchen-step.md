## 2. Mud on the Kitchen Step

The storm came in from the west an hour past midnight, and by the time the first thunder rolled down off the Thornfells and broke against the palace walls like a dropped platter, Wren Quintana was already awake.

They were always awake before storms. It was not fear, exactly—or if it was, it had worn itself smooth as river stone over nine years, until it felt more like habit than anything with teeth. But storms made the kitchens restless. Storms made cooks jumpy, and jumpy cooks oversalted the pottage or underbaked the king's morning rolls, and a taster who did not know what his kitchen was feeling on any given morning was a taster who might miss something. So Wren lay in their narrow cot above the scullery and listened to the rain hammering the leaded glass, and catalogued, as they did every night, every small danger the coming day might hold: the new shipment of spices from Callowmere that had sat too long in a customs house; the chancellor's visiting cousin, who had asked twice about the taster's duties with too much interest; the king's fondness for raw oysters in autumn, which was its own quiet madness.

Nine years. Nine years of tasting, and Wren had built their life the way a mason builds a wall—one careful stone at a time, each one fitted so precisely into the next that no one ever thought to look for mortar. They were forgettable by design. Medium height, medium coloring, a face that people described afterward as *the taster* and nothing more. They spoke when spoken to, laughed at the right moments in the servants' hall without committing to any opinion worth repeating, and kept their maps—ah, but the maps were another matter entirely.

Wren rose in the dark, dressed, and pulled the loose floorboard beneath their cot. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the thing that was most truly theirs: three sheets of good paper covered edge to edge in fine, cramped ink. Smuggler roads. The old drover paths through the Thornfells that the crown had never bothered to map because the crown believed the mountains empty—and the mountains were not empty, they were merely quiet, the way a held breath is quiet. Wren had learned those paths from their mother before she died, and had spent nine years adding to them, inch by inch, trading favors with peddlers and charcoal-burners, walking the high passes themselves on the two weeks' leave they took each year and told everyone was visits to a fictitious aunt in Lowmark. The maps went nowhere near the palace archives. The palace archives belonged to Chancellor Vess, and Chancellor Vess belonged to himself.

Wren slid the board back into place just as the bell in the scullery below rang—the night cook, wanting help hauling water before the cisterns froze.

Down the spiral stair, the kitchens swallowed them whole. Even in the dead hours, the great room breathed: banked fires breathing orange under the cauldrons, the smell of yesterday's bread still ghosting in the warm dark, rows of copper pots hanging from the beams like sleeping birds. Cook Brammel stood at the door to the yard in her shawl, wrestling it open against the wind, and the rain came sideways through the gap and spattered the flagstones.

"There's a tree down across the well-walk," she shouted over the gale. "And the cellar grate's backing up. Get the sand, will you, before we're swimming."

Wren got the sand. That was the shape of their days—small tasks, done promptly, noticed by no one. They worked with their sleeves pushed up, sand hissing across wet stone, while the storm threw itself against the palace as though it meant to come in and be fed. Lightning turned the yard white for a heartbeat, and in that heartbeat Wren saw the courtyard streaming silver, the woodpile hunched under its tarpaulin, the servants' door standing open—

—and nothing else. No figure. No small dark shape huddled against the step.

It was only on the third flash, when Wren had gone back inside and was wringing out their sleeves, that Brammel said, in a voice pitched strangely flat, "There's a child on my step."

"What?"

"Child. On the step." She jerked her chin toward the door. "Been there since I opened up, I'd swear. Didn't see him come. Thought he was a bundle of rags the wind blew in." Her hands were busy kneading nothing, working dough that wasn't there. "He won't speak. Or he speaks, but it's naught I know. Sounds like—" She stopped, frowned, tried again. "Sounds like a man talking underwater."

Wren dried their hands slowly. Something had shifted in their chest, some stone rolling loose in the wall they'd spent nine years building, though they could not yet have said why. "I'll look."

The child stood where the eaves threw the thinnest possible shelter, pressed back against the doorframe as if trying to become part of the palace itself. He was mud to the eyebrows. Mud in his hair, mud crusted thick on his bare feet and shins, a smear of it across his mouth as though he'd eaten his way through the fells rather than walked them. He wore what had once been a coat of good grey wool, now ruined beyond saving, cut in a style Wren didn't recognize—too long in the body, fastened with bone toggles instead of buttons. He couldn't have been more than seven or eight. His eyes were the color of deep water under ice, and they fixed on Wren with an intensity that had nothing childlike in it at all.

"You'd best come in," Wren said, in the tone they used for everything—level, mild, unremarkable.

The child answered.

It was not shouting; it was worse than shouting. It was a stream of words, quick and liquid and utterly strange, syllables rising and falling like birdsong with the bones taken out of it, sounds Wren's mouth did not know how to make and their ear did not know how to hold. Brammel, behind them, made a small involuntary noise and crossed herself with floury fingers.

But beneath the strangeness—beneath it, threaded through it like gold wire through black cloth—something moved. A cadence. A rise at the end of the phrase, a softening of the last consonant, a lilt that fell on the second-to-last word the way a foot falls on a stair you've climbed ten thousand times. Wren's whole body went cold and hot at once. Their mother's voice rose unbidden from nine years of deliberate forgetting, humming low over a cooking fire in a border cottage: *Ael suvven, ael dorathen, mi little wren, mi heart's own—*

They understood perhaps one word in twenty. And the one word in twenty was enough to stop their heart.

"*Suvven*," the child said again, urgent, grabbing at Wren's sleeve with muddy fingers. "*Suvven, suvven!*"

Storm, Wren thought. Or wind. Or cold, or lost, or afraid—it could have been any of them, all of them braided together. In their mother's songs, *suvven* had been the wind that came down off the high fells in autumn, the wind that carried things away.

"I don't—" Wren began, and heard their own voice crack, and hated it, and mastered it. "Come inside. Come. *In.*" And then, before they knew they were going to say it, in the half-language of their childhood, the kitchen-table pidgin of a mother singing to herself while her child listened at her knee: "*Dorath in. Dorath.*"

Warmth. In. Warmth.

The child went absolutely still. The rain ran off his chin. His eyes—deep-water eyes, ice-over-eyes—searched Wren's face with a hunger that made Wren take an involuntary step backward, and then, slowly, wonderingly, he reached up and touched Wren's hand. Just touched it. Two muddy fingers against their knuckles.

He said a word then that Wren did understand—not from lullabies but from nowhere, from the marrow of them, from some locked room in their blood thrown suddenly open: a name-shaped word, a question-shaped word, and Wren would spend the rest of the night failing to remember it exactly, the way one fails to hold a dream after waking.

Then Brammel said sharply, "Get him in before the whole yard hears," and the moment broke.

* * *

They put him in the wash-room off the buttery, the one place at that hour where a fire already burned under the copper, and Brammel—practical, unsentimental Brammel, who had buried two husbands and run the king's kitchens through famine and feast alike—attacked the mud with a bucket and a scrubbing brush while Wren found clothes. There was a delivery boy's spare shirt, and hose that would do doubled at the knee, and a pair of boots two sizes too large that the child refused to wear and then, ten minutes later, put on with tremendous dignity, as though he had decided boots were a custom of this country he would permit.

Under the mud he was pale as milk, thin as a winter branch, with a fading bruise along one jaw and scratches on his arms that Wren looked at too long. Not animal scratches. Too straight. Too regular. Four lines, parallel, from elbow to wrist, as though something with long fingers had caught him and let go.

"Eat first or sleep first?" Brammel demanded of him, and he looked at her blankly, and she sighed and thrust bread at him anyway, and he ate the way the starving eat—carefully, ferociously, watching everyone between bites.

Wren stood by the fire and watched him and felt the ground shifting under their feet.

Here was the problem, laid out plain as a ledger: a child appears at the palace in a storm, speaking no tongue known to anyone, wearing clothes from nowhere, marked by hands, and every sensible instinct Wren possessed—the instincts that had kept them alive for nine years—said *walk away*. Say nothing. Let Brammel report him to the steward in the morning, let the steward send for the guard, let the machinery of the palace grind him up however it liked. It was not Wren's business. Nothing was Wren's business. Wren's business was tasting the king's breakfast at six and being forgettable while doing it, and forgettable people did not involve themselves with mud-caked children who spoke in bird-song.

But the child looked up at them over his bread, and said something soft and questioning in that impossible language, and in the middle of it, unmistakable, was a word Wren's mother had sung a hundred times: *dorathen*. Belonging. Home. Or possibly hearth-fire. The border pidgin had never been precise; it had been a language of gestures and warmth, a language for pointing at things and loving them.

And beneath the question in his voice was something else, something that raised every hair on Wren's arms: fear. Not the ordinary fear of a lost child. The fear of a hunted thing that has finally, briefly, stopped running.

"He's hurt," Wren said to Brammel. "Those marks on his arm."

"I saw them." Brammel scrubbed hard at a pot that did not need scrubbing. "And I'll tell you what I told myself when I opened that door and saw him sitting there like he'd grown out of the step. I'm sixty-one years old, Wren Quintana, and I've seen every kind of trouble walk into these kitchens, and I know trouble when it sits down to my fire. That boy isn't running *from* something small." She set the pot down with a clank. "Steward's going to want him logged and handed to the guard by morning bell. Guard'll ask questions. Questions get written down. Written-down things go to the chancellor's office." She gave Wren a long, level look, and Wren realized with a jolt that Brammel had been watching them far more closely than Wren's invisibility had ever allowed them to notice. "Now, I've got four hundred rolls to proof and a storm drowning my yard, and I can't be everywhere at once. Terrible oversight, that. Can't imagine how a boy might sit in the pantry loft all night unbeknownst to anyone."

"Brammel—"

"Don't thank me. Don't tell me anything either. I'm an old woman, I don't want to know things." But her voice gentled as she gathered her bucket. "Your mother sang like that, you know. When she thought nobody was listening. Same tune underneath the words." At the door she paused. "Whatever he is—he came to *you*, love. Out of all the doors in this city, yours."

She left, and the wash-room settled into fire-crackle and rain-drum, and Wren and the child regarded one another across the warm space like two animals meeting at a waterhole.

* * *

The pantry loft was a low crawl of a space above the dry stores, reached by a ladder behind the flour sacks, smelling of grain and bay leaves and old cedar. Wren had slept there their first year at court, before the cot above the scullery was assigned, and knew it was warm and dark and invisible. They brought blankets, and the heel of a honey-cake, and a candle stub they had no business taking.

The child climbed the ladder like he'd been born in rigging. He wedged himself into the corner where the roof beam met the floor and arranged the blanket around himself with a formality that nearly made Wren laugh—the first time in the whole long night that anything in them had wanted to laugh—and then, when Wren turned to go, he said the word again.

The same name-shaped, question-shaped word from the doorstep. But slower this time, deliberate, and he pointed—at Wren's chest, at their own self, unmistakably.

Asking their name.

"Wren," they said. "My name is Wren."

He shook his head slightly. Tried the sound: "*Ren.*" Then, patiently, as though instructing a very slow student, he pointed at his own chest. "*Iven.*"

"Iven," Wren repeated.

He nodded, satisfied, and pointed at Wren again, and said the strange word again, and this time Wren heard it properly, heard the shape of it, and the bottom dropped out of their stomach.

Because it wasn't asking *what are you called*. It was asking *what are you*. And the word—a word Wren had never heard spoken aloud in their life but knew instantly, completely, the way one knows one's own reflection—meant, in whatever buried tongue it came from:

*Grey.*

Half-and-half. Between. The border-born. The children their mother had sung to sleep in a cottage on the Thornfell marches, the ones the valley folk crossed the road to avoid, the ones the old stories called luckless and the older stories called something else entirely.

The candle guttered. Rain hammered the roof overhead. Iven watched Wren from his nest of blanket with those deep-water eyes, waiting, certain, patient as the mountains he had walked out of, and Wren Quintana—who had spent nine years building themselves into a wall so perfect that even they had forgotten what was underneath—felt a single stone come loose and fall.

"No," Wren heard themselves say. Quietly. Firmly. To a child of eight in a pantry loft, as though denial could reach back nine years and unmake a mother's lullabies. "You're wrong. You're wrong about me."

Iven tilted his head. And then, softly, in that language older than crowns, he began to sing—the same rising-falling tune Wren's mother had hummed over a border hearth, note for note, word for word, a song Wren had not heard since they were six years old and had never once succeeded in forgetting.

He knew it the way one knows one's own name.
