## 3. Words Older Than Crowns

The storm had not let up by the time Wren made their decision, and perhaps that was the deciding of it—the rain hammering the kitchen roof like a fist on a door no one wanted to answer. In such weather, no one came to the kitchens who did not belong there. No one would come looking.

Wren crouched beside the child at the hearth's end and spoke slowly, the way one speaks to a frightened animal or a drunk lord: *You are safe here. Do you understand? Safe.*

The boy watched Wren's mouth as though the words were food he was learning to chew. Then he answered—not in the king's tongue, not in any tongue the kitchens knew—and the sound of it went through Wren like a fishhook through soft flesh.

It was the language of the lullabies.

Nine years Wren had kept those songs locked in the attic of their memory, in a room they did not open. Their mother's voice, low and smoke-roughened, singing over the banked fire of a border cottage while rain did exactly this to the thatch: *Hush now, hush, the thorn-bells ring, the mountain keeps what mountains bring—* Words Wren had never understood, only felt, the way you feel warmth through a closed window. And now here they were, falling out of a mud-caked child's mouth in the middle of the king's own kitchen, whole sentences of them, urgent and liquid and old.

"Stop," Wren whispered, and hated how their voice shook. "Stop saying that."

The boy stopped. His eyes were enormous and green-gold, the color of moss over river stones, and they did not waver the way human eyes wavered. He looked at Wren the way no one at court had ever looked at them: as though Wren were something worth finding.

That was the danger of it. That was precisely the danger.

Wren stood, wiped their hands on their apron out of habit, and surveyed the kitchen. Empty but for the banked ovens and the sleeping bulk of the great spits. The night cook had gone to her cot; the scullery boys were abed in the loft above the stables. The storm covered all sounds but its own.

There was a place. Of course there was a place—Wren knew every inch of this palace the way a spider knows its web, every thread and tremor. The pantry loft above the dried stores, where the salted hams hung like hanged men and the smell of juniper and bay thickened the dark. It was warm from the ovens below, dry, and—this was the important part—it was never inventoried after dusk. The steward's men counted the pantry at dawn and again at midday, and between those counts the loft belonged to dust and mice and no one.

"Come," Wren said, and then, absurdly, tried it in the other tongue—a word fished up from nine years underwater, mispronounced, probably meaning nothing or something terrible. "*Tal.* Come."

The child's breath caught. He said the word back, correcting it gently, the way you'd correct a child: "*Tael.*" And then he took Wren's hand.

His fingers were cold as well-water and far too light, as though his bones were willow switches. But he held on. He held on as if he had decided, somewhere in the storm and the mud and the running, that this forgettable person in an apron was the door he had been looking for, and he would not be letting go.

Wren led him through the dark kitchen, past the hanging copper, past the great table scarred by two hundred years of knives, up the narrow stair behind the flour bins to the loft where the hams swayed faintly in some draft Wren could not find. They spread a sack of oat sacking in the corner behind the last row of shelves, where even a searching lantern's light would pool and fail.

"Eat." Wren pressed a heel of yesterday's bread into his hands, and a wedge of hard cheese, and after a moment's thought—the thought arriving before permission could be sought—an apple from the king's own winter store. Let the crown feed a fugitive for once. It owed more than that.

The boy ate the way starved things eat: fast, then deliberately slower, watching Wren watch him, learning manners in real time. When he finished he said something long and soft, and though Wren could not have translated a word of it, they understood the shape of it perfectly. *Thank you* has the same shape in every language. So does *please don't send me away.*

"I'm not sending you away," Wren said. "I'm hiding you. There's a difference, and I'd thank you not to make me regret it."

He tilted his head. He didn't understand the words. He understood the tone, which was, Wren admitted privately, the same tone in every language too.

Then he pointed at himself, at his own hollow chest, and said a name. "*Iven.*"

Wren repeated it. It sat strangely on the tongue, like a stone smoothed by a different river than theirs. "Wren," they offered in return, touching their own breastbone.

And Iven—Wren saw it happen, saw the moment land—went very still. He looked at Wren with a new intensity, and said something rapid and wondering, and reached toward them, and Wren flinched back out of reach before they knew they'd moved.

"No," Wren said. "No touching. House rule."

But the boy only drew back his hand, patient as winter, and lay down in the oat sacking, and within moments—he was a child, whatever else he was; children fall asleep the way stones fall—he was breathing slow and deep among the hams and the juniper smoke-smell, asleep in the heart of the king's palace like a spark in a haystack.

Wren sat on the stair below for a long time, listening to the storm, listening to that small alien breathing, and told themselves a story about charity. About how anyone would have done it. About how tomorrow they would decide what to do properly, sensibly, safely.

They did not believe a word of it. But it was late, and the lie was warm, and they slept sitting up against the flour bins with their mother's lullabies ringing in ears that had finally, after nine years, been given leave to hear them.

* * *

*Iven dreamed the way his people dreamed: sideways, downward, in roots.*

He woke once in the black hours beneath the hanging meats, and for a heartbeat he did not know where he was, because the ceiling was beams and not living boughs, and the air smelled of dead salt instead of green water, and there was no song in the walls. Then he remembered. Remembering was a door he had learned to close quickly, these past weeks, or the grief came through it like floodwater.

The grey-eyed one was still there. Asleep against the grain sacks, chin dropped to chest, hands folded like a person accustomed to waiting. Watching over him even in sleep—or simply having nowhere better to be. Iven considered both possibilities and found he liked the second less and trusted it more.

*Tal*, the grey-eyed one had said. Broken, badly said, a word with its wings pulled off—but said. Spoken aloud, in this dead stone place, by someone whose blood sang wrong and right at once. Iven had known it the moment their eyes met across the hearth-fire: the flicker of recognition, the flinch, the way the grey-eyed one's mouth had shaped the old words when Iven spoke them, half a beat behind, like an echo remembering itself.

Grey-blood. Here, in the serpent's house, wearing an apron, feeding poison-testers' courage to a king.

Grandmother had spoken of grey-bloods. *When the covenant broke,* she said, *the border children were the ones who bled both ways. Some hid. Some forgot on purpose. Forgetting is a kind of survival, little root, but do not envy it. A tree that forgets its roots still drinks from them.*

Iven lay in the dark and listened to the grey-blood breathe, and practiced the words he must say. He had been carrying them since the burning, since the running, since the Choir's white voices first rose behind him in the passes. He carried them the way he carried the amulet: against his skin, always, because there was nowhere else safe to put them. But the grey-eyed one did not know the words. Not really. Knew their music, maybe, the way a gull knows the sea's music without knowing the sea.

So he would teach. One word at a time. One stone at a time, laid across the gap between them, until there was a path.

He found the word in his chest, the oldest one, the one that burned.

*Covenant.*

* * *

Morning came grey and dripping. Wren rose before the kitchen did, as always, and spent the day being magnificently unremarkable: tasting the king's morning porridge (safe), the chancellor's spiced wine (safe, though Vess watched Wren drink it with those flat ledger-keeper's eyes that missed nothing, and Wren performed boredom so convincingly they almost believed it themselves), the afternoon broth (safe). Between tastings they stole upward with bread and apples and a bucket, and found Iven awake and cross-legged in the loft like a small patient gargoyle among the hams.

They worked all that grey afternoon, the two of them, in the juniper-scented dark.

It went slowly. Wren had no grammar, only fragments—lullaby-words for sleep and stars and thorn-bells, a curse their mother had used on stubborn stove-lids, the names of three birds and, inexplicably, a kind of eel. Iven had infinite patience and a genius for pointing. He taught like water filling cracks: *that is bread; that is fire; that is you; that is me.* Wren taught back in the king's tongue, and Iven gathered those words too, hoarding them visibly, repeating them under his breath like counting coins.

By the time the evening bells rang, Wren could say *eat*, *sleep*, *hide*, *danger*, *tomorrow*, and *no touching*. Iven could say *bread*, *fire*, *thank you*, and—with a gravity that made Wren's neck prickle—one word more.

Because all afternoon, between the lessons, between the pointing and the naming, Iven kept returning to it. He would touch the floorboards. He would touch his own chest, then the earth beyond the walls, eastward, toward the Thornfells. And he would say:

"*Caelith.*"

At first Wren took it for another noun. Mountain. Home. Something like that. Everything in this language seemed to be something like that.

But he kept saying it. At the bread, he said it. At the sound of distant boots in the yard, he said it, urgent, gripping Wren's sleeve. When the bells rang he said it with tears standing bright in his green-gold eyes, and struck the floor twice with his small fist, and said it again, louder, and Wren realized with a slow cold turning in their gut that this was not a noun at all.

This was the message. This was the whole of why a child had crossed the Thornfells alone in a storm, mud to the eyebrows, hunted by whatever had put that terror in him. He had crossed a kingdom to find—what? Whom?—and all he could carry of his errand in his mouth was this one word, said over and over like a bell that would not stop ringing.

"*Caelith,*" he said again, softly now, pleading. He pressed his palm flat over Wren's heart. "*Caelith. Tael.*"

Come. The word meant come, or go, or both. *Covenant, come. Covenant, go.* As if the word were a place. As if the word were a task.

"I don't understand," Wren said helplessly, and for once the confession cost them nothing, because it was true in every direction. "I don't understand you. I don't understand any of this. I taste soup. That's my entire life. I taste soup and I stay invisible and I draw maps nobody sees, and you come in out of the rain speaking the language of a country that's been dead for three hundred years asking me to— to *caelith*, whatever that is, and I—"

Iven was watching them with that unnerving stillness. Then he did the thing Wren would remember all their life: he reached into his shirt and drew out something on a cord, and held it cupped in both palms like a wounded bird.

A stone. Half a stone, rather—broken clean across, ancient, its cut face veined with lines that might have been writing or might have been frost-pattern or might have been veins. Even in the dimness of the loft it seemed to hold light the way deep water holds it.

And it was singing.

Not loudly. Not even certainly—Wren might have called it imagination, a hum in the ears, the ovens' drone below. But the sound rose as the stone came near, a thin sweet note like a wet finger on a glass rim, like a voice heard through a wall, and Iven's face as he held it out was the face of someone offering the last of his bread.

"*Caelith,*" he whispered.

Wren did not take it. Their hands stayed at their sides, fists forming without permission. Every instinct honed over nine years of invisibility screamed the same instruction: *do not touch the strange glowing thing the strange child carries; do not be seen; do not be chosen; disappear.*

But underneath the screaming, quieter, older, their mother's voice was singing in a language Wren had never been allowed to understand, and the stone's thin note had found the key of it, and the two sounds were locking together like hands.

Slowly—hating themselves, unable to stop—Wren reached out and laid one fingertip against the broken stone.

The note swelled. The loft filled with it, with a chord like dawn coming up over water, and every hair on Wren's arms stood, and somewhere far below a pot lid rattled as though the whole palace had shivered. Iven's eyes went wide and wet and triumphant all at once.

And down in the yard, faint through the storm-washed quiet, a door slammed, and Chancellor Vess's dry voice said something to someone about archives, about missing pages, about *finding out who*, and the sound of it climbed the stairwell like cold water rising.

Wren snatched their hand back. The note died. In the sudden silence, Iven tucked the stone away and looked at Wren with absolute, uncomplicated trust, and Wren thought: *Oh no. Oh, you foolish child. You have no idea what you've just done to us both.*

Above the pantry, through the slatted vent, the last light failed over the Thornfells—and far off, at the edge of hearing, something in the high passes began, very softly, to sing back.
