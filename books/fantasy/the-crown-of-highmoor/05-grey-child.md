## 5. Grey Child

The pantry loft smelled of dried apples and dust and the ghost of last winter's smoke, and for three days it had been the safest place in the palace.

Wren climbed the ladder after midnight with a heel of dark bread, a wedge of hard cheese, and a cup of watered wine balanced on a board across their forearms—a taster's trick, carrying without spilling, hands free for doors and knives. The loft was a low crawl of rafters above the spice shelves where the heat of the kitchens rose through the floorboards like a slow animal breathing. No one came up here. The head cook's knees had given out years ago, and the apprentices feared the spiders more than they feared the dark.

Iven was awake. He was always awake when Wren came, curled in the corner where two beams met, watching the ladder hole with those eyes that caught the lamplight wrong—too green, too quick, like light through river ice.

"You're not sleeping," Wren said in court speech, then, clumsily, in the other tongue: *You are not sleeping.*

The words still felt strange in their mouth, like wearing someone else's boots. The child had been teaching them, patiently, the way one teaches a limping bird to fly: *no, softer; no, the breath goes here.* And Wren, who had spent nine years making themselves unremarkable, found that learning made them feel enormous. Every new word was a door opening in a wall they'd believed solid.

*Not tired,* Iven answered. Then, tapping his own chest, then the beam beneath him, then the whole shadowed loft with a sweep of his small hand: *Not-tired-here. Listening.*

"Listening to what?"

He didn't have the words to answer. Instead he pressed his palm flat against the rafter and closed his eyes, and Wren felt it—that faint hum that lived in old wood and older stone, the one every servant learned to ignore, the settling of a building that was four hundred years old and had never once settled. Except now, watching Iven listen, Wren wondered if the building wasn't settling at all. If it was remembering.

They sat together on the boards, backs against the warm chimney stone, and shared the bread. Iven ate the way he did everything—ferociously, watchfully, as though the food might be reclaimed. Three days of feeding him had taught Wren the shape of his hunger, and it was not only for bread.

The child talked constantly. That was the thing no one warned you about feral children, Wren thought—they were not silent creatures of the wild but rivers in flood, pouring out language whether or not anyone could swim in it. Most of it Wren caught in fragments now. Enough to know that Iven spoke of a *court under the root*, of *mothers who sang doors open*, of something he called—

*Covenant.*

He said it every day. Sometimes many times a day. He said it the way drowning men say a name.

"Covenant," Wren repeated now, because repetition seemed to comfort him. "I don't know the word. Not in any tongue I speak."

Iven set down his bread. He looked at Wren with an expression far too old for his face—the look of a small clerk confronted with a large ignorance—and then he began, with great deliberation, to draw in the flour dust on the boards between them.

First a mountain. The Thornfells, unmistakably, with their double crown of peaks. Then, below the drawn line of the peaks, below even the drawn ground, a second shape: a hall, columns, a tree growing up through its roof and out into the air above the mountain. Two realms, one tree. His finger hovered over the space between them, the borderland where the roots crossed some invisible line, and there he drew a circle, and inside the circle, two marks—one like a crown, one like a leaf.

"Covenant," he said again, and touched the circle.

Then he took Wren's hand.

It happened quickly, without ceremony, the way children do everything that matters. He simply reached over and turned Wren's palm upward, both his small hands wrapped around theirs, and looked at the lines of it—the calluses from knife work, the faint burn scar from a copper pot nine years ago, the ordinary topography of an ordinary servant's hand.

And then he went very still.

Wren felt the stillness move up through the contact like cold water rising. Iven's grip tightened. His eyes came up from the palm to Wren's face, and whatever he saw there made him breathe in sharply, a small wounded sound.

He spoke then—not the flood of language, not the patient teaching-words. One word. A name.

*"Seren."*

The bread went dry in Wren's throat.

That was impossible. That was a name spoken perhaps twice a year, by Wren alone, in the dark, on the anniversary of nothing in particular—a name kept folded away like a letter too dangerous to leave lying about. Seren. Their mother. Dead twenty-two years in a ditch outside Coldfern with her throat opened by men who were never caught, whose body Wren had been taken from at six years old to be raised by the crown's kindness, which was to say, its kitchen.

"How do you know that name?" Wren heard themselves ask. Their voice came out level. Nine years of tasting had trained them well; even terror could be swallowed without changing the face. "Who told you my mother's name?"

But Iven was shaking his head, urgent, frustrated, and he said it again—*Seren*—and then a rush of Old Highmoor that Wren's half-understanding caught in pieces like fish through fingers:

*...knew her... all knew her... she carried... she was...*

He pressed Wren's palm harder between his hands, and this time he didn't say the name. He said another word instead, one Wren hadn't heard before, and the sound of it went through them strangely—not like a foreign word at all, but like a word they had always known and never been permitted to say aloud.

*Ithri-el.* Grey-blooded. Though it took Wren a moment to understand that, because the literal sense of the word was different, richer: *of-the-space-between*. Born in the seam. Child of the crossing place.

"No." Wren pulled their hand back. The motion was too fast, too violent; the watered wine slopped over the board and soaked into the flour-dust map, erasing the mountain, the tree, the little crowned circle. "No, I'm not—I'm a taster. I'm nobody. My mother was a smuggler's woman from the Fells, she sang nonsense songs, that's all—"

*Songs,* Iven said, and his eyes were fierce and wet at once. *Songs. What songs? Sing them.*

"I don't remember them."

That was a lie, and the child knew it was a lie, and Wren knew he knew, and the knowledge hung between them in the lamplit dark like smoke.

Because Wren remembered. Of course they remembered. They remembered everything about her, hoarded it the way misers hoard coin, the way the starving hoard crumbs: the smell of juniper smoke in her hair. The way she counted in the old tongue when she thought Wren was asleep—*neth, oth, serai*—and how the numbers sounded like a spell. And the song. The one lullaby she sang when the storms came over the pass, the one with words Wren had never understood but had carried in their mouth for thirty years like a stone under the tongue:

*Oro ven i thalassë, oro ven i duin—*

Sleep, the mountain says, sleep, the river says.

Except that wasn't what it meant. Wren had always known, in the way you know things you cannot prove, that the translation was wrong—or rather, that it was a disguise, the way a smuggler's cart of turnips is a disguise for what lies underneath. Now, sitting in the dark with a mud-caked child looking at them as though they were a door he'd been searching half the realm for, the true meaning rose up through the nonsense like a drowned body finally surfacing:

*Sleep, the mountain says—but the mountain is only sleeping. Sleep, the river says—but the river remembers its source.*

A covenant-song. A keeping-song. Sung by an envoy's daughter to a grey-blooded child in a border hovel, so that even if everything else was lost, the words would survive somewhere, hidden in the most forgettable vessel the realm contained.

A taster. A nobody. A person built, dish by dish, year by year, to be overlooked.

"Oh," Wren said. It was a very small word for such a large collapse.

---

They denied it for another hour. Wren wants to be honest about that, later; honesty becomes important to them eventually, though it isn't yet. They denied it with the same thoroughness they brought to everything—cataloguing the evidence against themselves the way they catalogued flavors. Their mother had been human; everyone said so. Their father was unknown, which proved nothing except that their mother had kept one secret successfully, and smugglers were good at secrets. Grey-blooded. Half of anything. Wren had spent nine years being *less* than half a person by design—an instrument, a palate, a pair of careful hands. The idea that they might be *more* than half of something was not comforting. It was obscene. It was a debt arriving decades late with interest.

More than that—it was dangerous. Wren had seen what happened to people who were interesting. Interesting people got noticed, and noticed people got used, and used people got broken. The king's own brother had been interesting once. There was a tower in the east range where no one slept anymore.

So they denied it, and Iven waited them out with the terrible patience of the very young, and when Wren finally ran out of arguments, the child did the cruelest and kindest thing available to him.

He sang.

The lullaby. Wren's lullaby—except it wasn't, it never had been, it was Highmoor's lullaby, the hidden court's lullaby, sung under the roots of the world for centuries, and Iven's voice was thin and cracked with hunger but the melody fit Wren's memory exactly, note for note, the way a key fits the lock it was cut for.

Wren put their face in their hands and wept, silently, the way they did everything—without being noticed, without disturbing anyone, without leaving a trace.

Iven crawled across the boards and leaned against their side, small and warm and smelling of hay and panic, and let them.

---

Later—much later, when the kitchens below had gone quiet and the coals had settled into their long red patience—Wren held their own palm up to the lamp and looked at it properly for the first time.

An ordinary hand. That was what they had always believed. But Iven had shown them, before sleep finally dragged the child under, how to look differently. *Not with eyes,* he'd mimed, tapping his chest, then Wren's. *With the between-place.*

And now, half-believing, half-mocking themselves, Wren breathed the way he'd shown them—in through the nose, slow, letting the attention sink beneath the skin—and looked.

The lines of the palm were ordinary. But around the hand, faint as heat-shimmer over summer cobbles, there was something else. A second outline. As if the hand cast two shadows, and one of them belonged to a different light entirely.

Grey. The space between. Of-the-crossing-place.

Their mother had named them for it without ever saying the word aloud. Wren Quintana—*quintana*, fifth-born, border-folk called such children, born in the fifth season, the season that doesn't appear in any calendar. The season between.

Wren laughed once, quietly, and it came out wrong, closer to grief than humor. All those years perfecting invisibility. All those years assuming the forgetting was a talent they'd built. When had they first learned to make themselves unremarkable? At six? At five? Or had they been born knowing—born understanding, in the marrow the old blood ran through, that a child of two worlds must choose to be neither, or be destroyed by both?

Below them, through the floorboards, the palace breathed. Four hundred years of kings. Four hundred years of a treaty burned and buried and officially nonexistent. And somewhere in that vast sleeping weight, Chancellor Vess was awake—Wren was sure of it the way one is sure of weather coming—turning over ledgers, hunting a missing page, following a thread that would lead, sooner or later, to a mud-caked child in a pantry loft and the forgettable person who sheltered him.

Wren looked at their doubled-shadowed hand and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the days of being no one were numbered. Whatever came next—the Choir Iven whimpered about in his sleep, the chancellor's cold arithmetic, the thirteenth moon the child counted down each night on his fingers—it would require them to be someone. Someone specific. Someone findable.

Someone who could carry an oath, maybe. Someone who was, impossibly, the only door.

*Not me,* they wanted to say. *Find someone else. I'm just the taster.*

But their palm shimmered in the lamplight, and the mountain under the palace hummed its long remembering note, and down in the loft the child slept with his fist closed around a small amulet Wren had never seen before—half of something, worn smooth, that seemed to catch the light a heartbeat after the lamp had finished with it.

Tomorrow, Wren decided. Tomorrow they would ask him about it.

They did not know that tomorrow, the poison would already be in the wine.
