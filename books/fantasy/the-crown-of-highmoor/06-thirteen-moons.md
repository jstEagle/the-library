## 6. Thirteen Moons

The dream always began with the bells.

Not the great bronze bells of the sunken court—Iven could not remember those without his chest going hollow—but the small silver ones, the *liraveth*, that hung from the eaves of every house in Highmoor and rang only when the wind came down off the glacier carrying the scent of blooming frost-fruit. His mother had told him they were tuned to the covenant itself, that each note was a thread of the old oath still humming in the world, and that as long as even one bell sang, the court beneath the mountain was not truly dead.

In the dream, all the bells were ringing at once.

He woke in the dark of the pantry loft with flour dust on his tongue and his heart slamming like a fist against a door. Above him, through the gap where the loft met the rafters, he could hear the palace settling into its night-sounds: the groan of cooling ovens, a cat's slow footsteps along a beam, the kitchen boy Doss muttering in his sleep two floors down. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. He made himself name them one by one, the way his mother had taught him—*name what is real, and the dream loses its teeth*—but his hands would not stop shaking, because in the dream the bells had been ringing over burning houses.

* * *

He had run for nine days.

That was the part of the memory that came back cleanest, cleaner than his mother's face or the shape of the throne room, cleaner than anything except the singing. Nine days of running through the deep ways under the Thornfells, and the whole time, behind him, the Hollow Choir was singing.

They did not hunt like soldiers. Soldiers shouted; soldiers crashed through the bracken and left their fear smeared across the air where anyone grey-blooded could read it. The Choir hunted in silence, in unison, their voices braided into one long sustained note that slid through stone as if stone were water. Iven's grandmother had called it the *unmaking song*. She had said that when you heard it, you must not listen—you must fill your ears with your own heartbeat, your own name, your own mother's voice saying your own true name in Old Highmoor, because the song worked by hollowing you out, by finding the shape of everything you loved and scooping it away until there was nothing left inside you but room.

Iven had heard the song come up through the soles of his feet on the ninth night, when he was crouched in a crevice no wider than a hearth-flue, three days' climb past the last of the standing stones. And he had done what she taught him. He had pressed his palms against the cold rock and said his name over and over—Iven, Iven, Iven of the line of Aerinhel, last-born of the hidden court—and he had thought about his mother's hands folding honey-cakes at midwinter, and the song had passed him by like a cold wind passing a lit window.

But he had heard, in the spaces between the notes, the voices of the people it had already taken. His aunt Sefra, who taught the year-children to whistle to moths. The twins from the terraced orchards who had once pulled him out of the ice-melt stream. Their voices were in the Choir now, woven into the braid, singing the unmaking song with mouths that no longer remembered why they had ever sung anything else.

That was the part he could never say aloud, not even to Wren, whose pantry loft smelled of cardamom and whose silences were kinder than most people's speech. That the dead were not gone. That the dead had been *kept*.

* * *

Daylight in the palace was a strange country. Iven learned its customs the way he had once learned the paths between the mushroom-farms: by watching, by keeping small, by memorizing which corridors filled with boots at which hours. Wren had drilled him in it with fierce, whispered urgency—*you are a scullery orphan from the coast, you are mute, you are nobody, nobody is the safest thing to be in this place*—and Iven had obeyed, because Wren's fear had a taste he recognized. It was the taste of someone who had survived by making themselves invisible, and Iven knew that flavor from the inside. Every child of Highmoor had learned it in the last days.

So he scrubbed pots. He hauled water. He kept his eyes down and his mouth shut and let the kitchen assume he was simple, and all the while he counted.

He counted because counting was the other thing his grandmother had given him before the end. On the final night, when the first bell-towers were falling and the adults were arguing in rapid, desperate Old Highmoor about roads and seals and who would carry what, she had knelt in front of him and gripped his shoulders hard enough to bruise.

"Listen to me, little wren-of-the-court." She had used the pet name even then, even with the sky coming down. "You know the moons?"

He had nodded. Thirteen moons hung in the sky above Highmoor—or rather, twelve hung there and the thirteenth was a secret, a sliver of shadow-stuff that only showed itself once a year, on the longest night, when the veil between what-is and what-was grew thin as frost on a windowpane. The year-children called it the Hidden Moon and dared each other to find it.

"The covenant was spoken under the Hidden Moon," his grandmother said. "Three hundred years ago, when the realms were woven together, the oath was sealed on the night the Hidden Moon crested the Thornfells. And an oath spoken under that moon can only be remade under that moon. Do you understand? The door opens once. Once a year, and—" her voice had cracked, and he had seen for the first time that she was not old and calm but old and terrified, "—and perhaps not again after this year. The magic is failing, Iven. The weaving is rotting. If the covenant is not reforged before the Hidden Moon turns—if it wanes without a new oath spoken—the threads will not just be broken. They will be *gone*. Both realms will forget they were ever one thing. And everything of Highmoor that still survives in the pockets and the deep places will fade like breath off glass."

"And if it is reforged?"

She had smiled then, and it was the worst smile he had ever seen, because it was full of hope and full of goodbye at the same time.

"Then the bells ring again," she said. "All of them."

Then she had pressed the amulet into his hand—the half-stone, warm as a held bird—and pushed him toward the deep ways, and that was the last time anyone touched him with love instead of hunger.

Now, scrubbing pots in a foreign palace, Iven counted nights on the inside of his wrist with a nub of charcoal, the way he had counted them through nine days of running. Twelve moons to turn. One hidden moon rising at the end of them, cresting the mountains he could see through the kitchen's high window, white-capped and indifferent, wearing the ruins of his whole world like a crown worn backwards.

* * *

Some nights Wren climbed the ladder to the loft after the kitchens went dark, bringing bread or cheese or questions wrapped in silence. Wren never asked for the whole story. Wren had a way of sitting down slowly, arranging themselves carefully, like a person approaching a bird that might startle—and then simply waiting, which Iven understood was its own kind of language. In Highmoor they had a word for that kind of listening: *selvarin*, the act of making room for another's grief without trying to carry it for them. There was no word for it in the common tongue. Wren had invented one anyway, or seemed to, simply by doing it.

It was on one of these nights, with rain needling the high window and the charcoal marks on Iven's wrist reading eleven-and-turning, that he finally tried to explain the deadline.

He started with the moons, drawing them in spilled flour on the floorboards: twelve circles, and then a thirteenth he drew as a circle with a hole in it, because that was honestly closer to the truth. Wren watched, elbows on knees, flour dusting their forearms, and when Iven tapped the holed circle and then pointed out the window at the Thornfells, then at himself, then made the gesture he had decided meant *before*—one palm sweeping flat, like a blade clearing a table—Wren went very still.

"Before the hidden moon," Wren said slowly, in the common tongue, and then, haltingly, shaping the sounds like a man crossing a frozen river one careful step at a time: "*Aen... vael... sithren.*"

The words went through Iven like the first sip of hot broth after nine cold days. *Before the veiled moon turns.* His mother's phrase. His grandmother's phrase. Hearing it in Wren's border-accented mouth, mangled and imperfect and *alive*, made his eyes sting so suddenly he had to look down at the flour and pretend to redraw the moons.

"Yes," he said, in the common tongue, one of the few words he trusted. Then, because Wren deserved more than one word, he switched to Old Highmoor and let it pour out of him—all of it, the bells and the burning, the Choir's braided song, his grandmother's grip on his shoulders, the amulet, the counting, the door that opened once a year and might never open again. He spoke fast, too fast, the way the dying speak, and he knew Wren caught maybe one word in five, but Wren didn't interrupt, didn't flinch, just listened with that *selvarin* stillness until Iven ran out of breath and sat back against the grain sacks, wrung out, lighter, emptier.

Wren was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the window.

Then Wren reached out—not touching, just near—and pointed at the flour-drawn moon with the hole in it, and said, in careful Old Highmoor, the words arriving like stones placed one atop another:

"How long."

Iven looked at the marks on his wrist. Eleven moons turned, and turning. He held up his hands and began to count, and somewhere in the middle of the counting Wren's face changed—went pale in the lamplight, went the color of the dough they kneaded at dawn—and Iven realized that Wren had understood. Not the words. The arithmetic. The terrible, simple arithmetic of a door that was already swinging shut.

* * *

After that, Wren stopped sleeping properly. Iven could tell by the shadows under their eyes, by the way they tasted the king's breakfast with a new sharpness, as though poison might be hiding anywhere, as though the whole palace had become a dish that needed testing. They began asking questions in fragments—pointing at the amulet under Iven's shirt, at the mountains, at the flour-map of smuggler roads Iven had glimpsed them sketching and quickly smearing—and Iven answered what he could, and what he couldn't answer he sang, softly, the opening bars of the covenant's melody, the one every Highmoor child learned before they learned their letters.

The first time he sang it, Wren's hands stopped moving entirely. They stood in the middle of the dark pantry with flour on their arms and something breaking quietly behind their face, and when Iven finished, Wren said, barely audible: "My mother used to hum that."

And Iven had looked at this grey-eyed, forgettable, careful person who had hidden themselves so well that even they had lost the seams, and felt the truth rise in him like groundwater—*you are one of us, you have been one of us all along, the blood remembers even when the tongue forgets*—and he had reached for the word *covenant*, the word he carried like a coal in his chest, ready to press it into Wren's palm the way his grandmother had pressed the stone into his.

But the palace bell rang the hour, and boots went past in the corridor, and the moment folded itself away like a letter unread.

There were eleven moons left. Perhaps fewer, if the magic was failing the way his grandmother feared. Somewhere beneath the Thornfells, in pockets of old light, whatever remained of his people was fading like breath off glass. Somewhere in this palace, men with ledgers and lanterns were already hunting the last heir of Highmoor. And here in a flour-smelling loft, the only person alive who could speak the oath in both tongues was pretending, with tremendous skill, not to know what they were.

Iven lay back against the grain sacks and closed his eyes, and in the dark behind his eyelids the silver bells rang over burning houses, and his grandmother's voice said *the door opens once*, and the thirteenth moon turned, and turned, and turned.
