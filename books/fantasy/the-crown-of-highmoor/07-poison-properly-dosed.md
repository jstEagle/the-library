## 7. Poison, Properly Dosed

The king's table was set for forty, and every one of those settings was a small theater of power: the placement of the salt cellar, the angle of the wine ewer, the order in which the dishes would arrive. Wren had learned to read that theater the way other people read scripture. Tonight it told them that Chancellor Vess had arranged the seating himself, which was unusual, and that the third course—lampreys in a green sauce, a dish the king loved and rarely permitted himself—had been added at the last hour by someone in the kitchens who did not normally add things.

Wren stood at their station behind the screen of gilded lattice near the king's right hand, where they could be seen when being seen was useful and forgotten when it wasn't. Nine years in that alcove had taught them the exact posture of useful invisibility: hands folded, chin level, eyes soft as if perpetually half-listening to something more interesting elsewhere. Nobody looked twice at a taster. A taster was furniture that swallowed.

But tonight Wren's eyes were not soft. Tonight they kept returning to the lampreys.

* * *

The trouble with an immunity built dish by dish over nine years is that it is not one immunity but a thousand small ones, each earned in its own private agony. There had been the winter of the arsenic-laced honey, three days of fever and a tongue that blistered so badly Wren had lived on snow for a week. The nightshade in the elderflower cordial, which had stopped their heart for four seconds—or so the old physician Brannic claimed, counting on his fingers with a look of scholarly delight while Wren lay gray-faced on his cot. The slow accumulation of heavy metals in the river fish served at court, which was why Wren's hair had gone iron-grey at the temples before they turned thirty, a vanity sacrificed without mourning. Each poison had left its sediment. Wren sometimes imagined their blood as a riverbed, layered with silt from every attempt on the king's life that had passed through their mouth first.

It made them, in the narrowest sense, the safest person in the palace. It also meant they could taste things no one else could. Bitterness beneath sweetness. The green metallic whisper of compounds that should not be in a sauce at all.

The lampreys arrived under a silver dome carried by a boy named Tam, whose hands were steady and whose face was blank in the way faces went blank when someone had paid for blankness or frightened it into place. Wren noted both. They stepped out from behind the lattice, took up the small silver fork reserved for this purpose, and cut a portion from the edge of the dish—the edge first, always, because poisons pooled where sauces thickened against porcelain—and put it in their mouth.

Green sauce. Sorrel, verjuice, a thread of mustard. Lamprey rich and riverine. And underneath, so faint that a lesser tongue would have called it imagination: a coldness. Not bitter, not sour, not any of the honest flavors poison wore when men made it. This was a flavor like standing in a doorway between seasons. Like the air off the Thornfells at dusk. Like—

Like the lullabies. Like the language Iven spoke in the pantry loft when he thought Wren was asleep below, murmuring words older than crowns into the dark because they were the only words that felt like home.

Wren did not swallow. Nine years of discipline closed their throat like a door. They turned, walked the six steps back to the lattice, and spat into the bronze spitting-cup that hung there for exactly this ceremony, and the whole room watched them do it, because a taster spitting was theater too—the best kind, the kind that ended careers and occasionally lives.

"Something wrong?" Chancellor Vess asked from the king's left hand. His voice was mild. His quill-thin fingers rested on the table beside his untouched wine, and he did not look at the lampreys at all. He looked at Wren.

"The sauce," Wren said, and heard their own voice come out level, bored even, pitched to carry only as far as the high table. "Off. Perhaps the sorrel."

A lie, delivered smoothly. Because the truth—that the poison in the king's favorite dish sang with the same cold music as a hidden child asleep above the flour barrels—was not a thing to say aloud in a hall full of courtiers already hungry for scandal.

King Aldous laughed, the big easy laugh that had made him beloved once, before grief and chancellors got between him and his people. "Then we'll eat the pheasant and count ourselves lucky. Wren, you've saved my life so often I ought to knight you." He said it the way he always said it, warmly and meaning nothing, and Wren bowed the way they always bowed, deeply and meaning less.

But under the bow, behind the lattice, Wren's heart was going like a war drum, and their mind was already moving through the palace the way water moves through cracks—fast, silent, finding every weakness.

* * *

They waited until the second course was cleared and the king was deep in negotiation-by-anecdote with the ambassador from Callowmere. Then Wren slipped out through the servants' passage, took the back stair two at a time, and let themselves into the kitchen's outer yard where the rain had softened to a fine drifting mist.

Tam was there, stacking domes to be scoured, alone for the moment.

"You carried the lampreys," Wren said. No accusation in it. Just fact, laid down like a card.

"I carried what Cook gave me." Tam didn't look up. His knuckles were white around the handle of a dome.

"And who gave it to Cook?"

Silence. Then, very quietly, without raising his eyes: "I don't know. A man. Paid in new coin. He asked about—" Tam's throat worked. "He asked whether the palace kept anyone strange lately. Any foundlings. Any children come out of nowhere."

The mist beaded on Wren's shoulders and did not feel like water. It felt like the coldness in the sauce.

"Describe him," Wren said.

"Pale. Grey-eyed. He hummed while he talked. Like singing, but wrong. Like singing remembered by someone who'd never heard it." Tam finally looked up, and his young face was naked with fear. "I didn't tell him anything. I swear it on my mother. But he knew, master Wren. Before he asked, he knew there was something to ask about."

Wren pressed a coin into the boy's hand—not payment, just weight, something real to hold—and went back inside through the door that smelled of bread and tallow and safety that no longer existed anywhere in the world.

* * *

The pantry loft was warm with the smell of grain. Iven sat cross-legged on the flour sacks with the stub of a charcoal pencil in his fist, drawing on the smooth shelf-board: spirals, mostly, and small precise stars, and once—a thing that had made Wren's chest ache for a week—a crooked little house with smoke curling from its chimney.

Iven looked up when Wren came through the hatch, and his face did the thing it always did now, the flicker from wariness to recognition to something dangerously close to trust. He was perhaps eight years old, all elbows and mud-colored hair, with eyes the grey-green of deep water. When he spoke it was in Old Highmoor, that buried language, and Wren understood perhaps one word in five—but the word he said most often, the word that had become the hinge of everything, Wren knew perfectly.

"Covenant," Iven said now, pointing at Wren, then at his own chest, then out toward the wall, toward the mountains beyond it. *Covenant. You. Me. There.*

"Not tonight," Wren said, in the common tongue, kneeling so their eyes were level with his. "Tonight we stay quiet. Tonight—"

Iven caught their sleeve. Small fingers, surprisingly strong. He said something rapid and urgent, and though Wren couldn't parse the words, they could parse the tone, because fear sounds like fear in every language ever spoken. The boy pointed at Wren's mouth. Then at his own ears. Then he made a gesture Wren had seen him make before, fingers splayed and trembling, and said a word Wren hadn't learned yet but had begun to dread: a low, hollow-sounding word, like wind through a broken flute.

*The Choir.* Or whatever the Choir was called in the tongue of a murdered kingdom.

"Yes," Wren said quietly. "I know. Someone's looking for you. I tasted it tonight—I mean, I tasted something, and it led me to knowing, and I'm explaining badly." They exhaled. "Someone poisoned the king's dinner with fae-work, Iven. With your people's craft. Do you understand? Someone wants the court to believe the fae realm struck at the crown. Someone wants a war, or the shape of one, so that hunting you becomes law instead of murder."

Iven stared at them. He didn't understand the sentences. But he understood *poisoned*, because Wren mimed it—cupped hand, lifted to lips, then the clutching, the falling—and the color drained out of the boy's face until he matched the flour sacks. He began to speak fast, gesturing, and one phrase recurred, rising like a refrain: *aen varath, aen varath,* and he touched his chest each time, and Wren finally understood that it meant *they will say it was us.*

"They will say it was us," Wren repeated, in common, tasting how true it was. "Yes."

* * *

That night Wren did not sleep. They sat in their narrow cell of a room above the mews with a single candle and their secret maps spread across the floor—the smuggler roads through the Thornfells, inked in their own hand over nine years of careful listening, every pass and hollow and shepherd's stair that the crown's soldiers had never found because smugglers don't talk to soldiers and food-tasters don't talk to anyone.

Nine years of building invisibility, and here was the truth of it, laid out in candlelight: every skill they had honed had been honed for escape. Every relationship maintained at arm's length had been maintained so that leaving would cost nothing. Wren had told themselves it was prudence. Sitting there, listening to the rain and to the faint sound of a child humming lullabies in a dead language two floors below, they admitted what it actually was. It was a life spent practicing departure. A life lived in the anteroom of loss, never entering any room far enough to be trapped in it when the fire came.

And now the fire was coming, and the maps were ready, and the only thing Wren had never planned for was the weight of a sleeping child they could not leave behind.

There was a knock. Soft. Wrong-hour soft.

Brannic stood in the corridor, ancient and rumpled, holding a covered dish as if it were a sleeping cat. "You spat tonight," he said. "Properly. Which means you tasted something worth spitting, and 'off sorrel' is a lie, because I made the sorrel myself and it was perfect." He pushed past Wren into the room without invitation, as old men do, and set the dish down. Under the cloth: the remains of the sauce, scraped from the king's plate by a physician who apparently still moved like a rumor.

"I want you to smell it," Brannic said. "Not taste. Smell, and tell me what you know that I don't, because I've been a poisoner's enemy for fifty years and I have never smelled anything like this."

Wren leaned over the dish. The coldness rose up like breath from a well. Green sorrel, yes, and under it that doorway-between-seasons chill, and under *that*—faintly, horribly—something almost beautiful. A scent like frost on rowan berries. Like moonlight given a smell.

"It isn't from here," Wren said. Their voice sounded far away to themselves. "It isn't from anywhere in the nine kingdoms. It's—" *From the lullabies. From the border. From the half of me my mother never explained.* "It's fae-craft. Old craft. Whoever brewed it wanted it recognized eventually, but not in time. A poison calibrated to kill slowly enough that the taster's warning comes too late to save the king, but clearly enough that everyone knows what realm it came from."

Brannic was quiet a long moment. "You're telling me someone wants the king dead and the fae blamed."

"I'm telling you someone wants a war with a country that doesn't officially exist, so that they can hunt something in these walls with the full blessing of the crown." Wren met the old man's eyes. "Chancellor Vess rearranged the seating tonight. The dish was added late. The carrier was questioned afterward by a grey-eyed man who hums wrong."

Brannic's weathered face went carefully still, the stillness of a man deciding how much terror to permit himself. "Vess has been pulling pages from the archives," he said at last. "My cousin is a scrivener there. Treaty of Ash material. Everything about the old covenant, gone or copied or—who knows. And now this." He rubbed his jaw. "You should run, Wren. Whatever you're mixed in—and you are mixed in, don't give me that furniture-face—you should take whoever you're hiding and run tonight."

*Whoever you're hiding.* Wren kept their expression smooth and their pulse did not, and Brannic smiled thinly, because he had been a poisoner's enemy for fifty years and missed nothing.

"Thank you," Wren said.

"Don't thank me. Get out. And Wren—" at the door, he paused, "—if it's fae-craft, and you can taste it, ask yourself why. Poisons don't sing to empty blood."

He left. The candle guttered. Wren sat among their maps with their hands flat on the floor as if steadying the world, and let the question Brannic had planted do its slow work.

Why could they taste it? Why had the coldness in the sauce felt like the lullabies? Why did a hidden child's language sit in Wren's ear like a key sitting in a lock, half-turned, waiting?

Because blood remembers what mouths are forbidden to say. Because somewhere in the silt-layered riverbed of them, under the arsenic winters and the iron-grey temples and the nine years of practiced departure, there was a second country, and it had just sent Wren a message written in poison:

*We see you, grey child. Choose.*

Downstairs, faint through the floors, Iven hummed a lullaby in his sleep. Wren blew out the candle, rolled the maps into a oilcloth sleeve, and began, methodically, calmly, with the same care they had once used to build an invisible life—to pack.
