## 8. The Smuggler's Road

They left before the fourth bell, when the palace slept thickest and even the night-porters dozed over their dice.

Wren had spent nine years learning the rhythms of that sleep—which stair groaned under the third step's weight, which corridor the watch avoided because of a draft that made candles gutter and men nervous, which gate the scullery boys bribed open with stolen honey-wine on feast nights. Every one of those small, shameful knowledges had been gathered for one purpose only: survival. To slip out unseen if the day ever came when the king died of something Wren failed to catch, and someone needed a taster to blame.

Wren had never imagined using them to run *toward* anything.

Now Iven's small hand was wrapped in theirs, cold as a river stone, and they were moving through the dark like water finding a crack.

*Not a word,* Wren signed—the old kitchen cant, half gesture, half superstition, invented by cooks who didn't want the steward knowing how much wine went missing. *Feet soft. Breathe through your nose.*

Iven nodded once. He'd learned fast, this child. Three days in a pantry loft, and he already moved like Wren did—weight on the balls of his feet, shoulders loose, eyes always finding the exits. It should have been heartbreaking, a boy of perhaps eight years with a fugitive's instincts already grown into his bones. Instead it filled Wren with a fierce, unfamiliar pride, as though some skill of theirs had finally found an heir.

The scullery gate opened to honey-wine and a copper coin pressed into a sleeping boy's palm. Beyond it, the palace gardens fell away into the terraced slopes of the lower city, and beyond *that*, black against the lesser blackness of the sky, rose the Thornfells.

Wren stopped at the garden wall and looked at them for a long moment. Nine years in the palace, and still those mountains looked back at Wren like a mother's face seen across a crowded room—known before memory, loved before sense.

"You're smiling," Iven said. His Common Tongue was improving by the hour, though the words came out round-edged, shaped by Old Highmoor vowels. "You smile wrong. Like it hurts."

"It does." Wren swung up onto the wall. "I haven't had much practice."

* * *

The first road was not a road.

It was a sheep-track that became a goat-path that became a suggestion of passage along a streambed, and Wren took it without hesitation because they had drawn it a hundred times in their head, inked it into the margins of recipe books and psalters, memorized every stone the way other people memorized prayers. The smugglers' roads through the Thornfells were the great secret of Wren's life—the inheritance no one had meant to leave them, mapped from stories their mother sang while kneading bread, completed over years of careful listening: a drunk carter here, a homesick guard there, each one leaking a mile of path that official maps swore did not exist.

The crown believed the Thornfells impassable except through Cold Pass, where the king's toll-gate stood and the king's soldiers counted every head. That belief had kept the hidden court hidden for three hundred years. Tonight it kept two fugitives alive.

By full dark they had climbed above the tree line, and the world opened up in a way that made Wren's chest ache. The moon—waxing toward its thirteenth turn, swollen and strange-colored, a bruised silver—poured light across the high fells until the whole range gleamed like the spine of some enormous sleeping animal. Wind came off the snowfields carrying the smell of stone and old ice and, faintly, impossibly, something green and sweet that had no business existing at this altitude.

Iven stopped walking. His nostrils flared.

"Do you smell it?" he whispered. "That's the court. That's—" He pressed both hands flat against his sternum, as though holding something in. "Under us. It smells like home."

Wren smelled it too. And underneath the sweetness, underneath the wonder, they felt the thing they had been refusing to feel since a mud-caked child appeared on the kitchen step: that the mountains knew them. That every step deeper into the Thornfells was less like flight and more like return.

"We keep moving," Wren said, rougher than intended. "Smell it again when we're not about to die."

* * *

They nearly died for the first time at moonset, in the narrows above Greywater Fall.

Wren heard it before understanding it—a sound like wind through a reed bed, except there were no reeds at this height, no wind either, just a stillness so complete that the smallest noise carried for miles. A whistling. Thin, tuneless, patient.

Iven went rigid. His hand closed on Wren's sleeve hard enough to bruise.

"The Choir," he breathed. "They're singing the finding-song."

"Choir? Those are scouts. You said—"

"I said the Choir hunts me. I didn't say they'd be *here*." His voice cracked with a child's helpless fury. "They shouldn't have crossed the pass so fast. Unless—" He swallowed. "Unless they don't need roads."

Wren pulled him down behind a shelf of granite, heart hammering against their ribs like a trapped bird. Below them, maybe three hundred yards down the slope, pale shapes moved through the boulder field. Not walking—*gliding*, almost, the way herons move through shallow water, unhurried and utterly certain. Six of them. They wore grey traveling cloaks, and even at this distance Wren could hear fragments of their song threading up the mountainside: a low, looping melody with words in a language that scraped against Wren's ears like a familiar voice speaking through a locked door.

Because Wren understood pieces of it. That was the horror of it. The finding-song was in Old Highmoor—or something near it, worn smooth and wrong, like a coin rubbed blank—and the words, when Wren could catch them, were simple. *Small one. Hidden one. Come home, come home, come home.*

"They're calling him," Wren realized aloud. "Like a shepherd calls a lost lamb."

"Worse." Iven had gone grey around the mouth. "My nurse used to sing that. Before. They take the songs of the court and turn them inside out. That's what they *are*. People who let the broken treaty hollow them out and fill them with service." He looked up at Wren, and his eyes in the moonlight were ancient and eight years old at once. "Grey-bloods. Like you."

Wren's throat closed. *Like you.* The words they'd been dodging for days, delivered now in the worst possible place—at the top of the world, with hunters below and nowhere to go but forward.

"Later," Wren managed. "We argue about my blood later. Right now we lose them."

Losing them was the work of the next four hours, and it was, Wren would admit later, the finest work of their life.

Every trick, every scrap of smuggler-craft hoarded like a miser's coins, spent all at once. They waded upstream through freezing shallows to kill scent. They doubled back along a ledge and dropped down through a waterfall's curtain, letting the spray scatter their trail. When the whistling drew close again, Wren did something they'd only ever heard described by a one-eyed peddler in a dockside tavern: they cracked open a twist of paper from their kit—chalk-powder and crushed bitterleaf, the mixture smugglers called *ghostdust*—and cast it to the wind, blurring whatever thread of scent the Choir followed into a thousand false ones.

And through all of it, Wren ran.

That was the part that astonished them most. Nine years of careful, deliberate smallness—of tasting poison in measured sips, of keeping their shoulders curved inward, of being furniture, being shadow, being nothing—and now their body remembered what it was for. Lungs working like bellows. Legs eating the slope. Blood singing loud enough to drown out fear. Somewhere between the second ridge and the third, Wren caught themselves grinning, teeth bared into the freezing wind, and thought: *this is what I was hiding all that speed for. This is what all that careful dying was saving me for.*

"What's wrong with your face?" Iven panted, when they paused in the lee of a cairn.

"Nothing," Wren said. "Everything. Keep moving."

* * *

Dawn found them in the hanging valley the smugglers called the Shepherd's Pocket—a bowl of green folded into the high fells, invisible from any direction but straight above, sheltered enough that a few stunted rowans grew there in defiance of all reason. A spring rose from the rocks, cold and iron-tasting. There was an old drystone shelter, roof fallen in, walls waist-high, that generations of runners had used and never spoken of.

Wren got a fire going in the shelter's corner—small, smokeless, fed with dry rowan and peat—because Iven was shivering in a way that frightened them more than the Choir had. Then they sat with their backs against the warm stone and watched the light come up over the peaks, gold flooding down the valleys like something poured, and let themselves feel, for exactly sixty seconds, magnificent.

"We lost them," Iven said. It wasn't quite a question.

"For now. Ghostdust buys hours, not days." Wren passed him a strip of dried venison. "Eat. You'll need it."

He ate, but his eyes stayed on Wren, and Wren recognized the look. It was the look of someone assembling courage piece by piece, the way Wren assembled immunity dish by dish.

"You enjoyed it," Iven said finally. "Running. Even with them behind us. You enjoyed it."

Wren considered lying. Decided the child had earned better. "Yes."

"Why? You lived safe. Warm kitchens. The king's own table. Why does running make you smile?"

Because, Wren thought, because for nine years every skill I had was pointed at nothing, at staying alive in place, at being a lock that never opened. Because competence without purpose is just a slower kind of drowning. Because last night, for the first time since my mother died, everything I know mattered—every secret path, every dirty trick, every hour spent listening to drunk carters—and I wasn't spending it to survive. I was spending it on *you*.

"Because it's the first time in nine years I've been good at something that counts," Wren said instead, and was surprised at how steady their voice came out.

Iven chewed on that, literally and otherwise. Then: "My mother says—I mean, she *said*—" His jaw tightened. He still stumbled on her, every time, like a tooth finding the hole where a tooth had been. "She said the border people were like that. Half of each world and at home in neither, so they learned everything twice as well, so they'd be needed somewhere." He glanced sideways. "She said that's why the covenant needs a grey child to speak it. Not despite the being-torn. *Because* of it."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, a bird cried out across the valley, thin and wild.

"And where is your mother's covenant now?" Wren asked quietly. "Your court is ash. Your choir hunts children. Being torn in half hasn't saved anyone, Iven. It's just made us useful to people who want doors opened."

"That's why they need it to be *freely* chosen." He said it simply, the way children say enormous things. "The steward told me. An oath forced is just a chain. An oath chosen—" He shrugged, small shoulders under the too-big coat Wren had stolen for him. "An oath chosen is a bridge."

Wren looked at him for a long time. Eight years old, orphaned, hunted across three hundred miles of hostile country, and still handing out truths like sweets from his pocket.

"Eat your venison," Wren said, because there was nothing else equal to the moment.

* * *

They were two days on the smuggler's road when the song found them again.

This time there was no mistaking it for wind. It came at dusk, as they picked their way down through a forest of black pines toward the valley of Coldfern, and it rose from *behind* them and *below* them at once, which should have been impossible, which meant there were more than six, which meant the Choir had split and swept wide and were closing like a hand.

The melody drifted up through the trees, beautiful, that was the obscene thing about it, beautiful the way a lullaby is beautiful, and the words slid into Wren's ear in the language of their cradle:

*Come home, come home, come home, little door,*

*the court is broken, the door remains,*

*come home, come home, and be unmade—*

Iven made a sound like a kicked animal and clapped his hands over his ears. Wren grabbed his wrist and ran.

Down through the pines, branches whipping, the ground falling away faster than feet could safely take it, sliding the last stretch in a controlled collapse of pine needles and loose stone. The song swelled behind them—not gaining, not yet, but *spreading*, voices layering on voices until the whole mountain seemed to hum with it, and Wren understood with cold clarity that this was not pursuit anymore. This was a net being drawn.

There was one place left. One card.

At the bottom of the valley, where the pine forest broke open onto rough pasture and a stream ran loud over white stones, stood a long low building with turf on its roof and a lantern burning yellow in its window. Smoke curled from the chimney. A horse stamped in a lean-to. It looked like nothing—a drover's inn, a shepherd's bothy, forgettable.

Wren had never been inside it. But Wren knew its name, knew the family that kept it, knew the sign scratched on its lintel that only runners could read: a circle with a line through it, the mark of the free road.

The wayhouse at Coldfern.

"Hold on to me," Wren said, "and don't stop for anything," and they broke from the treeline into the open, running for the lantern-light with the Hollow Choir's song rising behind them like a tide, and the door ahead opened before they reached it—as if someone inside had been watching the treeline all evening, waiting for exactly this.
