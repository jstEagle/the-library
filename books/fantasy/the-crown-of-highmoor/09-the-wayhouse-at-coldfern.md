## 9. The Wayhouse at Coldfern

The rain found them before the road did.

It came down the Thornfells in grey sheets, turning the smuggler's path into a riverbed, and by the time the trees broke open around the clearing at Coldfern, Iven was shivering so hard his teeth clicked like knucklebones. Wren carried him on their back for the last mile, the boy's thin arms looped under their chin, his breath hot and ragged against their ear. He had stopped speaking somewhere past the second ridge—not from fear, Wren thought, but from exhaustion, the way a candle gutters not because the wind takes it but because there is simply nothing left to burn.

The wayhouse crouched at the treeline like something grown rather than built: low walls of fieldstone furred with moss, a roof of old slate sagging under decades of lichen, one chimney breathing smoke into the wet dark. No sign hung above its door. No road led to it that any honest map admitted to. It existed the way smugglers' things existed—in the negative space between what the crown knew and what it chose not to see.

Wren had been here twice before, nine years ago and then seven, carrying messages sewn into coat linings and tins of preserved plums with hollowed centers. They had been younger then, newer to the palace, still learning how to be invisible. The woman who ran the place had fed them stew and never once asked their name.

They knocked the way smugglers knocked—three slow, two fast, one slow—and waited while something heavy shifted inside.

The door opened on a chain, then all the way. Maren Coldfern filled the frame the way she always had, broad as a door herself, iron-grey hair braided tight to her skull, a cleaver held loosely in one hand out of habit rather than threat. Her eyes went to Wren first, narrowed, cataloguing—the mud, the blood crusted along their jaw where a branch had caught them on the descent, the child on their back.

"Well," she said. "Nine years, and you come knocking in weather like this with a drowned cat on your shoulders."

"He's not a cat," Wren said. Their voice came out cracked. "He's—" They didn't have a word yet. Heir. Fugitive. Reason. "He's mine to keep safe."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes—recalculation, the quick arithmetic of a woman who had survived thirty years on the wrong side of every law by knowing exactly when a sentence changed its weight. She stepped back and pulled the door wide.

"In, then. Both of you. And Wren—" her voice dropped, quiet under the drumming rain, "—you were followed over the pass. Not close. But followed."

* * *

Inside, the wayhouse smelled of peat smoke, tallow, and dried herbs hanging from the rafters in bundles that brushed the crown of Wren's head as they ducked through. A fire burned low in the great hearth. Two smugglers sat at the long table playing cards with a deck soft as cloth from handling—one of them, a lean man called Ferro with a scar splitting his eyebrow, looked up and gave Wren a nod that was almost warm. Almost. Smugglers did not do warm. They did *recognized*, which was worth more.

Maren took Iven without asking, lifted him off Wren's back as easily as lifting a sack of grain, and settled him in the chair nearest the fire. The boy flinched at first—Wren saw it, the whole-body flinch of someone who had learned that hands meant harm—but Maren's hands were already retreating, wrapping a blanket around his shoulders instead of touching him, and slowly the flinch eased into something like wonder. He stared at the fire the way he stared at everything in the lowlands: as if it might be a trick, as if it might vanish if he blinked too long.

"Soup," Maren said, and it was not a question. She ladled barley soup into three bowls, set bread and hard cheese beside them, and only then sat down across from Wren, folding her arms, waiting with the patience of a woman who had outlasted tax collectors, border patrols, and two husbands.

Wren ate. It was habit older than thought—to taste everything first, to hold each mouthful on the tongue and count. Barley, leek, salt pork, a bitterness at the edge that was wild sorrel and nothing else. Safe. They caught themselves doing it even now, even here, nine years of the king's table ground into them deeper than any oath, and felt something twist in their chest that was not quite shame and not quite grief.

Iven ate like a wolf cub, hunched over his bowl, guarding it with one arm. Halfway through he paused, looked up at Maren, and said something in Old Highmoor—a question, by the lift of it, pointing at the fire and then at the roof beams.

Maren didn't answer. But Wren saw it happen: a flicker across her face, quick as a fish under ice. Recognition. Not of the words—of the *sound* of them.

"You know that language," Wren said quietly.

"I know a song in it." Maren's arms stayed folded. "My grandmother sang it. Said it came down from her grandmother, who heard it from a lady who passed through these hills before the Thornfells were closed." She tilted her head at Iven. "Never met a living soul who spoke it, though. Thought they were all dead or gone under."

"They nearly are," Wren said. And then, because the fire was warm and the rain sealed the world outside and Maren had just risked her neck opening her door to them: "There are people hunting him. They wear grey cloaks and they sing when they kill. They're called the Hollow Choir."

Ferro's cards stopped mid-shuffle. The other smuggler, a round-faced youth Wren didn't know, went pale.

"The Choir." Maren said it flatly, tasting the words. "Grey cloaks. Singing." She was silent a moment. "We lost a runner in the spring. Dara, her name was. Found her at the ford below Ravenmere, no wound on her, smiling like she'd seen something beautiful." Her jaw tightened. "The river folk said she was singing when they found her. A song nobody knew."

Nobody spoke. In the hearth, a log shifted and collapsed into embers, throwing sparks up the flue like startled birds.

"They want him dead," Wren said. "Or worse than dead. And they'll burn anything between them and him. If you want us gone by morning, say it now and we go."

Maren looked at Iven—at the boy asleep on his feet, swaying over his empty bowl, too exhausted even to be afraid anymore—and her face did something complicated and final.

"You'll sleep in the loft," she said. "And tomorrow you'll tell me the rest. All of it, Wren Quintana. Every piece you've been chewing on alone since you walked through my door looking like a ghost wearing your mother's face."

The words landed like a dropped stone.

"My mother's face," Wren repeated.

Maren stood, unhurried, and began stacking bowls. "Eat," she said over her shoulder. "Then we talk."

* * *

Later, when Iven slept curled in the loft's straw like a fox in a den, and Ferro and the youth had gone out into the rain to walk the perimeter and see whether the followers had come closer, Maren set a bottle of plum brandy on the table between herself and Wren and poured two measures without asking.

Wren turned theirs slowly on the wood, watching the lamplight swim in it.

"You knew my mother."

"I knew her the way anyone in this trade knew her." Maren drank. "She came through Coldfern four times that I remember. Five, maybe. Always alone, always in autumn, always heading into the deep fells where no honest person goes and no dishonest one admits to." A dry almost-smile. "She paid her tolls in stories. Best ones I ever heard. Songs, too—songs in that tongue your boy speaks. My grandmother would have traded a year's harvest to hear her."

"She told me she was a herb-woman." Wren's voice sounded strange to them, distant, as though someone else were speaking through their mouth. "From the border villages. That's what she told me. That my father was a carter who died on the roads."

"And was he?"

"I don't know. I was six when she died. Everything before that is—" Wren searched. "Warmth. Smoke. Her hands. A song I can't finish."

Maren refilled her cup. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—lower, more careful, the voice she used for prices and confessions.

"Your mother wasn't a herb-woman, Wren. Or she wasn't only that. She was an envoy. Highmoor's envoy—the hidden court's, the court under the fells. Last one, near as I could ever learn. After the treaty broke and the passes closed, someone had to carry word between what was left down there and whatever friends remained up here. Smugglers. Sympathizers. People like us who moved goods and grudges both ways and asked few questions." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table. "She carried letters. Oaths. Once—I remember this clear as frost—she carried a bird in a wicker cage, and I swear on my grandmother's grave that bird sang harmonies with itself."

Wren's hands had gone still around the brandy cup. "You're saying she worked for the fae court."

"I'm saying she worked for a dying country, and the fae court was that country. Call it what you like." Maren's eyes were steady. "And the last time she came through—fifteen years ago now, the autumn before she died—she wasn't carrying letters."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a beam settled. Wren realized they had stopped breathing and made themselves start again.

"What was she carrying?"

"A stone." Maren held up one hand, fingers spread, measuring something the size of a heart. "About so big. Grey, but not plain grey—it moved, if you looked at it wrong. Like oil on water. She kept it wrapped in wool against her skin, and she was frightened of it, Wren. I'd seen that woman stare down a customs patrol with a smile on her face, and she was frightened of this. She told me—" Maren paused, and for the first time since Wren had known her, the old smuggler looked away toward the fire. "She told me it was half of something that used to be whole. That it was an oath, made solid. That when the treaty shattered, the oath-stone broke with it, and the pieces were scattered so no one could ever speak the covenant again—or so no one could unmake what was left of it. She wouldn't say which."

"Half of something that used to be whole." The words went through Wren like cold water through cloth. They thought of Iven in the pantry loft, pressing that single word into the air again and again—*covenant, covenant*—like a man tapping on the wall of a cell. They thought of the boy's amulet, the worn thing on a leather cord that he touched in his sleep the way other children touched a mother's sleeve.

"She died carrying it," Wren said. It wasn't a question. They remembered—suddenly, horribly—they remembered being six, being told their mother had died of fever, being shown a grave with no body because the fever had been bad, they said, so very bad—

"She died on the fells' far side," Maren said quietly. "Not of fever. Of wounds. She made it as far as the shepherd's hut at Grey Sike, and there were men behind her, and she gave the stone to the shepherd's wife and told her to get it to Coldfern, to me, if ever a person came asking with—" Maren stopped.

"With what?" Wren's voice was barely there.

"With a mark. A birthmark, she said, over the heart, shaped like a split leaf. Grey-blooded, she called it. She said if such a one ever came, the stone was theirs by right, and I was to give it up and ask nothing." Maren finally looked back at Wren, and her gaze dropped, deliberate, to Wren's chest—as if she could see through wool and linen and skin to the small pale mark Wren had spent a lifetime keeping covered, the mark they had told no living soul about, the mark the palace physician had once frowned at and called a common blemish while Wren held their breath and agreed.

"How long have you known?" Wren whispered.

"That you were hers? Since the night you first knocked, nine years ago. You have her way of standing in a doorway like you're deciding whether the room deserves you." Maren's mouth curved, briefly, without much humor. "That you were marked? I guessed. Tonight, when you said the Choir was hunting the boy and you put yourself between him and the door without thinking. Grey-bloods always stand in doors, my grandmother said. It's where both worlds meet in them."

Wren looked down at their own hands—the taster's hands, steady, unremarkable, hands that had learned to be nothing—and felt the ground shift beneath nine careful years. An envoy's child. A courier of oaths. Born on the border, marked for the border, and all this time they had thought their survival was a matter of being forgettable, when all along they had been *hidden*—tucked into the crown's kitchens like a letter sewn into a coat lining, waiting for someone to come asking.

The rain intensified against the shutters. Upstairs, faintly, Iven murmured in his sleep.

"There's more," Maren said. "Isn't there always." She rose, crossed to the great hearth, and knelt. Wren watched her work loose a stone near the base of the chimney—a stone fitted so cunningly that Wren, who had slept in this room twice, had never once marked it as different from its brothers. Behind it lay a cavity lined with oilcloth, and from it Maren drew a bundle wrapped in wool gone stiff with age, tied with cord, small enough to hold in two hands.

She brought it to the table and set it down between them without ceremony, the way one sets down a debt long owed.

"Fifteen years I've kept this," she said. "Waiting on a split-leaf mark that I half believed was a story old women tell. Untie it, Wren. It's yours by right, and I'll be glad to be rid of the weight of it."

Wren's fingers were not steady. Nine years of steady hands, and now this. They picked at the knot, and the cord fell away, and the wool unfolded layer by layer like something exhaling after a long sleep.

The stone was the size of a heart, exactly as Maren had said. Grey, but not plain grey—its surface moved when Wren's eyes weren't fixed on it, a slow swirl like oil on water, like fog over the fells at dusk. It was cool to the touch and heavier than it should have been, and along its broken edge ran a seam of crystal that caught the lamplight and held it, glittering, as though the fracture itself remembered being whole.

And it was humming.

So faintly that Wren might have imagined it—a vibration more felt than heard, rising through their palms, through the bones of their wrists, into the cage of their ribs. A note. A single sustained note, patient as a mountain, as if the stone had been holding its breath for fifteen years and had only now, under Wren's hands, begun to let it out.

Above them, in the loft, Iven cried out.

Wren was moving before thought caught up—up the ladder, across the straw, to where the boy sat bolt upright, both hands pressed to his chest, to the amulet on its leather cord. His eyes were huge and black in the dark, fixed not on Wren but on the stone in their hands, and his lips were moving, shaping words in Old Highmoor, over and over, and this time Wren understood them without trying, understood them the way they understood their own heartbeat:

*It sings. It sings. The other half sings.*

From beneath his small fists, muffled but unmistakable, rising to meet the hum of the stone in Wren's grasp like a voice answering a voice across a valley—

the amulet was singing too.
