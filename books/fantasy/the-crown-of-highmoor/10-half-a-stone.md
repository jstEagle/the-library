## 10. Half a Stone

The wayhouse at Coldfern smelled of peat smoke and wet wool, and for the first time in nine years, Wren woke without listening for footsteps in the corridor.

Not that there were no sounds. There were always sounds at Coldfern—the groan of the old timber settling as the fire warmed it, the mutter of the river under the ice shelf outside, Dagny's snores through the wall like a saw working a knot of oak. But none of those sounds meant *someone is coming to kill you*. None of them required Wren to lie still and catalog them, breath by breath, the way they had learned to do in the servants' loft above the palace kitchens, where every creak had a name and three of those names wore boots.

Here, the creaks were only creaks.

Wren lay on the narrow pallet beneath the eaves and let that truth settle over them like a second blanket. Below, the common room was already stirring: the clatter of the kettle hook, the thump of Dagny's cane on the floorboards, the low murmur of smugglers' voices doing what smugglers' voices did before dawn—arguing about routes, about tolls, about whether the ice on the Fenmarch would hold a loaded sledge. Ordinary sounds. Human sounds. The sounds of people who had never once been asked to taste a king's breakfast and smile about it.

Iven was still asleep in the corner, curled into the shape children curl into when they have learned that smallness is safety. He had not slept well. He had slept badly every night since Coldfern, in fact—tossing, whimpering, once crying out in that liquid, ringing language that made the hairs on Wren's arms rise even now, even after two weeks of piecing it together word by word like a mosaic assembled from broken glass.

Wren knew more of Old Highmoor now than they had ever admitted knowing. Enough to understand the shape of his nightmares, if not their details. Enough to know that when he said *sennavar*, he meant *the singers*, and that his voice went thin and gray around the word.

The Hollow Choir. The hunters. Whatever waited behind them in the Thornfells, patient as winter.

Wren rose quietly, dressed, and went down to help with the morning bread, because hands that had nothing to do started thinking, and thinking was a luxury they could not yet afford.

* * *

Dagny had run this wayhouse for forty years, back when running a wayhouse on the Coldfern road had meant something simpler than hiding fugitives from the crown's own chancellor. She was broad as a door and moved like one too—slowly, and only when she chose to—and she had taken one look at Wren and Iven arriving mud-blind through the storm and asked exactly no questions, which was its own kind of eloquence.

"Your boy eats like a starling," she said now, setting a bowl of porridge hard enough to stand a spoon in. "Small bites, constant, always watching the door."

"He's not my boy."

"Mm." Dagny's eyes, pale as river ice, stayed on Iven where he sat by the hearth turning a wooden button over and over in his fingers. "And I'm not a smuggler, I just keep house for forty of them. Eat your own breakfast, taster."

Wren ate. Around them the common room filled and emptied with the morning's traffic: a wool factor who paid his toll in gossip, two brothers hauling charcoal down from the high passes, an old woman with a hand-cart of dried fish who stopped to warm her feet and stayed an hour because nobody ever hurried her anywhere. It struck Wren, watching her, that this was what a border place was—not a wall but a kitchen. Not a line drawn but a table set. Their mother might have stood in this very room. Might have sat in that very chair by the window, warming her feet after some crossing Wren would never learn the shape of.

They were still watching the old woman when Iven came to stand beside them, and pressed something into their hand.

It happened quickly, the way important things do—one moment Wren's palm was empty and cold, the next it held a weight that should not have existed in a child's pocket.

An amulet. A flat shard of stone the size of a thumbnail, dark as wet slate, veined with lines so fine they might have been drawn with a needle. It was threaded on a leather cord worn soft and shiny with handling, and it was warm—warm as skin, though Iven had carried it outside against his chest all night in a house whose fires burned low.

"What—" Wren began.

Iven said a word.

He said it softly, almost shyly, the way you offer someone a gift you are afraid they will refuse. And Wren knew the word. They had known it since the pantry loft, since the first night, since the child had stood dripping on the kitchen step saying it over and over like a prayer or a plea:

*Covenar.*

Covenant.

"I know," Wren whispered. "I know that one. That's the thing you keep asking about. The oath. The broken—" They stopped, because the stone had moved.

No. Not moved. *Sounded.*

It was faint at first—a hum, felt in the bones of the hand rather than heard, like a tuning fork pressed to a wrist. Then it deepened, gathered, and became unmistakably, impossibly, a note. A single sustained tone, silver-bright, rising out of the dark grain of the stone as if the stone had been holding its breath for three hundred years and had just remembered how to sing.

Every head in the common room turned.

The old woman with the fish cart crossed herself with a fish still in her hand. One of the charcoal brothers knocked over his stool getting up. Dagny's cane stopped mid-tap, and her face did something complicated—recognition and grief and a fierce, sudden hope all fighting for the same ground.

And Wren understood, in the space of a heartbeat, why.

Because the sound was not coming only from Iven's amulet. It was coming from *below*. From the floorboards, from the foundations, from somewhere deep in the earth beneath the wayhouse—an answering tone, lower and older, rising up through the stone of the world itself like water finding its level. Two notes reaching for each other across whatever distance separated them, and where they met, in the air between Wren's hand and Iven's chest, the sound became harmony. Became music. Became something that made the back of Wren's throat ache with a homesickness for a place they had never been.

"The other half," Wren breathed. "You have the other half."

Iven nodded. His eyes were enormous, and wet, and fixed on Wren's face with an expression Wren had last seen on the faces of starving men looking at bread.

"Covenar," he said again, and then, carefully, in the trade-speech he had been learning word by painful word: "Two. Made... one. You say. You *say*, grey-child. Only you."

*Only you.* The words landed in Wren's chest like stones dropped down a well. They thought of their mother's lullabies sung in a language she'd called *kitchen-talk*, of the immunity built dish by dish in their blood, of the maps of smuggler roads they'd memorized without ever asking themselves why a food-taster needed escape routes through the Thornfells. All of it suddenly legible. All of it suddenly a path leading here, to a singing stone in their palm and a child asking them for the impossible.

"Dagny," Wren said, and their voice came out steadier than they felt. "What's under this house?"

* * *

Under the wayhouse was a cellar, and under the cellar was a door, and under the door was a stair cut into living rock, going down further than any cellar had a right to go.

Dagny led them with a lamp, her cane abandoned, her free hand trailing along the wall as if she had walked this stair many times in the dark and trusted it more than her eyes. Behind her came Iven, clutching his amulet, and behind him Wren, and behind Wren, uninvited but immovable, came Brannic—one of Dagny's people, a big quiet man with a crossbow and the particular calm of someone who had decided that if trouble was coming, he preferred to meet it standing up.

The stair opened into a chamber that took Wren's breath away.

It was natural rock, mostly—a cave hollowed by water in some ancient century—but it had been *finished*. Someone had smoothed the floor. Someone had set slender pillars of pale stone where the roof needed help, carved with spirals that caught the lamplight and seemed to move. And at the chamber's center, sunk into the floor like a keel into a hull, was a basin of black stone wide enough to lie down in, ringed with letters in Old Highmoor that glowed faintly, fitfully, like embers deciding whether to die.

"It was a shrine," Dagny said. Her voice had gone rough. "My grandmother called it the Listening Place. Said the old folk used to come down here before the Breaking, when the road ran different and the courts still spoke to each other. After the Treaty of Ash..." She shrugged, a gesture that tried to be casual and failed. "We kept it. Smugglers keep things. It's most of what we're good for."

The humming was stronger here. Strong enough now that Wren could feel it in their teeth, in the plates of their skull, and the answering note from below—from *beneath*, from miles down where the mountain's roots drank darkness—pulsed slow and patient as a sleeping heart.

Iven walked to the basin as if pulled on a string. He knelt beside it. He held his amulet out over the black stone, and the glow within the ringed letters brightened, and the two notes climbed toward each other, closer, closer—

"No," Wren said.

The word came out of them before thought, sharp as a slap. Iven flinched. Even Dagny turned.

"Wren?" Brannic said.

Wren stood at the foot of the stair with their heart hammering and their mind moving faster than it had ever moved, laying pieces side by side the way they laid dishes side by side on a tasting board, reading the pattern, reading the *poison* in the beauty of it.

Think. Think the way you think at the king's table, when everything looks like honey and roast quail and one of it is death.

A shrine that sang. A stone that answered. A signal that had just sounded through solid rock loud enough to make smugglers drop their stools—smugglers who dealt in secrets, who knew what such a sound meant, who would talk, because everyone talks eventually, and Chancellor Vess had informants in every market town between here and the capital, Wren would stake their immunity on it.

"They can't hear it," Wren said. "The Choir. If the stone sings, they'll hear it. That's how they found the hidden court, isn't it? That's how they've been tracking you." They looked at Iven, and gentled their voice as much as fear allowed. "Iven. Has the amulet ever sung before?"

Iven's face did something terrible. The look of a child realizing the pretty thing in his hand has teeth.

"...When near mother-stone," he whispered. "Near... half. Near *you*." His eyes dropped to Wren's hand—to the place where, for one impossible moment in the storm two weeks ago, Iven's bare fingers had touched theirs and the world had rung like a struck bell. "Grey-child sing too. Small. Small song. But Choir hear small songs. Choir hear *everything*."

The silence in the cave was total except for the fading hum of the stone, sinking back toward silence now that Wren had stepped back from the basin, now that the halves hung apart.

"So we don't bring them together," Brannic said slowly. "Simple as that. Boy keeps his charm, we bury the basin, we—"

"You don't understand." Wren heard their own voice crack and didn't care. "It's not just the stone. It's me. I'm the other half—or I'm part of it, or my blood is, I don't—I touched him in the storm and it rang, Brannic, it rang through the whole pass. Which means it's already happened. Which means they may already know. Which means every hour we sit here being grateful for warm bread and locked doors, we're sitting in a bell tower waiting for someone to pull the rope."

Dagny had gone very still by the pillar, one hand flat against the carved spiral as if taking counsel from it. When she spoke, her voice was quieter than Wren had ever heard it.

"My grandmother told me a story," she said. "About the night the court fell. She was a girl; her mother worked the Listening Place. She said the last envoy out of Highmoor came through here carrying a stone wrapped in oilcloth, bleeding from a wound no bandage would hold, and she asked my grandmother's mother to keep the shrine ready. Said, *Keep it ready. Someone will come who can finish the song.*" Dagny looked at Wren, and her ice-pale eyes were wet. "Forty years I've run this house. Sixty years my family's kept that promise. I'd begun to think it was a story we told ourselves to make the keeping mean something."

"Dagny—"

"Hush. I'm not done." She straightened, and the grief in her face hardened into something with edges. "If the stone sings and the Choir comes, then the Choir comes to *my* door, and my door has been expecting them for sixty years. We have ways out of this valley that aren't on any crown map—we dug half of them ourselves. But you need time, taster. Time to learn what the boy knows. Time to learn what *you* are. So here's what happens now." She counted it off on thick fingers. "One: the boy's charm goes in a lead-lined strongbox my grandfather used for tax-evasion, which muffles it near enough to nothing. Two: nobody touches the basin until we're ready to leave this valley for good. Three—" and here she pointed the finger at Wren like a blade, "—you stop apologizing for existing and start learning the language you've been pretending not to know since you walked in. Because when the time comes, there won't be anyone else. There never was going to be anyone else. That's what *only you* means."

The words hung in the cold air of the shrine, among the glowing letters and the sleeping basin and the long patience of the stone.

Wren looked at Iven. Iven looked back, and slowly, deliberately, slipped the cord of the amulet over his own head and held it out—not to put away, Wren realized, but to show them the underside, the back of the shard, where fine scratches marked a script too small to read by lamplight.

Words. Instructions, maybe. Or a name.

Or half a name, waiting for its other half.

"Take it to the box," Wren said hoarsely. "Quickly. Please."

Brannic bore the amulet away up the stair at a pace just short of running, and as it receded, the last thread of song faded from the chamber, and the letters in the basin dimmed to embers, and the mountain's deep answering pulse slowed, slowed, and stilled—as if something far below had sighed, and settled, and begun again to wait.

* * *

They spent the evening as if it were any evening, because the appearance of ordinariness was armor too. Dagny cooked. The smugglers argued about the Fenmarch ice. The old woman with the fish cart finally left, an hour past sensible, pausing at the door to press a dried herring into Iven's hands with the gravity of a queen bestowing a duchy.

But Wren noticed what Wren always noticed, because noticing was the whole of their trade.

They noticed that Brannic had taken the strongbox not to the cellar but up, to the hayloft, on Dagny's orders—*high places carry sound worse than deep ones*, she'd muttered, though Wren suspected the truth was that she wanted the song as far from the Listening Place as it could get. They noticed that two of the younger smugglers had ridden out at dusk without explanation, and that Dagny had watched them go with an expression she wiped clean the moment she saw Wren looking. Scouting, probably. Checking the passes. Probably.

They noticed that Iven, sitting close beside them at the long table, had gone rigid as a snared rabbit, staring at the window.

Frost ferned the glass. Beyond it the yard lay blue with moonlight, and the Thornfells stood up black against the stars, and the wind came down off them carrying nothing—no sound, no scent, no warning.

And yet Iven's lips had gone white, and his hand had found Wren's sleeve, and he said, in a whisper barely louder than breath, in the language of a buried court, the word he had said on the first night, the word that had started everything:

"*Sennavar.*"

Wren followed his gaze to the ridgeline above the eastern pass.

For a moment there was nothing. Snow, stone, starlight.

Then—far off, so faint it might have been the wind worrying the ice, might have been imagination, might have been anything at all—they heard it.

Singing.

Many voices, in unison, wordless, sweet, and wrong—the way honey is wrong when you know one dish on the board is poison.

The Hollow Choir had heard the bell.

And they were coming down the mountain, singing as they came.
