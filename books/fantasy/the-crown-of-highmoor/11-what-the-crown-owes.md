## 11. What the Crown Owes

The king had not eaten in three days.

Chancellor Vess knew this because she made it her business to know everything that passed between the king's mouth and the world, and grief had closed that border entirely. She stood at the window of her office above the Treasury Court and watched the rain come down on Ashfall City like a judgment, silver threads stitching the rooftops to the grey sky, and she permitted herself the small luxury of satisfaction. Fear was a useful servant. Grief was better. Grief did not ask questions.

Below, in the courtyard, soldiers drilled in the wet despite the weather, their pikes catching what little light remained. She had doubled the guard rotations two nights ago—since the poisoning, since the taster's dramatic collapse into the king's wine with his throat already closing—and the sound of them came up through the stones like a second heartbeat. Good. A frightened city should hear its own strength.

On her desk lay the ledger.

It was an unremarkable thing to look at: calf-bound, corner-worn, its spine cracked from three centuries of being opened by clerks who had no idea what they were holding. The Ledger of Accounts Pertaining to the Northern Reaches, 214 entries, most of them grain tariffs and garrison wages. But Vess had spent forty years learning to read what kingdoms wrote when they were not trying to say anything, and the ledger had been screaming at her for six days now.

Entry ninety-one ended mid-line. The ink simply stopped, the quill-lift still visible after all this time, as though the clerk had been called away mid-word—or as though the page following had never existed at all. Except the binding said otherwise. The stitching along the spine showed the ghost-grooves of a removed signature: one leaf, cut close and clean, taken by someone who understood that archives forget documents but never forget wounds.

She had pulled every record of the Treaty of Ash herself. Burned, officially. Burned by royal decree in the year the last moon of Highmoor waned, along with the correspondence, the maps, the census of the hidden court. Three hundred years of careful forgetting, ordered by a king who had understood something his descendants had let themselves forget: that treaties are only dangerous when someone remains alive who remembers both sides.

And now, apparently, someone did.

Vess turned to the window again. Somewhere beyond the Thornfells, past the smuggler roads she pretended not to know about because knowing would require doing something about them, the fae realm sat in its long exile. And somewhere inside her own palace, a food-taster—a *grey child*, if the Choir's reports were to be believed—had tasted poison meant for the king, named its origin, and then vanished into the night with a feral boy who spoke a dead language.

She had liked Wren Quintana, once, in the way one likes a well-made tool. Quiet. Punctual. Immune to half the poisons in the civilized world through nine years of patient self-murder in spoonfuls. Vess had even recommended the stipend increase herself. It was almost elegant, how thoroughly the little taster had used invisibility as armor. Almost elegant enough to forgive.

But elegance was not loyalty, and the crown could not afford ambiguity. Not now. Not with the king drowning and the realm needing a firm hand on the tiller.

A knock. "Enter."

Her underclerk Pellam came in shaking rain from his shoulders, a folio clutched to his chest like a shield. He was a nervous man, Pellam, but honest in the way of men too timid to be otherwise, and he had served her for eleven years without once asking why she wanted the things she wanted.

"The infirmary records, Chancellor. And the kitchen roster you asked for."

"Set them down." She did not look at him yet; she looked at the folio as though weighing it. "Wren Quintana's quarters were cleared?"

"By your order, yes. Personal effects inventoried." Pellam hesitated. "There wasn't much. Clothing. A tin of willowbark. Some... drawings, my lady. Maps. Of the northern passes."

"Maps," Vess repeated softly.

"Very good ones, I'm told. The surveyors' guild couldn't have done better. Roads through the Thornfells that aren't on any crown chart. Smuggler roads."

Of course they were. Nine years of tasting the king's food, and every spare hour bent over ink and parchment charting the ways out of the kingdom. She had wondered, more than once, why a person so careful about everything else would keep such incriminating things in a palace drawer. Now she understood: Wren had never expected to flee. Wren had expected to *need* the way out someday and had prepared for that day with the same patience applied to poisons. It was, Vess admitted privately, the most respectable thing about the traitor.

"And the boy?"

"No sign, Chancellor. The kitchen staff say he appeared during the storm, stayed a night in the pantry loft, gone by morning. Along with Quintana." Pellam swallowed. "There's talk below stairs. They're saying Quintana poisoned the wine themselves and staged the rescue to cover the flight."

"Yes," Vess said. "I know. I started it."

Pellam went very still, the way small animals do.

"Not publicly, obviously," she continued, turning back to the desk, unhurried, sorting the infirmary records with two fingers. "Rumors are like water, Pellam. You don't build them. You dig the channel and let gravity do the work. A word here—the taster survived a dose that killed a courtier. A word there—grey eyes, border-born mother, no family anyone can name. Servants do the rest. They always do." She glanced up. "You look distressed."

"I—" Pellam set his jaw. "Wren Quintana saved the king's life. The physicians said so. Another minute and—"

"The physicians said the poison was fae-worked, which means the poison came from the north, which means either the fae sent assassins into my king's hall"—she let the sentence hang, then finished it gently, like laying a knife on velvet—"or someone in this palace carried it to them. Both stories end at the same place, Pellam. The question is only which one frightens the king into acting, and which one frightens him into paralysis. I have chosen for him."

"You've decided it's war."

"I've decided it's *certainty*," Vess said. "War is merely the shape certainty takes when a kingdom has been sleeping for three hundred years." She rose, crossing to the tall mirror beside the bookcase, adjusting the silver chain of her office with fingers that never trembled. In the glass, behind her reflection, Pellam stood wringing his hat. "Grief is a door, and I intend to walk through it before it closes. Kings in mourning want one of two things: someone to blame, or someone to make the blame unnecessary. I will give him the first today and the second within the season."

"And Quintana? If they're found?"

"Then they'll stand trial for high treason, and the trial will be short, and it will be public, and it will teach every grey-blooded child in this realm exactly what the crown owes them." She smiled at her reflection. It was not a warm smile, but it was a controlled one, which in Ashfall counted for more. "Nothing, Pellam. That is what the crown owes them. Nothing. It always has. I am merely making policy of precedent."

* * *

The king received her in the blue drawing room, where the light through the windows had been stained melancholy by the leaded glass, so that everything in the room appeared drowned. King Aldric sat beneath the portrait of his father looking less like a monarch than like a man who had been left out in the rain too long and had not bothered to come inside. He was fifty-one years old and had ruled for thirty of those years without once having to decide anything that Vess had not first decided for him, and she had always considered this the great achievement of her career: a realm so stable that its king could remain, in essence, a ceremonial vessel. Until now. Until a cup of wine and a boy from the north had cracked the vessel.

"Chancellor." His voice was hoarse. He had wept again that morning; she could see it in the rims of his eyes, the rawness. Not for the dead courtier—Lord Halloway had meant nothing to him—but for Lord Halloway's daughter, who had screamed, and whose scream had lodged somewhere in the king's chest and refused to leave. "Sit. No—stand. I can't sit today. I keep sitting down and finding I can't get up again."

"Then we shall both stand, Your Majesty."

"They tell me it was fae poison." He turned the ring on his thumb, an old habit, worrying it around and around. "My grandfather's stories said the Thornfells were empty. Said the old courts died out or fled beyond the world. Was my grandfather wrong? Is there something in the north that hates us enough to do this?"

"There is something in the north that has *waited*, Majesty." Vess kept her voice low, level, pitched to carry only to him. Around them the room held its breath—two guards by the door, a maid pretending to tend the fire, the painted ancestors watching from their gilt frames. "Something that has spent three centuries nursing an old grievance and growing patient in the dark. What happened at your table was not an assassination. It was a message. And messages are only sent by parties who expect a reply."

"A reply." He laughed, briefly, horribly. "What reply does a man send to a shadow? My father fought bandits and quarrelsome dukes. No one ever taught me how to make war on fog."

"One begins," Vess said, "by naming the fog."

She drew the folded paper from her sleeve—not a decree, not yet, nothing so crude, but a brief in her own fair hand, three pages, each sentence load-bearing. She had rewritten it eleven times in the night, shaving away every word that might later be questioned, leaving only the load-bearing stone.

"The archive irregularities I reported last week have deepened. The missing page concerned the Treaty of Ash—the covenant your ancestor broke and burned. Someone has been removing evidence of the old realm from our records. Someone with access. And the same week, a poison of unmistakable northern craft enters your wine, and the one soul in this palace who identified it—a border-born taster with no recorded family and, we now discover, maps of forbidden roads hidden in their rooms—flees the city the same night, taking with them a child who speaks the language of Highmoor."

The king had stopped turning his ring. "Wren?" He said the name like a man testing whether a floorboard would hold. "Little Quintana? They've tasted for me since I was crowned. Nine years. They found the hemlock in the coronation feast when they were barely grown—they *saved* me before I ever trusted them, I—" He faltered. Vess watched, with the patience of a fisherman, the exact moment the current took him. "Why would they run, if not guilty?"

"Why indeed, Majesty." She let him answer himself. This was the whole art: never push. Open the gate and let grief do the driving. "I do not claim to know their heart. Perhaps they were coerced. Perhaps they were always the enemy's creature, placed in your household at birth, waiting. What I know is what was done: the poison is real, the flight is real, the missing treaty is real, and the child is real. Four facts, Majesty. Facts do not grieve, and so they do not lie."

He looked at the brief in her hands without taking it. "If I sign what you've brought me, what happens?"

"Nothing happens, at first. A proclamation declaring the northern realm hostile—formally, legally, ending the pretense that the Thornfells are empty. Warrants for the fugitives. Increased watch on the passes." She paused, precisely. "And the recall of the border garrisons to full strength, which we should have done years ago regardless. If I am wrong, Majesty—if some other hand holds the poison—you will have spent coin and ink. If I am right, you will have saved your kingdom while it slept."

"And if the north truly means us harm?"

"Then you will have answered a message with a message." She finally extended the pages. "They struck at your table, Majesty. Let them learn what your table answers."

For a long moment the king did not move. Rain traced the leaded glass. In the portrait above him, his father gazed out with painted, sightless confidence, the confidence of a man who had burned a treaty and believed fire was the end of things rather than the beginning of waiting.

"I liked them," Aldric said quietly. "The taster. They had kind eyes. Tired, but kind." He took the pages. "That's a foolish thing for a king to say, isn't it."

"It is a human thing, Majesty. Which is why kings must be careful with such things." She watched him read. Watched his eyes slow at the third page, where she had written the words *high treason* and *the realm's protection requires* in the same breath, so that refusing one seemed to refuse the other. "The ink is dry on the northern line. Only the seal remains."

He reached for it. Stopped. "If they're innocent, Chancellor—Quintana—if they ran because they were afraid—"

"Then they will have every chance to prove it at trial," Vess said, and this was even true, in the sense that a trap is a chance. "The law does not chase the innocent forever, Majesty. It waits for them to come home."

Which was also true, in its way. The law waited. So did she.

The king pressed his seal into the wax. The scent of hot resin filled the drowned-blue room, sweet and final, and outside the rain went on falling on a city that did not yet know what had been signed in its name.

* * *

By evening the proclamation was nailed to the doors of every guildhall in Ashfall, and Vess stood at her window listening to the city digest it. Bells. Shouting. Somewhere a mob had already formed around the northern gate, hurling stones at a carter whose accent marked him as border-born. She noted it without feeling. The channel dug; the water running.

Pellam appeared at her shoulder with a lantern. "The Choir sends word, Chancellor. Their scouts reached the Coldfern road. The wayhouse was attacked." A pause. "The fugitives escaped. Again. But they lost people, and the smugglers are angry, and angry smugglers sell information."

"Then buy it," Vess said. "Whatever it costs."

"And the stone halves, my lady? The scouts report the amulet and the—"

"I know what they report." She turned from the window. Beyond the city, beyond the rain, the Thornfells stood black against the last bruised light of the sky, and somewhere inside them a broken mountain held a sunken court and a covenant waiting to be spoken. Vess had read the missing ledger page's description before she destroyed it herself, years ago, in another purge no one had ever discovered. She knew what the halves made whole. She knew what the oath-keeper would hold.

Which was precisely why there would be no oath-keeper.

"Two orders, Pellam. First: the Choir moves on the mountains in force at the dark of the moon. Everything they have. I want the fugitives taken alive—the boy especially. Alive and *unspoiled*. Second—" she lifted the lantern, and its flame steadied in her unwavering hand, "—find me the cave shrines. The old ones, in the high passes, the ones the border folk still whisper at. If Quintana is going to learn what they are, I want to know it the same hour they do."

Pellam hesitated at the door. "May I ask why the shrines, Chancellor?"

"Because language, Pellam, is a door," Vess said, "and I intend to be standing on the far side of it when they open theirs."

The lantern light caught the ledger on her desk, entry ninety-one, the wound in the binding where a page had been torn away. Three centuries ago a king had answered a covenant with fire. Tonight his heir had answered it with a proclamation, and both, Vess reflected, mistook burning for ending.

She snuffed the lantern herself and stood alone in the dark, listening to the bells, counting the moons left until the thirteenth.

Three.

She had time. She intended to spend every hour of it.
