## 12. The Hollow Choir Sings

The wayhouse at Coldfern smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath it all, of the river-stone sweetness that Iven had learned to read as *safety*. He had not known, in the first moon of his flight, that safety had a smell. In the hidden court everything had smelled of candlewax and cold water and, at the last, of ash. Here there was bread rising in a covered pan, and a dog with one folded ear who had decided Iven belonged to her, and Wren across the table mending a strap with their teeth set in concentration like a person doing arithmetic.

Iven watched them the way he watched everything now—hungrily, suspiciously, as if any beloved thing might be the next thing taken.

The smugglers were loud in the good way. Brannic, the wide-shouldered woman who owned the wayhouse, was arguing cheerfully with a drover about whether goats or mules made better mountain company, and her voice filled the low room the way his mother's voice used to fill the long hall at Highmoor—not because it was loud, but because everyone else arranged themselves around it without noticing they did.

He was learning their words faster than he let on. That was a secret he kept even from Wren: that the border-tongue came into him like water into sand, that by now he understood most of what was said over cards and kettles, and said little, because saying little kept him small, and small things were overlooked, and overlooked things survived. Nine years old and already he knew the shape of survival. It was not a lesson any child should have learned so well. He knew that too, in the wordless way he knew the thirteenth moon was thinning overhead like a coin rubbed between fingers.

*Three nights,* the stone sang against his chest. *Three nights and the door closes for another age.*

The amulet lay warm under his shirt, and lately it had begun to sing more often, a thin silver thread of sound only he could hear—or so he believed until the night Wren's half of it, wrapped in wool in Wren's pack by the fire, began to hum in answer, and Wren went still as a hare and looked at the pack and then at Iven, and neither of them said anything, because saying it would make it true, and true things could be hunted.

That was the night the Choir found them.

* * *

It began with the dog.

She lifted her head from the hearthstone first, ears up, and made a sound Iven had never heard a dog make—a low, continuous note, almost musical, almost afraid. Then the room's sounds changed underneath themselves. The fire crackled on. The dice clicked. But beneath it all, like a second river running under the first, came a humming.

Iven knew that humming. He had heard it through the walls of the hidden court on the last night, when the candles guttered blue and his nurse pressed him into the hollow beneath the root-cellar stairs and told him, in Old Highmoor, the words every Highmoor child was taught before any other: *when the singing comes, do not sing back.*

His blood turned to winter.

"Wren." His voice came out small. He hated how small. "Wren. They're here."

Wren's head came up. Whatever they saw in his face made them move without question—no *what*, no *are you sure*—just the strap dropped, the hand out, the pack caught up. Around the room the smugglers were pausing too, one by one, the way animals go quiet before a rockslide. Brannic set down her mug. Her eyes went to the shuttered window, and Iven saw her nostrils flare, tasting the air the way he did, and he understood suddenly that she had heard stories, old ones, the kind smugglers trade at borders where the worlds rub thin.

"How many?" Wren asked him quietly, in the pidgin of gesture and borrowed words they had built together. *How many?*

Iven closed his eyes and listened with the listening his nurse had drilled into him until it lived in his bones. The humming was layered, many voices braided into one, each voice wrong in a way he couldn't name—too smooth, like stones worn featureless by water. He counted the strands the way you count threads in a braid.

"Seven," he whispered. "Maybe eight."

Brannic swore softly and beautifully. Then the wayhouse became something Iven had never seen a building do: it became a machine. Shutters barred. The dog sent down the cellar hatch with a boy of maybe twelve—the drover's son—and the trapdoor dragged over him with a rug thrown atop. Knives appeared from places knives should not be. A crossbow came off pegs above the mantel already strung, as if it had waited years for this exact hour.

"You know these things," Iven said to Wren, staring. "You knew this would happen someday."

"Everyone who lives by the border knows," Wren said, and their face was the careful blank they wore for the crown, but their hands shook as they buckled the pack. "Get behind the bar. Stay down. If they take the room, you run for the cellar—I'll be right behind you."

They would not be right behind him. Iven was nine years old, not stupid. He could see it in the way Wren positioned themselves between the door and him, in the way Brannic pressed her knife into Wren's hand and kept the crossbow herself. The big ones stood in front of the small ones. That was the oldest law there was.

The humming stopped.

The silence after it was worse—so complete Iven could hear his own heart, and the fire, and far off the river, and then footsteps on the frozen mud of the yard. Unhurried. Seven sets, nearly in unison, like one creature walking with seven feet.

Then a voice, sweet and hollow as a reed cut open: *"Keepers of the road. We have no quarrel with you. Give us the child and the thief who shelters him, and go back to your fires."*

Brannic answered through the shutters, her voice flat as slate. "Road's free. House is mine. Neither's yours."

A pause. Then, gently, horribly: *"As you choose. We will add your names to our song."*

What happened next, Iven's memory kept in pieces, the way a shattered cup keeps its pieces—all of them real, none of them able to be put back into the shape of the whole.

The door did not open. It simply ceased to be an obstacle, folding inward like paper though no hand touched it, and through the doorway came figures in grey robes gone dark with travel-mud, faces pale and ordinary, so ordinary that for one heartbeat Iven thought: *travelers, pilgrims, lost folk.* Then the nearest one opened its mouth and sang, and the note that came out was the note from beneath the walls of Highmoor, the note that had preceded the falling of the towers, and the fire bent sideways as if bowing.

Brannic fired the crossbow. The bolt took the singer high in the chest, and the singer did not fall. It looked down at the bolt with mild interest, the way one looks at a moth on one's sleeve, and pulled it out, and the wound closed like water closing over a stone.

But the others moved, and the room became a storm of ordinary, mortal violence—knives, a swung poker, a table overturned, the drover roaring like an ox—and Iven learned in those minutes that the Hollow Choir could not easily be killed but could be *slowed*, could be grappled and tripped and burned, that grey robes could soak blood as well as any cloth. He learned that smugglers die loudly and without apology. He learned that Wren fought like someone who had spent nine years making sure no one ever looked at them, which is to say: precisely, quietly, and only as much as needed, a knife doing its work in the space between one heartbeat and the next.

"Cellar!" Brannic bellowed, wrestling a singer against the wall with her forearm across its throat. "Boy! The rug! *Go!*"

Wren grabbed Iven's wrist and ran.

Behind them the wayhouse roared. Someone threw the lamp; flame went up the wall with a sound like a gasp. And in the middle of it Iven looked back—one look, the look he would carry the rest of his life—and saw the singer Brannic held pull its hood back with its free hand, deliberately, offering its face to the burning light.

It was a woman. Middle-aged, tired-eyed, a scar through one eyebrow. Ordinary. Border-born. He could see it in the color of her, in the faint double-shadow the fae-light of the burning lamp cast around her—two shadows, one human and one not, the mark of mixed blood.

The mark he himself carried. The mark Wren carried.

Her eyes met his across the burning room, and they were not cruel. That was the worst thing. They were *sorry*, and weary, and resigned as an old horse's eyes, and she mouthed something at him—words without sound, or with sound swallowed by the fire—and though he could not hear it, he knew the shape of the words in Old Highmoor, because his nurse had mouthed them once through a closing door:

*I'm sorry. Run.*

Then Wren hauled him down the cellar steps and the world became darkness and the smell of earth and the dog trembling against his legs, and above them, muffled by floorboards and flame, the singing rose into something vast and many-voiced and grief-shaped, and the screaming started, and then stopped, one voice at a time, like bells being silenced.

* * *

They came out of the cellar through the spring-house tunnel, because of course Brannic's wayhouse had a spring-house tunnel; smugglers built like spiders build, everything twice, everything hidden. The cold hit Iven like a slap after the furnace heat of the burning house. Snow was coming down fine and dry, hissing where it met the smoke. Behind them Coldfern burned orange against the black fells, and the singing had followed them outside—it was in the trees now, in the rocks, everywhere and nowhere, a lullaby sung by something that had forgotten what lullabies were for.

Wren ran with one arm around him and one hand on the knife, and Iven felt, through the contact of their sides, that Wren was crying without any change in their breathing at all, tears just sliding down a face held perfectly still, and something in Iven's chest cracked along a line he didn't know he had.

They ran until the fire was a smudge and the singing faded to a thread, and then Wren pulled them both into the lee of a split boulder and finally, finally stopped, and the stillness broke. Wren put their forehead against the freezing stone and breathed like a bellows, and their shoulders shook, and Iven stood beside them in the snow and did not know what to do, because he was nine, and the people who had fed him and joked with him and given him a chair by their fire were dead, and the only other person left in the world was breaking apart in front of him.

So he did the only thing he knew. He took Wren's hand—the scarred, steady, poison-hardened hand—and held it in both of his, the way his mother had held his on the last night, and he sang. Softly. Three notes of a cradle-song from the hidden court, the one about the river that runs under every river. His voice cracked on the second note. He sang anyway.

Wren went very still. Then they turned, and knelt in the snow to be at his height, and in the light of the distant fire Iven saw their face properly—ash-streaked, tear-tracked, older than it had been an hour ago.

"They were like me," Wren said. Not a question. They had seen too. Of course they had seen; Wren missed nothing; it was how they had stayed alive. "In there. Under the hoods. Grey-bloods."

Iven nodded. His throat hurt.

"Why?" The word came out of Wren scraped raw. "Why would anyone *choose*—" They stopped. Started again, quieter, worse: "They serve the ones who killed your court. They hunt children. And they're *us*."

And Iven, who had seen the woman's sorry eyes, who had heard the grief under the hollow singing if you listened with fae ears, who had been told all his life that the world beyond the Thornfells was simple—crown bad, court good, border folk kind—shook his head slowly.

"Not chosen like you think," he said, in his careful borrowed words, and then, because some things could not be said badly, he reached for Old Highmoor, the language that was his alone now, almost alone, and spoke slowly enough that Wren's mother-lullaby blood might follow: *"No one chooses the choir with open eyes. First they take your song. Then they give you theirs, and tell you it was always yours. Then you cannot remember the difference."*

Wren stared at him. Understanding moved through their face like ice cracking on a pond—understanding, and horror, and beneath the horror something Iven recognized because he felt it too: the terrible thought, the thought that lives in the marrow.

*That could be me. That could be us. That is only luck and choice standing between.*

"The Choir aren't monsters," Wren said at last, barely audible. "They're what's left. They're what happens when—" Their hand went unconsciously to their own chest, to the place where a grey-blood's two shadows meet. "Nine years I made myself invisible so no one would ever ask what I was. Was I hiding from the crown, or hiding from *that*?"

Iven had no answer. He was nine. He put his cold hand in Wren's cold hand, and the amulet under his shirt pulsed once, warm, insistent, and he realized with a lurch that the singing in the distance had changed key.

It was no longer searching.

It was converging—on *them*, drawn through the snow and the dark by the one thing the Choir wanted more than anything in the broken world: the two halves of the oath-stone coming together, singing to each other across his skin like a struck bell.

"Wren," he whispered. "They can hear it. They've always been able to hear it. That's how they found the wayhouse—that's why they came *now*."

Wren looked down at the pack, at the wool-wrapped half-stone that hummed against Iven's amulet like two hands finding each other, and Iven watched the truth land: hope itself was the beacon. The thing that could save the realms was the thing that would bring the Choir down on them before dawn.

The distant singing swelled, sweet and hollow and sorrowful, seven voices braided into one, coming down out of the fells.

Wren stood, and wiped their face with the heel of their hand, and when they spoke their voice was the steadiest Iven had ever heard it.

"Then we stop running toward the road," they said, "and start running toward the mountain. There's a shrine in the high pass—my mother's people left it. If anywhere in the Thornfells can hide a song, it's there."

Above them, through the parting clouds, the thirteenth moon showed its thinning face—two nights left, maybe three—and the snow fell on the burned wayhouse behind them and on the dark road ahead, on the living and the dead alike, indifferent as starlight.

Iven tightened his grip on Wren's hand, and they ran.
