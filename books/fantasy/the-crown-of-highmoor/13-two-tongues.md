## 13. Two Tongues

The blood had dried stiff on Wren's sleeve by the time they found the cave.

It was not a hiding place so much as a wound in the mountain—a split in the grey flank of the Thornfells where the rock had cracked and healed wrong, leaving a gap barely wide enough for a body to squeeze through. Wren went first, because Iven's shoulder was wrenched from the fall at Coldfern and because Wren had spent nine years learning which spaces could hold them: the pantry loft, the shadow behind the wine racks, the seam between one heartbeat and the next. This space would hold them too. It had to.

Inside, the dark opened wider than it should have.

Wren felt it before they understood it—the way the cold changed, how the wind that chased them up the switchback trail simply stopped at the threshold as though it had been politely declined entry. The air within smelled of stone and water and something older underneath, something green that had no business growing this high above the tree line.

"Iven." Their voice came out rougher than they intended. "There's a light."

There wasn't. Not yet. But when Wren blinked, the dark seemed to remember itself, and along the walls faint veins of silver woke like frost blooming across a window—thin threads of luminescence tracing patterns that were too regular to be accidental, too worn to be new. They spiraled inward, deeper into the mountain, toward a chamber whose shape Wren could feel with their skin rather than see.

Iven pressed close against their back, small and shaking. He had stopped trying to speak hours ago, somewhere past the second stream crossing, when his voice gave out from screaming Iven *run* in a language only two people alive still carried. Now he touched Wren's hand—just touched it, the way he did when words failed—and said one syllable, soft as an apology.

"*Shaelen.*"

Shrine. Wren knew that word now. It had taken three weeks of pantry-loft lessons, of Iven pointing at bread and doors and stars while Wren's half-remembered lullabies surfaced like bodies rising in a lake, but *shaelen* they knew. Holy place. Listening place.

They went deeper.

* * *

The chamber opened like the inside of a held breath.

Wren had seen the great hall of the palace—pillars of imported marble, a ceiling painted with the king's victories—and thought it beautiful once, in the first year, before beauty became just another thing they tasted on behalf of someone else. This was nothing like that. This was older than ambition. The walls curved naturally, uncut, but threaded through with that same silver light until the whole space glowed like moonrise trapped under ice. Water fell somewhere in the dark, slow and rhythmic, filling the silence without breaking it.

At the center stood a basin of black stone, wide as a cartwheel, carved over every surface with letters.

Wren knew the letters. That was the thing that stopped their feet, that made their heart stumble and then race. They knew them the way you know a face in a crowd before you know why—visceral, immediate, before thought. The same looping script ran down the margins of their mother's one surviving letter, the one Wren kept folded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. The same shapes she'd traced on Wren's palm when they couldn't sleep, humming, saying words that meant nothing and everything: *go to sleep, little border-child, the mountain holds you, the river knows your name.*

"Nobody wrote this," Wren whispered, uselessly, in the common tongue. "This is—this is three hundred years old. Older. It shouldn't—"

Iven tugged their sleeve and pointed, urgent, at the basin's rim. There, in a recess shaped precisely to fit it, lay a socket of empty stone. And around the socket, worn nearly smooth by centuries of absence, more words—which Wren could not read, but which Iven clearly could, because he made a sound low in his throat like grief catching on a nail.

He tapped the hollow. Then he touched the amulet at his throat—the broken half of the oath-stone that had sung so sweetly and so fatally at Coldfern, that had brought the Hollow Choir down on smugglers who'd shown them nothing but kindness. Brannick was dead. Old Maret was dead. The wayhouse's chimney would be cold tonight, and it was Wren's fault, and the stone's, and the song's.

"It wants its other half," Wren said quietly. "Your mother's half. My mother's half." The realization settled over them with a strange, terrible gentleness. Two women. Two halves. One envoy from the hidden court who fled east and married a border trapper and died of fever in a village that never learned her real name; one queen of a buried realm who sent her youngest son running west with her heart's last piece in his fist. "They were carrying the same oath between them. All this time."

Iven nodded. His eyes were wet, but he didn't cry. He was past crying; Wren recognized that country, having emigrated there years ago.

And then Iven pointed—at the basin, at the socket, at the walls, at Wren—and said something long and pleading in Old Highmoor, words tumbling out too fast, and Wren caught perhaps one phrase in ten. But the tenth phrase landed like a stone dropped down a well:

"*...vel shaelen kess iri veth...*" — the shrine listens to blood — "*...grey-blood, grey-tongue, say it, say it, say it—*"

"No." Wren stepped back. The word came out flat and final and frightened. "No, I can't. I'm not—I don't *speak* it, Iven. I know lullabies. I know maybe sixty words and half of them are about sleeping. I'm a food-taster. I taste things and survive them and keep my mouth shut about everything else. That's the whole of me. That's all I've ever been allowed to be."

Even as they said it, they heard how it sounded. *Allowed.* As if invisibility were a garment someone else had tailored for them, rather than one they'd sewn themselves, stitch by careful stitch, dish by dish, year by year. Nine years of making themselves small enough that no chancellor would look twice, no king would ask questions, no assassin's ledger would bother to list them. Nine years of being nobody on purpose, because nobody was safe, and safety was the only inheritance a grey child got.

Except it hadn't been safe, had it. Not really. The Choir had found them anyway. Vess's men had hunted them anyway. The crown they'd served with their tongue and their caution had turned on them the moment they became useful to someone else's fear. Invisibility had been a debt they paid daily and the interest had come due regardless.

Iven was watching them with those too-old eyes. He didn't argue. He simply took Wren's hand—the wounded one, the one sliced open on the rocks below Coldfern—and turned it palm-up, and held it in both of his small cold ones, and began to sing.

It was their mother's song.

Not exactly. The melody was different, the words fuller, adult-voiced where hers had been softened for a child's ears—but beneath the differences it was unmistakably the same song, the one about the mountain holding and the river knowing, and hearing it from another mouth, in a place built to receive it, did something to Wren that no poison ever had. It went through their defenses like water through chalk.

Their lips moved before they decided anything. That was the horror and the mercy of it. Sixty words, half-forgotten, mispronounced, a child's fragments carried twenty-five years in the body's deepest cellar—and they came up singing, or trying to, cracked and stumbling, Wren's voice joining Iven's in the dark like a lame foot finding an old dance.

The mountain answered.

Not metaphor. Not imagination. The veins of silver light in the walls *pulsed*, once, like a great slow heart taking notice, and the falling water changed its rhythm to match the song's cadence, and the letters on the black basin kindled from within until the whole chamber burned with soft white fire and Wren felt—not heard, *felt*—the mountain speak.

It spoke in Old Highmoor.

And Wren understood it.

Every word. Whole sentences pouring through the stone and the light and the marrow of their own bones, vast and patient and old beyond reckoning, and there was no translation lag, no straining, no reaching. The language simply arrived in them already known, the way hunger arrives, the way grief arrives—as if it had always lived there and had merely been waiting for permission to stand up.

*"Who wakes the shrine?"* the mountain asked, in a voice like roots shifting under soil. *"Two small fires in a cold age. A broken oath and half a blood. Speak, children of the sundering. What do you carry?"*

Wren's knees nearly buckled. They gripped the basin's rim, and the black stone was warm, impossibly warm, warm as a living thing, and the answer rose in them unbidden—in Old Highmoor, fluent and strange, their own voice sounding like someone else's:

"We carry the oath-half of the hidden court. We carry the road-knowledge of the border. We carry—" their voice broke, and they let it, here, where no chancellor could hear weakness and price it— "we carry a dead woman's lullabies and we never knew what they were for."

Silence. The long listening kind.

Then the light moved. It gathered out of the walls and pooled above the basin, shaping itself—not into a face, nothing so crude, but into a presence, an attention, something regarding them the way winter regards a seed. And it said, gently, with something that in any other voice might have been sorrow:

*"Grey child. You have spoken your mother's tongue as if chewing glass. Why do you hate it?"*

"I don't hate it," Wren said—and then stopped, because the lie dissolved even as they tried to speak it, the way certain poisons dissolve on the tongue and reveal themselves by what they refuse to be. They knew poisons. They knew this one. "I'm afraid of it," they said instead. "It's—it's the thing that got my mother killed. It's the reason there's a bounty on us now. Every time I used it, even in secret, even just humming, I was—I was carrying something flammable through a house full of lamps. So I put it down. I put *myself* down. Both halves. I made myself small and grey and forgettable and I told myself that was survival, that was competence, that was—" their laugh came out jagged— "that was the best a border child could hope for."

*"And was it?"*

The question hung there. Wren looked down at Iven, who was watching them with his hand still resting on their wounded palm, who had crossed a broken realm alone at eight years old because everyone else who could have gone was dead, who had never once had the option of being forgettable. Who had looked at Wren on a storm-black kitchen step and seen, instantly and without shame, something Wren had spent nine years refusing to look at.

"No," Wren said. "It was dying slowly and calling it prudence."

*"Good,"* said the mountain, and the light warmed. *"Now listen, half-born, twice-tongued. The oath cannot be remade by kings, for crowns are the knife that cut it. It cannot be remade by the fae lords, for their pride is the hand that held the knife. It can only be remade by one of both bloods who chooses it freely, with no throne's command and no fear's compulsion—only want. Only belonging, claimed aloud."* The presence drew closer, and its attention settled on Wren like a mantle of snow, heavy and cold and strangely comforting. *"You are the door, grey child. You have been the door since your mother sang you into this world on the exact line where the realms were severed. The question is not whether you can open it. The question is whether you dare be a doorway—whether you can bear to stand in the threshold forever, belonging wholly to neither side, so that both sides may pass."*

Wren breathed. The air tasted of stone-water and green old things and, faintly, incredibly, of their mother's kitchen—of barley and woodsmoke and the lavender she hung over the door. Memory or magic; the distinction had stopped mattering.

"I spent nine years being invisible," Wren said slowly, testing each word in both languages, finding it fit equally well in either. "Invisible means no one sees where you stand. A doorway is the opposite. Everyone sees. Everyone walks through you. It's the most visible thing there is." Their hand tightened on the warm black stone. "But it's also the most *useful*. And I've only ever wanted to be useful. Maybe I just had the wrong idea about what that meant."

Iven made a small sound. When Wren glanced down, he was smiling—tentatively, the way the first green comes back after a burn—and he pointed at Wren's chest, then at his own, then joined his hands together, and said a single word that needed no translating:

"*Vessaen.*"

Whole. Together. Woven.

"Yes," Wren said. "All right. Yes."

And the mountain sighed—a vast exhalation through a thousand silver veins, and the light sank down into the basin, and deep beneath their feet, so far down it was less sound than certainty, something ancient turned in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

* * *

Afterward, they sat against the warm wall sharing the last of the wayhouse bread, and Wren did something they had never done in twenty-five years of careful life: they spoke aloud in Old Highmoor, at length, for pleasure. Haltingly at first, then less so. Telling Iven about the kitchens, about the king's bland palate, about the cook Marta who suspected everything and confirmed nothing. Watching delight break across the boy's face at each new word, correcting Wren's grammar with the grave patience of an eight-year-old heir teaching a grown woman to talk.

"You laughed," Wren realized. "You understood my joke. I just made a joke in a language I've known for three weeks and you *laughed*."

Iven shrugged elaborately, as if to say, *what of it*, and stole the rest of the bread.

But when the boy finally slept, curled against Wren's side with the oath-half rising and falling on its cord, Wren stayed awake and listened. The mountain was still murmuring. Far below, that waking thing was stirring faster now, and the silver veins pulsed in a rhythm that was almost—almost—a direction. Not downward, exactly. *Inward.* Toward some deep heart of the Thornfells where the light gathered thickest, where the basin's empty socket ached like a missing tooth.

And threading through the murmur, unmistakable, the mountain repeated one phrase over and over, patient as dripping water, until Wren had it entire and could not unknow it:

*The court beneath is not dead. The court beneath is waiting. Come before the moon turns.*

Wren looked at Iven's sleeping face, and at the amulet, and at the far dark where the light ran deepest, and understood that the choice they had feared most—belonging or survival, crown or covenant—had never really been a choice at all. There was only forward. There was only down.

"Sleep fast, little prince," they murmured in his own tongue, and marveled at how natural it sounded. "Tomorrow we go home."

Far off, muffled by miles of stone, something that was not a wolf and not a man began to sing.
