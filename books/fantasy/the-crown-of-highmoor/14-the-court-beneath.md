## 14. The Court Beneath

The stone sang us down.

Not with words—never words—but with a hum that lived in the marrow of my teeth, a thread of sound that pulled tighter the deeper we went into the mountain's throat. Iven carried his half of the oath-stone against his chest, and I carried my mother's, wrapped in wool at my breastbone, and between them the song rose and fell like breathing. When we turned wrong, it thinned to a whisper. When we turned right, it swelled until the cave walls themselves seemed to shiver with it, frost of old light blooming along seams in the rock that no torch had ever touched.

Wren. *This way.* The mountain had spoken to me two nights ago in the cave shrine, and it had not stopped speaking since.

I had spent nine years learning every road through the Thornfells from above—the smuggler cuts, the goat tracks, the passes that opened for three weeks in high summer and closed like a fist. I had thought myself a cartographer of these mountains. I had been a man reading the cover of a book and calling himself its author.

Below the smuggler roads there were older roads. Below the older roads, stairs.

We found them on the third day, past the point where my maps ended and my nerve should have ended too: a stairway cut into living rock, descending in long spirals, each step worn smooth as river stone by feet that had not walked it in three hundred years. No dust lay on them. That was the first thing that frightened me properly—not the dark, not the depth, but the *cleanliness*, as though the mountain had been keeping the way ready all this time, sweeping it with wind and patience for someone who would never come.

Iven went down those stairs like a boy going home.

He was eleven years old and half-starved and he had watched smugglers die for him at Coldfern, and still he took each step with the certainty of someone who had walked this descent in dreams so many times his body remembered what his mind could not. His small hand found mine in the dark without either of us deciding it. His palm was cold. Mine must have been too, because he did not let go.

*The court is sleeping,* he said in Old Highmoor, and then, in the broken border-tongue he was teaching me word by word, patient as water wearing stone: *Sleeping. Not dead. Mother said. Sleeping is not dead.*

*Then we wake it,* I said, and my accent made him smile—the first smile since the Choir burned the wayhouse—and that smile cost me more than any wound I'd taken. I carried it down the dark like a coal cupped in both hands.

* * *

The air changed before the light did.

It came up the stairwell to meet us, warm and green-smelling, thick with the scent of wet moss and something sweeter underneath—meadowsweet, maybe, or the memory of meadowsweet preserved in stone the way amber preserves an insect. My lungs, trained on tallow smoke and chamber-pot reek and the cloying perfumes of the royal table, seized on it like a drowning man seizing driftwood. Nine years I had breathed the palace. I had not known I was holding my breath the whole time.

Then the light.

It did not come from torches or windows. It seeped out of the walls themselves, pale gold veined through grey rock, pulsing slow as a sleeper's heartbeat. And where it touched, things grew. That was the only word for it. Moss the color of new copper. Fungi that glowed faintly blue and folded closed when our shadows passed over them, shy as snails. A tree—an actual tree, silver-barked, leafless yet somehow luminous—growing sideways out of a wall with its roots drinking from a vein of light, its branches arched over the stairwell like a hand held out in blessing or warning.

Iven stopped beneath it and pressed his forehead to the bark.

*I know you,* he whispered. *I know you, I know you.* The repetition was how he prayed; I had learned that much. He said it the way other children said their own names, to remind themselves they existed.

The tree's leaves—there were no leaves, and yet something rustled—something rustled anyway.

Beyond the tree, the stair opened.

I will try to describe what we saw, and I will fail, because I am a novelist and failure here is the honest thing. But I will fail beautifully, and perhaps that is all any of us can do.

A cavern vast enough to hold weather. That was my first thought—that the space was so large it had its own sky, a ceiling lost in soft golden haze, and drifting through that haze motes of light that moved with intention, circling, settling, rising again like insects over summer water. Below us, terraces descended in wide arcs toward a lake of perfect black water, and around the lake stood the ruins.

Ruins, I say, and yet—

Towers broken at their crowns, yes. Walls cracked. Roofs open to the false sky. Three centuries of catastrophe written in every stone. But the *shapes* remained, and the shapes were nothing human hands had ever taught me. Arches that curved like heron necks. Doorways shaped like the negative space between two standing stones. Balconies that spiraled outward from towers in patterns that hurt the eye if I tried to follow them logically and soothed it if I let them move. Everything built to be looked at the way one looks at running water—not analyzed, but *received*.

And everywhere, everywhere, the pockets of old magic, alive in exile.

A fountain still playing in a courtyard though no spring fed it, its water rising in ribbons that braided and unbraided midair. A garden gone wild and wonderful, flowers blooming in colors I had no names for, colors that existed somewhere between violet and grief. A bridge of woven moonlight spanning a chasm, humming faintly, holding. Lanterns burning in windows of houses whose owners had been ash for three hundred years—burning steadily, faithfully, the way a dog waits at a door.

*They left the lights on,* I said, and my voice cracked apart on the second word.

Iven looked up at me. In the golden haze his eyes were enormous, ancient, eleven years old. *So home knows the way back,* he said. *When they come home.*

No one had come home. Everyone who might have was dead or scattered or hollowed out into the Choir's grey servitude. The lights had burned for three hundred years for no one.

I sat down on the top step, right there, in full view of a dead nation's beauty, and I wept. Not gracefully. Not the single dignified tear of a courtier. I sobbed like the child I had never allowed myself to be, hunched over my mother's oath-stone, crying for lanterns lit for ghosts, for a fountain playing to empty courtyards, for a boy who believed sleeping was not dead because believing anything else would kill him.

Iven sat beside me and put his arms around my arm and waited, because children understand grief better than kings do—they know it cannot be hurried, only accompanied.

* * *

We went down among the ruins as the false day dimmed—the light in the walls dimming with it, I noticed, the whole cavern breathing on some rhythm older than clocks.

The silence was the worst part. Not emptiness-silence but *absence*-silence, the silence of a room after a party, of a bed after a death. We walked streets built for thousands and met no one. Our footsteps echoed off walls that had once echoed music. In a great hall whose roof had fallen inward, tables still stood in rows, and I understood with a lurch that they were banquet tables, set for celebration, and whatever celebration had been planned on the night the treaty broke had never happened. Dust lay on those tables. The only dust in Highmoor. Grief, even magic will not sweep away.

But then—Iven found the cradle.

He heard it before I did, a sound so faint I took it for the fountain-song drifting through broken streets: a low, sweet chiming, rhythmic, patient. He followed it through a doorway shaped like a crescent moon into a small round chamber, and there, in an alcove lined with soft grey fur gone stiff with age, stood a cradle of white wood, and above the cradle hung a mobile of tiny glass birds, and the birds were singing.

Three hundred years, and the birds were singing. Some enchantment laid down by a parent with more love than foresight, meant to soothe a baby through its first winters, still working, still faithful, spinning its little melody into an empty room for a child who was now a king in exile hiding behind my legs.

Iven reached up and stopped the mobile with one finger. The birds went silent. The quiet that rushed in was enormous.

*This was mine,* he said. Very quietly. *I think this was mine.*

And then, because he was eleven and had walked out of a slaughtered world: *Why did everyone leave?*

What could I tell him? That the world above had grown afraid of beauty it couldn't tax? That men with ledgers and seals had decided an old covenant was an old inconvenience? That Chancellor Vess's kind had existed in every century, in every realm, filing the miraculous under *threats* and the sacred under *assets*?

*Grown people broke something,* I said finally, in his language, badly. *Broken things frighten them, so they run. It's the oldest cowardice there is.*

He considered this with terrible seriousness. Then: *You didn't run.*

*I ran for nine years,* I said. *I just called it something else.*

* * *

We made camp that night in what had clearly been a guardhouse—a low stone building squatting beside the lake shore, its hearth still drawing, its walls still warm. Whether the warmth was residual magic or the mountain itself exhaling, I couldn't say. I cooked the last of our wayhouse provisions on a fire that burned gold instead of orange, and Iven slept curled against my side with both halves of the oath-stone near him, and in the night the halves began to sing to each other across the space between them, softly, like lovers murmuring in the next room.

I lay awake listening, and the mountain listened with me.

That was when I felt it—not heard, *felt*, a pressure against my awareness like a face pressed to the far side of a veil. Attention. Old, vast, sorrowful attention, turning toward us the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice. The pockets of light in the walls brightened fractionally. The glass birds in the distant cradle-chamber gave one tentative chirp.

Something down here knew the stones had come home.

I did not sleep again. Toward what I judged to be morning—though who could say, under a sky of haze—I rose and stood at the guardhouse door and looked out across the black lake, and saw, on the far shore, a structure I had taken for a hillside the day before.

It was not a hillside. It was a palace. Smaller than the ruin-terraces around the lake, older-looking, its architecture less ornate and more severe, built of stone so dark it seemed cut from night itself. And at its heart, one window burned with steady silver light—the only light in all of Highmoor that moved with purpose rather than memory.

As I watched, the light in the walls along the lakeshore began to change. Pulse by pulse, terrace by terrace, the gold brightened and shifted toward silver, spreading outward from the dark palace like dawn rolling backward across a landscape, and the motes in the false sky gathered and streamed toward the far shore, and the fountain in the ruined courtyard played louder, and the garden flowers turned on their stems as if following a sun that had finally remembered them.

The mountain was waking. Or something in it was.

Behind me, Iven stirred and woke, and came to stand at my shoulder, and together we watched the light walk across the water toward us.

*Someone's home,* he whispered.

The oath-stones at our breasts sang one clear, ringing note, harmonizing perfectly, and across the lake the silver window flared in answer.

And then, from the dark palace's gates, a figure emerged—small at this distance, upright, unhurried—carrying a staff tipped with light, and began, without haste, without hesitation, to walk around the lake's edge toward the two strangers standing in the doorway of a guardhouse three hundred years abandoned.

Iven's grip found my hand. His was shaking now.

*The Last Steward,* he breathed. *Mother told me. If you ever get home, find the Steward. The Steward keeps the door.*

The figure drew closer, and closer, and the light of the staff resolved into a face older than the ruins, older than the treaty, older perhaps than the mountain itself—and it was looking directly at me. Not at the heir. At me. At the grey-blooded taster, the invisible man, the border child who had spent a lifetime being no one.

Its mouth opened, and it spoke—in Old Highmoor, in the border-tongue, in both at once, the way I had spoken in the cave shrine—and what it said was:

*"You are late, oath-keeper. But you are here."*
