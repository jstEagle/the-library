## 15. The Last Steward

The song of the stones did not stop at the water's edge.

Wren had expected it to—had braced for silence the way a swimmer braces for cold—but the half-oath at their throat and the amulet against Iven's chest kept singing as they waded into the flooded gallery of the sunken court, and the sound passed through the water unchanged, as if water were no more an obstacle to it than air. It was not a melody, exactly. It was closer to the feeling of a name remembered after years: *oh, yes. Oh, it was always this.*

The water came to their waists, then their ribs. Above them, the mountain's roots arched in vaults of living rock threaded with veins of pale light—not torchlight, not moonlight, but something older that seemed to seep out of the stone itself, the way warmth seeps from a hearth long after the fire has died. Pillars rose around them, carved with figures whose faces had been worn smooth by three centuries of dark water. Wren touched one as they passed. The stone was warm.

"They're still here," Iven whispered. He was speaking Old Highmoor now without thinking, the way he breathed. "The pillars. They're listening."

"Listening to what?"

"To whoever comes home." His voice caught on the last word. In the strange light his face looked both younger and impossibly old, a child carrying a court's worth of grief in a body barely tall enough to see over a kitchen counter. "My mother said the pillars remember every oath ever spoken beneath them. She said if you stood very still, you could hear the old ones humming."

Wren stood very still.

And heard them—or told themselves they did—a low harmonic under the water-sound, under the stone-song, like a hall full of people breathing quietly in the next room. The hair rose on their arms. Nine years in the palace had taught Wren every register of human power: the king's bluster, Vess's silk, the kitchen's grumbling chorus. This was none of those things. This was power that did not need to raise its voice because it had never once been disobeyed.

"Keep moving," Wren said, and was grateful their voice came out steady. "The song's getting louder. We're close."

They were. The gallery opened—abruptly, the way a held breath opens—and the water shallowed over a floor of white mosaic, thousands upon thousands of tiles laid in patterns that resolved, as Wren's eyes adjusted, into a map. Not of Highmoor. Of *both*. The mortal realm and the hidden one interlocked like two hands clasped, rivers running through both without regard for any border, mountains rising on one side and continuing, root for root, into the other. At the center of the map, where clasped fingers would meet, stood an altar.

It was modest. That was the first shock of it. After the vaulted galleries and the light-veined stone, Wren had expected something vast, and instead the altar was a simple block of grey granite, knee-high, uncarved, its surface worn concave by centuries of hands. Two halves of a broken circle were set into its top—two shallow channels forming a ring with a gap in it, waiting.

The stones sang so loudly now that Wren felt them in their teeth.

Iven climbed onto the altar's base before Wren could stop him, kneeling, pressing his amulet into one channel. The half-stone fit perfectly. Of course it did. And when it settled against its twin socket, the singing changed—it gained words, almost, syllables just below the threshold of hearing, a voice trying desperately to finish a sentence begun three hundred years ago.

"It wants the other half," Iven said. He looked up at Wren, and his eyes were wet. "It's been waiting. They've all been waiting." He gestured—at the pillars, the map, the light in the stone. "Everyone. Everything. It's all been holding its breath since the Treaty of Ash."

"Then we finish it." Wren reached for the cord at their own neck.

A voice said: **"Not yet."**

* * *

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the pillars and the water and the marrow of Wren's own bones, and it was old the way bedrock is old. Wren's hand froze above the second half of the oath-stone.

From behind the farthest pillar, where the light pooled deepest, someone stood up.

Wren had taken them for part of the architecture—a statue, a shadow, a trick of the seeping glow. But the figure moved, unfolding joint by joint with the care of extreme age, and came forward into the open, and Wren saw that it was neither man nor woman nor anything so simple. It was tall and stooped at once, robed in what might have been grey cloth or might have been cobweb or might have been lichen grown over centuries, and its face—

Its face was like the carved pillars. Smooth where features should have been, worn soft by time, and yet somehow unmistakably *attentive*, the way a landscape can look attentive, the way a mountain looks down.

"I know you," Iven breathed. He had gone white. "You're in the memory-dreams. You held me when I was born. You—" His voice broke. "You're the Steward."

"I am what remains of the office," the figure said. Its voice, up close, was less overwhelming—dry as autumn leaves, kind as a banked fire. "The court had seven stewards when I took the post. Now there is one, and he is mostly stone and stubbornness, and he has waited three hundred years for someone to walk through that door." A hand like driftwood gestured at the altar. "So you will forgive an old servant his caution. Not yet. First, we must be sure."

"Sure of what?" Wren asked. Their hand was still hovering over the oath-half. They made themselves lower it. "We have the stone. Both halves. We have until the thirteenth moon—which is *tonight*, if your mountain keeps time the way the sky does. There's no time for—"

"There is exactly enough time," the Steward said, "for the only thing that matters. Come here, taster of kings."

Wren went cold. "How do you—"

"The pillars told me you were coming nine days ago. They taste everything that enters this place, as you taste everything that enters your king's mouth." Something that was almost humor moved across the smooth face. "Yes. We know what you are. We have known since the moment your mother's blood crossed my threshold, though she never spoke of it either. Grey children are alike in that. You hide the best part of yourselves and call the hiding prudence."

"I'm not—" Wren started, and stopped, because the denial was automatic and they had run out of strength for automatic things somewhere back on the smuggler's road, somewhere between the wayhouse burning and the Choir's song in the dark. "What do you want?"

"To ask you questions. To hear your answers with my own ears, which are better than the pillars'." The Steward lowered itself onto a bench of stone that seemed to have been waiting for him for three centuries, and folded its long hands. "Sit. Both of you. The moon will wait; it always does. It is the only patient thing left in either realm."

Iven sat immediately, cross-legged on the mosaic, right on the map of the borderlands, which struck Wren as either irreverence or perfect instinct. Wren remained standing. Old habits. One did not sit in the presence of power; one made oneself small and useful and memorized the exits.

The Steward watched them not sit, and nodded slowly, as if this too were an answer.

"Question the first," it said. "Who sent you?"

"No one."

"Think carefully. Every soul who has ever approached this altar was sent by someone—a crown, a god, a grievance, a dead parent's ghost. Who sent you, Wren Thornbury, born of the border, taster of the crown of Ashfall?"

Wren opened their mouth to say *no one* again and found the words wouldn't come, because they weren't true. They thought of their mother's lullabies, hummed low so the neighbors wouldn't hear the old tongue. They thought of the maps sewn into their memory, every smuggler road through the Thornfells, learned in childhood and kept sharp through nine years of palace service for reasons they'd never examined—reasons that looked, from this distance, unbearably like hope. They thought of a mud-caked child on a kitchen step during a storm, and how some door inside Wren had swung open before their mind had agreed to anything.

"My mother," Wren said finally. "She died carrying her half of this. And I—" Their voice did something humiliating. They mastered it. "I don't know who sent me. Maybe nobody. Maybe I just... couldn't keep walking past it anymore."

The Steward was silent for a long moment. Then: "Good. An honest non-answer. The last king who stood here answered that question with a list of titles." Something flickered across the smooth face—grief, perhaps, or contempt, or the place where grief and contempt had fused long ago. "Continue. Question the second. What do you believe the covenant is?"

"A treaty," Wren said. "Between the human realms and the fae. Terms of passage, trade, mutual—"

"Wrong."

"—alliance—"

"*Wrong.*" The word rang off the pillars, and every vein of light in the vaults dimmed at once, and the water went still as glass. When the Steward spoke again its voice was quiet, and that was worse. "That is what your archives say. That is what Chancellor Vess's ledger says, in the pages he has not yet burned. A treaty. Terms. As if the binding of two worlds were a matter of tariffs and borders." It leaned forward. "Child, the covenant is not an agreement *between* the realms. The covenant is the reason there are two realms *at all*—and the reason they were never meant to be strangers. Before it, there was one world, and it was dying of its own weight, too much of everything pressed too close together. The first covenanters split the world the way you split a log: along the grain, cleanly, so that each half could breathe. And the covenant is the scar-line. The promise that the split would heal into strength, not rot. That the two halves would grow *interwoven*, each feeding the other, root to branch. Your ancestors did not break a treaty three hundred years ago. They broke a *healing*. Do you understand what it is you would reforge?"

Wren's mouth was dry. "No," they said. "Tell me."

"Good again. 'No' is the beginning of wisdom. Kings begin with 'yes' and never recover." The light in the veins brightened slowly, like breath returning. "Now. The last question. And I warn you, taster, that it is the only question, and the other two were merely its scaffolding."

The Steward rose. It came toward Wren with steps that made no sound on the mosaic, and stopped close enough that Wren could smell it—petrichor, woodsmoke, and underneath, faintly, their mother's lullabies made into a scent, which should have been impossible and was not.

"When the thirteenth moon crests tonight," the Steward said, "the gap in that ring must be closed by a living voice speaking the covenant whole—in both tongues, at once, from a throat that carries both bloods. There are perhaps four such throats left in existence. Three of them belong to the Hollow Choir, who sold their doubleness to the treaty-breakers and can no longer speak honestly in either tongue. The fourth is yours." The smooth face inclined. "A king cannot speak it. Power cannot speak it. A fae lord cannot speak it—for the fae gave up their claim to the mortal side long ago, and the words would die in their mouths. It requires someone who is *both*, and who *chooses* it. Not someone conscripted. Not someone fulfilling a dead mother's wish, or avenging a burned wayhouse, or protecting a frightened child out of pity. Those are fine reasons. They are not sufficient ones. The covenant must be chosen freely, wholly, knowing its full cost." The driftwood hand gestured, gentle, at Wren's chest. "So tell me, grey child. Knowing what it costs—knowing that the moment you speak, you belong to the border and nothing else, that no crown will shelter you and no court will claim you, that you will spend the rest of your life standing precisely where the two worlds grind against each other, taking the friction of both forever—"

"Do you choose it?"

* * *

The silence afterward lasted a year. Or a heartbeat. Wren could not have said.

They became aware, distantly, of Iven watching them with his whole body, the way children watch—their eyes enormous, their breath held, their hands curled in their lap like small animals gone still. Aware of the altar behind them, the two channels, the gap. Aware of the water dripping somewhere in the high dark, keeping time like a patient clock.

And aware—this was the strangest thing—of the palace, nine days and a world away. The kitchens at dawn. The weight of the tasting spoon. The particular loneliness of being indispensable and invisible at once, of knowing every dish that passed the king's lips and having no dish of one's own, no table where anyone saved a seat. Nine years Wren had survived by being forgettable, and they had called it competence, and it had even *been* competence, but underneath the competence had always been this question, asked in their mother's voice, in the old tongue, in the dark before waking:

*What would it be to belong somewhere?*

To speak the covenant was to answer it. And answering it meant giving up every protection invisibility had ever bought them. No more walls to hide behind. Just the border, and the wind, and the endless friction of two worlds learning to touch again.

"You're asking me," Wren said slowly, "to give up the only skill I've ever had. Disappearing."

"I am asking you," the Steward said, "to stop mistaking survival for a life."

Wren laughed. It came out cracked and startled, half sob, and echoed strangely off the pillars, and several of the light-veins pulsed as if laughing too. "You sound like my mother."

"I sound like everyone who has ever stood at a threshold," the Steward said. "That is what thresholds do. They talk."

Wren looked at Iven. Iven looked back, and did not plead, and did not promise, and did not do any of the things a child might do to tip a scale. He simply looked, with the terrible clean honesty of the very young, and Wren understood that whatever Wren chose, this child would carry the shape of it for the rest of his life—the way Wren carried the shape of a mother who chose duty and died of it, or maybe lived by it, the stories disagreed, the stories always disagreed.

"I'm afraid," Wren said aloud. It seemed important to say it in front of witnesses. "I've been afraid for nine years and I'm very good at it. Fear is practically my native language now. I have two, apparently, and I'm fluent in both." They turned to the Steward. "If I say yes—if I stand at that altar tonight and speak—will it hurt?"

"Yes," the Steward said, without cruelty. "Everything true does, the first time."

"And afterward? Will I regret it?"

"That," said the Steward, "is the one question in creation I cannot answer for you. That is why it must be *you*."

Wren closed their eyes. Behind their eyelids: the kitchen step, the storm, the mud-caked child saying *covenant* like a prayer. The wayhouse burning. The stones singing. Their mother's voice, rising at last above the lullabies, saying what she had perhaps been saying all along in the old tongue while Wren slept and half-heard:

*Come home. Come home. Come home.*

"Yes," Wren said.

The word fell into the water and the water carried it outward, ring upon ring, to the pillars, to the map, to the altar, and every vein of light in the sunken court flared gold at once, and the stones did not sing anymore—they *answered*.

"So be it," said the Steward, and bowed—bowed, to a food-taster in river-soaked clothes, bowed as one bows to a monarch, and the gesture did not feel absurd; it felt like a key turning. "Then hear the terms of your office, Keeper of the Scar-Line, before the night takes us all. The covenant cannot be spoken from memory alone. It must be given. Three hundred years I have held it, word by word, in a head going slowly to stone. Tonight, before the moon crests, I will pour it into you—all of it, both tongues, every clause and cadence—and it will burn going in, and you will hate me, and then you will understand it, and then you will love it, and there is no order in which those three things can be skipped." The smooth face lifted toward the distant ceiling, toward the mountain, toward the thin sliver of sky beyond. "We have perhaps six hours. Sit down, Wren Thornbury. Your life as a forgotten man ends now, and your life as the only door begins."

Wren sat.

Iven slid across the mosaic and pressed against Wren's side, small and solid and warm, and Wren put an arm around him without thinking about it, the way you hold something you have already decided to keep.

The Steward drew breath—a vast, geological breath, three hundred years deep.

And that was when the light in the highest gallery, far above, guttered.

Not dimmed. *Guttered*—the way candlelight gutters when a door opens somewhere in the house, when a draft arrives from a room that should be sealed. The Steward stopped mid-breath. Its smooth face turned upward, and for the first time since they had met it, it looked old in a way that had nothing to do with age.

"They are in the mountain," it said quietly. "The ones who sing hollow. They have found the way down."

Far overhead, faint but unmistakable, threading down through miles of stone like poison through water, came the sound of voices. Many voices. Perfectly harmonized.

Perfectly empty.

The Steward looked down at Wren, and Iven, and the altar with its waiting gap, and said, with the terrible calm of a man reading a ledger he has read before:

"We will need to work faster than I promised. And child—" this to Wren, and the driftwood hand closed briefly, hard, around Wren's wrist, "—whatever enters this court behind them, however it wears the faces of the grieving—you must not stop speaking. No matter what you hear. No matter *who* you hear. Promise me."

Above them, closer now, the Hollow Choir began to sing.
