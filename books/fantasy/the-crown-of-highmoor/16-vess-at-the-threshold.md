## 16. Vess at the Threshold

The steward's warning came as a change in the water.

Wren had learned to read the sunken court the way they had once learned to read a king's face across a breakfast table—the small tell-tales, the tremors beneath stillness. Here, the tell was in the flooded gallery that served as Highmoor's eastern gate: the black water that pooled between the drowned arches began, very quietly, to run backward. Toward the mountain's heart. As if the ruins themselves were inhaling.

"They're coming," Wren said.

The Last Steward stood at the edge of the gallery, robes trailing into water that did not wet them, and inclined his head with the terrible patience of someone who had waited three hundred years and could wait three hundred more. "I know," he said. "Iron first. Then men. Then the thing that wears the men."

"How long?"

"Long enough for you to decide what you are." His eyes—old, opal-flecked, kind in the way of things that have outlived kindness's usefulness—settled on Wren. "Or long enough for you to fail to. Time is generous that way. It always offers both."

Behind them, deeper in the sunken court, Iven was singing.

He didn't know he was doing it, Wren thought. That was the heartbreak of the boy—that grief poured out of him in Old Highmoor melodies his murdered tutors had taught him, half-lullaby, half-keening, and he sang them while he worked, sorting the shards of the shattered covenant altar by the light of moss that glowed like drowned stars. The oath-stone halves hung together now on a cord around his neck, and where they touched they hummed, a thin silver thread of sound that never quite stopped. For three days it had been their compass, their comfort, and their curse—a beacon singing *here, here, here* into every hollow vein of the Thornfells.

Wren had bound it in wool. It hadn't helped. The stone sang through wool, through flesh, through Wren's own ribs, which had begun, unsettlingly, to answer it.

*Long enough for you to decide what you are.*

"I've decided," Wren said.

"No," the Steward said gently. "You have decided what you will do. What you *are* is still being written. There is a difference, grey child, and the next hour is going to teach it to you."

* * *

They came up through the drowned gate an hour before dusk, when the light in the cavern went the color of old honey and the glowing moss along the vaulted ceiling dimmed to embers.

Wren heard the soldiers before seeing them—not boots, but the absence of the ruins' own sounds. The drip of water stopped. The faint chime of the stones went silent, as if the whole buried world were holding its breath around the intrusion. Then torchlight bloomed in the far gallery, orange and brutal, and shadows lunged up the walls of a place that had never been built to know them.

Twelve soldiers. Chainmail wrapped in cloth to muffle it, but iron is iron, and the ruins flinched from every step. And ahead of them, unhurried, her boots dry on ground that soaked everyone else—

Chancellor Vess.

She had changed since Wren had last seen her across the width of a council chamber. Or rather, she had stopped changing—stopped performing, stopped wearing the soft gray modesty she'd used for years to seem like merely the king's careful bookkeeper. Walking now through the court of Highmoor with her chin level and her eyes cataloguing everything, she looked like what she apparently had been all along: a woman who had read the Treaty of Ash down to its missing page and found her life's work in the ashes.

"Wren Quintana." Her voice carried strangely well in the vaulted dark, neither loud nor soft, pitched the way she pitched everything—to be remembered accurately. "You look terrible. The mountain air disagrees with you."

"You brought twelve men and a king's seal into a place that eats iron," Wren said. "And you're worried about my complexion."

Something flickered at the corner of Vess's mouth. Not quite a smile. Approval, maybe, or its taxidermied cousin. She stopped ten paces away, close enough for the torchlight to find the details Wren remembered too well: ink-stained middle finger, the ledger-clasp at her belt, the small scar through one eyebrow from a story she had never told anyone and never would.

"I'm going to make you an offer," Vess said. "Before I do, I want you to understand that I have already accounted for your refusal. I have drafted the response to your refusal. I have planned for the response to *that*. This is not because I think you are predictable, Wren. It is because I think you are *good*, and good people are the only ones whose choices can be relied upon." She drew something from inside her coat. "So. Let us waste no time pretending otherwise."

The seal of Aldric III caught the torchlight—wax the size of a plum, gold leaf pressed with the crowned stag, still warm-looking, still official. Wren's stomach turned over. They knew what it meant. Everyone in the palace had known what it meant, the way everyone knows what a drawn blade means without ever saying it: the king's seal, carried loose in a chancellor's hand, was the crown admitting it could not speak for itself anymore.

"The king grieves," Vess said. "Poisoned at his own table, in front of his own court, by a taster he trusted—or so the story goes now, and stories are so much easier to carry than truth. He has signed a proclamation declaring the fae realm hostile. He has signed a warrant naming you a traitor. And this morning, before we marched, he signed—" she held it up between two fingers, delicately, the way she handled anything valuable, "—a full pardon. Restitution. A pension. A house in the capital, if you want it. Your name scrubbed clean. Your mother's name scrubbed clean. Everything you did for nine years, every dish you swallowed so that man could eat his breakfast in peace, acknowledged at last. Publicly. With honors."

The words landed exactly where she aimed them. Wren felt each one strike—*your mother's name* most of all—and hated how precisely she had measured the shot.

"And in exchange," Wren said.

"In exchange you hand over the boy." Vess said it plainly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Contained. That is the word the proclamation uses. Contained. He would be kept—safely, comfortably, I give you my word on that—in the deep cells beneath the archive, where the old treaty magic still has roots. He would live. The realm would be stable. The Hollow Choir would be called off, because the Choir answers to the terms of the broken treaty, and a contained heir satisfies those terms better than a dead one." She tilted her head. "You would save his life, Wren. Do you see? That is the elegance of it. You would not be betraying him. You would be the only person in either world trying to keep him alive."

"That's not true."

"It is *almost* true," Vess said, "which is the same thing, in practice, for almost everyone. But you are not almost anyone, are you? That is the entire problem with you." She took a step closer. The soldiers behind her shifted; somewhere in the dark, water dripped once and went quiet again. "So let me say the part I did not put in the draft. If you refuse, I take him anyway. I have twelve men, and you have a boy, a smuggler with a broken arm, and whatever the ruins choose to do—which, I will note, they have done remarkably little of so far. Ruins, Wren. That is what remains when a people cannot protect their own treasures. And if you die defending him, you die a traitor, and your mother dies again in every record I rewrite, and the girl in chapter twenty-two of your sad little life never gets born into a world with a door open in it. Whereas if you accept—" she spread her hands, "—everyone lives. Stably. Boringly. Forever. I am offering you the kingdom's favorite virtue, Wren. I am offering you *enough*."

The oath-stone sang against Iven's chest, far back in the dark. Wren could feel it through the floor, through their own bones, a thin silver insistence: *here, here, here.*

"You've been reading the archives," Wren said slowly. "Three centuries of them. The missing page. The Treaty of Ash. Tell me what the covenant actually says, Chancellor. Since you know everything else."

For the first time, Vess hesitated.

It was small—a half-breath, a blink held a moment too long—but Wren had spent nine years learning to read faces across tables where being wrong meant dying, and they saw it land. Saw the knowledge move behind her eyes and find nowhere safe to set itself down.

"The covenant says," Vess said at last, evenly, "that the two realms are bound. Interwoven. That neither may be sealed, severed, or ruled over the other. That the oath must be spoken by one of both blood, freely, at the turning of the thirteenth moon." Her jaw tightened. "Yes. I know what it says. I have known for eleven years. I burned the page myself, twenty years ago, when I was young enough to believe that knowing a thing and approving of it were the same act."

"Then why—"

"Because I watched what the last covenant cost!" It cracked out of her, sudden and human, echoing off the drowned arches, and every soldier flinched. Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them she was composed again, but the composure had seams in it now, and Wren could see the thing underneath: not ambition. Fear. Cold, patient, decades-deep fear. "Do you know what the borderlands were, before Ash? I do. My grandmother's grandmother came from Coldfern. Grey-blooded, like your mother, like half the smugglers on those roads. And I grew up on the stories of what happened when the realms were *woven*—the fae taking human debts, humans selling fae names, children traded across the veil like coin. The covenant didn't protect people. It *entangled* them. The Treaty of Ash cut the knot because the knot was strangling everyone tied into it. Yes, it broke Highmoor. Yes, it left that boy—" a flick of her eyes toward the dark where Iven had gone silent, listening, "—alone in the world. I have read the casualty ledgers, Wren. I recite them to myself when I can't sleep. But the alternative to a clean severance is three hundred years of entanglement, and I chose the severance, and I have spent my whole life making certain the choice holds. Because the other road ends with *you*—" her finger came up, steady, aimed, "—standing at a border forever, a door that both sides walk through whenever they want, and I have read enough history to know what doors get used for."

"So you'd rather there be no door at all."

"I would rather no one be *made* into a door." Her voice dropped, and for one unguarded moment she sounded tired beyond counting. "You think I don't understand what they're asking of you? The Steward, the stone, the moon? They need a body, Wren. A grey-blooded body willing to stand in the gap and be used as a hinge until it rusts. Your mother refused. Did you know that? She refused, and they sent her out anyway, carrying half a stone through a war zone, and it killed her. I have read *that* ledger too." Something moved through her face—grief, maybe, or its efficient administrative equivalent. "I am offering you the only exit from that story. Take the pardon. Give me the boy. Live a long, dull, free life, and let the old world stay properly dead."

The silence after was enormous.

Far off in the dark, Iven began to walk toward them. Wren heard his bare feet on the stone, unhurried, and the soft double-note of the oath-halves swinging against each other, and then the boy himself stepped into the honey-colored light—small, mud-scrubbed, nine years old and older than the mountains, looking up at Chancellor Vess with the flat, ancient calm of his vanished court.

"You knew my mother's mother," Iven said. In accented, careful Common Tongue—he had been learning it from Wren for weeks, word by word, the way Wren had been learning his. "In the ledgers."

Vess went very still.

"She wrote poems," Iven said. "My tutor read them to us. About the border. About how a door is not a wound." He touched the oath-stone at his throat. "You burned her words too?"

"I archived them," Vess said, barely audible. "Everything I burned, I copied first. I am not a monster, child. I am a librarian of the apocalypse."

"My mother said," Iven went on, relentless, gentle, "that people who copy everything they burn are the saddest people in any world. Because they know exactly what they destroyed." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and absolutely unafraid. "You are offering Wren a cage with a pension. I have heard this offer before. The Choir makes it too. They also say *contained*. They also say *safe*. They also say *everyone lives*." He turned to Wren then, and held out his hand—small, grimy, steady. "Grey child. Say no. Please. Say it in whichever tongue you like. Just say it standing up."

Wren looked at the offered hand. At the seal in Vess's fingers, gleaming with borrowed authority. At the twelve soldiers, young and scared, gripping weapons in a place that flinched from their iron. At the honey-dark vaults of a court that had waited three hundred years for someone—anyone—to stand in the gap freely.

Nine years of being forgettable. Nine years of swallowing poison so other people could pretend the world was safe. Nine years of maps drawn in secret and lullabies sung in secret and a self kept in secret, folded small enough to survive.

*I am offering you enough,* Vess had said.

"No," Wren said.

It came out in both tongues at once—*no* and *nau*, refusal and refusal, and the ruins heard it. Every glowing seam of moss brightened. The black water in the galleries shivered. The oath-stone rang like a struck bell, one clear note that went up through the vaulted dark and out through the bones of the mountain, and Wren felt it leave their own chest as if a second voice had joined theirs from inside.

Vess exhaled slowly. She looked, Wren thought, almost relieved—as though some part of her had drafted this ending too and needed only to watch it arrive.

"I truly did hope," she said quietly. She tucked the king's seal back into her coat, and nodded once to her soldiers, and the torches began to advance. "Take the boy. Kill the traitor if they resist. And if the ruins object—" she glanced up at the brightening dark overhead, and her voice went dry as ash, "—then someone had better explain to me why they've let us walk this far."

From the tunnel behind the soldiers came a sound that was not soldiers at all.

A humming. Many voices, sweet and hollow, harmonizing down the flooded gallery in a melody Wren had heard once before, on the smuggler's road, and never forgotten: the Hollow Choir, arriving late to a bargain already struck—and singing, as they came, the first notes of a hunt.

The Steward's voice rose somewhere behind Wren, calm and terribly old:

"Now, grey child. Now you learn what you are."
