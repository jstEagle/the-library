## 17. Betrayal in the Ruins

The song woke Wren before the danger did.

It came through the stone the way water comes through chalk—slowly, then all at once—a thin silver thread of sound threading up through the floor of the sunken court and into their ribs. The two halves of the oath-stone had been singing to each other for three days now, ever since Iven set his amulet on the steward's altar beside the broken half that had ridden against Wren's mother's heart until the end. But this was different. This was louder. This was *urgent*.

Wren sat up in the dark of the antechamber where they'd made their beds among fallen columns, and knew, with the cold certainty of nine years spent reading poison in the curl of steam off a broth, that something had changed.

"Iven." They touched the boy's shoulder. He slept curled like a fern frond, one hand still closed around the amulet even in dreams. "Iven, wake up."

His eyes opened all at once, no drowsiness in them at all—the way of children who have run too long to sleep deeply. He looked at Wren. He looked at the ceiling, where the vaulted stone of Highmoor's court arched away into darkness shot through with veins of faint blue light, the old magic that kept this place alive beneath the mountain like a heart beating in a drowned chest.

Then he said, in Old Highmoor, the word Wren had learned first and hated most: *"They are coming."*

* * *

The steward was already awake.

Of course he was. Wren had never once seen Aldous sleep, though they had been three days in the ruins and had shared bread and melted snow-water with him every evening. He stood at the edge of the great hall where the covenant altar waited under its canopy of living root, and he was listening to the stones the way an old physician listens to a failing lung.

"It is not the stone singing," he said, without turning. His voice was dry as autumn leaves, and it carried strangely in the vast dark, as if the court itself were inclined to hear him. "Or not only. The oath-halves call to each other, yes. But there is another voice underneath. A human voice. Carried on Choir-craft."

Wren's stomach dropped. "How far?"

"Half a day. Perhaps less." Aldous turned then, and in the blue half-light his face looked carved from the same pale stone as the pillars—ancient, patient, and terribly tired. "They did not find you, child. Someone told them."

"No one knew." Wren heard their own voice go flat and hard, the voice they used in the king's kitchens when a dish was suspect and panic would kill faster than arsenic. "No one alive knew we were coming here. The smugglers at Coldfern—" They stopped.

The wayhouse at Coldfern. Maren's rough hands wrapping bread and cheese for the road. Old Tam laughing by the fire, saying *go safe, grey child, go safe*. The Choir had hit the wayhouse four nights ago. Smugglers had died covering their escape—Wren had heard the screams fade behind them as Iven dragged them up the goat track into the snow. They had believed everyone left behind was dead.

But not everyone.

"Bren," Wren said. The name came out like a stone coughed up from the throat. Bren, who had taken a crossbow bolt through the shoulder getting them out the back door. Bren, who had pressed his bloodied forehead to Wren's hand and gasped, *run, I'll hold them, run.* "Bren survived."

Aldous nodded slowly. "And the Hollow Choir are not cruel, which is worse than cruelty. Cruelty hardens a man. Kindness unmakes him. They bound his wounds. They fed him. And they asked him only one question, over and over, gently, while his shoulder festered: *where does the grey child go?*" The steward's eyes, pale as frost, settled on Wren with terrible gentleness. "He held out for two days, I think. Perhaps three. It is not a judgment on him. It is simply what bodies do."

Wren's hands had become fists without their permission. Rage rose in them like floodwater—rage at Bren, at the Choir, at Vess, at the crown that had bought nine years of their life for a copper stipend and a title no one spoke aloud—and beneath the rage, shamefully, a small cold voice that said: *you would have talked too. You know exactly how long you would have lasted. You built your whole life around knowing.*

"We can still run," Wren said. "There are deeper ways. You said the court goes down—"

"The court goes down," Aldous agreed, "and the altar stays here. The covenant must be spoken at the altar, under the thirteenth moon, at its crest. There is no other place. There is no other time." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Wren had seen since their mother's lullabies stopped. "You have perhaps six hours before the moon crests. They will be here in five."

Iven tugged Wren's sleeve. When Wren looked down, the boy was holding out the amulet—the half-stone on its worn leather cord—and his chin was set in the particular stubborn line that Wren had learned meant *I have decided something and you will not enjoy it.*

"No," Wren said, though he hadn't said anything yet.

"You take it," Iven said in his careful, accented Common Tongue, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "If they take me, they do not take both halves. The song cannot finish with one half."

"I'm not taking your—"

"You *take it*." And there, just for a moment, beneath the child, Wren heard something older—the cadence of a court, of a lineage, of a people who had hidden beneath a mountain for three hundred years rather than let their oath die. Iven pushed the cord into Wren's hand and closed their fingers over it, and the stone was warm, warmer than skin, and it hummed against Wren's palm like a trapped bird.

Aldous watched this exchange and said nothing for a long moment. Then: "Come. If we are to be besieged, we will be besieged properly. Highmoor's court has defended itself before."

* * *

The steward showed them the old defenses the way a grandfather shows grandchildren a garden he planted long ago and expects never to see bloom again.

The sunken court was not defenseless—it had merely been sleeping. At Aldous's word, spoken in the old tongue that made the air taste of rain, channels opened in the floor and filled with water black and mirror-still, fed from some deep vein of the mountain. Bridges of living root retracted or extended at his gesture. The gallery above the great hall, where archers must once have stood, could be reached only by stairs that folded themselves flat as a hand closing.

"There were wardens once," Aldous said, walking the perimeter with his hands clasped behind his back, as if inspecting troops three centuries dead. "Two hundred of them, in the last days. Grey-bloods, every one, because only grey blood can stand in both worlds at once and fight in either." He glanced sideways at Wren. "You understand what the Hollow Choir are now. What they were."

"Wardens," Wren said. "Who stayed behind when the court went down. Who kept serving the treaty's enforcers after the treaty broke."

"They were promised the realms would be rejoined. They were promised it for three hundred years. Promises are a kind of leash, child, and a comfortable leash is the worst kind." Aldous stopped at the head of the grand stair, where the last light from the surface—thin, grey, pre-dawn—came down through a fissure in the mountain's bones. "When they come, they will offer you the same bargain they offered Bren. They will be gentle. That is their weapon. Not the iron."

"And if I say yes?" Wren asked, because some reckless honest part of them needed to hear the answer.

Aldous smiled again, that autumn-leaf smile. "Then you will be very well treated for the rest of a very long life, and the thirteenth moon will pass, and the last door will close, and everything your mother carried, everything Iven ran through winter to save, will be a story with no ending. That is all they want. Not your death. Your *yes*."

Above them, far up the mountain, something began to sing.

* * *

It was not music. Wren had heard the Choir's name spoken in whispers their whole life and had imagined choirs—hymns, harmonies, something holy twisted wrong. The reality was worse. It was voices braided together into a single sound that slid between the notes, that found the frequency of grief itself and leaned on it. It came down the fissure and through the stone and into the water channels, so that the black mirrors of the flooded floors shivered and rang.

Iven clapped his hands over his ears and made a small hurt sound, and Wren pulled him back from the gallery edge and held him, feeling the boy's heartbeat hammering against their own.

"What is it doing?" Wren shouted over it.

"Calling," Aldous called back. He stood at the stair-head unbowed, his robes stirring in a wind that should not exist underground. "It is a summons in the shape of a song. Every grey-blood within earshot hears it and feels *homesick*. That is how they recruit. That is how they always recruited. Listen to me, Wren Thornbury—" and his voice cut through the Choir-song like a knife through silk, sudden and sharp, "—when it finds your blood, and it will, you will feel that you have been lonely your entire life and that these voices are the end of loneliness. You will feel that they understand you. That is the trap. Loneliness lies. Do you hear me? *Loneliness lies.*"

Wren heard. And felt it anyway—a sweetness rising in the chest like the first swallow of wine after a fasting day, a sense of being *called by name*, of voices that knew about the pantry loft and the secret maps and the mother's lullabies hummed into a child's hair before the world took her. For three heartbeats Wren wanted nothing in the world but to climb the stair and walk into that song.

Then Iven's fingers tightened in theirs, small and grimy and certain, and the sweetness curdled, and Wren understood: *this is what it felt like to be invisible. This is the ache I built my life around. They haven't found my weakness. They've found my wound.*

"They're in the upper galleries," Wren said, and their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "Grey-bloods. How many?"

Aldous tilted his head, listening past the song. "Twelve in the song. More who do not sing. Soldiers of the crown, I think—Vess will not trust the Choir alone with his prize." He drew himself up, and for the first time Wren saw what he must have been, three hundred years ago, when the world broke: not a caretaker. A warden-captain. "Twelve singers, forty soldiers, one chancellor's ambition. Against an old man, a child, and a food-taster." He looked at Wren, and his frost-pale eyes were almost merry. "I have seen worse odds. I have *been* worse odds."

* * *

They came down the grand stair at moonset-minus-three-hours, and the battle for Highmoor's court lasted less than an hour and cost more than Wren knew how to count.

The soldiers came first, torchlight bobbing down the fissure stair, and the mountain fought them the way a mountain fights—slowly, indifferently, with gravity and water. The first rank reached the bottom of the stair and the bridge that wasn't there anymore; the root-ways had folded shut, and men in crown livery stepped onto darkness and fell into black water that swallowed them without a ripple. Torches hissed out. Screaming, muffled and brief.

Then the Choir came down, and the mountain's tricks meant nothing, because the Choir walked on the water.

Wren watched them cross the flooded floor as if it were marble—twelve figures in undyed wool, faces calm, barefoot, *barefoot in the black water*, singing—and felt the homesickness rise again and beat it down with the memory of Iven's grip. Beside them, Aldous stepped forward onto the altar steps, raised both hands, and spoke a single word in Old Highmoor that Wren's blood understood before their mind did:

*Stand.*

The court answered. Pillars that had slept for centuries groaned awake; veins of blue light flared along the vaulted ceiling; the water itself rose in slow walls on either side of the hall, a corridor of liquid glass. The Choir's song faltered—for the first time, faltered—and in the faltering Wren heard something heartbreaking: twelve voices, suddenly only human, suddenly tired.

"Aldous!" One of them called out—not a threat, an appeal, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Old man, the court is *dead*, you're keeping a corpse breathing! Come home! All of you—come home, and no one else needs to fall!"

"The court is not dead," Aldous said, mild as morning. "It is waiting. There is a difference, and your masters have spent three hundred years making sure you never learned it."

She wept, that grey-blood woman, even as she raised her hand and the water-walls came crashing down.

After that, Wren remembered the battle in pieces, the way trauma keeps its own ledger:

—Iven's small body shoved behind a fallen column, Wren's arm around him, the amulet-stone burning in their fist like a coal.

—Soldiers wading ashore through the ruined water-walls, and Wren doing the one thing they were truly trained for: watching, reading, *tasting the danger in the air*. Seeing that the soldiers' torches guttered blue where the old magic touched them, seeing that the Choir's circle broke formation whenever they passed the altar steps, seeing the gap—there, between the second pillar and the flooded channel, a lane of clean stone no wider than a market stall.

—Pulling Iven up. Running that lane. Hearing boots behind them and the song reaching for their spine like hooks.

—And Aldous.

The steward stood on the altar steps with his arms spread wide and his feet planted like roots, and he sang back.

It was the first time Wren had heard the old tongue sung instead of spoken, and it went through them like sunlight through water. His voice was old and cracked and it did not matter; the court carried it, multiplied it, gave it back from every pillar until one old man sounded like two hundred wardens. The Choir faltered again. Soldiers stopped in the shallows, staring. And Aldous—Aldous laughed, high and wild and young, a boy's laugh three hundred years out of its grave—

—and the grey-blood woman struck him down.

Not with iron. With the song, focused, all twelve voices bent to a single point, and the sound hit the steward like a wave hits a sea-stack. He folded. He went down onto the altar steps slowly, almost courteously, the way old men sit down when they intend to stay a while.

Wren screamed. They didn't remember deciding to scream. They were running back toward the steps with Iven's hand torn from theirs and the whole court tilting around them, and the Choir turned toward them with those calm, grieving faces, and the woman said—not unkindly—*"Child, put down the stone. Come home."*

And Wren, crouched over the steward's body, felt the old man's hand close weakly around their wrist.

"Altar," Aldous whispered. Blood-dark lips. Frost-bright eyes finding theirs with the last of their impossible patience. "Moon is near crest. *Go.* Being the door means walking through, child. Not standing in it." His thumb pressed something into Wren's palm—the steward's ring, a band of grey metal worn smooth by centuries. "For the next keeper. Tell them... tell them the odds were fine."

The Choir reached the steps.

Wren ran.

They grabbed Iven's hand out of the dark—they would learn later that the boy had been holding position at the lane's mouth, refusing to flee without them, stubborn as his whole buried nation—and they ran for the far arch, the deep way, the road down to the covenant altar, with the song tearing at their backs and the blue light of the dying court flaring behind them like a sunset.

At the archway Wren looked back, once, because some debts must be witnessed.

Aldous had risen. Nobody knew how—an old man with death already in him, rising to his feet on the altar steps with his arms spread wide and the whole waking weight of Highmoor's court pouring through him—and the last thing Wren saw before the arch swallowed the view was the roof of the great hall beginning, deliberately, majestically, to come down.

Stone met stone. Darkness met water. The song was silenced under ten thousand tons of mountain.

Iven was crying without any sound at all, running blind beside Wren, and Wren held the steward's ring in one fist and the singing stone in the other and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *the moon crests soon. Everything ends tonight. Make it end right.*

Ahead, down the deep way, the dark opened—and at the bottom of it, faint as a heartbeat, the altar was already glowing.
