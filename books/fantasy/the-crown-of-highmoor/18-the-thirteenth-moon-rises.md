## 18. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The mountain had been singing to us for three days.

Not with sound, exactly—or not only. It sang in the soles of my feet when we climbed, in the ache of my healing ribs, in the way the oath-stone halves at my throat and Iven's wrist pulled toward each other like two hands remembering how to hold on. The song had grown louder as we descended past the shrine, down through corridors of living rock where the walls glimmered with veins of something that was not quite silver and not quite light. Now, in the last hour before moonrise, it filled the whole hollow dark like water filling a cup.

Iven walked ahead of me, small shoulders set, his bare feet finding purchase on stone that seemed to rise up to meet him. He had not spoken much since the steward fell. Neither had I. There are griefs too large for language, and there are fears the same shape, and we were carrying both, and silence was the only vessel that would hold them.

But I could hear him breathing. That was enough. For nine years I had listened to other people breathe—the king's wet morning rasp, Vess's careful measured inhale, the kitchen girls' sighs—and told myself I did not care whether any of them continued. Now I knew what a lie that had been. I cared about this child's breath the way I cared about my own heartbeat: without decision, without permission, absolutely.

*You could still turn back,* said the voice that had lived behind my sternum since Coldfern. *You could give him to Vess. You could go back to your little room above the kitchens, your little maps, your little life of being no one.*

I put my hand against the wall and kept walking.

* * *

The passage opened without warning.

One moment we were squeezing through a seam barely wide enough for my shoulders; the next, the mountain exhaled, and we stood at the edge of a cavern so vast the darkness above us had its own weather. Pale lichen grew in spirals across the floor, shedding a light like drowned moonlight, and in the center of that light rose the altar.

It was smaller than I had imagined. Smaller than anything deserved to be, after all this. A low table of black stone, no taller than a well's lip, its surface carved with lines that might have been words or might have been rivers or might have been both—script flowing in two directions at once, one current human, one current fae, braided together so tightly you could not say where one tongue ended and the other began. Around it, the air itself seemed thicker, sweeter, as though the cavern remembered ten thousand years of oaths and had gone drunk on them.

And above us—above everything—a shaft opened through the mountain's crown, a chimney of night sky, and in it hung the thirteenth moon.

It was nearly full. Not yet. The crest would come within the hour, and the stone halves at our throats knew it; they had begun to hum, softly, steadily, a note I felt in my teeth and behind my eyes. Iven lifted his amulet from his chest and watched it tremble against his palm like a moth trying to fly home.

"Highmoor," he said, in Old Highmoor, and then again in the common tongue, practicing, because he had been teaching me and I had been teaching him and somewhere in the last three days the two languages had begun to braid in my mouth the way they were braided on the altar. "We are here. We came."

"We came," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and he looked back at me with those old, old eyes in that young face, and he did not laugh at me, which was its own mercy.

We crossed the lichen-light together. When I laid my half of the oath-stone on the altar, the humming deepened into something almost like speech. When Iven added his, the two halves slid toward each other across the black stone—not touching, not yet, but close enough that the space between them filled with a sound like a held chord, like a choir drawing one long breath before the first note.

Like a Choir, said a cold voice in my memory, and I flinched.

"They are coming." Iven said it quietly, the way you state the weather. He had felt them too, ever since the ambush at the wayhouse—ever since Marta fell with her axe still swinging and Brannic turned back into the smoke and never came out. The Hollow Choir did not move like soldiers. They moved like weather fronts, like pressure changes, like the silence after a bell stops ringing. And they had been moving toward us all day.

"How long?"

He tilted his head, listening to something beneath the mountain's song. "Before the moon crests. Maybe as the moon crests." His jaw tightened. "They will want us to stop. They will not kill us before the moon. Killing us now would be—" he searched for the word, found it in my tongue first, "—wasteful."

"Then we have until the crest."

"Yes." He looked up at me, and in the lichen-light his face was very grave and very young. "Wren. You know what it costs."

I knew. The steward had made certain of that, in her last hour among the ruins, when she had taken my face in her ancient hands and spoken the terms as plainly as a merchant naming a price. The covenant could not be spoken by a king, who served a throne. It could not be spoken by a fae lord, who served a court. It could only be spoken by someone of both bloods who chose it freely—and choosing it meant binding themselves to the border forever. Not to either realm. To the seam between them. To the place where the worlds met and rubbed and sometimes caught fire.

To a border. Not a palace. Not a hidden court. Not a home in any shape I had ever known.

"You will lose them," Iven said. "The kitchens. The king. Even the—" he hesitated over the word, "—the forgetting. Your skill. If you are bound to the border, you cannot be invisible anymore. Everyone will see you. Forever."

I laughed. It came out wrong, ragged, half a sob dressed in laughter's clothing, and I sat down on the altar's edge because my legs had opinions of their own.

"Iven," I said, "do you know what I was, three weeks ago?"

He waited. He was good at waiting. He had spent his whole short life being hunted; patience was written into his bones.

"I was a man who tasted soup." I turned my hands over, looked at the palms—the calluses from rope and penknife, the faint burn scars from a hundred dishes tested dish by dish, poison by poison, building an immunity the way you build a wall, stone by patient stone. "Nine years. I learned every poison in seven kingdoms so that a fat man in a gold chair could eat eggs without worrying. I memorized smuggler roads through the Thornfells and told myself it was just habit, just caution, just a hobby for a bored taster. I kept my head down and my name small and I told myself—" my voice caught, and I let it catch, because there was no one left in the world whose opinion mattered enough to hide from, "—I told myself I didn't want anything more."

The chord between the stone halves swelled, gently, like a hand laid on a grieving shoulder.

"And then a mud-caked child fell through my door speaking dead words, and everything I ever buried stood up and started walking." I looked at him. "I don't lose a place at court, Iven. I lost that place the moment Vess wrote my name in his ledger. There are soldiers behind us and a Choir behind them and a king who thinks I'm a traitor. The only thing I can lose tonight is the chance to stop being afraid."

He considered this with the terrible seriousness of children, and then he nodded once, the way his people must have nodded to envoys and oath-keepers for ten thousand years, and something in my chest went quiet.

* * *

We prepared as the steward had taught us, in the fragments she had time to give.

We washed our hands in the spring that seeped from the eastern wall, water so cold it burned. We marked the altar's four corners with the things the covenant required: bread, for the human tongue; honeycomb, dark and crystalline, for the fae; salt, for the grief between them; and a lock of hair, twined together, mine and Iven's, for the blood that bridged. My hands shook doing it. They had never shaken over a poisoned dish, not once in nine years, and they shook now over bread and honey, and I decided that was right. Fear was the honest portion. Fear meant I understood.

The moon climbed.

Through the chimney of sky I watched it rise, white and vast and pitiless, and as it climbed the song of the stones changed key—climbed with it, climbing, climbing, a chord stretched tighter and tighter toward its crest. The lichen brightened. The carvings on the altar began to move, slowly, the twin currents of script flowing around each other like fish circling in a pool, and I understood suddenly, viscerally, why the covenant had needed a speaker of two tongues. The words were not written. They were *swimming*. You could not read them standing still. You could only catch them if you moved the way they moved—and I could move that way. Only me. Only the grey child. Only the taster who had spent nine years learning to hold two flavors in his mouth at once and tell the truth about both.

"Wren." Iven's hand found mine. His palm was damp and small and steady. "Listen."

I listened.

Beneath the mountain's song, beneath the chord of the stones, there was another sound now—distant, descending, echoing down the passages we had come by. Voices. Not shouting. Singing. The Hollow Choir sang as they came, and their song was beautiful, and that was the most horrifying thing about them: they had taken the music of the covenant and worn it inside-out, harmony turned to hunting-call, and every note of it said *stop, stop, yield, sleep, be contained, be safe, be nothing.*

My knees softened. My eyelids dragged. For one vertiginous moment I was back in the palace kitchens, warm and small and invisible, and someone kind was telling me I could rest now, I had worked so hard, I could finally stop—

"No," I said aloud, and bit the inside of my cheek until I tasted copper, and the vision tore. Iven was swaying beside me, eyes glazed, and I gripped his shoulder hard. "Iven. Look at me. It's a taste. It's a poison in the air, and I have tasted poisons all my life, and I am *still here.*"

His eyes focused. "Still here," he repeated, and planted his feet.

The singing grew closer. And beneath it now—footsteps. Many. Unhurried. They had all the time in the world, because they believed the world belonged to them.

I turned back to the altar. The moon was a hand's width from the crest of the chimney's rim. The stone halves sang on the black table, trembling, straining toward each other across a gap the width of my hand.

"The words," Iven whispered. "Say them with me. In both tongues. At the crest."

"I know the words." I did. Three days in a cave shrine with a mountain answering my voice had burned them into me deeper than any mother's lullaby. But knowing words and giving yourself to them forever are different animals, and the difference has a name, and the name is *vow*, and I had never in my life vowed anything except to keep my head down and stay alive.

The footsteps entered the great cavern's outer passages. The singing swelled. Lichen-light flickered as shadows moved along the far galleries—figures in grey, faces I recognized and did not, grey-blooded ones, ones like me who had chosen servitude, chosen containment, chosen the long slow death of being owned—

*I choose,* I thought, and the thought shook like a leaf in wind. *God help me, I choose.*

The moon touched the crest.

The chord between the stones broke open into a single ringing note, high and clear and older than crowns, and Iven's hand tightened in mine, and from the galleries the Hollow Choir began to run.

I opened my mouth, and spoke.
