## 19. Crown of Highmoor

The thirteenth moon rose like a wound in the sky.

Wren had seen it from the kitchen yard a hundred times in nine years—had counted its slow silver turning without ever knowing what it counted toward. Now it climbed above the shattered crown of the Thornfells, and it was not silver at all. It was the color of oath-stone, of mother-of-pearl, of the pale seam that ran through the black rock of the sunken court. It was the color of something waiting to be finished.

The altar hall lay open to the sky where the mountain's roof had fallen centuries ago, so the moonlight fell straight down into the water-flooded nave like a column of pale rope. Wren stood at the edge of it with Iven's hand small and cold in theirs, and behind them, at the far end of the ruined colonnade, came the sound they had been running from for three days: singing.

The Hollow Choir did not sing as people sang. Their voices came braided together, many throats making one note, and the note was wrong in a way that made the teeth ache and the old magic in the stones flinch. It rolled down through the flooded halls, echoing off pillars carved with faces worn smooth by three hundred years of dark water, and where it touched, the pockets of living light—the green-gold glimmer that had kept this court alive in exile—guttered like candles in wind.

"They are close," Iven said. He spoke Common now more easily than he had, but under stress the Old Highmoor came up in him like groundwater, and his words came out doubled, one tongue laid over the other like a shadow. "Wren. They are *close*."

"I know." Wren's voice was steadier than their hands. They looked down at him—nine years old, mud still dried in the creases of his ears from a road he should never have walked alone, carrying half a broken stone against his chest the way other children carried grief they couldn't name—and felt the thing rise in them that had been rising since the pantry loft, since the lullabies, since the word *covenant* first cracked open the sealed room of their life. Not courage exactly. Courage was for people who had something to lose and knew what it was. This was simpler and more terrible. This was *yes*.

"Tell me again," Wren said. "All of it. Every word. If I falter, you say it louder."

Iven nodded, solemn as any steward. He drew out the amulet—the half-stone on its leather cord, worn smooth by generations of hidden-court hands—and held it up, and Wren drew from their coat the other half: the shard their mother had died carrying over the border, wrapped in oilcloth, kept all these years in a smuggler's strongbox at Coldfern because no one could bear to throw away a dead woman's luggage.

When the halves came within an arm's length of each other, they began to sing.

It was not the Choir's song. It was thin and high and sweet, a single sustained tone like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and it harmonized—impossibly, perfectly—with the moonlight itself, so that the whole drowned hall seemed suddenly to be an instrument someone had finally learned to play. The water in the nave shivered into rings. The carved faces on the pillars seemed, in the corner of the eye, to open them.

And far off across the ruins, the Hollow Choir's braid of voices hitched, and quickened, and came on faster.

* * *

They waded into the nave together, because the altar stood at its center on a plinth of black stone, and the water was waist-deep and cold enough to make Wren gasp. Old magic pressed against their skin like a second current. Every step forward, the song of the stones grew louder; every step, Wren felt the two halves of themselves—the taster who had made himself forgettable dish by dish, and the grey child who had spent nine years pretending the lullabies meant nothing—pull less against each other and more *together*, like hands joining.

"You're afraid," Iven said. Not accusing. Wondering.

"Yes."

"My nurse said fear is just the body counting what matters." He looked up at Wren, moonlight caught in his eyes, and for a moment he was not a feral child on a kitchen step but the last heir of a murdered court, standing in his ancestors' house. "Count everything," he said. "Then speak anyway."

They reached the altar. The plinth was waist-high, its surface carved with two interlocking rings—one patterned with wheat and river and hearth-smoke, the human world; the other with thorn and star and moth-wing, the fae—and where the rings overlapped, the carving deepened into a groove shaped exactly like the broken stone. Two halves. One whole.

Wren set the halves into the groove.

The click when they joined was very small. The sound that followed was not. The rejoined stone rang like a bell struck underwater, and the note went out through the flooded halls, through the roots of the mountain, through whatever thin membrane still separated this world from the other one, and everywhere it passed, the green-gold light came back—not guttering now but blazing, running along the carvings like fire along a fuse, until the whole sunken court stood lit as it must have stood on the night it fell.

And the Choir stopped singing.

The silence was worse. In it, Wren could hear them coming—many feet in the shallows, splashing, unhurried now, certain. Moonlight spilled through the broken roof, and in it Wren saw the first of them round the colonnade: tall figures in grey, faces veiled, and beneath the veils—

Beneath the veils, when the lead singer pulled hers back, was a face like anyone's. A tired woman's face, forty or fifty years old, with laugh-lines gone slack and eyes that had cried themselves past crying. A border face. A grey-blooded face. Behind her, a dozen more, pulling back their veils one by one: men and women of both bloods, hollowed out, their grief worn on the outside like armor worn too long.

"We were told," the woman said, and her voice alone was almost human, almost kind. "We were told the covenant was a cage. That breaking it would free us. We were told—" Her voice frayed. She gripped her veil like a rosary. "We were told there was nothing left to save. Give us the boy, oath-keeper. Let us finish what we were promised would end our hurting."

"No," Wren said.

The word came out in both tongues at once—*no* and *nai*, Common and Old Highmoor laid over each other like two hands clasped—and the stone on the altar rang in answer, and every face in the Choir flinched as if struck.

Because that was the thing Wren finally understood, standing waist-deep in the water of a drowned court with the thirteenth moon directly overhead: the covenant had never been a wall between realms. It had been a bridge, and bridges have two ends, and the people who broke it had only ever been able to break it *in the middle* because no one of one blood alone could speak it whole. It needed both tongues in one mouth. It needed a door that chose to open.

It needed someone forgettable. Someone who had spent a lifetime learning to stand between.

"Iven," Wren said quietly. "Now."

* * *

The heir of Highmoor began to recite the covenant in the language of the fae, high and clear, a child's voice carrying words older than crowns, and each line he spoke, Wren answered in Common—the trade-tongue, the kitchen-tongue, the tongue of bread and ledgers and the king's breakfast tasted dish by dish—and where the two languages met, the air itself began to weave.

That was the only word Wren's mind could hold onto afterward: *weaving*. The moonlight thickened into threads. The water rose in fine bright strands. The green-gold light of the old court and the grey-silver light of the human moon twisted together around the altar, around the stone, around Wren's own body, and Wren spoke and spoke, and understood, line by line, what they were saying—that the harvest would feed the thorn-people and the star-moths would pollinate the wheat; that the rivers would run both ways; that neither realm would be shut nor swallowed, but interlaced, each holding the other the way a knot holds two ropes without making either one disappear.

The Choir came on. They were in the water now, close enough that Wren could see the cracks in the lead woman's composure, the way her hands shook. The old magic rose around the altar like a tide, and Wren kept speaking, voice cracking on the long lines, and Iven's voice never wavered once, and the threads of light spun faster—

—and the Choir reached the edge of the woven light, and stepped into it, and unraveled.

Not destroyed. *Unraveled.* The braid of their stolen power came apart thread by thread, and what was underneath was only people. The lead woman sank to her knees in the shallows, veils falling away, and began to weep—not the dry tearing weep of the hollowed but the deep, ugly, human weeping of someone setting down a weight carried thirty years. All down the nave they knelt, one after another, grey cloaks going dark with water, and the wrongness drained out of them and left only grief, and grief, Wren knew—had always known—was not the opposite of love. Grief was love with nowhere left to go.

The covenant gave it somewhere to go. The threads of light passed through the kneeling Choir like warmth through a cold room, and here and there a face lifted, bewildered, looking at their own bare hands as if seeing them for the first time in decades.

"It doesn't hurt," the lead woman whispered. "Oh—it doesn't—it *doesn't hurt*—"

Wren spoke the last line.

They spoke it in both tongues at once, fully, for the first time—no translation lag, no shadow-language underneath, but one voice carrying one meaning in two shapes simultaneously, and the sensation was like a bone setting, like a held breath released after nine years. The rejoined stone blazed white. The two carved rings on the altar surface filled with light and turned—slowly, majestically—until they overlapped completely, and the overlap spread outward through the water, through the stone, through the roots of the mountain, through the membrane between worlds that had been torn and scarred for three hundred years—

—and the realms reweave.

There was no thunder. There was a sound like a great loom completing its final pass, like ten thousand small bells heard from very far away, like a mother's lullaby remembered all at once. Above the broken roof, the thirteenth moon flared and settled into an ordinary beautiful silver. And through the archways of the sunken court, where for centuries there had been only dark water and dead ends, Wren saw—clearly, unmistakably—the other side. Green hills under a different starscape. Lantern-light. Figures emerging, hesitant, from doorways that had been sealed since before the burning of the Treaty of Ash, staring through at the human world with the same stunned wonder with which the human world stared back.

The bridge stood. Both ends anchored. Nothing shut, nothing swallowed.

* * *

Chancellor Vess arrived eleven minutes too late.

Later, Wren would learn she had ridden hard from the pass with twenty soldiers and the king's seal in an iron case, and that she had heard the last line of the covenant from the colonnade, and that she had come into the nave at a walk because running would have admitted what she already knew. She crossed the flooded floor with her boots sending rings across the water, and the soldiers behind her halted at the threshold as if the air there were glass.

She stopped before the altar. Before Wren, soaked to the ribs, and Iven, glowing faintly at the edges like paper held before a lamp, and the stone between them, quiet now, whole, humming like a hive at rest.

"Wren Quintana," Vess said. Her voice was level. It was always level; that had been the horror of her from the beginning. "By order of the crown, you are—"

"The crown has no order here," said a voice from the archway.

The steward came slowly through the water—ancient, bent, his robes trailing weed and centuries—and beside him walked others: fae lords and grey-blooded envoys, the survivors of the hidden court, stepping through the rewoven threshold into the human realm for the first time in three hundred years. The steward did not look at Vess. He looked at the altar, at the whole stone, and something in his ancient face let go, the way a fist lets go.

"The old magic acknowledges no crown," he said, and his voice was soft and it carried to every corner of the drowned hall regardless. "It never did. That was the first lie of the Treaty of Ash—that sovereignty could be seized instead of sworn. The covenant knows one office only." He turned, then, and his eyes found Wren, and bowed. "The oath-keeper."

Vess opened the iron case. Held up the king's seal. And the seal—Wren saw it clearly, everyone saw it—dimmed in her hands like a coal going out, wax-grey, inert, a stamp with nothing left to press. Because the authority it represented had been built on the broken treaty, and the treaty was broken no longer, and the old magic had simply, gently, set it aside.

For a moment—only a moment—Chancellor Vess's mask slipped, and Wren saw underneath: not malice. Fear. A woman who had spent her whole life believing the world was a ledger that must balance by any means, standing in a flooded hall watching the ledger burn. "You've doomed us," she whispered. "You don't know what you've—"

"I know exactly what I've done," Wren said. "I've opened a door. Doors can be watched. Bridges can be tended." They looked at her, and found—to their own surprise—not hatred, but the weary clarity of someone who had finally stopped being afraid. "Go home, Chancellor. Tell the king the truth. All of it. He'll want to hear it from you while he still believes you're telling it."

Vess looked at the seal in her hand for a long time. Then she closed the empty iron case, turned, and walked out through the water, and the soldiers parted to let her pass, and none of them looked back.

* * *

Afterward there was lantern-light on the water and voices in two languages learning each other's music, and Iven asleep against Wren's shoulder with the whole stone cradled in his arms like a sleeping animal, and the thirteenth moon riding high and ordinary and new.

But Wren stood apart a while at the nave's edge, looking east, where beyond the mountains the human realm lay dark under its own stars—the palace, the kitchens, the king's breakfast table where a place had been set for a taster who would never come. Nine years of careful invisibility, ended in one spoken hour. No throne waited for them. No anonymity either. Both doors had closed, and something better stood open between them.

The steward came and stood beside them, following their gaze.

"The border will need keeping," he said. "Not guarding. Keeping. There is a difference, and it is the whole difference."

"I know," Wren said. And found they were smiling. "I know a road."

Far below, in the foothills where the smuggler paths began, a light answered—a single lamp, as if the land itself had already chosen the spot—and Iven stirred in his sleep and murmured a word in Old Highmoor that Wren, for the first time, understood completely:

*Home.*
