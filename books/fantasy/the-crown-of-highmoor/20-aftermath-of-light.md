## 20. Aftermath of Light

The light came first.

I had thought the sunken court would end in darkness—had braced myself for it the way you brace for a blow, eyes shut, breath held. But when Wren spoke the covenant in two tongues at once, the darkness did not flee. It *opened*. Like a hand unclenching after three hundred years.

I stood on the covenant altar with Wren's blood-warm fingers laced through mine, and I watched the light pour upward through the drowned halls of Highmoor like water running backward, like rain remembering it was once a river. It moved through stone that had been dead since before my grandmother's grandmother sang her first song, and where it touched, the stone woke. Veins of silver kindled in the walls. The great lanterns of the court—the ones carved as sleeping moths, wings folded for centuries—unfurled one by one, each releasing a slow exhale of gold. Somewhere far above us, through leagues of mountain, I heard the Thornfells groan and settle, an old animal turning over in its sleep.

And then the doors opened.

All of them. Every sealed gate, every warded archway, every hidden stair my people had cut into the roots of the world when the breaking came. The survivors emerged the way seeds emerge—not all at once, not easily, but with a patience that made my chest ache to watch.

They came up from the seed-vaults beneath the eastern terraces, blinking, carrying children who had been born underground and had never seen anything brighter than moth-lanterns. They came down from the high galleries where the last singers had kept the old songs alive by humming them into stone so the walls would remember even if no throat did. They came from the deep archive where my aunt Sereth had spent nine years copying every surviving record onto vellum made of birch-bark, because paper rots but bark remembers. They came carrying what remained: three hundred souls out of a court that had held ten thousand. A library half-saved. A garden of nightblooming silverbells grown pale and leggy in the dark, blooming anyway, because that is what Highmoor flowers do.

I was eleven years old, and I was their king.

I want to be honest about what that felt like, because the ballads will get it wrong. The ballads will say I stood tall on the altar with moonlight in my hair. What actually happened is that my legs gave out, and Wren caught me under the arms before I hit the stones, and I cried into their shoulder—loudly, uglily, the way a child cries—in front of three hundred people who needed me to be something other than a child. And Wren held me up, literally held me upright, and said nothing at all, which was the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me.

* * *

The first days were a strange arithmetic of grief and wonder.

The steward's body we laid in state on a bier of woven rowan, in the great hall where he had served four generations of my line. His name had been Ondrell, though almost no one living remembered it; he had been "the steward" so long that the word had eaten the man. Wren stood vigil beside me the whole first night, and near dawn they told me something I have never repeated and never will—that Ondrell had tested them not with riddles but with hunger, offering them a crown-shaped lie and watching to see if they would reach for it. "He wanted to know if I'd choose," Wren said. "Not whether I'd choose right. Just whether I'd choose at all." Then they fell silent, and the moths above burned low and gold, and I understood that my steward had died the way he had lived: buying time for someone else's choice.

Sereth found me on the second day, in the archive, running my hands along the shelves as if I could read history through my fingertips.

"You'll need a regency council," she said. She was very old and very dry, with the particular patience of someone who has catalogued ten thousand disasters and expects an eleventh. "You cannot sign a treaty, let alone rule, until you've seen at least fifteen winters."

"I know."

"Do you? You've been saying 'I know' since you were six, and knowing has never once stopped you from doing the thing anyway." She pulled a scroll from a shelf without looking, the way she always did, and unrolled it across the table between us. It was a list. Names, ranks, survivals. Three hundred lines, some of them crossed out in ink that had gone brown with age. "So. Who stands beside you?"

"Everyone," I said. "A council. All of them."

"That is not how courts work, little king."

"It's how this one works now." I put my finger on the empty space below the last name. "That's the point of what Wren did. The realms aren't separate anymore. Neither are we. No more hidden chambers, no more one person holding everything alone until they die of it. That's what broke us the first time—one oath, one keeper, one door. If there's only one door, someone will always come to burn it down."

Sereth looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something she had not done in my entire life: she smiled, briefly, like a crack of light under a door. "Your mother talked exactly like this," she said. "It got her killed. But it was good talk." She dipped her pen. "Then we begin with the seat beside yours. Who?"

There was only ever going to be one answer. But I made myself sit with it for a day anyway, because Wren had taught me—without meaning to, just by being what they were—that choices weigh more when you carry them a while before setting them down.

* * *

I found Wren on the fifth morning, sitting alone at the edge of the flooded terrace where the court's great reflecting pool had become part of the mountain's groundwater. The water was clear as glass now, and full of reflected moths, and Wren was doing the thing they did when the world became too much: making themselves small, hands wrapped around a cup of nettle tea, face arranged into pleasant nothing. Nine years of practice does not unlearn itself in five days. I suspected it might take nine years more.

I sat down beside them. For a while we both watched the water.

"The survivors keep looking at you," I said.

"I noticed."

"They're not sure what to make of you. Half of them think you're a hero. Half think you're a spy for the human crown who happened to trip into doing the right thing."

"And the other half?"

"There is no other half. Courts run on halves." I hugged my knees. Below us, in the pool, a silverbell petal drifted past our reflections. "Wren. I have to ask you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you answer, because if you interrupt me I'll lose my nerve, and I'm the king, and kings shouldn't lose their nerve in front of their stewards."

Wren went very still. "Stewards," they repeated carefully.

"A regent rules when the king can't. A steward *keeps*—the household, the oaths, the daily work of a realm. It's the oldest office in Highmoor. Older than crowns. Ondrell held it for four reigns." I turned to look at them properly. Their face was still arranged into that careful blankness, but their hands had tightened around the cup until the knuckles went pale, and I knew—I knew the way I knew the taste of rain—that underneath the blankness they were already refusing. Already composing the speech about how they weren't fae enough, weren't noble enough, belonged on the border, belonged nowhere, belonged in a kitchen tasting soup for a king who had declared them a traitor five days ago.

So I said it fast, before the refusal could finish dressing itself:

"I'm eleven years old. I watched my whole court die. I don't know how to rule, and everyone who could teach me is either dead or older than the mountains, and the human kingdom next door thinks my existence is a casus belli. I cannot do this alone. Not won't—*can't*. And you—" My voice cracked, and I hated it, and I kept going anyway, because Wren had taught me that too. "You're the only person alive who speaks both tongues. You're the only person alive who has lived on both sides of the border and survived both. You carried the oath when no one else could. And you keep choosing us. Every time there's a choice, you choose us, and then you apologize for it afterward, and I am formally requesting, as your king, that you stop apologizing and start staying."

The silence stretched. A moth-lantern overhead dimmed and brightened, dimmed and brightened, breathing its slow gold.

"Wren," I said, quieter. "Will you stand as steward beside me?"

Their cup rattled against the stone as they set it down. When they finally spoke, their voice was rough.

"Iven. I spent nine years learning to be invisible. It kept me alive. And everything in me is screaming that if I stand in front of three hundred people and take an office—if I let myself be *seen*—then the next knife finds me, and worse, it finds *you*, because I've made myself something worth killing." They laughed, short and joyless. "I don't know how to be visible. I genuinely don't know if I can learn."

"Good," I said.

They stared at me. "*Good?*"

"If you said yes easily, I'd worry. Stewardship isn't a thing you should be able to say yes to easily. Ondrell used to say the office fits like a too-small coat—you wear it anyway, and it keeps you honest." I reached over and took their hand, the way they'd taken mine on the altar, the way they'd caught me when my legs gave out. Their hand was scarred in a dozen places from a decade of dosing themselves with poison grain by grain. "You learned to taste poison by taking a little at a time. Learn this the same way. One ceremony. One season. If it kills you, I'll say something nice at the funeral."

That startled a real laugh out of them—short, surprised, the first I'd heard from them since the battle. "You're supposed to be solemn. Kings are supposed to be solemn."

"I'm eleven. Ask me again when I'm fifteen." I squeezed their hand. "Well? One season?"

Wren looked out over the pool, at the drowned court waking in the reflection, at the moths burning steady now instead of breathing. I watched them do it—the thing I would see them do a thousand times in the years after, the small invisible war behind their eyes between the person who survived by disappearing and the person who had spoken a broken world back together. I waited. I had gotten good at waiting. Waiting was most of what my childhood had been.

"One season," Wren said at last. "And I keep my house on the border. And Iven—" their voice went fierce, and for a moment they sounded less like a servant and more like exactly what they were, the oath-keeper, the grey child, the door that had chosen to open— "if I ever start becoming what Vess is, you tell me. Promise me. Stewardship is a short walk from control, and I've seen where that road ends."

"I promise," I said. And the moths overhead flared bright, all of them at once, as if the court itself had been listening and approved.

* * *

The king of Aurelan came to the mountains himself, which surprised everyone, including, I suspect, the king of Aurelan.

He arrived with a modest retinue—twenty soldiers, no banners—and Chancellor Vess was notably absent, having been taken into custody two weeks prior when it emerged that the Treaty of Ash, the Hollow Choir, and the poisoning of her own sovereign traced back to her ledger-lined ambitions. They said she did not weep when they arrested her. They said she asked, very calmly, whether the archives would remain properly catalogued in her absence. Some people are hollow all the way down, and no covenant can reforge them.

The king walked into the sunken court on his own feet, past the rowan bier where Ondrell lay, past the archive shelves and the pale silverbells, with his hat in his hands like a schoolboy called before a magistrate. Which, in fairness, he was. He stopped before the dais where I sat—small, eleven years old, wearing my mother's circlet because it was the only one that fit—and he bowed. Deeply. Longer than protocol required.

"Your Majesty," he said. "I come to ask forgiveness for my kingdom's crimes, and my own. We broke what should never have been broken. We hunted children." His voice caught on that. I later learned that he had a son of his own, and that the thought of Iven fleeing through the snow with the Choir behind him had been eating him alive since Wren's testimony reached the capital. "I cannot undo it. I can only ask what reparation Your Majesty will accept."

I had prepared a speech. Sereth had helped me draft it, all formal phrases and historical gravity. I abandoned it entirely.

"You'll return the lands along the Thornfell foothills that your grandfather seized," I said. "You'll open the border passes year-round. You'll fund a school in Coldfern where children of both realms learn both tongues, and neither tongue will be called shameful in it." I paused. "And you'll pardon Wren Quintana. Fully. Publicly. With honors."

At the sound of their name, Wren—who stood at my shoulder, newly sworn, still wearing the too-small coat of their office like they expected it to be snatched away—went rigid.

The king turned to them. And here is the thing I will remember longest about that day: he did not offer a title. He did not offer land or gold or a place at court. He offered the things Wren had been starved of for nine years, and he seemed to know it.

"Wren Quintana," he said. "I owe you my life twice over—once from the poison, once from my own blindness. Name any honor in my gift, and it is yours. A knighthood. An estate. A seat on my council. Anything."

I felt Wren gather themselves. I had learned by then to read the signs: the slight settling of the shoulders, the flattening of the face into pleasant nothing, the invisible person preparing to decline invisibly.

But then they didn't. Something shifted. Maybe it was the moths, maybe the memory of Ondrell, maybe just exhaustion—but instead of the smooth refusal, what came out was honest and a little ragged:

"Thank you. Truly. But I spent nine years being owned by a crown, and I've just finished choosing a different life. If I took your honors, I'd be choosing yours instead, and I'm done letting thrones decide where I belong." They glanced at me, and something warm passed between us, quick as a struck match. "What I'll ask instead is smaller. Send teachers to the border school. Feed the grey-blooded children who are hiding in your cities the way I hid. And when people speak of what happened here—don't erase the hard parts. Don't make it a pretty story. Let it stay true, even the ugly parts. Especially those."

The king was quiet a long moment. "Those are strange honors to refuse and stranger ones to ask."

"I'm told I'm a strange subject."

"You're not my subject at all," the king said, and there was no anger in it—only a kind of wondering grief, like a man discovering a door in a wall he'd walked past his whole life. "That's rather the point, isn't it?" He bowed again, lower than before. "Then I'll give you what I can: the truth, kept true. It's less than you deserve and more than I'm practiced at."

After he left, Wren stood at the terrace edge for a long time, watching his retinue dwindle down the mountain road. I came to stand beside them.

"You refused a knighthood," I said.

"Mm."

"Sereth is going to write that down in the annals. 'Declined all honors; requested truth.' Future students are going to think you were insufferably noble."

"I was insufferably terrified," Wren said. "It looks similar from the outside." They were quiet a moment. Then, softer: "He offered me everything I used to want. Did you feel that? For about three heartbeats, I wanted to say yes. To go back to the palace, to the warm kitchens, to being safe and fed and *known*—" They stopped. Shook their head. "But it wouldn't have been known. It would have been *owned*, dressed up in silk. I know the difference now. It cost me everything to learn the difference."

Below us, in the waking court, the evening bells rang—real bells, cast new from the melted fragments of the old, rung by survivors who were learning, tentatively, to make noise again after three hundred years of silence. The sound rolled up through the terraces and out through the reopened gates, into the foothills, toward the border, toward a certain plot of land where, I happen to know, a certain steward had already begun sketching the foundations of a small house in the margins of a treaty document, during meetings, when they thought no one was looking.

I let them stand there a while longer. Then I tugged their sleeve, because kings may be solemn but eleven-year-olds needn't be.

"Come on," I said. "Sereth wants us both in the archive. There's a matter she says can't wait."

"What matter?"

"She wouldn't say. Only that a messenger came up from Coldfern this morning—smuggler's seal, mud on his boots—and that he brought something for you. She said—" I frowned, trying to recall her exact words, which had been unusually careful for Sereth. "She said, 'Tell the steward it seems the road home has started sending guests.'"

Wren went still beside me. And I saw it cross their face—not fear, not quite hope, but that border-country weather where the two fronts meet and neither wins.

"Guests," they repeated quietly.

We went down together, through the waking halls, past the moths burning gold, as the bells rang on and on into the darkening mountainside.
