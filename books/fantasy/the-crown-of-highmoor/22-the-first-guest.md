## 22. The First Guest

A year, and the border still smelled of rain.

I had learned to read it the way I had once read the king's breakfast—by scent, by the smallest tremor in the air. On the old side of the world, rain came down the Thornfells smelling of stone and pine and the cold iron of the crown's roads. On the new side—the side that had been hidden for three hundred years and was hidden no longer—it arrived carrying green things I still could not name: a sweetness like honey cut with frost, the ghost of flowers that bloomed only where no one watched. When both smells braided together over my roof, I knew the veil between the realms was breathing easy. Open. Interwoven. The word the steward had used at the end, when the mountain was singing through my own mouth.

*Interwoven.* Not apart. Never again apart.

I stood in the doorway of the wayhouse with a bowl of porridge cooling in my hands and let the morning come in.

The house sat exactly where the smuggler road crested the last hill before the Thornfell pass—a place I had chosen because I knew every stone of it, because I had once mapped these roads in secret ink on the backs of laundry lists, hiding them from the crown that owned my life. The crown did not own my life anymore. That was the strangest sentence I knew how to think, even now, even after a year of practicing it. *The crown does not own me.* Some mornings I woke reaching for the palace kitchens, for the weight of the tasting spoon, for the careful grayness of being nobody—and then I heard the kettle on my own hearth, and the river talking to itself below the window, and I remembered.

I had built the wayhouse myself, mostly. Smugglers helped—they came up from Coldfern with timber and opinions, and Marta of the wayhouse there, who had known my mother, stood at my shoulder the day we raised the first beam and said nothing at all, which from Marta was a eulogy and a blessing both. Iven sent stones. Real stones, carried down out of the mountains by children of the hidden court who wanted, they said, to put their hands on something that would last. They were pale, those stones, shot through with veins that caught the light wrong, the way fae-worked things do. I set them into the hearth. On cold nights they glowed faintly, like coals that remembered being stars.

The door was wide. That had been the whole design, the whole argument I'd had with myself over a winter of drafting: doors in the old world were narrow, guarded, tasted-before-opened. This door would be wide enough for two to enter shoulder to shoulder, human and fae, grey or gold or mud-caked, without anyone having to go first.

And the table. The table was long enough for twelve, though most days I ate alone at the near end, and that was fine—that was *chosen*, which made all the difference between solitude and exile. Two bowls always sat ready in the cupboard, stacked, waiting. One for me. One for whoever came.

Mostly, travelers came. Traders from the lowlands hauling salt toward the mountain markets, their carts now rolling straight through passes that had been death to cross before the covenant turned the old hostility into mere weather. Fae folk too, shy at first, then less so—the tall ones with antlered shadows, the small ones who rode foxes and paid in songs. They came to my table and ate my cooking and discovered, as I had discovered long ago in another life, that immunity is not the absence of poison but the mastery of it. Nothing served under my roof could harm anyone. I tested everything still, out of habit, out of craft—but now the testing was hospitality instead of servitude. I dosed the wine against fae vertigo. I steeped the mountain herbs that kept human sleep from turning prophetic. Little kindnesses, invisible ones. Some skills you keep.

Iven visited when he could, which was never often enough, because stewarding a court of survivors rebuilding itself inside a waking mountain turned out to be a great deal of work, and he was thirteen, and he complained about it with the magnificent self-pity of a boy who had once been a prince and was now, as he put it, "a clerk with a crown." He arrived usually at dusk, muddy, ravenous, trailing other children behind him like goslings. He always went straight to the cupboard, took down the second bowl himself, and set it across from mine.

"That one's yours," I told him, the first time. "It waits for guests."

"It waits for *me*," he said, with the absolute certainty of royalty, and sat down, and that was true too. Both things were true. I was learning to live with both things being true.

But today there was no Iven. Today was the anniversary—the thirteenth moon risen full again, one full turn since I had stood on the covenant altar with blood in my mouth and two languages tangled on my tongue, and spoken. Since the Choir had unraveled into ordinary grieving men and women who wept in the ruins like children coming home from a war no one had declared. Since Vess's grand claim had dissolved like sugar in rain, because the old magic looked at his seal and his soldiers and his carefully hoarded ledgers and acknowledged none of it. *No crown,* the mountain had said, in the voice the mountain says things. *Only the oath-keeper.*

Since I had walked out of the sunken court with a wound in my side and a future I had not planned for, because I had never dared plan for one.

One year. And this morning the air smelled of both rains at once, and the porridge was good—I had found wild honey in the high meadow two days past, and hazelnuts, and I had cooked the way my mother must have cooked once, before borders, before burning, before she died on a smuggler's road carrying half a broken oath-stone in her coat—and I heard footsteps on the gravel before I saw anyone.

Small footsteps. Hesitant. The particular rhythm of someone who has stopped twice already and started again.

I set the bowl down and went to the door.

She stood at the edge of the yard where the gravel met the grass, maybe thirty paces off, and I knew what she was before I knew anything else about her, because I had been her. Grey-blooded children have a look—not the fae glow, not the human plainness, but something between, a face like a room lit from two windows at once, so that neither light quite claims it. She was perhaps ten. Her dress was lowland wool, patched, too warm for the season; her hair was dark and cut badly, the cut of someone who had done it herself with a knife, or had it done in anger. Mud to her knees. She had walked a long way. You could see the walk in her.

And she was speaking.

Not to me. To herself, or to the yard, or to whatever god watches children make brave decisions—she was whispering under her breath, over and over, words in Old Highmoor, the language of the realm that had been broken and buried beneath the Thornfells for three hundred years. The language my mother sang lullabies in, half-swallowed, glancing over her shoulder. The language I had not spoken aloud until a cave shrine forced it out of me with a wound and a moon and no other choice left in the world.

I knew the words she was saying. That was the thing that stopped my heart and restarted it sideways. She was reciting the covenant—or the child's version of it, the first eight lines, the part every Highmoor child learned before they learned their own name. *Stone remembers. River remembers. Root and star remember. What was woven, let it be woven again.*

Someone had taught her. Someone had whispered it to her in a cellar or a kitchen or a bed at night, the way my mother had whispered it to me, and then someone else had taught her to be ashamed of it. I could see the shame on her like a second, thinner skin. She held her hands the way I used to hold mine in the palace kitchens—tucked, closed, taking up as little of the world as possible. Forgettable. Practicing forgettable. And failing, because the words kept leaking out of her in the old tongue, and the old tongue will not be quiet, not anymore, not since the covenant wove the realms back together and every buried syllable woke up and started looking for its speakers.

She saw me in the doorway and flinched. Stopped mid-word. Her mouth snapped shut around the rest of the line like a trap.

For a moment neither of us moved. Wind came down off the Thornfells carrying that double smell, stone-rain and honey-frost, and stirred the loose hair at her temple, and I thought: *she has been told this language is shameful. She has been told she is shameful. She has walked—who knows how far?—to the one place the stories say is safe, and now she is here, and she is afraid the stories are wrong, because everyone she has ever trusted told her stories were wrong.*

I had been afraid of exactly that, once, standing at a servants' door in a storm with a mud-caked boy in front of me, hearing a dead language fall out of his mouth like water.

"Come in," I said. In Old Highmoor.

Her whole body changed. It was like watching a knot untie itself—shoulders dropping, chin lifting, hands unclenching finger by finger. Her eyes went wide and wet and furious all at once, the way children's eyes do when the relief is bigger than they can carry politely.

"You *spoke* it," she said. Still in the old tongue, rushing now, tumbling. "You spoke it right, you said it right, the *rivers*—" She stopped herself, swallowed, tried again in the trade-tongue, clumsy: "You—you speak it. Properly."

"I speak it properly," I agreed, and stepped aside, and held the door wide. Wide enough for two. "Come in out of the wind. There's porridge."

She didn't move yet. Children who have been taught shame learn caution the way other children learn games, thoroughly and forever. "Are you the oath-keeper?" she asked. "The stories say the oath-keeper keeps a house on the border. The stories say—" and here her voice dropped, went sly and wondering, "—the stories say the food can't hurt you there. Any of you. Whatever you are."

"The stories are right," I said. "I'm the taster. Nothing served under this roof can harm anyone. It's rather the point of me."

That almost got a smile. Almost. She looked past me into the house—at the long table, at the hearthstones glowing their faint remembered-star glow, at the second bowl sitting upside down in the cupboard behind me, waiting—and I watched her do the arithmetic children do, the terrible quick sum of *is this real, can I trust it, what will it cost.*

"It's free," I said quietly, in the old tongue, because some things should be said in the language they were nearly lost in. "The bowl costs nothing. The seat costs nothing. You cost nothing. Whoever told you otherwise was wrong, and I'm sorry they had the teaching of you."

Her lip trembled. She bit it hard, mastered it, the way I had mastered a hundred flinches in a palace kitchen, and I wanted to gather up every person who had ever taught this child to bite down and feed them a very long lesson in consequences. But that was the old Wren, the invisible Wren, who had survived by swallowing. The Wren of the wayhouse had learned better uses for a voice.

So I said, gently, in both tongues at once—because I could, because the mountain had given me that, because the covenant lived in my mouth now the way the tasting craft once did: "*Welcome. Come and eat.*"

And she came.

She crossed the yard slowly, mud sucking at her shoes, and paused once at the threshold—just at the threshold, one foot on the stone, looking up at me with an expression I recognized from the inside, the expression of a person asking permission to exist somewhere. I nodded. That was all it took. She stepped through.

Inside, she hovered while I ladled the porridge—wild honey, hazelnuts, a thread of cream from the farmer down the valley—and I let her hover, because hovering is its own kind of settling. She touched the back of a chair like it might be hot. She looked at the hearthstones and gasped, very softly, and I said, "They were a gift. From the court beneath. You'll see it someday, if you want. It's still there. It's waking up." She looked at me like I'd told her the sky was edible.

Then I set the bowl in front of her, at the place across from mine, the place that waited, and sat down opposite with my own, and for a while there was only the sound of eating and the river below the window and the wind doing its slow work on the eaves.

She ate the way Iven had eaten that first night a year ago—fast, guarding the bowl, then slower, then actually tasting it, then looking up at me with dawning outrage: "This is the best thing I've ever eaten."

"I know," I said. "Everyone says that. It's the honey. Or possibly the freedom."

That got the smile, whole this time, crooked and astonished, and something in my chest that had been clenched for—years, decades, a lifetime—loosened another degree.

Her name was Sef. She gave it reluctantly, halfway through the bowl, watching my face for the flinch. Serafin, she said, but her mother had called her Sef, and her mother had taught her the eight lines of the covenant in secret, and her mother was dead now—dead the way grey-blooded mothers died, quietly, inconveniently, of hard work and harder silence—and the aunt she'd been left with had burned the lullaby out of the house and called the old tongue *snake-speech* and called Sef, more than once, *the reason for everything wrong.* So Sef had run. Walked eleven days, following stories passed hand to hand among people who had learned, in one miraculous year, that they were allowed to pass them.

"There are others," she said, staring into her bowl. "Grey ones. Back home. We don't say it. But we know each other. You can tell." She glanced up. "Can't you?"

"I could always tell," I said. "I just spent nine years pretending I couldn't. Don't do that. It's a waste of a life."

She nodded slowly, filing it away, and I understood that I had just become one of her stories—the oath-keeper at the border house, the one who speaks snake-speech properly, the one whose door is wide. Stories are how the cycle breaks, I thought. Not proclamations. Not treaties. A child walks eleven days on a story, and eats a bowl of porridge, and carries the story home changed.

Afterward she washed the bowls without being asked—lowland manners, drilled deep—and then stood in the middle of the room, drying her hands, visibly working up to something. I let her. I had learned patience from a feral boy in a pantry loft, piecing a language together one repeated word at a time. *Covenant. Covenant. Covenant.* Everything important arrives eventually, if you leave the door open.

"My aunt said," Sef began, stopped, began again. "My aunt said there wasn't any place for—for what I am. That we were mistakes. Leftovers." Her jaw set. "Is that true?"

I looked at her—this fierce, mud-legged, badly-haired scrap of both worlds, standing in a house built of smuggler timber and fae stones on the seam between realms, in the light of a morning that smelled of two rains at once—and I thought about the girl I had been, the border-born taster who had made invisibility into an art because belonging seemed like a country that did not issue passports to people like us. Who had needed a child covered in river mud to show me that the door had been open the whole time.

"No," I said. "It isn't true. It was never true. Come here—I'll teach you the rest of the covenant. All twelve lines. And after that, if you're staying, there's work: bread to bake, travelers to feed, a garden that grows on both sides of the fence whether I ask it to or not. And if you're not staying, you'll leave fed, and you'll leave knowing the way back."

She blinked. "There's a *back*?"

"There's everywhere," I said. "That's rather what the covenant means."

Outside, far up the pass, something bright flickered along the ridgeline—fox-riders, probably, or Iven's scouts, or just morning light moving through the interwoven air where the two worlds breathed together. Somewhere beyond the mountains, a hidden court was waking, and a boy with a crown was complaining about ledgers, and a king in a humbled palace was learning to rule a realm that no longer had anywhere to hide its shame. All of that was real. All of it mattered.

But none of it mattered as much as this: a child at my table, licking honey off her thumb, unashamed.

I got up and took a third bowl down from the shelf, and set it beside the other two, where it would wait—stacked and patient and sure of itself—for whoever came next.

The door stayed wide.
