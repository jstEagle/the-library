## 1. The Gatekeeper's Winter

The seal sang at dawn, the way it always did.

Lior Blackwood stood in the cold throat of the gatehouse with a lantern in one hand and a tuning fork of black iron in the other, and listened. The gate itself was thirty feet of veined stone set into the mountain's flank, carved with spirals that predated every kingdom on the maps, and when struck properly—fork against the seventh spiral, always the seventh—it answered with a note too low to hear and too deep to ignore. Lior felt it in the teeth. In the breastbone. In the old scar along the left forearm that ached every winter and had never once been explained.

The note held for eleven seconds.

"Eleven," Lior said aloud, to no one, and wrote it down.

The ledger was a heavy thing, calfskin bound, its corners worn round from two hundred years of hands that were all, in one way or another, Lior's hands. *Seventh day of Deepwinter. Seal tone: eleven counts. Frost on the north approach. No travelers.* The entry joined ten thousand others just like it, in ledgers stacked floor to ceiling in the gatehouse's back room, and if anyone had ever read them, Lior had never met that person. Father had kept them. Grandmother before him. Back and back, a river of ink flowing toward some sea no Blackwood had ever described.

Lior blew out the lantern, because daylight had come gray through the pass, and went to make tea.

* * *

Ironhollow in winter was a study in smallness done well.

The gatehouse sat alone where the valley narrowed to a stone throat, three rooms over one, slate roof furred with frost, chimney smoking a thin gray thread into a sky the color of dishwater. Below it, the valley ran four miles down to the village of Lowmark—two hundred souls, a mill, a shrine nobody agreed on—and beyond Lowmark the road gave up pretending and became mule track, then goat track, then nothing at all until it climbed again on the far side of the mountains and became, eventually, the world.

That was the arrangement. The world stayed over there. Lior stayed here.

There were duties enough. The seals wanted checking daily—the great gate had seven, one for each spiral, and each had its own iron fork and its own count, and if any count changed by more than a breath, the ledger said to write it down and the ledger did not say what came after that, because in living memory nothing ever had. The cisterns wanted ice broken. The smokehouse wanted turning. The wall walk wanted sweeping, though snow made the task Sisyphean and Lior did it anyway, because the doing filled the morning, and the mornings were the long part.

Twice a month, weather permitting, the smugglers came.

They called themselves traders, which was generous, and Lior called them traders right back, which was diplomacy. Ferro Askan's people mostly—six or eight of them, mule-borne, wrapped in gray wool with their faces half-hidden by hoods they never lowered even indoors. They brought salt, lamp oil, nails, dried fish, the year's paper, and once, memorably, a crate of oranges from who-knew-where that Lior ate alone over three days like a man committing a slow sin. In exchange they took ironwork from the little forge—hinges, hooks, the fine cold-forged needles the Askan caravans could sell in the free cities for more than their weight in silver—and they took, always, a look at the gate.

Not up close. Never up close. But they'd pause in the yard on their way through, and one of them would tilt her hooded head back and consider the sealed door the way a pilgrim considers a shrine, and then she'd say something pleasant and meaningless, and they'd move on.

Ferro herself came at midwinter, as she did every year. That was tomorrow, by Lior's count, if the weather held, and the weather would not hold, because it was Ironhollow, and it never held.

Lior spent the afternoon restocking the trade room and rehearsing, without quite admitting to it, the shape of the conversation to come. Ferro Askan collected debts the way other people collected coins—carefully, in the dark, with an accountant's patience—and she held one against the Blackwoods that Lior had inherited alongside the ledgers and the forks and the scar. What the debt was for, exactly, Father had never said. Only that it existed, that it was old, that Ferro would call it someday, and that when she did, Lior was to pay it without argument and without asking twice.

*Pay what you owe and keep the gate,* Father used to say, in the flat voice he saved for the only subjects he had. He'd been dead nine years now, and his voice lived on in Lior's head with perfect fidelity, which was either devotion or a haunting, and Lior had stopped trying to decide which.

*Keep the gate.* As if the gate needed keeping. As if anything on this side of it had ever so much as knocked.

* * *

Evening came early, the way it did. Lior lit lamps, fed the stove, and ate standing at the window: barley and salt pork and the last of a jar of pickled onions Ferro's people had traded two winters back. Beyond the glass, the valley lay blue under new snow, and the peaks above the pass caught the last light and burned a dull copper, and it was, objectively, beautiful. Lior looked at it the way you look at a painting you own and have owned so long you can no longer see it.

There was a game Lior played sometimes, on nights like this, when the silence got loud. It was called *someone else's life*, and the rules were simple: pick a person in the world and imagine being them until sleep came. Tonight it was the miller's youngest in Lowmark, the one who'd waved at Lior last market day with flour to the elbows. In the game, that boy would grow up, marry the cooper's daughter or maybe not, take over the mill or maybe not, and his whole life would be a series of doors opening onto other people—weddings, funerals, arguments over grain prices, children with fevers, neighbors leaning on fences to gossip about nothing. Doors opening. Always doors opening.

Lior washed the bowl. Set it in the rack. Sat down with the current ledger and read back through the winter's entries, because reading your own handwriting was, apparently, company now.

*Fourth of Deepwinter. Seal tone: twelve counts. Noted.*

Twelve. Eleven was the number. Eleven since the first entry Lior had ever made at fourteen years old with Father watching from the doorway saying nothing, which was how Father watched. Eleven for twenty-six years of entries across three different hands. Twelve on the fourth, and Lior had written it down, and closed the book, and felt the small cold finger of something touch the back of the neck and withdraw.

It was probably the cold. Everything up here was probably the cold.

Lior took the black iron fork out of its case anyway, and carried it down the stair to the gate chamber, and stood in front of the door with breath fogging, and struck the seventh spiral.

The note came. Held. Ten seconds. Eleven.

Twelve.

And underneath it—not after it, *underneath* it, the way you might hear, beneath a bell's ring, someone very far away clearing their throat—something else. A second tone. Fainter. Wronger. Like the gate humming to itself in a register the fork couldn't reach, or like an answer arriving from the far side of thirty feet of stone that had been silent for three hundred years.

Lior stood there a long time, listening, while the lamp guttered and the cold climbed through boot leather.

Then Lior did what a Blackwood does. Wrote it down. *Seventh of Deepwinter. Secondary resonance noted. Source unknown. Will monitor.*

The ledger did not say what came next. The ledger had never needed to.

* * *

Snow began at midnight, fat and windless, the kind that meant the storm proper was still gathering somewhere over the high peaks and would arrive by morning with opinions. Lior checked the yard, checked the shutters, banked the stove, and climbed to the wall walk one last time out of habit rather than need, because habit was the load-bearing wall of the whole structure, and you didn't pull those out to see what happened.

From the wall, the pass was a pale wound between black shoulders of rock. Nothing moved in it. Nothing ever moved in it, except weather, and mules, and once—a story Grandmother told, or maybe Father, the tellings had blurred—a king's messenger who came up the valley in summer, read a proclamation at the gate that no one answered, and rode away again, and that was the closest thing to history Ironhollow had produced in Lior's lifetime.

Lior's breath hung in the lamplight. Somewhere below, a wolf tested a note and thought better of it.

*I am content,* Lior told the pass, silently, the way you tell a lie often enough that it starts standing up straight. *I have work. I have walls. I have a purpose older than any kingdom currently claiming to matter. I want nothing.*

The pass said nothing back. The pass was good at that.

It occurred to Lior—not for the first time, but tonight with unusual clarity—that Father had died in this same season, at this same window, probably, or one much like it, having kept the gate for forty years and explained it for zero, and that Lior had buried him under the cairn behind the woodshed and come inside and struck the seventh spiral and written *eleven counts* in a hand that shook, and that the ledger had absorbed the grief without comment, the way it absorbed everything, and that this—this ledger, this wall, this beautiful unbearable silence—was the entire inheritance. Not land. Not gold. A post with no relief, guarding a door with no visitors, for reasons razored out of the record by hands long dead.

And it occurred to Lior, standing in the falling snow with the lamp hissing, that tomorrow Ferro Askan would come up the valley, and she would look at the gate the way she always did, and she would drink Lior's tea, and she would smile her account-book smile, and she would say something in that offhand way of hers that was never offhand—

—and that Lior would answer it, whatever it was, honestly, because that was the other rule, the unwritten one, the one Ferro had built her fortune on: *a Blackwood doesn't lie.*

Which meant that everything Lior had, everything Lior was, was available for purchase, and the only question was the price.

The snow thickened. The lamp hissed. Down in the gate chamber, unattended, the great door held its twelve-count note in the dark like a held breath, like a word someone on the far side had finally decided to say.

Lior went inside, barred the door, and did not sleep.

Near dawn, through the shutter cracks, with the storm arriving at last in white ropes and the wind beginning to scream down the pass like something with a voice—near dawn, Lior heard it clearly for the first time, and knew it wasn't the cold, and knew it wasn't the mountain settling, and knew that every ledger in the back room was about to become obsolete:

Someone was knocking.
