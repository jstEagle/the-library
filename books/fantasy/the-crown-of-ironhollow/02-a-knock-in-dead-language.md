## 2. A Knock in Dead Language

The blizzard came down the valley like a debt collector.

Lior had watched weather for ten years from the gatehouse window—had learned its moods the way other people learned faces—and this one had a particular quality to it, a flat white hunger that ate sound and distance both. By midmorning the mountain was gone. By noon the wall was gone. By the time Lior had checked the seals twice and stoked the hearth three times out of pure superstition, even the gate itself was only a suggestion of iron under a shroud of snow.

Good, Lior thought. A night like this, nobody travels. Nobody knocks.

The thought arrived with the smugness of all premonitions that are about to be proven wrong.

The routine held anyway. That was the point of routine—it held when nothing else did. Lior lit the lamp over the ledger desk, opened the book to the current year, and made the evening's entries in a hand so practiced it barely required attention: *Seals intact. Wards cold but stable. Trade due with Askan's people on the thaw, four casks salt pork, two of tar.* Ten years of entries just like it. Before that, Father's hand in the same book, and before him Grandmother's, and before her a line of Blackwoods stretching back through pages gone soft as cloth with handling, all writing the same things in different inks.

*Seals intact.*

Nobody read them. Lior had concluded this years ago. Once, in the third winter, a courier had come up the pass from the lowlands with a tax notice and an expression of profound confusion when Lior asked whether anyone in the provincial office wanted the annual report. *You keep records?* the man had said, as if Lior had admitted to cataloging dust. *For what? There's nothing behind there but rock and old ghosts.*

Rock and old ghosts. The official position of the Crown of Malrec's new order, inherited from the old order, was that Vael had been buried for three hundred years and everyone inside was dead, and the gate at Ironhollow was less a door than a headstone. Lior's family had been hired to watch a grave.

Except nobody hires a family for three centuries to watch a grave. Graves don't need watching. Whatever the ledgers were really for, whatever the seals were really keeping—Father had died without saying, and his father before him, each generation passing down the duty like a sealed box: *keep the gate, keep the books, ask nothing.*

Lior asked nothing. Lior kept the books. And if some nights the silence behind the iron felt less like emptiness than like held breath—if some nights Lior lay awake listening for a sound on the far side that never came—that was between Lior and the dark, and neither told.

* * *

The knock came at dusk, or what would have been dusk if the sky hadn't already surrendered to snow. Three blows, small ones, spaced wrong—the rhythm of someone who didn't know you knocked on doors, or who'd forgotten, or who was too weak to do it properly.

Lior took the poker from beside the hearth first. Habit. Then the lantern. Then, after a breath at the door with the wind screaming like something skinned alive, the latch.

The child fell across the threshold before the door was fully open.

It went into Lior's arms more than anything—a bundle of rags and ice, weightless in the way that starvation makes people weightless, hollowed out from the inside. Snow crusted the black hair, the gray cheeks, the eyelashes frozen half-shut. Under the rags Lior could feel ribs like a washboard and a heartbeat going fast and shallow, a bird's heart, a heart running out of road.

"No fire," the child gasped, in the Trader's Tongue, slurred with cold. "No fire, no light, please—" and then the words broke apart, dissolved, and what came out instead was something else entirely.

Lior had grown up on three languages. The Trader's Tongue for market days. The lowland dialect for the tax man. And Old Ironhollow speech, the archaic border talk Grandmother had drilled into Lior with the arithmetic, because a keeper needed to read the oldest inscriptions on the gate. This was none of them. It was older. It moved differently—vowels that climbed where they should fall, consonants stacked like stones—and it came out of the child in a flood, feverish, desperate, the way drowning people talk.

Lior got the door barred, got the child onto the pallet by the hearth—but no fire, the child had said, and something in the voice made Lior obey, banking the coals down to a sullen red glow instead of building it up. Got off the wet rags, which fought back, frozen stiff as bark. Got wool blankets from the chest. Got broth heated on the banked stove and got perhaps a quarter of it into the child, who swallowed by reflex more than will.

Then, while the child shivered toward warmth, Lior did the thing that defined a keeper, the thing Father would have done without thinking: reached for the ledgers.

There was a shelf of them behind the desk that wasn't trade records. Older. Thicker. Bound in leather gone black with age, their spines unlabeled because everyone who needed to know, knew. Lior pulled down the lexicon—Grandmother's copy, copied from her father's copy, copied back and back into the dark—and set it by the lamp and began, slowly, working from memory of the sounds, to match them against the script.

The vowels climbed. The consonants stacked. The grammar put its verbs where no living language put its verbs.

Old Vaelish.

Lior sat back from the book and looked at the child for a long moment.

Dead language. Three centuries dead—dead with the kingdom that spoke it, dead with the king whose face was chiseled on the gate's lintel with his eyes put out by some pious chisel-work generations ago. Scholars argued about its pronunciation. Priests called it cursed. The exile villages down-country still used fragments of it in prayers they didn't understand, words worn smooth as river stones, meaning lost.

And this child—eight years old, maybe nine, starving, frostbitten, alone in the worst blizzard in a decade—spoke it fluently. Natively. The way you speak the language you dream in.

Lior turned back to the lexicon and began translating, word by slow word, what the fever was saying.

Much of it was untranslatable—fever has no grammar. But phrases surfaced. *Don't burn. Don't let them find me. Mother.* And then, repeated, over and over, in a small cracked voice pitched to carry across a burning village square:

*"I am the crown that is not yet whole. I am the crown that is not yet whole."*

A formula. Lior knew formulas; the gate inscriptions were full of them. But this one wasn't in the lexicon. It wasn't in any of the shelf-books. Which meant either the child was reciting nonsense—or the child was reciting something older than the books.

Lior pushed back the blanket from the child's collarbone to check for frostbite along the neck, and stopped.

There, below the left collarbone, where the skin had finally warmed enough to show color: a birthmark. Dark red-brown, the size of a coin, and shaped—not roughly, not approximately, but precisely—like a crown. A crown broken at its peak, split down the middle, the halves leaning away from each other like something that had been whole once and dropped.

Lior had seen that shape before. Carved into the keystone of the outer gate, weathered nearly smooth. Rubbed into wax seals on letters three hundred years old, preserved in the deepest of the ledger boxes. The sigil of the royal house of Vael.

The house that was extinct. The house whose last member had died behind this very door, if the histories were true, in the fall of the kingdom.

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. Outside, the blizzard roared. Inside, the silence had changed quality entirely—it had become the silence of a room where something important has just happened and doesn't know it yet.

Lior sat very still and felt the first cold thread of dread work down through the wonder.

*Seals intact,* tonight's entry said, downstairs in the day-book. Lior looked at the child sleeping now, truly sleeping, breath deepening toward something like safety. Looked at the mark. Looked, without meaning to, toward the inner door—the one at the back of the gatehouse, the one that led down to the gate itself, the door Lior opened every morning to walk the seal-line with lamp and chalk.

The child whimpered in sleep. A small sound. Grief-shaped.

And from beyond the inner door—from beyond three feet of stone and three hundred years of silence—came an answering sound.

Faint. Almost subsonic. Less heard than felt, a vibration in the floorboards, in the lamp flame, in the bones of the skull. A hum, low and steady, rising gently as the child cried and falling as the crying eased, the way a plucked string answers its tuning fork.

Lior stood up so fast the stool went over.

Crossed the room. Put a palm flat against the inner door.

The wood was humming. Not the wood—the thing beyond it. The seals. The great iron bands and the ward-lattice behind them, cold and stable every morning of Lior's life, were resonating like a struck bell, and the pitch of the resonance rose and fell with the breath-sobs of a starving child wrapped in wool blankets ten feet away.

Lior stood with a hand on the humming door and understood, with total clarity and no understanding at all, two things at once.

First: the seal knew this child. Knew the child's blood the way a lock knows its key.

Second: everything the ledgers said about why the Blackwoods kept this gate was either incomplete or a lie.

Behind Lior, the crying stopped. The hum faded with it, note by note, sinking back beneath the threshold of sense, until the gatehouse was quiet again—only the wind, only the banked coals ticking, only a child breathing slow and even in sleep.

On the desk, the lexicon lay open to the page where Grandmother's careful hand had copied out the royal formulas of Vael. At the bottom of the page, in ink darker than the rest, added by some later hand, someone had written a single sentence Lior had read a thousand times and never once understood:

*The blood calls to the door. Keep them apart.*

Lior looked at the sleeping child. At the broken-crown mark rising and falling with each breath.

Outside, the storm showed no sign of ending—and somewhere down-valley, past the white curtain, Lior found themselves thinking of the smoke that had been rising from the direction of Thornfell for two days now, thin and gray, that Lior had been telling themselves was a chimney fire.

The child stirred and murmured again in Old Vaelish, one word this time, clear as a bell:

*Home.*

The hum answered.
