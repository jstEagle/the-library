## 3. What the Ledgers Omit

The ledgers of Ironhollow filled one wall of the gatehouse study, floor to ceiling, spines cracked and numbered by hand. Lior's father had kept them the way a monk keeps scripture: dusted daily, never questioned.

Lior had inherited the dusting and skipped most of it.

Now, with Sef asleep upstairs under two blankets and a chair wedged under the door handle—Lior's own idea, though they told themselves it was for the child's protection—the lamplight was theirs alone, and the wall of books waited like a jury.

The mark was simple enough to describe. Lior had drawn it three times on scrap paper before the shape felt right: a crown, but broken at its base, the points snapped off clean, like something that had been whole once and then deliberately ruined. Sef wore it on the left collarbone, small as a coin, dark as old ink.

Old Vaelish. A broken crown. Lior's ledger books had given the language a name and nothing else. The rest would have to come from the family records—if the family records said anything at all.

They started at the beginning. *Volume One, Aldric Blackwood, Gatekeeper.* The handwriting was beautiful. The content was almost entirely about weather.

*Third of Thawmonth. Snow to the second rung of the gate ladder. Seals checked, all sound.*

*Fifth of Thawmonth. Same.*

Aldric Blackwood had recorded thirty years of weather and seal inspections in a hand fit for a royal decree. No explanation of what he was sealing. No mention of why a soldier's discipline had turned into a hereditary post. Lior turned page after page, faster now, looking for anything—*the king*, *the war*, *what we did*, even a name that wasn't his own.

Nothing.

Then, near the end of the third winter, a gap. Not a missing entry. A missing *strip*. A slice through the page, close to the spine, so precise it might have been done with a razor or a very thin knife. The remaining text ended mid-sentence: *and when His Majesty understood what we had*

And then nothing. The next line began, in the same immaculate hand, *Seals checked, all sound.*

Lior sat back.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, the wind worried at the eaves, and beneath it—faint, so faint Lior might have imagined it—came a hum from the direction of the gate. Low and steady, like a struck bell that refused to finish dying.

The child was asleep. Children cried in their sleep. That was all.

Lior turned the page anyway.

* * *

"You're reading my ledgers."

His father stood in the doorway of the study, snow melting off his shoulders, ten years younger than Lior was now—or would be, Lior thought, in the memory's strange arithmetic. Twelve years old, cross-legged on the floor with Volume Nine open, caught.

"Nobody ever reads them," young Lior said. "I wanted to know if anything interesting ever happened."

Tomas Blackwood hung his cloak on the peg. He did that thing he always did—checked the seals' report, checked the window latches, checked his son—and then, instead of taking the book away, he crouched down to the boy's level.

"What are you reading?"

"The year Grandfather died. There's pages gone." Young Lior held it up. "Cut out. Did rats do it?"

For a long moment his father didn't answer. Lior remembered that silence better than anything else about the scene—the way Tomas looked at the razored strip the way other men looked at wounds.

"Rats," Tomas said finally, "don't use knives." He closed the volume gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping person. "Some things in these books were removed on purpose. By keepers who thought future keepers shouldn't carry more weight than they had to."

"Then why keep the books at all?"

"Because forgetting everything is how you lose the gate." He tapped the cover once. "And remembering too much is how you lose yourself. The ones before us found a middle road. Walk it."

Young Lior had nodded, because children nod, and the memory dissolved there—but the older Lior, sitting in the same room twenty-odd years later, heard the sentence again with new ears.

*A middle road.* As if the truth were a hazard to be detoured around. As if the family had spent three centuries rationing its own history like hardtack.

* * *

By the fourth volume the gaps became regular. Once a generation, sometimes twice—a strip cut away, always near the spine, always clean. Whoever did it knew books. Knew how to cut without leaving a ragged edge, knew which passages mattered. And every keeper afterward had apparently looked at the wound in the record and decided, like Tomas, not to bleed on it.

Lior worked through the night. Made notes. Drew the timeline in charcoal on butcher paper spread across the study table:

*Year 1 — Aldric Blackwood appointed Gatekeeper. By whom? (razored)*

*Year 2 — "when His Majesty understood what we had—" (razored)*

*Year 4 — reference to "the ones below" surviving an earthquake; eleven lines removed*

*Year 9 — "the King's own sister came to the door, and we—" (razored)*

*Year 9 — "we did what was asked of us, and the price was agreed" (razored)*

*Year 11 — last full entry: "It is done. Let no Blackwood speak of it again. We keep the door, and we keep our silence, and both are the same duty now."*

After Year 11, the tone changed forever. Thirty years of weather reports. Three hundred years of weather reports. Generation after generation of Blackwoods checking seals and recording snowfall and cutting out anything that sounded like a story.

Lior stood over the butcher paper until their legs went numb, and the pattern stared back, patient and obscene. You didn't erase a crime scene unless there'd been a crime. You didn't ration your own history unless the truth was poison. And you didn't teach your children to dust the shelves and never read the books—not out of reverence, but out of shame dressed up as discipline.

*We keep the door, and we keep our silence, and both are the same duty now.*

What had been asked of them? Asked by whom?

The hum from the gate had grown. Not louder, exactly—closer, the way a sound gets when it stops being background and becomes attention. Lior pressed a palm flat against the study wall and felt it: a tremor in the stone itself, rhythmic, almost like breath.

Upstairs, muffled through the floorboards, Sef made a small sound. Not quite a cry. A whimper, the kind that comes from inside a dream.

The stone breathed in time with it.

Lior took their hand off the wall very carefully, the way you take your hand off something hot, or holy, or both.

* * *

There was one more place to look. Every gatehouse had one—the *vade mecum*, the keeper's private book, passed hand to hand outside the official ledgers, where a Blackwood could note things too dangerous even for the razored volumes. Tomas had kept his in a locked drawer of the desk. Lior had inherited the desk, and the drawer, and had never once opened it, because opening it meant admitting there was something to open it *for*.

The key was where it had always been, hanging behind the loose brick by the hearth, because Tomas trusted locks less than hiding places.

Inside the drawer, wrapped in oilcloth: a slim book bound in gray leather, unmarked. Lior unwrapped it at the table and opened it to find their father's handwriting, cramped and hurried compared to the ledger script, as if here at last he'd written the way a man talks instead of the way a monument reads.

*My son will find this someday, probably too late to thank him for it and too early to forgive me. So I'll be brief.*

*The mark on the left collarbone, a broken crown: this is the sigil of the Vaeli royal house. It appears in none of our records because we cut it out, every generation, beginning with Aldric himself. Ask why, and the ledgers will lie by omission. So I'll say it plain, once, in the only place I could bring myself to:*

*The gate does not keep something in. It keeps someone OUT. Or it did. Or it was supposed to. What Aldric sealed behind that door, and why the crown was broken, and what price was "agreed"—I don't know. My father didn't know either. The erasure started so early and went so deep that we've been guarding a door for three hundred years without knowing what side of it we're standing on.*

*If a marked one ever comes to your gate, boy, remember: the seal knows its own blood. Watch what the stone does. The stone has never once lied to a Blackwood. Only Blackwoods have lied to each other.*

*Forgive me the middle road. I chose it because it was the only one I could see.*

*T.*

Lior read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting each sentence land like a step onto ice that holds, then doesn't.

*Watch what the stone does.*

The stone was humming. The stone had been humming since the night a starving child collapsed at the gate speaking a dead language, and it hadn't stopped, and Lior had been telling themselves it was the wind.

Above, the whimper came again, longer this time, sliding toward waking.

Lior put the vade mecum down and stood in the middle of the study, in the house their family had built on top of an omission, and understood—for the first time, fully, in the gut rather than the head—that inheritance was not a gift. It was a wound that got handed down, bandaged in weather reports, and every keeper along the line had chosen to leave the bandage on because pulling it off hurt more than the rotting underneath.

Well. The bandage was off now.

* * *

Dawn came gray and mean. Lior climbed to the wall walk with cold hands and the vade mecum tucked inside their coat, and looked down into the valley, and stopped breathing for a moment.

Riders. Four of them, strung out along the frozen river road, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who owned whatever ground they crossed. Gray cloaks, hooded against the wind. At this distance they were smudges—but the lead rider carried a banner furled tight, and the two outriders flanking wore the high-collared cut of the Inquisition, the order that had grown fat in Malrec's shadow, burning first and asking questions of the ashes.

They weren't coming to the gate. Not yet. They turned off the river road toward Thornfell's direction, down-valley, and Lior watched them go with the particular sickness of a man watching weather turn.

Gray riders in the valley. A marked child asleep under the roof of the one house whose family had helped break the crown that mark represented. And somewhere behind those four riders, certainly, came more.

Lior thought of the razored strips. Of Aldric writing *let no Blackwood speak of it again.* Of a woman—the king's own sister—coming to the door in Year 9, and whatever had been done to her getting cut out of history before the ink was dry.

Three hundred years of silence, bought and paid for. And now the past had knocked on the door in a dead language, wearing a broken crown, and the bill had come due.

Behind Lior, from inside the gatehouse, faint through stone and timber, Sef woke crying—and the gate, deep in its foundations, answered.

Down in the valley, one of the outriders paused. Turned in the saddle. Looked up toward Ironhollow, a long moment, as if listening too.

Then the four riders urged their horses on, and Lior ran for the stairs.
