## 4. Ash on the Wind

*Run.*

That is the first word. The first thing Sef knows, older than names, older than the shape of their own hands: *run*, pressed into them like a brand, like a stone set under the skin of the foot so that every step remembers it.

The village is burning. Or the village has already burned — time is a door that swings both ways when you are small and afraid. Smoke stands up from Thornfell like black trees growing fast, faster than any tree should grow, and the snow comes down gray because the sky has been fed to the fire.

Mama's hands on Sef's shoulders. Her hands are shaking but her voice is not.

"The mountain," she says. "The tall gate. Find the keeper."

Sef does not know what a keeper is. Sef knows the mountain the way they know their own heartbeat — always there, at the edge of everything, wearing its crown of cloud. There is a door in it. Everyone knows there is a door in it and everyone knows never to speak of it, the way you know not to speak of death at supper.

"Go," Mama says, and then she says something else, in the old tongue, the words Grandmother used to whisper over the bread before anyone ate. Words with edges. Words that sound like a lock turning.

Then she pushes Sef into the treeline and turns around and does not follow.

* * *

Afterward — after could be minutes or years; the snow keeps falling either way — Sef lies in a hollow under a fallen pine and watches the riders go by.

Gray cloaks. Gray hoods. Their horses make no sound on the snow, which is wrong, which is the most wrong thing Sef has ever seen or will ever see, horses moving like thoughts through a dream, and the men on them do not speak to one another. They don't need to. When one raises a hand, the others turn, all together, like fish.

One of them stops near the pine. Sef can hear breathing inside the hood, slow and even, someone very calm about a burned village, someone who has done this many times and expects to do it many more.

The rider dismounts. Walks to the edge of the hollow. Stands there.

Sef holds their breath until their chest screams. Counts heartbeats instead. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

The rider crouches and picks something out of the snow — a scrap of cloth, a doll's arm, Sef will never know — and turns it over in gray-gloved fingers. Then the rider looks directly at the hollow. Directly at Sef. Sef can feel the look land on them like a physical weight, like a hand pressing down on the back of the neck.

The rider stands. Mounts. Rides away.

Sef learns two things in that hollow, and both of them stay forever:

First: sometimes the hunters leave you alive, and no one can tell you why, and you must live inside the *why* for the rest of your life.

Second: being still can save you. Stillness is a weapon. A child with no sword, no knife, no mother — stillness is the only weapon left.

Sef practices it. In the days that follow, in the weeks that might be months, Sef becomes very good at stillness. So good that some part of them stops moving altogether and never starts again.

* * *

There was a woman. This comes later, or earlier, the fragments don't file themselves in order. Memory in Sef's head is a river with the dam broken — everything downstream and upstream all at once, floating logs and drowned lamps and once, horribly, a shoe.

The woman was not Mama. Mama stayed behind at the treeline and did not follow, and Sef has learned not to say her name even inside their own skull, because saying it makes the hole open again, and the hole is bottomless.

But there was a woman. Three villages gone, maybe four, Sef walked through the bones of them — chimneys standing in ash-fields like little gray teeth, doors hanging open on nothing — and in the last one, under a collapsed roof beam, there was a woman still breathing.

She was old in the way of people who have been young recently. Burned along one side. She saw Sef come through the doorway and she smiled, and the smile was worse than screaming would have been.

"You're one of ours," she said. Not in the lowland tongue. In the other one. The old words. Sef had heard them all their life in lullabies and blessings and never once understood that hearing them was dangerous until this winter, when everyone who spoke them started dying.

"I'm nobody's," Sef said, because that felt true and also safe.

"Yes." The woman laughed, and the laugh cost her something, blood at the corner of her mouth. "That's exactly right. That's the whole prophecy, child. *Nobody's.*"

She fumbled at her neck with a hand that didn't work well anymore, and pulled free a cord, and on the cord a token — copper, worn smooth, stamped with a mark Sef knew without knowing how: a crown with a piece broken out of it. Broken the way a plate breaks. Broken on purpose.

"Take it."

"No."

"*Take it.*" And her voice changed then, went deep and formal, the voice of a priestess or a queen or just an old woman who had decided to die giving orders. "They're looking for it. For what it opens. You carry it and you never show it, never name it, never let them see your wrist where the mark—"

She stopped. She was looking at Sef's arm, at the birthmark there below the sleeve, the broken-crown mark that Mama had always kept covered, always said was nothing, always said too quickly.

"Oh," the woman said softly. "Oh, you poor thing. It isn't the token they want."

The roof beam shifted. Somewhere outside, hooves on frozen ground — real hooves this time, loud ones, careless ones.

"Listen," she said, fast now, gripping Sef's wrist with shocking strength. "The gate. The tall keeper. Old Vaelish — if he knows the words, he knows the debt. Say to him: *the door was never locked against us.* Do you have it?"

"The door was never—"

"Locked against us. Go. Don't run in a straight line. Don't be kind to anyone who offers you food first."

She pushed Sef toward the back wall, where a gap yawned where a board had rotted away, and Sef went through it into the snow, and the last thing they heard from inside the house was the woman beginning to sing. Old words. Lullaby words. Sung loud, on purpose, so the riders would know exactly where she was and not think to wonder about the gap in the wall.

Sef ran. Did not run in a straight line.

Has been running crooked ever since.

* * *

The blizzard finds Sef on the third day, or the ninth. Days have stopped meaning anything. Hunger has become a flat country Sef lives in now, with its own weather, and the cold is a landlord that takes a little more rent each night.

In dreams — in the white, in the walking-sleep — things surface.

Grandmother at the hearth, saying: *"We were kings' neighbors once. Then we were kings' ghosts. Ghosts keep quiet, little one. That's the whole trick of surviving."*

Papa sharpening the scythe, saying nothing, never saying anything, but stopping to rest his hand on Sef's head each evening like checking a fence post for rot.

Mama sewing the mark-cover into every sleeve Sef owned. *"If anyone asks, you fell in brambles. Brambles. Say it back."*

*"Brambles."*

And underneath all of it, the oldest memory, the one from before language: warmth. A great slow warmth like a sleeping animal the size of a mountain, breathing somewhere far below the world, and a hum in the bones of the earth, and — though this cannot be a real memory, though Sef knows it cannot — a voice, vast and tired and kind, saying:

*Not yet.*

*Not yet, little one. But soon.*

* * *

Then: light. Yellow light, square-shaped, coming through a window, which means walls, which means indoors, which means captured or dead or saved and Sef cannot tell which and has stopped caring much.

A room. Small. A stove ticking as it cools. Shelves of books, more books than Sef has ever seen in one place, spines cracked and faded, and the smell of paper and lamp oil and — faintly, underneath — cold stone, mountain-deep stone.

Someone has taken off Sef's boots. Someone has put blankets on Sef. Someone has left water and bread within reach and then gone away, which is either kindness or a trap, and Sef has learned the hard arithmetic that traps usually involve less effort than kindness.

The bread is real. The water is real. Sef eats and drinks with the flat efficiency of an animal and feels nothing about it.

The copper token is still on its cord under the shirt, against the skin. Sef checks it with two fingers, the way you check a wound. Still there. Warm. Warmer than body-warm, which it shouldn't be, which it has been doing more and more lately, especially at night, especially when—

When the humming starts.

Because there is a humming. Sef noticed it first in the storm, half-dreaming against whatever wall the mountain provided, and told themselves it was wind in the rocks, blood in the ears, hunger singing its one song. But it isn't. It comes from below. From the floor, the stone, the deep of the mountain itself — a tone too low to properly hear, felt in the teeth and the breastbone instead, patient as tide.

It got louder as Sef walked toward the gate. Sef had thought that was fear.

Now, in the warm room, wrapped in a stranger's blankets, Sef listens to it and understands with a certainty that arrives whole, like an egg already hatched:

The mountain is not humming at the storm.

The mountain is humming at Sef.

And the mark on Sef's arm — the brambles, the *brambles, say it back* — is warm too, and answering, note for note, like a second bell struck in sympathy.

Sef pulls the sleeve down over it and holds their own wrist, hard, as if holding shut a door.

* * *

The tall one comes back in the grey part of the morning. Big, in the way of furniture and mountains — present whether you look or not. Moves slowly, deliberately, the way big careful people move around small breakable things. Sef catalogs everything automatically, the way the hollow taught them: hands calloused in the pattern of rope and pen, not sword. Eyes sad. Sad in the specific way of someone who has practiced not showing it and mostly succeeds. A ring of keys at the belt — many keys, heavy keys — and Sef's whole body goes tight at the sight, because keys mean locks and locks mean cages, and the woman's voice is still in their ear: *it isn't the token they want.*

The tall one speaks the lowland tongue, slow and simple, the way people talk to fools or children.

"You gave me a scare. I'm Lior. I keep this gate."

Keep. Keeps it for whom? Keeps it shut? Keeps whatever's inside from getting out, or whatever's outside from getting in? The word sits in Sef's chest like a swallowed stone.

"You've been fevered. Two days. You're safe here."

Safe. Another word that means nothing. Safe is a story adults tell children while they bar the door, and the bar never quite holds, and Sef has watched it fail twice now in one winter.

Lior kneels — slowly, showing empty hands, the way you'd approach a hurt animal, and Sef hates that it works, hates that the body wants to believe it even while the mind files the gesture under *techniques*. "Do you have a name?"

Sef says nothing. Stillness. The oldest weapon.

"Can you tell me where you came from?"

Nothing.

"All right." No anger. No frustration. Something almost like respect, which is more frightening than anger, because respect means the tall one has understood exactly what Sef is doing and why. "You don't have to. There's soup when you want it. It's on the shelf. I'll leave you alone."

And the strange thing — the unbearable thing — is that Lior does. Leaves. Closes the door with a soft click and lets the silence stand, unguarded, trusting a starving stranger alone in a house full of keys.

Sef waits a long time. Then eats the soup, because survival instinct outranks suspicion, and survival instinct says: eat whenever food exists, trust no one, sleep in shifts, run crooked.

While eating, Sef hears it clearly for the first time, now that the fever is gone and the room is quiet:

The tall one, on the other side of the door, speaking to himself in a low voice. Reading. Working through a page of some old book with the halting patience of a man sounding out a foreign alphabet —

— and pronouncing the old tongue. Badly. Wrongly. Like a child stumbling through a prayer learned from a dying grandmother.

But pronouncing it.

*The door was never locked against us,* the burned woman said. *If he knows the words, he knows the debt.*

Sef sets down the bowl. Puts their back to the wall where they can watch the door. Presses two fingers to the warm token beneath the shirt, and answers the mountain's hum with the only reply a ghost knows how to give:

Quiet. Patient. Awake.

Outside, far off down the valley, a dog begins to bark — not at wolves, not at weather, but in that short repeated panic-voice that means *people, strangers, many* — and Sef goes absolutely, perfectly still.
