## 5. The Smuggler's Toll

The Saltwife came up the valley on the third night after the blizzard broke, riding low in the water like a knife held flat.

I saw her lanterns first from the wall—three lights in the pattern that meant cargo and no questions—and felt something I hadn't let myself feel in years: relief. Ferro Askan's ship ran the frozen river twice a season if the ice allowed, once at midwinter's turn and again before thaw. Her crews traded salt, iron nails, dried fish, and news. Mostly they took my ledger-stamped chits and left goods in the snow-cache below the gatehouse stairs, but twice a year Ferro herself climbed the path, drank my terrible tea, and reminded me that somewhere south of Ironhollow there was a world where people still laughed.

Tonight I needed more than tea.

Inside, Sef slept by the hearth where I'd moved the cot close enough to feel the fire without being burned. The child slept badly—twitching, murmuring syllables of that dead language like a man reciting debts—and every time the crying started in sleep, the seal hummed. I had stopped pretending I didn't notice. Three hundred years of Blackwood records said the gate was stone and silence. Three nights of a starving child's nightmares said otherwise.

I banked the fire, draped my own coat over the small shoulders—it swallowed Sef whole—and went down to meet the ship.

* * *

Ferro Askan met me at the foot of the stairs, and as always she made the frozen riverbank look like her drawing room. She was sixty if she was a day, broad as a door, wrapped in a gray wolf-fur coat worth more than everything I owned including the gatehouse, and her hands were bare despite the cold because, she claimed once, "cold respects me." She wore six rings. Four of them, I happened to know, were poison rings. She never told me about the other two.

"Keeper Blackwood." She spread her arms. "You've gotten thinner. That's an insult to your cooking."

"You've never eaten my cooking."

"And yet I insult it anyway. That's what friendship is." She looked past me up the dark stair. "Something's wrong. You have the face of a man who's done arithmetic he didn't like."

"Come inside. Tea first. Arithmetic after."

"Tea," she said gravely, "is how you soften me up. It won't work." It always worked.

We climbed together. At the top of the stair she paused, and I watched her do it—the thing she did with everyone, the reading of a room through its silences. The extra cot. The coat too small for anyone I knew. The second cup I hadn't thought to hide.

"Well," said Ferro Askan softly, "you've found yourself a secret."

"I need passage. South. Two travelers, quietly, off the record routes. No chits, no ledgers, nothing with my stamp on it."

She sat down at my table uninvited, the way she always did, and poured her own tea. "Off the record. From the man whose entire life is a record." She blew across the cup. "Say the rest."

"The rest costs you nothing tonight."

"Everything costs me something. I'm just deciding whether it's paid now or later, with interest." She sipped, grimaced on principle—"still terrible"—and set the cup down. "Two travelers. One of them is you. Who's the other?"

"Ferro."

"Lior." She used my whole name like a key turning. "Ten years you've kept my secrets when the tax boats came sniffing. Ten years I've owed you the winter the *Kestrel* sank and you logged my cargo as lost so the harbor-masters didn't strip my hull. I pay my debts. But I pay them *informed*, or not at all." Her eyes, small and bright as a weasel's, went again to the curtained corner. "So either show me, or tell me, but one of those happens before this tea goes cold."

I could have refused. I want to be honest with whoever reads these pages eventually, if anyone does: I considered refusing. Considered putting Sef in the cisterns and sending Ferro back down the mountain with salt and lies. But the riders had been seen in the valley—I hadn't told her that yet either—and a lie to Ferro Askan has a way of coming back wearing armor.

I pulled the curtain aside.

Sef woke the way Sef always woke now: instantly, silently, pressed back against the wall with eyes too old for the face. The birthmark caught the firelight along the collarbone—the broken crown, the circle split by a jagged line, ink-dark against skin gone brown with road dust.

Ferro Askan went very still.

In thirty years of smuggling, in a life spent among men who killed each other over cargo manifests, I had never seen Ferro Askan still. Motionless, she looked suddenly ancient, and suddenly afraid, and both of those things frightened me more than anything the inquisitors might have done.

"Where," she said quietly, "did you get that child?"

"The child got themselves here. Collapsed at my gate in the blizzard. Speaks Old Vaelish."

"Speaks—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. When she spoke again her voice was the smooth merchant voice, the dangerous one, the one she used when the price was going up. "Do you know what that mark is, keeper? Do you actually know, or are you playing nursemaid to a brand you can't read?"

"It's Vaeli. Royal line, by the old genealogies. Which ended three centuries ago, which is why I need passage and not a herald."

"Ended." Ferro laughed, one short bark with no humor in it anywhere. "Lior. I'm from the coast settlements. Ashfall stock, three generations back from the refugees who walked out of the wars. My grandmother sang us lullabies in half-dead Vaelish and told us the royal line didn't end. It went *under*. Into Vael. Behind doors like yours." She pointed one ringed finger at the sealed gate visible through the window slit, black against the starlit snowfield. "Every smuggler family on the coast has some version of that story. We thought it was a story."

"So did I," I said. "Until last week."

Sef spoke then. Not Old Vaelish—for the first time since arriving, plain trade-tongue, cracked and rusty as an unused hinge:

"You know the mark."

Ferro turned to look at the child fully, and whatever she saw in that small fierce face seemed to settle some argument inside her. She inclined her head—not a bow, Ferro Askan didn't bow, but a merchant's acknowledgment of a superior party at the table.

"I know it, little one. And I know what it means, which is that half the world will want you dead and the other half will want you *owned*. So." She turned back to me, brisk as weather. "Passage. South. Quietly. Done, and gladly, for the debt between us. But you asked for off-record routes, which means my crew, my hull, my neck. And that means my price."

"Name it."

"The truth of what's behind your gate."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the river ice boomed and settled, the sound carrying up the valley like distant artillery.

"That's not a secret I own to sell," I said carefully.

"Everything is owned by someone. Usually by someone who doesn't know they own it." Ferro leaned forward. "Here is what I know, keeper, and you'll forgive me for knowing it, since knowing things is my entire trade. Your family has held that gate for three hundred years. Paid for it, provisioned it, bled for it. Nobody pays that much for a door unless there's something behind it worth guarding—or worth hiding. Now a living heir of the buried kingdom turns up on your doorstep speaking a language that's been dead since your great-great-grandsires, and within days you're begging off-the-book passage south. Either you know what's behind that gate, or you know what's coming for the child. I suspect both." She folded her bare hands on my table. "Tell me true. All of it. What's behind the gate, and what you're running from. Then we sail at first light."

I looked at Sef. The child was watching Ferro with that unnerving stillness, and I thought: *this is the first adult besides me who hasn't flinched from them.* I thought: *the child needs allies more than I need my family's privacy.*

And underneath that, quieter, in the voice I didn't like listening to: *you don't actually know what's behind the gate. You only know what your family wrote—which is nothing—and what they razored out—which is everything.*

"I don't know," I said finally. "That's the truth of it. My family kept the seal. The records say we guard the world from what's under Vael. But someone in every generation cut pages out of our own ledgers, deliberately, carefully, like surgeons. Whatever we're really guarding—or hiding—the truth was removed before my grandfather was born." I spread my hands. "What's behind the gate, Ferro, is a question. And I think the answer is why the inquisitors are burning villages two valleys east looking for this child."

Silence. Ferro studied me the way she studied cargo she suspected was mislabeled.

Then she nodded slowly. "A question. Hm. I collect secrets, Lior. Do you know that about me? Everyone thinks I deal in salt and stolen silk. But secrets are the only currency that appreciates. And you've just given me a rare one—that the invincible Blackwoods are keeping a lock they've lost the meaning of." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "That plus the debt covers passage. But hear my terms, because I'll only say them once."

"Terms."

"One: the child travels under my protection, which means under my rules. No heroics, no fires lit above decks, no telling my crew who they are until I say. Two: we land at Marrowgate, nowhere else. If there's any place south that remembers Vael kindly, it's Marrowgate's refugee quarter—if any place will recognize that mark and kneel, it's there too, and kneeling gets people killed, so we go in quiet or not at all." She ticked a third ringed finger. "Three: whatever you learn about what's behind that gate—you tell me. First. Before councils, before kings, before priests. I'm buying a share of the question, keeper. Don't make me come collect it with knives."

"Agreed," I said, and hated how easily it came. Every ally costs something. I was beginning to understand that the cost was never money.

"Good." She drained her tea and stood. "Wake the child. We load in an hour; the tide doesn't wait and neither does the ice. And Lior—" she paused at the door, and for a moment the merchant polish dropped and something older and wearier looked out, "—the riders you haven't mentioned. How far?"

My stomach dropped. Of course she knew. "Two valleys east as of yesterday. Gray cloaks. Inquisitor outriders."

"Mm. Captain Corhane's company, if the reports I bought in Thornfell hold true." She said it lightly, watching me. Watching me *not* react fast enough. "Ah," she said softly. "You know the name. Interesting. Later. Wake the child."

* * *

Loading the Saltwife was quick brutal work in the dark—crates of salt up, crates of my winter-traded furs down, my chit-ledger conspicuously left locked in the gatehouse because this voyage would appear nowhere. Sef carried a sack of provisions bigger than they were, refusing help with the stubbornness of someone who had learned that accepting things gets you hurt. Ferro watched them work with an expression I couldn't read, and murmured to her quartermaster in coast-cant, and the word *little crown* passed between them exactly once.

We were casting off the last line when the dog started barking.

Ferro kept dogs aboard—a pair of shaggy river-hounds that slept through storms and woke for trouble. This one stood at the rail facing upstream, hackles up, growling at the empty white valley.

I followed its gaze. High on the eastern ridge, where the pass road came down toward Ironhollow, torches. Many torches. Moving in formation, unhurried, the way people move when they believe nothing ahead of them can run.

"Gray cloaks?" Ferro asked quietly.

"Gray cloaks."

"How long to saddle horses and ride them down instead?"

"Hours, in this cold. They weren't due for days." I stared at the torchline and felt the arithmetic finish itself. *They weren't due.* Someone had told them. Or someone's network had heard mine. "They're not searching the passes anymore, Ferro. They're coming here. To the gate."

"To you." She was already signaling her helmsman, voice dropping into the crisp commands of a woman who had run blockades before breakfast. "Or to the child. Either way, keeper, we're leaving now, and you should pray those torches stop at your door and knock politely." A pause, and the weasel-bright eyes glittered at me sideways. "Because if they don't—if they burn Ironhollow behind us like they burned Thornfell—then you and I are going to have a very long voyage south to discuss exactly why an inquisitor captain knows your name before you know hers."

The Saltwife slid out onto the black water. Behind us, Ironhollow's gate stood silent under the stars, and the torches began, at last, to descend.
