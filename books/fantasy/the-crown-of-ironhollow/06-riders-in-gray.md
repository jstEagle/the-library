## 6. Riders in Gray

The dogs started barking an hour before the riders crested the pass.

Lior knew every sound Ironhollow made—the groan of the gate in cold snaps, the whistle of wind through the seal-channels, the particular complaint of the roof beams under snow. The dogs were not part of that vocabulary. The dogs were Ferro Askan's gift, three years back, half a joke: *So you'll know when someone's coming to kill you, keeper.* Lior had fed them, named them, and mostly used them as an excuse to talk to something that wasn't a ledger.

They were barking like the world was ending.

Lior set down the wax stylus, crossed to the arrow slit, and looked out at the valley road. Gray cloaks. A dozen riders in the lead, and behind them—Lior counted twice, hoping to be wrong—forty more, fanned out across the lower slopes in pairs, moving through the pine with the unhurried discipline of people who had done this many times before.

Searchers. Not passing through. Searching.

Behind Lior, from the loft above the hearth, came a small sound. Not a word. Sef didn't speak much around Lior yet—not in any tongue—but the child had learned to make one noise, a soft click of the tongue, that meant *I heard it too.*

"I see them," Lior said quietly. "Stay up there. Don't come down until I call you, no matter what you hear."

A pause. Then the click again, and the faint creak of a small body pressing itself flat against the floorboards, the way children do when they have already learned what hiding means.

Lior pulled on the gatekeeper's coat—heavy wool, iron-gray buttons, the Blackwood sigil stitched at the collar—and went out into the snow to meet the Inquisition with a straight spine and a face that felt, even then, like something borrowed.

* * *

Captain Vess Corhane dismounted before her soldiers did, which told Lior two things: she was confident, and she wanted to be seen being confident. She was younger than Lior expected—mid-thirties, maybe, wind-burned, with a scar through one eyebrow that had been sewn by someone in a hurry. Her gray cloak was regulation, unadorned except for the inquisitor's badge: a closed eye over a keyhole.

An eye over a keyhole. Someone in the Chancellery had a sense of humor.

"Keeper Blackwood." She pronounced it correctly, which meant she'd read the file. "Vess Corhane, Captain of Inquiry, Third Vigil. Thank you for the smoke signal—we saw your beacon from the pass."

"I light it every dusk," Lior said. "It's not a signal. It's habit."

"Everything's a signal if you're watching." She smiled. It was a good smile. That was the worst part. "May we trouble you for water and barn space? My outriders have been in the field nine days."

"You may." Lior stepped aside from the gatehouse door. "The barn's east wall. Water's from the cistern pump—don't drink from the old well, it runs bitter."

"The famous Ironhollow cisterns." Corhane unwound her scarf, stamping snow from her boots with the easy motions of someone making herself at home on purpose. "My predecessor wrote about them. Pre-Imperial stonework, he said. Older than the kingdom itself."

"They hold water," Lior said. "That's all I know about them."

"That," said Captain Corhane pleasantly, "is what everyone says about everything worth knowing."

* * *

The next six hours were the longest of Lior's life, and they were made entirely of nothing.

That was the trick of it, Lior realized somewhere around the second pot of tea. Nothing happened. Corhane sat at the gatehouse table, drank tea she complimented too specifically—*mountain sage, and not the valley strain; you grow this yourself?*—and asked questions that were all perfectly reasonable and all slightly wrong, like a lockpick dressed up as a key.

How long has the Blackwood family held this post? Three hundred years, give or take. And before that? Before that there was no post. Who appointed the first Blackwood? There's a letter in the archive; I could show you. Please don't trouble yourself. No trouble at all—

And so on. Around it, through the windows, Lior watched the gray riders work the slopes. They moved in pairs, probing the treeline, checking the smuggler caches along the low road—Lior saw one pair crack open a supply drop that Ferro's people had buried under a cairn, and noted, with distant professional admiration, that they marked it and left it, baiting the trap rather than springing it. They weren't looking for contraband.

They were looking for footprints. Small ones.

"Your firewood," Corhane observed, mid-afternoon, nodding toward the stack by the door. "You split it yourself?"

"When my shoulder allows."

"Mm. It's split small." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Kindling-size. My grandmother used to say you can tell who lives in a house by the size of their wood. Big pieces, strong backs. Small pieces—" she glanced up, smiling that good smile, "—small hands helping."

"My hands are average," Lior said.

"I'm sure they are."

Sef was in the cisterns. Lior had put the child there an hour before the riders reached the gatehouse—down the ladder behind the grain store, into the dry eastern gallery where the pre-Imperial masons had once banked meltwater for a garrison that hadn't existed for four hundred years. It was cold down there, and dark, and smelled of wet stone and older things. Lior had left bread, a skin of water, and the single instruction: *if anyone opens the hatch, climb the far shaft and wait in the dark until I come.*

There was a second way out of the gallery. Only Lior knew it. Only Lior, and whoever had built it, and whatever else might live in the deep places of Ironhollow that the ledgers didn't mention.

Lior poured more tea and hated how steady the pour was.

* * *

At dusk, Corhane asked to see the gate.

It was the moment Lior had been waiting for and dreading, and it arrived without ceremony, over the bones of a rabbit stew. "Indulge me," she said. "I've read every report on the Ironhollow seal since I took my commission. I'd like to stand in front of it once before I die of paperwork."

"No one stands in front of it," Lior said. "That's rather the point of it."

"Then I'll be the first in nine days of disappointment." She stood, buckling her sword belt with a soldier's economy. "Come, Keeper. Walk me down. You can tell me all the things the reports get wrong."

The tunnel to the gate ran forty paces under the wall, and Lior walked it slowly, listening to the echo of two sets of boots and doing arithmetic. Behind them, above them, thirty feet of mountain and one wooden hatch. If Sef cried—if the child had a nightmare, or panicked, or simply got lonely the way children do in the dark—Lior did not know what would happen. But Lior knew what had happened during the blizzard, when the seal had hummed like a struck bell every time Sef wept, and the memory of that sound sat in Lior's chest like a swallowed coal.

The gate filled the end of the tunnel the way a cliff fills a horizon.

Even Corhane stopped talking when she saw it. Everyone did. Thirty feet of black stone, seamless, uncarved, older than the empire that claimed to have conquered the lands above it—and across its face, the seal: a lattice of silver lines that caught torchlight and gave back something that wasn't quite reflection. Lior had looked at it every day for ten years and still sometimes forgot to breathe in front of it.

"Well," Corhane said softly. "The reports undersold it."

"They usually do."

She walked the width of the tunnel, hands clasped behind her back, a scholar's stroll with a killer's hips—the sword rode easy, Lior noticed, worn ten thousand hours. She stopped an arm's length from the stone. Close enough that protocol should have made Lior object. Lior didn't object. Objecting was a signal too.

"Three centuries sealed," she murmured. "Do you know what's behind it, Keeper?"

"Stone halls and dead kings, according to the histories."

"And what do the histories say killed the kingdom of Vael?"

"A war," Lior said. "A long time ago. By people better at remembering it than me."

Corhane turned. In the torchlight her face was open, almost kind, and that was how Lior knew the kindness was the weapon. "You're not what I expected, Keeper Blackwood."

"What did you expect?"

"Someone who cared." She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to. Then, lightly: "We found tracks yesterday. Two days old, maybe three. Small boots, wrong gait for a goat, heading up-valley toward this pass. You've had no visitors?"

"Smugglers come and go. They pay a toll, they don't leave tracks I bother reading."

"Small tracks."

"Children follow smugglers sometimes. Runaways. It's the mountains—they come looking for the free cities and find snow instead." Lior shrugged, and heard their own voice do something it had never done before: it lied, fluently, warmly, with the easy texture of truth laid over it like glaze on clay. "If a child came through here, they kept walking. Or they're under an avalanche. Either way, Captain, they're not my problem, and they're not yours either."

"No," Corhane agreed. "They're the Chancellery's problem. Which makes them mine, since I'm what the Chancellery looks like when it's standing in a room." She smiled again—that good smile—and offered her hand. "Thank you for the hospitality, Keeper. We'll ride at first light. There are three more passes north of here, and my orders are thorough."

Lior shook the hand. It was warm and dry and very strong.

"Ride safe," Lior said. "The weather turns fast up here."

"So I've heard." At the mouth of the tunnel she paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the last gray light. "One more thing, Keeper. Off the record."

"Captain?"

"The Third Vigil burned Thornfell on the ninth of Deepmonth." Her voice was level, conversational, terrible. "Officially, bandits. Unofficially—" she tapped the closed-eye badge at her collar, once, "—the eye sees what it sees. If a child came to your door out of that ash, Keeper Blackwood, I want you to ask yourself one question before you answer any of mine. Ask yourself what the Chancellery wants with a Vaeli heir badly enough to burn a village for one. Because men who burn villages for a child do not want to crown that child."

She let the silence carry it up the tunnel like smoke.

"Goodnight, Keeper. Mind your woodpile."

* * *

Lior waited until the camp sounds settled into soldier-sleep rhythm—two hours, measured by heartbeat and the drip of the eaves—before going down the ladder into the cisterns with a shuttered lamp.

The eastern gallery was black and cold and smelled of three hundred years of nothing. The lamp found Sef curled in the angle of the far wall, awake—of course awake, children who had run from Thornfell did not sleep on command—with the copper token clenched in one fist and eyes that tracked the lamp like a small animal tracking a hawk.

"It's me," Lior said. "It's over. They're leaving at dawn."

Sef didn't move. Didn't blink. Just watched, and waited, and Lior understood with a lurch that the child was not deciding whether to trust Lior. The child was deciding whether trusting Lior would be fatal. Those were different calculations, and Sef had clearly made the second kind before.

Lior sat down on the wet stone, six feet away, and put the lamp between them like a border marker.

"She knows," Lior said quietly. "Not for certain. But she knows. She told me as much—told me the Chancellery wants you alive, and warned me what that means." Lior laughed, short and airless. "The woman hunting you gave me a warning to protect you. I haven't decided yet whether that makes her decent or the most dangerous person I've ever met."

Sef's fist tightened around the token. Then, slowly, the child uncurled one finger and pointed—at Lior, then at the ceiling, then made a shape with both hands: wings folding. Closing. *The bird comes back to the cage.*

"No," Lior said. Then, because lying had gotten very easy today and Lior refused to practice it here: "I don't know. Maybe. Maybe keeping you is just another way of catching you." Lior rubbed a hand over their face. "Here's what I know instead. Tomorrow night, when they're gone, Ferro Askan's people come through the low pass. I'm going to buy passage off her, and we're going south, through roads the Inquisition doesn't have on its maps, to Marrowgate. To people who speak your language. To people who'll kneel to you, probably, which I'm told is unpleasant."

Sef stared. Then, in a voice rusty from disuse, in trade-tongue thickly accented but perfectly clear:

"Why?"

It was the whole question. Why risk the post, the name, the ten years of quiet. Why burn a lifetime of belonging for a stranger's child. Lior looked at the copper token, at the broken-crown birthmark visible at the collar of the too-big coat, at the ash-colored eyes of the last heir of a buried kingdom, and discovered that the honest answer was one Lior hadn't admitted to anyone, least of all themselves.

"Because my family built that gate," Lior said, "and I've spent ten years guarding it without ever once asking what we locked inside. You're the first thing that's ever knocked. I think—I think I owe the answer more than I owe the door."

Sef considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then the child did something that undid Lior completely: scooted six inches closer, and held out the copper token, palm open, offering it up for inspection the way children offer treasures—look, see, this is mine, and I am showing you.

Lior didn't touch it. "Keep it safe. Keep it secret. Tomorrow we go south."

Above them, faint through thirty feet of stone, a dog began to bark.

Not the alarm bark. Not the stranger bark. The third bark—the one Lior had never heard before tonight, high and repeated, the one Ferro's letter had described in a postscript Lior had skimmed and now, with a cold dropping feeling, remembered word for word: *If they give the third cry, it's not soldiers. It's her people, and it means the deal's gone wrong.*

Lior snuffed the lamp.

In the dark, Sef's hand found Lior's sleeve and gripped it, and together they listened to hoofbeats coming down the valley road—many, fast, unscheduled—while somewhere above, Captain Vess Corhane's camp erupted into lantern-light and shouting, and the night that was supposed to be empty filled up with riders.

Through the stone, muffled but unmistakable, came a voice Lior knew: Ferro Askan's, carrying the way her voice always carried, like a blade wrapped in velvet.

"Captain Corhane! What a coincidence. I was just riding to collect a debt."
