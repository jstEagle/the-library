## 7. The Road Under the Mountain

**Lior**

The smuggler road began where Thornfell's ash ended.

Ferro Askan's map was a scrap of oiled leather, inked in her own cramped hand, and it showed a door that did not exist: a seam in the cliffside two miles east of the burned village, hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall. *Knock twice on the stone below the ice,* the note read. *If it doesn't open, you've knocked wrong. If it opens and something inside doesn't like your face, you should have paid me more.*

Lior had paid her more.

They traveled by night and slept by day, curled in hollows under pine boughs, because Corhane's riders worked the high passes by daylight and Lior had spent ten years learning how soldiers thought. Sef walked without complaint, which frightened Lior more than crying would have. Children who had learned not to cry had learned it for a reason.

"Here." Lior stopped at the base of the cliff, where the waterfall hung like gray glass, six feet of ice veining down black rock. The air beneath it smelled wrong—old, mineral, faintly sweet, like the inside of a sealed jar. "Stand back from the wall."

Sef stood closer to the wall. Of course they did.

Lior found the seam by touch, below the ice where Ferro's note promised, and knocked twice. The sound went into the stone and kept going, a long rolling echo that no natural cave should make, and then—silence. Then a click, deep and heavy, like a lock the size of a coffin turning over.

A slab of rock swung inward on hinges that had no business still working after three hundred years.

Warm air breathed out. That was the part that stopped Lior's heart. The mountain was cold to its bones; every tunnel Lior had ever read about in the ledgers froze solid by midwinter. But this air came up from below like breath from a sleeping mouth, and it carried a smell Lior knew from nowhere and recognized instantly: lamp oil, bread, wet wool. The smell of people living underground.

"That's not possible," Lior said, mostly to themselves.

"You said that about the wardstone too," Sef said. "And the bridge. And the door at Kellin's Ford."

"The bridge was a ruin."

"It held my weight."

The child slipped past Lior into the dark before Lior could decide whether to be annoyed or terrified, and after a moment Lior followed, because the alternative was standing alone in the snow with inquisitors in the valley behind and no better plan than the one already walking away from them.

* * *

**Sef**

Inside was a road.

Not a tunnel—a road, wide enough for four ox-carts, paved with stones fitted so close together that the seams were thinner than a hair. Lamplight glowed along the walls from glass globes set into niches every twenty paces, and each one lit as Sef passed it and dimmed again behind them, so that they walked always in a small moving island of gold.

Mama would have called this witchwork. Mama had called most things witchwork, but she'd said it differently about Vael things—softer, almost fond, the way you talk about a relative who shamed the family. *Old blood remembers*, she used to say, pressing her thumb against the mark on Sef's shoulder. *It just needs a reason.*

The tall keeper walked three steps behind, which Sef had noticed was their habit. Behind meant watching. Watching meant either protecting or guarding, and Sef hadn't decided yet which one the keeper was doing. The keeper gave food first and ate last. The keeper slept facing the door. The keeper's hands were scarred in the pattern of someone who had spent years working locks and seals, and when Sef cried in the night—the bad nights, the Thornfell nights—the keeper woke instantly, every time, as if the crying were a bell rung only for them.

"Why does the gate hum?" Sef asked, because they had been carrying the question for days and it was getting heavy.

The keeper's boots stopped. "What?"

"When I cry. At the gatehouse. It hummed. Low. Like—" Sef pressed a hand flat against their own chest, searching for the word. "Like a dog that knows its name."

The lamplight made the keeper's face hard to read. "You heard that."

"I hear lots of things." The stones underfoot, for instance. They had been murmuring since the door opened, a sound below sound, and the longer Sef walked the more it resolved into something almost like words. Not Old Vaelish. Older. The language underneath the language, the one the prayers were cut down from. "The stones are counting us."

"Counting us," the keeper repeated flatly.

"One. Two." Sef pointed at the walls. "It stops at two. It's been stopping at two since we came in. I think it's checking."

"Checking for what?"

Sef didn't answer, because they didn't know, and because the keeper's face had gone the color of the ice outside, and because somewhere far ahead—far below—a third voice had joined the count.

*Three,* said the stones. *Three. Three.*

* * *

**Lior**

They descended for what felt like a full day, though without sun there was no way to measure it except by hunger, and hunger said evening. The road spiraled down through the mountain's roots in long switchbacks, past side galleries bricked shut with masonry so old the mortar had gone the color of bone. Lior counted the bricked archways out of habit—gatekeepers counted—and lost track at forty.

At the forty-first, the bricks were broken open from the other side.

"Stop." Lior caught Sef's shoulder. The breach was recent—not recent, exactly, but newer than three centuries. Rubble lay scattered in the road, and the edges of the broken brick were crumbled soft, decades old at least. Beyond the gap, a smaller passage ran off into darkness, and nailed beside it, at the height of a human eye, hung a strip of green cloth.

Smuggler sign. Ferro Askan's color.

"She has people down here," Lior murmured. "Or she did."

"Is that bad?"

"It means the road is used. Used roads have watchers." Lior crouched, touched the rubble. Dust lay undisturbed across it, thick as snowfall. "Not recently. Ten years, maybe more. Maybe since the last time anyone cared what was under Thornfell."

Sef was staring at the green cloth. Their fingers twitched toward it and stopped. "There's writing on the back."

Lior turned the strip over. Charcoal, in a hurried hand—not Ferro's neat cartographer's script but something scratched, urgent:

*IF THE DOORS OPEN FOR THE CHILD, GO DOWN. WE LEFT THE LAMPS BURNING. FORGIVE US FOR NOT WAITING.*

Below the words, a symbol: a circle broken by a vertical line, struck through with a horizontal bar.

Lior knew that symbol. It was stamped on the spine of every ledger in the Ironhollow archive. It was branded into the underside of the great seal itself, where no visitor would ever look. It was the Blackwood mark, and it had been left here, in a smuggler tunnel under a burned village, by someone who had expected a marked child to come this way.

"How old is this?" Lior whispered, though the dust had already answered.

Sef touched the cloth once, gently, the way you touch a grave. "The stones say a long time. The stones say they were sorry too."

* * *

**Sef**

The lamps were still burning.

That was the thing Sef couldn't stop thinking about, hours later, as they walked down and down through galleries where the glass globes kindled ahead of them like a procession of small patient souls. Someone had trimmed those wicks. Someone had filled them with oil. And then that someone had gone away and left them, trusting the mountain to keep them fed—or trusting something else to come and find them burning.

"They wanted you to come here," the keeper said. It wasn't a question anymore. They'd been circling it all day, the way a tongue circles a cracked tooth.

"I know."

"Do you understand what that means? These tunnels were sealed before your grandmother's grandmother was born. Whatever's down here has been waiting for a child with your face on a three-hundred-year-old chance." The keeper's voice stayed level, but Sef had learned to hear the strain under it, the way you hear wind under a closed door. "I need to know you understand that."

"I understand." Sef kicked a pebble and watched it roll away into the lamplight. "You don't have to keep asking if I'm scared. I'm always scared. Being scared is just walking anyway."

The keeper was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke again, the strain had changed shape into something else. "Who taught you that?"

"Mama. After Papa didn't come home." Sef shrugged, because it wasn't a big thing, it was just true. "She said fear is a heavy coat. You can wear it or carry it, but either way it's yours."

Down in the dark ahead, the stones murmured their count—*three, three, three*—and the third voice was louder now, and Sef could finally tell what it was saying between the numbers. It was singing. An old song, half-remembered even by the stones, about a king who locked a door from the inside so that no one he loved would follow him in.

Sef sang the next line back to it.

Every lamp within a mile flared white.

* * *

**Lior**

Lior had drawn a knife before conscious thought caught up, spinning toward the light—but nothing was coming. The gallery simply blazed, every globe burning like a captured star, and in that light Lior saw the walls properly for the first time.

They were carved. All of them. Miles of relief work rising out of the stone: processions, harvests, a city stacked in terraces down an impossible cavern, and everywhere faces—thousands of faces, generations of them, rendered with such tenderness that Lior could see laugh-lines chiseled around eyes three centuries dead.

And above the largest figure, a crowned man with his hand on a door, ran an inscription in Old Vaelish.

Lior couldn't read it. Ten years of ledgers, and the archive's grammar books had been among the razored pages, and now the failure landed like a physical blow. But Sef stood before the inscription with their head tipped back, lips moving, translating the way other children breathed.

"It says: *I am Aldric's shame and Vael's hope, and I hold both until one of them grows up.*"

The name went through Lior like cold water. "Say it again."

"Aldric." Sef frowned. "Why do you look like that?"

Because Aldric Blackwood built the gate. Because Aldric Blackwood's signature was on the founding charter, in ink Lior had been taught to revere. Because Lior's father had made them copy that signature ten thousand times until their hand ached, saying *this is who we are, this is why we watch.*

"What else does it say?" Lior's voice came out strange. "Underneath. There's more underneath."

Sef bent closer. The child's finger traced the grooves of letters, and the stone seemed to lean into the touch, the way a cat leans into a known hand. "It says... *the key is not a thing. The key is a debt.*" A pause. "And then it says a word I don't know. A name, maybe. It's your name."

"My name is Lior."

"No." Sef shook their head slowly, and the blazing lamps threw the birthmark into sharp relief, the broken crown stark against pale skin. "It's older than that. It says *Blackwood*. But the way it's written—" The child looked up, and for the first time since the gatehouse, Sef looked afraid of something that wasn't fire. "The way it's written, it's not a family name. It's a sentence. It means *the ones who owe*."

Far below, the third voice stopped counting.

In the silence that followed, every lamp on the road went dark at once—all except one, a single globe burning at the mouth of a stairway none of Ferro's maps had shown, descending into a depth the mountain had no right to contain.

From the bottom of that stair, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a door being unbarred.

By someone on the inside.
