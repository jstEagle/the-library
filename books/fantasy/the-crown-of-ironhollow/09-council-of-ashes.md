## 9. Council of Ashes

They gave us a room with no windows and called it an honor.

The Council of Ashes met in the undercroft of the Salters' Guildhall, down a stair so old its steps had been worn into shallow bowls by three centuries of feet. Torches guttered in iron brackets. The air smelled of tallow and wet stone and something older underneath—incense, maybe, or just the memory of it, soaked into the walls by generations of people praying to a country that no longer existed.

Lior counted eleven chairs at the long table. Eleven faces turned toward Sef when we entered, and I watched each of them do the same thing: look first at the child's hand, where the birthmark sat like a broken crown, and only afterward at the child's face.

I had been in rooms full of wolves before. Ferro Askan's deck, the night she named her price, had been one. But wolves are honest about their hunger. These people smiled.

"This is the Council," said the woman who rose to greet us. Her name was Maren Solis, and she wore her gray hair braided with copper wire, and she had the kind of voice that had spent decades being listened to. "Elected by the Vaeli quarters of all four free cities. We have waited a long time for you."

*You*, plural. She said it to both of us, but her eyes never left Sef.

"I'm not—" Sef started, and stopped, because they'd learned already that finishing that sentence changed nothing.

I put my hand on the child's shoulder. It was becoming a habit I didn't trust myself with. "The child is exhausted," I said. "And hungry. And eight years old, which none of you seem to have noticed."

"Of course." Maren's smile widened by exactly one degree. "We've prepared a room. Food. Whatever the heir requires."

*The heir.* Not *the child*. I filed that away the way I filed everything, in ledgers no one read, and let them take Sef through a side door into warmth and lamplight. The child looked back at me once before the door closed. I nodded. It was a lie of a nod. I had no idea what happened behind doors in Marrowgate, and less idea whether I could follow if it went wrong.

Then I turned around, alone, facing eleven of the freest people in the world.

* * *

They debated me for two hours as though I weren't there, which was itself a kind of answer.

The council chamber had a second table, smaller, set against the wall for petitioners and witnesses. I sat at it with my hands folded and listened, and took notes, because taking notes was what I did instead of screaming.

Maren Solis led the first faction. Crown the child. March on Malrec while his armies were still digging at mountain passes he didn't understand. Restore the line of Vael, rally the exile quarters of every free city, and end three hundred years of ash with a coronation and a war.

"The usurper rules by fear of what's buried," she said, striking the table with the flat of her hand. "Every tax, every inquisitor, every burned village—all of it justified by the terror of Vael rising again. So let it rise. Let it rise wearing a child's face and carrying a rightful claim, and watch his fear become his gallows."

Applause from five chairs. Real applause, warm and ringing, and I understood these were not bad people. That was the worst part. They had lost villages too. They had names of the dead stitched into their sleeves—I saw the embroidery now, little gray knots, one for each family member lost to Malrec's purges. They wanted their dead avenged and their children safe, and they believed a crowned child would buy both.

Opposite them sat a thin man named Odo Ferran, a salt merchant with ink-stained fingers and the flat voice of someone who had done arithmetic no one wanted done.

"You're proposing to dig up a war," he said, "and hand it to an eight-year-old to carry. Malrec has forty thousand soldiers and a navy. We have quarter-cities, smuggler roads, and a symbol. Symbols don't hold shield walls."

"So we wait?" Maren spat. "Wait for his inquisitors to finish what they started at Thornfell? There are no more children coming out of those mountains, Odo. This one is the last."

"Then this one is the last," Odo said, "and the question is what we do with the last of anything. You spend it. Or you keep it."

The room went quiet in a way that made my skin crawl, because I had heard that exact quality of silence before. In the gatehouse, some mornings, when I caught myself thinking about the seal in terms of maintenance rather than meaning. The silence of people discussing a thing as though it were a thing.

"What does *keep* mean?" I asked.

Eleven heads turned. Maren frowned. "Keeper Blackwood. This is council business."

"The child is council business," I said. "So I'm asking what *keep* means."

Odo Ferran looked at me for a long moment, and to his credit—or whatever you'd call it—he didn't dress it up.

"It means the heir grows up here, in Marrowgate, unmarked. A new name. A trade. The mark covered, always. And when the generation that remembers Thornfell is gone, and the generation after that, the bloodline continues without ever being a banner anyone can march behind." He spread his ink-stained hands. "The war stays buried. The child lives. Everyone eats."

"And who decides that?" I asked. "Has anyone asked—"

"The child is eight," Maren snapped. "Eight-year-olds want sweets and stories. They do not want thrones, which is precisely why crowning one is safe—the regency governs, the child reigns, and by the time the child understands the crown, the crown will already be a fact of the world."

"Do you hear yourselves?" I hadn't meant to say it aloud. My voice came out flat and gatekeeper-quiet, the voice I used on smugglers testing my patience, and it cut the room anyway. "One faction wants to put a war on a child's shoulders. The other wants to erase a child's name. Neither of you has said a single word about what the child wants, because you've already decided the child isn't a person. It's a key. One of you wants to turn it. The other wants to bury it in a drawer. But it's a *key* either way, to both of you, and I have spent ten years keeping a door, and I am telling you: keys are how people lie to themselves about locks."

Silence. Somewhere above us, the guildhall creaked in the wind off the harbor.

Odo Ferran smiled thinly. "And what would you do, Keeper? You guarded a door for a decade. Guarding is all you know."

He thought it was an insult. I let him think it, because the truth—that he was right, and that it terrified me—wasn't his to have.

* * *

They gave me a cell in the guest wing, which told me something about how free the free cities were. A cot, a candle, a bolt on the outside of the door. I lay on the cot and listened to Marrowgate breathe through the stone: cart wheels, gull cries, a drunk singing something in broken Old Vaelish two streets over, getting the vowels wrong in a way that would have made my father flinch.

My father. Ten years dead, and still the loudest voice in every quiet room I entered. *The ledger is the duty, Lior. The duty is the door. Don't ask what the door is for.* He'd kept the secret so well he'd kept it from himself, maybe. Or maybe he'd known, and the knowing was why he drank, and why he taught me the ledgers before he taught me to swim.

I got up. The bolt was laughable; I'd picked harder locks at fourteen to sneak onto the wall. The corridor was empty. Sef's room was two doors down, and the door stood open, and the lamplight inside was wrong—it flickered, moved, more than one shadow.

I went cold all over and crossed the distance fast and silent, the way ten years of empty passes teaches you, and looked in.

Sef was asleep. Truly asleep, curled small under a blanket, the copper token clutched in one fist the way it always was. Around the bed, kneeling on the floor with their heads bowed, were six people I hadn't seen enter. Old women, mostly. Two men. They held candles, and they were whispering—not Old Vaelish, but almost, a pidgin of it, prayers worn smooth by three centuries of mouths that no longer knew what the words meant. Prayers *to*, I realized. Not for. They were praying to the sleeping child the way you pray to an icon.

One of the women saw me in the doorway. She didn't stop. She smiled at me, serene, certain, and pressed one finger to her lips, and went back to her whispering.

I stood there until my legs decided for me. Then I stepped into the room, loud enough to be rude, and said, "The child needs sleep, not an audience," and herded them out with the same flat gatekeeper voice, and they went, smiling, unhurried, as though I were a door that would open again tomorrow. Because to them I was. Everything was. Doors, children, kingdoms—weather to be waited out.

Sef stirred as the last of them left. "Lior?" Small voice, thick with sleep. "Were they praying at me again?"

"Yes."

A pause. "At. Not for." The child had noticed the difference too. Eight years old, and noticing. "In Thornfell, before, Mama said the mark meant we were chosen. After the riders came, I thought maybe chosen meant chosen *for* things. Bad things." The child's eyes found mine in the dark, ancient and young at once. "These people smile like the riders' captain smiled. Polite. Like they already know how the story ends and I'm just the part that hasn't been told yet."

My chest did something complicated and painful. "I won't let them tell your story without you," I said.

"You can't promise that." No bitterness in it. Just accuracy. "Nobody can. That's what a story is."

I sat on the floor beside the bed until the child slept again, and I thought about Odo Ferran's arithmetic and Maren Solis's applause and six candle-holding strangers kneeling in the dark, and I understood that I had walked out of one siege straight into another, and that in this one nobody wore gray cloaks. They wore copper wire and ink stains and kind smiles, and they loved Vael so much they were prepared to spend a child to get it back—or to bury a child to keep it safe—and neither of them could see that from where they knelt, the two options were the same option.

Freedom's defenders. Ashes upon ashes. I had expected Malrec's cruelty to be the enemy. I hadn't expected the cure to come in the same shape as the disease.

* * *

Near dawn, footsteps in the corridor. I was awake before I heard them—you sleep differently when you're guarding something—and I was standing when the knock came.

It was one of Maren Solis's people, a young man with a courier's lean face. He glanced past me into the room where Sef slept, then back, and handed me a folded paper sealed with plain wax.

"For the Keeper of Ironhollow," he said. "From a friend of the road."

Ferro's seal was subtler than that—a smudge of pitch, a thumbprint. I broke it and read three lines by candlelight.

*Your council votes at midday. Both motions carry. Ask them what happens to the keeper afterward. — F.A.*

I read it twice. Then I went and woke Sef gently, and packed our things, and when the child asked where we were going, I said, "Away," and when the child asked why, I said, "Because I finally asked the right question," and when the child asked what the question was, I couldn't answer yet.

But I knew. I'd known since Odo Ferran spread his clean, reasonable hands. Kingdoms that need keys also need people who guard them, and people who guard keys have a way of ending up locked inside with the secret. Maren's regency would need a loyal warden for the heir. Odo's silence would need a jailer for the name. Either way, either vote, there was a role written for me in it, and it ended with me standing at another door for the rest of my life, keeping a child the way my family had kept a kingdom: safely, quietly, and against its will.

Midday was four hours off. The harbor fog was lifting. I took Sef's hand, and we went down the back stair, toward the smell of salt and tar, looking for a friend of the road.
