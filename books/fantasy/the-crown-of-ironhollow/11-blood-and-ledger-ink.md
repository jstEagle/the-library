## 11. Blood and Ledger Ink

The scriptorium of Marrowgate smelled of tallow, dust, and the particular sourness of old men who had spent their lives arguing with paper.

Lior climbed the stairs to it on the third morning after the council meeting, before dawn, when the refugee quarter was still dark and the only sounds were gulls fighting over fish guts down by the harbor and Sef's slow breathing in the room below. The child slept curled against the wall, one hand closed around the copper token even in sleep. Lior had watched that for a long time last night, and had not slept much themselves.

The council had voted nothing. That was the thing about councils—they never voted anything when it mattered. They had argued until midnight about whether the heir should be crowned at the spring equinox or the summer solstice, as if the difference between those dates was the difference between a life and a death, and then they had argued about whether to argue more, and Lior had stood at the back with their arms crossed feeling like a man watching two wolves fight over a lamb while both claimed to be protecting it.

So: the scriptorium.

Ferro Askan's interest had been paid—secrets for secrets—and one of the secrets Lior had given her, back on the mountain road, was a small one that had grown teeth since. *The Blackwood ledgers have gaps. Pages razored out across three hundred years. Someone in my family has been cutting out their own history.*

Ferro had listened with that stillness she did, turning her cup slowly on its saucer, and then she'd said, "There's an archivist in Marrowgate who buys razored pages. Name of Ondrell. Tell him the smuggler queen sent you and that you're paying in iron, not coin." She had smiled then, thin and unpleasant. "He'll know what I mean."

* * *

Ondrell turned out to be seventy if he was a day, half-blind, and built like a bundle of kindling tied with a belt. He let Lior into the scriptorium's reading room by candlelight because, he said, daylight was bad for the vellum, though Lior suspected it was mostly bad for his eyes.

"You're the gatekeeper," he said. Not a question. "The Blackwood one."

"I'm a keeper of ledgers."

"Same thing, in your family." He lowered himself onto a stool behind a desk stacked so high with folios it looked like a fortification. "Ferro said you'd come asking about cut pages. Vaelish material?"

"Blackwood material."

That made him pause. His clouded eyes came up, and there was something in them—not pity, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who has seen many people find out what their grandfathers did.

"Sit," he said.

* * *

He talked while he worked, which seemed to be how he did everything. He pulled trays from under the desk, each one lined with fragments—slivers of vellum, corners of pages, strips no wider than a finger, all of them bearing ink.

"When someone razors a page, they think they've destroyed it," Ondrell said. "But razors slip. Corners survive in bindings. Ink transfers to facing pages over centuries—ghost text, we call it. And sometimes—" he held up a strip with something like tenderness "—sometimes the cutter keeps what they cut. Sentimentality. Or insurance. The Blackwoods kept theirs."

Lior's mouth was dry. "You have them."

"I have eleven fragments attributable to Blackwood hands, spanning from the Sealing year to roughly sixty years after. Bought over forty years from families, estates, one tomb-robber who didn't know what he had." He set the trays out in a row, oldest first, and Lior noticed his hands were steady in the way of a man performing a ritual he'd performed many times. "I'll be honest with you, keeper. I've reconstructed most of it. I've known what your family did for twenty years. I've been waiting to see if anyone would ever come ask."

"Why wait?"

"Because knowing is a burden," Ondrell said simply, "and I don't hand burdens to people who haven't asked for them." He pushed the first tray across. "Read."

* * *

The first fragment was small—a corner, really, with perhaps thirty words surviving in a cramped, careful hand Lior recognized instantly, because Lior had spent ten years copying that hand in the gatehouse ledgers. Aldric Blackwood's writing. The founder's writing. The handwriting Lior had learned their letters from.

*...the king came to us at dusk with perhaps four hundred souls, the rest scattered along the pass. He asked for the gate to be opened and sanctuary granted. My father's terms with the new crown are already sworn. If I open the door, I lose everything my name has bought...*

Lior read it twice. Then a third time, because the words refused to mean anything the first two times, sliding off the mind like water off wax.

*My father's terms with the new crown are already sworn.*

"That's not possible," Lior heard themselves say. Their voice sounded far away. "Aldric sealed the gate to protect the lowlands. From what was in Vael. From the war. Everyone knows—the whole reason the Blackwoods kept the watch—"

"Everyone knows what the Blackwoods wrote down afterward," Ondrell said mildly. "History is written by whoever holds the pen, and your family held the pen for three hundred years. Read the next one."

The second fragment was larger. More of Aldric's hand, dated—by internal reference—to the winter of the Sealing itself.

*The knocking has not stopped for six days. They have food, some of them; they will live weeks yet. The king stands at the door each evening and speaks through the stone. He does not curse us. That is the worst of it. Today he said: "Keeper, whatever bargain you have made, remember that doors are remembered longer than bargains." I have begun sleeping in the lower chamber so I cannot hear him.*

*I have told my sons the gate must never open because of plague. It is even partly true. There is sickness in the crowd. But I will not tell them the rest, because the rest is that I am a man who sold four thousand people for a title, and a man cannot ask his children to guard a door if he tells them what the door is for.*

Lior put the fragment down very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

Four thousand. Not a horde. Not an army of the buried dead, not the plague-ridden menace of every story Lior had been raised on—stories told by Blackwoods, to Blackwoods, for Blackwoods. Four thousand people. Families. A king who knocked politely and spoke through stone without cursing the man who had nailed the door shut on him.

And the king was still in there. Still speaking. Three hundred years later, something behind the gate had hummed when Sef cried, and Lior had felt it in their teeth, and had told themselves it was wind in the seals.

"The rest," Lior said. Their voice came out flat. Good. Flat was manageable.

Ondrell passed the remaining trays down the row without comment, and Lior read them all. The later fragments were in other hands—Aldric's sons, grandsons—each generation discovering the truth in turn and each generation making the same choice. One grandson wrote, in a shaking hand:

*Father confessed everything on his deathbed. I have considered opening the gate. I have considered burning these records instead. I find I lack the courage for either, so I have done what Blackwoods do: I have cut out the page and kept the watch. Perhaps courage skips a generation. Perhaps it never comes at all.*

Another, sixty years on, colder, administrative: *Renewed the arrangement with the Crown. Payment continues. The inquisitors' archives confirm no living soul remains within. We do not speak of the knocking, which continued until approximately the fortieth year.* 

Until approximately the fortieth year.

Lior did the arithmetic without wanting to. Four thousand people, sealed in with whatever stores they'd carried. Forty years of knocking. Then silence.

"There's one more," Ondrell said quietly. "The final page. The one that started the cutting. It's not a fragment—it survived whole, hidden in a binding of a book nobody wanted. A tax codex. Fitting, I suppose." He lifted a thick, unlovely volume from the bottom of the stack and opened it to a place near the middle where the binding bulged slightly, wrong, like a body with something swallowed that wouldn't go down.

He turned it toward Lior. "This is the letter Aldric wrote but never sent. To the king himself. Found in his effects after his death, hidden again by whoever found it, and again, and again. Your family has been passing this shame down like an heirloom none of you could throw away."

Lior took the book.

*To His Majesty Aurelian, Last King of Vael, from Aldric Blackwood, whom he trusted:*

*You asked me once why our family keeps the gate, and I told you it was duty. Here is the truth, which I will say only once, to no one, on paper I intend to burn:*

*The new crown offered my family wardenship of the passes, lands in the valley, and a place in the order of things, in exchange for one service—that the gate of Ironhollow be sealed and stay sealed, whatever came knocking. They feared what slept beneath Vael, or claimed to. I did not believe them then and I do not believe them now. What they feared was not what slept beneath. It was who might come up out of it claiming a throne.*

*A dead kingdom is safe. A kingdom in exile is a wound that reopens.*

*So when you came to my gate with your four thousand, I had already signed away my honor, and I chose the signature. I told myself you would find another way. I told myself the mountain would show mercy. I told myself every lie a coward tells, and then I drove the sealing-stones home while you stood on the other side and thanked me for my hospitality.*

*You thanked me. Do you understand? You stood in the snow with your starving people and you thanked me, because you believed I was a good man, and I let you believe it because it was easier than watching you stop.*

*I do not ask forgiveness. I ask remembrance. Let someone, someday, write down what was done here, so that the Blackwood name means what it means and not what I made it mean.*

*If anyone reads this: the key is split. Half sleeps with my blood, half went into the world with the king's own token-bearer. The gate answers to both together. This was the crown's design, so that no single traitor could ever open what I closed—but I begin to think it may also be the only way a traitor's work can ever be undone.*

*Two halves. Two heirs. One door.*

*A.B.*

The candle had burned down considerably. Lior didn't remember time passing.

They read it again. And again. Looking for the flaw in it, the misreading, the archivist's error, anything. Because the alternative was that everything was true: the duty, the vigil, the ten winters of checking seals alone in the cold, the father who died without explaining, the whole architecture of Lior's life—all of it built on top of a betrayal, funded by it, justified by it, *designed* by it. The Blackwoods hadn't guarded the world against Vael. They'd been paid guards on a mass grave, and paid in land, and the paymaster's descendants were still collecting, and somewhere in the capital a man named Malrec wore a usurper's crown and needed royal blood, and the crown's original design—the split key—was sitting in the pocket of a sleeping child downstairs who trusted the tall keeper with sad eyes.

Lior stood up too fast.

The vomiting happened in the yard behind the scriptorium, into a drainage channel, violently and without dignity, while Ondrell waited inside pretending to catalog things. When it was done Lior knelt on the cold stones with their forehead nearly touching them and breathed, and the breathing went on longer than it needed to, and somewhere in the middle of it something strange happened: the horror drained out, and what settled in underneath was quiet. Very quiet. The same quiet that came over Lior during blizzards, during avalanches, during any crisis large enough that panic became a luxury.

Because here was the thing the calm understood, before the rest of Lior caught up: rage was useless, grief was useless, self-pity most useless of all. What was useful was information. Two halves. Two heirs. The copper token sang near the ledger sigil. Sef carried half the key and had carried it out of a burning village, which meant someone in Thornfell had known, which meant the token-bearer's line had survived three centuries of hiding, which meant—

Which meant Malrec knew too. An inquisitor captain doesn't burn villages hunting a birthmark on a rumor.

Lior rinsed their mouth with snowmelt from the rain barrel, went back inside, and counted out Ondrell's payment in iron nails, as Ferro had instructed, though the archivist waved most of it off.

"Keep one thing," Ondrell said, pressing the unsent letter into Lior's hands. "Not for you. For whoever comes after. Burdens should travel with witnesses." He studied Lior with his clouded eyes. "What will you do, keeper?"

Lior looked at the letter. At the handwriting that had taught them their letters, in spirit if not in fact. At three hundred years of ancestors who had each, in turn, found the truth and chosen the razor.

"I'm going to go downstairs," Lior said, "and wake a child, and tell her the truth about my family before she hears it from someone who uses it as a knife. And then—" They stopped. Started again, calmer. "And then I think the watch ends. Tonight. Three hundred years is long enough."

Ondrell nodded slowly, as if Lior had confirmed something. "Her," he said. "It's a girl, then."

"It's a girl," Lior said. "And she's the other heir. And there are men in gray riding toward this city who want her blood for a ritual, and a council upstairs that wants her head for a coronation, and I am apparently the only person in the world whose job description is keeping her alive rather than using her." Lior tucked the letter inside their coat, against the chest, where it lay like a coal. "So I'm going to need a back way out of Marrowgate, master archivist. And I suspect you know every one."

Ondrell smiled for the first time, and it transformed his kindling-bundle face into something almost young.

"Ferro owes me a favor," he said. "And I owe you a warning: the hardline faction on your council—the ones who favor crowning the child quickly? Their leader visited me last month. Asking whether I'd found any Blackwood documents." He tapped the desk. "They know the key is split, gatekeeper. They just don't yet know where the other half sleeps."

Lior went very still.

"And tonight," Ondrell added gently, "one of their runners followed you here. He's sitting in the tavern across the street. He's been there since before dawn."

Through the window, down in the gray waking street, Lior could see the tavern's lit windows, and the silhouette of a man in the doorway who was very carefully not looking up at the scriptorium.

Lior thought of Sef asleep downstairs, one hand around a singing piece of copper. Thought of the letter against their chest. Thought of their father, dying without a word, sparing Lior the truth the way all those Blackwoods had spared each other—and how sparing had never once saved anybody.

"Well," Lior said, moving toward the stairs with the terrible calm still holding, "then we'd best not keep him waiting."
