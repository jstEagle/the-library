## 12. Corhane's Mercy

The dispatch arrived at the third bell of the morning watch, sealed in gray wax with the sun-and-shackle of the Inquisition, and Vess Corhane read it four times before she let herself believe it.

*The heir is to be taken alive. Unmarked. Unbled. Deliver to the Hand of the Warden at Ironhollow by the turning of the moon. This supersedes all prior instruction.*

Alive. She had burned three villages on the strength of her prior instructions, and every one of those orders had ended in the same word, written in Malrec's own cramped hand: *cleansed.*

She sat in the command tent outside Thornfell's ashes and read it a fifth time, and then she did what she always did when the world refused to make sense: she cleaned her weapons.

The sword first. Whetstone along the edge in long, even strokes, the rhythm of it steadying her the way prayer was supposed to and never had. Her sergeant, Doss, watched from across the brazier with the particular patience of a man who had followed her for eleven years and learned that questions came cheaper after the whetstone was put away.

"Bad news, Captain?"

"New news." She turned the blade. Firelight ran along it like water. "The heir's to be taken alive."

Doss frowned. "That's good, isn't it? Fewer funerals."

"That's what I keep telling myself."

He waited. He was good at waiting. That was half of why she kept him.

"The villages we burned," she said. "Thornfell. Harrow's Bend. The one up past the slate quarry whose name I never learned because there wasn't anyone left to say it." The whetstone paused. "If the order had come a month earlier, those people would be alive. If it comes again next month, whoever's left will die anyway. So either the Warden changed his mind about what the child is worth—" she set the stone down, "—or he never wanted them dead at all, and everything we did was theater."

Doss looked into the fire. "Which do you think it is?"

"I think," Corhane said, "that I've stopped being able to tell the difference between serving the realm and being used by it, and that frightens me more than any heretic ever has."

* * *

She had joined the Inquisition at sixteen, which everyone said was young until they heard why.

Her father had been a free-city magistrate who took bribes from slavers and called it commerce. Her mother had died of a fever that a working physic could have cured for three coppers, in a city where the physic guilds were owned by the same men who owned her father. Vess had stood at the funeral between two men in silk who spoke about her mother in the past tense as if she'd been a shipment gone astray, and something in her had closed like a door.

The Inquisition, at least, pretended its lies were truths. It gave her a code instead of a contract. It told her: *the strong protect the weak or they are nothing.* For twenty years she had believed it hard enough to make it true, most days.

Most days.

She walked the perimeter of the camp that evening, alone, because captains who couldn't be seen walking alone stopped being trusted with anything else. Below the ridge, Thornfell smoked still—three weeks burned and the peat underneath wouldn't stop smoldering, as if the ground itself were trying to cough the atrocity back up.

A girl had run out of the burning chapel at Harrow's Bend. Vess remembered her with the terrible clarity she reserved for things she would carry to her grave: maybe eight years old, nightdress, bare feet, running not away from the soldiers but toward the tree line where someone small was already hiding. One of her men had loosed an arrow without orders. Vess had reached him before the second arrow, broken his bow across her knee, and put him on report—and the girl had still gone down, and the small shape in the trees had still screamed, and Vess had stood in the smoke holding a broken bow and understood that putting a man on report was what justice looked like when you bought it wholesale.

The report had been filed. The man had been fined a week's pay. The girl was ash now, presumably, because the order had said *cleansed*, and Vess had enforced the order, because that was the whole architecture of her life: enforce the order, file the grief, sharpen the blade, begin again.

And now: *alive.*

She went back to her tent and unrolled the second document—the private one, the one that had come under separate seal, addressed not to Captain Corhane of the Inquisition but to *Vess*, in handwriting she hadn't seen since her oath-day. The Warden's own hand. Malrec wrote his orders through clerks; this had come direct.

*Captain. You have served with distinction and without question, which is precisely why I send this. The heir must reach me breathing and whole. You will hear things in the field—rumors, whispers from the mountain folk, perhaps from your own officers. Disregard them. What is done at Ironhollow will be done for the good of every soul in the free cities. History will thank us. —M.*

*History will thank us.*

No one in history had ever thanked anyone, in her experience. History thanked nobody. History just buried people and grew grass over them.

* * *

The whisper came on the fourth day, from exactly the direction Malrec had predicted, which was itself a kind of answer.

A peddler taken at the pass checkpoint—a Ferro Askan man, by the cut of his coat, which meant he'd already given up anything worth giving before Vess reached him—babbled about the gatekeeper of Ironhollow. About the keeper who'd kept the ledgers for ten years, quiet as snowfall, taking no side, owing no one. About how the keeper had gone into the mountains with a child. About how the child spoke Old Vaelish like it was milk instead of blood.

"And the mark?" Vess asked. She kept her voice flat. Interrogation was mostly tone management; the tools were for the ones who didn't respond to tone.

The peddler shook so hard his chains sang. "Broken crown, lady. On the throat, like a—like a brand, but born there. The smugglers are saying it's the lost heir of Vael, the real one, and that the keeper's carrying them to Marrowgate—"

Vess signaled, and Doss took the man away to be held, humanely, because whatever else she was, she was going to be precise about what she was.

Alone in her map tent, she weighted the corners of a chart of the high passes and thought about geometry. Marrowgate lay nine days east through smuggler country. Ironhollow lay six days north, if you knew the old roads, and she knew the old roads better than anyone in the column, because she'd spent two years of her early commission mapping them for a Warden who had praised her thoroughness and never once explained what the maps were for.

Six days north. Nine days east. And somewhere between them, a tired gatekeeper and a child who had already survived one burning.

She should take the direct route. Intercept. That was the mission.

Instead she found herself doing arithmetic of a different kind. *Bring the heir alive to the Hand of the Warden at Ironhollow.* Not to the capital. Not to the council of the free cities, which still technically outranked the Inquisition on paper. To Ironhollow—to the one sealed door in the world, the door her own survey maps had been quietly cataloguing for years. Every wardstone. Every approach. Every thickness of the ancient walls.

Why did a man need royal blood delivered to a door?

She pulled her old surveys out of the campaign chest and spread them under the lamp, and for the first time she actually looked at them—not as routes but as a text. The annotations weren't hers. They'd been added later, in the clerk-hand of the Inquisition's archive: measurements of the gate's inner chamber. Soundings of the hollow beneath. A note, in ink darker than the rest, that she had never let herself read closely:

*Chamber capacity, per Blackwood ledger (seized 214): sufficient.*

Sufficient for what? She'd assumed grain. Garrison stores. Some bureaucratic nothing.

Now, with *alive, unbled, unmarked* ringing in her ears, she read it again and felt the floor of her certainty tilt.

*Unbled.*

Who writes *unbled* unless bleeding is on the table?

* * *

"You're going to hate this," Doss said, ducking into the tent without knocking, which only he was permitted to do. "Scout came back off the eastern ridgeline. Says he found a campsite. Cold, two days old. Small fire, banked proper—one person built it who knows fire. But there were boot prints, and beside them—" he hesitated, "—small ones. Bare feet, wrapped in rag. Someone made the kid wraps from their own shirt, looks like."

Corhane looked down at the maps. Six days north. Nine days east.

"They're not going to Marrowgate," she said slowly. "They're coming home. To the gate."

"Why would they do that? The whole valley's crawling with us."

"Because the keeper's a Blackwood," she said, "and Blackwoods don't run from that door. Ask anyone." She stood. "Sound the column. We ride for Ironhollow."

Doss lingered. "Captain—if we catch them. The alive order. What happens to the child after we hand them over?"

It was the question she'd been circling for six days, and she still didn't have an answer she could say out loud, so she said the truest thing adjacent to it.

"I don't know, Sergeant. But I know this much—I'm going to be standing there when it happens. Whatever it is. I'm done delivering people into rooms I don't look inside."

Doss nodded once, the way soldiers nod when they understand more than their officers want them to, and went to sound the column.

* * *

They rode north through country that hated them, and Vess rode at the front where the hatred landed first.

On the second day they passed a shrine at a crossroads—old, pre-Imperial, its carved figures worn faceless by weather. Someone had hung fresh offerings on it recently: bread, a copper token on a string, a child's shoe. Offerings for safe passage, or for the dead, or both. One of her troopers reached for the bread, and Vess said, "Leave it," in a voice that surprised her with its sharpness, and the troop rode on, and she did not explain to herself or anyone else why her eyes had stung.

That night she dreamed of Harrow's Bend. She dreamed of the girl in the nightdress running toward the trees, and in the dream Vess finally caught her, knelt, held her—and the girl turned around and had the broken-crown mark on her throat, and said, in a language Vess somehow understood without knowing it: *You're late.*

She woke with her hand on her sword hilt and the camp silent around her, and she lay looking at the stars through the tent flap and admitted it, finally, in the privacy of her own skull:

*I don't think I'm saving this child. I think I'm retrieving an ingredient.*

The thought should have broken her. Instead it clarified her, the way cold water clarifies. Because if she was retrieving an ingredient, then the question was no longer whether she was a good soldier. The question was what the recipe was for—and she was close enough to Ironhollow now, close enough to smell the old stone on the wind, that she could go and find out.

Malrec had sent her because she was loyal and thorough and asked nothing. Fine. Loyalty and thoroughness worked in both directions. She would deliver the heir alive, exactly as ordered—and she would stand in that chamber, and she would see with her own eyes what the blood was for, and if it was what the whispers said, if it was what the dark note in her own survey margins implied—

Then she would decide. For the first time in twenty years, she would decide.

* * *

Ironhollow rose out of the dawn mist on the fifth day, and it was wrong.

Not besieged-wrong. Emptied-wrong. The gatehouse stood open—not the great gate itself, which stayed shut behind its seals, but the gatehouse doors, swinging loose. No smoke from the chimney. No keeper in the yard. The ledgers' trade post shuttered, the smuggler toll-stones unattended.

And on the gatehouse door, nailed up where any rider would see it, a sheet of ledger paper, covered in the neat, patient hand of a person who had written ten years of records no one read.

Vess dismounted and read it while her column waited in the road.

*To whoever comes with warrants: There is no heir at Ironhollow. There is only a child who was starving, whom I fed, and who deserves what none of us got—which is a choice. I have taken the mountain road. If you burn another village looking for them, know that I kept every ledger my family ever wrote, and I have begun reading the ones with pages missing. Come to the gate yourself, Captain, if you want answers. The key fits more than one lock. —L. Blackwood, Keeper*

Below the signature, in different ink, smaller, almost shy:

*P.S. The child says thank you for the boots.*

Vess stood a long time with the paper in her hands.

The child said thank you for the boots. Somewhere on a mountain road right now, a barefoot eight-year-old was walking in boots given by the last of the family that had locked their ancestors behind a door—and Vess's own orders were to drag that child, alive and unbled, to that same door, for purposes her own maps whispered about in the dark.

"Captain?" Doss, at her shoulder. "Orders?"

She looked up at the gate—the iron-bound, three-hundred-years-sealed heart of the world, humming faintly, she realized, the way a struck bell hums, the way it had hummed in her dreams since Harrow's Bend.

"We make camp below the wall," she said. "Nobody touches the gatehouse. Nobody burns anything." She folded the letter into her breastplate, over her heart, where she kept the other things she wasn't ready to read. "And Sergeant—send a rider back down the pass. Quietly. I want to know everything the smugglers know about a tunnel road under Thornfell."

Doss blinked. "Under Thornfell? That's not on our route."

"No," Vess said, turning toward the mountain, toward the humming door, toward whatever waited behind it wearing her Warden's face. "But I'm starting to think it's on mine."
