## 13. Night of Knives

I woke to the sound of Sef breathing wrong.

Not crying. Not talking. Just a small, hitching rhythm from the pallet beside mine in the council-guest room, like a bellows with a torn seam. I'd learned the child's sounds over weeks of shared road—the wet snore of exhausted sleep, the mutter of Old Vaelish dreams. This was new.

Then I heard the footsteps on the stairs.

Three sets. Light. Deliberate. The Marrowgate council had posted no guards outside our door—Dame Ilvane's faction had promised us safety within these walls, and promises in this city were currency spent carefully. But someone was climbing toward the third floor at the hour when even smugglers slept, and they were not trying to be quiet enough to fail.

"Sef." I put my hand over the child's mouth before the eyes opened wide, before any cry could come. "Quiet now. It's me."

The dark eyes above my fingers went from terror to recognition and back to something harder—Sef deciding, again, whether to trust me. I'd seen that decision made a hundred times since Thornfell's ash. Each time it cost more.

"Listen," I whispered. "We're going to move fast and we're going to be quiet, and if I say run, you run and you don't look back. Do you remember the cistern stair? Where we came in?"

A nod against my palm.

"Good." I took up my pack—I'd kept it packed for eleven days, ever since Councilor Veyne had smiled too warmly at Sef across the argument table—and pulled the child off the pallet. Through the shutter slats, Marrowgate lay below in its usual sprawl of lamplight: the refugee terraces, the salt-market, the harbor where Ferro Askan's ships rode at anchor. And beyond the walls, somewhere in the hills, the gray cloaks of Corhane's inquisition, patient as winter.

The footsteps reached the landing.

There was no second exit. There never is, until you need one. So I did the only thing left and blew out the lamp, wedged myself against the wall beside the door, and waited with a fire iron in my hand.

The lock didn't turn. It simply broke—oil on the hinges first, then one hard shoulder, wood splitting along the grain. A shape poured through the doorway, hooded, knife low, moving for the bed where Sef should have been sleeping.

I hit him across the wrist. Something cracked—the fire iron or his arm, I couldn't tell in the dark—and he made a sound like a kicked dog. The second man came through behind him faster than I expected, and then I was fighting in earnest, which is to say I was losing, because gatekeepers fight seals and weather and boredom, not men with knives.

He got me by the collar and turned me into the wall. I saw, very close, the little ash-gray badge stitched inside his hood. Not inquisitor gray. Marrowgate gray. The free cities' own colors.

*Sef*, I thought, absurdly, *run*, but what came out of my mouth was just air—

And then the second man folded sideways, all at once, like a puppet with cut strings, and Vess Corhane stepped over him into the room.

She was out of uniform. Plain traveler's leathers, hair bound back, an inquisitor's short blade in her hand already red to the hilt. Her face in the lamplight spilling up the broken stair was the same as I remembered from Ironhollow: courteous, unreadable, tired around the eyes in a way that had nothing to do with sleeplessness.

"Captain," I said, because my mouth does stupid things under pressure.

"Not tonight." She kicked the first man's knife away and stood over him. "Councilor Veyne's people. Yours too, probably—the ones who want the coronation tomorrow instead of next month. They mean to crown the child tonight, in front of witnesses, before anyone can vote otherwise." She looked at me. "Where were you keeping them?"

I pointed. Sef stood pressed into the corner shadows, small hands fisted, and I watched Corhane take in the birthmark, the fear, the age of the thing she'd been hunting across half a mountain range. I watched her decide something.

"They sent four," she said. "Two down, one running, one—" she tilted her head toward the window, listening, "—still coming up the outside wall. We leave now or we don't leave at all."

"Why?" It was the only question I had room for. "You've been hunting this child since Thornfell. Your orders—"

"My orders are to bring the heir to Malrec alive." She said it flatly, without shame, which somehow made it worse. "And I'm going to disobey them. Because I've read what the ritual requires, Blackwood, and Malrec doesn't want a crowned heir. He wants royal blood in a working that will seal your gate permanently—with everything still alive on the far side of it entombed behind the stone. Forever." Her jaw tightened. "I burn villages. I don't build tombs. There's a line, and I found it late, but I found it."

Outside the window, a handhook scraped against stone.

Corhane moved like water finding a drain—one step, one throw, and the climbing assassin dropped past the shutters with a scream that cut off at the flagstones. She turned back to me, breathing hard, offering nothing in her own defense.

"I can get us out through the salt-market. Askan keeps a warehouse there; your debt will buy one night's hiding, maybe two. After that we're fugitives to everyone—to Malrec, to the council, to every exile in this city who wants a war more than they want a child." She sheathed her blade. "Or you can stay here, trust Dame Ilvane, and see how gentle a forced coronation is. Choose fast."

Sef looked at me. That was the whole choice, really. Whatever I decided, the child would follow it, because ten years of gatekeeping had taught me exactly one skill: being the person people obeyed when the alternative was worse.

"We go," I said.

* * *

Marrowgate at night is a maze built by smugglers and improved by refugees, which means it is a maze designed to confuse soldiers and tax collectors and no one else. Corhane moved through it like she'd been born in it—down a laundry chute, across three rooftops with rotten tiles she somehow always avoided, through a fish-cellar whose stench alone could have stopped a cavalry charge.

Sef ran in silence beside me, hand locked in mine, and I thought about the last time I'd held that hand—on the mountain road, teaching the child which stones hold weight and which lie about it. Everything since then had happened to us. Tonight was the first time we were choosing anything, and we were choosing it while running from people who called themselves liberators.

At the mouth of the salt-market alley, Corhane stopped so abruptly I walked into her.

Torches ahead. A dozen figures in the open square between us and Askan's warehouse, arranging themselves with the unhurried competence of professionals who know their prey has only one exit. At their center stood a woman in exile gray with a chain of office over her shoulders, and I knew her voice before I placed her face.

Councilor Veyne. The one who wanted to march on Malrec by spring.

"Gatekeeper." Her voice carried pleasantly across the square. "Bring the heir out. No one needs to be hurt. The child will be crowned at dawn, properly, with the army behind them—and Vael will have its vengeance before winter. Isn't that what you want? You've carried them all this way."

"What I want," I called back, "is for everyone to stop deciding what's best for a nine-year-old."

"The child is not a child. The child is a claim." She spread her hands, reasonable, monstrous. "Claims must be exercised or they rot. Come out, Lior Blackwood. Your family has hidden behind that gate long enough."

Beside me, Sef's grip went rigid. I felt the child start to tremble—not with fear. With fury. Three centuries of dead language lived in that small body somewhere, and for one terrible moment I thought Sef might actually answer her.

Instead, Corhane grabbed us both and shoved us sideways through a doorway I hadn't even registered—a gap in the alley wall barely wider than a coffin, breathing cold air and old smoke.

"Smuggler tunnels," she breathed. "Under the market. Askan showed me once, years ago, when she was selling me information instead of dodging me. Don't ask why I know. Move."

* * *

The tunnels beneath Marrowgate are older than the free cities themselves—Vaeli work, the guides say, though no one says it loudly. Wardstone pillars held up the ceilings at intervals, and as Sef passed each one, it woke. A faint blue pulse, a hum like a struck glass, light following the child down the dark like a procession of lantern-bearers.

Corhane stared at the first one too long. Nearly tripped over a root.

"That's not possible," she said. "Those wards have been dead for three hundred years. My order's records—"

"Your order's records are wrong about a great many things," I said. "So are mine. So is everyone's."

We ran. Behind us, torchlight spilled into the tunnel mouths and voices echoed—angry, then confused, then receding as the passages branched in ways no pursuer could track without a Vaeli-born guide. Once, far off, I heard Veyne shouting orders that dissolved into the stone.

Then, ahead, a barred gate of black iron, and behind it a lantern, and behind the lantern the broad, scarred, unimpressed face of Ferro Askan.

She looked at the three of us—at the inquisitor captain, at the marked child glowing faintly in ward-light, at me bleeding from a split eyebrow—and she laughed, low and genuine.

"Well," she said, unlocking the gate. "Now *this* is worth the toll."

* * *

Her warehouse back room smelled of tar and pepper bales. She fed us salt pork and hot wine and listened to the whole story without interrupting, which frightened me more than the knives had. Ferro Askan interrupts people for a living.

"So," she said at last, setting down her cup. "The council wants a war. The usurper wants a tomb. The inquisitors—" she nodded at Corhane, "—want whatever Malrec told them to want, except this one's developed a conscience, which is going to be awkward for her pension." She leaned forward. "And you, keeper. What do you want?"

I looked at Sef, asleep at last against my side, one hand still curled around the copper token that had started all of this.

"I want to know the truth about my family," I said. "All of it. Before anyone else decides what to do with it."

Askan smiled like a knife being drawn slowly. "Then you're in luck. Because there's a scriptorium in the Tallow District that buys secrets from my caravans, and I happen to know they keep a certain Blackwood ledger-page that your ancestors paid good money to have disappear." Her eyes glittered. "Shall we talk price?"

Outside, the bells of Marrowgate began to toll the hour, and somewhere beyond the walls, in the dark hills, the armies were already moving.
