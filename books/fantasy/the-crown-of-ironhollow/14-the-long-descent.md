## 14. The Long Descent

**Lior**

The first thing the mountains took was the road.

We climbed out of Marrowgate through a smuggler's cut Ferro Askan had once described to me, drunk and laughing, as "the way you run when everyone's bought." She hadn't been laughing about it now. She'd pressed a strip of oiled leather into my hand at the city gate—a map with half its lines drawn in charcoal, half in memory—and told me not to come back.

"Debts are done," she said. "Whatever you're carrying, don't carry it here."

What I was carrying was a child who knelt for crowds, an inquisitor who'd turned her sword against her own soldiers, and a secret that could unmake two nations. I didn't tell her that. Some debts you pay by leaving.

Behind us, Marrowgate's bells rang wrong—fast, arrhythmic, the alarm pattern. Ahead of us, the pass went up into cloud like a throat going up into silence.

Sef walked between us without being asked. That was the strange part. Three days ago the child flinched when Corhane's shadow crossed their path; now they matched her stride on the scree as if they'd rehearsed it.

Corhane set the pace. Of course she did. She knew these highlands from the hunting side, which meant she knew every place a fugitive breaks cover, and so we never did. She moved us by night and hid us by day, and she did it without once saying *I used to do this to people like you*, though the sentence hung in the thin air anyway, unsaid, every morning.

On the third day we ate the last of the hardtack. On the fourth, Sef caught a marmot with a snare made from my bootlace, using a technique no eight-year-old should know, and when I asked where they'd learned it, Sef said only: "Before."

I stopped asking after that.

* * *

**Corhane**

I have hunted fugitives across eleven ranges and I will tell you what none of the manuals say: you do not catch them by being faster. You catch them by being more patient than their hunger.

So I watched them eat the last bread and said nothing. I watched the Blackwood give the child the larger share and take the smaller, and I noted it—not as weakness, as the manuals would have it, but as data. People who starve themselves for others make predictable choices at forks in the road. They choose the route with water over the route with cover. They stop to bury things.

And I watched the child watch me. That was new. In Thornfell, in the villages, they had looked at my gray cloak the way livestock looks at weather. This one looked at me the way a locksmith looks at another locksmith's hands.

"You were at Thornfell," Sef said, on the fifth morning, while Nakamura slept. Not accusing. Just placing the fact down between us like a stone.

"Yes."

"Did you burn it?"

"No. I arrived after. I read the ashes." I kept my voice level because a level voice is the only honesty I can afford. "I reported what I found. My superiors filed it as *acceptable losses*."

"What's that mean?"

"It means someone wrote those words instead of my words."

The child considered this with a gravity that made my chest hurt, then nodded once, the way a magistrate nods, and went back to whittling a stick into something. Later I saw it was a tiny door. They gave it to Nakamura without explanation, and Nakamura held it too long, turning it over like a coin from a country that no longer existed.

I had orders, once. Bring the heir alive. The heir's blood for the binding. I had believed—I still half believe—that sealing the gate forever was mercy, that entombing whatever waited behind it was kinder than war. Then I read Malrec's dispatches, and I learned what *forever* meant, and whose signature was at the bottom of the working.

Now both armies wanted us. The usurper wanted the child alive. The council wanted the child crowned or dead, and had sent knives to settle which. We were three people on a mountain with every flag in the world pointed at us, and the only law left was the one I'd spent my career enforcing from the other direction:

Keep moving. Trust no one. Except—

Except the Blackwood had stood between me and an assassin's blade in Marrowgate without hesitating, and I had not yet decided what to do with that fact. So I carried it, like everything else, uphill.

* * *

**Lior**

The avalanche came on the sixth day, and it saved our lives.

I'll explain that. We were crossing a bowl beneath a cornice on the northern shoulder of the Grey Teeth—the map said safe, but maps are written by survivors—and Sef stopped walking. Stopped dead, in the middle of the slope, and cocked their head the way they did near wardstones.

"The mountain's humming," they said.

"Sef, keep moving—"

"It's humming *wrong*."

Corhane understood before I did. She'd heard avalanches start; she grabbed us both and hauled us toward the rock ribs on the east side, and thirty seconds later the cornice let go with a sound like the sky clearing its throat, and the world became white noise and white death and the taste of snow in your teeth even through a scarf.

When it settled, the slope we'd been climbing was gone. So was the trail. So was the smoke from our fire, our tracks, every sign of us that either army could follow.

Buried under forty feet of powder, somewhere below, lay our pursuers' certainty.

We stood in the ringing quiet afterward. Sef was shaking—not fear, I realized. Rage. Small fists clenched, eyes wet, glaring at the mountain as if it had interrupted something.

"I *heard* it," they whispered. "It was singing and then it screamed and nobody listened. Nobody ever listens until things fall down."

Corhane crouched to their height, which cost her something visibly—her knees, her pride, I couldn't say. "You listen," she said. "That's why you're alive."

Sef looked at her for a long moment. Then, wordlessly, handed her the second snared marmot of the day.

She ate it. Inquisitors don't accept food from Vaeli children. She ate it anyway, and something in the geometry of the three of us quietly rearranged itself, and none of us mentioned it, because mentioning it might have broken it.

* * *

**Sef**

The cave had old carvings and Lior couldn't read them and Corhane pretended not to look at them, but Sef could read them the way you can read your own name written in frost on a window. Not remembering. Knowing.

*THE DOOR IS NOT THE ENEMY,* said the carving. *THE HUNGER BEHIND THE DOOR IS.*

Sef traced the letters with a cold finger. Behind them, the tall keeper was boiling snow in a dented pot, and the gray-cloak was rewrapping her hands where the rope burn from Marrowgate had opened again, and the fire was small and good and the wind outside was enormous, and Sef thought: *these are my people now.* And immediately thought: *don't think that. Things you love get taken.* Thornfell. Mama. The copper token warm in their pocket like a small sleeping animal.

But the thought wouldn't go back in its box.

"Lior," they said. The keeper's face came around, firelight catching the tired lines of it. "Is home a place or a promise?"

The spoon stopped stirring. For a long time there was only the wind and the water sounds.

"My father said home was a duty," Lior said finally. "He stood at a gate his whole life for a thing he never explained, and he called that home, and I think—" A breath. "I think he was lying to himself, or I'm lying to myself, one of the two. Because when I picture home I don't picture the gatehouse. I picture this. A fire. Boiling snow. Two people who shouldn't be anywhere near me."

"Three," said Corhane, without looking up from her bandages.

"Three," Lior agreed, very quietly, as if the number might break.

Sef thought about promises. Promises were things grown-ups said and then the village burned anyway. But places burned too. Maybe that was the answer: both of them failed unless somebody stayed. Home wasn't the fire or the promise. It was whoever was still there in the morning, boiling snow, passing you the bigger share.

"That's a bad plan," Sef announced, because children are allowed to be honest. "Home should be safer than a cave."

Lior laughed—an actual laugh, cracked and rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in years. Corhane almost smiled. Almost. On an inquisitor that was practically a festival.

* * *

**Corhane**

We came down out of the high passes on the eleventh day, starving, half-frozen, and alive, and the first thing we saw was the valley of Ironhollow—and the second thing we saw was the fires.

Not the gatehouse. Not yet. The fires were on the approach roads, ringed and orderly. Siege camps. Two of them, facing each other across the valley like duelists: the black-and-gold of Malrec's regulars on the southern road, and the ash-gray banners of the exile council's hardliners on the north.

Both armies had beaten us to the door.

I put the glass to my eye and swept the camps, and my stomach did what stomachs do. The southern camp flew the Marshal's standard—but at its center stood a command pavilion far too fine for a vanguard, hung with white lanterns, and white lanterns meant one thing in inquisitorial protocol.

Malrec was there himself.

"He came in person," I said. "Why would he—"

And then I understood, all at once, the way you understand a trap only after your foot is in it. He hadn't come for the child. The child was a component. He'd come for the gate. For the ritual. For the tomb he meant to build out of a kingdom, with himself as its immortal warden, and every army, every council, every burning village had just been him clearing his way to the door.

Nakamura was looking at the valley too, and I watched ten years of gatekeeping settle onto their face like armor—or like a shroud.

"They're digging at the seals already," Nakamura said. "Look. South road, the sappers' lines. He knows the gate. He knows what's buried there better than anyone alive except—"

They stopped. Their hand went to their coat, to the shape hidden inside it. The key-half. The Blackwood half. The thing their family had hoarded for three hundred years while people burned for the other half.

"Nakamura," I said. I almost never used their name. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking," they said, "that I've spent my whole life keeping a door shut, and I'm the only person on this mountain who knows how to open it." They turned to Sef, and the child looked up at them with those terrible old eyes. "And I'm thinking it's time somebody knocked."

Below us, in the southern camp, the white lanterns began to move.
