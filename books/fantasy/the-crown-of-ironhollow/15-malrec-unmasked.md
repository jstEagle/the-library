## 15. Malrec Unmasked

The dispatch came in with the snow.

Vess Corhane read it three times by lamplight in a waystation above the Thornfell pass, her gloves still stiff with frost, her escort of six soldiers asleep or pretending to be in the loft above. The courier who'd carried it was dead—found frozen two miles back down the trail, a gray cloak and a cipher tube and nothing else worth taking. She'd broken the seal herself because she no longer trusted anyone else's hands.

*By order of the Radiant Throne: the vessel is to be delivered living and unmarked to the Ironhollow gate upon the night of the Long Dark. The Rite of Sealing requires royal blood freely flowing. Casualties among the vessel's protectors are authorized but not preferred. The Warden-to-Be will attend in person.*

She read it a fourth time anyway. Some sentences don't improve with repetition; they just get heavier.

*Royal blood freely flowing.*

She had told herself a great many comfortable things over the past season. That the heir was to be taken alive for ransom, or hostage exchange, or some political theater Malrec needed to legitimize his throne. That the child would be frightened but unharmed. That Corhane was a soldier doing a soldier's work in an ugly world, and that ugly work done cleanly was its own kind of mercy.

*Rite of Sealing.* She knew what that phrase meant. Every inquisitor did. It was in the oldest training manuals, the ones bound in black leather that you were allowed to look at exactly once, under supervision, before they were locked away again. Three hundred years ago, when Vael fell, the first Sealing had been performed at Ironhollow—a working so vast it had swallowed a kingdom whole, turned every road and pass into stone and silence, buried a nation behind a door that did not open. The manuals said the rite required a king's blood. The manuals said it had only ever been performed once, because no one sane would perform it twice.

The dispatch said Malrec intended to perform it *again*. Permanently this time. And he wanted the last living heir of Vael bled for it on the threshold of her own kingdom.

Corhane set the paper down. Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself the way you notice weather—distantly, without approval.

Above her, one of the soldiers shifted on the loft boards. A boot creaked. She slid the dispatch into her coat, against her ribs, where the cold couldn't get at it, and banked the lamp low.

Sleep didn't come. Instead she did what she always did instead of sleeping: she took out her little book of questions—the one she'd kept since her novitiate, the one that would have gotten her flogged if any superior had ever found it—and added a line by memory in the dark.

*Who is the Warden-to-Be?*

Because that was the part that didn't fit. Kings attended coronations. Kings did not attend *sealings*, unless the sealing was the coronation. Unless whatever Malrec meant to make of himself on the night of the Long Dark was something no word in the common tongue quite covered.

* * *

The answer arrived four days later, and it arrived the way answers always did in Corhane's experience: wrapped in someone else's mistake.

They'd caught a messenger on the Marrowgate road—not one of theirs, not one of the exile council's either, but a free-lance courier running sealed tubes for coin, the kind of gray-market post that served every faction in the mountains and asked no questions. Corhane's sergeant, Doss, wanted to hang him. Corhane wanted the tubes. They compromised: the courier gave up the tubes and got to keep his life and his horse, minus the horse's shoes, which Doss pulled as a matter of professional pride.

There were three tubes. Two were routine—garrison rotations, grain requisitions, the dull machinery of occupation. The third was written in a hand Corhane recognized, because she'd spent eleven years copying it in the scriptoriums of the Inquisition: the small, precise, joyless letters of Chancellor Iven Marek, Malrec's spymaster, addressed not to a garrison commander but to a name she had never seen in any dispatch before.

*To the Custodian of the Deep Archive, by hand only.*

She should have forwarded it unread. Protocol demanded it. Eleven years of service demanded it. She broke the wax in the lee of a rock wall while Doss pretended very hard to be checking the horses.

*Brother Custodian—*

*Your inquiry regarding the provenance of His Radiance is noted, and your discretion is commended even as your curiosity is corrected. For the record, then, and let this be the last word on the subject:*

*His Radiance is not of the old blood of Vael, nor of the free cities' merchant stock, as the popular accounts hold. He is descended of the line of Aldric Blackwood, gate-warden and betrayer, whose treachery purchased the family's elevation when the kingdom fell. The Blackwoods were rewarded with wardenship, wealth, and erasure from the histories of their own crime. One branch kept the gate. Another branch kept the gold. Ours kept the ambition.*

*You will understand now why His Radiance does not fear the heir. Royal blood is required for the Rite, yes—but it is the blood of the betrayer that must command it. Only the hand that closed the door can hold it shut forever. On the night of the Long Dark, His Radiance will stand upon the threshold of Ironhollow as Aldric's heir, spill the heir of Vael across the seals, and step through the working into the office prepared for him: Warden Eternal, keeper of the tomb, deathless for as long as the door endures.*

*He does not mean to rule the world, Brother. He means to outlast it.*

*Burn this.*

Corhane read it twice. Then she sat down on the rock wall, which was not like her, and looked out at the white valley and the dark thread of the road, and felt the entire architecture of her life quietly rearrange itself around a new load-bearing fact.

Everyone had been fighting over a grave.

* * *

She thought about Thornfell.

She had been there after. Everyone in the service had been there somewhere after—after some village, some valley, some smoking ruin that the dispatches called a security action and the survivors called by its name. Thornfell had been hers. She'd walked the ash-line where the houses had been, counting foundations the way you count coffins when there are no coffins, and she had found, half-buried near the well, a child's wooden toy: a little carved gate, the kind mountain folk made because the gate at Ironhollow was the only wonder their world contained.

She'd reported it as evidence of Vaeli sympathies. That was the phrase. *Evidence of sympathies.* She had been proud of how clean her report was.

Now she stood in the snow above the same valley and understood, with the slow sick certainty of a woman reading her own autopsy, what the burning had actually been for. Not security. Not loyalty. Malrec hadn't been hunting the heir because the heir threatened his throne.

He'd been hunting the heir because the heir was *material*. Blood for a ritual. Fuel for a machine meant to run forever. And the villages, the ash, the toy in the cinders—all of it, every gray-cloaked rider and every burned roof from here to the free cities—was just the sound of a man clearing his throat before he spoke the only sentence that mattered to him.

*I will never die, and none of them will ever leave.*

Because that was the other thing, wasn't it. The thing the dispatches danced around and the archive letter said outright. The Rite of Sealing didn't just close the gate. It entombed. Whatever was behind Ironhollow—refugees, survivors, a people sealed in by fear three centuries ago, alive or dead or something between—would stay there. Forever. Under a warden who could not die, could not be relieved, could not be argued with, because he would *be* the door.

And the free cities would cheer. That was the worst of it. The free cities, her cities, the cities she'd sworn the Inquisition protected—they would light lamps and sing songs about the mad heir finally put down and the haunted gate finally shut for good, and they would never know they were celebrating the largest grave in human history, dug by the descendant of the man who'd dug the first one.

"Captain." Doss, beside her, breath steaming. "You've been standing there a long time."

"I've been thinking."

"That's usually bad news for someone."

She almost smiled. Doss had been with her nine years. He'd followed her out of the scriptoriums into the field because, he said, someone honest ought to see what the honesty cost. He was the only person in the column she'd trust with a truth this size, and she was about to test that trust past all reason.

"Doss. If I told you the Lord Radiant intends to bury a nation alive behind Ironhollow, and make himself immortal doing it, what would you say?"

Doss considered this with the gravity he brought to everything, including boot inventory. "I'd say I'd want to see the paper."

She gave him the archive letter. She watched him read it—watched his jaw change, watched the moment the words *outlast it* landed—and when he finished he folded it along her creases, precisely, and handed it back.

"And the second one?" he said. "The one about the blood."

"You haven't seen that one."

"No, Captain. But you've had a face like a held breath since the courier died, and I can do arithmetic." He looked out at the valley. "Thornfell?"

"Thornfell."

He nodded slowly. "I burned a granary at Coldmere once. Requisition denial, the orders said. There were families living off that grain." He said it flatly, the way men say things they've decided never to feel again, and she recognized the technique because she'd invented half of it. "So. What are we doing, Captain?"

That was the question, wasn't it. She ran it the way she ran everything: options, costs, likelihoods.

Option one: forward both dispatches to Inquisition headquarters, request clarification, await orders. Cost: the clarification would come back signed by Marek, ordering her to proceed, and she'd have spent a week learning nothing except that the machinery worked exactly as designed. Likelihood of changing anything: nil.

Option two: deliver the heir to the gate as ordered, and find some way—any way—to sabotage the rite from inside. Cost: she'd be handing a child to an altar on the promise of her own cleverness. She had seen her own cleverness operate. It was good at reports.

Option three.

"The heir's protectors," she said. "The gatekeeper and the child. They went to ground in Marrowgate. The council there wants to crown the girl—or the boy, nobody seems sure and nobody's asked—which means marching a nation of exiles back through the passes to die on Malrec's walls. Or the hardliners want to kill the heir quietly and keep the war buried." She paused. "Neither of those is a rescue."

"And we're offering one?"

"We're offering the truth," Corhane said. "Which is more than anyone else in this war has offered anybody." She looked east, toward Marrowgate's smoke smudged faint against the horizon. "Malrec needs the heir alive and the gatekeeper dead or scattered. The council needs the heir crowned and me hanged. Both sides need the same child for opposite reasons, and neither side knows what the child is actually *for*." She tapped the archive letter against her palm. "But we know. We're the only ones who know the whole shape of it. That makes us the most dangerous people in these mountains, Doss, and I intend to spend it badly and probably die of it."

"That's the most encouraging briefing I've had in years," Doss said. "What's the play?"

"Simple version? We defect." She said it aloud and heard how insane it was and said it again, firmer. "We defect. Not to the council—they'd use us and crown the child and start the war, and the war ends at Ironhollow with Malrec getting exactly the battlefield he wants. We go to the gatekeeper. The Blackwood. Because there's a piece of this puzzle only a Blackwood can touch, and if the archive letter is true, then the gatekeeper's family built the lock Malrec means to turn." She met Doss's eyes. "And because the gatekeeper is the only person in this story who might still choose the child over the cause. Everyone else has already chosen."

Doss was quiet a moment. Snow ticked against their cloaks.

"The column won't follow us," he said. "Six soldiers, Captain. Maybe two."

"Two is enough to carry messages. Send the rest back down-valley with the routine dispatches and orders to winter at Coldmere. Anyone asks, we're scouting the high passes." She paused. "Anyone asks *carefully*, we're scouting the high passes."

"And if the gatekeeper shoots us on sight?"

"Then I'll die knowing I finally sent an honest report," Corhane said, "and it'll be the first one."

* * *

They moved at dusk, two riders on the high track, leaving the column's cookfires below like a handful of dropped coins. Corhane rode with both dispatches against her ribs and her little book of questions in her pocket, and somewhere outside Coldmere, in the blue hour between the sun going and the stars coming, she took the book out and crossed out the question she'd written four nights ago.

*Who is the Warden-to-Be?*

Underneath, she wrote the answer, small and exact:

*Aldric's heir. The betrayer's blood, come back to finish the bargain. He doesn't want the kingdom. He wants the door.*

And beneath that, because the book was hers and no superior would ever read it, one more line:

*Then the door is the battleground, and the gatekeeper holds the ground. Ride faster.*

Behind them, far to the south, the sky over the direction of Ironhollow glowed faintly—not firelight, not dawn, but something steadier, a pale pulse at the horizon like a heartbeat under skin. Doss saw it too and reined in.

"Captain. That light. Is that supposed to be there?"

Corhane looked at it for a long moment. She had grown up on stories of the gate, like everyone; the stories said the seals slept. The light on the horizon did not look like sleeping.

"No," she said. "And I think we have less time than the dispatches say. Ride."

They rode. Behind them the pulse brightened, patient as a held breath, and ahead the passes climbed toward the one door in the world where every secret in the war was about to arrive at once—carried by a hunter coming to beg shelter from her prey.
