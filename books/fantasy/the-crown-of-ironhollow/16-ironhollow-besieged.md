## 16. Ironhollow Besieged

They smelled the camp before they saw it.

Smoke, yes—every army smells of smoke—but under it something worse: latrines and wet wool and the sour tang of too many men living in too small a space. Lior had spent ten years learning every scent the valley offered, from pine resin to the mineral breath of snowmelt. This one was new. This one meant war.

"Down." Lior pulled Sef behind a shelf of granite as the trail bent toward the last overlook before Ironhollow. Corhane crouched on their other side, gray cloak pulled close, her face unreadable in the way of someone who had decided not to have one.

Below them, the valley floor was wrong.

Lior's valley had always been a study in emptiness—a green throat between two shoulders of mountain, with the gatehouse perched at its dead end like a stone swallow's nest. Now it was infested. Tents sprawled across the meadow Lior had once watched deer cross at dusk. Cookfires dotted the slope in disciplined rows. And at the far end, clustered against the cliff face beneath the gate itself, scaffolding climbed the rock like ivy made of murder.

Men were digging at the seals.

"They're fast," Sef whispered. The child had learned to whisper in Old Vaelish first and Common second, and both languages came out small when frightened. "Faster than Ferro said they'd be."

"Ferro said three days," Corhane said. Her voice stayed flat, but Lior had spent enough weeks beside her now to hear the hairline crack beneath it. Three days had been the margin. They'd made the passes in four, starving, half-frozen, driving each other up the mountain by turns—one carrying Sef, one carrying the other when the altitude took its toll—and the margin was gone. "She lied."

"She quoted a price," Lior said. "The lie was free."

Sef laughed, a single startled syllable, then clapped a hand over their mouth as if the sound might carry down to the tents. Maybe it did. Maybe the whole valley already knew they were coming. Malrec's vanguard hadn't dug through three hundred years of Blackwood seals by being careless about who walked the high trails.

Lior looked at the gatehouse—at *home*—and felt something twist that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite fear. The tower still stood. The walls still stood. But the great door itself, the door Lior's family had sealed and guarded and lied about for ten generations, was wrapped in scaffolding, and men with chisels and rope harnesses swarmed over it like flies on a wound. Even at this distance Lior could see the pale scars they'd already cut into the stone. Could see, or thought they could see, the faint shimmer where old wards bled light trying to hold what remained.

"They'll breach the outer seal within the week," Corhane said. She said it like a report because she was still, in whatever broken way, a soldier. "The inner ones will kill them. Then Malrec brings his ritual-casters, and we lose the only say any of us has left in what happens next."

"So we take the say back."

"With forty people?" Corhane glanced sideways. In the weeks since the Night of Knives—since she had turned her blade against her own soldiers to keep a child out of a tomb—the captain had developed a habit of looking at Lior when she wanted to say something cynical and stopping herself halfway. It was almost companionable. "I count three. One child, one disgraced keeper, one traitor. We are not an army."

"We don't need to be." Lior settled back against the granite and let the cold of it seep through their coat. "We need to be a debt."

* * *

Ferro Askan found them before they found her, which was how it always went with smuggler queens.

One moment the three of them were picking their way down a goat track toward the treeline above the gatehouse; the next, a hooded figure stepped out from behind a pine with a cocked crossbow and an expression of professional boredom, and then there were six more shadows peeling off the rocks, and then Ferro herself emerged from the middle of them like she'd been there all along and the rest were decoration.

"Well," she said. "The dead walk."

"Ferro."

"Lior Blackwood." She swept back her hood. Up close she looked exactly as she had in Marrowgate: silver-streaked hair cropped short, rings on eight fingers, eyes that weighed everything and paid for nothing. But thinner. There were new lines around her mouth, and Lior, who had spent a lifetime reading ledgers, read them easily. The last months had cost her. "You look terrible. All three of you. Starvation suits the child better than the adults—you've got that holy-waif glow. Very useful for fundraising."

"We need to get inside the gatehouse."

"Mm." Ferro turned and looked down at the valley, at the tents and the scaffolding and the men chiseling at the door Lior's family had died keeping shut. "And here I thought you'd come to apologize for selling my trade routes."

Lior didn't flinch. It had been the price, back in Marrowgate, on the night the council's knives came out: passage, boats, safe roads through territory no honest traveler could cross—and Ferro had named her fee. Not coin. Not secrets, though she collected those like other women collected jewels. She'd wanted the Blackwood ledger. Ten generations of recorded traffic through Ironhollow: which passes held smuggler tunnels, which lords took bribes, which caravans moved contraband and which moved worse. A map of a decade's worth of corruption, written in Lior's own careful hand.

"You knew why I needed it," Lior said.

"I knew why you *said* you needed it." Ferro's smile showed teeth. "There's a difference. There's always a difference. That's rather my trade."

Corhane shifted, hand drifting toward her sword, and Ferro's archers shifted with her, and for a moment the goat track was very quiet except for wind and the distant ringing of chisels on ancient stone.

Then Sef stepped forward.

The child stood between the armed adults with the particular gravity they'd developed since the antechamber, since the king beneath the door—since whatever had passed between Sef and the deathless remnant of Vael's last monarch in that long dark hour Lior still couldn't fully picture without their chest tightening. Something in Sef had been rearranged. They carried it lightly, the way children carry everything, but they carried it.

"Ferro Askan," Sef said. "You owe the road a toll."

"I owe the road nothing. The road owes *me*."

"The road remembers." Sef's voice had gone strange—not deep, not changed, but *placed*, each word set down like a stone on a cairn. "Every tunnel under Thornfell. Every wardstone that opened for us. You walked those roads your grandmother's grandmother walked when Vael was still sealed, and you never asked why the ways were kept open. Someone kept them open for the day the door would stand wide again. That day is close. Ask yourself who you want standing on the right side of it."

Silence again. Wind. Chisels.

Ferro looked at the child for a long time. Then she laughed—short, sharp, genuine—and rubbed a ringed finger across her mouth.

"Gods below. You sound just like the stories say the old kings sounded. No wonder Malrec is terrified of you." She dropped her hand. "Fine. Here's the truth, since everyone's so fond of truth lately. I'm not here for you, little heir. I'm not here out of love, or loyalty, or the stirring ballad we'll all pretend happened afterward. I'm here because Lior Blackwood paid in advance."

Lior frowned. "I paid for passage. Once. Already spent."

"You paid with routes, and routes are a currency that keeps paying." Ferro began walking down the trail, gesturing for them to follow, and her shadows melted into the trees ahead like water finding channels. "Your ledger told me which garrisons are bought, which granaries are full, which lords would sell their mothers for a share of the northern salt trade. Do you know what I did with that? I built a web. Half the free companies between here and Marrowgate owe me favors now, and half of *those* owe me because I called in favors owed to people whose names were in your ledger. You handed me a kingdom's worth of leverage, Blackwood. I've spent three months cashing it."

"And that bought you here?"

"That bought me *everything*." She glanced back, and for one unguarded instant Lior saw past the queen to the woman—the exhaustion, the calculation running hot beneath it. "Malrec's vanguard is four hundred strong. His main host is a week behind with another two thousand. Against that I have ninety smugglers, sixty mercenaries I bought with your routes, and a promise from two border lords to close the eastern pass if I deliver them the salt contracts. None of it is enough. All of it is what exists. So yes—it bought me here, and it will buy you your gatehouse, and when it's done you and I are going to sit down and renegotiate, because I am *owed*."

"Renegotiate," Corhane muttered. "With a smuggler queen. What could go wrong."

"Captain," Ferro said pleasantly, "I remember your name from certain dispatches. Would you like to discuss what else I remember?"

Corhane wisely said nothing.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, got drawn in dirt.

They gathered in a hollow below the treeline while Ferro's lieutenants filtered in—one-eyed Wren, who'd lost the eye to a customs officer's knife and considered it a fair trade; a huge, silent man called Brakka who communicated mostly in shrugs; a nervous boy named Tam who kept a tally-book like he was Ferro's accountant, which in a sense he was. Around them the afternoon dimmed toward evening, and below, the enemy camp kindled its cookfires one by one until the valley looked like a sky fallen into the grass.

"Four hundred men," Ferro said, scratching lines into the dirt with a dagger. "But not four hundred soldiers. Two hundred are diggers and laborers—engineers' crews, sappers, porters. Malrec wants the seals opened gently. He doesn't want rubble; he wants a working door. Which means his fighting strength is maybe one-eighty, camped in terrain they chose badly because they didn't expect resistance this far north."

"They didn't expect anyone," Lior said.

"No one expects the smugglers, Blackwood. That's the entire business model." The dagger tapped. "Here's the shape of it. Their pickets watch the south trail and the valley mouth—that's where an army comes from. Nobody watches the north face, because nobody in their right mind climbs the north face in thaw season."

"Nobody in their right mind," Corhane repeated.

"You've met us," Wren said, deadpan.

"Sappers are cutting at the third seal ring, here—" the dagger jabbed near the base of the gatehouse cliff, "—under a work-awning, lit through the night shift. If we can reach the gatehouse interior, we control the winch-house and the old guard-tunnel, which connects to the cisterns. From the cisterns, we can flood the lower works. Drowning men drop chisels. It's undignified but effective."

"And after?" Lior asked. "After we hold the gatehouse, we're besieged. Ninety of you, a hundred and fifty of them, and their main force coming up the road."

"After that," Ferro said, and smiled the smile she wore when the news was worst, "we hold until the eastern pass closes. Your border lords, Blackwood. Bought with salt. When Malrec's supply line snaps, a siege reverses direction very quickly. Sieges always do. Hunger is loyal to whoever's patient."

"Hunger is loyal to no one," Corhane said quietly. "It just takes orders."

For a moment nobody answered. Below them the chisels rang on, tiny and bright and ceaseless, eating at a door that had held for three hundred years.

Sef sat apart from the planning circle, knees drawn up, staring down at the gate. Lior went and sat beside the child. For a while neither spoke. That was one of the things the journey had taught them: silence could be shared without being surrendered.

"When we get inside," Sef said finally, "will you show me your father's room?"

Lior blinked. "My father's room?"

"The keeper's chamber. Where you slept when you were small." Sef picked at a thread on their sleeve. "In the deep halls, the king showed me... he showed me what the gate remembers. It remembers everyone who ever touched it. Including keepers. Including a small angry boy who talked to the door when he thought no one was listening."

Heat crawled up Lior's neck. Ten years old, alone in a tower at the roof of the world, telling the sealed door about their day because there was no one else. Of course the door remembered. Doors like that remembered everything.

"He also showed me what your ancestors did," Sef said, quieter. "What Aldric did. I know you know. I watched you read it in Marrowgate. I watched your face." The child looked up then, and their eyes were old in a way no child's should be. "I'm not saying it to hurt you. I'm saying it because when we go inside, I don't want either of us pretending. This isn't homecoming for me and it isn't duty for you anymore. It's a debt. Both our families owe it. We pay it together or not at all."

Lior looked at the child—this starved, burned-out-of-everything child who had somehow become the most honest person in a story full of liars—and nodded once.

"Together," Lior said. "Not at all otherwise."

"Good." Sef managed something that was almost a smile. "Also I'm hungry."

"There's dried mutton in my pack."

"There's *always* dried mutton in your pack. It's the saddest thing about you."

Despite everything—despite the campfires and the scaffolds and the week of starvation behind them and the battle ahead—Lior laughed. It came out rusty, like a winch long unused, but it came.

* * *

They went up the north face in the dark.

Rope and iron pitons and the smugglers' obscene competence at doing illegal things vertically. Lior climbed behind Wren, boots finding holds by touch, the valley dropping away below until the enemy fires looked like scattered coins. Sef climbed strapped to Brakka's back like a kit-bag, because some arguments weren't worth having. Corhane climbed like she'd been born to it, which, given her history, she probably had.

Twice the wind tried to peel them off the rock. Twice it failed.

Near midnight they crested the shoulder of the cliff above the gatehouse, and Lior saw their home from above for the first time in their life—from the outside, the way strangers saw it. Small. Stubborn. A fist of stone raised against the sky for three centuries. The scaffolding crawled up the great door below like something parasitic, and torchlight glimmered off the awning over the sappers' works, and men moved down there in the orange glow, small as ants, chewing at the foundations of the world.

Wren pressed close. "Winch-house first?"

"Winch-house first," Lior whispered.

They went down the inside of the tower on the old maintenance chains, hand over hand, breath held. Through a window slit Lior glimpsed the sentry walk—two guards, bored, backs to the wind. Ferro's people took them without a sound, and then the night split into motion: shadows flowing along walls, the cistern sluice grinding open, the soft terrible *thunk* of the flood-gates releasing water that hadn't moved in years.

Down at the cliff base, the screaming started.

The lower works flooded fast—Lior had grown up knowing exactly how fast, which galleries drained where, which crawlspaces drowned first. Men spilled out of the awning coughing and clawing, dropping tools, scrambling up scaffolds that groaned under sudden weight. Somewhere a horn blew, ragged and urgent. Fires toppled. The neat ant-city of the camp boiled awake into chaos, officers shouting formations into a darkness full of drowning sounds and moving shapes that seemed to come from nowhere, because smugglers were nowhere, smugglers were always nowhere until they were behind you.

Lior stood in the winch-house with Sef and Corhane and watched the valley burn with borrowed fires, and felt nothing like triumph. Felt, instead, the ledger-keeper's cold clarity: this bought days. Days, not victory. Somewhere down the south road, two thousand more men marched with ritual-casters among them, and Malrec himself would not be far behind.

Ferro appeared in the doorway, soot-streaked, a cut on her forearm she clearly hadn't noticed, and leaned against the frame.

"Gatehouse is ours," she reported. "Eastern pass closes tomorrow if my lords keep their word—they will; I have their sons' debts. We can hold maybe five days, six if we're cruel about rations." She looked at Lior, and the mercenary mask slipped just far enough to show the woman underneath, tired and sharp and oddly honest in the firelight. "You paid for this with a book, Blackwood. A book. I hope whatever's behind that door is worth what it costs to open it."

"It's people," Lior said. "Thousands of them. Alive. Sealed in for three hundred years."

Ferro stared. Then she laughed, low and disbelieving, and shook her head.

"People. Of course. The one cargo no one ever pays to move." She pushed off from the doorframe and headed back into the noise of the fight, calling orders to Wren. At the threshold she paused, without turning. "Five days, keeper. Make them count."

Beyond the burning camp, beyond the black shoulder of the southern mountains, thunder rolled—or something like thunder, too rhythmic, too steady.

Drums. An army on the march, coming to take back its grave.
