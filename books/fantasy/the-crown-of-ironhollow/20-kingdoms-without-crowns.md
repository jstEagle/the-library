## 20. Kingdoms Without Crowns

The treaty talks were held in the Marrowgate salt exchange, because it was the only hall in the free cities large enough to hold everyone who wanted to watch and ugly enough to remind both sides what they'd been fighting over. Salt had built Marrowgate. Salt had buried Vael too, in a way—Malrec's ritual had called for a ton of it, packed into the gate's hinges to choke the old wards. The merchants thought this was poetic. I thought it was the kind of poetry you only write when you've never watched someone dig at a door with their bare hands.

I sat at the long table on the Vaeli side, which was itself an argument. I wasn't Vaeli. I was the daughter of the family that had locked them in. But Sef had asked for me—"the keeper sits with us," they said, in that flat way children have of stating things that are actually demands—and so there I was, three months after the gate opened, still not entirely sure anyone had told me why.

The hall smelled of tallow and wet wool. Delegates from the six free cities filled one arc of benches: Marrowgate's salt barons, Kellin's shipwrights, the gray-robed magistrates of Dunmark who never spoke above a whisper because they believed loudness was a form of theft. Across from them, the Vaeli delegation: gaunt people in layered clothes, three centuries deep in a mountain, blinking at torchlight like men raised in a cellar. Which, I suppose, they had been.

And between them, on a plain wooden chair that someone had tried to drape with cloth until Sef pulled it off, sat my child.

Not mine. I want to be clear about that, even now. But the word *mine* kept trying to attach itself, the way burrs do.

* * *

The first week nearly killed the whole thing twice.

The free cities wanted guarantees. Reparations for the war Malrec had started in Vael's name. Trade concessions—the salt barons had done the arithmetic on a reopened kingdom's mineral wealth before the gate was even cool. And above all, they wanted a single throat to hold accountable. A crown. A king or queen of Vael, signatory, liable, corralled.

"You cannot make treaties with a crowd," said Magistrate Ovel of Dunmark, in her whisper that somehow carried further than shouting. "A crowd disperses. A crown persists."

The Vaeli delegates looked at each other the way people do when they've spent three hundred years surviving by consensus and no one alive remembers how kings worked except as a story about betrayal. Because that was the truth under everything: the last king of Vael had been betrayed by my ancestor, and the crown had become, in the deep halls, less a symbol than a warning. Mothers used it to frighten children. *Trust no one who wants to be above you.*

Sef listened to all of it for four days without speaking. I watched them do it—watched a twelve-year-old sit through hours of procedural argument in two languages, hands folded, the broken-crown birthmark half-hidden under their collar. They'd learned to keep it covered in crowds. Adoration had taught them that much. In the first weeks after the gate opened, people had knelt to them in the streets of Marrowgate, had pressed their fingers to the hem of Sef's coat, had wept. Sef had come back to our rooms those nights gray-faced and silent, and once, only once, said: "It felt like being eaten slowly."

On the fifth day, they stood up.

"I don't want the crown," Sef said, in accented but fluent trade-tongue—they'd been drilling it with a Marrowgate schoolteacher every night, stubborn as a mule about it. "Nobody should have it."

Ovel's whisper cut across the murmur: "Then there is nothing to negotiate with."

"There's me," Sef said. "But not as a queen. As a Voice."

They'd rehearsed this with me, actually, two nights before, walking the wall at Ironhollow while I did my morning rounds out of habit rather than duty. They'd made me argue the other side. I'd been good at it. I'd argued that symbols move armies, that a nameless speaker is a speaker nobody fears, that the world outside the mountains understood crowns the way fish understand water. Sef had listened, nodded, and then said: "That's exactly why it has to be different. Fish don't know they're drowning anything."

Now, in the salt exchange, they laid it out plainly. One Voice, elected by the Vaeli councils every seven years. No army answerable to them. No treasury. No heir. Power to speak for the nation in treaty and to speak *against* it—one veto, usable once per year, non-transferable, because Sef had insisted the Voice needed teeth even if the teeth could only bite once.

"A person," Sef finished, "who can be replaced. That's the whole point. You can't own a country that can fire its king."

The silence afterward went on so long I could hear the salt sifting somewhere in the building's bones.

It was Ferro Askan, of all people, who broke it. She'd wedged herself into the gallery with her smuggler captains, ostensibly as observers, actually as creditors—half the free cities' delegations owed her money, and everyone knew her nod was worth more than a magistrate's signature. She leaned over the rail and said, loudly enough for the record-keepers to hear:

"For what it's worth, I've moved contraband past every crown on this coast, and I've never once had to bribe a committee. Think about what that tells you."

Laughter broke the spell. Ovel's mouth twitched, which for Dunmark was a standing ovation. And the thing that had seemed impossible on Monday became, by Friday, merely difficult.

* * *

Difficult took another month.

There were the reparations, which the Vaeli refused to pay for a war Malrec had manufactured, and which the free cities eventually converted into a joint reconstruction fund—my suggestion, though I said it quietly, through Sef, because a Blackwood proposing terms still made certain delegates reach for their knives. There was the question of land: the valley villages burned by Corhane's inquisitors, Thornfell among them, sat on the approaches to Ironhollow, and the returning Vaeli wanted them, and the survivors of Thornfell—who had fled *from* the Vaeli question, not toward it—wanted nothing so much as to be left alone. We split it. Shared grazing rights, a mixed council for the valley towns, a border that ran down the middle of the river road so that neither side ever stood entirely on their own ground. It was a mess. It was the kind of mess that requires talking, which was the entire point.

Corhane came to the talks in the third week.

She arrived without escort, in civilian gray, and asked permission to enter the city like any traveler, which caused a small panic at the gates until Sef heard about it and said, "Let her in, obviously," with the exasperation of a child forced to explain something obvious to adults. She sat through two days of sessions in the public gallery, hands folded, watching the machinery of peace assemble itself out of parts she'd spent her career trying to break.

I went to her on the second evening. I found her on the exchange steps, eating street food with the careful neutrality of a woman who knew she might be poisoned and had decided the skewers were worth the risk.

"Everyone's waiting to see what you'll ask for," I said.

"I know. It's making them nervous. Nobody negotiates well against a blank page." She ate. Below us, lamplighters were working the square. "Your delegate is very good, Keeper. The veto clause is elegant. Did you teach them that?"

"The ledgers taught me that a lock with no release strangles whatever it holds. Sef drew the rest."

"Mm." She turned the skewer stick over. "I've been offered reinstatement. The new commandant in Kellin wants the inquisition reformed, not dissolved. Rebranded. 'Wardens of the Accord.'" Her voice did something complicated on the last word. "They'd want me to train the next generation. I'm apparently very good at finding hidden heirs."

"And?"

"And I spent eleven years believing I was saving lives." She said it evenly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "I carried orders I thought were mercy. I would carry them again, given what I knew then. That's the part I can't reform. So no. I won't stand in front of a room full of students and pretend the next set of orders will be kinder." She looked at me. "I've requested exile. Formal, documented, the whole ceremony. There's a settlement of former inquisitors starting on the southern coast—people who need to be somewhere their names aren't known. I'm going to build walls for them. Actual ones. Stone. Nothing behind them but houses."

"It's a strange penance," I said. "Building walls after all the doors you closed."

"It's not penance." For the first time, something moved behind her eyes—not warmth, exactly. Recognition. "It's the only honest work I know. Walls aren't lies, Keeper. Only the things we tell about them are." She stood, brushed crumbs from her coat, and paused. "Tell your delegate—I mean, tell the Voice—that of all the people hunting them last year, I'm the only one who's sorry. The others are just sorry they lost."

She walked down into the square and didn't look back. I watched until the lamplight took her, and I found, to my surprise, that I hoped the southern coast was kind to her. Some part of me always would. You fight beside someone in the dark, and some part of you belongs to them forever, whether you want it or not.

* * *

The signing was held at Ironhollow.

Sef insisted on that, over the objections of every delegation that would have preferred a capital with plumbing. "Treaties signed in palaces belong to palaces," they said. "This one belongs to a door." So they built a platform against the gatehouse wall, in the shadow of the arch that had been sealed for three hundred years and now stood open onto a road busy with carts, pilgrims, and children running messages between two worlds that were learning each other's names.

I stood where I had stood for ten years—at the base of the wall, near the place where the key mechanism had been—and watched Sef sign first, as Voice, in Old Vaelish and trade-tongue both, the characters flowing off their pen like water finding its level. Then the six cities. Then the witnesses. My name was read out among them—Lior Blackwood, last Gatekeeper of Ironhollow—and I put my signature beneath Sef's, and my hand did not shake, which surprised me, because inside I felt like a house whose last tenant has finally left.

Afterward, in the thin gold light of late afternoon, Sef found me on the wall. My wall. Except it wasn't anymore—it belonged to the school now, officially, and unofficially to whoever walked it.

"They're going to melt the keys," Sef said, leaning on the parapet beside me. "Both halves. Mine and yours. Into a bell for the council hall in Thornfell. They're calling it the Bell of the Open Door." A pause. "You knew?"

"Ferro told me. She collects secrets, and lately she's been giving them away for free, which worries me more than the toll ever did."

"Does it bother you? Your key. Your family's key."

I thought about it honestly, the way I'd learned to think about most things since the gate opened: slowly, and without flinching if I could manage it. "My family used that key to close a door on ten thousand living people," I said. "Letting it become a bell that calls people together is the only good ending it could have had." I looked at the child beside me—no, not a child anymore, not quite; the year had put years on them the way hard winters put rings on trees. "Does it bother you? It sang to you. It was yours."

"That's why it has to go in the bell." Sef shrugged, a gesture they'd learned from the Marrowgate dock kids, perfect and infuriating. "If I keep it, someday somebody will kneel to it. Somebody always does."

Below us, the last of the delegates' carriages wound down the valley road. Somewhere in the deep halls, the Vaeli survivors were lighting lamps in rooms that hadn't seen flame in three centuries, and in the valley villages, farmers who had feared the mountain their whole lives were learning the names of their new neighbors, badly, laughing at their own mistakes. Peace. Not victory—there had been no victory, not really, only the war dying of starvation the way I'd once dared to hope. A peace built out of compromise and shared rivers and a bell that didn't exist yet.

"So," Sef said. "The council confirmed me today. First Voice. Seven-year term. One veto." They were quiet a moment. "First act of the office: I'm appointing a warden of the gatehouse school. Someone who knows the old wards, the ledgers, the roads. Someone the Vaeli trust and the free cities trust, which is a very short list." They turned and looked up at me, and their eyes were steady in a way that made my chest hurt. "The position comes with a house. Students. Terrible pay. Absolutely no authority whatsoever."

"Sef—"

"It's not a crown, Lior. It's better. It's a job." The ghost of a grin. "You'd be allowed to quit."

I looked out over the valley—the road open, the smoke rising straight and peaceful from a dozen chimneys, the mountain holding its dead gently at last instead of hiding them. Ten years I had guarded a door. Now the door was a threshold, and thresholds don't need wardens. They need teachers. They need someone to sweep the stones and greet the morning and show children which words in Old Vaelish mean *home*.

"I'll need to see the ledgers first," I said, which in Blackwood meant yes, and Sef, who had lived in my gatehouse long enough to speak fluent Blackwood, nodded solemnly and said, "Of course. Very thorough. Take all evening."

We stood on the wall until dark, saying nothing, guarding nothing, belonging—for the first time in either of our lives—to something we had chosen.
