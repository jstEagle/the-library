## 22. Spring at Ironhollow

The snowmelt came down the mountain in a hundred small voices.

Lior heard it before they opened their eyes—water finding its old channels in the rock below the gatehouse, gullies running silver in the early light. For ten years that sound had meant one thing: melt season, when the smugglers came up from the lowlands and the ledgers filled with new columns of trade. Now it just meant spring.

They lay a moment longer in the narrow bed, listening to the mountain wake. Then they rose, dressed in the same plain wool they'd worn every day for eleven years, and climbed the stairs to the wall.

This was the part of the morning Lior had never been willing to give up, even now. Especially now.

The walk was the same as it had always been—the full circuit of the wall above the gate, boots on stone worn smooth by four generations of Blackwood feet. But everything about it had changed, the way a room changes when you take the furniture out and realize how big it always was. Lior used to walk this circuit looking for trouble: loose mortar, strange fires in the valley, riders where there should be none. Their eyes had been trained to find threat the way a falcon's eyes are trained to find mice.

Now their eyes found other things. The crocus pushing up through a crack by the second tower, purple as a bruise, stubborn as anything alive. The pair of choughs arguing over nesting rights on the old warning brazier, which hadn't held fire since the autumn. The valley below, green coming up through last year's brown like ink spreading through wet paper.

Nobody was watching them back. That was the difference. For ten years Lior had stood on this wall as a sentry, and somewhere in the back of their mind had always been the knowledge that if the gate ever opened wrong—if the seal ever failed, if the thing their family had done ever came due—they would be the first to know and possibly the last to be blamed. A sentry's walk is a kind of prayer performed facing outward.

Lior still walked it every morning. But they walked it facing all directions now, including inward, and what they were doing wasn't guarding against anything. It was greeting the day. It had taken them most of a year to learn to call it that without flinching.

At the third tower they stopped, as they did every morning, and looked down at the gate.

* * *

The gate of Ironhollow stood open.

It had stood open for three hundred and forty days, and Lior had stopped counting after the first month because counting had felt too much like waiting for something to go wrong. The great doors of black ironwood and Vaeli bronze—one of which their ancestors had sealed and the other which the deep folk had sealed from within—now stood folded back against the rock on either side of the archway like wings at rest. Sunlight went into the tunnel mouth every morning and did not come out again until evening, swallowed by the passage that ran down into the buried halls, and Lior had learned to think of it not as a wound or a door but as a throat: something that took light in and gave other things back.

It gave back people. That was the miracle Lior still hadn't gotten used to. Every morning there was traffic through the gate—Vaeli children going up to lessons, free-folk traders going down to market, an old stonemason named Hetta who made the descent twice a week to teach joint-carving to anyone who'd sit still for it. The deep halls held more survivors than anyone had guessed. Nine thousand souls who had lived three centuries behind their own sealed doors, waiting for a world that had forgotten them, keeping their language alive the way you keep coals alive under ash—not burning, but never quite dead.

Sef said once, in one of those flat, terrible child-observations that landed like dropped glass: "You kept the gate locked and we kept it locked. Everyone guarding everyone from everyone. It worked perfectly."

Lior had laughed, because there was nothing else to do with a sentence like that.

From the tower, the gate below was a dark arch with morning moving through it. Two Vaeli girls came up the switchback road carrying baskets of the pale mushrooms the deep gardens grew, headed for the kitchens of the school. One of them waved up at Lior. Lior waved back.

Then they finished the circuit, went down, and went to work.

* * *

The gatehouse smelled of chalk and bread and ink.

Lior paused in the doorway of the main hall—it had been the guardroom, once, then the ledger office, and now it was the largest classroom—and watched the morning assemble itself. Benches dragged into rows. A fight over the good quills resolved by the ancient diplomatic method of splitting them. At the front, Sef stood on the step-stool they still needed to reach the top of the slate board, writing out the day's first word in Old Vaelish while thirty children watched with the particular ferocious attention of young people who have decided that a dead language is the most interesting contraband in the world.

The word was *sennava*. Homecoming. Sef drew the final character—a spiral with a broken line through it—and turned around.

"Who can tell me why we break the line?"

"Because homecoming isn't finished!" shouted a freckled boy from Marrowgate, whose name was Doss and who had the volume of a landslide.

"Partly," Sef said. "Anyone else?"

"Because the king is dead," said a small Vaeli girl near the front, matter-of-fact, the way children say enormous things. "So nobody's homecoming is whole. You break the line so the word remembers."

Sef looked at her for a moment. Lior saw the flicker cross the child's face—the grief that would live in them forever, carried like a smooth stone in a pocket, touched sometimes but no longer cutting. Then Sef nodded.

"That's exactly right, Ivi. Break the line so the word remembers." They picked up the chalk again. "Now, all of you, this is the hardest character you'll learn this month, so pay attention, because Lior says if I let any of you skip it they'll make me copy it fifty times, and Lior is very mean about handwriting."

"Meanest keeper on the mountain," agreed Doss solemnly, and the class dissolved into giggles, and Lior came fully into the room and began, as they did every day, the work of teaching.

That was the joke of it, the beautiful absurd joke that Lior suspected they would spend the rest of their life laughing at: the last Blackwood gatekeeper of Ironhollow, heir to the family that had sold a kingdom shut, now spent their mornings teaching the grandchildren of that kingdom's survivors to read the language their ancestors had tried to bury. Not as penance. Penance had been the first few months, when Lior had done everything with white knuckles and a taste of ash, when every corrected stroke of a Vaeli character had felt like paying down a debt measured in bones. Somewhere along the way—Lior couldn't name the day, though they remembered the season, late summer, the smell of cut hay—penance had quietly become work. Work became craft. Craft, some mornings, became joy, arriving unannounced like the choughs on the brazier.

The Blackwoods had kept ledgers no one read. Lior kept a classroom no one had ordered. It was better.

They moved among the benches correcting hands. Doss's spirals were too tight; he wrote like he was strangling the alphabet. Ivi's hand was lovely but she pressed so hard the chalk snapped, and Lior showed her how to hold it loose, the way you hold anything you don't want to break—including, Lior was learning, yourself. Near the window sat the oldest student, a gray-bearded Vaeli deep-folk man named Oren who had come up through the gate in the second month and enrolled in the children's class without embarrassment, because as he put it, "I know the prayers by ear. I want to know what I've been saying." He wrote slowly and beautifully. His granddaughter sat beside him and teased him gently when his spirals drifted.

Through the open windows came the sounds of the settlement that had grown up around the gate in a single year—hammering from the new roofs going up along the old smuggler road, Ferro Askan's people mostly, who had discovered that a legal frontier town was even more profitable than smuggling and had converted with the untroubled pragmatism of true professionals. Ferro herself had taken the old toll house as an office and charged honest customs fees, which she insisted on calling tolls, because, she told Lior, "a woman should keep at least one vice."

The bell rang mid-morning—the treaty bell, cast from the melted key-halves and Corhane's surrendered signet ring, hanging now in the archway where it rang on the hour and could be heard both above and below. Lior still felt a small shock every time they heard it. That sound had been a key once. Their key. The thing they had owned more surely than they had ever owned anything, the inheritance and the burden and the identity all in one shape of iron. Now it told children when to stop working and start eating.

Some days the shock was grief. Most days it was relief. Lior had stopped trying to sort the two apart.

* * *

Midday, they ate on the wall.

This had become ritual too—Lior's and Sef's, the two of them sitting side by side on the wide parapet above the gate with their legs dangling over the drop, sharing bread and cheese and whatever the kitchen sent up. Today it was spring greens and hard-boiled eggs dyed yellow with some root the Vaeli cooks used, and Sef ate theirs with the concentrated efficiency of someone who had known real hunger and never entirely unlearned it.

Below them, the valley. The burned villages—Thornfell and the others—were rebuilt now or rebuilding, and from this height you could see the smoke of chimneys instead of pyres. Inquisitor gray had vanished from the roads months ago. Corhane was gone east, into exile, taking the soldiers who'd chosen to follow her rather than serve either crown; she'd written once, a short letter full of military precision and almost-concealed warmth, reporting that the eastern passes were quiet and that she was, improbably, teaching border militia officers how to read terrain maps, and closing with a single sentence that Lior kept folded in the ledger book: *Tell the child the line stays unbroken.* Lior had shown it to Sef. Sef had read it twice and said nothing, and that night Lior had seen the letter pinned above the child's bed, and understood that some things didn't need answering aloud.

"You're staring at the gate again," Sef said.

"I'm allowed."

"You're allowed. But you do it every day, and your face does a thing." Sef squinted at them with unnerving accuracy. "Like you're checking it's still open."

Lior considered that. Below, a party of traders went down into the passage with lanterns unlit—the tunnel had lamplight now, Vaeli-worked glowstones set at intervals, another small miracle that had stopped feeling like one.

"Maybe I am," they admitted. "Eleven years I checked that it was shut. Apparently checking is just something my family does."

"No," Sef said. "Checking is something *you* do. Your family checked so they wouldn't have to look." The child bit into an egg. "You look."

Lior looked at them—at this person who had arrived as a starving scrap in a blizzard and become, through no process either of them had chosen, something like family. Family not inherited but built, plank by plank, in cisterns and tunnels and council chambers and long desperate passes. Family that could say sharp true things because it was safe to.

"When do the new students arrive?" Sef asked, mouth full.

"Tomorrow. Six from Marrowgate, four from the valley, two more from below." Lior hesitated. "And one from further east. Corhane sent word—a militia officer's daughter. Wants to learn the language. Wants—" they searched for the right words, "—wants to learn what her people's orders were, I think. What they were for."

Sef was quiet a moment. Then: "Good. She should sit next to Oren. He'll make her write the homecoming character until she cries, and then she'll understand it properly."

"You're bloodthirsty for a teacher."

"I learned from a very mean keeper."

They sat in the sun while the valley steamed and the bell rang the afternoon hour, and neither of them said anything else for a while, because it was the kind of silence that two people who had survived a war together could afford to keep.

* * *

Evening, Lior made the circuit again.

They had decided, sometime in the winter, that they would keep the morning walk and add an evening one, and that these would be the only rituals left from the old life. Everything else had been given away or repurposed—the ledgers donated to the treaty archive in Marrowgate, the guard rotations dissolved into a town watch that answered to an elected council, the gatehouse keys (ordinary ones, for ordinary doors) hung on a peg by the kitchen where anyone could reach them.

But the wall walks stayed, because Lior had finally understood what they were for. Not vigilance. Not duty. Attention. Someone had to notice the crocuses. Someone had to see the valley turn green. Ten years of watching for danger had trained their eyes exquisitely, and it seemed a waste—all right, Lior admitted, it seemed a sin—to let that attention die unused when the world had finally provided it something worth looking at.

At the third tower they stopped. Below, the gate stood open in the dusk, and the glowstones lit the tunnel like a string of patient stars going down into the earth, and the evening traffic moved through it—deep-folk coming up for the evening markets, singing something in rounds, the harmonies rising out of the ground itself. Above, the first stars were coming out over the peaks. Between the two lights, the mountain breathed.

Lior thought about their father, who had died without explaining anything, and forgave him a little more, the way you forgive someone the way a river forgives a stone—not all at once, but a little more each year, wearing the hardness down. He had guarded a secret so heavy he couldn't lift it enough to show his child what was underneath. Lior had carried it up into the light instead, and set it down in a classroom, where children broke the line in the homecoming word so it would remember.

Somewhere down in the deep halls, nine thousand people were lighting their own evening lamps in halls that had known only fear-lamps for three centuries. Somewhere east, an exile captain was reading terrain maps by candlelight and not hating herself as much as she used to. Somewhere south, in Marrowgate, an elected Voice with a broken-crown birthmark was presumably arguing with a council and winning, and would arrive at the school in three weeks for the spring term's opening, and would sleep in the narrow bed in the gatehouse loft that was theirs whenever they wanted it, because home, Sef had finally answered, a year and a war ago, halfway up a freezing pass with blood on the snow—

Home is a promise, they'd said. Places are just where you keep it.

The bell rang the last hour of daylight. Lior stood a moment longer at the tower, letting the wind off the snowfields move through their hair, saying nothing, guarding nothing, belonging here—not by inheritance, not by key, not by blood or duty or debt, but by the simple daily act of showing up to greet the day.

Then they went down the stairs, out of the wind, toward the smell of bread and the sound of lessons being prepared for tomorrow, and the gate of Ironhollow stood open behind them all night, and no one watched it, and that was the whole point.
