## 1. The Valley That Forgot

The Hollow woke the way it always did: slowly, and pretending it had never been asleep.

August Okafor stood at the window of the Wayhouse at Millbrook's Bend, watching fog peel off the river in long gray ribbons, and counted the things she knew for certain. One: the fog would burn off by midmorning. Two: the merchant caravan she'd been hired to guard would be an hour late, because merchants were always an hour late and then complained about losing time. Three: somewhere down the road, trouble was waiting with her name on it—not her name, precisely, but the shape of it. After eleven years of this work, she could feel trouble coming the way sailors felt weather in their teeth.

She rolled her shoulders, checked the straps of her sword belt out of habit rather than need—she'd checked them twice already—and went down to the common room to drink bad tea and look unbothered.

The Wayhouse smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool. The caravan master, a round man named Hobb whose worry sat on him like a poorly fitted coat, was already at the corner table, tearing bread into pieces he didn't eat.

"They said bandits on the north fork," he told her for the third time since dawn. "You heard that? Bandits."

"I heard." August sat across from him, laid her hands flat on the table—a trick she'd learned from her mentor, Brenna, rest her: open palms make liars of everyone around you. "Bandits on the north fork is a rumor. Bandits who actually rob people leave witnesses, and nobody in Millbrook has seen so much as a bent horseshoe. What we have is three farms along the north fork reporting missing livestock."

"Missing livestock is bandits."

"Missing livestock is hungry men before winter. Hungry men I can talk to. Bandits I can fight. Either way, you'll get your wool to the mill." She took a sip of the tea and did not wince, mostly. "Drink your tea, Hobb. Worry after breakfast like a sensible man."

He laughed, because she'd built the laugh into the sentence for him, and for a moment the room felt ordinary again. That was the craft of it, August thought. Not the sword work—anyone with shoulders and a grudge could learn sword work. The craft was making frightened people believe the world still had rails under it. She had been doing it so long she sometimes forgot there had been a time when she hadn't known how.

Outside, the fog thinned. The mountains that ringed the valley—the ones the old-timers called the Rims, though no one remembered why—showed their white shoulders through the gray, patient as sleeping animals.

August looked at them a moment longer than she meant to.

There was a feeling she'd been carrying for two weeks now, light as a coin in a pocket, easy to ignore until your hand brushed against it. The feeling that the valley was thinner than it used to be. That was the only word she had for it. *Thin.* As if the Hollow were a wall she'd leaned against all her life without noticing, and lately the wall had developed a draft.

Everyone in the Hollow lived inside a kind of quiet. Nobody talked about it, because there was nothing specific to say. It wasn't that they lacked history—the valley had its ruins, its old boundary stones, its half-ruined shrines to gods whose names the moss had eaten. It was that memory itself behaved strangely here, like water finding its level. Old grudges drained away. Old griefs went soft at the edges. Her mother had died when August was nineteen, and by twenty-five August could remember the fact of it, the funeral, the black dress, but not the sound of her mother's voice, and not for lack of trying. Everyone in the Hollow was the same. They kept their names and their faces and the shape of their lives, but anything deeper than a decade turned smooth and featureless, like a stone carried too long in a river.

*It keeps us gentle,* the elders said. *A valley at peace.*

August had made her peace with the peace. You could do anything if you stopped asking questions early enough.

She finished the tea. She went to ready the caravan.

* * *

The north fork ran narrow between alder slopes, the river loud on one side and the tree shadow cool on the other, and for six miles everything went exactly the way August had promised Hobb it would, which should have been her first warning. Good days on the road were borrowed days. She'd learned to count the interest.

They made camp at dusk in a bend where the ground rose high enough to see both directions of the track. August set the watch herself, walked the perimeter twice, ate standing up, and was just settling onto her bedroll with her boots off—boots off, a small luxury, a mistake—when the horses started screaming.

Not spooked-screaming. *Pain.* She was up with her boots half-laced and her blade bare before her mind caught up with her body, which was how she liked it; thinking was for after.

Firelight. Shapes. Four of them coming through the alders low and fast, and these weren't starving farmers—these moved like men who had done this before, spreading wide to cut the picket line from the herd. One had a crossbow. August put herself between the bowman and the horse line, called the warning she was contractually obligated to call—"Robbery! Stand off or stand to!"—and got, in answer, a bolt that she swatted aside with a sound like a struck bell and a rush of adrenaline so clean it felt almost like joy.

Then it was work. The first man came at her with an axe and a bad temper; she broke his wrist with the flat and let him fall. The second was better, a swordsman with a scarred knuckle-guard, and they traded four strokes in the firelight, steel singing, before she found the flaw in his footwork and put him down hard enough to keep him down. Behind her, Hobb's drivers were shouting and flailing torches, which was useless but noisy, and noise helped. Noise made bandits believe a camp was worth more than it was.

The crossbowman had gone for the horses anyway. August reached him as he cut loose the last two animals, and she put her point at the gap beneath his jaw and said, very quietly, "Drop it."

He dropped it.

And then, because she was breathing hard and flushed with the animal satisfaction of a fight cleanly won, she made her second mistake of the night. She relaxed.

"You're her," the man said.

August kept her voice flat. "I'm whoever you want me to be, friend, as long as what you want is to lie face-down in the dirt."

"The Okafor woman." He was grinning. That was wrong—that was the thing she noticed even through the pulse in her ears. Men with a blade at their throat didn't grin. "From the Hollow. The one who doesn't remember."

The words went through her colder than any steel had all night.

"Careful," she said.

"Oh, we know all about you lot." He was older than she'd taken him for, forty maybe, with a rope-burn scar on his neck and eyes that had seen country she couldn't imagine. He wasn't from the Hollow. His accent came from over the Rims—from outside, from the world that the valley's silence kept out, or kept in, depending on how you liked your metaphors. "Whole valley full of people who can't hold a memory past ten years. Whole valley walking around with holes where their lives ought to be, calling it peace." He laughed, and the laugh had something underneath it that made the hair on her arms rise. "We've been selling passage through the pass to folk who want to see it. Come gawk at the amnesiacs. And then—"

"Then what?" Her voice came out steadier than her hands. She tightened her grip on the sword.

"And then word comes down from the capital." He lowered his voice, delighted with himself, a man savoring a secret he'd been paid well enough to spend. "Says there's a name going around. A name out of your valley that nobody's supposed to remember. Says even you people are starting to say it in your sleep."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind her, one of the freed horses moved in the dark.

"What name?" August asked.

And the man—who should have been terrified, who had every reason to beg, whose partner lay groaning ten feet away with a broken wrist—looked straight at her and said:

"Tomas."

* * *

Later—much later, when the bandits were trussed for the constable at Millbrook and Hobb had pressed extra coin into her palm with shaking gratitude and gone to sleep snoring like a landslide—August sat alone by the dead fire and tried to understand what had happened to her.

Because the name had meant nothing. That was the truth of it, the plain and testable truth: *Tomas* was just a name, a sound, four letters' worth of air. She searched herself for it the way you search a room where you think you've left a candle burning, checking every corner, and found nothing. No face attached. No childhood summer, no voice, no grief. Nothing.

But her body had known.

Her body had reacted before her mind finished hearing the syllables—a lurch behind the sternum, a cold drop, the way you flinch at a stair that isn't where your feet expect it. Some part of her had braced for that name the way you brace for a blow you don't see coming, and the rest of her, the part that had lived thirty-one years in the Hollow and trusted its gentle forgetting, had been left standing in the wreckage wondering what exactly had just been taken from her, or given back, or *revealed*.

She did what she always did with problems: she took them apart.

Possibility one: coincidence. A bandit says a common name, her nerves jump, end of story. But *Tomas* wasn't common in the Hollow—she'd have been able to list every man in Millbrook and the surrounding farms with that name, and she could list none. Possibility two: the bandit had been playing her, fishing with a hook dressed to look like knowledge, hoping a mercenary would flinch and give something away. Possible. But he'd grinned like a man who'd gotten what he paid for, and his partners had looked *afraid of him* for saying it, which fishers don't manage.

Possibility three was the one she didn't want.

That the valley was thinner than it used to be. That whatever kept the Hollow gentle and smooth and safe was fraying at the edges like an old blanket, and things were starting to show through—names, first, small things, the way water comes through a roof before the ceiling falls. That somewhere out past the Rims, people spoke about the Hollow's forgetting the way you'd speak about a mine collapse or a plague: something engineered, something *done*. Something with a cause.

And if it had a cause, it might have a cure. And if it had a cure—

She pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars.

Here was the thing she had never let herself think in thirty-one years, the thing that lived below the floorboards of her mind and never, ever came upstairs: she had spent her whole life mourning people she couldn't remember. Her mother. Whatever family the valley had smoothed away before her mother died. There were gaps in her childhood shaped exactly like people. She'd catch them sometimes—a scent of cedar oil that made her heart ache for someone she couldn't name, a half-dream of a kitchen and laughter and a hand ruffling her hair—and each time, the Hollow's quiet would close back over the ache like water, and she'd go on, gently, peacefully, hollowly.

*Tomas* had torn that water open for one second, and in that second she had felt the shape of an entire person, and the loss of him had hit her like grief for a death she'd never been allowed to attend.

"No," she said aloud, to the dark, to the dead fire, to the white shoulders of the Rims beyond it. "No, thank you."

The valley didn't answer. The valley never answered. That was the whole arrangement.

But when she finally slept, near dawn, she dreamed of a river ford and a man's laugh, warm and familiar and utterly unplaceable, and she woke with tears drying on her face and no idea whose they were.

* * *

Three days later, having collected her pay and told Hobb the road was safe for another season, August rode east instead of west. East was toward the ford at Graywater, where the river left the valley through the last gap in the Rims. East was toward the edge of everything she knew.

She told herself it was a patrol. She told herself she wanted a word with the ferryman about the missing livestock. She told herself a great many competent, professional things, and she believed none of them, and the horse knew it, tossing its head the whole way like it was laughing at her.

The morning was bright and cold. Frost silvered the fence wires. At the top of the last hill before the ford, August reined in and looked back once at the valley—the patchwork fields, the woodsmoke standing straight in the still air, the little world that had held her like a cupped hand her entire life—and felt, unmistakably, the draft in the walls.

Something was coming through it. She could feel it in her teeth.

She turned east and rode down toward the water, and the water, when she reached it, was running red.

Not red—dark. Dark with something worse than mud. And on the gravel shoal at the crossing, half in the shallows, lay a man on a lathered horse that was swaying like a drunk, and the man lifted his head at the sound of her hooves, and pressed one bloodied hand against a saddlebag sealed with green wax, and mouthed words the river stole before she could hear them.

August swore, kicked her horse forward, and went to meet the first day of the rest of a life she suddenly understood she had never really known.
