## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The ford at Marrow Creek was the kind of place where a woman could stand for an hour and be no one at all.

August Okafor had learned to prize that. In the Hollow, being no one was not merely a comfort; it was a discipline, almost a devotion. The valley taught its people to forget—names of neighbors who moved on, faces of travelers who passed through, even, in bad years, the shapes of their own dead—and August had made a trade of it. She forgot professionally. She protected other people's secrets so thoroughly that her own had worn smooth as river stone, featureless and easy to carry.

She stood hip-deep in the shallows now, boots slung around her neck by their laces, trousers rolled to the knee, watching the water braid itself white around her calves. The job was simple: hold the ford until the Vess merchant caravan crossed, discourage anyone who looked like trouble, and ask no questions about what the wagons carried under their oilcloth. The Vesses paid well and asked nothing in return except that she remain exactly as forgettable as the valley wanted her to be.

Three days after the bandits and the name that should have been dust, she was still turning that word over in her mind like a coin she suspected was counterfeit. *Okafor.* Her mother's voice saying it. A kitchen with steam on the windows. The memory had come back whole, which was the wrong part—the Hollow did not return things. It only took. Whatever had loosened in her when those men spoke her childhood name had not closed up again. She could feel the gap like a missing tooth, the tongue going back and back to it.

The creek ran cold enough to ache. Above the far bank, the hills went up gold and brown into a sky the color of old pewter, and the wind came down the valley carrying the smell of frost deciding whether to commit. Autumn in the Hollow was brief and businesslike. Winter would arrive without announcement, the way it always did, sealing the passes and the valley's silence both.

August was watching a heron stalk the reeds with the patience of a creditor when the man came out of the trees.

He did not walk so much as fall forward in installments—one lurching step, a catch against a birch trunk, another step. His horse was nowhere. His coat had been fine once, courier-gray with silver piping at the cuffs, and was now mostly mud and something darker. He saw her and his face did something complicated: relief and terror arriving together, fighting for the same features.

"No," he said, from thirty feet away. "No, please—you have to be someone. Please be someone."

August had heard dying men say many things. *Help me. Tell my wife. It was the tall one.* Never that. Never a plea to exist.

"Easy." She waded toward the bank, water streaming off her. "You're at Marrow Creek ford. You're safe."

"I'm not." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent him double against the birch. When he straightened, there was blood on his lips, bright as berry juice. "That's rather the entire point. I'm not safe, I'm not anyone, and in an hour I won't even be that."

Up close he was younger than his ruined coat suggested—mid-thirties maybe, hollow-cheeked, with the gray stubble of a man who hadn't slept in a bed in weeks. His eyes were the worst part. They kept sliding off her face, not the way eyes slide when a man is fainting, but the way they slide when they are being *made* to slide, dragged away by some effort of will that was visibly costing him everything he had left.

"Look at me," he said. "Please. Try to remember me. One minute. That's all I need."

"I'm looking at you."

"Then remember." He gripped her wrist with fingers cold as the creek water. "My name is Corvin Ashe. I carry dispatches for the Crown Road service, southern circuit, eleven years. I have a sister in Lowmark named Bree who thinks I'm dead already, and she is half right, and I need—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I need one person in this cursed valley to remember me for one hour. Can you do that? Can you do one hour?"

The Hollow's amnesia was gentle, ambient, a fog rather than a wall. Travelers passed through and were forgotten by supper. But this was different. This was a man actively being erased while she watched, fighting it with sheer stubborn narration, reciting himself like a prayer against the dark.

"What happened to you?" she asked quietly.

"The crown remembers." He said it the way other men said holy names, or curses. "That's the message. That's the whole dispatch. Eleven years I carried sealed letters and never once asked what was in them, and now I know, and knowing is—" Another cough. He sagged, and she caught him, took his weight across her shoulder, and lowered him onto the flat stone at the water's edge with the ease of long practice. He weighed less than he should have. Men losing blood fast always felt like they were already halfway to smoke.

"Let me see the wounds."

"Don't bother the wounds. Wounds are the least of it." But he let her peel back the coat anyway, because he was past refusing anything.

August had stitched knife cuts, arrow holes, dog bites, a boar goring, once a crossbow bolt through a man's thigh that she still dreamed about. She knew what steel did to flesh. She knew what teeth did, what claws did, what frostbite did, what poison did.

She did not know what *this* was.

There were four wounds across his chest and side, roughly parallel, each the width of two fingers. No blade she knew left edges like that—clean but *unhealing*, the skin around them gray and slightly translucent, like wax held too near a flame. And inside the wounds, where there should have been the ordinary red ruin of torn meat, there was instead a faint tracery of silver, fine as spider silk, glinting when the light moved. It looked less like injury than like stitching. As if something had sewn him shut from the inside—or begun sewing something else into him.

"That's why you're forgetting," she said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "It's in you. Whatever did this."

"It's *reading* me." He managed something like a smile. "Like a ledger. Every memory it takes, it leaves a thread. I can feel them. Little tugs. My sister first—that hurt most, so it took her first, clever thing. Then my commanding officer. Then the taste of my mother's bread, which I didn't even know I remembered until it was gone." His hand found hers and gripped. "You'll feel it too, soon. Standing this close. I'm sorry. I tried to reach the deep country, where it thins, but the valley—"

"The valley holds things in," August finished. "And holds things out."

"You've noticed." Something flickered in his fading eyes—not surprise exactly. Recognition. "You're the one who notices. Good. That's why it has to be you."

"It doesn't have to be anyone. I'm holding a ford for a merchant caravan."

"You were." He fumbled at his breast with clumsy fingers, found the inner pocket, and pulled out a leather scroll case sealed at both ends with black wax stamped with a sigil she didn't recognize—a circle broken by seven lines, like a wheel with the spokes torn out. "Caravan's a mile back. You have time if you stop reading this conversation right now and start reading this."

"I don't take deliveries."

"You do now." He pressed the case into her hands and folded her fingers over it with both of his, and his grip had the desperate strength of the nearly gone. "Listen. The map inside shows the way east, over the Steppe, to Meridian. The center of the map is blank because the center of the truth is blank, and cartography is honest even when kings aren't. There are people along the way who remember pieces. Find them. The crown remembers, August Okafor—the crown remembers *everything*, and that is the problem, and you are the answer, though God help me I don't know why yet. I only know the shape of the instruction. Deliver to the one who notices."

"How do you know my name?"

His smile was terrible and tender. "Because I've been carrying your file for three years, love. Everyone in the Hollow has a file. Yours just says *watch* instead of *act*, and I never understood why until today." He coughed again, longer, and when it passed he lay back against the stone, spent, the silver threads in his wounds glinting like frost. "One hour. That's all I asked. Remember me one hour, and then you can go back to being nobody, if nobody still wants you."

August sat on the wet stone beside a dying stranger and did the arithmetic she always did. Risk. Payment. Exit routes. The caravan would arrive within the hour; she could hand him off, claim she'd found him, wash her hands. The Vesses valued discretion above all things, and a courier bleeding silver thread was the least discreet object in the valley.

But his eyes were still dragging off her face, and she could feel it now herself—a faint pressure behind her temples, like a hand trying gently to close a book she was reading. She thought of the bandits' voices in the dark. *Okafor.* She thought of the gap in her that wouldn't close.

Something in the valley's machinery had slipped a gear. And this man had crawled out of the trees carrying proof of it, addressed to her, postage paid in his life.

"Corvin Ashe," she said deliberately. "Courier, Crown Road service, southern circuit, eleven years. Sister named Bree, in Lowmark, who thinks you're dead."

His whole body eased, as if she'd set down a weight he'd been carrying miles. "Half right," he whispered.

"Half right. I'll remember the half that matters."

"The map. Don't look at it where others can see. Don't sleep beside it. And when the ink moves—" He stopped, frowned, as if the next words had been taken from him mid-sentence. He tried again. "When the ink moves, don't—"

"Don't what?"

"I had it." Frustration cracked his voice. "I had it this morning. It's like reaching into a pocket and finding the pocket's been sewn shut." He laughed softly, hopelessly. "Sewn shut. That's the joke of it, isn't it. Everything's sewn shut."

Downstream, the heron lifted off the water on slow wings, unhurried, indifferent, and August watched it go and understood that she was watching the last ordinary thing she would see for a very long time.

"Stay with me," she said. "Talk. It slows it down, doesn't it? Narrating."

"A little. Like bailing a boat with a cup." But he talked. He told her about Bree, who was a cooper's widow and stubborn as a mule and made a plum brandy that could strip paint. He told her about the southern circuit, the inns, the dog he'd traveled with for six years before the dog died of honest old age, which he seemed to consider the finest death available in this world. He told her about the last delivery, three weeks ago, a sealed letter to the royal chancery in Meridian, and how he'd waited in the antechamber and seen the king pass through on the arm of his ministers, and how King Aldric had stopped—just for a moment—in the middle of the marble hall and looked directly at Corvin through the crowd with eyes that were lucid and drowning and *begging*, before the ministers steered him on and his face smoothed over like water closing above a stone.

"He mouthed something," Corvin said. His voice had gone thin as paper. "I've turned it over ten thousand times since. Two words. I thought it was 'good day.' Couriers hear 'good day' a hundred times a week. But it wasn't." His hand tightened on hers. "It was 'help me.' Or possibly 'hold me.' Isn't that dreadful? At the end, I can't remember which. Hold me. Help me. A king alone in a marble hall, and either one would break your heart."

The light was going amber and long across the water. Somewhere up the road, faintly, came the creak of wagon wheels—the caravan, early. August did not move toward it.

"Corvin," she said. "Who did this to you?"

"They don't send people anymore." His eyes had fixed on the sky above the far hills, on the first star or perhaps only a fleck in his vision. "They send the crown. The crown remembers where everyone is. The crown remembers what everyone was. And lately—" the silver threads pulsed, once, like something breathing—"lately it's started remembering *forward*. Things that haven't happened yet. Things it means to make happen." He turned his head with enormous effort and looked at her, and for a moment the drag on his eyes stopped entirely, and he saw her completely, and she felt seen the way she hadn't since she was a child in a steaming kitchen. "It knows you're here, August. It sent me to die in front of you. Not despite its plans—*as* its plans. So whatever you choose next, choose it like a free woman. Even if you're not one. Especially then."

The wagon wheels were closer. Voices, the Vess drivers calling to their teams.

"August Okafor," he said, and smiled, and the smile used up the last of him. "Crown Road service thanks you for your discretion. Bree makes the brandy in autumn. If you ever—"

He didn't finish. The silver threads dimmed all at once, like lamps snuffed in sequence down a corridor, and the pressure behind August's temples released, and she knew with a horrible certainty that somewhere in the world a sister in Lowmark had just lost the second half of her brother.

She sat with him while the light failed. She closed his eyes, which seemed only polite, and arranged his hands on his chest, and noted—for reasons she couldn't yet articulate—that the wounds had stopped bleeding entirely, and that the skin around them was beginning, very slowly, to smooth over, as though his body were being edited rather than buried.

Then she stood, slung her boots back on, waded out to retrieve her sword belt from the reeds where she'd left it, and stood in the shallows with the scroll case heavy against her ribs, doing new arithmetic.

The caravan would want to know about the body. The valley would want her to forget him by morning. The crown, apparently, already knew her name.

August Okafor looked down at the black wax seal in her hands—the broken wheel, the seven torn spokes—and made the choice that ended the life she'd built, though it would take her days yet to understand that.

She put the case inside her shirt, next to her skin, where it lay cold as the creek.

Behind her, on the flat stone, the dead man was already becoming difficult to look at directly—not gone, not yet, but going, her eyes sliding off him the way his had slid off hers. By full dark he would be a shape. By morning, a suggestion. By the time the Vess wagons rattled down to the water, there would be nothing at the ford but a mercenary standing guard, alone, for no reason she could name.

*One hour,* he'd asked. She meant to give him more than that.

She turned east, toward the hills and the dark coming down, and began, quietly, to plan.
