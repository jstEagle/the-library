## 3. What the Map Refuses to Show

The map did not want to be looked at.

August realized it on the second night, in the back room of the Lamplighter's Rest with the shutters barred and a single candle turned low, when she caught herself looking everywhere in the room except at the thing unrolled on her table. Her eyes kept sliding off it the way a thumb slides off wet stone. She had hung her sword within reach out of pure habit and then, absurdly, drawn it once already—not because anything had threatened her, but because the silence in the room had felt *watchful*, and twenty years of mercenary work had taught her that watchful silence was never empty.

She made herself look.

By candlelight the map was beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful. The Glass Steppe spread across the vellum in washes of pale blue and salt-white, rendered so finely she could see the cartographer had stippled every crystal field individually, thousands upon thousands of tiny points of light trapped in ink, as if the artist had ground up starlight to make his pigment. Rivers ran off the eastern edge in silver threads. Mountain ranges marched along the northern border in careful hatched strokes, each peak named in a small, precise hand.

And at the center: nothing.

Not blankness exactly. Blankness was a thing, an absence with edges, and this had no edges. The center of the map simply stopped being a map—a gradient of fading detail, like fog rolling across vellum, like a memory going soft at its middle. She tilted the candle. She held the parchment up against the flame's glow, looking for watermarks, for palimpsest, for the ghost of whatever had been scraped away. Nothing. The light passed through the empty center and threw a clean circle on the wall behind it, and August found she could not make herself keep her finger inside that circle for more than a few seconds at a time.

*The crown remembers,* the courier had said, and died with her name—her actual name, August, the one the valley was supposed to have eaten—in his mouth.

She sat back down and poured two fingers of the inn's bad whiskey and didn't drink it, just held the glass, because holding something was easier than thinking about what she was holding.

Her mother had taught her to read maps before she'd taught her letters. *A map is a promise,* Sera Okafor used to say, smoothing the creases flat with a palm scarred from rope-work. *Whoever drew it is promising you the world looks this way. So the first question you ask any map is: what does the drawer get out of my believing it?*

August asked the question now. What did the drawer get? What kind of person drew a map this fine—the work of months, maybe years—and left its heart deliberately empty?

Or, worse, the other kind of person: the kind who'd drawn the center honestly, and something else had eaten it since.

* * *

Among her possessions there was a bundle of letters tied with green cord, and August untied it for the first time in three years.

Maren Vashti had been her mentor for eleven years, from the age of fourteen until the winter the fever took her, and in all that time she had never once called August by her name. Not Okafor, not girl, not even *you* most days—it was always "mercenary," delivered dry as old bone, usually with one eyebrow raised. Maren had been the best blade in the Hollow and the worst liar, which in a valley built on forgetting was its own peculiar kind of courage. She had taught August how to hold a sword, how to read a contract's fine print, how to sleep in trees, and how to be paid properly. She had never spoken of her life before the Hollow. When August asked, once, young and blunt, Maren had said: *I came here because I wanted to forget. Be grateful someone built me a place where I could.*

August read the oldest letter first, because it was the only order that felt right, and because grief, she had learned, preferred to move in one direction.

---

*Mercenary —*

*You'll forgive the delay; my hands are worse this winter. You'll also forgive the brevity. I have been thinking about what you said in your last—that you feel watched in the deep valley, past the boundary stones, where nothing lives and nothing grows and the birds will not sing. You are not wrong to feel it. But do not go looking for the watcher. Some silences are load-bearing.*

*I say this as your teacher and not your friend, because if I were your friend I would say: come home, and we will drink too much and laugh at the young ones, and neither of us will ever speak of the deep valley again.*

*Your blade angle is still sloppy on the draw. Fix it.*

*— M.V.*

---

August remembered that letter. She'd been twenty-two, restless, poking at the edges of the world the way she poked at everything, and she'd filed Maren's warning under *mentor being cryptic* and gone on with her life. Now she read *load-bearing* three times and set the letter down with a care that bordered on ritual.

Some silences are load-bearing. As though the valley's quiet were a pillar holding up a roof. As though if you pulled it out—

The candle guttered. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a dog began barking and stopped mid-note, and August was on her feet with her knife drawn before she'd decided to stand, heart slamming, listening to a silence that now seemed to have texture, weight, intent. Ten seconds. Twenty. The dog resumed barking at nothing in particular.

She sat back down. Her hand wasn't quite steady when she picked up the next letter.

---

*Mercenary —*

*Strange news travels even into the Hollow, it seems, though it arrives wearing strange clothes. A courier passed through last month asking after the eastern passes, and he talked too much for a man in his trade. He spoke of the capital. Of the king's new crown, forged—I quote him—*from a metal that sings.* I laughed and told him crowns are made of gold or they are made of trouble, and he went pale and would not finish his cup.*

*A metal that sings. Mercenary, you will think me drunk. But I knew a man once—before—who worked metals, and he swore to me that everything has a voice if you listen at the right frequency. Stone sings slow. Water sings quick. And there are things under the world whose song is so long and so low that whole kingdoms rise and fall inside a single note.*

*If such a thing were taken and bound—if its note were cut short mid-phrase—what do you suppose the silence would sound like?*

*I ask only as a curiosity. Burn this letter if it troubles you. Or don't, and keep it with the others, and someday when we are both old and foolish we will drink too much and I will tell you what I ran away from, and you will wish I hadn't.*

*Fix your draw.*

*— M.V.*

---

August read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting each sentence settle like silt.

*A metal that sings.* The king's crown. Aldric's crown. The courier had pressed the map into her hands and said *the crown remembers*, and August had assumed he meant it the way people meant such things—poetically, deliriously, the way dying men meant anything. But Maren, dead three years, had written down the same word—*sings*—in connection with the same object, years before any courier bled out at the ford.

And underneath it, quieter, the sentence August kept circling back to like a tongue returning to a broken tooth:

*What do you suppose the silence would sound like?*

She thought of the Hollow. She thought of the way magic didn't work there—not weakly, not dimly, but *not at all*, a clean surgical absence that everyone accepted the way fish accept water. She thought of the boundary stones, and the moment you crossed them, that faint pressure in the ears, like surfacing from a dive. Everyone said it was altitude. It was not altitude. August had known it was not altitude since she was nine years old and had simply agreed, along with everyone else, not to know it.

The valley's silence wasn't natural. It was built. And things that are built can be unbuilt, and things that are bound—

She got up and walked around the small room once, twice, three times, the way she did when the alternative was thinking too fast. The map lay on the table behind her like a patient waiting for diagnosis.

Maren had known. Not everything—maybe not even much—but she had known enough to be afraid in a specific direction, and she had spent thirty years in the Hollow drinking too much and telling no one, and she had died without telling August, and the grief of that hit August sideways, unexpectedly, a fist below the ribs. *Someday when we are both old and foolish I will tell you what I ran away from.* There had been no someday. There was a bundle of letters tied with green cord, and a grave behind the temple with a marker that said only MAREN VASHTI, BELOVED, because in the Hollow even headstones forgot.

"You knew," August said aloud, to no one, to the room, to the dead. "You absolute mule. You knew."

* * *

On the fourth night, the ink moved.

She had fallen asleep over the table—whiskey finally drunk, candle burned to a thumb's width—and woke in the grey hour before dawn with her cheek on her forearm and the sense of having heard her name spoken. The room was cold. The window's gap breathed thin light across the table, across the map, and August, blinking, watched a river change course.

It happened slowly, at the speed of a minute hand. The silver thread of the Vessel River, which had bent east toward the mountains, straightened—no, not straightened, *migrated*, its curve easing northward grain by grain, and where it moved, the stippled crystal fields closed over its old bed like water closing over a stone. She stared until her eyes watered. When she blinked hard and looked again, the river had settled into its new path and showed no sign it had ever lain anywhere else.

August lit the candle with hands that were almost steady—almost—and did what any sensible person would do. She marked it. Ink dot at the river's western bend, timestamped in the corner of the vellum in her own neat mercenary script. Then she sat down to wait, watching, refusing to blink more than necessary.

The map behaved for six hours. Perfectly. Beautifully. The obedient promise of a well-made thing.

Then she went down for food—ten minutes, no more, she counted them by the inn bell—and came back to find her ink dot sitting half a mile from the river it touched.

So. It moved when unwatched. Which meant it was not a map of the Steppe. It was a map *of the present tense*—or of something present-tense—or it was alive, or haunted, or spelled, and none of those possibilities changed the arithmetic of her situation, which was this: a man had died to put this thing in her hands. Men with crossbow bolts through them did not spend their last breath on poetry. Whatever the map was, it mattered enough to kill for, and it had chosen her—or been given to her, which amounted to nearly the same thing—and every hour she sat in the Lamplighter's Rest pretending to be between contracts, the odds shortened on whoever had killed the courier coming to finish the job.

She should burn it. She knew that. Any sensible woman in the Hollow would burn it, take a long honest contract guarding a wool caravan, and let the wide world keep its own secrets. That was the whole point of the valley. That was what it was *for*. You came here to forget, or to be forgotten, and the silence wrapped around you like snowfall and after enough years you couldn't remember why you'd ever needed anything else.

Except the silence was thinner than it used to be. Except a bandit had spoken a name from her childhood. Except a dying stranger had known hers.

Except Maren had known, and Maren had never once lied to her, and Maren's letters read like a woman leaving a trail of breadcrumbs out of a forest she'd never escaped.

August stood at the table a long time, the candle burning down between her and the map, and let herself feel the full shape of the choice, because she was disciplined enough to know that choices made hastily get remade badly later.

Burn it, and stay invisible. Keep it, and become a person with a destination—which was to say, a person who could be followed, found, fought, and buried.

She thought about the empty center. She thought about how she had not been able to hold her gaze inside it, and how that failure had felt less like fear than like etiquette—as though some part of her had been taught, long ago, not to look at certain things, and had only now been reminded.

She thought: *whoever drew this left the middle out on purpose, because the middle is not a place. The middle is a wound.*

"Damn you, Maren," she said softly, and it came out sounding like *thank you*, which was the same word in the language they'd spoken to each other for eleven years.

She fetched her travel roll, her oilskin, her spare knives. She folded the map along its existing creases—carefully, respectfully, the way you handle a snake you've decided not to kill—and slid it into the oilskin sleeve, and put the sleeve against her spine where the important things rode.

The whiskey glass still sat on the table. She looked at it, thought of Maren's *when we are both old and foolish*, and drank what was left standing in one swallow that tasted like commitment.

Outside, the valley lay under its usual soft hush. Smoke rose from chimneys. Somewhere a hammer rang on a farrier's nail, unhurried. Everything exactly as it had always been, everything designed to seem exactly as it had always been, and for the first time in her life August Okafor looked at her home and saw the architecture of it—the deliberate, engineered, *load-bearing* silence—and understood that she had been living inside the answer to a question no one in the Hollow was allowed to ask.

She was halfway through packing when she heard the knock.

Three raps, spaced evenly, unhurried—the knock of someone who expected the door to open. August went silent, knife sliding free, weight settling onto the balls of her feet. Through the gap under the door: lamplight interrupted by boots. Two sets. No—one set of boots, and the hem of a coat that pooled strangely, as though the wearer stood a half-inch above the floorboards.

"Mistress Okafor." The voice was pleasant, cultured, and utterly wrong, the way the map's blank center was wrong. "We know you have it. We only want to talk."

August blew out the candle.
