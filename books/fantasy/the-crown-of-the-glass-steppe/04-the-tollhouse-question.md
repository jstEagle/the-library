## 4. The Tollhouse Question

Tomas Reyes had a rule about lies: he didn't tell them, and he didn't forgive them cheaply. Fifteen years of working beside August Okafor had taught him that she held to the same standard, only more strictly. August lied the way other people bled—rarely, and never without cost. Her honesty was not kindness; it was architecture. It was the thing her whole life stood on, and he had built his own house against hers, trusting the shared wall.

So when she looked him in the eye over breakfast at the Tollhouse and said, "Nothing's wrong," Tomas knew something was catastrophically wrong.

The morning was ordinary enough to make it worse. Rain had come in off the western ridges overnight, drumming on the Tollhouse's slate roof, and the common room smelled of wet wool, woodsmoke, and Hesta's barley porridge scorched at the bottom of the pot as always. A pair of quarrymen argued over dice by the window. The fire spat. Everything exactly as it had been for years.

August sat across from him with her boots up on the bench, eating like a woman who intended to keep doing it for a long time—which was itself strange, because August ate like a soldier, fast and without ceremony, as if meals were an interruption to be endured. Today she'd taken three helpings. She'd asked Hesta for honey. Honey. In eleven years of sharing this table, Tomas had never once seen August ask for honey.

And now: *nothing's wrong*, delivered flat and steady, her eyes holding his the way they did when she wanted him to stop asking questions.

"Right," Tomas said. "Nothing's wrong."

"Good."

"You've got honey on your chin."

She wiped it off without looking, which meant she wasn't embarrassed, which meant she hadn't noticed, which meant her mind was somewhere very far away. He watched her catalog the room out of habit—the doors, the windows, the quarrymen, the rain—and then, unusually, watch herself doing it, and frown slightly, as if she'd caught her own hands moving without permission.

"The courier job paid well," she said. "I'm thinking of taking the winter easy."

Tomas put down his spoon. "You hate winters idle. You told me last month that idleness is how people rot."

"People change."

"People change." He said it slowly, tasting it. "August. Fifteen years. I have watched you take a knife wound in the shoulder rather than lie to a client about how bad it was, because you said a man paying for your protection deserved to know what he was protecting. I have watched you return stolen money to a widow who'd hired us to steal it back from her, because the contract was badly worded and you said words matter even when they're stupid. And now you're telling me you want a quiet winter, and you're wearing the face you wear when you're lying to bandits about where we've hidden their horses."

The quarrymen's dice rattled. Rain filled the pause.

August looked at him then—really looked, and there was something underneath the steadiness that frightened him more than any lie would have. She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. She looked like someone carrying water in cupped hands across a long distance, terrified of spilling.

"Tomas." His name, just his name, gentle as a hand on a door being closed. "Some things are mine to carry."

"That's not how we work."

"It's how I work."

"Not fifteen minutes ago it wasn't."

She smiled, and it was the worst thing yet, because it was warm and real and it made everything else worse. "Eat your porridge," she said. "It's getting cold."

He ate his porridge. It tasted like nothing. Across the table, August finished her third helping, thanked Hesta by name, left coins too many by half, and walked out into the rain without her cloak, which she had never once forgotten in fifteen years.

* * *

He gave her until noon.

Not because he lacked the nerve to follow her sooner, but because following August Okafor required patience the way swimming required water. She had eyes in the back of her head—not a figure of speech; he'd tested it once, young and cocky, and spent a week hearing about it. If she wanted to lose him, she would lose him. If she let him follow, some part of her wanted to be followed, and that part needed time to win the argument with the rest of her.

So he worked. There was a contract outstanding—a merchant who wanted his warehouse watched—and Tomas sat in the loft above it all afternoon with rain coming through a broken shutter, watching August's window across the lane instead of the warehouse door. Light moved behind her shutters. Once, a shadow crossed it and stopped, and stayed stopped for a long time, and he imagined her standing over the table where she kept her few possessions, deciding what to do with them.

At dusk she came out carrying a satchel.

Not a travel pack. A satchel—small, light, the kind of bag you took if you expected to come back, or if you were lying to yourself about expecting to come back. She wore her cloak this time, hood up, and she went not down toward the market or the guildhall but up, along the mill road, past the last of the lamplighters, toward the valley's edge.

The Hollow ended at a line most people never thought about. That was the trick of it, Tomas supposed—that no one thought about it. The valley simply felt complete, the way a room feels complete, and the ridge roads that climbed out of it felt like the edges of a map drawn by someone who lost interest. Merchants went out and came back and could never quite say what was out there. Hunters swore the deer beyond the high passes were bigger, stranger, wrong somehow. Children who wandered too far up the switchbacks came home with scraped knees and no memory of the afternoon, and their mothers laughed about it, and nobody found the laughing strange.

Tomas had gone to the edge exactly once, at nineteen, drunk on plum brandy and grief after his father's funeral, determined to walk out of the valley and see the world he'd been promised existed. He remembered the climb. He remembered the rain. He remembered reaching the boundary stone at the top of the pass—an old thing, moss-eaten, carved with a spiral worn nearly smooth—and he remembered stopping, because the air beyond it looked like the air inside a struck bell. He remembered turning around. He remembered walking home. He did not remember deciding to turn around, and that absence had lived in him ever since, small and cold, like a stone in a boot you eventually stop noticing.

Now he climbed the same road sober, in the dark, keeping August's lantern-glow in sight through the trees.

She moved well. She always moved well, but tonight there was something almost ritualistic in it, checking her backtrail at intervals so precise they might have been prayer. Twice she paused long enough that he had to press himself against wet bark and count his heartbeats. The rain helped. The rain covered sound and blurred lantern-light into a smudge, and the valley seemed to hold its breath around them both, the way it did before storms—or, he realized with a chill that had nothing to do with cold, the way it did whenever anyone climbed too high.

She reached the boundary stone near midnight.

Tomas watched from thirty yards below, flattened behind a granite shelf, rain running down his neck, as August stopped before the mossy spiral and stood there a long moment. Then she did something he had never seen her do. She put her bare hand flat against the stone, fingers spread, the way you'd touch a door you weren't sure you wanted to open—or a grave.

Her lips moved. Too far to hear the words. But he saw her shoulders shake once, saw her bow her head over her own hand, and every instinct in his body screamed at him to go to her, and every year of knowing her screamed louder to stay still. August would rather be flayed than be pitied. August would rather die than be stopped by someone who loved her, if being stopped meant being kept.

That was the trouble with loving August Okafor. You learned early that love, offered carelessly, read as a cage.

She stepped over the line.

Tomas saw it happen—he would swear to his dying day that he saw it—as a kind of shimmer, like heat off summer stone, except it was midnight and raining and cold. One stride she was on the valley side, lantern lit, solid, herself. Next stride she was beyond the stone, and the lantern-flame bent strangely, and the darkness past the boundary seemed to lean toward her, hungry, attentive, awake.

Then she was gone down the far side of the pass, and the night closed behind her like water behind a swimmer.

Tomas stayed crouched in the rain for a long time, doing arithmetic he hated.

She had lied to him. First time in fifteen years. She had packed a satchel, not a pack. She had touched the boundary stone like a mourner. She had gone alone, at night, out of the Hollow—out of the world, maybe—carrying whatever it was she wouldn't share, and the valley, which forgot children's afternoons and merchants' roads, would forget her too. By tomorrow, half the Tollhouse would struggle to place her face. By next week, Hesta would serve two bowls instead of one and not know why the second felt like habit. He had seen it happen with others who left. Nobody spoke of them. Nobody could.

Which meant that if he let her walk out of his life tonight, she would not merely be gone. She would be *unremembered*, and the only person in the world who carried the shape of her would be—

Him. Standing in the rain. Holding it all.

"No," he said aloud, and the word was swallowed by weather, and he said it again anyway. "No. Damn you. No."

He turned back down the mountain, fast now, no longer caring about stealth, because stealth was for following and he was done following. By the time he reached the Tollhouse the eastern sky had gone the grey of old ash, and Hesta was lighting the kitchen fires, and Tomas went upstairs to his room and pulled out the good pack, the one with the waterproofed canvas, and began packing with the cold efficiency of a man loading a weapon.

Rope. Flint. Dried meat. The whetstone. His father's knife, which he'd sworn never to carry on a job and was carrying now, because this was not a job. Coins. And then, at the bottom of the drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing he had never shown anyone: a small wooden bird, badly carved, that August had made him when they were nineteen and broke and she'd been drunk on victory after their first contract, laughing, saying *so you remember me when I'm famous.* He had kept it fourteen years and told himself it meant nothing, the way he told himself a great many things meant nothing.

He wrapped it in his spare shirt and put it in the pack.

Downstairs, Hesta looked up from her porridge pot, and her face did the terrible soft thing faces did in the Hollow when they couldn't quite find a name. "Morning, love. Early start? Visiting?"

"Hesta." He set coins on the counter, more than the room was worth. "I'm going over the pass. Might be a while."

Something flickered behind her eyes—grief arriving without its reason, a bell tolling with the clapper removed. "Over the pass," she repeated, tasting strangeness. "Well. Mind the weather." And then, helplessly, as he shouldered the pack: "Will you... will you write?"

"I'll come back," Tomas said. "And I'll bring her."

"Bring who, love?"

The question landed like a blade between his ribs, clean and total. This was the price. This was the toll, collected here at the tollhouse, before he'd even taken a step: the valley already loosening its grip on her, already smoothing August Okafor out of the world's memory, leaving only him to carry the debt of her.

"A friend," he said, and his voice held, barely. "The best one I ever had."

Outside, the rain had stopped. The sky over the pass was bruise-colored and clearing, and somewhere beyond the ridge, past the boundary stone, past the edge of everything safe and known, August Okafor was walking into a silence that had swallowed better people than either of them.

Tomas Reyes tightened the straps on his pack, touched the little wooden bird through the canvas once, like a man touching a talisman, and started climbing.

Behind him, the Hollow began, gently and without malice, to forget his name.
