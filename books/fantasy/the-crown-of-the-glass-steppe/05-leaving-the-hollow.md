## 5. Leaving the Hollow

August Okafor had crossed the valley's edge eleven times in her life, and every crossing had been a lie she told with her feet.

Not a lie, exactly. More like an errand. The Hollow's boundary was porous at the eastern rim, where the river cut through the last stand of birch and spilled down the escarpment toward the lowlands. Merchants went out and came back. Messengers went out and came back. August herself had gone out twice for contracts—protection work for a salt merchant who feared his own cousins—and each time she'd returned to find the valley exactly as she'd left it, her cot still holding the shape of her body, her whetstone where she'd left it, Tomas's mug on her table with the chipped rim turned away from the door because he knew it annoyed her.

The valley kept things. That was its gift and its cage. It kept your house warm, kept your debts small, kept your grudges soft-edged until they dissolved. And it kept you—kept your name in the mouths of neighbors, kept your face familiar to the innkeeper's dog. The Hollow remembered so that no one else had to. That was the bargain nobody remembered making.

But today the valley was letting go of something, and August could feel it in the way the morning light came through the birches: too sharp, too specific, like light through a crack in a wall that had always been solid.

She stood at the tree line above the ford with the courier's map pressed flat against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth beneath her coat, and looked back the way she'd come.

* * *

Tomas had followed her. Of course he had. She'd known it before she reached the second mile marker—the particular quality of silence behind her that meant someone skilled was trying not to be heard, and only one person in the valley was skilled enough that she almost hadn't caught it. Almost. Fifteen years of partnership bought you that much: you learned the sound of a man deciding whether to speak.

He'd caught up at the split willow, falling into step without invitation, and they'd walked half a mile in the kind of quiet that was itself an argument.

"You lied to me," he said finally.

"I didn't."

"You told Reyes—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "You told *me* the courier died of a gut wound. You said it plain as bread. I've watched men die of gut wounds my whole career, August. That wasn't a gut wound."

"It was a wound. In the gut region."

"Saints' teeth." He laughed once, without any humor in it. "Fifteen years. You've never lied to me. Not even when lying would've been kinder. Not even about the Voss job, and we agreed never to discuss the Voss job." He walked three more paces. "So either you're dying, or something out there is worse than dying, and you're going alone because that's what you do—you carry things off alone and bring them back solved, like a cat with a dead bird."

"Tomas—"

"Don't." His voice cracked, just slightly, and he hated it; she could see him hating it. "Don't manage me. Don't decide for me. Whatever's under that map, whatever killed that man at the ford—I'm coming."

They had stopped then, in the shadow of the split willow, and August had looked at him—at the gray coming into his beard early because he refused to slow down, at the scar through his eyebrow from the winter they were nineteen, at all the years of unspoken thing sitting between them like a third traveler—and she had said the hardest words of her life:

"No."

And then, because she owed him more than refusal, she'd added: "If I'm wrong, you lose nothing but a walk. If I'm right, everyone who follows me becomes part of it. The bandits said a name, Tomas. A name that shouldn't exist. The valley is forgetting harder this season, and forgetting isn't weather—it has a cause, and causes have edges, and I intend to find the edge." She'd touched the map through her coat. "I go out. I look. If there's nothing, I come back in a month and buy you dinner and let you be insufferable about it. If there's something—" She'd shrugged, and hated how casual she made it sound. "Then the fewer people tied to me, the better."

"And if the valley won't let you back?"

That was the question, wasn't it. She hadn't answered. He'd understood anyway, because he always did, and the understanding had sat between them like a blade neither would pick up.

He'd stopped at the tree line. She'd told him to. He'd obeyed, which frightened her more than argument would have, and now she stood looking back at him—a dark shape against pale trunks, arms crossed, not waving, not calling out, just watching, the way you watch someone walk into deep water.

* * *

The boundary was invisible. Everyone knew that. Old-timers swore you could feel it on humid days, a pressure like the moment before a storm; children dared each other to run past the marker stones and back, giggling at nothing. There was nothing to see. There was only the sense—universal, unquestioned—that beyond the last birch the world became *other*, and that otherness was best left to merchants and messengers and people whose business required them to be forgettable.

August walked down through the birches toward the marker stone at the river's edge. Her boots crunched frost. Her breath hung in the air. Ordinary morning. Ordinary valley. Except that the light kept doing that thing—coming too sharp, too defined, as if the valley were already thinning around her, losing its grip, letting details slip through its fingers like water.

Halfway down the slope she passed a shepherd boy she'd known since he was born. Maret. Good kid. Quick smile, quicker hands. She raised a hand in greeting.

Maret looked straight through her.

Not rudely. Not fearfully. He simply didn't see her, the way you don't see a chair. His eyes slid across hers and continued to the path behind, tracking something that wasn't there, and he walked on, crook over shoulder, humming a tune she'd taught him herself three summers ago.

August stood very still.

The valley was forgetting her early. Before she'd even crossed. As if the boundary, sensing her intent, had begun the work already—as if leaving and being forgotten were the same act, two sides of one motion, and there was no version of this where she stepped out clean.

Her hands shook. She watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to a woman in a story she'd been told long ago. Then she breathed—once, twice, the way she'd trained herself to breathe before a fight, because that's what this was, whatever else it was—and kept walking.

At the marker stone she stopped. It was a squat granite thing, older than the town, older than any record, worn smooth by centuries of hands touching it the way you touch wood after telling a superstition. Travelers touched it going out. Travelers touched it coming back. Nobody remembered why.

August put her palm flat against the stone.

It was warm. That was the first wrong thing. Granite in late autumn should hold the night's cold, and this stone held warmth instead—body-warm, blood-warm, as though something enormous slept just beneath the valley floor and the stone was a window onto its skin.

The second wrong thing came as a sound. Or rather, as the absence of a sound she'd never noticed until it began to change: a hum underneath everything, so constant and total that it had been inaudible her entire life, the way a fish doesn't hear water. Now, with her hand on the stone and her feet on the line, the hum faltered. Stuttered. Thinned like a voice running out of breath.

*Oh,* August thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *It's not the valley humming. It's the valley being silenced.*

She lifted her hand. The warmth stayed in her palm like a brand.

She stepped across the line.

* * *

There was no flash. No thunderclap. No curtain of shimmering air parting before her, which was somehow worse—all those childhood stories about the edge of the world, and the truth was just one footfall, grass to grass, birch-shadow to open sky.

What there was, was pressure.

It hit her ears first, a sudden deep compression like diving into cold water, and she staggered, clapping a hand to one ear as if she could pop the blockage, as if the whole world had changed altitude in a single step. Her sinuses ached. Her teeth buzzed. The colors ahead of her—the rolling grassland falling away toward the escarpment, the river glinting silver-brown below, the enormous naked sky—were too vivid, oversaturated, each blade of grass distinct and insisting on itself, and August realized with a lurch that this was what magic felt like from the outside: not fireworks, not wonder, but *attention*. The world paying attention to itself, and to her, insistently, overwhelmingly, after a lifetime of living inside a muffle.

She knelt in the wet grass and was quietly, thoroughly sick.

When she finished, she stayed kneeling, hands braced on her thighs, breathing. The pressure eased slowly, the way ears adjust to depth, and as it eased, the sounds came back—but changed. Wind in grass, yes. River over stones, yes. But underneath both, steady and vast and patient as geology:

A heartbeat.

Under the ground. Under everything. Slow—slower than any heart she'd ever heard, each beat arriving with the weight of a bell tolling in a cathedral built out of the earth itself. *Thum. Thum.* She pressed her palm flat to the soil and felt it more than heard it, a tremor in the bones of the world, and tears came to her eyes without permission, because the sound was grief. There was no other word for it. Something impossibly large, buried alive, beating in the dark, beating and beating, and no one above ground listening except a mercenary with sick on her chin kneeling in the grass of a country she'd never seen.

"The crown remembers," the courier had whispered. "The god is not silent. The god is *bound*."

August wiped her face with her sleeve and stood up.

Behind her—she felt it rather than saw it, a sensation like a stitch pulling loose somewhere behind her sternum—the valley closed. She turned. The Hollow lay below in its cup of hills, smoke rising from chimneys, ordinary and complete, and it looked no different than it ever had. But she knew, the way you know a door has locked behind you, that the valley had already smoothed over the space where she'd been. Maret would not remember teaching-lessons or stolen apples. The innkeeper's dog would not lift its head at her name. Tomas—

Tomas was still standing at the tree line. She could see him, a small dark figure, and she raised her arm—not a wave, exactly, more a salute, a soldier's acknowledgment across a distance that had become uncrossable.

He raised his arm back.

Then he turned and walked down into the valley, into the place that would forget him by nightfall, that would fold him back into itself and smooth him over like a thumbprint in wax, and August watched until the birches took him, and then she watched the empty tree line for a while longer, because some things deserved watching even after they were gone.

* * *

She walked east all day.

The land rose gently away from the river, grassland giving way to broken ground, and by afternoon she could see the Glass Steppe far ahead—a faint glitter at the horizon, like sun on ice, though the season was too early for ice. Between her and it stretched emptiness: no farms, no roads worth the name, just wind and grass going gold and gray, and the heartbeat beneath it all growing—she was almost sure—marginally stronger as she moved east. Or perhaps she was merely learning to hear it. Perhaps it had always been this loud and the valley had spent her whole life teaching her deafness.

She made camp at dusk in the lee of a rock formation, ate hard bread and dried meat, checked the map by the last light. The blank center stared back at her, ink restless at its edges when she wasn't looking directly at it. She wrapped it away quickly.

Sleep came in fragments. In the gaps between them, she dreamed of the split willow, of Tomas's face saying *don't decide for me*, of a name spoken by bandits in the dark that had no right to exist in anyone's mouth. She dreamed of a heartbeat, and woke once in full darkness certain she could feel it through the bedroll, through the rock, rising up through her spine like a second pulse laid over her own, slower, vaster, patient as debt.

Near dawn, she woke to light where there should have been none.

Not firelight. Not dawn—dawn was hours off. This glow came from the east, from the Steppe, a pale luminance along the ground like moonrise happening sideways, and as she watched, it pulsed. Once. Twice.

On the same rhythm as the heartbeat.

August sat up, hand finding her knife out of habit, and looked east at the shining ground, and understood that she was looking at memory—pooled and stored and lit from within, an ocean of everything the world had forgotten, waiting for her at the horizon.

Somewhere out there, a king wore a crown that screamed. Somewhere behind her, a man she loved was learning to live in a valley that no longer contained her. And somewhere beneath her knees, a god beat its heart in the dark, counting the seconds of a captivity measured in centuries.

August broke camp in the dark and walked toward the light.
