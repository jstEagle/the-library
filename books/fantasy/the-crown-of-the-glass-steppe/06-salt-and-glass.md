## 6. Salt and Glass

The Hollow had taught August Okafor to read ground the way other people read faces. A scuffed stone said *someone passed here, hurrying*. A snapped branch said *not long ago, and not careful*. So when the valley's edge finally fell away behind her and the land opened into the Steppe, she did what she always did: she read it.

And the Steppe looked back.

It began gently enough—a plain of pale grass gone silver at the tips, bending in a wind that carried no smell of rain. But within an hour of walking, the grass thinned, then surrendered altogether, and the ground beneath her boots turned to glass. Not smooth glass, either. It was rippled, folded, frozen mid-motion like the surface of a river caught in the instant of breaking over stones. Light moved under it in slow veins of green and gold, and when she crouched to touch it, it was warm. Warm as skin.

She straightened slowly, hand still tingling.

"Tomas would hate this," she murmured, and was surprised by how much she missed him already—missed the weight of his skepticism beside her, the way he'd have said something dry and grounding about walking on a giant pane of ice with nothing underneath but whatever wanted to be underneath. She'd left him at the boundary without goodbye. That had been necessary. Necessary things were rarely kind.

The map rode against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, and every few miles she checked it out of habit rather than need. The blank center remained blank. The ink remained restless. She had stopped trying to catch it moving; it only moved when unwatched, like a cat, like guilt.

By late afternoon the terrain changed again, and this time there was no mistaking it for anything natural.

They were bodies.

Not whole—not anymore—but shapes. Ridges beneath the glass where men and horses had fallen, thousands upon thousands of them, their outlines preserved the way flies are preserved in amber. Some lay in ranks so orderly they must have died standing in formation. Others were tangled, one reaching toward another across a gap of centuries. Here and there a spear still stood upright, its shaft crystallized to the same clarity as the ground, its point dissolving into the glass as if the weapon had been drinking the earth and forgotten how to stop.

August walked between them for a long time before she let herself speak.

"Who fought here?"

No answer came, of course. But the light under the glass pulsed once, faintly, like a heartbeat heard through a wall—and she thought of the sound she'd felt crossing the boundary line, that deep, patient rhythm beneath the world. Louder now. Closer. Whatever slept under the Steppe, she was walking toward it with every step, and it seemed to know.

She made camp that night in a hollow between two fallen giants whose armor had fused with the ground. She built no fire. Something in her gut said fire would be rude here, or worse—visible. She chewed dried meat and watched the stars come out, doubled, because the sky above her was reflected perfectly in the glass below, and for a moment she could not tell which way was up. She lay in the middle of the heavens and did not sleep much.

* * *

Morning brought the pools.

She smelled them before she saw them—salt and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun. Then the glass opened into shallow basins, dozens of them scattered across the plain, each filled with liquid so clear it seemed less like water than like absence given shape. They caught the dawn and held it. When the wind moved across them they didn't ripple the way water should. They shivered, all together, like a herd of animals sensing a predator.

August circled the nearest pool three times before she touched it.

The liquid was cool, then warm, then cool again, as though it couldn't decide what temperature a memory should be. She cupped a handful and watched it sit in her palm, heavier than water had any right to be, catching the light in colors that shifted when she wasn't looking directly at them—the same trick as the map's ink. Memory pooled like water, the courier hadn't said, but the map's blankness had whispered it, and now the whisper was everywhere.

She should have poured it out. She knew that even as she raised her hand to her mouth. Fifteen years of mercenary work had built her survival on a single principle: *don't put strange things inside your body.* But the Hollow had taken everything from her once—her neighbors' names, her mother's face, the reason she flinched at bells—and the silence of that theft had shaped her whole life. And here, spread across a battlefield older than any kingdom she'd served, was the opposite of silence. Here was remembering, thick on the ground, free for the drinking.

Thirst won. Not thirst of the throat. Thirst of the thirty years spent as a woman made of gaps.

She drank.

* * *

It took her like a wave takes a swimmer who has waded out past her depth.

She was kneeling on stone steps in a hall of white pillars, and her hands—*not her hands*, broad and ringed and trembling—were pressed flat against cold marble. She was old. She could feel it in the ache of her back, the looseness of her skin, the way her breath came shallow. She was wearing a crown, and the crown was heavy in a way that had nothing to do with gold.

Someone knelt before her. She could not see the face—memory kept sliding off it like rain off oiled leather—but she could see the hands, young hands, scarred across the knuckles, clasped around a sword hilt laid flat on the steps in surrender.

"I did not know," the king heard himself say, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "I swear it by the salt and the glass—I did not know what they were forging until it was done."

The kneeling figure said nothing. The silence had a texture. It was the silence of someone who has already forgiven and cannot say so, or has already condemned and sees no point.

"There were seven of them," the king went on, and his hands curled against the marble, nails scraping stone. "Seven ministers. They told me the god's voice was a danger—that Veyl sang the winters long and short, decided which fields drowned and which went dry, and that no people should live at the mercy of a song. I believed them. Gods, help me, I *wanted* to believe them, because if the voice could be bound, then I would never again kneel in the dark wondering whether the singing would stop." He laughed, and it was the worst sound August had ever heard, worse than the bandits saying her childhood name, worse than the courier's last wet breath. "And now I kneel in the dark anyway, and the thing they bound wears me like a coat, and I can feel it smiling with my mouth."

He reached down—one hand lifting from the marble, shaking—and touched the kneeling figure's bowed head. Almost a blessing. Almost a beggar's grip.

"Take it off me," he whispered. "Take the crown off me. I will give up the throne, the name, the years—I will go into the Steppe and lie down among my soldiers and let the glass have me. Only take it off. Only let me be *no one* again."

The memory shuddered. The pillars flickered like candlelight behind smoke. And through the king's fading senses August felt it—the crown—felt it *notice* her noticing, a cold turning at the back of the skull, vast and patient and interested, the attention of something that had been waiting a very long time to be looked at—

August tore herself sideways out of the pool and was sick into the glass grass, hands braced on ground that was warm as a living throat.

* * *

She sat back on her heels for a long time afterward, wiping her mouth, watching the pool she'd drunk from. Its surface had gone still and ordinary. If she hadn't known better she'd have called it rainwater.

*A king begging forgiveness.* The words turned over in her mind like stones in a current. A king from centuries past—or was it? The hands had been old, but the hall had been whole, unfrozen, unruined. The ministers had been alive and named. Which meant the memory was recent enough to matter and old enough that no one had ever answered it.

No one had ever taken the crown off him.

August looked down the long slope of the Steppe, toward the horizon where the light under the glass gathered thickest, green-gold and pulsing, slow as a sleeping pulse. The heartbeat. Louder here than it had been at the boundary line. She pressed her palm flat against the ground and felt it answer through her bones, and beneath the awe that rose in her chest—genuine awe, the first she'd felt since childhood—something colder coiled and tightened.

Because she understood now what the pools were. They weren't water. They were runoff. Every soul that had fallen on this field had emptied its remembering into the ground, and the ground had nowhere to put it, because the thing that should have been carrying memory—the voice, the song, the god—had been sewn shut and stolen away. The Steppe was drowning in what it could not hold. The pools were the world's grief, standing in low places like floodwater after a dam broke.

And somewhere ahead, a king was still kneeling on those white steps, still begging, still wearing the smile of the thing that had eaten his life one year at a time.

The courier's words came back to her: *the crown remembers.* She had thought it a warning. Standing on a battlefield of the remembered dead, feeling a god's heart beat under her knees, August understood it differently now.

It wasn't a warning.

It was a confession. The crown remembered everything it had ever taken—and it was still hungry.

Far off across the glass, at the edge of sight where heat shimmer blurred the world, a figure was walking toward her. Small yet. A dark speck against the silvered plain, unhurried, following a line as straight as if drawn on a chart. Straight toward the pools. Straight toward her.

August rose, wiped her hands on her thighs, and settled her weight the way she did before trouble—balanced, quiet, ready. Whoever crossed a battlefield alone, unarmed, walking that certain, had either nothing left to lose or something they wanted badly.

Both kinds, in her experience, were dangerous.

She watched the speck grow, and under her boots the god's heart beat on, and the pools shivered together in a wind that smelled of salt and honey and, faintly now, of snow.
