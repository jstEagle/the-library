## 7. The Cartographer's Daughter

The tent smelled of salt, lamp oil, and other people's lives.

Sefa had arranged the bottles that morning the way she arranged them every morning: by weight of grief. The heaviest sat nearest her sleeping roll—jars of amber glass holding a fisherman's last summer, a bride's first quarrel, a shepherd's memory of his mother's hands kneading bread. Those were the ones people wept over when they came to trade. The lighter ones—the color of a stranger's eyes, the taste of rain on a particular roof—she kept in wicker baskets by the door, cheap as buttons, sold to children who wanted to know what it felt like to be someone else for an afternoon.

She was capping a fresh bottle with wax when the flap of her tent moved and two figures stood silhouetted against the white glare of the Steppe.

One was tall and broad-shouldered, sunburned, moving like a man who expected the ground to argue with him. The other was smaller, dark-skinned, with a sword worn low on the hip and eyes that went around the tent once, cataloguing exits, before settling on Sefa herself.

"Memory-trader," the tall one said, in the flat accent of the far valleys. "We were told you buy and sell."

"I buy," Sefa said. She set down the bottle. "What I sell depends on what you're carrying."

"We're not selling." The woman stepped forward into the light of the hanging lamps, and Sefa saw her properly: a scar splitting one eyebrow, hands calloused in the pattern of someone who'd held weapons since girlhood, and an expression carefully emptied of everything except polite attention. A mercenary's face. Sefa had grown up among people who traded faces like currency; she knew a mask when she saw one. "We're told you know the Steppe. All of it. Even the parts that move."

"The Steppe doesn't move," Sefa said. "People just refuse to stand still long enough to map it."

Something flickered across the woman's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Then perhaps you can help us stand still."

* * *

Her name was August Okafor. Her companion was Tomas Reyes. They drank Sefa's weak barley tea without complaint, which raised them slightly in her estimation, and they asked their questions in the careful way of people who have already decided how much truth they can afford.

There was a map. There was a silence at the center of it. There was a heartbeat under the ice that August claimed she could hear when the wind dropped—a sound she'd been following since the valley's edge, fainter some days, louder on others, patient as a pulse.

"And you want me to take you to it." Sefa turned her cup slowly on its saucer. Outside, the wind worried the tent ropes, and somewhere beyond the canvas the glass fields sang their high thin note, the one that meant cold coming. "Why would I do that? The heartlands are death this time of year even without whatever's calling you. Snow comes early now. Snow that eats."

"Eats what?"

"Footprints. Names. Last week a caravan came through from the east—thirty people, maybe more. By the time they reached my tent they'd forgotten which direction they'd come from, and two of them couldn't remember their own faces." She let that sit. Tomas had stopped drinking. "They paid me in memories of home just so they'd have something to carry forward. That's the winter now. It isn't weather. It's an appetite."

August set her cup down with the deliberate care of a woman keeping her hands busy. "You've heard it too. The heartbeat."

"I hear everything on this Steppe," Sefa said. "It's what I do. I listen, and I bottle what I catch." She rose, crossed to the shelf near her bedroll, and took down a jar of green glass, old, its wax seal cracked and mended twice. Inside, instead of the usual amber shimmer, something moved like smoke underwater. "I caught this three years ago, standing on the ridge above the Saltglass Flats. Listen."

She unstopped the jar, and the tent filled—not with sound exactly, but with the memory of sound, the way a shell holds the sea. A rhythm. Deep, slow, enormous. *Lub-dub. Lub-dub.* The floor of the world, breathing.

Tomas made a small involuntary noise. August had gone very still, the stillness of a hunting cat or a woman hearing a voice she'd been promised was only wind.

"You've had this for three years," August said quietly, "and you haven't followed it?"

"I'm a trader, not a pilgrim." Sefa recorked the jar. "Things that beat under the ground don't pay."

"No," August agreed. "But maps might."

And she unrolled it on Sefa's table.

* * *

Later, Sefa would think about that moment often—how close she'd come to throwing them both out, and how the whole shape of her life had turned on the angle of lamplight falling across a sheet of vellum.

The map was beautiful. That was the first thing. Even travel-worn, even stained with river water and blood that wasn't its owner's, it was drawn with a hand so sure and strange that the Steppe seemed to rise off it: the salt flats rendered as fine crosshatching like frost on a window, the glass fields done in washes of pale blue and green, each crystalline ridge picked out with a precision that could only have come from someone who had walked every step of it. And at the center—

At the center, nothing. A blank space the size of a man's palm, where the ink simply refused. Not faded. Not scraped away. *Refused*, the way a mouth refuses a word. Sefa had seen blankness like that before, in her father's late work, and it had always frightened her more than any monster out of a fireside tale.

Because she knew the hand.

She knew the way he drew ridgelines like ribs. She knew the little compass roses with eight points instead of four, because he'd insisted true directions were a courtesy the world didn't deserve. She knew the tiny annotation in the lower margin, half rubbed away: *measure twice, forgive never.*

Sefa's father had walked out onto the Steppe eleven years ago with a satchel of fresh vellum and a promise to be back before the first snow. The snow had come early that year too. She'd waited through it, and through the next winter, and then she'd stopped waiting and started trading—because a cartographer's daughter knows that if you can't find a person, you find the traces they leave, and traces can be bought and sold like anything else.

"This is my father's work," she said.

August looked up. For a moment the mercenary's mask slipped, and underneath it Sefa saw something raw and tired and honest. "Tell me."

"He's been missing eleven years. He mapped this Steppe when no one else would—said it was the only place left where the world hadn't finished deciding what it was." Sefa traced the blank space with one finger, not quite touching it. The ink at the edges shivered, very slightly, the way water trembles when something swims beneath. "He used to say a map is a promise you make to strangers. Draw it right, and you're telling everyone who comes after: *you will survive this road.*" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she kept going anyway. "He left this blank on purpose. Or it left itself blank against his will. Either way, he knew what was at the center. And either way—" she looked up, met August's eyes, and made her voice a merchant's voice, flat and hard, "—I want passage. You want a guide. Here's my price: you take me with you. All the way. Wherever this leads, I go, until we find him or find his end. Then we're square."

"That's not payment," Tomas said. "That's a burial party."

"Tomas." August didn't raise her voice. Something passed between them—old, wordless, worn smooth by years—and he subsided, though his jaw stayed tight.

"It's honest, though," Sefa said. "I'm not pretending I'm doing this for your pretty eyes. I want my father. You want your god. Neither of us gets there alone. Two women using each other—that's the most reliable alliance there is. Nobody has to pretend gratitude."

August studied her for a long moment. Sefa let her. Let her look at the bottles, the jars of other people's summers and sorrows, the cracked wax seals and the neat rows—all of it saying the same thing: *I deal in what people can't bear to lose, and I've lost the one thing I couldn't.*

"All right," August said finally. "Passage for guidance. But I'll tell you the same thing I tell every client: I don't lie to people who bleed beside me. If you come, you come knowing I may ask you to walk toward the thing everyone else runs from."

"My father ran toward things too," Sefa said. "Look how well that worked. At least this way I'll know which direction to grieve."

* * *

They left at dawn, because Sefa refused to travel the glass fields after midday, when the light came off the crystals sideways and showed you reflections that weren't yours.

She broke camp with the efficiency of twenty years' practice—tents down, bottles padded in wool and packed in the reinforced chest she'd built herself, the heavy grief-jars wrapped separately so they wouldn't knock against each other and leak. August watched her work and offered to help, and Sefa waved her off, and then August watched anyway, tracking every motion, and Sefa understood: the woman was memorizing her. Deciding how much to trust her by watching how she did ordinary things.

Good. Sefa was doing the same.

By full light they were three small figures on a sea of pale crystal, the horizon bending up at the edges the way it did here, as though the world were the inside of a bowl. The glass rang faintly under their boots—each step a small clear note, so that walking across the Steppe was a kind of music, and three travelers wrote a thin, wandering melody behind them.

"Does it always sing?" Tomas asked.

"When it wants to." Sefa walked point, as she'd told them she would and they'd accepted without argument, which told her they were smarter than they looked. "My father taught me to read it. Different notes mean different things. This—" she tapped her boot twice, listening to the double chime, "—means hollow underneath. Old battlefield, probably. The war that made all this. Don't wander. The glass holds what fell here, and it likes to show you."

"Show us what?"

"Whatever you'll stop walking for."

They made good distance that day. Toward evening the wind dropped, as it sometimes did, all at once, like a door closing—and in the sudden silence August stopped dead, head tilting, one hand pressed flat against her sternum.

"There," she breathed. "Louder today."

Sefa listened the way her father had taught her—not with her ears but with the soles of her feet, through the crystal, through the packed salt beneath. And there it was. Faint. Enormous. Patient. Not a sound so much as a certainty, rising through the ground like heat through a stone floor.

*It's getting stronger,* she thought, and found her hands were shaking, and hated them too.

They made camp in the lee of a glass ridge, and while Tomas saw to the fire—small, screened, the way you learned to build fires on a landscape that reflected everything—Sefa opened her chest and took out a bottle she rarely showed anyone. Green glass, small, sealed with black wax stamped with a compass rose of eight points.

"My father's last bottle," she said, when August looked at it. "Or the last one I got from him. He sent it ahead with a tinker, six months before he vanished. No letter. Just this."

"What's in it?"

"A memory. His." She turned the bottle in the lamplight. Inside, the memory swam like a silver fish, restless, bumping against the glass as if it wanted out—as if it had spent eleven years trying to finish whatever thought its owner had interrupted. "Memories keep best when they're settled. Finished. A memory of a meal keeps forever. A memory cut off mid-moment goes bad, goes *restless*. This one's the most restless I've ever owned." She looked up. "I've never dared open it. Trader's superstition—you don't uncork what might not go back in. But tomorrow we cross the Saltglass Flats, and past that is the edge of his last survey. If he's alive, he's past there. If he's not—" her thumb moved over the compass rose, back and forth, back and forth, "—then this bottle is the last conversation I'll ever get to have with him. And I think I want to have it standing on his map, not hiding in a tent."

August was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice was lower than before, stripped of the client's politeness. "My mother died when I was nine. In the Hollow, where nothing stays remembered. I have three memories of her and I ration them like water." She nodded at the bottle. "Open it on the flats. I'll stand watch."

That night Sefa lay in her bedroll listening to the glass tick and settle as the cold deepened, and to the heartbeat underneath it all, slow as a funeral drum, and she thought about promises. Her father's maps were promises to strangers. This journey was one too, though she couldn't yet have said to whom—to him, maybe, or to the blank space at the center of everything, saying: *you will not stay unwritten. Someone is coming to measure you.*

She slept, and dreamed of ink refusing paper, and woke before dawn to a sky gone the wrong color—pale, bruised at the eastern edge, like skin over something swelling.

Snow, she thought. Early again. Earlier every year.

She crawled out of the tent and found August already standing at the camp's edge, facing east, her breath pluming, utterly still.

"It changed," August said, without turning. "The heartbeat. Last night, between midnight and morning—it skipped. Like something startled it." She turned then, and in the grey pre-light her face was grim as the horizon. "Something woke it. Or something touched it. We need to move faster than we planned."

Behind them, far to the north, a line of white was building along the sky, silent and total, like a wall being raised by no visible hand.

Sefa looked at it, and at the green bottle in her pack, and at the two strangers who had walked into her tent carrying her father's map like an answered prayer.

"Well," she said, and began striking the tent. "Then we'd better outrun the winter."
