## 8. Winter's Advance Guard

The snow began as a rumor.

Sefa felt it before she saw it—a hush sliding down from the north like a hand pressed over a mouth. The Glass Steppe had always been quiet in the way of dead places, but this was different. This was the silence of something *listening*.

"Stop," she said, and August reined in beside her without argument. That was the thing about the mercenary woman: she argued with everything except the moments that mattered. Three days on the road together and Sefa had already catalogued her habits—the way she checked her knife twice before sleeping, the way she touched the map through her coat like a man touching a wound he'd decided not to look at, the way she said Tomas's name only when she thought no one was listening, which was never, because Sefa heard everything. A childhood spent trading in memories taught you to hear what people carried even when they didn't speak it.

"What?" August asked.

Sefa pointed north. "Snow."

August squinted. "Sky's clear."

"It will be." Sefa swung down from her mule and crouched, pressing two fingers to the glassy ground. Beneath them—far beneath, deeper than any root had ever reached—the god's heartbeat pulsed its slow, wounded rhythm. She'd been tracking it by feel since they'd found the mountain that slept in the shape of a man, its mouth sewn shut with silver thread, and she did not like what the heartbeat was doing now. It had quickened. Or perhaps it hadn't; perhaps everything else had slowed around it, the way a drowning man seems to move faster than the river carrying him under.

"The winter isn't coming," she said. "It's been sent. There's a difference. Weather wanders. This is walking toward us with intent."

August looked at her for a long moment. "You say things like that as if they're ordinary."

"My father said things like that as if they were ordinary too." Sefa stood, brushing salt-glass dust from her knees. "Look where it got him. Missing, and his map in a stranger's hands." She didn't mean it bitterly—or she told herself she didn't—but August flinched anyway, and Sefa noted that too, filed it away with all the other small debts between them. They were using each other honestly. Her father had taught her that honesty between strangers was worth more than love between liars, though he'd also taught her how to bottle a memory so it looked like wine, so perhaps his proverbs deserved suspicion.

They made camp early, in the lee of a shattered siege-engine half-buried in crystal. The Steppe was full of such things: the crystallized bones of an old war, catapults and pikes and men turned to translucent stone mid-scream, their faces still wearing whatever they'd worn in their last moment. Sefa had grown up among them. As a girl she'd played hide-and-seek in the ribs of a frozen giant and thought nothing of it. Now, watching August avoid looking at the soldier-statue nearest their fire—a young man, mouth open, one hand raised as if to catch something falling—she understood that outsiders saw graves where steppe-dwellers saw furniture. Both were wrong. They were neither. They were *sentences*, and the Steppe remembered every word.

She bottled a little of the evening light while August slept. Old habit. Memory pooled here like water in low places, and a trader who couldn't gather it was a trader who starved. But tonight the pooling felt wrong—thick, sluggish, like honey going to sugar. She held a vial up to the fire and frowned at what she'd caught: not a memory at all, just a smear of gray, formless, like fog trapped in glass.

*Something is eating the memories before I can reach them.*

She didn't sleep much after that.

* * *

The snow arrived at dawn, and it arrived wrong.

It fell upward first. Sefa watched flakes rise off the ground like startled birds, hang in the windless air, then drift back down in patterns too deliberate to be chance. The flakes themselves were wrong—each one identical, six-armed, precise as a stamp, where real snow wore chaos like a birthright. And where each flake landed, the ground forgot a little more of itself. She watched it happen: a patch of crystal that had held the impression of an ancient boot-print smoothed over, blanked, polished into featureless white.

"Your footprints," August said quietly. She was standing at the edge of camp, looking back the way they'd come.

Sefa looked. Their trail from yesterday was gone. Not covered—*gone*. No drifts, no softening. The snow had simply erased it, the way a scribe scrapes parchment, and the Steppe beneath lay bare and unmarked as if no one had ever walked there at all.

They walked anyway. What else was there? Behind them the winter advanced; ahead, somewhere south and east, the capital waited with its crown and its stolen god. Sefa navigated by the heartbeat now, feeling for the pulse beneath the ice the way her father had taught her to feel for memory-pools—by faith and fingertip. By noon the snow had stopped pretending to be weather entirely. It fell in columns, in spirals, in shapes that resolved, when she almost looked directly at them, into letters in a script she almost recognized. Her father's script. Her own handwriting, maybe, from some future she hadn't written yet.

"Tell me about him," August said suddenly. "Your father."

"Why?"

"Because you talk about him like a man talking about a debt. Debts have details."

Sefa was silent for a long time. The snow ticked against her hood like insects. "He could hold a memory in his mouth for three days without swallowing it," she said finally. "Party tricks at the markets. He'd take a stranger's happiest hour, keep it behind his teeth overnight, give it back in the morning fresher than they'd left it. People wept. People paid. And underneath—" she shrugged, angry at herself for the thickness in her throat— "underneath he was mapping the Steppe. Every pool, every vein of remembering. He said the Steppe wasn't a battlefield. He said it was a *reservoir*, and someone had dammed it, and if you followed the water far enough upstream you'd find the thief."

"And did he?"

"He got close enough to make enemies." She met August's eyes. "You're not the only one carrying a map you can't explain, mercenary. Mine's just drawn on the inside of my skull."

That night they found the refugees.

* * *

Sefa smelled the smoke before she saw the fires—a thin, greasy burning, wet wood and desperation—and crested a rise to find a camp strung out along a frozen creek bed like beads on a wire. Thirty fires, maybe forty. Wagons. Tents of patched sailcloth. And people, hundreds of people, sitting very still in the snowfall, watching it come down with expressions Sefa recognized from her trade: the blankness of those between memories, waiting for the next one to arrive.

"They're fleeing the winter," August murmured. "From the north."

"No." Sefa's stomach had gone cold in a way the cold around them couldn't account for. "Look at their tracks. Look at the direction of their wagons."

The tracks led *into* the snowfield. South to north. These people had been marching toward the winter, not away from it—and the snow had erased their trail behind them so thoroughly that, Sefa realized with a lurch, they might no longer know which way they'd come. Might no longer know they'd come from anywhere at all.

A sentry challenged them at the camp's edge—an old woman with a billhook and the posture of someone who'd once known how to use it. "State your names," the woman said, and then, before either of them could answer, laughed at herself, short and terrible. "Names. Listen to me. Half this camp's forgotten theirs. We ask so we don't end up the same. You remember yours?"

"Sefa."

"August."

The old woman nodded slowly, tasting the syllables like a trader testing coin. "Good names. Heavy ones. Hold on to them." She jerked her chin toward the fires. "You'll want warmth. Everyone wants warmth. Warmth's the last thing to go."

They gave their names to the fire, in a manner of speaking—paid a cut of their dried meat for a place near the largest blaze—and Sefa spent the evening doing what she did best: listening. The refugees talked the way frightened people talk, in circles, in fragments, and the fragments told a story that made her hands shake inside her gloves.

A dyer from a town called Vell's Crossing—except he wasn't sure it was called that anymore, because the sign had gone blank in his mind, just a white rectangle where letters used to be. A grandmother who kept introducing her grandchildren to each other because she couldn't hold their faces together in one thought; the children had stopped correcting her, which was worse. A priest who prayed to a god whose name slid off him like rain off oilcloth, and who prayed anyway, on the theory that devotion might survive where language failed.

And everywhere, the same sentence, repeated like a bell struck in every tent: *"We fled because—"*

And then nothing. The sentence ended. It ended every time, in every mouth, and the speaker would frown, and start again, and arrive at the same cliff edge. *We fled because.* The reason had been taken. Not lost—taken, scraped clean as the boot-print on the Steppe, and what remained was the shape of an absence, which is the most frightening shape there is.

"They're not refugees," Sefa told August in a low voice, hunched over their shared fire past midnight. "They're the winter's harvest. It's moving through them. It takes the reason first—you can function without a reason—then the names, then the faces. It's advance work. It's clearing the ground."

"Clearing it for what?"

"For forgetting *everything*. For a world with no footprints in it at all." Sefa stared into the flames. "My father wrote that the long winter wouldn't kill anyone. That was the horror of it. It would simply make the world forget it had ever been inhabited. Empty houses, full tables, warm bread going stale because no one remembers eating exists." She paused. "He wrote that the last thing to go, in every case he studied, was love. Not because love is strongest. Because love is the most *specific*. It's harder to erase a particular face than the idea of faces. So it saves love for last, the way you save the best bite."

August said nothing for a long moment. When Sefa glanced over, the mercenary was looking south, toward Meridian, toward a man named Tomas whom she spoke of like a debt.

"Hold on to yours," Sefa said quietly. "Whatever it costs."

"You don't know what it costs."

"I bottle memories for a living. I know exactly what they cost. That's why I charge so much."

* * *

Near dawn, the grandmother died.

Not in body—in body she simply didn't wake, curled in her wagon among children whose names she'd given away one by one like coins from a failing purse. But Sefa, who had risen to check the sky, saw what happened above the wagon, and would see it every night afterward behind her closed eyes.

The snow came down over the old woman in a slow spiral, deliberate as a hand smoothing a bedsheet, and where it touched her face, her face changed. Not aged. Not decayed. *Smoothed.* The lines around her eyes filled in. The scar on her chin faded. Feature by feature, the winter erased her—not into death, which leaves a shape behind, but into anonymity, into the kind of face you forget while looking at it. By the time her grandchildren woke, they stood over a woman who was nobody's grandmother, because she was nobody at all, and they wept without knowing why, which Sefa thought might be the cruelest thing the winter had yet done: it left the grief and took the beloved, so the grief had nothing to attach to and simply drifted, orphaned, through the camp.

The camp buried her in an unmarked grave because no one could agree on what to write on the marker. Some suggested leaving it blank. An argument started. Sefa walked away before she heard how it ended.

She found August awake, sharpening a knife that was already sharp, which is what soldiers do instead of screaming.

"We leave today," Sefa said. "Now, before the light's fully up. This camp is a meal being eaten slowly, and I won't watch."

"The old woman—"

"Is the point. Don't you see it yet?" Sefa's voice cracked and she hated it. "This is what your crown is doing. This is what the thing wearing your king has planned for everyone. Not armies. Not fire. Just—this. Snow that takes your name while you sleep. A world where the graves are blank because the mourners can't remember who's in them." She grabbed August's sleeve, hard. "You wanted stakes, mercenary? You've seen the god sewn shut. Now you've seen the customers. This is what the silence is *for*."

August looked at her. Then, unexpectedly, she reached into her coat and drew out the map—the wax seal broken, the ink shifting restlessly in the gray light, the great blank space at its center seeming, in this moment, less like absence than like appetite. She held it open between them.

"The center's blank," August said. "I've been telling myself it's damaged. Worn away. But watching that woman's face—" She swallowed. "It's not damaged. It's *hungry*. The map isn't missing the middle. The middle is eating."

Above them, the snow fell in its patient, identical flakes, and somewhere beneath the ice the god's heartbeat stuttered—once, twice—like a sleeper turning toward a sound, or a prisoner hearing footsteps that might be rescue or might be the executioner coming to finish the job.

Sefa folded the map with hands that almost didn't shake. "Then we'd better get to Meridian," she said, "before it finishes the appetizers."

They broke camp as the sun rose behind clouds the color of unmarked paper, and behind them the refugee settlement grew small and pale, and the snow went on falling, gently, endlessly, erasing the road ahead and the road behind and, soon, if no one moved fast enough, the roads themselves—the whole winding network of ways that people had walked into meaning since the world was sung.

Three days south, the walls of Meridian rose out of the whiteness like the last sentence of a dying language.

And on the gate, fresh-cut that morning, someone had chiseled a single line that made Sefa's blood run cold, because it was in her father's hand:

*THE CROWN REMEMBERS WHAT YOU FORGET. COME AND TAKE IT BACK.*
