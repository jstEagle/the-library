## 9. The God Beneath the Ice

The heartbeat grew louder as they climbed.

August had felt it first at the edge of the salt flats, three days back—a low pulse through her boot soles, so faint she'd assumed it was her own blood. Sefa had felt it too; the cartographer's daughter had gone still in the middle of breaking camp, one hand pressed flat against the glassy ground, her face doing something complicated that August chose not to name. Tomas had heard nothing at all. He'd joked about it, because Tomas joked about everything: *maybe you two are just pregnant with the same earthquake.* But he'd walked closer to August after that, and stayed closer, and said nothing more about earthquakes.

Now, on the fourth morning of the climb, the pulse was no longer something August felt. It was something she stood inside. Each beat rolled up through the mountain's bones and into her ribs, and for a moment between beats there was a silence so complete it rang.

They had left the horses tethered at the last stand of wind-bent pines. Above that, the Steppe gave way to stone—black basalt veined with seams of white crystal that caught the early light and threw it back in long, cold splinters. The snow here did not erase things the way the winter-snow on the flats had. It lay thin and honest, and their footprints held.

"Veyl," Sefa said, not turning around. She was a few steps ahead, wrapped in a coat made of layered felt and waxed canvas, her breath streaming behind her like a banner. "My father wrote about this place. He called it the Sleeper's Spine." She paused, steadied herself against an outcrop, and looked back at August with eyes the color of wet slate. "I thought it was a metaphor. He was always writing metaphors when he meant to write the truth. It was safer."

"Safer how?" Tomas asked. He was carrying most of their supplies, because Tomas carried things—that was simply what he did—and even now, breathing hard in the thin air, he managed to look like a man out for a walk rather than a man climbing toward whatever this was.

"Safer than writing *there is a god under the world and someone sewed its mouth shut,*" Sefa said. "You can burn a metaphor. You can't arrest one."

August said nothing. She was listening—to the pulse, yes, but also to something underneath the pulse, a texture in it she couldn't yet describe. Like hearing a voice through a wall. Like knowing someone is speaking without being able to make out the words.

They climbed until noon, and then they came over a shoulder of black rock, and August stopped understanding where she was.

* * *

It was not a mountain range. That was the lie her eyes kept trying to tell her, the comfortable lie, the one that said: *peaks and valleys, ridgelines and scree slopes, geology, nothing more.* She stood on a high shelf of stone and looked across a basin five miles wide, ringed by peaks, and the peaks were wrong. They were too regular. They curved. One great ridge swept down from the north and bent at what could only be called an elbow, and another rose and fell in the unmistakable rhythm of a ribcage, and between them the basin floor dipped and swelled in a way that no glacier had ever carved, because glaciers do not carve the shape of a sleeping body.

A face, August realized. The northern face of the range—the snowfields and crags arranged in a profile turned toward the sky—was a face. A jaw. A closed eye the size of a lake, its lid a sweep of ice that glittered like a cataract. And below that jaw, running from ear to ear—if a thing that size could be said to have ears—was a line.

A seam.

Silver thread, miles of it, stitched through living stone. Even at this distance it caught the light, each stitch a bright point along the dark line of the mouth, and the whole seam pulsed—faintly, slowly—with the same rhythm August had been feeling in her boots for four days.

"Oh," Tomas said quietly. All the jokes had gone out of him. "Oh, that's—"

"Don't," August said. Her own voice sounded strange to her, flattened, careful. "Don't say it's beautiful. Don't say anything yet."

Sefa had sunk to her knees. Not in worship—or not only in worship. She had her father's map out of its oilcloth, spread flat against the stone, and she was holding it up beside the horizon, comparing. "He drew it," she whispered. "He drew all of it. The spine, the—the hand, look, that ridge is a hand, folded under the cheek—" Her voice cracked. "He knew. He stood right here and he drew it and then he went to Meridian to tell them, and they took his research and erased him."

The pulse beat. Between beats, the silence.

And in that silence, August finally identified the texture she'd been hearing underneath the rhythm. It wasn't just a heartbeat. It was a heartbeat *and* breathing—slow, labored, obstructed breathing, the kind of breath a person draws through clenched teeth or a crushed throat. The god was not sleeping peacefully beneath the ice. The god was suffocating. Had been suffocating, stitch by silver stitch, for centuries.

Something in August's chest went very cold and very quiet, the way it used to go before a fight, back when fights were simple.

"We're going down there," she said.

* * *

The descent into the basin took the rest of the day. The going was strange—not dangerous, exactly, but disorienting, because the ground itself seemed to lean toward them, or perhaps they leaned toward it, drawn down the slope of that vast chest by something older than gravity. The crystal veins thickened as they descended, widening from threads to ropes to bands wide enough to walk on, and the light they gave off shifted from white to a deep, warm gold, like lamplight seen through honey.

"It's memory," Sefa said, running her gloved hand along one of the golden veins. On the Steppe, memory pooled like water in the glass; here it ran like sap. "Concentrated. Centuries of it. This whole body is—" She stopped, swallowed. "This whole body is *full*. Of everything it ever saw. Everything it sang."

Tomas had gone ahead a few paces, and now he called back, his voice carefully level. "August. You need to see this."

She found him standing at the edge of a hollow in the god's collarbone—a depression the size of a village square, sheltered from the wind, floored with smooth black stone. And the hollow was occupied.

There were structures there. Small ones, crude ones—lean-tos of hide and bone, fire rings black with old ash, racks for drying meat. A camp. An old camp, abandoned, but not ancient: the hides were cracked but intact, the bone tools still sharp. Someone had lived here. Someone had lived *here*, inside the collarbone of a god, and worked—

August stepped past Tomas and saw the work.

The vein of gold memory-light passed directly through the hollow, thick as a fallen tree. And along its length, someone had driven stakes. Iron stakes, black with age, hammered deep into the crystalline flesh, and from stake to stake ran lines of braided cord—hair, August realized with a lurch, human hair, thousands of strands woven together—and the cords were taut, and the gold light strained against them like a river against a weir, pooling, backing up, being *harvested*. At the center of the weir stood a stone basin, and around the basin's rim, incised over and over in a script August didn't recognize but her hindbrain flinched from anyway, was a single repeated word.

Sefa read it aloud, and her voice shook. "*Tithe. Tithe. Tithe.*"

"They've been milking it," Tomas said. He'd gone pale under his windburn. "All this time. The silence in the valley, the crown, the winter—it's not just a prison. It's a farm."

August crouched by the nearest iron stake. The metal was corroded, but the hole around it was clean-edged, recent-maintained. And there, half-buried in drifts against the hollow's wall, she found the rest: sledges with runners worn smooth. Coil after coil of silver thread, fine as spider silk, wound on spools of antler. A ledger, its leather cover stiff with frost, its pages filled with columns of figures in a clerk's precise hand—dates going back further than any kingdom August had ever served, quantities measured in some unit she didn't know, and at the head of each column, a stamp: a crown above a circle.

The crown's mark. The same seal that had been pressed into the wax of the dying courier's map.

"They never stopped," August said. She heard her own voice as if from far away, very calm, which was how she knew she was closest to the edge. "Whatever forged the crown, whoever bound Veyl—they didn't do it once, centuries ago, and walk away. There have been crews here. Harvesting. Maintaining the stitches. Feeding." She looked up at the seam miles above them, the silver thread glinting along the god's sealed mouth. "That's why the thread is still bright. It's being *replaced*."

"Why?" Tomas asked. "What are they doing with it? What does stolen god-voice even *buy*?"

"Everything," Sefa said softly. She was staring at the ledger, at the columns, and August watched her do the arithmetic her father must have died trying to finish. "A voice that sang the world into being. Do you understand? If you can siphon it, filter it, bind it—you don't just get magic. You get *authority*. You get a king whose word reshapes reality. You get winters you can aim at people who disobey. You get a valley where nobody remembers what you took." She looked up, and there was fury in her face now, grief's twin sister. "You get to be the only one who remembers. Forever. That's what they bought."

The pulse beat beneath them. The labored breath dragged through the sealed mouth. And August put her bare hand—she didn't remember deciding to pull off her glove—flat against the golden vein, and the memory in it flooded up her arm like scalding water.

* * *

She was singing.

No—she was *hearing* singing, but the hearing was total, was being the song, every note a law and every law a joy. She hung above a world still soft and unfinished, and her voice poured out and became rivers, became the grammar of roots, became the specific weight of rain on a specific leaf, and it was good, it was so good, creation was not labor it was *laughter*, and beside her—no, within her, around her, part of her—were others, voices in harmony, and one of them was closest of all, one voice that wove counterpoint to hers, and she loved that voice the way fire loves air—

Then hands. Small hands, from below, from the soft new world, reaching up with thread and needle and *purpose*, and the first stitch went through her lips and the pain was not pain, it was *subtraction*, the note she'd been singing torn out mid-breath, and the harmony staggered, and the voice she loved cried out her name—

Her true name. She almost caught it. It dissolved like snow on skin even as she grasped it, but the *cry* remained, the shape of it, the desperation, and then stitch after stitch after stitch, centuries of stitches, and the small hands never tired because they were not hands anymore, they were institutions, they were offices, they were a crown passed from head to head, and the voice below grew weaker and the harvest grew heavier and somewhere in the middle of forever, the other voices stopped calling her name because they had been silenced too, scattered, buried, forgotten, and she was alone, alone under the ice, alone with the tithe-tithe-tithe of her own stolen song—

August came back to herself on her knees in the snow, vomiting bile, Tomas's arm around her shoulders, Sefa shouting her name.

"I'm here," she rasped. "I'm—I'm fine." She wasn't fine. Her hands wouldn't stop shaking. She wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist and looked up at the seam, at the miles of silver thread, and the shaking got worse, and she let it, because what was rising in her now deserved to shake her.

"That was it," she said. "I touched the vein and I *was* it. For a moment. Sefa—" She grabbed the younger woman's sleeve. "It wasn't alone. When it sang. There were others. It said—there was a voice it loved, and it called out, and nothing answered, because they'd already been silenced. The crown isn't holding one prisoner. It's holding the *last* one."

"The others," Tomas said slowly. "The Hollow. Your valley. The silence there—"

"Is a grave," August said. "Or a mass grave. I grew up walking on it." She thought of the Hollow then—its quiet lanes, its forgetful kindness, the way grief slid off everything like rain off oiled cloth—and felt the full weight of it land on her at last. Her whole life had been spent inside the echo of this crime. Every person who'd ever hired her to keep a secret, every neighbor who'd lost a name and smiled about it, every sunset over that valley that felt like a held breath—all of it, all of it, downstream from these stitches.

The courier's blood on her hands came back to her, unbidden. *The crown remembers,* he'd said, and died so the map could travel. And Sefa's father had come here, drawn what he saw, and been erased for it. And the king in Meridian wore a thing that had been eating a god alive since before either of their grandmothers' grandmothers drew breath.

"This is personal now," August said. She stood. Her legs held. "I want that understood. This stopped being a job at the ford, and it stopped being a mystery somewhere on the Steppe, but this—" she gestured at the hollow, the weir, the ledger with its neat crowned stamps, the seam shining in the failing light, "—this is mine now. Whatever it costs. Whoever it costs."

Nobody argued. Tomas put his hand briefly on the back of her neck, the old gesture, wordless, and let it go.

They made camp in the lee of the collarbone, as close to the heartbeat as they could bear, because the warmth of the golden veins was real and the night above the Steppe was lethal. Sefa copied figures from the ledger by crystal-light. Tomas cleaned their blades out of habit more than need. And August sat facing the god's face, watching the ice-lidded eye gleam under the first stars, and spoke into the pulse, quietly, the way you speak to a wounded animal you're trying not to startle.

"I'm going to say your name," she murmured. "Not tonight. But soon. The courier died getting me the map, and Sefa's father died drawing it, and I don't know yet what saying your name will cost—but I know someone has to pay it, and I've decided it's going to be me."

The pulse stuttered.

Just once—one skipped beat, one hitch in the labored breath—and in the silence that followed, August would have sworn, later, all her life, that she felt something turn toward her through miles of stone and ice. Something vast and starving and gentle, straining against silver thread, listening.

Far to the south, back along the way they'd come, a light flickered on the horizon. Too steady for lightning. Too red for a star.

Tomas saw it too. "Campfires," he said. "A lot of them. Moving fast."

Sefa folded her father's map with hands that weren't quite steady. "Crownsguard," she said. "They've found us."

Above them, the god breathed its ruined breath, and waited.
