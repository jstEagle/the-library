## 10. Crownsguard

The dreams began in the third week of autumn, which was also the week Captain Ilsa Vane stopped sleeping well and started sleeping *deeply*—two things that should not have been able to coexist, and yet did, the way a blade can be both sharp and brittle.

She dreamed of a garden she had never walked.

It was always the same garden: walled, overgrown, with a dry fountain at its center shaped like a bird mid-flight. Someone had planted roses along the south wall once, long ago, and no one had pruned them since, so they climbed wild and thorned into the gray light. In the dream she stood among them in a body that was not hers—heavier, older, one knee that ached when the weather turned—and she watched a woman kneel by the fountain and wash her hands in water that wasn't there.

"You could have told me," the woman said.

And Ilsa—wearing the dreamer's mouth, the dreamer's grief—answered, "I was afraid you'd look at me differently."

She woke each morning with the taste of rose petals gone to rot, and each morning she wrote down what she remembered in a small ledger she kept locked in her footlocker, because a captain of the Crownsguard who reported strange dreams would find herself reassigned to gate duty in some border town where nothing happened until she died of boredom or worse. And because—Ilsa admitted this only to herself, in the hour before dawn when honesty is cheapest—the dreams were not unpleasant. They were *intimate*. They felt like being trusted with something.

That was the horror of them, though it took her another two weeks to name it: they felt like being trusted with someone else's most private grief, without that someone's permission.

They were the king's dreams. She understood this the way one understands a thing read in bad light—slowly, reluctantly, and then all at once. King Aldric had never kept a garden; the palace grounds were all clipped yew and gravel, designed by a minister's architect, beautiful the way arithmetic is beautiful. But the man in her dream wept at that dry fountain like a boy, and the man in her dream had a scar on his left palm that Ilsa had seen herself, once, when she'd handed His Majesty a ceremonial sword hilt-first and his glove had slipped. She had said nothing. Captains notice; captains do not comment.

So: the king dreamed of a garden he had never planted, and now Ilsa dreamed it too, and there was no mechanism by which that should be possible, and every explanation she constructed for it ended in a place she did not want to go.

* * *

The orders came on a Tuesday, delivered by a courier whose boots were wrong for the road he claimed to have ridden.

"Captain Vane." He was young, smooth-cheeked, with the particular pallor of men who worked indoors near powerful people. "From Lord Chancellor Merrick's office. Sealed."

She broke the seal in front of him, as protocol demanded, and read twice.

*Captain—A courier of the royal map service, one Edran Voss, has failed to report from the eastern circuit. Intelligence suggests he carried documents of significant state sensitivity and that he may have perished or been robbed in the region known as the Hollow, or upon the approaches thereto. You will proceed with a detachment of six, recover the documents, ascertain the fate of Voss, and return by the winter roads. Discretion is absolute. The Chancellor reminds you that the Hollow is sovereign territory under old treaty and that your warrant does not extend past its boundary line. What lies within belongs to the valley. What lies outside belongs to the crown.*

"What lies outside belongs to the crown," she repeated aloud, and the courier flinched slightly, as men did when she quoted their masters back to them. "Was there anything else?"

"He said—" The young man hesitated. "He said to tell you the king has been asking after maps lately. Asking in his sleep. The Chancellor thought you should know the matter is considered urgent."

"Asking in his sleep," Ilsa said.

"Yes, Captain."

She dismissed him and stood a long while at her window, looking out over the training yard where her guardsmen ran drills in the cold, their breath rising in white flags. Below her, the city of Meridian went about its business: fishwives shouting, bells ringing the quarter-hour, children chasing a hoop down Copperlane. A city that believed its king ruled it. A city that did not know its king had been asking for maps in his sleep—or rather, that something behind the king's eyes had been asking, the way a hand asks for a key it intends to use on a door the hand's owner never chose.

Ilsa had served the crown for nineteen years. She had taken the oath at seventeen with her father's sword, the same sword her grandmother had carried, and the oath had seemed simple then: protect the king, obey the lawful chain of command, keep faith with the realm. Nineteen years later she knew the oath was not simple at all. It was a knot, and every year she pulled one thread taut, another went loose.

Protect the king. But which king? The man who wept at fountains in dreams, or the smiling figure on the throne whose eyes sometimes went still as glass, whose gaze passed through petitioners like light through water?

Obey the lawful chain of command. But the Chancellor's courier wore indoor boots on an outdoor errand, and the Chancellor's seal sat on orders that smelled less like recovery and more like erasure. *Ascertain the fate of Voss.* As if Voss were already a closed file. As if the question were not whether he lived but how thoroughly he had been made to stop.

Keep faith with the realm. And the realm, she thought, watching the children in Copperlane, was not the Chancellor. The realm was the hoop and the fishwives and the bells. The realm was a garden with a dry fountain that a tired man missed so badly he wept.

She packed her kit herself. Six guardsmen, chosen carefully: loyal, competent, and none of them prone to questions. She told herself this was standard practice. She told herself a great many things that week.

* * *

The road east was ugly with early winter. Snow came sideways off the steppe in long gray sheets, and by the fourth day the world had been reduced to white above and white below and the sound of hooves muffled to a whisper, as if the land itself were asking them to be quiet.

On the fifth night, sheltering in a waystation whose keeper had fled or frozen or simply ceased, Ilsa dreamed the garden again—but this time it was different.

This time the woman at the fountain turned around.

In all the previous dreams the woman had kept her back to her, kneeling, washing hands in absent water. Now she stood, and Ilsa saw her face, and the seeing was like a hook going in behind her sternum. It was not a face she knew. It was a face she *recognized*, which is a different and more dangerous thing—a face that fit some shape in her that she hadn't known was empty. Dark-skinned, sharp-eyed, with a scar through one eyebrow and the posture of someone who had learned to stand slightly apart from every room she entered. A mercenary's face. A survivor's face.

"She's coming," the woman said—in the dreamer's voice, the king's voice, coming out of her own mouth while her own mouth hung useless. "The one who carries my song. I can feel her walking. Every step, the thread pulls tighter."

"Who?" Ilsa tried to say. Her dream-tongue wouldn't form it. "Who is coming?"

But the woman only looked at her with terrible gentleness, the way one looks at a guard dog that has begun, against all its training, to whimper.

"You're wearing him," she said. "You're both wearing him. Don't you feel how thin the glove is getting?"

Ilsa woke with snow ticking against the shuttered window and her heart slamming and her hands—she checked—her own. Around her, the six guardsmen slept in their bedrolls, breath steaming, ordinary, human, dreaming whatever small dreams soldiers dream. None of them woke. None of them ever woke during the dreams, and she had begun to suspect that if she screamed, they would not wake even then.

She got up, lit the lamp, and opened her ledger.

By lamplight she read back through three weeks of entries. The garden. The fountain. The woman. And scattered among them, growing more frequent, fragments that were not the garden at all: a corridor of silver thread, thick as ship's cable, running in every direction and all of it anchored somewhere above; a sensation of being *spoken through*, words arriving fully formed in a mouth that merely shaped them; and once, horribly, a memory of memory itself being pulled out—she had written it as *the feeling of a tooth leaving the jaw, but everywhere, but endless*.

At the bottom of the current page, her pen hovering, she wrote:

*The king is not dreaming these dreams. Something is dreaming them through him, and now through me. Question: why me? Answer, possible: proximity is not required. Attention is. I have spent nineteen years paying attention to the crown.*

She sat with that a moment. Then she added:

*Voss carried a map. The Chancellor wants it recovered—or wants confirmation it cannot be recovered. These are not the same order. If Voss gave it to someone—if someone is carrying it east toward the steppe—then the thing in the crown knows, and is pulling, and I am one of the threads.*

She blew out the lamp and lay awake listening to the snow.

* * *

They found the courier's trail on the eighth day, or rather they found where the trail stopped.

It stopped at a river ford on the steppe's western margin, a shallow braided crossing where the water ran glass-clear over pale stone. There had been blood on the bank once—days old, snow-scoured, but the stains had soaked deep and the frost preserved them in brown lace. Two sets of tracks led away from the ford: one limping, one steady. And pressed into the mud near the waterline, half-filled with ice, a wax seal. Royal blue. Broken.

Ilsa crouched over it for a long time while her guardsmen saw to the horses.

"Bandits, Captain?" asked Sergeant Corwin, a solid, unimaginative man she trusted precisely for those qualities. "Robbery on the crossing?"

"Bandits don't leave seals," Ilsa said. "Bandits take purses and ride on." She lifted the wax fragment free of the ice with her knife tip and turned it in the flat gray light. The impression was crisp: the crowned tower of the royal cartographic service. "Someone took the document and left the proof it existed. Or someone was given the document and left the proof behind deliberately, so anyone following would know exactly what had changed hands."

Corwin frowned. "You think whoever has it wants us to follow?"

"I think," Ilsa said slowly, "that everything about this ford is a message, and I haven't finished reading it." She looked east, across the ford, to where the Glass Steppe began its long bright climb toward the horizon—miles of crystallized ground catching the low sun, glittering like a sea that had decided to hold still forever. Somewhere out there, a limping courier had handed a dying man's trust to a stranger, and a stranger was walking into the whitest, strangest country in the world carrying a piece of the king's stolen silence.

Her warrant ended at the waterline. *What lies within belongs to the valley.* But the valley had forgotten itself; everyone knew that much, the treaty's polite fiction. And the tracks—the steady ones—went east, onto the steppe, where treaties were abstractions and snow was busy erasing everything else.

"Captain?" Corwin again. "Orders?"

Ilsa stood. Her knee ached—not her own ache, she realized, the dreamer's ache, the heavy body's ache, bleeding upward into her waking flesh now, the membrane between his life and hers wearing through like old boot leather. The thing in the crown was reaching further every day. Through Aldric. Through her. Perhaps through others; perhaps every soul in Meridian who had ever looked at the king with attention was a thread, and the web was tightening around all of them, and the only person who might cut it was walking east with a dead man's map.

Nineteen years of oath said: recover the document, return, report.

Nineteen years of attention said: the Chancellor's orders are the glove's orders, and you are done being worn.

"We cross," Ilsa said.

Corwin blinked. "The treaty—"

"The treaty protects a valley that can't remember it exists." She stepped to the waterline, to the edge of her warrant, to the edge of everything she had been. "We crossed the real line weeks ago, Sergeant, the first night I started dreaming a dead garden. We're just making the paperwork honest."

She led them over the ford, boots breaking the ice skin on the shallows, and did not look back at the west. Behind them the snow was already filling their tracks, patient and thorough, the winter's advance work. Ahead the steppe blazed under the sun like a field of fallen stars, and far out in that brightness—too far to see, close enough to feel, the way one feels a held breath—Ilsa sensed the pull of the thread go taut.

Somewhere east, the dark-eyed woman with the scarred eyebrow was walking, and the thing in the crown was afraid of her.

Good, Ilsa thought, and was startled by the ferocity of it, and then was not startled at all.

"Hold formation," she called to her six. "We ride until the light fails."

And under the horses' hooves, faint beneath the crystal ground, so faint she might have invented it—she was almost sure she might have invented it—she heard a heartbeat. Slow. Enormous. Patient as winter, and waking.
