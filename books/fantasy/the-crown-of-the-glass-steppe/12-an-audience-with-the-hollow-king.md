## 12. An Audience with the Hollow King

The invitation came on paper the color of old teeth.

August turned it over twice before she believed it. *By order of His Majesty King Aldric the Second of that name, Protector of Meridian and Warden of the Long Peace: August Okafor is granted audience at the winter court, third bell after dawn.* The seal was green wax stamped with a crown whose points curved inward, like talons closing around nothing.

"They know you're here," Sefa said from the window of their rented room, where she'd spent three days watching the street below with a spyglass she'd bought from a man who sold bottled laughter. "Of course they know you're here. The question is whether they knew before Ilsa Vane did, or after."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously." Sefa lowered the glass. In the grey light her face looked older than it had on the Steppe, the lines around her mouth carved deeper by the weeks of snow and silence between them and the city. "If they knew first, this invitation is a trap wearing courtesy. If Vane told them second, then she's playing both sides, and we should be very careful about which side of her we're standing on when the boards give way."

Tomas said nothing. He'd said very little since the night at the inn when he'd caught up to them—since the reckoning, since the words he'd finally spoken aloud that August had spent fifteen years not hearing. He sat sharpening knives he didn't need to sharpen, the whetstone whispering against steel in long, patient strokes, and when August looked at him he looked back with an expression she was still learning how to read: love without apology, fear without shame.

"You don't have to go," he said now.

"Yes, I do."

"The king's own guard tried to take the map off Vane's courier. The thing in that crown knows what you're carrying. You walk into that throne room and you're handing it your name on a plate."

"It already has my name." She folded the invitation along its crease. "Ilsa told us that much. The dreams—the king's dreams leaking into hers. It sees through him. If it wants me found, no wall in Meridian would keep it out." She met Tomas's eyes. "Better to walk in on my feet than wait for it to reach through someone else's hands."

"And if it reaches through yours?"

The whetstone had gone quiet. In the silence August could hear the city waking below them—the bells, the carts, the thin cries of vendors selling bread and hot wine—and beneath all of it, so faint she might have imagined it, the heartbeat. It had grown louder every day since the Steppe. Some mornings she woke with her palm pressed flat against the floorboards, as if her body had gone looking for the sound while her mind slept.

"Then it reaches through mine," she said, "and finds out I'm harder to hold than a king."

* * *

They dressed her for court the way you'd dress a knife for a sheath.

Sefa procured the clothes—a deep blue gown with a high collar, gloves dyed the grey of river stones, a cloak lined with fox fur that smelled of another woman's cedar chest. August stood before the spotted mirror in the rented room and barely recognized the figure there. Not because of the finery. Because of the posture. She'd spent her whole life making herself small, forgettable, a shadow hired by people who valued discretion. Now she stood like someone who expected to be seen.

"You look like a noblewoman," Sefa said critically, tugging a sleeve straight. "Which is a lie, but a well-made one. Remember: curtsy shallow, speak little, let them think you're awed. Awed people are boring, and bored people stop looking."

"And if the king looks at me?"

Sefa's hands stilled. For a moment the cartographer's daughter looked very young, and very far from home. "Then you'll know," she said quietly. "My father wrote that once. About the crown. He wrote—" She stopped, jaw tightening. She still couldn't say the word *father* without something in her voice cracking along an old seam. "He wrote that being looked at by it was like being read. All the way through. Like a letter opened by the wrong hands."

Tomas walked them as far as the palace gates. He wasn't invited—he was nobody, a mercenary with a sword too honest for court—and the guards' eyes slid past him the way eyes slide past anything unimportant, which was exactly how he wanted it. But at the gate he caught August's gloved hand in his bare one, and held it a moment longer than the crowd allowed.

"I'll be outside," he said. "Every bell. If you're not out by dusk, I'm coming in, and I don't care what it costs."

"That's a stupid promise."

"It's the only kind I make." His thumb traced her knuckles through the glove, and his voice dropped to something meant only for her. "Come back remembering me."

She wanted to laugh. She wanted to tell him that of all the things the crown could take, that memory was the one thing she'd defend with her life. But the words caught somewhere behind her sternum, because lately—ever since the Steppe, ever since the pools of glass where other people's memories pooled like rainwater—she'd begun to understand that memory was not a possession. It was a country. And countries could be invaded.

"I will," she said, and meant it the way you mean a vow.

* * *

Meridian's throne room had been built to make visitors feel two things at once: smallness and gratitude for being permitted the smallness.

Pillars of white stone rose into a vaulted dark painted with constellations that didn't match any real sky. Banners hung from the clerestory, the colors of forty vassal houses, and beneath them courtiers clustered in murmuring knots, wrapped in furs against the cold that seeped through even here. Braziers burned at intervals, but their heat seemed afraid to travel. The air had a stillness to it, a held-breath quality, like the inside of a church or a tomb.

August moved through it as Sefa had coached her—shallow curtsy, lowered eyes, boring. A chamberlain with a voice like dry paper announced names ahead of her: petitioners, envoys, a merchant guild delegation asking relief from the winter tariffs. Each group approached the dais, spoke, withdrew. The rhythm of it was almost soothing. Almost ordinary.

Then it was her turn, and she looked up, and saw the king.

Aldric sat on a throne of black wood carved with climbing vines—or perhaps drowning figures; the style made it impossible to say which. He was younger than she'd expected, early forties perhaps, broad-shouldered, with the kind of handsome that courts preferred: symmetrical, unremarkable, easy to paint. He wore white and gold, and he was smiling.

And above his brow, on a circlet of silver thread worked so fine it seemed woven from spider silk and moonlight, sat the crown.

It didn't gleam. That was the first wrong thing. Every surface in the room caught the brazier-light—the pillars, the banners, the courtiers' rings and clasps—but the crown drank it. Light went in and did not come out. Looking at it was like looking down a well at midnight. August felt her stomach drop with the vertigo of depth where there should be none.

And the second wrong thing: the king's smile did not move.

It was a good smile. Warm, even. The corners of his eyes crinkled just enough to suggest genuine pleasure at each petitioner's approach. But it was static. It had been smiling, August realized, since before she entered the room, and it would go on smiling after she left, and it had nothing—*nothing*—to do with whatever was happening behind his eyes.

"Approach," the chamberlain intoned, and August walked the length of the carpet, feeling every eye she was supposed to be too boring to attract, and stopped at the prescribed distance, and curtsied shallowly.

"Lady Okafor," the chamberlain said. "Of the Hollow territories."

A ripple went through the nearest courtiers. Small, quickly suppressed. The Hollow was a joke in Meridian—a valley of mud and silence, famous for nothing. A lady of the Hollow was a contradiction, like a duke of ditches. Let them wonder. Wondering people were distracted people.

"Your Majesty honors me," August said.

The king inclined his head. The smile held. And then—

For half a heartbeat, the smile *flickered*. Not faded—flickered, like a candle in wind, like a mask slipping on a sweaty face. And underneath it August saw the man.

His eyes were wet. Not tears exactly—tears would have been simpler. This was the wetness of exhaustion so profound it had passed through grief and come out the other side into something glassy and strange. His gaze fixed on her with sudden, desperate precision, the way a drowning man fixes on a rope, and his lips began to move.

No sound. None at all. But she could read the shapes.

*Help.*

His lips shaped it again. *Help me.* And then a third word, faster, urgent, his whole face straining against the smile that fought to reassert itself—*run*—

The flicker closed. The smile smoothed back over his features like water healing over a dropped stone, and King Aldric said, in a voice mild and pleasant and utterly empty:

"We are pleased to receive a guest from the valley provinces. The crown values all its subjects, near and far."

The voice was wrong too. August couldn't have said how—not pitch, not accent, nothing so simple. It was wrong the way a puppet's voice is wrong when the puppeteer isn't quite bothering to hide himself. There was no breath behind the words. No weight. A king speaking should sound like a man who had carried decisions until they bent his spine. This voice had never carried anything but itself.

"Your Majesty is gracious," August managed.

"We understand you have traveled a long road." The empty eyes regarded her with polite interest. "Through difficult country. The winter has been... hungry this year."

Somewhere in the room, a courtier laughed softly at nothing. August's skin crawled.

*It's listening through him,* she thought. *This whole audience is it looking at me through a window it keeps clean.*

She kept her face bland, her curtsy precise, her answers short. Yes, she had traveled the Steppe. Yes, the roads had been hard. No, she sought no boon; she came only to pay respects to the Warden of the Long Peace. Each lie tasted like copper. She had never been good at lying—Tomas had said so for fifteen years, half-accusing, half-admiring—and every false syllable felt like walking on river ice, listening for the crack.

And then the king rose from the throne.

It was not a kingly motion. Kings rise slowly, deliberately, letting the room adjust to them. Aldric rose the way a marionette rises when its strings are pulled all at once—too fast, too smooth, joints bending at angles slightly too graceful. The courtiers fell silent mid-murmur. The chamberlain went rigid. And the king descended the three steps of the dais and walked toward August across the frozen carpet, unhurried, smiling, until he stood close enough that she could smell him: cold stone, candle wax, and beneath it something organic and faintly sweet, like fruit beginning to turn.

Up close, the crown was worse. The silver threads weren't decorative. They moved. Slowly, subtly, the way a nest of pale worms moves when you lift the log they're under—writhe and settle, writhe and settle, threading down into the king's hair, into his scalp, vanishing beneath the skin at his temples where faint silver traceries showed through, branching like frost across a windowpane.

"Lady Okafor," the king said, and the pleasant emptiness of the voice remained, but something else entered it now, something that leaned forward through the sound like a face pressing against gauze. "The Hollow. What a curious place. So quiet. So *faithful*."

"Your Majesty is kind to remember it."

"Oh, we remember everything." The smile widened a fraction. Only a fraction. "That is rather our purpose."

His eyes changed.

There was no better word for it. The glassy, exhausted eyes of the man stayed exactly where they were, but something behind them *turned*, the way a lighthouse turns, and the beam came around and fixed on her. August felt it physically—a pressure against her sternum, a coldness moving up her throat, the sensation Sefa's father had written of: being read. All the way through. Her name, her childhood, the ford, the dying courier's blood-warm map, the glass pools, the heartbeat under the ice, Tomas's thumb tracing her knuckles at the gate—all of it laid out and leafed through by something patient and vast and *hungry*, something that had been eating a kingdom one memory at a time for longer than any of these trembling courtiers had been alive.

"August Okafor," the thing in the crown said.

Not the king's voice anymore. This voice came from everywhere—from the pillars, from the painted constellations, from the space behind her ears. The courtiers heard nothing. To them, their king was merely leaning close to exchange pleasantries with a provincial lady. But August heard it fill the entire vaulted dark, soft as snowfall and heavy as a landslide.

"The valley kept you," it murmured. "How clever of the valley. How *generous*. A vessel sealed against forgetting. Do you know how rare you are? Do you know how long we have waited for something preserved?" The smile never wavered. The silver threads writhed. Somewhere very deep behind the king's wet eyes, the man screamed without sound. "We could show you such things. We could show you what your god looked like before we taught it silence. We could—"

The king staggered.

Just slightly—one foot sliding on the carpet, one hand rising to his temple as if against a sudden headache. And in the gap, in the half-second the pressure took to reassert itself, Aldric's true voice scraped out of his throat, cracked and human and ruined:

"*Don't listen—it lies—everything it offers is a door it opens from its side—*"

The pressure slammed back. The king straightened. The smile resumed its post. And the vast soft voice said, with something almost like amusement:

"He tires easily. We apologize for our host." The crowned head tilted, studying her the way a scholar studies a specimen pinned under glass. "Go home to your inn, August Okafor. Walk carefully. Think quietly. We will call for you when we are ready—and we are always, eventually, ready."

The king turned and ascended the dais and sat, and the audience continued, and the chamberlain's dry voice called the next petitioner as though nothing whatsoever had occurred.

August curtsied. Her knees held. She did not know how. She walked the length of the carpet with her spine straight and her face boring and her heart hammering so hard she was certain it echoed off the pillars, and she did not run until the great doors had closed behind her and swallowed the throne room whole.

* * *

Outside, the winter afternoon was already failing. Tomas was where he'd promised—at the gate, every bell, snow gathering on his shoulders because he hadn't moved enough to shake it off. He saw her face and crossed to her in four strides.

"What happened? August. *What happened?*"

She opened her mouth to answer and discovered she was shaking—fine, continuous tremors starting in her hands and working inward, as if her body had only now permitted itself the terror her dignity had deferred. Tomas steadied her with both hands on her arms, and she let him, and for a moment she simply breathed the cold air and watched her breath cloud and vanish.

"He's still in there," she said at last. "The king. Whatever it is, whatever's wearing him—he's still alive in there, Tomas, and he's begging." Her voice cracked on the last word. "Seconds. He gets seconds, maybe less, and he spends them trying to warn me. *Help me. Run. Don't listen.* Fifteen years of that. Maybe more. Trapped behind his own smiling face while it eats him from the inside out."

Tomas's jaw tightened. "And the thing itself?"

"It knows my name." She looked up at him, and the terror in her was already hardening into something else—something colder and more useful, the way fear always had for her, the way it had on every battlefield and back-alley contract of her mercenary years. "It knows what I am. It called me a vessel. It said the valley kept me, preserved me, and that it's been *waiting*." She pulled the map's edge from inside her cloak, just enough to feel the wax seal with her gloved thumb. "It's not trying to stop us anymore. It's inviting us. Which means either it's arrogant—"

"—or it needs you to come," Tomas finished grimly. "And it wants you to think it's your idea."

Behind them, up on the palace walls, a figure in Crownsguard grey and silver stood watching. Too far to see her face clearly, but August knew the set of those shoulders, the stillness of that watchful stance. Captain Ilsa Vane raised one hand—not a wave, not quite a salute. Three fingers extended, then curled closed.

Three. August had seen the guard rotation charts in Ilsa's cramped handwriting. Third watch. Midnight.

Tomorrow night, something was going to happen in that palace—and Ilsa Vane was telling her to be ready.

August closed her fist around the map and turned toward the inn, toward Sefa and the journals and the long dark hours of preparation ahead, while above the city the snow began again—soft, patient, erasing the streets one footprint at a time, like the world practicing for its own forgetting.
