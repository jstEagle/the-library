## 13. Ilsa's Bargain

The dream came again that night, and this time Ilsa Vane did not fight it.

She had learned, over the past three years, to recognize its texture before it fully took her: a warmth like sun through honey-colored glass, the smell of beeswax and old parchment, the particular weight of a crown that was not hers settling on a brow that was not hers either. Most nights she woke gasping, clawing her way back to her own skull as if surfacing from deep water. Tonight she lay still beneath the covers of her narrow bed in the Crownsguard barracks and let the king's memories wash through her like silt-laden river water.

*A garden. Aldric is young in this one—twenty-five, perhaps twenty-six—and his father is still alive, which means the dream predates the coronation by at least a decade. He is kneeling in the dirt with his sleeves rolled up, showing his small daughter how to press a seedling into dark soil. "Gently," he is saying. "You can't force a living thing. You make room for it, and you wait." The girl laughs. Dirt on her nose. His hands, careful as a surgeon's.*

Ilsa had never met the king's daughter. Few people had. Princess Elowen had been sent to her aunt in the southern provinces when she was nine years old, officially for her health. Ilsa had signed the escort orders herself, four years into her captaincy, and had thought nothing of it at the time beyond noting that the king seemed unusually pale that morning.

Now she knew why. Now she understood that the man who had waved goodbye to his daughter from the palace steps had been, even then, only partly present behind his own eyes.

The dream shifted. They always shifted the same way, toward the same terrible shore: *Aldric alone in his study at night, holding a letter he has read a hundred times, mouthing the words because he cannot trust his memory to hold them anymore. "Elowen," he whispers. "My daughter is named Elowen." He writes it on his hand. He writes it on the inside of his cuff. By morning both inscriptions are gone—not smudged, not washed away, simply gone, as though the ink had been drunk.*

Ilsa opened her eyes in the dark. Her heart was hammering, and her cheeks were wet.

Three years of these dreams. Three years of waking with someone else's grief pooled in her chest like cold rainwater. She had told herself at first that it was stress, that the burden of command sat strangely on her, that any officer responsible for the king's personal security might begin to dream his fears. She had confessed it once, carefully, obliquely, to the court physician—an old man named Halloway who had looked at her for a long moment with an expression she had not been able to read until much later, when she understood it had been pity.

"You should sleep more deeply, Captain," he had said. "And perhaps avoid the west wing after dusk."

She had taken his advice. It had not helped. The dreams did not come from proximity; they came from the crown itself, reaching out along whatever thread connected it to the people sworn to protect it, drinking where it could, testing what it could take. She was almost certain of that now. Almost certain, too, of the corollary she had spent two years refusing to examine: that the crown did not only eat the king's memories. It ate anything it was given access to. And somewhere in the archives of that hungry thing, filed among a dead gardener-king's lost decades, were pieces of Captain Ilsa Vane that she could no longer find.

She could not remember her mother's voice. She knew her mother had sung to her—knew it the way you know a fact from a ledger—but the sound itself had been scooped out, leaving a smooth-walled absence. She could not remember the face of the sergeant who had trained her, though she remembered his name, his rank, the scar on his knuckles. She remembered everything about him except him.

*It takes the edges first,* she thought, lying in the dark. *Then it works inward.*

She rose, dressed in the gray working uniform she wore off-duty, buckled her sword out of habit rather than need, and stood for a long moment looking at herself in the small mirror above the basin. A woman of thirty-eight looked back at her: broad-shouldered, close-cropped hair going iron at the temples, a face built for giving orders and receiving bad news without flinching. Her eyes, she noticed, looked tired in a way that no amount of sleep would fix. They looked like the eyes of a woman who had finally finished doing arithmetic she'd been avoiding for years.

Because the stranger was still in the city. The mercenary woman—Okafor—who had walked into Meridian three days ago under a forged identity so competent that Ilsa's own clerks had approved it, and who had then had the audacity to attend a court function wearing another woman's name like a borrowed cloak. Ilsa had watched her across the throne room, watched the false Lady Verene watch the king, and had seen something in the mercenary's face that Ilsa recognized with a shock that had kept her awake ever since: pity. Unhidden, unguarded, terrible pity—the look of someone seeing a caged animal and understanding exactly what the cage was made of.

No one else in that gilded room had looked at the king that way. No one else had *seen* him. They saw the smiling mask, the gracious monarch, the hollow man performing hollow majesty, and they saw nothing wrong, because they had forgotten there was ever anything to compare it against.

But the stranger had seen. Which meant the stranger knew—or suspected enough to be dangerous, or hoped enough to be useful. And Ilsa Vane, who had spent fifteen years building a reputation for absolute, mechanical loyalty, found herself standing in front of a mirror at midnight knowing that she was about to do the single most treasonous thing of her life.

She just hadn't yet decided whether it would also be the most loyal thing she had ever done.

* * *

She chose the meeting place with a tactician's care: the disused chapel in the eastern range of the palace, deconsecrated forty years ago and now used for storing ceremonial furniture. It had one entrance, thick stone walls, and a bell tower whose lower chamber was accessible by a ladder from inside—a detail Ilsa verified personally, because she intended to leave nothing to chance and no one alive who could report the conversation secondhand.

She arranged the approach through channels so indirect that they barely qualified as channels: a note slipped into the lining of a glove returned to the mercenary woman's maid, written in cipher Ilsa had learned from her father, a customs officer who had never trusted governments. *The east chapel. Tomorrow, the hour before dawn. Come armed if it makes you feel safer. Come alone or not at all.—V*

Then she spent the intervening day doing her job so flawlessly that no one could have guessed anything was wrong. She inspected the guard rotations. She corrected a lieutenant's sloppy muster reports. She stood her post at the council chamber door while ministers argued about grain tariffs in voices pitched deliberately loud, aware—as everyone in the palace was quietly aware—that the winter pressing against the walls had stopped being weather and started being siege. Snow fell that afternoon with a strange deliberateness, in fat, silent flakes that did not drift but descended, straight down, like ash. In the outer districts, Ilsa knew, whole streets had gone quiet in ways that had nothing to do with curfew. People stopped mid-sentence and remained stopped. Doors stayed shut. The city was forgetting itself one household at a time, and the council's response was to argue about tariffs.

At the changing of the evening watch, her second-in-command, Lieutenant Corren, paused beside her on the rampart and said, without looking at her, "You've been dreaming again, Captain."

Ilsa kept her eyes on the snow. "What makes you say that?"

"You stand like my grandmother did. She said the dead visit her at night." Corren was a solid, unimaginative man, and Ilsa had always valued him precisely because he noticed things without wanting to understand them. "Whatever it is you're carrying, Captain—if it gets heavy enough that you set it down somewhere, I'd rather not know where."

"That's insubordination, Lieutenant."

"Yes, Captain." He touched his brow in salute and walked away, and Ilsa felt the strange, cold comfort of being known better than she wished to be.

* * *

The hour before dawn was the hour when the palace slept least honestly—servants moving in the walls, guards fighting drowsiness, every corridor full of the particular tension of a place pretending to rest. Ilsa crossed the eastern range by routes she had memorized as a young officer on night drills, avoided the two patrols whose timing she knew to the minute, and let herself into the deconsecrated chapel with a key that officially did not exist.

The stranger was already there.

August Okafor stood between the dusty ranks of stacked chairs, half in shadow, and Ilsa noted with professional approval that she had chosen a position with her back to stone and a clear line to the door. She was smaller than Ilsa had expected—compact, economical, a woman built like a tool kept sharp—and she watched Ilsa enter with eyes the color of river slate that gave away absolutely nothing.

"Captain Vane." Her voice was low, unhurried. "Your note said come armed. I notice you didn't."

"I'm always armed." Ilsa tapped her own temple. "This is the only weapon I trust in a closed room. You'll forgive me if I don't offer you wine. I assume you'd refuse it anyway."

"I'd test it first, and we haven't got time for the ceremony." The mercenary tilted her head. "You went to considerable trouble to reach me. That troubles me. Captains of the Crownsguard don't arrange secret meetings with foreign mercenaries over glove linings unless something has broken."

"Something broke a long time ago." Ilsa walked slowly down the aisle between the furniture, stopping six feet away—close enough to speak softly, far enough that neither of them could grab the other without warning. "I'm going to tell you things that constitute high treason. Before I do, I want you to understand what I am, so you can weigh what I say accordingly. Fifteen years in the Crownsguard. Every commendation available. I have never taken a bribe, never bent a rule, never lied in an official report. My loyalty to the crown is the load-bearing wall of my entire life. Do you follow?"

"I follow."

"And yet." Ilsa heard her own voice go quiet, and hated how much it cost her to continue. "And yet three years ago I began dreaming another woman's—another *man's* memories. The king's. I see his childhood. I see his daughter. I watch him try to write his own child's name on his skin and lose it by morning. And I have checked, Okafor. I have checked like a madwoman checks. The princess's departure papers. The letters between the king and his sister. The physician's records. Ten years ago, Aldric was a different man. Not a perfect man—he was vain, he was stubborn, he once nearly bankrupted the treasury rebuilding the western aqueducts out of sheer aesthetic pride—but he was *present*. He loved loudly. He forgot nothing. And year by year since the coronation, the record shows a man thinning. Fewer letters in his own hand. Fewer appearances. More decisions issued through the chancellery. More smiles that don't reach anywhere."

She drew a breath. The chapel was very cold; their breath hung faintly visible between them.

"The crown is eating him," she said. "Not metaphorically. Not politically. It is consuming his memories the way fire consumes a candle, and it has been doing it for years, and every institution I serve is either complicit or blind, and I—" Her jaw tightened. "I have spent two years telling myself I imagined it, because the alternative was to admit that everything I've built my life on stands on top of a crime."

August Okafor had not moved throughout any of this. But something in her stillness had changed quality—from the wariness of a mercenary assessing a threat to the attention of a person listening to a confession. When she spoke, her voice was gentler than Ilsa expected.

"How long has he got? If nothing changes."

"Months. Perhaps less." Ilsa's throat worked. "The rate is accelerating. The dreams come more often. What reaches me is what the crown discards—it spits out what it can't digest, and some of it lands in the heads of anyone sworn close enough. I get his childhood. I get his grief. Last month I dreamed his wedding, and I woke up weeping for a woman I've never met." She laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what the worst part is? Sometimes those dreams are the closest thing I have to company. I know the king better than his own ministers do. I know him better than I know myself—I *would*, except the crown has been taking from me too. Small things. Edges. My mother's voice. My first sergeant's face. It drinks wherever it can reach, and I let it, because noticing meant acting, and acting meant treason."

"And now?"

"Now the winter is at the gates and a stranger walks into my throne room and looks at my king with pity instead of protocol." Ilsa stepped closer, one deliberate pace, and held the mercenary's gaze. "So here is my bargain, Okafor, and I will only make it once. I can give you the guard rotations. All of them—the shift changes, the patrol timings, the blind spots in the warding, the codes my officers use to pass through the inner checkpoints. With those, a competent infiltrator could reach the king. Without them, your little band of steppe-wanderers dies in an antechamber and the crown keeps feeding for another century."

August's breath caught—just barely, but Ilsa saw it, and saw the calculation begin behind the slate eyes. Good. Let her calculate. Let her understand the size of what was being offered.

"In exchange," Ilsa continued, "you swear me an oath. Not to spare the king. Not to spare the crown. Here is my oath, and you will match it or we part ways right now and I arrest you on general principles: *the king dies free, or he does not die at all.* Whatever you intend—whatever rite, whatever unbinding, whatever violence—you will not kill Aldric to save yourselves. If the binding can't be severed while he lives, you walk away, and I keep serving a monster because the alternative is worse. Swear it."

Silence. Somewhere in the palace, a clock struck the half hour, muffled by stone. Snow ticked faintly against the chapel's high, dark windows.

"You love him," August said at last. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an accusation. It sounded more like a diagnosis.

"I love what he was." Ilsa's voice cracked and she let it, because hiding it now would be a kind of lie, and she had promised herself this meeting would contain none. "I never met that man. I inherited his ghost and his cage. But I have dreamed his garden, Okafor. I have dreamed him teaching his daughter to plant things gently, making room and waiting. And I have stood post outside the council chamber listening to vultures discuss grain tariffs while he starves behind a smile, and I have decided that I would rather hang for treason than stand that post one more year pretending I don't hear the screaming." She steadied herself. "So. The oath. Or the door."

For a long moment the mercenary said nothing. Then she reached up and, deliberately, pulled the glove from her left hand, baring her palm—a gesture Ilsa recognized from border oaths, from soldiers' bargains, from the old forms that predated courts and crowns. August Okafor pressed her bare palm flat against her own sternum, over the heart.

"On my life and what remains of my name," she said formally, "the king dies free, or he does not die at all. If severance requires his death, I abandon the attempt and carry the failure out of this city myself. So sworn."

"So witnessed," Ilsa answered, and the old formula settled over the cold chapel like a weight, binding and ancient, older than the crown it conspired against.

They stood a moment in the silence after oaths, two women on opposite sides of a line that had just quietly ceased to exist.

"The rotations change at dawn tomorrow," Ilsa said, pulling a folded paper from inside her coat—written in her own hand, in her father's cipher, because some habits of distrust ran deeper than treason. "Corren has the north stair watch from the third bell. He asks no questions; he's already decided not to know things. The warding on the throne room has a gap during the king's private devotions, the hour after midnight—that's when the crown is loudest, and whoever maintains the wards has learned to stay away, though I doubt they know why." She held out the paper but did not release it when August took the other end. "One more thing. The crown knows faces, and it knows mine. If it learns what I've done, it won't send soldiers. It will send my own memories back to me, twisted, until I can't trust my mind enough to act. If that happens—if you see me standing against you with a smile I don't wear—remember the oath. Remember I swore it free."

August studied her across the paper between them. "You're asking me to kill you if it comes to that."

"I'm asking you to remember that I died free, whichever way it goes." Ilsa released the paper. "That's more than the crown will grant any of us."

The mercenary folded the rotations away inside her coat, and for the first time her composure shifted into something almost human—almost warm. "Captain Vane. When this is done, if any of us are alive, I'll buy you a drink and tell you what your king's garden smelled like. He kept bees, you know. In the dream, I mean. You'd know that already."

"I know," Ilsa said, and found, absurdly, that her eyes were burning. "He named all of them. Every hive. He wrote their names in a ledger, and the crown ate the ledger, and I dreamed the names for a week and lost them by morning." She turned toward the door, then paused. "The winter breaks soon, Okafor. Whatever you're going to do—do it fast. The city doesn't have many nights left in it. Last week a whole district went blank between one bell and the next. Four hundred people, standing in the streets, not knowing their own children." Her hand tightened on the door latch. "I arrested two of my own soldiers yesterday for looting bread for families they no longer remembered. They didn't even remember why they'd stolen it. That's what's coming for all of us."

She slipped out into the frozen corridors of the palace, leaving the mercenary alone among the stored chairs and the dust and the God-only-knew-what future, and walked back toward her post with the strange lightness of a woman who had finally, after years of standing on a cracking floor, jumped—and found, in the falling, that she could breathe.

Behind her, in the dark of the chapel, the crown felt the oath settle into the world like a splinter working into flesh, and began, patiently, to plan.
