## 15. What August Remembers

The archive smelled of dust and tallow and the particular sweetness of old vellum, and August stood in it for a long time before she understood what Sefa had found.

She had read the passage three times. The words were plain enough—*the unbinding requires a vessel of living memory, freely given; the vessel must be whole, unfractured by the winter's forgetting*—and she had read them the way she read contracts, scanning for the trap, the clause that would let her walk away. There was no clause. There was only the requirement, sitting on the page like a stone on a road she had to travel.

*A vessel of living memory.*

Everyone in Meridian was forgetting. That was the whole horror of the long winter—it came through the streets like a tide and took names first, then faces, then the reasons people had for standing where they were standing. The refugees in the temple district could not say why they had fled their villages. The guardsmen forgot which doors they had been assigned to watch. Even Ilsa Vane, that iron woman, had taken to writing her own name on her wrist each morning in ink that would not wash, as though she feared waking as no one at all.

But August remembered everything.

She had thought it a small mercy of the valley—that the Hollow, having erased so much from its people, had left her intact. She had carried her mother's face, her father's hands, every job and every grave of twenty mercenary years, across the boundary line like a traveler carrying contraband. She had assumed the valley's silence simply hadn't reached her. She had never once asked *why*.

Now she did, and the answer assembled itself with the terrible neatness of a lock accepting the right key.

The valley took memory. It had taken it from everyone—forgetting was the price of living under Veyl's silence, the tax levied on every soul born between those hills. But it could not take what did not exist to be taken, and so it had done something else instead: it had *preserved*. August's memories were not untouched by accident. They were untouched because the valley needed them whole. A vessel must be unfractured. The god's voice, when it was finally pried loose from Aldric's crown, would need somewhere to go—and the Hollow had spent generations preparing one place, one body, one life kept in amber against this exact night.

Her.

August set her palm flat against the archive table and breathed until the shaking stopped. Around her, Sefa moved among the shelves with a lamp, pulling down her father's research volume by volume, and Tomas sat sharpening a knife he did not need to sharpen, because that was what his hands did when his heart was busy.

"Say it out loud," Sefa said without turning. She had heard the silence change. "Whatever you just figured out. Say it."

"The valley kept me whole," August said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Not because it was kind. Because it was *saving me*. Like a cellar keeps apples for winter." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I'm not a survivor who stumbled into this. I'm the harvest."

The knife stopped moving. Tomas looked up, and in the lamplight his face did the thing it always did when he was afraid for her—went very still, very careful, a man walking on river ice.

"Then we find another way," he said.

"There is no other way. You heard Sefa. Willing vessel of living memory. Unfractured." She spread her hands. "Find me another unfractured person in this city. Find me anyone who can still tell you what they ate yesterday with certainty. The winter is eating everyone else alive, Tomas. I'm the last full cup in a drought."

"Then we—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "There has to be a way to do this that doesn't cost you *everything*."

And there it was. Not *doesn't cost you your life*. He had already done that arithmetic days ago, she realized—he had stood in this same room and quietly accepted that she might die, because August Okafor dying was a thing his mind knew how to hold. It was the other thing that broke him. The losing of her, while she went on breathing.

"I don't know yet what it costs," August said, which was a lie, or close enough to one that she felt it scrape going down. "But I know this: if I'm going to pour myself into that crown, I want to decide what gets poured. And I want—" Her voice caught. She made herself finish. "I want to leave instructions. For whoever I am afterward."

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She took a room in the safehouse above the dyer's shop—Ilsa's arrangement, one of the few buildings in Meridian whose owner still remembered owning it—and she bought paper. Eleven sheets, good rag paper, and a pot of ink, and the stub of a candle she would burn to nothing before morning.

She sat with the blank page for a long time.

This was the problem no one warned you about: memory does not come when called. It comes sideways, in smells and half-songs, in the weight of a specific door handle. If you ask yourself *what do I remember*, you get a list. But a list is not a life. And whatever she wrote tonight would be all that remained—not for the world, but for *her*, for the stranger who would wake in her body holding pages written in her own hand and no idea why they mattered.

So she began the way her mother used to begin stories: at the beginning, and honestly.

*My name is August Okafor. I was born in the Hollow, in the village of Lowmere, in the house with the crooked chimney on the lane below the mill. My mother was Wren Okafor, a midwife, and she sang off-key and did not care who knew it. My father died when I was six—a roof collapse, a bad storm—and I remember mostly his hands, which were enormous, and how he used to balance a copper coin on his knuckle to make me laugh. Do not grieve him too much. He would hate being grieved. He'd want you to fix the crooked chimney instead.*

The ink dried. She kept writing.

It got harder after that, and easier, both at once. Harder because some things fought her—the face of the first man she'd killed, which surfaced reluctantly and then would not leave, sitting on the page in her own handwriting like an accusation she'd finally signed. Easier because once the dam cracked, the water came fast. Twenty-nine years of a life, pouring out. The winter she nearly froze crossing the pass at Corren's Gap and learned that fear has a taste, like coins. The dog, Bracken, who followed her for two years and died old and fat in the sun, which was more than most things in her life had gotten. The mentor, Maren, dead now, whose letters had started all of this—*if you ever see ink move, girl, run*—and whose last lesson August wrote out word for word because she could not bear to paraphrase it.

Somewhere past midnight she reached Tomas, and stopped.

The candle had burned to a thumb's width. Outside, the snow fell with that terrible patience it had learned, erasing the street below her window stroke by stroke. She could hear it settling on the world like a hand smoothing a bedsheet.

What do you write about love, when the reader will be yourself?

She tried the facts first. *Tomas Reyes. Born in the Hollow, same year as me. We met when we were fourteen, over a stolen pie, and he lied about it badly and I lied about it well and we've been partners fifteen years.* True, all true, and useless as a list of supplies. The page stared back at her, waiting.

So she told the truth instead.

*I love him. Write that first, future-self, before anything else, because everything else will make more sense if you know it. I have loved him since I was nineteen and didn't have a word for it, and I never said it, because saying it felt like handing someone a knife and showing them exactly where I keep my heart. I told myself it was practical. Mercenaries shouldn't have entanglements. I told myself he didn't feel the same. I told myself a hundred clever lies, and here is the truth underneath all of them: I was a coward, and he let me be one, because he is kinder than I am, and he waited.*

*If you are reading this and you don't feel it—if the name Tomas Reyes means nothing to you—then understand that this is not a debt you owe. Don't perform it. Don't marry him out of obligation to a dead woman's handwriting. Just—read this part twice. Read it until something in you recognizes it, the way a body recognizes a stair in the dark. The way he hums when he thinks no one can hear, badly, songs he invents. The way he stands between me and doorways without noticing he does it. The scar on his left forearm that he says came from a boar and actually came from protecting me, and I have never once corrected him because watching him maintain the lie is the closest thing to devotion I know how to witness.*

*He loves you. He loved me. Same thing. Try to deserve it slowly.*

The candle guttered. She lit another from its corpse and kept going, because the night was short and the winter was long and there was so much left. Her mother's funeral, and the smell of the lilies, which she had hated ever since. The name of every person she had buried. The recipe for the barley soup she made when she was sick, which she had learned from a woman whose name she suddenly, desperately, could not recall—and she sat frozen, pen dripping, feeling the winter reach even backward into her, taking things she hadn't offered—until it came back, Sister Odile, Odile, thank the dark, and she wrote it down three times and underlined it.

Toward dawn her hand cramped and her eyes burned and she had filled eleven sheets and started a twelfth, and she understood, finally, what the emotional arithmetic of this actually was. Everyone did the math in the archive—Sefa with her father's map, Tomas with his silence, Ilsa with her codes. But the math was different for each of them, and hers was this: she could save the world, or she could keep herself, and the crown did not accept partial payment.

Unless.

She put down the pen. Rubbed her eyes. Looked at the stack of pages—her whole life, weighed in ink—and thought about the wording again. *A vessel of living memory, freely given.* It did not say *all* of it. It did not say the vessel must be emptied to the last drop. Sefa's father's notes were precise; precision cut both ways. Perhaps a vessel need only be *full enough*. Perhaps she could choose what to pour and what to keep, the way you decant wine and leave the sediment.

Or perhaps that was hope talking, and hope in Meridian currently wore the face of a thing that ate kings.

She would find out. But either way, the pages mattered. If she lost everything, these were the raft. If she lost only some, these were the map of what was missing—a catalogue of the holes in herself, so that future-August could stand in front of the wreckage and know what had been there.

She wrote the last lines as the window went grey:

*Instructions for August Okafor, whoever she turns out to be:*

*One. Read these every morning until they live in you instead of on the page.*

*Two. Do not trust any minister who smiles first.*

*Three. The soup needs more thyme than you think.*

*Four. Check on Tomas. Whatever you feel or don't feel, check on him. He will pretend he's fine. He is lying. He has been lying beautifully for fifteen years and it is the best lie I ever loved.*

*Five. You paid for something. I don't know yet if it was worth it. Make it worth it.*

She signed it, blotted it, folded the pages together, and then—because she was who she was, a woman who checked locks twice and counted her blades before sleeping—she read the whole stack through one final time, hunting for anything forgotten, anything the winter might take that she'd failed to pin to paper.

She found one thing. Near the bottom of the ninth sheet, in her own exhausted hand, words she had no memory of writing:

*The valley remembers for a reason. Ask Veyl why it chose you. Ask what you were before Lowmere.*

She stared at it for a long moment. The candle flame leaned in the draft from the window, and somewhere far beneath the city—far beneath the ice and the Steppe and the sewn-shut mouth of a sleeping god—she felt the heartbeat shift, just slightly, like a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice.

Outside, the snow had stopped. In its strange, deliberate quiet, she heard boots on the stairs—two sets, one heavy and one light, Tomas and Sefa both—and beneath them, fainter, a third set moving with military precision, and Ilsa Vane's voice, low and urgent, saying words she couldn't make out yet but already dreaded:

The winter had reached the walls.
