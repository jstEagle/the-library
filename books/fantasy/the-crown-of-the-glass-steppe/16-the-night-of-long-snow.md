## 16. The Night of Long Snow

The snow came through the walls the way a rumor comes through a market: everywhere at once, and no one able to say where it started.

Ilsa Vane stood at the north battlements of Meridian and watched it happen. Not snow falling—snow *arriving*, drifting sideways through solid stone as though the walls were suggestions rather than fact. It did not pile. It did not melt. It hung in the air over the lower city in slow, glittering sheets, and wherever it touched, people stopped.

A baker mid-shout to his apprentice simply closed his mouth and stood there, flour on his hands, looking at nothing with the mild patience of a man waiting for a name he'd forgotten he was waiting for. A woman drawing water from a public pump let the bucket fall back down the well and forgot, visibly, why she had come outside. She smiled at Ilsa across the distance. It was the worst thing Ilsa had ever seen—that smile, warm and empty, the smile of someone who has been let off a burden they never knew they carried.

"Captain." Sergeant Brell at her elbow, breath fogging. "The districts are going in order. Riverside first, then Tanner's Row. It's moving toward the palace quarter."

"It's not moving," Ilsa said. "It's remembering its way there."

She had dreamed the king's dream again the night before: a corridor of silver thread, and something vast turning over beneath it like a sleeper under blankets, and a voice—not Aldric's, not anything's—counting. Counting memories the way a miser counts coins. In the dream she had understood that the counting was nearly done, that the ledger was almost balanced, that when the last entry was made the winter would have everything it needed and the world would simply—

Stop. Mid-sentence. Like the baker.

"How long to evacuate the palace quarter?" she asked.

Brell consulted his slate. "With the roads what they are? Two hours. Three if the panic spreads."

"We don't have three hours." Ilsa turned from the wall. Her hands were steady. She noted this about herself with a kind of distant professional interest, the way she'd once noted her own pulse before her first battle. "We don't have two. Get me every Crownsguard in the palace district. Now."

* * *

August felt the winter arrive as a pressure behind her eyes.

She was in the safehouse above the chandler's shop on Vellum Street, the map spread between them, Sefa's bottled memories glowing on the shelf like a congregation of fireflies, when the light changed. Not darkened—*changed*, the way light changes underwater. The lamp flames bent toward the window, all of them, as if listening.

"It's here," Sefa said. She said it without drama, the way she said most things, already packing bottles into her satchel with the economy of someone who had fled things before. "Faster than my father's notes predicted. The crown is pulling harder now. Something spooked it."

"Something did," August said. She thought of the throne room, of the minute of clarity she'd bought with a dead god's first word, of the thing behind Aldric's eyes turning its attention on her like a lighthouse beam. *August Okafor.* It knew her name. Names were currency here; she should have known better than to spend hers.

Tomas Reyes came up the stairs two at a time, which he only did when the news was bad or the wine was free. Tonight his face said both possibilities had died together.

"Walls are breached. Not broken—breached. The snow's inside the city. Riverside's gone blank—whole streets standing in their doorways smiling at each other. They don't know their own children." He caught his breath. "Ilsa Vane is holding the palace gates with thirty soldiers against a hundred of her own coming back from the wall. She sent for you. By name."

"Ilsa sent for me?"

"She said—" Tomas hesitated, and August watched him decide whether to soften it, and watched him decide not to, because he had never once softened anything for her in fifteen years and wasn't going to start at the end of the world. "She said, 'Tell the woman with the map that I'm buying her one hour, and she'd better spend it well.'"

Sefa snorted. "One hour. To walk into the seat of the thing that eats names, unbind a god, and save a king. Generous."

"It's more than anyone else has bought us," August said. She rolled the map, tied it, slung it across her back. Her hands were doing the work while the rest of her stood very still somewhere behind her own ribs, looking at the shape of what she was about to do. "Where's the archive entrance?"

"The servants' stair off the east cloister," Sefa said. "And August—the vessel rite needs an hour itself. If Ilsa's hour is real, you'll be cutting it to the bone."

"Then we won't waste any of it talking."

But Tomas didn't move. He stood in the doorway with the lamplight bending around him, and the snow-light coming through the window put a strange pale shine on his face, and August realized she was memorizing him. Doing it without meaning to. The set of his jaw. The scar through his left eyebrow from the Tollhouse job. The way he always stood slightly angled toward her, even now, even furious, like a compass needle that had given up pretending.

"No," he said.

"Tomas—"

"You heard me. No. You're not walking into that palace alone."

"I'm not alone. Sefa knows the archive. Ilsa's buying the door."

"And I'm supposed to do what, exactly? Stand here?" His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, she could see him hating it, fifteen years of partnership and he still treated every feeling like a breach of contract. "August. I have followed you into burning buildings, sieges, one wedding we were definitely not invited to. Every single time because you asked. So ask me. Ask me to stay behind. Say it out loud and mean it, and I'll stay."

The room went quiet. The lamp flames leaned. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog began to howl at the snow.

August opened her mouth to say it—to say *stay, please stay, I can't carry what I'm about to carry if I'm also carrying you*—and found she couldn't. Not because it wasn't true. Because it was true, and saying it would make it a lie.

"I can't ask that," she said finally. "Because I need you too much to be fair about it."

Tomas exhaled, long and shaking, and something in his shoulders came loose—a knot fifteen years tight.

"Well," he said. "Finally some honesty in this partnership." He picked up his axe. "Let's go spend a dead woman's hour."

* * *

Ilsa Vane arrested her own soldiers at the palace gate, and it took less time than she expected, because most of them let her.

That was the part she would think about later, in whatever time later turned out to hold. Sergeant Duval, who had served under her for six years, who had named his daughter after her, looked at the drawn sword and said, "Is it the dreams, Captain? Do you have them too?" And when she said yes, he laid his weapon down in the snow himself and held out his wrists.

"They're in our heads," Duval said. "All of us on the wall rotation. We dream what he dreams. And tonight the dreams say *bring her the map*. And I don't—" his voice wavered— "I don't want to be the hand that does it. So arrest me. Please. Before I stop wanting to."

Ilsa bound him gently. Around them, the mutiny folded up like a tent. Thirty loyal, seventy bound, and the snow drifting down through the gatehouse roof to settle on everyone equally, patient as tax collectors.

She had perhaps an hour before the rest of the garrison overcame their consciences or lost them. She spent the first ten minutes of it clearing the route: east cloister, servants' stair, archive corridor. She spent the next five writing a letter to the Crownsguard's quartermaster detailing, in dry administrative language, exactly which ministers had signed the requisitions for the crown's forging, forty years ago, with whose seals. Evidence kept in duplicate. One copy sewn into her coat lining. Whatever happened tonight, the institution would answer for its founding sin—or she would leave the proof lying around like a lit fuse.

Then she went to the gate and waited, sword drawn, watching the white street, and tried not to count the minutes, and counted them anyway.

They came out of the snowfall at the half-hour mark: three figures, moving fast. The tall mercenary with the axe. The steppe-woman with the clinking satchel. And August Okafor, wrapped in a borrowed cloak, the map-case riding her shoulder like a yoke.

"You're shorter than your reputation," Ilsa said.

"You're punctual," August said. "That's rarer."

"One hour. Fifty-one minutes left. The servants' stair is clear; my loyalists are holding the archive corridor, but the throne room is beyond my reach. The king holds court tonight—he always holds court when the winter comes close. It feeds him." She hesitated, then said the rest quickly, like pulling a tooth. "The crown knows you're coming. It's known since the ford. This isn't an infiltration, Okafor. It's an invitation. Walk in anyway."

"Why help us?" Tomas asked bluntly. "You're the crown's own blade."

Ilsa looked at him, and then past him, at the snow settling over the bound soldiers, over the empty smiling city, and her voice when it came was quiet and stripped of everything but the truth.

"Because I've dreamed his dreams for a year, and in every one of them the king is screaming. Loyalty means nothing if the man I serve is a glove. Either I'm a traitor tonight or I'm the last honest officer in Meridian, and I've decided I'd rather find out which." She stepped aside and gestured them through the gate. "Go. And Okafor—if you get him lucid, tell him Vane remembers his daughter's name. Tell him someone does."

* * *

The palace swallowed them.

August had expected grandeur; what she got was silence so complete it had texture, like wool pressed against the ears. The corridors were lit by lamps that burned without flickering, and the snow had gotten inside here too—it fell in the great hall in slow spirals, never landing, hanging at head height like a held breath. Servants stood among the drifts, motionless, their faces smooth with that terrible patience. One young page had been frozen mid-cry, tears still wet on his cheeks, and August made herself look away because looking too long felt like theft.

Sefa navigated by her father's notes, murmuring turns and counts. "Left at the tapestry of the hunt. My father marked this corridor twice—see, the ink's worn. He came here. He came here and then he disappeared." Her jaw tightened. "Keep walking."

At the archive door, the plan split along its one seam.

"The rite requires the vessel to be prepared in the archive," Sefa said. "The binding threads run beneath the throne, but they're anchored to the crown through the archive vault—the old cartographers built it that way so the work could be maintained. I open the vault. I prepare the anchor. But the speaking of the name—" she looked at August— "that happens in the throne room, at the same moment, or the vault opening means nothing. Two rooms. One hour. We cannot see each other."

"So we go on trust," August said.

"We go on arithmetic," Sefa said, but her hand found August's arm and gripped it, brief and hard, which from Sefa was a sonnet.

Tomas planted himself in front of August, axe across his chest, and looked at her the way he had looked at her across fifteen years of campfires and contracts and narrow escapes—the look that had never once required translation.

"I take the throne room door," he said. "Whatever comes out of it that isn't you, meets me."

"Tomas, the crown—"

"—isn't hiring me. Move, August."

She moved. And at the mouth of the archive corridor she stopped, just once, and looked back at him standing guard in the falling snow that never landed, axe ready, scarred eyebrow raised in the old familiar challenge, and she thought: *whatever I lose tomorrow, I will have had this. Him, standing between me and the dark, one more time, by choice.*

"Hold the door," she said.

"Always," said Tomas Reyes. "It's the one job I've never quit."

* * *

In the archive, Sefa knelt before her father's vault and spoke the opening words he had hidden in her childhood lullabies, and the lock—silver thread wound through brass—shivered and began, slowly, to unwind.

Above her, through floors of stone, she could feel it: the crown pulling, the winter leaning, the whole vast machine of theft grinding toward its final entry. Fifty minutes. She worked faster.

Behind the throne room doors, Tomas Reyes rolled his shoulders, spat on his hands, and gripped his axe.

And in the snow that never landed, in the silence before the scream, August Okafor walked toward the throne of her enemy with a god's first word held behind her teeth like a coal, and did not look back again.
