## 17. Throne of Thread and Silver

The hour Ilsa bought them was sixty minutes long, and August spent the first ten of them learning to breathe like a woman who had never been afraid.

That was the trick of it—Tomas had taught her that years ago in the Hollow, back when they were young enough to think fear was a thing you conquered rather than a thing you carried. *You don't kill it,* he'd said, sharpening a blade by candlelight with the patience of a man who loved small rituals. *You just make it sit quietly in the corner while you work.* She carried it now, her fear, folded up small against her ribs where the pages of her own handwriting also lived—the letter she had written to herself, sealed with wax, to be opened only if she did not come back down from the palace. *If you are reading this, you were brave. Try to remember being brave.*

She remembered nothing else of what she'd written. She had made sure of that.

Now she moved through Meridian's palace the way water moves through cracks: patiently, without argument, filling every space the light didn't reach. The servant's corridor behind the great kitchens smelled of tallow and old bread and, beneath it, something sweeter and wronger—a scent like snow falling on hot iron. The winter's breath had gotten inside the walls. August could feel it pressing at the edges of her thoughts, soft as a thumb smudging wet ink, trying to find the seam of her and pull.

She pressed her hand flat against the stone and thought, deliberately, of things with weight. Her mother's hands kneading bread. The river ford where a dying man had bled out against her knee. Tomas's laugh, low and startled, the first time she'd ever made him laugh. The winter wanted names; she fed it names until it gagged on them.

*Left at the second arch. Up the stair that doesn't exist on any plan because it was built for a king who wanted to escape his own coronation.* Sefa had found that in her father's notes, three nights ago, ink still crawling across the page like something alive and eager. *Kings always build two doors,* Sefa had said, rolling the map closed with fingers stained silver-gray. *One for entering in glory. One for leaving in shame. My father said Aldric built his before he ever needed either.*

The stair was there. Of course it was there. Narrow, unlit, its walls furred with frost that grew thicker with every step—not ordinary frost. It fell in patterns. Feathers, August thought. Or scales. Or the shapes a scream makes if you could freeze one mid-air and look at it closely.

Halfway up, the sound began.

At first she took it for wind. Then for the palace groaning under snow-load. But it was neither, and she knew what it was the way you know your own heartbeat: it was singing. Something above her was singing—or rather, something above her was wearing a song the way a thief wears a stolen coat, badly, the seams straining, the original owner's shape still visible underneath. Veyl's voice. Bound into gold and glass and thread, forced through decades to say only what a crown says: *kneel, obey, rejoice, rejoice, rejoice.*

August stood on the stairs with her hand on the cold wall and let herself hear all of it—the beauty underneath the theft, the way the melody kept trying to bend home no matter how the binding wrenched it straight. Grief came up her throat like floodwater. Not her grief alone. She understood suddenly that this was what the Steppe's glass pools had shown her, what the dead king begging forgiveness had been drowning in: the sound of a god singing to an empty room, for centuries, because it was the only prayer left to it.

*Hold on,* she told it silently, absurdly, the way you'd speak to a wounded animal you couldn't yet reach. *I'm coming. I know your name.*

She climbed.

* * *

The door at the top was silver, and it was sewn shut.

Not locked—sewn. Great looping stitches of silver thread ran through the metal itself, the same thread she had seen in her dreams since the mountain, the same thread that held a god's mouth closed half a world away. Someone had stitched this door with the same needle, or the same intention, and the sight of it made August's teeth ache. She drew the knife Sefa had given her—a steppe-blade, glass-edged, humming faintly with borrowed memory—and set it against the first stitch.

The thread screamed.

Not the door. The thread. A thin, high, furious sound like a wire drawn to breaking, and along its whole length the silver glowed white-hot, and somewhere far below the ground August felt the god's heartbeat stutter—then quicken. Hope, she realized. The heartbeat had hope in it now. It hadn't had hope in it when they'd first knelt on the ice above the sleeping mountain. Things changed their hearts slowly, gods most of all, but they changed them completely.

*You want out too,* she breathed. *Good. So do I.*

She cut. Stitch by stitch, the blade singing counterpoint to the thread, each severed length curling away like burnt hair. Sweat froze on her temples. The winter pressed harder, whispering now, offering her rest, offering her blankness, offering her the mercy of not-remembering why she had come here at all. She answered it the only way she knew how: with inventory. *My name is August Okafor. My mother baked bread on mornings the fog came down. I have killed eleven men and buried nine of them myself. I love a man who follows me even when I lie to him, especially when I lie to him. I am going to save a king I've never met and a god I can't see, and then I am going to lose everything, and it will be worth it, and I will need someone to tell me that later because I won't remember thinking it.*

The last stitch parted. The door swung inward without a sound.

* * *

The throne room of Meridian was built to make human beings feel like insects, and it succeeded. Pillars rose into darkness that no chandelier reached. Snow had come through the high windows and lay drifted across the marble floor in long white dunes, undisturbed, as though even the wind had been forbidden entry. And at the far end, on a dais of black glass, beneath a canopy of woven silver thread that hung like a spider's web given architecture—

King Aldric sat on his throne, and the Crown of the Glass Steppe sat on King Aldric.

August stopped breathing.

She had imagined the crown as an object. That had been her mistake, and she saw it now with terrible clarity: it was not an object. It was a posture. It was a shape of gold and crystal that had learned, over long years, how a head should tilt, how shoulders should square, how a mouth should curve into the smile that was currently turned toward her with perfect, patient attention—as though it had heard her footsteps on the stair, heard the thread die, and had simply been waiting, curious, to see what would crawl into its web.

Aldric's face above the collar of state was gray as old wax. His eyes were open. They were not his.

"August Okafor," the crown said, using his voice the way a musician uses an instrument—beautifully, and with total indifference to the instrument's suffering. "The mercenary from the silent valley. The courier's thief. The map-reader." Each title landed like a card laid on a table. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone brought me anything new?"

"You know my name," August said. Her voice sounded small in all that whiteness, and she let it. Small things got underestimated. "That means you're afraid of me."

Something moved behind the king's eyes—irritation, or its cousin, recognition. The smile stayed fixed. "I am not capable of fear. Fear is a function of memory, and I have eaten all of mine." The head tilted, that practiced, regal tilt. "But he is afraid. The old one under the ice. He has been afraid of you since the ford. Isn't that interesting? He remembers you. Memory is such a stubborn material. We burn it and burn it and still it smolders."

"Then let me add some fuel." August walked forward. Every step was a decision; she made each one separately, the way you cross ice. "I know his name. His true name. The first word of the song he sang to make the world. Your whole kingdom is built on a theft, and I'm holding the receipt."

The temperature dropped so fast her breath cracked audibly in the air. Frost raced across the floor toward her feet and stopped, abruptly, as though hitting a wall she couldn't see. The crown's smile did not move, but the canopy of silver thread above the throne shivered, all of it at once, like a web feeling a fly land.

"You will not," the crown said, and for the first time the instrument-quality dropped away, and what spoke through Aldric's mouth was old and vast and hungry and, underneath the hunger, very slightly unsure. "That word was unwound from the world before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. It does not exist anymore. There is nothing left to say."

"There's one place left," August said. "One valley where nothing gets erased. Where everything that was taken went to be kept safe, like seed corn. Like embers banked for winter." She was close enough now to see the king's throat working, the tendons standing out like cables, the man inside fighting for millimeters of himself. "You didn't miss a single thing when you stole the world's voice. You missed one valley. And I walked out of it carrying everything you forgot to burn."

She looked at Aldric. At the man, not the glove. His lips were moving—had been moving, she realized, since she entered, tiny silent repetitions hidden beneath the crown's speech. She read them the way she'd learned to read lips across smoky rooms during negotiations that ended in knives.

*Help me. Help me. Help me.*

"I see you," August told him quietly. "I remember you. Hold on to that. One minute. That's all I need—one minute of you being a man instead of a throne."

And she spoke the name.

It was not a word, exactly. It was older than words—it was the shape a word makes before meaning arrives, the first syllable of the song that had pulled light out of dark and persuaded it to become rivers, sparrows, salt, sleep, the particular blue of evening. It came out of her sounding like her own voice and nothing like it, and the moment it left her mouth the whole room *changed key*. The snow on the floor lifted, all of it, every flake at once, hanging motionless in the air like a held breath. The pillars rang. Somewhere far below the city, deeper than foundations, deeper than bedrock, a heartbeat that had limped for centuries suddenly *ran*.

The crown screamed.

There was no other word for it. Gold and crystal and stolen voice tore loose from Aldric's brow in a sound that was not sound—a shriek that bypassed the ear entirely and scraped directly along the nerves, along the memory, dragging its claws through every mind in Meridian. August felt it take her name in its teeth and try to shake it out of her, and she gripped her own self with both hands—*my name is August Okafor, my mother baked bread, I love a man who follows me*—and screamed the true name again, louder, into the heart of the thing.

The silver threads of the canopy snapped, one after another, like harp strings dying.

The crown fell.

It hit the black glass dais and lay there, twitching, smaller than it had any right to be—all those centuries of terror reduced to a circlet of dull gold, its crystals gone gray, its shape collapsing like a glove with no hand in it. And King Aldric slid sideways off his throne and caught himself on the armrest, sixty years old in a single motion, a man waking from a fever that had lasted half his life.

He looked at August.

His eyes were brown. Ordinary, tired, red-rimmed, humanly brown, and they were *his*, and they were clear, and they were weeping without his permission.

"How long," he said. His voice was rust and gravel, a voice unused for its own purposes. "How long have I—"

"One minute," August said. "Maybe less. Use it well."

He laughed. It broke in the middle and became a sob and became, somehow, still a laugh. "Use it—" He pushed himself upright, swaying, gripping the throne like a man gripping a rail in a storm, and looked around the frozen hall, at the snow hanging in the air, at the dead crown smoking faintly on the dais. "I dreamed all of it, you know. Every year. I dreamed I was in a room with no doors, signing papers that weren't paper, smiling at people I was eating. I dreamed my wife's funeral and I don't remember my wife." His gaze came back to her, fierce and drowning. "Who are you? What did you give me? What did it *cost*?"

"That's the wrong question," August said gently. "The right one is: what do you want to do with your minute?"

Aldric looked at the crown. At his own hands, turning them over as though checking whether they were still his. And then he did the thing that August would carry with her past the edge of everything she was about to lose: he stepped down off the dais. Off the throne. Down onto the level floor, onto the snow, a king descending to stand eye-to-eye with a mercenary in a stolen servant's coat.

"I want," he said, "to help you finish it. Whatever comes next. Whatever it takes." His jaw tightened. "Because I can feel it still. In the walls. In the ground. The binding isn't broken—it's *loosened*, like a knot with one rope end free. And I know where the other end is tied."

"So do I," August said. Her heart was hammering now, hammering with dread and triumph in equal measure, because she had known since the archive, since Sefa's father's notes, since the night she wrote pages she would never read. *A willing vessel of living memory.* "It's tied to a person. It always needed a person. And I've already done the arithmetic."

"No," said a voice from the doorway behind her—rough, furious, breathless, unmistakable, a voice that had followed her across half a dying world and through a door that had been sewn shut against kings.

Tomas stood in the shattered frame of the entrance, sword drawn, snow melting on his shoulders, looking at her like a man looking at the last thing left in the world.

"It's tied to *someone*," he said. "And you don't get to decide it's you."
