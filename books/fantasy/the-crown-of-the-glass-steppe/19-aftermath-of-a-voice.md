## 19. Aftermath of a Voice

The silence after the crown screamed was the loudest thing Ilsa Vane had ever heard.

She stood in the doorway of the throne room with her sword drawn and her orders ash in her mouth, watching dawn pour through the shattered clerestory windows like something spilled. The light moved strangely. It pooled. It gathered along the flagstones in slow rivulets, as if the room itself were drinking, and where it touched, the frost that had crept up the pillars for weeks—the frost no one had been permitted to name—receded like a tide going out.

King Aldric sat slumped on his throne, and he was weeping.

Not the king's weeping. Ilsa had seen that before, twice, on state occasions: a controlled glistening, dignified, performed for ambassadors. This was different. This was ugly and human and endless, the crying of a man who had just woken from a dream forty years long and found his hands old and his wife dead and his kingdom hollowed out around him. His crown lay on the steps below the dais, cracked down the middle like an eggshell, and from the crack seeped a thin silver smoke that unraveled upward and did not stop unraveling—thread after thread after thread, going up and up until Ilsa's eyes watered trying to follow them into the rafters, into the sky, into whatever waited beyond the sky.

And August Okafor stood beside him with her hand on his shoulder, saying nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the light wasn't already saying better.

* * *

Ilsa had served the crown for twenty-two years. She had sworn her oath at seventeen with her father's sword, which had been her grandfather's sword, in a ceremony so old that half its words had worn smooth of meaning. *The crown endures. The crown remembers. The crown is the realm.* She had believed every syllable. She had built her entire self out of those syllables, the way a mason builds a wall, one stone laid carefully upon another, and she had never once looked at the mortar.

Now she could not stop looking at it.

Because she knew what the ministers would say. She had already heard it, in the small hours, in the voice of Chancellor Vess himself, patient and reasonable, the voice he used when explaining why a village must burn to save a province: *the binding was necessary. The god was mad with grief; its song was unmaking the world. Someone had to hold the voice still. We chose a king. We gave him a crown. We saved everything.* And beneath that reasonable voice, the other thing, the true thing, which Ilsa had only begun to hear since the dreams started: *and then we kept the crown, because a held god is useful, and because no one asked us to give it back.*

She turned on her heel and walked out of the throne room, and she did not look back, because if she looked back she would kneel, and she was not done yet. There were men in this palace who needed arresting first.

* * *

Chancellor Vess received the news of the crown's destruction with the composure of a man who had rehearsed for every catastrophe except the one that came.

He was in the map room when Ilsa found him, standing over the great table with four other ministers—Corvane, Halloway, the twins Esten and Brecht—and they were all looking at the blank center of the map of the Glass Steppe as though staring hard enough might restore what had just been unmade. None of them heard her come in. That was how far their attention had collapsed: five men who had run a kingdom for decades, deaf to a captain in full plate walking across their own floor.

"Captain Vane." Vess recovered first. He always recovered first. "You are remiss. There is a situation in the throne room that requires—"

"I know," Ilsa said. "I was there."

Something flickered across his face—not fear, not yet, but the rapid recalculation of a man discovering that the ground had rules he hadn't read. "Then you understand the gravity. The king has been compromised. Whatever that woman did to him—"

"She unbound him." Ilsa let each word fall separately, like coins onto a table. "From you."

Vess smiled. It was his court smile, warm as a hearth, and Ilsa realized she had spent twenty-two years mistaking warmth for goodness. "Captain. Sit. Let me explain what you clearly don't understand about the founding. About the *necessity*—"

"No," Ilsa said.

The word came out smaller than she intended. She had planned a speech. In the corridor outside, marching here, she had composed paragraphs—about the dreams, about the king mouthing *help me* behind his own face, about the snow that ate names, about the courier dying at a ford with wounds no blade made. But standing in front of Vess, she found the speech unnecessary. He knew. They all knew. Five old men in a room with a map whose center they had personally erased, and every one of them was waiting to see whether she would be reasonable.

"The crown ate him," she said instead. "Forty years. You fed him to it a memory at a time and called it stability. And when the god's silence started leaking out of the palace and freezing the world—" she gestured at the window, at the impossible June snow still drifting past it, thinning now, losing conviction, "—you sent soldiers to arrest cartographers and kill couriers. I know. I carried some of the orders myself."

"Captain Vane." Corvane, the treasurer, had found his voice. It shook. "You are one woman. The guard is sworn to the crown, and the crown—"

"The crown is cracked on the floor of the throne room," Ilsa said, "and the man who wore it is asking me, right now, in words, to bring him the men who did this. So." She drew herself up, and twenty-two years of drill settled over her like armor that finally fit. "By authority of Aldric, fourth of that name, King of Meridian and the Steppe Marches, lucid and speaking—I am placing you all under arrest for high treason against the person of the king. Not against the institution. Against the *man*. Which is worse. Which none of you ever understood."

Esten reached for the bell cord. Ilsa's sergeant, Doss—a broad, silent woman who had followed her captain without being told, because Doss had also been dreaming the king's dreams lately, everyone in the palace had been dreaming the king's dreams, the crown had leaked like a cracked dam for years and no one had thought to wonder why—stepped out of the doorway with six guards behind her, and the bell cord stayed untouched.

Vess looked at the guards. Then at Ilsa. And then he did the thing that confirmed everything, the thing she would carry to her own grave as proof: he straightened his robes, lifted his chin, and said, with genuine bewilderment:

"We *saved* the world, Captain. Who will thank us?"

"Perhaps the world," Ilsa said, "when it finishes waking up."

* * *

They took the ministers down through the palace in irons, past servants who pressed themselves against walls and stared, past tapestries woven with the founding myth—the kneeling god, the wise king, the gift of silence—and Ilsa watched the servants watch, and saw the moment the story broke in their faces too. A myth survives on not being examined. All it took was one morning, one walk of shame, one chancellor in shackles passing under his own propaganda, and twenty years of careful weaving came apart like wet thread.

In the cells beneath the east wing—the same cells, Ilsa noted, that had once held the cartographer Sefa's father, though the records of his imprisonment had been burned, though the record of the burning had also been burned, though somewhere at the bottom of all that burning there was presumably a first fire—she locked them away, five men in five rooms, and stood a while in the corridor listening to Vess begin, faintly, to argue with the stone.

Then she went back upstairs to do the hardest part.

* * *

Aldric had been moved to his private chambers, though *private* was doing generous work; half the palace had found reasons to drift past his doors all morning, courtiers and cooks and laundresses, drawn by the same pull that draws people to a well in drought. The god's voice was back in the world. Ilsa could feel it now that she knew to listen—not a sound exactly, but a pressure behind the ears, a sense that the air was *occupied*, that the silence she had lived inside her whole life had been a held breath and the breath was releasing. Her grandmother's lullabies, which she had never been able to remember past the first line, now continued in her head unbidden, verse after verse, in a voice that was not quite hers.

August Okafor met her at the chamber door. Up close, the mercenary looked wrung out, gray-faced, her eyes red-rimmed, and there was something else—something Ilsa had seen in soldiers after their first battle, a look of someone taking inventory of themselves and finding items missing.

"Captain Vane," August said. "The king asks for you."

"He knows my name?"

"He knows more than his name." August stepped aside. "He's remembering. Slowly. The god is... giving things back, I think. What the crown took. Some of it." She hesitated, and Ilsa watched her decide to say the rest honestly, because apparently that was the kind of day it was. "Not all of it can come back. Some of what the crown ate was eaten completely. He'll have reigns he ruled through as a stranger. People he loved who are strangers now. The god said—" August's voice caught, just slightly, "—the god said grief is the interest paid on stolen time. Or something like that. Gods shouldn't try metaphors."

Inside, the king sat by the window in a plain robe, having refused, Doss reported, all finery. He looked smaller than his coinage. His hands were folded in his lap like a scholar's, and they trembled, and he did not hide the trembling, and Ilsa understood suddenly that the hiding had been the crown's work too—that everything she had ever read as royal dignity had been a puppet's rigging.

"Captain Vane," he said. His voice was rough, unused to itself. "They tell me you arrested the chancellors."

"Yes, Majesty."

"They tell me you hesitated first." He looked at her, and his eyes were old and clear and terribly sad. "Good. Hesitation means you weighed it. Men who don't hesitate are the ones who build crowns like mine." He unfolded his hands. "Kneel, Captain. I have something to ask of you, and I'd rather ask it properly, while I still remember how."

Ilsa knelt. The stone was cold through her plate. Twenty-two years ago she had knelt on stone like this to swear to the crown, and the words had been given to her, and she had said them without understanding, and here was the bill.

"Aldric," the king said—and stopped, and laughed, short and broken. "Forgive me. I was going to say *the crown commands*, and I find I don't want to say it. I don't know if I ever wanted to say it. I think perhaps the crown said it and I merely moved my mouth." He leaned forward. "Here is what I ask, and it is not a command, because I am finished with commanding. I am going to abdicate. Today, if the paperwork can be found; tomorrow at the latest. I am going to stand before whatever remains of my court and tell them the truth of the founding—that the throne was built on a theft, that the theft was maintained by my predecessors and by me, unknowing and then, near the end, knowing. And then I am going to leave, and I need someone to keep the peace while the realm decides what it wants to be without a thief on the throne."

"Majesty—"

"Not queen," he said, raising a hand. "I know what you're about to say. Not regent, either. Regent is just a crown with a delay on it. I want a keeper of the interregnum. A warden of the question. Someone whose job is to *not* answer, and to keep anyone else from answering too fast." He almost smiled. "You dreamed my dreams, Captain. Don't look shocked; I felt you there, near the end, knocking at the edges. You know better than anyone alive what a crown costs. So I'm asking: will you hold the empty throne? Not wear it. Hold it. Like one holds a door."

Ilsa knelt on the cold stone and felt twenty-two years rearrange themselves around a new word. Not *queen*. Not *regent*. *Warden.*

"The guard swore to the crown," she said slowly. "If there's no crown—"

"Then they may swear to the door," Aldric said. "Or to each other. Or to the god, if any of them are brave enough. That's rather the point of an interregnum, Captain. Everyone gets to choose again. Even me." He looked out the window, at the snow thinning over the city, at the roofs emerging from white like islands at a returning tide. "Especially me."

* * *

The abdication was held at dusk in the great hall, because Aldric refused to wait, and Ilsa suspected—watching him walk the corridor, upright, deliberate, gripping Doss's arm—that he feared the lucidity might not keep. That the man was a candle in a draft and knew it.

The hall had never held so many people for so small a ceremony. Every courtier, every guard off duty, servants lining the galleries, and at the edges, incongruous as gulls at a funeral, the steppe-folk: Sefa the cartographer's daughter with her bottles of memory clinking softly at her belt, and Tomas Reyes standing with his arms crossed and his eyes never leaving August Okafor, who stood near the dais looking like a woman attending her own funeral and not entirely minding.

There was no crown on the cushion. There was no cushion. Aldric came to the dais in his plain robe and stood where the throne was, and did not sit on it, and the whole hall noticed, and the whole hall would remember.

"My people," he began, and then stopped, and Ilsa saw him set aside forty years of coronation rhetoric like a man setting down a heavy pack. "No. Not that. I don't know if I have people. I don't know what I am to you, or you to me. Here is what I know." His voice strengthened. "This throne was built on a theft. The god Veyl sang the world, and my ancestors stole the song and sewed it into metal and called the silence peace. For forty years I wore the instrument of that crime on my head and called it duty. I did not forge it. But I kept it warm." He paused. "Some of you knew. Some of you suspected. Most of you, like me, slept. Sleep is not innocence. I say that as a man who is owed a great deal of sympathy and is claiming none of it."

Silence in the hall—but a different silence than the crown's silence. This one had people in it.

"Therefore I abdicate. Not in favor of an heir—there is no heir I trust, and no heir who should inherit what I am refusing. The throne stands empty. Captain Ilsa Vane holds it as Warden of the Interregnum, keeper of the door, until such time as the realm—through whatever voices it chooses to gather, and I counsel you to gather many, and to listen to the ones you used to silence—decides what belongs on it. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps that would be best of all." He stepped down from the dais—one step, two—and then did the thing that broke the hall open: he bowed. The king bowed, low and long, to the assembled ordinary faces of his kingdom, and when he rose his cheeks were wet and he did not wipe them.

"I am going north," he said simply. "I'm told the god sleeps under a mountain shaped like a sleeping figure, which seems fitting. I would like to apologize. I don't expect forgiveness. I expect it will take a very long time, and I intend to spend what time I have beginning it."

* * *

Later—much later, when the hall had emptied and the torches had burned down and the snow outside had stopped altogether for the first time in months—Ilsa climbed the stairs to the empty throne room and stood in the doorway, sword at her hip, keys at her belt, warden of a door.

The cracked crown still lay on the steps where it had fallen. No one had dared move it. The silver threads had finished unraveling; only a faint shimmer remained, like heat-haze, rising off the metal and dissolving. She crouched beside it, not touching, and listened.

Under the floor, under the mountain, under everything, the heartbeat. Steady. Patient. Awake.

"You're loud," she told it quietly. "For a god."

The pressure behind her ears shifted—amused, she thought, or she flattered herself—and somewhere far below, vast and slow and gentle, something that had sung the world began, very softly, to hum.

Ilsa rose, sheathed her sword, and went to write the first order of the interregnum: the prisons opened, the archives unsealed, and the burned records of a cartographer reconstructed from whatever memories the steppe-folk still carried in their bottles.

It arrived at her desk before she finished writing it: a single sheet, slipped under the door, unsigned, in a hand she almost recognized from the ministers' intercepted correspondence.

*The crown was not the only vessel. Check the northern vaults. — a friend of the founding*

Ilsa read it twice, and reached for her sword.
