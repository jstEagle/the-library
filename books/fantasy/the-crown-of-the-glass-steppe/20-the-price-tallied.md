## 20. The Price, Tallied

She woke to the smell of ink.

Not blood—she knew blood the way she knew her own pulse, had woken to it often enough in ditches and doorways and once, memorably, in a burning granary—but ink. Old ink, iron-gall ink, the kind that bit into paper and stayed. It hung in the air of the small room like a presence, like someone had been sitting beside her bed all night, writing.

August Okafor opened her eyes.

The ceiling above her was whitewashed plaster, cracked in one corner like a river delta seen from very high up. Morning light came through shutters she did not recognize, laying warm stripes across a blanket that smelled of cedar and someone else's soap. Her body ached in the deep, marrow-deep way it always ached after—the rite, the crown, the screaming silver thread, Tomas's hands on her shoulders, the god's voice coming back into the world like dawn poured through a wound. She remembered all of it.

She remembered all of it, and then she reached for the morning before it, and found nothing.

Not fog. Not the soft blur of too much drink or a blow to the head. Nothing. A clean absence, edged and precise, as if a page had been cut out of a book with a razor. She lay still and went looking for herself the way she'd go looking for a knife she'd misplaced: methodically, by feel.

Her name. *August.* That was there. The Hollow—that word meant something, a valley, a silence, a place where magic couldn't follow. But when she reached for the valley itself—for the smell of its river, the particular gray of its winter sky, the tollhouse where she'd—

Nothing. Smooth blankness where a life should have been.

She sat up too fast and the room tilted. On the chair beside the bed, folded with a care that was not hers—or was it? whose care was it now?—lay her clothes, cleaned of ash and snow. And in the pocket of the coat, something heavy. Paper.

She pulled it out with hands that shook only slightly, because her body remembered how to be calm even if she didn't remember why it mattered.

Pages. Eleven of them, written close and neat on both sides, in handwriting she recognized the way you recognize your own face in a window at night—with a jolt of wrongness, of *that's me but I'm not doing it*. The ink had dried unevenly in places, blotted where the writer's hand had paused too long. The first line said:

*If you are reading this, then it worked, and I am sorry.*

August read it three times before the words would behave.

*I am sorry* — from herself, to herself. She almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was a sound she hadn't made since childhood, a small cracked thing, and she pressed her knuckles against her mouth until it stopped.

Then she did what she had always done with hard things. She sat down, and she started at the beginning, and she read.

---

*My mother's name was Adaeze Okafor,* the second page began. *You will not remember this, so I will tell it plainly, the way she taught me to tell anything that mattered: without decoration, without flinching.*

*She kept bees. That is the first fact and in some ways the last one, because everything about her was in the keeping—how she moved slow around frightened things, how she never swatted, how she could stand among ten thousand stinging bodies in the summer heat and come out unmarked, talking to them in a low voice about the weather. You have her hands. When you're afraid, look down at them. They were steady for forty years among things that could hurt her, and they will be steady now.*

*She died in the fever winter when I was nine. I don't remember her face anymore—I haven't for years, that's what long silence does to a memory, it wears it smooth—and I am writing this so that some version of us gets to keep her. Her laugh was low and surprised, like she'd caught herself at it. She burned rosemary at the door when anyone left on a journey. She told me once that grief was just love with nowhere to put itself, and that the trick was to find somewhere to put it.*

*Find somewhere to put it. That's an instruction, August. Write it somewhere you'll find it again.*

August looked down. Her hands were steady. She hadn't noticed until the page told her to look.

---

The middle pages were harder.

They were about work—her work, fifteen years of it, contracts and clients and the specific grammar of violence she'd spoken fluently since she was nineteen. The woman who wrote these pages described herself the way she'd describe any stranger she respected: competent, careful, expensive, alone by design. *I built a life where no one needed to know me,* the page said, *because knowing people was the one job I was bad at.*

And threaded through all of it, appearing on every page like a figure who keeps walking past the edge of a portrait:

*Tomas.*

*Tomas Reyes has known me since we were both nobody. He is loud where I am quiet, generous where I am careful, and he has lied to me exactly twice in fifteen years, both times to protect me, both times badly. He follows me the way weather follows a mountain. He loves me. I know this the way I know the weight of my own sword—not because anyone said it, though he finally did say it, in Meridian, in a doorway, furious—and I loved him back for years without letting myself know it, because knowing it would have cost me the shape of my life, and I was a coward about the shape of my life.*

*You are going to meet him. Or you already have. Either way: he will be kind to you and it will break your heart, because his kindness won't be new to him even though you are new to him. He will look at you like you're a house he lived in that burned down. Let him. Don't run. Running is the one thing I did that I'd undo.*

*He takes his coffee bitter and pretends otherwise. He hums when he thinks no one can hear—he's always slightly flat. He keeps every promise, including the stupid ones, especially the stupid ones. If he offers to teach you yourself, say yes. He knows me better than I ever let me.*

August set the pages down. Her eyes were wet and she regarded this fact with distant curiosity, the way she might regard rain falling indoors.

Somewhere below her, in this house she didn't know, a floorboard creaked. Footsteps. Pausing outside her door. Not entering. Standing there a long moment, the way a person stands outside a sickroom, and then moving away again, quiet as an apology.

Tomas. Presumably. The man her own hand had spent pages teaching her to grieve.

Because that was the truth of it, and the pages didn't soften it: *You have not lost him. That's the cruelest part. He is alive, he is well, he is probably downstairs right now burning coffee, and you will never feel about him what he felt about you, because the woman he loved paid for the world with the exact part of herself that loved him back. You are the change left over after the price was counted. Be gentle with him anyway. Being gentle with him is the closest you will get to being her.*

---

The last pages were instructions.

*Veyl is real. Veyl sings. If you stand where the ground hums loudest—you'll know it in your teeth—you may hear it, and hearing it will ache in a way you won't have a word for, and that ache is mine, it's the residue of me, treat it kindly.*

*Sefa owes you nothing and will act like it. Feed her before arguing with her; she goes reasonable on a full stomach. Captain Vane is trustworthy in the way a drawn blade is trustworthy: only if you hold it correctly. King Aldric abdicated. The ministers are in chains. The world did not end. You did that. Whatever else you are now, you are the woman who did that.*

*One more thing. There is a valley east of here called the Hollow. Do not go looking for it expecting to find home. The valley forgets; that is its nature and its wound. It has already forgotten you. You cannot mourn a place that has erased your name. But if someday the forgetting thins—if the silence cracks the way it cracked for me—then maybe the valley will give back what it took, and you will get to choose which parts of yourself to carry forward. Until then: the pages are enough. I wrote them slowly. I tried not to lie in them. They are the truest thing I ever made, and they are yours.*

*—August Okafor, who was, who is, who is learning to be*

August folded the pages along their existing creases, carefully, and held them a moment against her sternum, over the heartbeat, as if the paper could be filed inside her where the missing years should go.

Then she got dressed, pocketed her life, and opened the door.

---

The kitchen smelled of burnt coffee and someone pretending it wasn't burnt. A man stood at the stove with his back to her—broad-shouldered, dark-haired going gray at the temples, humming something slightly flat—and August stood in the doorway and checked the description against the man, line by line, like verifying a map.

Bitter coffee, drunk without complaint. Hums flat. Shoulders that looked like they'd carried things a long time.

He turned before she spoke. She watched him do it—watched the hope arrive in his face first, ahead of the fear, the way dawn comes before the sun—and she understood that she was looking at a man who had rehearsed this moment every morning for however many days she'd slept, and who had never once gotten it right in rehearsal.

"Morning," he said. His voice was steady. His hands, wrapped around a chipped cup, were not. "How much of it—do you remember?"

"Everything since the throne room," she said. "Nothing before." She hesitated, then offered the truth like a coin across a palm. "I know you from eleven pages."

Something moved through his face—grief and tenderness wrestling for the same ground—and lost to neither. "Eleven," he repeated softly. "She was thorough."

"She said you'd offer to teach me myself."

"I did promise her I would." He set down the cup. "No pressure. No schedule. Just—" He stopped, started again, and this time his voice roughened. "I spent fifteen years loving a woman who wouldn't let herself be known. I'm not going to waste the second chance she bought. Whenever you want to start, I'm here. That's the whole offer. That's all of it."

August looked at him—at this stranger wearing a grief she could see but not share, holding a love she could read about but not feel—and she did the bravest thing the eleven pages had asked of her.

She didn't run.

"Start now," she said. "Tell me something about her that isn't in the pages."

Tomas laughed—a broken, grateful sound—and wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, and began: "Her mother kept bees. She told me that once, drunk, at a ford, and swore me to secrecy, and I've kept it for twenty years, and it's killing me that you know it before I got to—" He stopped. Laughed again. "There's a lot of that going around."

Outside the window, far to the west, the morning light lay along the land like a laid-out table. And beneath the floorboards, beneath the foundations, faint and patient and unmistakable, the ground was humming—the god's song, returned to the world, waiting.

August touched the pages in her pocket. Eleven sheets of paper, and a whole self to rebuild between them.

"Tell me about the bees," she said.
