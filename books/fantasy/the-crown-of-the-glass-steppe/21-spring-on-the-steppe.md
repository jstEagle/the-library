## 21. Spring on the Steppe

The winter broke on a morning so quiet that Sefa heard the god before she saw the sky.

She had been sleeping in the lee of the cart, wrapped in every hide she owned, when the sound reached her — a low, patient hum rising through the frozen ground like a note held by a throat that had forgotten how to stop singing. For a moment she thought it was the ice cracking, the great sheets of the Steppe splitting as they always did at the turn of seasons. But this was different. This had *shape*. It moved in intervals, swelled and rested, and when she pressed her palm flat against the snow it came up through her bones and settled somewhere behind her sternum, warm as a hand.

*The heartbeat,* she thought. *We found the heartbeat.*

She crawled out of the tent into a dawn the color of pale honey. The long snow had thinned to a glittering crust, and beyond it, far to the east where the mountains lay down like a sleeping figure, the light was doing something impossible: it was bending. Curving toward the range as if drawn by breath, pooling in the hollow of what might have been a collarbone, catching along what might have been closed eyes. The whole horizon looked like a face beneath silk.

Sefa stood in the cold with her bare hands burning and laughed until she cried, because her father's map was right, had always been right, and he was dead, and spring had come anyway.

---

They buried the map three days later, once the melt had made the ground soft enough to dig.

It had been August's suggestion. She'd said it quietly, over the last of the tea, turning the wax-sealed roll in fingers that still hesitated sometimes, as if surprised by their own competence. "It shouldn't live in anyone's pocket," she said. "Maps like this get people killed. It should go back to him."

Sefa hadn't argued. She had carried the map across half the Steppe looking for her father, and instead she had found his handwriting in a palace archive, his careful ink describing the forging of a crown that swallowed a god's voice, and then she had found his grave marker in a refugee camp ledger — one line among four hundred, no date, cause unknown. Grief, she had learned that winter, does not shatter you all at once. It comes in installments. You pay it out slowly or it takes everything.

Now she knelt in the thawing earth at the base of the mountain that was not a mountain, and dug with her hands because it felt wrong to use a tool. The soil here was strange — dark and loose, threaded with veins of glass that caught the light like roots made of dawn. When her fingers touched the silver thread that stitched the great mouth shut, so far below and yet somehow present in every inch of this ground, the hum deepened. Not threatening. Attentive.

"I don't know if you can hear me," she told it, because her father had talked to his maps his whole life and she had inherited the habit. "He drew you. Every line of this, he drew from memory, from stories his mother told him. He spent thirty years trying to prove you were real and they called him a madman and he kept drawing anyway." Her voice cracked. She let it. The god could have her cracks; everyone else here had seen them already. "So I'm giving you his map. So you know somebody believed."

She laid the roll in the small grave. The wax seal had softened with handling; the blank center of the Steppe, the place the ink refused to show, now faced upward toward the sky. And as she filled the earth in, something happened that she would spend years failing to describe properly: the blankness filled. Not with ink. With *light* — a faint luminescence bleeding up through the parchment from underneath, as though the map had finally been given permission to tell the truth. Through the thin skin of new soil she watched it glow for a long moment, and then fade, content, like a lamp turned low at the end of a long day.

Above her, along the ridge of the sleeping figure, the first birds of the season landed on what might have been an eyelid and began to sing.

* * *

August did not come to the burial. Sefa understood why without asking. The woman who had crossed the Steppe with her, who had spoken Veyl's name into a screaming throne room, who had paid a price Sefa still couldn't think about without flinching — that woman existed now only in journals, in other people's memories, in a valley that had erased her name from its stones. The August who walked around camp in the mornings was a reconstruction. A house rebuilt after fire, furnished from descriptions.

But she came afterward. Sefa saw her picking her way up the slope with Tomas a few paces behind — close enough to catch her, far enough to pretend he wasn't hovering — carrying two tin cups of something steaming. She stopped beside the fresh-turned earth and stood there for a while, reading nothing, because there was no marker. Just a mound and a god humming under it.

"Your father," August said finally, handing her a cup. The steam smelled of pine needles and the last hoarded honey. "What was his name?"

"Corvin." Sefa blinked. "Why?"

"Because you're going to say it out loud someday, standing here, and I want to already know it when you do." August sipped her drink. "I've learned that about myself recently. Apparently I plan conversations in advance. There are forty pages about it in my journal."

"You read yourself like scripture."

"I read myself like a stranger's letters." August's mouth did something complicated — not quite a smile. "Every morning. Tomas brings me the book and I sit by the fire and meet a woman who is braver than I feel and funnier than I believe and who loved—" She stopped. Started again. "Who loved someone. It says so on page sixty-one. Very matter-of-fact about it, past-me. No poetry at all. Just facts, like she was writing instructions." She looked down at the grave. "I keep waiting for it to hurt in the way the journal says it's supposed to hurt. It doesn't. Not yet. It's more like... hearing music through a wall. You know there's a song. You can't hum it."

Tomas had come up beside them without either woman noticing, the way he did everything — large and quiet as weather. He said nothing. But Sefa watched his hand find the space just behind August's shoulder blade, not touching, never touching unless invited, and hover there like a promise kept in advance. Page sixty-one, Sefa thought. The man had read the journal too. Had probably memorized it. Was learning her alongside her, matching his grief to her absence of memory, waiting for the song through the wall to become audible to them both.

It was, Sefa decided, the most patient love she had ever witnessed. It deserved better prose than page sixty-one gave it.

"He'll wait forever, you know," she said to August, later, as they walked back down toward camp. Tomas had gone ahead to argue with the mule, which was winning.

"I know." August's jaw tightened. "That's what makes it unbearable. If he pushed, I could push back. If he grieved loudly I could apologize and be done. But he just—" she gestured helplessly, "—*stays*. Brings me tea the way the journal says I take it. Remembers things I told him and tells me them like gifts. 'You hate coriander.' 'You sleep on your left side.' 'You cried at the ford, but you were laughing while you did it.'" She shook her head. "I'm falling in love with a stranger's love story. Mine. Ours. I don't even know which pronoun to use."

"Does it matter?"

August considered this with the ferocious seriousness she brought to everything since the unbinding — as if every question, however small, was a door that might open onto a god. "Maybe not," she said at last. "Maybe the feeling doesn't need my permission. Maybe it just needs time to learn my name."

* * *

Spring on the Steppe was less a season than a negotiation. The snow retreated in patches, revealing glass fields that blazed in the sun, and between them the first green — stubborn, astonished green, pushing up through crystal. The refugees began moving south again in slow processions, and Sefa traded bottled memories at their fires for passage and provisions, though she noticed she was selling less and keeping more. Some of the bottles she'd recovered from the winter camps — names mostly, small bright things, a grandmother's laugh, the smell of a specific kitchen — she had begun returning free of charge, pressing them into hands that had forgotten they were missing anything.

"That one's yours," she would say. "You dropped it. Mind how you go."

The word spread, the way such words do. By the third week people were seeking out her cart specifically, and she woke one morning to find a queue and a child drawing pictures in the mud beside her wheel: a woman, a cart, a bottle with a star inside. She kept the drawing. It seemed important to be recorded accurately by at least one historian.

Tomas built her a second shelf for the bottles. He was good with wood, patient with joints and grain, and he worked in silence except for the occasional muttered negotiation with the materials. One evening August sat watching him, chin on her knees, firelight on her face, and Sefa heard her say — quietly, not meant for an audience, but the Steppe carries voices —

"Tell me the story again. The one where we met."

Tomas didn't look up from his sanding. "You were seventeen. I was nineteen. You beat me at cards with a hand so crooked I'm convinced to this day you cheated, and when I accused you, you smiled like the sunrise owed you money and said, 'Prove it.'"

"Did I cheat?"

"No idea. That's the thing about you. Even when I knew you, I only ever knew maybe half." He blew dust off the shelf, set it down, tested it with his weight. Then he looked at her, and Sefa, pretending very hard to inventory her bottles, saw his face do the unguarded thing it only ever did when August wasn't watching. "Half was enough to cross the world for. I figure the other half is worth the wait."

August reached over and took his hand. Just that. Held it. Two people sitting side by side in the enormous quiet of the Steppe, holding hands like it was the first draft of something.

Sefa inventoried the same bottle eleven times.

* * *

The valley sent no one. That was the part Sefa found herself thinking about, walking the perimeter of their little camp at dusk, listening to the god hum its vast slow note beneath the glass.

Word traveled; she'd made sure of it. Traders heading south carried the news the way traders carry everything: a god wakes on the Steppe, a king has set down his crown, magic runs in the old places like water finding old channels. And surely — *surely* — some piece of that news would wash up against the Hollow, against whatever ward or wound kept that valley silent, and someone would come looking. Someone whose life had been built next to a stolen voice for a hundred years without knowing it.

No one came. Or rather — Sefa corrected herself, crouching to touch a shard of battlefield glass that sang faintly at her fingertips — no one came *from* the valley. Travelers passed on the southern road, and twice she heard them speak of the Hollow in the offhand way people speak of places that have always been as they are. *Quiet folk down there. Keep to themselves. Funny thing, though — nobody seems to remember why the valley got its name.* The forgetting held. Whatever August had paid to unbind king and god, the valley had kept its own price, and its own silence, and its own erasure of the woman who had walked out of it trailing her whole life behind her like a torn hem.

August took the news the way she took everything now: seriously, completely, and without flinching. "Good," she said, and meant it, mostly. "If they came, I'd have to decide whether to go back, and I've decided. It's in the journal. Page ninety-four. Past-me was very clear."

"And what does page ninety-four say?"

"'The Hollow raised me well. It also ate my mother's face from my memory and charged me rent in names. Let it forget. I'm busy.'" August recited it flatly, but her hand went to her pocket where the journal lived, the way another person might touch a wound or a talisman. "Past-me sounds insufferable."

"She sounds right."

"She usually does. It's infuriating." But there was something under the joke, and Sefa had spent a winter learning to hear it: the grief of a woman mourning a home that had already finished forgetting her. You cannot mourn a place that denies it ever held you. The loss has nowhere to land. It just circles, looking for a roof.

So they built her one instead. None of them said it aloud — that would have ruined it — but the camp grew walls that month. Tomas raised a proper frame for Sefa's cart and a lean-to for the stores. August, following diagrams from her own journal, built a stone hearth with her own hands and got it wrong twice and laughed harder at the failures than Sefa had heard her laugh at anything. By the time the first true warm night arrived, there was a structure where there had been a waypoint, a hearth where there had been a fire pit, and when travelers came up the road they saw not refugees but a settlement beginning, three people and a mule and a shelf of bottled memories at the edge of a god's dream.

"Why here?" Sefa asked her one night, both of them awake while Tomas snored with the dignified totality of a landslide. "There's a whole world waking up. Cities. Coastlines. You could see the ocean — it's in the journal, page twelve, you've never seen it."

"I know. I'm saving it." August lay with her hands laced behind her head, watching stars wheel above the silhouette of the sleeping god. "Here, I can hear it. Him. Veyl. All the time, if I listen. And sometimes when I listen, I remember things nobody wrote down." She was quiet a moment. "The journal has gaps. Things past-me couldn't capture — the smell of rain in the Hollow, the sound of Tomas saying my name when he thinks I'm asleep. Feelings. You can't bottle those, Sefa. Believe me, I've seen your stock." A ghost of a smile. "But the god remembers everything that ever happened on this Steppe. Every battle. Every mercy. Maybe every love. And when I press my hands to the ground and listen, some of it leaks through. Fragments. Other people's lives, and mine mixed in among them, the parts ink can't hold." She turned her head. "I'm relearning myself from the ground up. Literally."

"The god as tutor."

"The god as archive." August's voice went soft. "It owes me. We have an arrangement."

* * *

On the last morning of the chapter of the world that Sefa would always think of as *the winter*, she walked alone to the grave of the map.

The grass had come in around it — impossible, weeks early, a circle of green in the shape of cupped hands. The humming was louder here, layered now with something new: fragments of melody threading through the great sustained note, the way a singer warming up will break a long tone into pieces of song. Veyl, remembering how to sing. Practicing.

Sefa knelt and put her ear to the earth, the way her father must have done in dreams his whole life.

"Corvin Almeida believed in you," she said. "His daughter does too."

The melody paused. Shifted. And then — she would swear this to her dying day, and did swear it, at festivals, to children, to skeptical cartographers — the god hummed three notes that were unmistakably, deliberately, the opening bars of the old steppe-song her father used to sing while he inked his maps. The one about the traveler who trades her shadow for a lantern and walks home lit from inside.

Sefa stayed there a long time, laughing and crying into the warm dark earth, while behind her, up the slope, a reconstructed woman read her own handwriting aloud to the man who loved her, and the Steppe glittered, and spring got boldly, recklessly on with the business of the world.

And far to the south, on the road out of Meridian, a rider in travel-stained gray turned her horse north toward the humming — a woman with a captain's bearing and a king's dreams still fading from her sleep, carrying a letter sealed with thread and silver, and a question she had ridden three hundred miles to ask.

Sefa did not see her yet. But the god did, and its song changed key, and it sounded almost like laughter.
