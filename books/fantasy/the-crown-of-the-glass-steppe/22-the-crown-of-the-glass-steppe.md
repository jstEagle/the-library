## 22. The Crown of the Glass Steppe

One year, to the day.

August Okafor stood where the god's heartbeat was loudest and felt it come up through the soles of her boots like a second pulse, patient and vast, the way she imagined a mother's heartbeat must feel to a child still carried. The snow had gone soft with spring at last, slumping off the shoulders of the sleeping mountain in slow gray ribbons, and where it melted, the ground hummed. She had learned to read the humming the way other people read weather: a low note for contentment, a tremor when something old turned over in its sleep, a bright ringing thread on mornings like this one, when the light came down the valley clean and gold and Veyl's voice was close enough to almost be words.

The mountain range shaped like a sleeping figure had not moved. It never would, perhaps. But the silver thread that had sewn its mouth shut was gone now—she had watched it burn away in a crown's dying scream—and what remained was not silence. What remained was breath.

She counted it, as she did every morning. In through the melting snowfields. Out through the glass pools, which steamed faintly even in winter, warmed from below by a voice learning to sing again after three hundred years of being worn as jewelry.

"You're showing off," she said to the mountain.

The heartbeat stuttered, which was how Veyl laughed.

* * *

She had been coming here for eleven months. The first time, she had not known why she came. She had woken in a waystation outside Meridian with pages in her pocket written in her own hand—*You are August Okafor. You are reading this because you paid a price you chose freely, and you will want to know what you bought*—and no memory of writing them. She had read her life like a stranger's story: a mother who sang off-key; a valley that forgot; fifteen years of mercenary work beside a man named Tomas Reyes whose name made something in her chest ache before she knew why it should.

The pages had told her about him. They could not make her feel him. That was the cruelty of the bargain, or its mercy, depending on the hour: memory returned as information, and feeling had to be rebuilt by hand, brick by brick, like everything else after the long winter.

So she had stayed near him. That was the first instruction in her own handwriting, and the only one she had followed without argument: *Stay near Tomas Reyes. Do not explain yourself to him. Let him tell you who you were.*

He had told her slowly, over months, in the careful voice of a man handling something breakable. He told her about the ford where they'd met as teenagers, both of them broke and lying about it. He told her about the tollhouse question—the night he'd followed her to the valley's edge because she lied to him for the first time in fifteen years, and how he had stood in the snow watching her walk out of the world and understood, finally, what he'd never said. He told her about taking her place halfway through the unbinding rite, pouring his memories of her into the crown to finish what she started, and how the thing wearing Aldric had unraveled screaming while Veyl's voice came back as dawn.

"And then I woke up," he'd said, "and you looked at me like I was a stranger."

"You were," she'd said. "I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize." He had smiled then, and it had hurt, because the pages said he smiled exactly that way and she couldn't feel it yet. "You get to fall in love with me twice. Most people only manage once."

* * *

Now, on the anniversary, she climbed the last slope to the place where the god's throat would have been, if mountains had throats, and sat down in the crook of stone like sitting against the ribs of something enormous and breathing. Below her, the Glass Steppe spread out in the morning light—a sea of crystallized battlefield, all those pools of pooled memory glittering like spilled coins. Sefa was out there somewhere, walking her rounds, trading bottled memories to refugees who wanted back the faces of their children. She buried her father's map at this site in the first week of spring, tamped the earth down with her boot, and said, "There. Now you're useful." Then she'd cried for an hour where nobody could see, which August pretended not to know about, because that was friendship too.

Ilsa Vane had gone back to Meridian with the abdicated king. Aldric lived now in a small house near the river, growing vegetables badly, visited weekly by ministers who came to account for themselves and left looking lighter or heavier depending on what they'd done. He remembered almost nothing of his reign. He remembered his wife's face, which Ilsa had fought to recover from the archive of unmade things, and he remembered, sometimes, a minute of terrible clarity in a throne room, a woman speaking a word that was also a song, and he would say to anyone who listened: *I was saved by someone who gave up more than I lost. Find out her name. I want to owe her properly.* August had let him learn it eventually. Debts, she'd decided, were better paid than refused.

And Tomas—

Tomas was at the bottom of the slope, climbing, carrying two tin cups and a wrapped bundle that smelled like the flatbread he insisted on making despite having no talent for it. She watched him come and felt the thing she had spent eleven months building: not the old love, which was gone with the rest of the Hollow's gift, but a new one, assembled honestly, chosen daily. Some mornings it fit like her own hands. Some mornings it fit like borrowed gloves. But it fit more often than not, and it was hers, and she had earned every seam of it.

"God's awake?" he called up.

"Louder than usual. I think it likes the thaw."

"Everything likes the thaw." He dropped down beside her, breathless, and handed her a cup. The tea inside was terrible. She drank it anyway, and found, with quiet satisfaction, that she loved it—not despite the taste but including it, the whole ridiculous fact of him included, the way love works when it's built instead of found.

They sat in the sun and drank bad tea above a sleeping god, and for a while neither of them spoke, because they had learned that too.

* * *

The voice came at midmorning, when the light hit the glass pools just so.

It did not arrive as sound, exactly. It arrived as pressure behind the eyes, as warmth in the sternum, as the sudden certainty that the air itself had leaned closer to listen. August set down her cup. Tomas went still beside her. They had both learned the signs.

*AUGUST OKAFOR.*

Even diminished—even reduced, after centuries of theft, to something smaller than it had been, a god like a lamp relit in a cathedral-sized dark—Veyl's voice still rearranged the inside of her skull pleasantly, like a hand smoothing rumpled sheets.

"I'm here," she said.

*I HAVE BEEN COUNTING,* the god said. *AS YOU COUNT MY BREATHS. TODAY MAKES A YEAR OF YOUR COMING HERE. A YEAR OF YOUR READING YOUR OWN LIFE EVERY MORNING LIKE SCRIPTURE. A YEAR OF HIM TELLING YOU WHO YOU WERE, ONE STORY AT A TIME, HOPING EACH ONE LANDS WHERE IT USED TO LIVE.*

Beside her, Tomas had stopped breathing. August reached over without looking and took his hand.

"What's your point, old one?"

*MY POINT,* said Veyl, with the enormous gentleness of something that could crack glaciers, *IS THAT I OWE YOU. THE VOICE WAS MINE. THE CROWN WAS MADE TO HOLD IT. YOU UNBOUND ME AND PAID WITH THE VALLEY'S HOARDING—EVERYTHING THEY EVER ERASED FROM YOU, KEPT SAFE IN THAT SILENT PLACE UNTIL YOU SPENT IT ALL AT ONCE. I HAVE BEEN GATHERING WHAT I COULD OF IT. MEMORY POOLS IN MY PRESENCE NOW, AS IT NEVER DID BEFORE. SOME OF WHAT YOU LOST HAS DRIFTED BACK TO ME LIKE SNOWMELT FINDING A RIVER.*

August's heart did something complicated. "You're offering to give it back."

*I AM OFFERING A CHOICE. WHICH IS THE ONLY THING THAT WAS EVER STOLEN FROM ANYONE IN THIS ENTIRE AFFAIR. CHOICE.* A pause, vast and deliberate. *COME TO THE THROAT-PLACE WHEN YOU ARE READY. NOT ALL OF IT SURVIVED. NOT ALL OF IT SHOULD RETURN. BUT SOME OF IT IS YOURS, AND I AM DONE LETTING OTHER PEOPLE DECIDE WHAT PEOPLE KEEP.*

Then the presence withdrew, folding away like a tide, and the morning was only a morning again, except that August was shaking and Tomas was gripping her hand hard enough to hurt.

"The throat-place," he said hoarsely. "That's—you're going to—"

"I don't know what I'm going to do." She laughed, and it came out wet. "A year ago I wrote myself instructions because I knew I wouldn't remember why any of it mattered. And now the god is offering me a refund, and I don't—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't trust myself to know which parts I want."

"Then take me with you," he said simply. "You trusted me with the rite. Trust me with this."

* * *

The throat-place was a hollow between two ridges of the sleeping figure's jaw, floored with pale glass that had once been a battlefield and was now something else: a mirror-field where the god's gathered memories lay like frost flowers, each one a small bright shape that flickered when approached. August knelt among them. Tomas knelt beside her. Above them the sky was the hard clean blue of early spring, and below them the heartbeat rolled on, steady as a drum in another room.

*CHOOSE,* said Veyl.

The first shape she touched showed her mother's kitchen—the yellow wall, the off-key singing, flour on dark hands. August pulled her fingers back as if burned.

"No," she whispered. "No, I—"

*IT HURTS BECAUSE IT IS TRUE,* the god said. *THAT IS HOW YOU KNOW IT IS REAL AND NOT LONGING. LONGING DOES NOT HURT. LONGING ONLY ACHES.*

She thought of the pages in her pocket. *Your mother sang off-key and burned the bread and loved you without conditions, which is rarer than magic.* She had read that line four hundred times and felt nothing but a scholar's interest. She touched the shape again, deliberately, and let it open.

Her mother's voice filled her skull, off-key and glorious, singing some valley lullaby about a river that forgot its source. August wept without deciding to, great ugly sobs that startled birds off the ridge, and Tomas put his arm around her and held on, and the memory settled into her chest like a coal into a grate, and it *hurt*, and she held onto it with both hands.

"That one," she gasped. "I want that one. Whatever it costs."

*NOTED. NEXT.*

Some shapes she declined. The face of the bandit leader from chapter one of her life, the one who'd spoken a forbidden name—she let that drift back into the frost. The specific geography of her mercenary years, the contracts and betrayals, the professional calluses: declined. *Those made me good at surviving,* she told the god. *I'm done optimizing for survival.* Veyl made a sound like wind through a canyon that might have been approval.

But some she took, one after another, kneeling in the glass until her knees ached: the smell of rain on the Hollow's red clay. Her mentor's laugh, which the letters had described but never delivered. The exact weight of a sword she'd carried for nine years. The taste of the water at the ford where a courier died and everything changed. And then—

Then a shape unlike the others. Not a scene. A feeling, folded into itself like a letter, and when she opened it there were no images at all, only warmth, only the sensation of being known completely and staying anyway, of hands that kept choosing her, of a voice saying *you get to fall in love with me twice*—

"This isn't mine," she said, confused. "This is—this is how he feels. This is *his*—"

*HE POURED HIS MEMORIES OF YOU INTO THE CROWN,* said Veyl. *THE CROWN DIED. WHAT IT HELD DID NOT VANISH. IT DRAINED INTO THE WORLD, AND THE WORLD DRAINS INTO ME. THIS ONE HAS BEEN WAITING A YEAR FOR SOMEONE TO CLAIM IT. IT WAS ALWAYS ADDRESSED TO YOU.*

August looked at Tomas. He was staring at her with an expression she suddenly, catastrophically recognized—recognized from the inside, from the memory she was holding, from fifteen years of partnership compressed into a single feeling that had never once been named until a night at the valley's edge.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you idiot. You wonderful idiot. You've been carrying this alone since the rite."

"It seemed rude," he managed, "to ask for it back."

She pressed the shape to her chest, and Veyl let it go, and the feeling of being loved—not the knowledge of it, not the word, but the *feeling*, warm and terrifying and entirely real—flooded into the space where the old love used to live, and found it furnished already, and settled in like it had always meant to stay.

* * *

They walked down from the mountain in the late afternoon, past the glass pools, past the place where Sefa's father's map lay buried under a cairn of white stones. At the edge of the Steppe, where the ground stopped humming and became merely ground, August stopped and turned back for one last look at the sleeping figure against the sky.

"Thank you," she said.

*DO NOT THANK ME,* said Veyl, drowsy now, sinking toward its ancient rhythms. *THANK HER. THE MENTOR. SHE KNEW WHAT THE VALLEY WAS. SHE LEFT YOU LETTERS SO YOU WOULD KNOW TOO. I ONLY DELIVERED WHAT SHE PACKED.* A pause, and then, softer, in the tone of a god making a joke it had been saving for three hundred years: *AND THANK THE KING. HE GROWS TERRIBLE VEGETABLES. SOMEONE SHOULD EAT THEM.*

August laughed all the way down the hill.

At the bottom, Tomas waited with the terrible flatbread and the two tin cups, and when she reached him she kissed him—first, this time, deliberately, choosing it the way she now chose everything—and the feeling she had carried down from the throat-place rose up to meet the kiss like a flame meeting oil.

"One year ago today," he said against her mouth, "I didn't know if you'd ever look at me like that again."

"I write it down every morning," she said. "In my own hand. So future-me doesn't forget what this feels like." She tapped her temple. "Turns out I've decided to keep this one the hard way. Earned, not given."

"Peace earned," he agreed, "tastes better than peace given. Like your tea."

"My tea is an abomination."

"Drink it anyway."

She did. They walked east together, toward the road, toward the rebuilt world with its returned names and its accountable ministers and its ex-king's disastrous garden, and behind them the mountain breathed, and the glass steppe caught the sunset and held it like a crown made of nothing but light—worn by no one, owned by everyone, and finally, at long last, silent in the way that things are silent when they have said everything they needed to say.

Somewhere ahead, spring was still arriving. They had time. For the first time in either of their lives, they had nothing but time—and they intended to spend every hour of it remembering, on purpose, together.
