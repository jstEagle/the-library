## 1. Charms That Don't Work

The rain came down on Ashfall City like a confession no one wanted to hear — steady, gray, and full of things better left unsaid. Jonah Galloway pulled the oilcloth over his stall and watched it bead and run, carrying soot from the tanneries into the gutter, carrying everything in this city downhill eventually.

His wares gleamed under the awning: three dozen charms arranged on scarred velvet like jewels at a funeral. Knotwork bracelets for safe travel. Iron nails wrapped in red thread against night-hags. Little clay houses with their doors glazed shut, for keeping a home's luck from leaking out. He'd made every one of them himself, which was the honest part of the trade. The dishonest part was that not a single charm among them did anything at all.

They hadn't in twelve years.

"Charm-seller," said a voice at his elbow, wet and wheedling, "you look prosperous."

Jonah didn't turn. "Prosperity's an illusion, Doss. Like most of what I sell."

Doss Merrow was a rat-faced collector of other people's debts who wore his guild badge polished bright so no one would notice the knife scars underneath. He leaned on the stall's edge hard enough to make the velvet shift, and Jonah's hand drifted, easy and idle, to the iron nail nearest him. Old habits. In another life he could have put that nail through a man's eye before the man finished blinking. In this life he sold it to grandmothers as protection against bad dreams.

"The Office of Purity swept Copperlane yesterday," Doss said. "Two arrests. A hedge-witch and a boy who was only lighting candles for his mother."

"Lighting candles isn't magic."

"It is now." Doss smiled with too many teeth. "Everything is now. Which makes your little shop of wonders either worthless or worth hanging, depending on the mood of whoever kicks the door in."

"Then I'm safe," Jonah said. "My goods are dead as doornails. Ask any witch-finder."

"I did ask one. Captain Vane's people walked my street on Thirdday. They bought a charm off old Maretta at the corner." Doss tapped the counter once, twice. "She swore it worked. Swore her husband's cough cleared right up after she hung it over the bed."

Jonah kept his face still, the way you learn to keep your face still when a wounded man asks how bad it looks. "Maretta swears her cat tells fortunes. Grief does strange work in the mind."

"Mm." Doss straightened, satisfied or pretending to be, and dropped a copper on the counter — payment for nothing, a reminder that everything in Ashfall had a price even when it had no value. "Mind yourself, Galloway. The city's got a fever coming. I can smell it under the rain."

He went. Jonah stood a moment longer watching the water sheet off the awning, then began arranging charms that would never work, in a city where working ones could get you burned.

* * *

Ashfall had outlawed magic the way a man outlaws drinking after his brother drowns drunk — completely, righteously, and without ever quite managing to stop thinking about it. The edicts went up in the first year after the Godswar ended. No covenant-craft, no prayer-craft, no hedge-work, no blessing, no binding. The gods themselves had gone silent in the war's last days, every one of the Ninefold, and the Court had declared the silence a mercy and a mandate: heaven had withdrawn; mortals must govern themselves; anyone who claimed otherwise was either a fraud or a blasphemer, and both burned equally well.

So Ashfall burned its witches, quietly at first and then less quietly, and the small magics died out of the streets like candle flames pinched one by one. And men like Jonah, who had once carried power the way other men carry knives, learned to sell the memory of it instead.

He was good at that memory. That was the cruelty of it. He knew exactly how a knot-charm *should* sit against the wrist, how the thread ought to be wound sunwise with the maker's breath caught in the loops. He made them perfectly. Dead things, but perfect. Desperate people could feel the craft in them, the shape of a working long gone, and feeling it was almost enough.

Almost enough was the whole economy of Ashfall, when you thought about it.

Midday brought the market crowd — fishwives with hems dark to the knee, porters steaming under their loads, a funeral party in gray walking slow behind a cart because coffins cost more than carts could carry comfortably. Jonah sold six charms before the bell. A sailor took a knotwork bracelet for the crossing south. A young wife bought a clay house for luck, turning it over in her hands like she expected it to hatch. An old soldier — Jonah marked the way he stood, weight back, always a wall at his spine — bought two iron nails and didn't ask questions, and Jonah didn't offer stories, and between them they maintained the polite fiction that neither of them recognized the other's trade.

"You were at Redford Bridge," the soldier said anyway, counting coppers.

"A lot of people were at Redford Bridge."

"You were the one who held the left flank when the sky went wrong." The soldier looked up, and there it was in his face, the thing Jonah spent twelve years scrubbing off himself: recognition, and under it, awe, and under *that*, hunger. Men wanted to touch the war the way they wanted to touch relics. It meant something had happened near them once. "Galloway. They sang about you in the camps."

"They sing about everyone in the camps. It passes the time." Jonah closed the till. "Nails are a copper each. Luck's free, but I don't recommend trusting it."

The soldier lingered, wanting more, wanting Jonah to be the song. Jonah gave him nothing, because giving it would mean being it again, and the man he'd been at Redford Bridge had died somewhere between the bridge and the silence — died badly, in pieces, along with forty-one others whose names Jonah still kept folded in a packet against his heart, written in a code that wasn't magic anymore but might as well have been, for all the good reciting it ever did him.

Forty-one names. One company. The Ninth Ashfall Irregulars, sworn under Veyra's sigil — the white feather, the closed eye — and unsworn by a god's silence in the space of a single afternoon.

He didn't think about the bridge. He was very skilled at not thinking about the bridge. He'd had twelve years of practice, and practice makes mastery, and mastery was the only kind of miracle left in Ashfall.

* * *

The child came at dusk, when the market was emptying and the lamps were being lit and the rain had thinned to a drifting mist that turned every lantern into a smudge of gold.

She couldn't have been more than seven, small enough that the crowd swallowed her twice before Jonah spotted her standing at the edge of his stall, soaked through, staring at the charms with the flat, total attention children give to things they've decided matter. She had the look of the harbor quarter on her — coal dust worked deep into her knuckles, shoes two sizes wrong, hair cut raggedly short the way it gets cut when someone in the house has lice or grief or both.

Her mother, probably. Everyone in Ashfall had someone dying slowly.

"Those aren't toys," Jonah said gently. "Move along, sprout. Your mother will wonder where you are."

"She's sick." The girl didn't look at him. She was looking at the candles — Jonah kept three stubs burning at the stall's corners, cheap tallow, more signal than light, the way every charm-seller in the city did. A lit flame said *open*. "Everyone says you're the best one. Everyone says your charms are real."

*Everyone says your charms are real.* Twelve years of careful mediocrity, of prices set low and claims set lower, and somehow the harbor quarter had decided otherwise. Or the child was lying, or desperate, or repeating what some neighbor had told her, which amounted to the same thing. Hope traveled fast through poor districts. It moved like water through a broken roof, finding every crack, pooling everywhere it shouldn't.

"My charms are thread and clay and nail," Jonah said. "Real objects. Real thread. But they don't do anything, sprout, and I won't tell you different, because the people who tell you different are the ones the Office of Purity comes looking for."

The girl finally looked up at him. Her eyes were gray as the rain, and very old in a way that had nothing to do with years. Seven years old, and already fluent in the language of doors closing.

"Healer-priests used to fix people," she said. "Before."

"Yes."

"What happened to them?"

Jonah had answered that question for buyers, for drunks, for himself, a thousand times, and he had a whole shelf of answers: the Court burned them, the gods abandoned us, the world grew up, the world grew tired. He opened his mouth to hand her one, the same way he handed sailors knots and soldiers nails.

What came out instead was the truth, quiet and unarmored, because her eyes wouldn't let him do otherwise.

"They stopped being able to hear the thing that made them healers. And nobody knows why."

The girl nodded, slowly, as if this confirmed something she'd suspected about the world all along. Then she reached into her dress and brought out a coin — a real silver bit, worn nearly smooth, the kind of money that represented somebody's entire savings or somebody's entire theft — and set it on the counter with both hands, the way you set down something heavy.

"For her," she said. "Make one work."

The rain ticked on the oilcloth. Somewhere down the lane, a lamplighter's pole knocked against glass, knock-knock-knock, the heartbeat of the ordinary evening. Jonah looked at the silver, and at the child, and felt the familiar machinery of refusal begin to turn — *I can't, it doesn't work, take it back, buy bread* —

And then the girl bowed her head over the counter, over the silver coin, and prayed.

Not to him. He understood that immediately, with a clarity that felt like cold water. She cupped her dirty hands around the coin and closed her eyes and whispered, barely audible under the rain, the words children learn before they learn they're not supposed to know them:

*"Veyra of the quiet hour, keeper of the closed eye, hear me. My mother can't get up. Please. Please. Anything. Please."*

Jonah's whole body went still.

Twelve years since he'd heard those words spoken aloud in earnest. The Office of Purity had done its work well; prayer in Ashfall survived only in kitchens and cellars and the mouths of the very old and the very young, whispered below the volume of consequence. But he'd known that prayer. He'd taught versions of it. He'd *written* versions of it, in another life, for soldiers who wanted the god of small mercies close before a fight. *Veyra of the quiet hour.* His god. His sworn lady. The white feather and the closed eye, who had watched over the Ninth Irregulars until the afternoon she didn't, until the sky above Redford Bridge went silent as a struck bell and stayed silent, forever, for everybody.

Nothing answered prayers anymore. That was the founding fact of the age, the bedrock under the law and the ash and the quiet. Nothing answered, and the proof was that Jonah Galloway was still alive — because if anything had been listening, it would have owed him, and debts like that don't go unpaid in silence.

The girl prayed. The rain fell. The tallow candle at the corner of the stall guttered low, drowned in its own wax, and went out.

The girl's whisper faltered. Her hands tightened around the coin.

And the candle relit itself.

No wind had turned. No match, no spark, no lamp-lighter passing close. The wick simply bloomed — a small, steady, golden flame rising out of smoke that should have had no fire left in it, burning straight and calm in the damp air as though it had never been anything but lit.

Jonah did not move. Every instinct he owned — and he owned the instincts of a man who had survived a war and twelve years of outlawry — screamed at him to move, to snatch the flame out, to kick the candle into the gutter, to grab the child and run. Because flames didn't relight. Wicks didn't bloom. The laws of the world had a clause in them, unwritten but absolute, and Jonah had built his entire second life inside that clause: *the gods are silent, the gods are gone, nothing hears, nothing answers.*

Something heard. Something answered.

It hadn't even taken much. A child, a coin, a candle — pocket change, cosmic bookkeeping, a mercy so small it was almost contemptible. Except that it wasn't small at all. It was the largest thing that had happened in Ashfall in twelve years, and it had happened at *his stall*, over *his counter*, in answer to a prayer to *his god*, and Jonah Galloway stood in the gold light of an impossible flame and felt terror move through him like winter moving through a house — room by room, floor by floor, settling into everything.

Because if Veyra was listening again, then she was awake again.

And if she was awake, she remembered Redford Bridge.

"Did you see?" The girl's head came up, eyes huge, rain and wax-light swimming in them. "Did you see it? Did she hear me?"

Jonah found his voice somewhere below his ribs and hauled it up. "Go home, sprout."

"But—"

"*Go home.*" Not gentle this time. The word came out with the old command-voice in it, parade-ground flat, and the child flinched — and then, being a child of the harbor quarter, read the fear underneath the tone correctly, the way such children always did. She snatched her hand back from the counter, leaving the silver bit where it lay, and ran. Her footsteps slapped away down the lane, quick and getting quicker, and the mist closed behind her.

Jonah stood alone under the awning with a lit candle and a dead man's heart rate.

Slowly, not letting himself think about it, he reached out and touched the flame with one finger. Heat. Ordinary heat, ordinary fire, wax and tallow doing what wax and tallow did. The flame bent around his finger and stood up straight again, patient as anything.

"Twelve years," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange to him, scraped thin. "Twelve years, and now? Now, of all the cursed—"

The flame flickered. Just once. Just slightly — toward him, against no draft at all, leaning in the way a person leans to catch a whisper across a table.

Jonah stepped back. His heel caught the stool; he caught himself on the counter, and the silver coin rang against the wood, a small bright bell-note in the emptying market.

Down the lane, the lamplighter was working his way toward the harbor road. Somewhere beyond it, out past the breakwater, thunder muttered — except there were no clouds left to thunder, and Jonah knew thunder, had fought under it, and this wasn't weather. It was deeper than weather. It was the sound a very large door makes, very far away, beginning to open after a very long time locked.

The candle burned on, steady, golden, impossible.

Jonah Galloway picked up the silver coin, closed his fist around it until the edges bit, and stood in the rain listening to a god he hated begin, very softly, to speak.
