## 2. The First Answered Prayer

By morning, the whole of Ashfall knew.

Jonah heard it first from the fishwives on Tallow Row, their voices cutting through the gray light like knives through brine. A sailor's drowned son, pulled from the harbor cold as slate and blue as a bruise, had coughed seawater and come back. The mother had prayed — not to the Ninefold Court, that was outlawed, but to *anything*, to the dark, to her own dead mother — and something had answered. That was the story they told, anyway, huddled at the fountain with their baskets of silver-scaled catch, their breath steaming in the cold.

"Third one this week," said a woman with a face like a walnut shell. "My cousin's girl in the Ropewalk. Fever burned right out of her overnight. They'd already dug the grave."

"The Crown says it's lies," said another. "The Crown says a lot of things."

They crossed themselves in the old way when they thought no one was looking — thumb to sternum, then brow, then the two shoulders, the gesture every child in Ashfall had learned before the Edicts and none had dared perform since. Jonah watched them do it and felt the ground tilt beneath his certainty.

He opened his stall anyway. Habit was a kind of armor, and he had worn his for twelve years: the awning with its faded stripes, the tray of charms laid out in careful rows — knotted red thread against bad luck, river-stones drilled and strung for safe passage, wax medallions stamped with sigils that meant nothing anymore except to collectors and fools. He priced them the way he always did, high enough to seem potent, low enough for grief to afford. And he lied the way he always did, gently, meeting each buyer's eyes: *This will hold. This will keep them. This will work.*

The lie had kept him alive. In Ashfall, where the Practice Edicts hung nailed to every customs post and church door, magic was sedition and faith was its accomplice. The gods were silent — had been silent since before Jonah could remember, since the Godswar ended and the Court proclaimed the Age of Reason with bonfires of holy books. What remained were people like him: charm-sellers, hedge-priests, bone-readers, the small magicians who sold the shapes of hope because the substance had gone out of the world. The witch-finders called them leeches. The Office of Purity burned them in the Corn Market square, politely, with a signed writ posted beforehand so the crowd would know justice was being done.

So Jonah sold nothing real, swore oaths to nothing, and slept each night in a room above his shop with the shutters barred and a knife under the mattress, telling himself the silence was mercy. Telling himself he preferred it that way.

By midmorning he couldn't pretend the stories were only stories.

A lamplighter came by with a tale of a guttering flame in the Lantern District that had refused to die through an entire storm, though the wick was bare and the oil long spent. A dyer's widow swore her husband's ghost had stood at the foot of her bed and told her where he'd hidden the rent money — and there it was, under a floorboard, exactly where he'd said. Two men argued in front of the stall about a shrine in the Undercrofts that had been bricked over for a decade and now wept clear water from between the stones, water that healed a cooper's ruined hands after one night's soaking.

"Healed," the man repeated, as if saying it louder would make it more believable. "Not eased. *Healed.*"

Jonah took their coin for two knot-charms they didn't need and listened until his ears rang. Around noon a boy ran past shouting about the temple district — the sealed temples, the ones the Crown had stripped and shuttered — and how lights had been seen moving behind the boarded windows of the House of the Drowned God, pale and greenish, like things swimming.

And beneath all of it, threading through the city like smoke through a keyhole, came the other word, the colder one: the Office of Purity was mobilizing. Wagons of white-cloaked witch-finders rolling out of the Citadel. Door-to-door sweeps announced in the Lantern and Tallow districts by nightfall. Captain Isolde Vane herself signing the writs.

Jonah closed early. His hands weren't steady enough to make change.

* * *

His shop sat on Cindervell Lane, wedged between a coffin-maker and a tanner, which suited him fine — neighbors whose trades made visitors unlikely. He locked the door, dropped the bar, and stood a long moment in the dimness, listening to the building settle. Dust motes turned in the light through the shutters' slats. Everything exactly as he'd left it. Everything exactly wrong.

Twelve years of silence, and in three days the world had started answering prayers.

He should have felt vindicated. That was what he'd expected, in the years right after the war, when he still caught himself listening — vindication, or joy, or at least the grim satisfaction of a man proven right. Instead what he felt was the cold sweat fear of a debtor hearing footsteps on the stair. Because Jonah Galloway knew something the fishwives didn't know, something he hadn't spoken aloud since the last embers of the Godswar went out:

Prayers got answered in the old days. And every answer had a price, and every price was written down somewhere, and somewhere a ledger existed with his name in it, in ink that didn't fade.

*Veyra.*

Her name rose in him unbidden, the way it always did — not summoned, just surfacing, like a body coming up from deep water. The Silent Sister. The Veiled One. The god who had held his oath for twelve years of soldiering and then, in the worst hour of his life, gone quiet. Not withdrawn. Not distant. *Gone* — the way a lamp goes out, the way a voice cuts off mid-word.

He had been twenty-six. He had commanded forty-one souls under Veyra's banner — hedge-knights and charm-soldiers, the covenant's small faithful, sworn to a god whose whole doctrine was protection of the overlooked. And in the pass at Keldrim Ford, with the enemy host coming down like a landslide and his company holding the line because their god's grace hardened their shields and steadied their aim, she had simply... stopped. Mid-prayer. Mid-*battle*. Forty-one men and women felt their god vanish between one heartbeat and the next, and the shields went soft, and the line broke.

Thirty-three had died. He had carried eight home. He had carried the silence longer.

Now he climbed the narrow stair to his room, dragged the old campaign chest from under the bed, and knelt before it with his heart going hard against his ribs.

Inside, under folded cloaks and a spare sword-belt, lay the thing he hadn't touched in twelve years: his oath-token. A disc of black iron the size of a coin, strung on a leather cord gone stiff with age. Veyra's mark was pressed into it — a veiled eye within a circle — and around the rim, in the old covenant-script, the words of his swearing, cut so fine he needed good light to read them: *What is owed shall be answered. What is asked freely shall be given.*

Covenant-terms. The grammar of the old bond between gods and mortals, back when devotion was a contract with clauses and obligations on both sides. Back when a sworn bearer could call on his god and be heard.

He held the token in his palm. It was cold. It had been cold for twelve years — dead iron, a relic, a souvenir of a faith that had died at Keldrim Ford along with thirty-three of his people. Every few years, drunk enough or desperate enough, he'd tried once. Held the token, spoke the old words into the dark, waited. Nothing. The silence had a texture by then; he knew it the way you know a missing tooth. Testing it again was just a way of reopening the wound.

But the city outside was full of answered prayers now. Weeping shrines. Boys coughing up harbor water. Lamps burning without oil.

Jonah closed his fist around the token and knelt there on the floorboards, in the dust and the failing light, feeling like a man about to knock on the door of a house where something terrible had happened long ago.

"*Veyra,*" he said. His voice cracked on the second syllable, twelve years of disuse splitting open. He swallowed and tried again, slower, the old formula, the covenant-greeting every sworn bearer learned first: "*Veiled One, hear your servant. What is asked freely, answer freely. I ask.*"

Nothing.

The room ticked and settled. Down in the lane, the coffin-maker's dog barked twice and gave up. Jonah exhaled, half relief, half something worse than disappointment, and began to lower the token—

The iron warmed.

Not much. A degree, maybe two. The sort of warmth a coin keeps from a hand. But Jonah had spent six years of war learning to read that metal the way a sailor reads weather, and he knew — knew with a certainty that dropped through him like a stone through deep water — that the warmth wasn't his own.

His heart stopped, then slammed. He almost threw the token across the room. Almost. His fingers wouldn't open. Twelve years of telling himself he was done with gods, done with oaths, done with the whole rotten architecture of devotion — and his hand closed tighter instead, the way a drowning man closes on a rope even when he can't see what's pulling it.

"*Veyra?*" Barely a whisper.

The warmth pulsed once. Slow. Deliberate. Like a heartbeat. Like a *knock*.

Then pressure — faint, far-off, unmistakable — the sensation he remembered from the war, from the nights when he'd lain awake in camp and felt her attention settle over him like a hand on the crown of his head. Except this wasn't the easy weight of old. This was effortful. Strained. The difference between a hand resting on a shoulder and a hand pressing against the far side of a locked door, pushing.

She was there. On the other side of something. Trying to get through — or trying to tell him something — and whatever lay between them was thick as grave-earth and just as final.

Jonah stayed kneeling until his knees ached and the light through the shutters went from gray to violet to black. The pressure never strengthened. It never faded either. Just that steady, patient push, pulse after pulse, like waves against a sea-wall, and Jonah sat inside the wall with his eyes shut and tears running into his beard without his permission, hating her and grieving her and *missing* her, all three tangled together past any hope of untangling.

Because that was the truth under everything, the one he'd never said out loud, not even to himself: he had missed her the way you miss a limb. Twelve years of silence and he had built a life around the absence, learned to favor the other side, and now here was the phantom ache suddenly, horribly *real*, a hand pressing through the wall of the world, and every part of him that wasn't terrified wanted to press back.

He set the token on the floor between his knees. The warmth held. The pulses came, slow and patient, keeping time with something.

"All right," he said hoarsely. "All right. I hear you."

The pressure didn't change. But for one moment — one breath — it gentled, the way a grip gentles when the person caught in it stops struggling.

* * *

He didn't sleep. Toward dawn he went down and lit the shop lamps and sat behind his counter watching the lane empty itself of dark, and that was when the knocking started on doors up and down Cindervell Lane.

Not Veyra. Boots. Fists. Official cadence.

He looked out through the shutter slats and saw them: white cloaks catching the first light, lanterns bobbing, the Office of Purity working the street in pairs. At the tannery first, then the coffin-maker's. Knock, questions, a paper checked against a list, a stamp of a boot turning toward the next door. Methodical as lice-combing. One of them carried a brazier, and the smell of hot iron reached Jonah even through the shutters — the branding irons, the mark they put on convicted practitioners before the fire or the gallows.

At the head of the pair working his side of the street stood a woman he recognized from the writs posted at the customs posts: sharp-faced, gray-eyed, unhurried, wearing her authority the way other officers wore decorations. Captain Isolde Vane. She consulted a list. She looked up at his sign — the faded awning, the painted name *J. GALLOWAY, CHARMS & CURIOSITIES* — and something in her expression suggested she had already known what she'd find on this street.

The fists hit his door three times, measured as a metronome.

"Office of Purity. Open in the name of the Protector."

Jonah stood very still in his dark shop, the oath-token warm against his chest where he'd strung it back around his neck without quite deciding to, and understood with perfect clarity that the world had tilted overnight and was not tilting back. Outside, the gods were waking and the Crown was sharpening its irons, and he was standing between them with a dead god's knock still echoing in his bones.

He thought of the pulses against the locked door. Patient. Steady. Still there, even now — he could feel it if he paid attention, faint as a pulse under a wrist.

*Please,* the pressure seemed to say. Or perhaps: *Hurry.*

Jonah Galloway unbolted his door, put on his best harmless smile, and opened up to the witch-finders with his heart hammering a war-drum rhythm he hadn't heard in twelve years.

"Captain," he said. "Come in. Mind the dust."
