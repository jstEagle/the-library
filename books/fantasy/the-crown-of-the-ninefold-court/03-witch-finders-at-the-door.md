## 3. Witch-Finders at the Door

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a customer's knock.

Jonah knew the difference the way he knew the difference between rain and blood on the wind — twelve years of living in Ashfall taught a man to hear intent in wood and iron. Customers knocked twice, apologetic, half-hoping no one was home so they could tell themselves they'd tried. This was three blows, evenly spaced, struck with the flat of a gauntlet. Authority's rhythm. The Office of Purity did not knock like men; it knocked like verdicts.

He had been awake since the fourth bell anyway, sitting behind his counter with the candle that had relit itself two nights ago burning in a dish of salt before him. He hadn't let it gutter since. He told himself it was because a lit candle drew customers — superstition sells, and desperate people trust a shop where the flame looks blessed. That was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and he kept the candle lit regardless, because every time he looked at it his chest did something complicated and painful, like a fist closing around an old scar.

Something was listening again.

And if something was listening to a child's prayer in a charm-seller's stall, then sooner or later, something would come asking what Jonah Galloway had been selling for twelve years.

He banked the candle under a clay pot, swept the dead charms into their trays with practiced ease, and opened the door before the third knock could fall.

Four of them on his step, gray cloaks beaded with harbor fog, iron badges shaped like open eyes glinting at their throats. Behind them, filling the narrow lane like a ship wedged between pilings, stood a woman in the black-lacquered half-plate of the Office's officer corps, her cloak dry despite the mist, her face the color of old parchment and about as expressive.

"Jonah Galloway," she said. Not a question. A reading. The way you'd read a name off a ledger you'd already balanced.

"That's me," Jonah said. "Charm-seller. Licensed under the Edict of Quiet, article nine, subsection—"

"I know the Edict." She stepped past him without waiting for invitation, and her four witch-finders flowed through the doorway around her like water finding cracks. "Captain Isolde Vane. You'll stand there and not touch anything while my people work."

"My shop—"

"Is now an active inquiry." She pulled off one glove, finger by finger, and ran her bare hand along the edge of his counter without touching the wood. Jonah had heard about that trick. Purity officers were trained from childhood to feel residue — the greasy aftertaste of working magic, the way a sailor's palm reads damp on a rope. If she felt anything on that counter, it would be twelve years of nothing, because nothing was exactly what he'd been selling.

She frowned slightly. He couldn't tell if the frown meant *I feel nothing* or *I expected to feel nothing and I don't like it.*

Her people moved through the shop with terrible efficiency. One lifted down jars of grave-salt and sniffed them. Another unrolled a bundle of knotted red cords — luck-strings, ten coppers each, worth maybe two — and passed a sliver of cold-iron over them. The iron stayed dull. Of course it stayed dull. Jonah had bought those cords from a weaver in the Ropewalk who charged him honest prices for honest string.

"You sell a lot of protection charms," Vane said, drifting toward the display wall. Rows of small amulets hung on hooks: the Ninefold Wheel, all nine sigils in a ring; the single eye of Korrath for justice-disputes; the coiled serpent of Marenne for fever-nights. All of them carved from riverbone and ashwood by Jonah's own hand, all of them dead as river stones, all of them sold with the same quiet lie: *it can't hurt to have it near.*

"The city likes its comforts," Jonah said.

"The city is forbidden its comforts." She turned an amulet over with one finger. "Edict of Quiet, year one of the Protectorate. All divine workings outlawed. All covenant-magic seized. All oath-bearers registered or renounced." Her eyes came up, gray as slate, and settled on him with the weight of a headstone. "You're registered, Galloway. I checked. Registered as a hedge-charmer, minor license, no oath-bond declared." A pause, thin as a blade. "That last part interests me."

"It shouldn't. I never took an oath."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You just fought in the Godswar under a sworn banner, at the Battle of the Weeping Ford, in the company of Captain-Divine Sarel Voss, whose entire command were oath-bearers to the god Veyra. Records survive, Galloway. Even records people try to burn." She let that sit. "Especially those."

One of the witch-finders — young, freckled, nervous — had drifted to the back shelf and stopped there, staring at the clay pot. Jonah's stomach dropped through the floorboards.

"The candle," the young one said. "Captain. There's wax burned under this pot. Fresh."

The shop went very quiet. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed over the rooftops, and the fog pressed against the windows like a crowd pressing against a door.

Vane crossed the room in four strides and lifted the pot.

The candle was still burning.

It should not have been. Jonah had covered it before dawn — nearly two hours ago. Wax pooled in the salt dish, wick bent and blackened, and yet the flame stood up out of it straight and calm, a little tongue of gold utterly indifferent to the clay that had smothered it, burning without air, burning the way fires used to burn when the gods still bothered to watch.

Nobody moved. The freckled witch-finder made a sound low in his throat, half prayer, half gag.

"Well," Jonah said, because silence was worse. "That's new."

Vane didn't look at him. She looked at the flame the way a woman looks at a snake she has been assured is dead — total attention, total recalibration. Then she did something Jonah did not expect. She reached out with her bare hand and pinched the wick.

The flame went out. Properly, this time. Smoke curled up, ordinary and acrid.

"Outside," she said to her people. "All of you. Wait in the lane."

"Captain—" the freckled one started.

"*Out.*"

They filed past Jonah without meeting his eyes. The door closed. Fog-gray light filled the shop, and Jonah Galloway stood alone with a captain of the Office of Purity, and understood with perfect clarity that he was going to die in this room unless he was very lucky or very useful, and that Isolde Vane had not yet decided which he was.

* * *

She walked the length of the counter slowly, trailing one bare fingertip along the wood, and Jonah used the time to take inventory of her the way he'd once inventoried enemies across a battlefield. Mid-thirties, maybe younger — the Office aged its officers fast. Sword worn at the hip, not displayed. Armor maintained with a soldier's discipline rather than a courtier's vanity. And her eyes — he kept coming back to her eyes. Witch-finders he'd known wore their certainty like armor plate. Vane's certainty had cracks in it, hairline fractures running through the slate, and behind the cracks something moved that looked uncomfortably like doubt.

"You covered that candle," she said, "because you knew we were coming."

"I covered it because it wouldn't stay out."

"When did it start?"

"Two nights ago. A girl prayed over it. It lit itself."

"What did she pray for?"

"Her mother's cough." Jonah shrugged, and hated how casual the shrug sounded even to him. "It's Ashfall. Everyone prays for someone's cough."

"And did the mother recover?"

"I don't know. I sell charms, Captain. I don't perform miracles." He held her gaze. "Whatever's happening out there — the well in Copperlane that runs sweet, the fever that broke in the foundling house, the man in the Ropewalk they say walked on water over the flood-pools — none of it came from me. I've got no more magic in me than that doorknob."

"Mm." She picked up one of his amulets, the Ninefold Wheel, and turned it in the light. "Then explain to me why a dead charm-seller with no oath-bond keeps a candle that burns under cover. Explain why your hands—" she nodded at them, "—are scarred in the pattern of a covenant-grip. I've seen that scarring before, Galloway. On relics. On the mummified hands of saints the Protectorate dug up and burned. Oath-bearers grip their gods' power through the palms, and it marks them." She set the amulet down with a soft click. "So. One more time. Who are you?"

The lie sat ready on his tongue. It had served him for twelve years — *hedge-charmer, minor license, never took an oath* — smooth and worn as a river pebble. He opened his mouth to say it.

And stopped.

Because the candle dish was cold under his fingers, and the fog was gray against the glass, and somewhere beneath the city something vast had turned over in its sleep two nights ago, and the world where his lie worked was ending whether he confessed or not. A man could drown holding onto driftwood. Or he could let go and find out if the water bore him.

"Jonah Galloway," he said quietly. "Oath-bearer to Veyra, the Silent Sister. Sworn at nineteen. Sworn power of the deep quiet — stillness, warding, the hush between heartbeats. I held a shield-line at the Weeping Ford that no engine of war could crack, because Veyra's silence held it for me." His voice roughened. "Until mid-battle. Until the moment the line broke. Then nothing. Twelve years of nothing."

Vane had gone very still. It was the stillness of a hunter who has heard the thing she tracked step out into the open.

"They call it the Godswar's great mystery," she said. "Every god in the Ninefold Court went silent in a single hour. Every answered prayer stopped. Every miracle failed. The armies on both sides collapsed within a season — you can't fight a holy war when heaven stops paying." Her eyes narrowed. "The Office teaches it was mercy. Divine exhaustion. The gods spent themselves and withdrew."

"The Office teaches what the Court tells it to teach."

"Yes," she said simply. "It does. That's rather the problem."

Jonah blinked. Of all the sentences he'd braced for, that wasn't among them.

She pulled off her other glove, folded both gloves together, and set them on the counter with the deliberation of a woman laying down cards. Under the black lacquer of her vambrace, when she pushed the sleeve back, he saw the badge burned into her forearm — the open eye of the Office — and around it, older, half-scraped but unmistakable, the faded outline of a different mark. A wheel. Nine-spoked.

A devotion-mark. Forbidden, scraped, but not erased.

"You were a believer," Jonah said.

"I was a child in Copperlane when the sweet-water well went dry the first time. My mother prayed at it every morning for a year. Nothing. I watched faith starve to death in her face, and then I watched the Office recruit me off that same hunger, because a girl who misses her god makes a fine witch-finder." She said it flatly, without self-pity, the way soldiers report casualties. "I believed in the Edict, Galloway. I believe in it now. Lawless magic killed forty thousand people in the Godswar. I've burned hedge-witches who deserved better and some who deserved exactly what they got, and I slept after every burning, because the alternative to law is the Weeping Ford." She leaned forward, both hands flat on his counter, and for the first time he saw what lived underneath the slate of her eyes, and it wasn't doubt after all.

It was fear. Cold, disciplined, officer-grade fear.

"But miracles are coming back," she said, "and they are not coming back through the Edict. They're coming back wild. Unlicensed. Unbound. Three days ago a prayer-crowd in the Shambles sang to Marenne and the tide came in four hours early and drowned six fisherfolk who weren't warned. Yesterday a man in the Ropewalk healed his daughter's crooked spine by holding her in the flood-pools, and by nightfall half the district was kneeling in the water and my own witch-finders were kneeling with them, and I had to drag them out by their collars." Her jaw tightened. "The gods are waking, and they are not waking gentle, and the Crown's answer is to arrest everyone whose grandmother ever owned an amulet. Which will work precisely until the first mob realizes the witch-finders' iron doesn't burn anymore."

She straightened. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register — lower, faster, the voice of someone stepping off a ledge and talking quickly so as not to think about it.

"I read the old records, Galloway. Not the Office's sanitized catechism — the actual records, the ones sealed under the Clerk's Tower. Covenants. Terms. Reciprocity. Worship given, power returned, boundaries drawn on both sides. Do you know what almost nobody alive remembers?" She tapped the counter. "*How it worked.* The mechanics. What bound a god and what freed one. What an oath-bearer actually was — not a worshipper, not a servant. An *intermediary*. A hinge between mortal need and divine power. There were thousands of you, once. Guilds of you. Now the records say maybe a dozen survived the purges, most of them mad or hidden, and every single one of them is either dead, missing, or—"

"Or selling dead charms in Saltrow," Jonah finished.

"Or that." Something that was almost a smile touched her mouth and died there. "I could arrest you. Don't mistake this for sentiment — I could, and the paperwork would be trivial, and by sundown you'd be in the Purity cells answering questions that make today's look polite. A registered oath-bearer concealing his bond for twelve years, in the middle of an awakening? The Protector himself would sign the order."

"But you're not going to."

"Not today." She picked up her gloves. "Today I'm going to walk out of here, report a clean sweep of an unremarkable charm-shop, and lose your file in a drawer for a week. Maybe two. In exchange—" she fixed him with the slate eyes, and now the fear in them was legible, and it was for the city, for the whole teetering edifice of law and order she'd given her life to, "—you're going to remember for me. Everything. How oaths were sworn and broken. What wakes a god and what soothes one. Whether what's happening can be *bound* again, properly, lawfully, before the Crown does something catastrophic or the gods do something worse. Because I have watched the Office's leadership these past three days, Galloway, and they are not afraid enough. They think this is a policing problem." She shook her head slowly. "This is a weather problem. And I need someone who remembers what the sky used to do."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then next week my memory improves, your file surfaces, and you'll answer these questions in a cell instead of a shop. I'd prefer the shop." She moved to the door, then paused with her hand on the latch, her back to him. "One more thing. The candle. The girl's prayer. Did the mother recover?"

"I told you, I don't—"

"I asked what you *believe*, Galloway. Not what you know."

Jonah was silent a moment. He thought of the child's face in the lamplight, the fierce whisper of *please*, the flame leaping up like a held breath released. He thought of twelve years of selling ash to the desperate and calling it kindness.

"Yeah," he said finally. "I believe she recovered."

Vane nodded once, as if a debt had been entered into a ledger. "Then whatever's listening," she said, "it's granting small mercies. Pray it stays small." She opened the door onto the fog and her waiting squad, and paused again, half-turned, fog curling past her shoulder into the shop like a slow tide.

"A week, Galloway. Have an answer ready. And light your candle or don't — but know that from tonight, everything you do in this shop is being watched. By us." A beat. "And possibly by something older."

The door closed behind her. Through the fogged glass Jonah watched the gray cloaks recede up the lane, Vane's black silhouette last, unhurried, a blade sliding back into its sheath.

He stood a long time in the empty shop. Then he uncovered the candle dish, set the clay pot aside, and looked at the dead wick in its crust of wax.

"Twelve years," he said aloud, to the empty air, to the fog, to whatever had turned over in its sleep beneath the city. "Twelve years of nothing, and now you send me witch-finders."

The wick did not light. But as he watched, the smoke that still curled from it bent sideways — against the draft, against the open door, against every law of fire he knew — and drifted, thin and deliberate, toward the back wall. Toward the floor. Toward *down*.

Toward the ruined temple that every map of Ashfall marked with a single word: *sealed*.

Jonah looked at it for a long moment. Then he began, with steady hands, to pack a bag.
