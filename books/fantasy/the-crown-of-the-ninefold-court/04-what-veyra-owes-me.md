## 4. What Veyra Owes Me

The voice came back on a Tuesday.

Not all at once — that was the cruel part, or maybe the merciful part, Jonah never decided which. It came in pieces, the way a drowned man comes up through dark water: first a pressure behind the eyes, then a warmth along the old scar at his collarbone where a spear had nearly taken his head off at Redford's Crossing, and finally — three days after the candle relit itself for a child named Pip Wrenlow — a single word, spoken in a register below hearing, felt rather than heard, like thunder remembered instead of witnessed.

*Jonah.*

He dropped a jar of tallow-and-iron salve. It cracked against the floorboards and spread a smell of rendered fat and cold metal through the shop, and he stood there with his heart hammering and his hands shaking and told himself, very reasonably, that it was nothing. That twelve years of silence did not end on a Tuesday. That gods did not come back the way bad debts do.

Then it came again.

*Jonah.*

And this time there was something underneath the word. Something that made his knees go soft and his eyes sting without permission. Not an apology. Gods didn't apologize. But a kind of *recognition*, the way you might say the name of someone you'd buried, and he stood in his little shop on Candler's Row surrounded by charms that didn't work and prices he'd learned to set just low enough that desperate people could almost afford them, and he said out loud, to the empty room, to the god who had left him standing in a field of corpses:

"No."

The word came out broken. He said it again anyway.

*No.*

* * *

He hadn't slept properly since the candle. Nobody in Ashfall had, if the talk in the markets was anything to judge by. Miracles were spreading through the city like fire through dry grass — a fishwife's prayer for her drowned son answered at the Saltgate, a lame carter walking, a whole street in the Ropewalk where every locked door had opened at once when the people inside prayed for escape from a tenement fire. The Crown's criers had gone quiet about outlawed magic and started talking about *unlicensed divinity*, which was worse. Witch-finders walked in pairs now, white cloaks bright as bone, and their ledgers had gotten thicker.

Jonah kept his head down and sold his dead charms and waited for the voice to speak again, and hated himself for waiting, and hated himself more when it came.

It came at night, always at night, in the hour when the city's noise thinned to a whisper and a man alone in bed above his shop had nowhere to hide from himself.

*I am sorry,* she said, on the fifth night. Or she said something shaped like *I am sorry*. Veyra's voice had never been words exactly — not even before, not even during the war, when every sworn soldier of the Silent Vigil carried her presence like a coal cupped in bare hands. She spoke in meanings, in pressures, in the particular weight of attention that a mortal mind translated into language because it couldn't bear the raw thing. Twelve years ago that weight had been a comfort, warm and constant as a hearthstone. Now it was a hand closing around his throat from the inside.

"You don't get to say that." He sat up in the dark, gripping his own knees, talking to the air like a madman. "You don't get to be sorry. Sorry is for people who *stay*. You went quiet, Veyra. Mid-battle. Mid-*battle*."

The pressure flinched. He felt it flinch, felt her awareness recoil from him the way a hand recoils from a hot stove, and some petty, starving animal part of him was glad.

"Do you want to know what happened after?" His voice was low and steady, which surprised him; he'd expected shouting. "Because I've had twelve years to rehearse this speech, so let me tell you what happened after. Brannoc held the line at the ford because your silence hit us all at once and half the company thought they'd been abandoned and half thought they'd been *punished*, and nobody ran, because I told them you hadn't left us, I told them it was a test, I told them —" His breath caught. He made himself keep going. "I told them to have faith. Forty-one men and women, Veyra. Forty-one people who trusted me because I carried you, and when you went out like a snuffed candle, the enemy's gods didn't. The Hierarchy still had theirs. Still had their fire-priests and their oath-blades and their certainty. We had pikes and a lie."

Silence. Of course. Silence was what she gave.

"We held for six hours," he whispered. "Six hours on faith alone. And then we broke, and I woke up under Brannoc's body with my shield arm ruined and no god in my chest, and I crawled out of that field and I swore — I *swore* — that I would never again mistake silence for a lesson."

The darkness pressed close. He could feel her wanting to speak, a fullness straining against some boundary, like water against ice. He lay back down and pulled the blanket over his head like a child and said, "Go away," and meant it with everything he had.

She went away.

That was the worst part. After everything, she still did what he asked. Even now, especially now, she obeyed him — and obedience, he was beginning to suspect, was its own kind of wound.

* * *

In the days that followed, she came back anyway, smaller. Fragments. A flicker of presence when he passed the shrine-niche where the old Vigil sigil had been plastered over by the Crown's inspectors, still faintly visible beneath whitewash like a face beneath snow. A pulse of warmth in his scarred collarbone when a beggar on Candler's Row prayed aloud for rain and got it, sudden and local, a curtain of silver falling on one street corner while the sun blazed everywhere else. Each time, the same meaning pushed through, patient as tide:

*Come.*

"I'm not coming anywhere," he muttered into his ale at the Drowned Lantern, where the charm-sellers and hedge-witches drank and pretended not to look at each other. Old Maret, whose charms had actually worked once upon a time, raised an eyebrow at him from two stools down.

"Talking to yourself, Galloway?"

"Arguing with myself. It's the only conversation worth having these days."

Maret grunted and turned back to his cup, but his eyes stayed thoughtful. Everyone in that room was feeling it, Jonah realized. The pull. The old hunger for something to listen. They'd spent twelve years pretending the silence was natural, that magic dying was like iron rusting, just the way of things — and now the sky had cracked open a finger's width and every one of them was peeking through it and hating how much they wanted it to open wider.

That was the trap, he thought. That was always the trap. The gods didn't need to compel anyone. They only needed to knock.

*Come,* she said again that night, and for the first time the meaning carried freight: an image, brief and searing, of stone stairs descending beneath the earth, of a doorway older than the city, of nine shadows arranged like seats around a table.

He knew that place. Every soldier of the Godswar knew it, though few had seen it. The Ninefold Court — the temple-complex the first Lord Protector had sealed beneath Ashfall three centuries ago, when the covenant between gods and mortals was signed in blood and law. The place where, everyone agreed, the gods had withdrawn from the world and fallen silent, all together, all at once, as if by agreement.

As if by *contract*.

Jonah sat bolt upright in the dark. The thought arrived fully formed, the way battlefield realizations arrive — no reasoning, just knowing, cold and total.

They hadn't left. They'd been *put away*. And whatever put them away was still down there, still holding the lock, and Veyra — bound, muffled, speaking to him in fragments through cracks in her own prison — wanted him to walk straight into it.

"No," he said again.

But he was already reaching for his boots.

* * *

He lasted four more days. He was proud of four days.

On the fourth day, Captain Isolde Vane came to Candler's Row with six witch-finders and a warrant, and swept every licensed vendor stall from the Saltgate to the Ropewalk, and Jonah watched her work from across the street with the flat calm of a man watching a flood approach his house. She was younger than him by ten years, sharp-faced, gray-eyed, with the terrible patience of someone who believed completely in the rightness of her ledger. She questioned a hedge-charmer named Olla for twenty minutes over a luck-knot that had, witnesses claimed, actually untangled itself. When she was done, Olla was crying and the knot was confiscated in a lead box, and Isolde wrote something in her book that would probably end Olla's life within the month.

That evening, the voice came stronger than it had ever been, and this time there was urgency braided through the meaning like red thread.

*They are counting the faithful. Come, Jonah. Before they finish counting.*

He stood in his shop among his dead wares and understood, with the clarity that only ever came too late, that the fragments weren't weakness. They were rationing. She was spending herself to reach him, coin by coin, through whatever held her down there, and each fragment cost her something he didn't dare imagine.

"You owe me," he said quietly. It wasn't a question or a plea. It was arithmetic. "Forty-one lives at Redford's Crossing. My arm. My faith. Twelve years of selling lies to people who deserved better. You owe me, Veyra. Whatever this is — whatever you're asking — understand that I'm not coming because I forgive you. I'm coming to collect."

The pressure that answered was not contrite. It was, absurdly, almost warm — a grim approval, the feeling of a commander hearing a soldier volunteer for a suicide detail and being sorry and glad at once.

*Yes,* she said. *Collect.*

* * *

He closed early. He took down the shutter, banked the stove, and packed the way he used to pack for contracts: blade, whetstone, dried meat, the good rope, and — after a long moment, with a laugh that hurt his throat — the old Vigil badge he'd kept sewn into the lining of his coat for twelve years like a tumor he couldn't bring himself to cut out. Cold iron, shaped like an open eye with a bar across it. The sigil of the god who watches and does not speak.

The entrance to the under-Court was in the Sump, the drowned quarter where the city's oldest foundations sank into marsh and memory. There was a stair behind the collapsed shell of the Temple of Records, a stair the Crown had bricked over three times and pilgrims had unbricked three times, until finally both sides pretended to forget it existed. Jonah had passed it a hundred times in twelve years and never once stopped. Tonight he stopped.

The brickwork had been broken open from the *inside*. Fresh rubble, dust still hanging in the air, and beyond it a breath of air rising from below that smelled of incense and old blood and something vast turning over in its sleep.

He stood at the top of those stairs for a long time. Long enough that the lamplighter's cart rattled past behind him. Long enough that the fear had ample opportunity to do its work, and did.

Twelve years ago he had followed this god into a war because her silence seemed like trust — because she asked nothing and therefore, he'd believed, demanded nothing. He'd learned what her silence really was at a river crossing in the rain, surrounded by the bodies of everyone who'd believed him.

Now she was silent again, but differently. Strained. Rationing herself. Reaching up through her own prison with bleeding hands, saying the only word she had left.

*Come.*

"Fine," Jonah Galloway said to the dark mouth of the stair, to the god below it, to forty-one ghosts and one ruined arm and twelve years of dead charms. "But I'm keeping score."

He lit his lantern and went down.

The stairs were older than the city above them, worn concave by centuries of feet, and they descended farther than any stair had a right to. Somewhere past the third hundred step, the walls changed — rough foundation-stone giving way to dressed granite, granite to marble, marble to something pale and veined that glowed faintly where his lantern touched it, like flesh remembering light. Carvings appeared. Nine figures, repeated over and over, seated around a table, and above the table a tenth figure — smaller, human, crowned — holding chains that ran from its fists to the throats of all nine.

Jonah stopped walking.

He looked at the carving for a long, cold moment. At the crowned figure. At the chains.

"Well," he said softly, to no one, to himself, to whatever was listening. "That doesn't match the official histories."

The pressure in his scarred collarbone pulsed — agreement, warning, grief, all tangled together — and far below, at the bottom of the stairs, in a hall he could not yet see, something enormous shifted its weight, and every lamp-flame in the passage leaned toward it like grass bending under wind.

And there, at the threshold of the light, where the stair opened onto a colonnade of impossible scale — someone was waiting. A figure in clerk's gray, standing with her hands folded, her face half-lit, familiar in the way a knife left in a wound is familiar.

Jonah's hand found his sword-hilt before his mind caught up with his eyes.

"Sera," he said.

The woman who had betrayed him smiled the way she'd smiled across a war-camp fire a lifetime ago, tired and brave and already apologizing for what she was about to say.

"Hello, Jonah," said Sera Ilvane, chief covenant-clerk of the Court of Ashfall. "You're going to want to sit down."
