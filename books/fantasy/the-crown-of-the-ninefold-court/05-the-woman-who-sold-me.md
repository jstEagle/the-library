## 5. The Woman Who Sold Me

The temple beneath the city had no door that I could find.

I had walked the drowned streets of Old Ashfall for three nights before I understood that was the point. The Ninefold Court's ruin did not want to be found by feet; it wanted to be found by faith, and mine had been in the grave for twelve years. So on the third night I stopped looking with my eyes and did the thing I swore I would never do again. I knelt in the ankle-deep water of a flooded cistern street, rain hammering the vaulted stone above me like applause from a hostile crowd, and I prayed.

Not words. Words were for priests and liars. Just the old shape of it — the way you'd hollow yourself out and hold the emptiness up like a cup. *Veyra. If you're there. If any of this is real.*

The water around my knees went warm as blood.

And then the wall ahead of me — a wall of black basalt furred with white mineral bloom, featureless as the face of a drowned man — opened. Not swung, not split. It simply stopped being a wall, the way sleep stops being sleep, and beyond it was a stairway going down into light that had no source.

I stood there a long time, letting the rain run off my chin, and thought about every reason not to go down those stairs.

Then I went down them anyway. That has always been my problem.

* * *

The stair was older than the city above it. I could feel that in my teeth, the way you can feel a storm coming. Each step was cut from a single piece of pale stone, and each step bore a worn inscription so old the letters had gone soft, though my fingers knew them before my mind caught up. Oath-script. The formal covenant-hand they'd taught us in the war-camps when the chaplains still believed the gods were listening. *By consent given freely, the bond is made. By consent withdrawn freely, the bond is broken.* Someone had carved that at the top of the stair, someone had carved it at the bottom, someone had carved it into the bones of the world once upon a time, and everyone since had spent their lives pretending it wasn't true.

At the bottom, the stair opened into an antechamber the size of a granary hall, and there was light — real light, lamplight, guttering yellow behind smoked glass lanterns — and there were tables, scrolls, ledgers stacked in careful columns, inkpots and wax-seals and the whole patient apparatus of clerical work. And standing at one of those tables with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a stylus tucked behind her ear like she'd never left her desk even for a moment, waiting for me with the particular patience of a person who has known exactly what time I would arrive for days —

Sera Ilvane.

I should have been surprised. I wasn't. That was the worst part. Some animal part of me had known, since the candle relit itself over a child's prayer, since the pressure against the locked door of my bond, that if I followed the thread far enough down it would end with her. Everything in my life that mattered had ended with Sera Ilvane, one way or another.

She hadn't changed. That was its own obscenity. Twelve years, and the war had carved lines into every face I knew except hers. Still the sharp jaw, the dark hair bound back in that clerk's knot, the ink stain on the side of her right hand where the stylus rested. She wore grey — the Court's grey, the color of people who file the world instead of living in it — but she wore it loosely, like a coat borrowed from someone bigger.

"Jonah." My name in her mouth after twelve years. It sounded like a key turning in a lock.

"Sera." I stayed where I was, near the foot of the stair, water dripping off my hem onto stone that drank it without a sound. "You sent for me."

"I asked Veyra to send for you." She set down her stylus. "There's a difference. I don't command her. No one commands her anymore. That's rather the whole of it."

"The whole of what?" I looked past her, at the ledgers, the seals, the neat little empire of paperwork in a temple that was supposed to have been dead and ruined for a century. "Because from up here it looks like the Court's chief covenant-clerk has been keeping house in the gods' tomb."

"Someone had to keep the accounts." Her voice didn't waver. It never had. It was one of the things I had loved about her, once — that voice, level as a mason's line, while the world burned around it. "Twelve years of accounts. Every prayer answered, every miracle suppressed, every charm that failed because we made it fail. It's all written down. All of it, Jonah. Every entry. In my hand."

"Why?" It came out rougher than I meant it to. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the accounts are coming due." She stepped out from behind the table and I saw then that she was thinner than I'd thought, that the grey coat hid a frame gone fragile as winter reeds, that her hands — her steady, clever hands — had a tremor in them she was holding still through sheer discipline. "And you're the last oath-bearer alive who isn't either dead, mad, or wearing a Crown collar. When they come to collect, they'll come to you. I needed you to hear it from me first."

"Hear what from you?"

She looked at me for a long moment. And I watched something move behind her eyes — I watched her decide, deliberately, to stop being a clerk. To let the woman I used to know stand up inside the uniform and speak.

"That I sold you," she said quietly. "All of us. The whole world. And I'd do it again. And I need you to understand why before the eight of them make you their pen."

* * *

She talked. I listened. That was always our arrangement, in the war-tents, in the narrow rented rooms in Kellin's Ford where the shelling shook dust from the rafters and we lay awake counting the intervals between impacts. She talked; I listened; somewhere in the listening I fell in love with her, which was the most dangerous thing I ever did in four years of soldiering.

She told it like a ledger entry. Of course she did.

"In the last year of the Godswar, the gods were feeding directly," she said. "You remember. You were there. You carried their marks on your arms."

I looked down at my forearms as though I could see them through my sleeves. The oath-scars. Eight of them, one per god who'd claimed me during the fighting, thin pale lines like old burns, each one a leash I hadn't chosen and couldn't remove. "I remember," I said. "I remember what it cost."

"A thousand souls a day. More, at the end. Not soldiers — anyone. Devotion was supposed to be freely given; the covenant said so; the covenant had said so for two thousand years. But the gods were starving, the war had burned through the willing faithful, and hungry divinity does not check its receipts. They started reaching. Pulling devotion out of people who'd never sworn a thing. A farmer in the Marches would wake up hollowed out and wouldn't know why, wouldn't know anything, just sit there smiling at nothing while his life drained toward some throne under the mountain." Her voice stayed level. Her knuckles went white around the edge of the table. "You saw the hollow ones too. Everyone called it plague."

"I called it plague," I admitted. "The chaplains called it plague."

"The chaplains knew. The high clergy all knew. They just couldn't stop it, because stopping it meant confronting nine gods, and confronting gods is how the war started in the first place." She pushed away from the table and began to walk, slowly, along the row of sealed lanterns, and the light moved across her face in bars. "So a group of us — clerks, mostly. Archivists. People who understood the covenant better than the priests did. We went looking for the original text. The first contract, signed between the first Lord Protector and the Nine. And we found it. And we found the clause."

"What clause?"

"There's always a clause, Jonah. Contracts are cages built by lawyers, and lawyers build doors for themselves." She stopped walking. She didn't turn around. "Clause eleven. *In the event that mortal devotion is taken rather than given — in breach of the covenant's foundation — the covenant may be suspended in its entirety, by act of the Crown's lawful authority, until such time as free consent can be restored.* Suspended. Not ended. Frozen. The gods would sleep. Their hunger would sleep with them. And the Court would hold the key."

"And you convinced them to use it."

"We didn't convince anyone." Now she turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely unrepentant, all three at once. "We forged the authority. We altered the succession records. We committed treason in triplicate, with proper margins and countersignatures, because the Lord Protector of the day was too much of a coward to sign it himself and ten thousand people a week were dying. And when it was done — when the silence came down over the world like snowfall — the war ended in six weeks. Six weeks, Jonah. Four years of slaughter, and it took the gods' silence six weeks to starve it out."

The antechamber was very quiet. Somewhere above us, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, Ashfall went about its evening: cart wheels, a drunk singing, the ordinary music of a city that didn't know it lived on top of a paused apocalypse.

"You could have told people," I said finally. "After. You could have—"

"Told them what? That the gods they mourned had been eating them alive? That the peace they were building their children's lives on rested on a forged signature?" She laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "The Court hanged people for less than the truth back then. It hanged people for less than the truth last month. I've kept this room secret for twelve years. Do you know what that costs? Do you know what it's like to sit in an office in the Covenant Ministry, stamping licenses for charms that don't work, knowing exactly why they don't work, writing the reports that keep the witch-finders pointed at harmless hedge-witches so they never dig deep enough to find—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "No. You don't know what that's like. Because you got to be angry. Anger is simple. I never got to be angry. I only got to be responsible."

"And me?" The question had been sitting in my chest since I saw her, growing heavier by the minute, and now it came out. "Where do I fit in your accounts, Sera? Because I've done the arithmetic myself, these last twelve years, and here's what I get: Veyra goes silent in the middle of the battle at Redford Bridge. Mid-battle. Mid-*prayer*. My company — thirty-one people who trusted me to keep them tethered to something — thirty-one people die in ninety seconds because the god whose oath I carried decided to stop answering. And the clerk who brokered the silence, the clerk I loved, the clerk I told everything — including exactly which oaths I carried, including exactly which god held my leash — vanishes the same week and resurfaces in the Court's employ." I took a breath. My hands were shaking. I let them. "They needed oath-bearers severed cleanly. And you handed them the list."

For the first time since I'd climbed down those stairs, Sera Ilvane flinched.

It was small. Anyone else would have missed it. But I had spent four years learning to read her face across burning rooms, and I saw it land — saw the truth of it hit her somewhere below the ribs — and I hated how much it satisfied me.

"Yes," she said.

"Just yes?"

"Yes. They needed the bonds cut, and a severed oath-bearer bleeds power for months afterward — power the suspension needed to take hold. Your company wasn't the cost of doing business, Jonah. It was the payment. I chose which names to give them, and I chose yours, and I sat in a safehouse in Kellin's Ford the night it happened and listened to the reports come in and I did not look away from a single number." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, and she mastered it. "Thirty-one. I've read the casualty ledger four hundred times. I can recite it. Aldous Wren. Maren Cotch. The twins, Pell and Perrin Osei. Little Tam Broadfoot, who was seventeen and lied about his age to enlist because he wanted to carry a god's mark like his father—"

"Stop."

"—and every night for twelve years I have added it up and gotten the same answer, which is that the alternative was the war running another decade and a hundred thousand more names, and every night for twelve years the answer doesn't help, it has never once helped—"

"*Stop.*" My voice came out with something underneath it, something that rattled the lantern glass, and we both froze, because neither of us had heard that sound come out of a human throat in twelve years. The oath-scars on my forearms had gone hot. Not painfully. Warm. Like a hand laid gently on each arm, steadying.

Sera stared at my sleeves. "She's close," she whispered. "She's closer than she's allowed to be. Jonah, listen to me — whatever you feel rising in you right now, that's not just anger. The suspension is failing. It's been failing for a year, and the last month it's failing fast. The gods aren't waking up angry. They're waking up *hungry*, and the covenant that used to govern their hunger is a frozen contract with a forged seal, and when it thaws—"

"When it thaws, what?"

She met my eyes. Twelve years dropped away, just like that, and she was the woman in the war-tent again telling me the odds before a battle, giving me the numbers straight because she respected me enough to let me decide with open eyes.

"When it thaws," she said, "the clause expires, and the covenant reverts to its original terms. Freely-given devotion — or taken devotion, if none is given. And Jonah, nobody in Ashfall gives anything freely anymore. Nobody remembers how. We raised two generations on the lie that the gods don't exist, and now eight of them are stirring under this city, and they are going to come up through the cracks looking for what they're owed, and the only person alive they might listen to is the man standing in front of me bleeding warmth from both arms."

I looked down at my forearms. The scars were glowing faintly through the fabric now, pale gold, like coals under ash.

"And you?" I asked. "When they come collecting — what did you put up as collateral, Sera? Because I know you. There's a ledger somewhere down here with your name in the margin. There always is."

She didn't answer. Which was an answer.

Above us, far above, through stone and clay and the roots of a city built on its own ruins, a bell began to ring — the Temple Quarter's midnight bell, or maybe not a bell at all but something imitating one, a sound with too much meaning in it. And in the antechamber's doorway, the one leading deeper, toward the thrones she hadn't shown me yet, the air began to shimmer like heat over summer cobblestones.

Eight somethings were turning over in their sleep down there.

One of them was watching me.

"They know you've arrived," Sera said softly. She picked up a lantern, and her hand had stopped trembling — resolve does that, I remembered, resolve and terror look identical from the outside. "Come and see what we woke up. And Jonah—" she paused at the threshold, not looking back, "—when you meet them, whatever they offer you, whatever they show you, remember that I'm the one who taught them the shape of the cage they've been sleeping in. Remember whose handwriting is on every page. Because they certainly haven't forgotten."

She lifted the lantern and walked into the dark, and after a moment — because it has always been my problem — I followed.
