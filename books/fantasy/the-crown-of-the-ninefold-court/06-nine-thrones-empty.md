## 6. Nine Thrones, Empty

The stair went down farther than Sera had ever let herself believe it would.

She had walked this descent a hundred times in twelve years — alone, with a lantern, with the ledgers of her office strapped to her back like penance — and every time the stairs felt longer, as though the dark beneath Ashfall were slowly learning to stretch itself. Tonight she counted steps out of old habit and lost count at four hundred, somewhere past the third landing where the brick changed color, older brick, pre-Court brick, mortared with something that glittered faintly when her lantern swung across it.

Jonah climbed down behind her without speaking. She could hear him breathing, hear the creak of his boots on stone worn hollow by other feet, other centuries. He had not spoken since the temple door above, since she had said his name and watched twelve years of grief and rage move through his face like weather.

He was carrying a candle stub in his pocket. She had seen him take it from the stall before he followed her — some instinct, some habit of a man who sold fire to people who had none. She wondered if he knew what it meant that he'd brought light into this place, or whether he only knew what it meant to him.

"Isolde Vane," Jonah said at last, his voice rough against the silence. "You knew she'd come for me."

"I knew she'd find you eventually. The Office keeps lists." Sera did not turn around. "I hoped I'd reach you first."

"Hoped." He said the word like he was testing it for rot. "Twelve years, Sera."

"I know."

"You wrote me a letter. One letter. *Forgive me, I did what I must.* And then you vanished into the Court like you'd never—"

"I know what I did." Her voice came out harder than she intended, echoing off the stairwell walls, coming back to her doubled. "I've known every day since. That's rather the point of what I'm about to show you."

The stairs ended.

* * *

Sera lifted her lantern high and stepped aside so he could see it all at once, because there was no gentler way — no way to tell a man about a cathedral except to let the cathedral hit him whole.

The Ninefold Court opened beneath Ashfall like the inverted image of a mountain: vaulted ceilings lost in darkness above, pillars thick as siege towers marching away into shadow, and at the center, on a raised dais of white stone veined with gold, nine thrones.

Eight were occupied.

Not by bodies — not exactly. By presence. Shapes of gathered light and pressure, half-formed, like figures seen through smoke or deep water. The nearest stirred as they entered, a slow turning, vast and unhurried, and Sera felt the air pressure change the way it does before a storm front crosses a valley. A voice that was not a voice moved through her bones, tasting them.

"They wake," she said quietly. "They've been waking for three years. Slowly. Like men surfacing from drowning."

Jonah had gone very still beside her. His hand had drifted to his belt, to the place where a sword would have hung twelve years ago, and found nothing, and she saw the moment he remembered he was only a charm-seller now, an old soldier with dead stock and a dead god.

"That one," he said. His eyes had found the ninth throne.

It sat at the center of the arc, slightly forward, slightly lower than the others — a throne built not for supremacy but for approachability, its arms worn smooth, its seat cushioned once in grey wool long since rotted to threads. And it was empty in a way the others were not. The eight thrones held their stirring gods the way a cup holds water. The ninth held nothing at all, and the nothing had weight. It pulled at the eye. It pulled at Sera's chest in a way she had never admitted to anyone.

"Veyra," Jonah said.

"Still bound," Sera said. "Fully bound. Whatever's happening to the others — whatever is pulling them up out of the silence — it isn't reaching her. Or she isn't letting it."

"She wouldn't." Something moved across his face — tenderness and fury braided so tight she couldn't separate them. "She never took anything she wasn't given. Even at the end. Even when—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Why are they waking, Sera? Twelve years of nothing. Priests starving, charms going dead, my whole trade a fraud I didn't know I was running. And now candles relight themselves in my stall and prayers get answered in the Ropewalk and witch-finders kick down doors. What changed?"

Sera set her lantern on the dais step. Then she reached into her coat and drew out the ledger.

It was not one of the Court's modern volumes. This book was older than the Office of Purity, older than the current Protectorate, bound in hide that had been cured with ash and salt and something darker. Its clasp bore the seal of the first Lord Protector — the nine-pointed crown, each point pierced by a nail.

"Do you remember the Godswar?" she asked.

"I fought in it."

"Then you remember what it was. Not a war between gods. A war over mortals. Nine divinities competing for worship the way lords compete for grain — blessing their faithful, cursing each other's, burning whole districts because a street shrine flew the wrong colors." She unlocked the clasp. Her hands were steady; she had practiced being steady with this book until steadiness stopped being an act. "The war ended in a night. You were there. You told me once — you said the gods simply went quiet between one battle and the next. Your company knelt to Veyra at dusk and she answered, and by dawn she was gone, and every priest in the theater of war woke to silence."

"I remember." His voice had gone flat. "I've spent twelve years remembering."

"Then let me show you why."

She opened the ledger to the page marked by a ribbon of grey silk, and turned the book toward the dais, toward the light of eight waking gods and one small lantern.

"The Covenant of Ashfall," she read aloud, because some things needed a human voice, needed breath behind them. "*Let the Nine withdraw their hands from the world of men. Let prayer go unanswered and charm lie fallow. In exchange, let the Court hold the worship of Ashfall in trust — hoarded, banked, accruing — until such time as the Crown sees fit to spend it.*"

Jonah stared at the page. "That's not divine script."

"No."

"It's contract law. It's—" He looked up at her, and she watched the understanding arrive in him the way frost arrives across a window, line by line, inevitable. "Mortal handwriting."

"Signed," Sera said, "by Aldric Vane, first Lord Protector of Ashfall. In his own blood. Here." She touched the signature — brown-black now, ancient, but unmistakably blood, and unmistakably mortal. "And countersigned by the nine. Their marks aren't signatures. They're wounds. Each god pressed their essence into the page to seal it. Look at the ninth."

He leaned close. She watched his lips form the name without sound.

*Veyra.*

Hers was the smallest mark, and the cleanest, and — Sera had studied this for years, had dreamed about it — hers was the only one that looked less like a wound and more like a scar. Old. Deliberate. Made by someone who understood exactly what she was cutting and chose to cut anyway.

"The Court didn't survive the Godswar," Jonah said slowly. "The Court *ended* it. They bought the gods' silence."

"With the only currency gods value." Sera closed the ledger. "Worship. Devotion. The accumulated faith of two million souls. Every prayer whispered in Ashfall for the last century and a half — it hasn't risen. It hasn't dissipated. It's been *collected*. Stored in the vaults beneath the Protector's Tower like grain against a famine that will never come. The Crown outlawed magic because magic unlicensed competes with the harvest. The witch-finders don't hunt sorcery, Jonah. They hunt *leakage*."

"Leakage."

"People who still pray too hard. Hedge-priests whose little rites draw power the ledgers say belongs to the Crown. Children whose wishes are strong enough to dent the account books." She laughed, and hated the sound of it, thin and bitter under the vaulted dark. "I'm the chief covenant-clerk of Ashfall. Do you know what my office actually does? We audit souls. We track devotion the way customs officers track cargo. When a grandmother lights a candle for her grandson's fever and means it with her whole heart, some clerk like me records the transaction and routes it to the Tower. That candle has burned in the dark for a hundred and fifty years, Jonah. All of them have."

The silence after her voice was enormous.

One of the waking gods moved — the far one, the one shaped like a column of slow gold fire, the one the oldest texts called Aurel of the Harvesting Hand. Its attention swept across the dais like a lighthouse beam and paused on Jonah, and Sera saw him sway, saw sweat spring cold at his hairline, saw his jaw lock against whatever pressure the god exerted on him.

"It knows you," she said softly.

"It can't. I'm nobody. I sell—"

"You're the last oath-bearer. You swore to Veyra in her own hearing, with her own power in your mouth, and the oath was never dissolved — because a covenant sealed by a god can't be dissolved by the god's absence. You're a loose thread in the Crown's accounting, Jonah Galloway. You have been for twelve years. And now the ledger is coming due." She wrapped her arms around herself. "The contract had a term clause. The first Lord Protector was careful — he always was — but even he couldn't write eternity into mortal ink. The binding holds while the Crown maintains the terms. And eighteen months ago, the current Lord Protector began drawing on the reserve."

"Drawing on it how?"

"He thinks he's going to become a god." She said it plainly, because there was no gentle version and she had promised herself, climbing those stairs, that she was done with gentle versions. "He's been siphoning the hoard into himself. Slowly. Carefully. Believing the silence would hold forever because it always had. But you cannot drink from a river and expect the river not to notice. The eight felt the theft. They began to wake. And a waking god who has been robbed for a century does not send ambassadors, Jonah. It sends hunger."

The gold-fire god turned again. Closer now. Sera heard it breathe, or heard the city above breathe through it — a vast tidal sound, the sound of something inhaling after a very long exhalation.

"Why tell me?" Jonah asked. His voice was low, controlled, the voice of a sergeant holding a line. "Twelve years of nothing from you, and tonight you lead me down here and hand me the end of the world. Why?"

Because I have carried it alone too long. Because you are the only person alive who loved both her and me and might understand why I chose. Because the eighth god has begun asking me questions in my sleep and I am afraid of the answers.

"Because someone has to decide what happens next," she said instead. "The Protector wants to finish drinking. The gods want their debt paid in flesh and faith. The Office wants everything nailed shut again, tighter. And the ninth throne—" She looked at it, at the beautiful aching emptiness of it. "The ninth throne is the only one that stayed honest, and it can't tell us why, because it's still silent. Still keeping the contract it signed. Even now. Even betrayed by it."

Jonah walked toward the empty throne.

She let him. She stood among the stirring shadows of eight waking gods with her dead ledger under her arm and watched the man she had ruined cross the dais of the conspiracy she had served, and kneel — actually kneel, one knee to the white stone, the way soldiers kneel, the way he must have kneeled a thousand times in a war she helped end by stealing the god he knelt to.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. The throne was empty. The silence was total.

But the candle stub came out of his pocket, and he lit it — struck flint to wick with hands that shook only a little — and set it on the dais before the ninth throne, a coin-sized flame in a cathedral of dark.

"For the company," he said quietly. "For the ones who prayed to you at dusk and got an answer, and knelt at dawn and got nothing. For me. I'm not forgiving you yet. But I'm listening. In case you ever can."

And Sera, watching, felt the smallest thing — so small she would doubt it later, would doubt it every day for the rest of her life.

The candle flame bent. Not from any draft. It bent toward the throne, held there a heartbeat, two, like a thing leaning close to a sleeping face —

— and straightened.

Above them, muffled through a hundred fathoms of stone and city, a bell began to ring. Then a second bell, wrong-toned, urgent. Then many.

Jonah was on his feet. "What is that?"

Sera already knew. She had read the schedule; she had audited the requisitions; she had signed, with her own clerk's hand, the supply orders that should have told her sooner. The Office of Purity had not waited for her to bring Jonah in. Isolde Vane's superiors had decided that if a covenant-clerk was visiting the Court beneath the city, then the Court beneath the city was due for inspection — and the Crown did not inspect. The Crown purged.

"Witch-finders," she said. "A full company. Sealing the temple doors above us." She met his eyes, and saw in them the same arithmetic she was already running: two people, nine gods, one exit, none of it good. "Jonah — they're not coming to arrest anyone. The order I countersigned yesterday, without reading the annex — the annex authorizes *fumigation*. They mean to collapse the approaches. Bury the Court. Bury the waking gods under ten thousand tons of rock and call it pest control."

"And us with them."

"And us with them."

Far down the black throat of the stairway, faint as a dying star, torchlight appeared — and began, steadily, to descend.
