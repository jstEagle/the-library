## 7. A God's Hunger

The summons came at dusk, and it did not knock.

Jonah was closing the shop — counting out the day's coppers with hands that had learned to count money before they'd learned to hold a sword, which was a lie he told himself every time — when the candle flames all leaned east at once. All of them. The tallow stubs on his counter, the votives in the window rack, the little devotional lights he sold three for a penny. They bent toward the harbor like grass under wind, though the evening air hung dead still.

Then the bells began.

Not the temple bells. Those had been silent twelve years, their ropes cut by order of the first Lord Protector, their bronze recast into cannon. These were older bells, deeper, rung from somewhere beneath the city — from the drowned quarter, maybe, or from farther down than any map admitted. Each toll passed through the floorboards and up through the soles of Jonah's boots and settled in his sternum like a fist closing around his heart.

He knew this feeling. He had felt it once before, on a ridge above the Vellmarsh, the night the Godswar ended — the night everything went quiet.

*Come,* said something vast and patient, in no language at all. *Oath-bearer.*

"I'm not," Jonah said aloud, to an empty shop. His voice cracked on the second word. "I'm not your oath-bearer. I'm a charm-seller. I sell dead things to desperate people."

The bells tolled again, and the candles stood back upright, and the pressure in his chest did not release. It simply waited, the way a creditor waits.

He thought about not going. He genuinely considered it — sat down on his stool behind the counter, wiped his palms on his apron, and gave it honest consideration. He was fifty-one years old. He had bad knees, a bad shoulder, and a talent for staying unnoticed that had kept him alive through a war and a purge and twelve years of peacetime poverty. Men who answered summons from gods did not, as a rule, die peacefully in bed.

But the pressure didn't fade. It grew roots. By full dark it had wrapped around his ribs like a bandage pulled too tight, and every breath reminded him: *you swore, you swore, you swore.* Twelve years ago he had sworn service to Veyra the Quiet, goddess of small mercies, and oaths made to gods did not dissolve when the gods went silent. They went dormant. Like seeds. Like debts accruing interest.

And now the creditors were awake.

Jonah blew out the candles — all but one, out of habit, out of some superstition he couldn't name — locked the shop, and walked down into the earth of Ashfall City to meet his god's creditors.

* * *

The way down was open.

That was the first horror. The Ninefold Court's ruined temple had been sealed since the Purges — bricked over, warded, forgotten beneath the Ropewalk district where the poor built their shanties on top of holy ground because holy ground was cheap. Jonah had found its entrance once, years ago, following Sera's old maps, and spent two hours chipping at mortar before giving up.

Tonight the bricks lay scattered across the flooded street like a child's abandoned blocks. Not broken. *Arranged.* Each one set neatly against the next, forming a corridor of walls that led down into darkness, and the darkness breathed cold air that smelled of incense and old blood and rain on hot stone.

Other people were walking it.

They came out of doorways and alleys as Jonah descended — a fishwife still in her scales, a lamplighter with his pole over his shoulder, two children holding hands, an old soldier with a wooden leg who moved with a rolling gait Jonah recognized from his own company's drill yard. None of them spoke. None of them looked afraid. Their faces wore the same expression: slack, wondering, lit from within by whatever was calling them. Moths, Jonah thought. We are moths, and something has struck a match.

He grabbed the fishwife's arm as she passed. "Hey. Hey! Do you know where you're going?"

She turned her head. Her eyes were ordinary eyes, brown and bloodshot, but behind them something else looked out — something that regarded him with the mild curiosity of a cat regarding a beetle.

"He calls us," she said. "All of us. Everyone who ever whispered a prayer into a closed hand."

"Who calls you?"

"The ones who are owed." She smiled, and it was the smile of a woman remembering her wedding day, and Jonah let go of her arm because there was nothing in it to hold onto. She continued down the brick corridor, and the lamplighter followed, and the children followed, hand in hand, and Jonah stood alone in the cold breathing dark and understood that he could still turn around.

He could turn around. He could go home, bar his door, stuff wax in his ears, and wait for whatever came next.

Except the oath in his chest pulled forward like a hook set behind his breastbone, and except — he admitted it, finally, standing in that corridor with his heart hammering — except that some traitorous part of him had been waiting twelve years for exactly this. For the silence to end. For someone, *anything*, to say his name again with meaning attached.

He went down.

* * *

The Ninefold Court had been beautiful once. He could see its bones beneath the ruin: nine tiers of white stone descending in a spiral, each tier carved with the likeness of a god, each god's face defaced by chisel and fire during the Purges. Water dripped somewhere in the blackness. The great doors at the bottom — bronze, taller than three men — hung open for the first time in living memory, and light poured out of them that was not torchlight and not daylight but something older, gold shot through with green, the color of the hour before dawn in high summer.

The crowd gathered on the lowest tier, hundreds strong now, pressing shoulder to shoulder before the doors. Jonah worked his way along the edge, keeping to shadow out of habit, until he reached the base of the ninth pillar — the one carved with Veyra's image, or what remained of it. Her statue had fared worst of all. The others had been merely mutilated; hers had been *unmade*, hammered down to a featureless stump, as if whoever held the chisel had wanted to erase even the memory of her shape.

He put his palm flat against the cold stone. Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. Veyra was still bound — Sera had told him that much in the temple doorway, in the conversation that had begun with *hello, Jonah* after twelve years and ended with the revelation that the silence of the gods had been purchased, signed, sealed in mortal blood by the man who founded the Crown. One god still bound. Eight waking.

The doors drank the crowd inward.

Jonah went with them, because the alternative was to be the one man in Ashfall who refused, and he had learned long ago what happened to men who stood apart from crowds moving toward power.

* * *

Inside, the Court was not ruined at all.

The crowd spilled onto a floor of polished obsidian that reflected eight thrones arranged in a crescent, and the thrones were *occupied*. That was the only word for it, though occupied was too small — each throne held a presence the size of the throne itself and the size of a mountain range simultaneously, forms that would not settle into a single shape. When Jonah tried to look directly at the nearest, he saw a crowned figure in robes of ledger-leather; when he blinked, he saw a storm wearing a crown; when he blinked again, he saw only a column of light and felt his teeth ache.

Eight gods. Eight presences that had gone silent for twelve years while mortals forgot them, mocked them, outlawed them, and now —

Now they were hungry. You could feel it the way you feel weather coming. The air itself tasted of appetite.

The crowd knelt. It wasn't commanded; it simply happened, wave upon wave, bodies folding like wheat. Jonah stayed on his feet for three heartbeats out of pure stubbornness, then the pressure of the oath behind his breastbone turned him like a key turns a lock, and he went down on one knee among strangers, jaw clenched, hating it.

A voice filled the chamber. It did not come from any single throne; it came from all of them at once, layered, harmonics stacked on harmonics until the words arrived less as sound than as certainty pressed directly into the mind.

**"OATH-BEARER."**

Every head in the crowd turned. Not the people — the *gods* turned them, gently, like a shepherd turning sheep, so that hundreds of faces fixed on Jonah kneeling alone at the crescent's center. Sweat ran cold down his spine.

"We see you, Jonah Galloway," said a second voice, lower, rougher, with the sound of grinding stone in it. "Last of those who swore. Last thread of the covenant that bound us to these people and these people to us."

"There were thousands of us," Jonah said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "Sworn servants. Priests. Soldiers. Where — " He stopped, because he knew where. The Purges. The witch-finders. Twelve years of ash.

"They burned," the grinding voice agreed, without grief. "Their oaths died with them. Yours endured, because you hid it, because you fed it scraps, because you could not bring yourself to break it. Sentiment." A pause, and something in the pause that might have been approval. "Useful sentiment."

Jonah got his feet under him. Standing felt important. "If you've called me here to collect, I've got nothing. My company's dead. My god's asleep. I sell charms that don't work."

"They work," said a third voice — silken, amused, female or close enough. From the third throne, a shape like smoke braided with moonlight leaned forward. "Poorly. Faintly. The way a lamp works after the oil is gone — a whisper of residue, a trick of faith. But they worked once, oath-bearer, and you know how they worked. You know the *mechanism*."

The covenant. The old architecture of exchange: devotion given freely upward, power flowing freely downward, prayer and answer locked together in mutual obligation. He'd sworn himself into it at nineteen, kneeling in a marsh shrine with mud on his knees, and it had been the proudest moment of his life.

"It's broken," Jonah said. "Shattered. Your silence was bought and paid for — I know. I've seen the contract."

"You have seen its *shadow*," said the grinding voice. "The mortal signatories believed they purchased our silence entire. They were mistaken. A covenant cannot be destroyed, Galloway. Only suspended. And suspension ends."

The light in the chamber deepened. The eight presences swelled, and for one naked instant Jonah saw them as they truly were — not shapes but *needs*, vast and starving, eight appetites that had gone twelve years unfed. Mortal worship was not flattery to a god. It was food. It was breath. The Court had starved them, and starving things do not stay patient forever.

"We wake," the voices said together, "and we find our tables empty. Our names unspoken. Our altars plowed under. We find mortals who pray into closed hands and expect nothing, because they were taught to expect nothing." The harmony fractured; each god spoke alone now, overlapping, a chorus of hunger. "We are owed, oath-bearer. Twelve years of owed devotion. Twelve years of withheld sacrifice. Twelve years of prayers that rose unanswered and curdled into despair. The debt is astronomical, and debts to gods are paid in the only coin mortals carry."

Jonah's mouth had gone dry. "Which is?"

"*Souls.*"

The word dropped into the chamber and the crowd murmured — not in fear, he realized with a fresh wave of horror, but in *wonder*. They heard *souls* and thought of heaven. They didn't understand yet. He did. He'd fought beside gods in the war. He'd seen what divine attention did to a battlefield, what happened to villages caught between rival pantheons collecting on old bargains. Worship taken by force wasn't worship. It was harvest. It was a god reaching into ten thousand minds at once and drinking, and what remained afterward was a smiling, hollowed thing that sang hymns in the streets and could not remember its mother's name.

"You can't," Jonah said. He was on his feet now, actually walking toward the thrones, which was either courage or madness or just the old mercenary reflex — move toward the threat, get inside its reach. "You burn this city's souls to feed yourselves and you'll get ash, not devotion. Fear isn't worship. Compulsion isn't faith. Your own covenant says so — it always has. That's why it was written with *consent* at its heart. Freely given, freely received. You'd be breaking your own law."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down like deep water.

Then the silken voice laughed, low and delighted. "Oh, he remembers. Twelve years selling trinkets and he *remembers the terms*." The smoke-shape drifted closer, and Jonah forced himself not to step back. "You are correct, little oath-bearer. We cannot compel. The architecture forbids it — the same architecture your mortal lords used to cage us. So we require a hand. A mortal hand, sworn and authorized, to carry the new terms to the people. To *offer* restoration. To make it lawful."

"And if the people refuse?"

"Then consent becomes irrelevant," said the grinding voice, with terrible patience, "and we take what the old law permits a creditor to take from defaulters. It will not be gentle. It will not be quick. Cities have been emptied before, Galloway. We remember how."

The crowd sighed, rapturous, oblivious. Jonah looked out at them — the fishwife, the lamplighter, the children — and saw exactly what the gods intended. Not these. These were the willing. The unwilling would learn obedience the hard way, or stop being people at all.

"One moon's turn," the voices said, harmonized again, final as a headsman's stroke. "You will stand at the covenant-table and speak the restoration. You will gather the mortal signatories and bind them to the old terms — renewed, expanded, *enforced*. In return, your god wakes whole, your oath is honored, and your name stands first in the new register of the faithful. Refuse, and we proceed without you, and every soul in Ashfall learns what it costs to default."

"And if I can't do it in a moon? If the city won't—"

"A moon," they repeated, and the light began to contract, the thrones dimming, the presences withdrawing like tide going out. "We have waited twelve years, oath-bearer. We will not wait thirteen."

The last thing Jonah saw before the vision collapsed was the ninth throne — empty, dust-covered, set apart from the other eight at the crescent's end. As the light failed, something flickered across its surface. Faint. Almost nothing.

A single line of text, glowing briefly on the armrest, in handwriting he recognized with a jolt that went through him like a blade:

*I'm sorry. Find another way. — V.*

* * *

He came back to himself on the lowest tier of the ruined temple, on his knees in shallow floodwater, with dawn graying the air above and the crowd dispersing around him in dazed silence. The fishwife was weeping quietly and smiling. The children were gone — someone had carried them home, perhaps, or perhaps they'd simply walked off into the morning, still holding hands.

Jonah knelt in the water and shook, and let himself shake, because no one was watching and a man could only carry so much upright.

One moon. Twenty-eight days to do the impossible: restore a covenant that should never be restored, or watch eight starving gods eat his city one soul at a time. And Veyra — bound, silent, *still* silent even now — had spent what little strength she had left on four words scratched into an empty throne.

*Find another way.*

He climbed out of the flooded temple as the sun came up over Ashfall, gilding the rooftops of the Ropewalk, lighting the harbor, touching the towers of the Covenant Ministry where Sera Ilvane kept her ledgers and her guilt. Somewhere up there, witch-finders were already drafting new lists. Somewhere below, eight gods were counting down the days.

Jonah Galloway stood in the ruins of his faith's front porch, watched the city wake around him, and began — for the first time in twelve years — to plan a war.

He needed allies. He needed hedge-priests and charm-sellers, the forgotten small magicians the old covenant had protected, the ones who remembered how the machinery worked. And he needed the one person in Ashfall who understood the covenant's fine print better than the gods themselves.

Even if she was the woman who'd sold him.

He started walking toward the Ministry, and behind him, in the dark of the temple, the empty ninth throne seemed almost — almost — to sigh.
