## 8. Terms of Service

Isolde Vane had signed one hundred and fourteen warrants in her three years as Captain of the Office of Purity, and she had never once hesitated over the ink.

She thought about that now, standing at the window of the Purity House with her hands clasped behind her back, watching the dawn come up gray and sour over Ashfall's rooftops. Somewhere down in the Ropewalk, a woman named Marda Cull had prayed to a dead river-god for her drowning son's breath to return — and it had returned. Somewhere in the Tanner's Quarter, a hedge-charm that hadn't worked in twelve years had burned a fever out of a child overnight. And somewhere in the Warrens, a candle had relit itself above a charm-seller's stall while a hundred witnesses watched.

One hundred and fourteen warrants. Not one of them for anything like *this*.

"Captain." Her lieutenant, Bren, stood in the doorway with his helmet under his arm and his face carefully blank, which was how he looked when he was about to say something she wouldn't want written down. "The sweep reports from last night."

"Summarize them."

"Forty-one households flagged for unlicensed devotion. Nineteen charms seized. Three arrests." He paused. "And eleven reports of — manifestations. Answered prayers, real ones. Not residual charge, not trickery. Real."

"Real," Isolde repeated.

"The men are frightened, Captain." Bren said it plainly, because he was the only man in the Office who could. "They've spent their whole careers confiscating trinkets that don't work. Now the trinkets work. Half of them want to burn everything they find. The other half want to pray to it."

"And you?"

Bren looked at the floor. "I haven't decided yet, Captain."

Honesty. She'd promoted him twice for exactly this, and she hated him a little for offering it now. "Log your indecision in your private journal, Lieutenant. In the field you'll enforce the law until the law is changed or I tell you otherwise. That is all any of us can do."

"Yes, Captain."

When he was gone, she sat at her desk and pulled the file she'd been avoiding since midnight: the Galloway file.

Jonah Galloway. Charm-monger, licensed under a false second name, three years of quiet trade in the Warrens. And beneath that, older, thinner, nearly worn through by handling: Jonah Galloway, oath-bearer of Veyra the Quiet, sergeant of the Ninth Irregulars, veteran of the Godswar. The war records were sealed — most of them were — but Isolde had pulled them anyway, because the Office of Purity did not survive on ignorance, whatever its founders had believed. You could not hunt what you refused to understand.

An oath-bearer. A living mortal still knotted by covenant-thread to a bound god. If the gods were waking — if the silence was breaking the way the reports suggested — then Galloway wasn't a smuggler or a fraud. He was a door. And doors, in Isolde's experience, opened whether you wanted them to or not.

She had let him go two nights ago with a warning and a debt unspoken between them. Time to collect.

* * *

His shop smelled of beeswax, dust, and old smoke — the smoke of things burned long ago, soaked so deep into the timber that no scrubbing would lift it. Shelves lined the walls, crowded with charms in labeled drawers: traveler's knots, fever-strings, luck-coins stamped with the faces of gods the Crown had outlawed before Isolde was born. Most of them, for twelve years, had been exactly what they appeared to be: wood, thread, base metal, and hope.

Galloway was behind the counter when she came in, sorting stock by lamplight, and he didn't look surprised. He looked tired, the way soldiers look tired — not in the body but somewhere behind the eyes.

"Captain Vane." He set down a handful of fever-strings. "Come to arrest me, or come to buy? We're out of luck-coins. Everyone is, lately."

"Everyone believes again," Isolde said. "It's bad for business and worse for the city."

"You think so?"

"I think that when people believe the gods answer, they stop believing the law matters. I've seen it. Faith is just another word for permission, and permission is the first thing a mob grants itself." She crossed to the counter and laid the file on it, open to the page with his old rank and his old god. "Sergeant Galloway. Ninth Irregulars. Oath-bearer of Veyra."

Something moved behind his eyes — not fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing a name he'd taught himself never to say.

"That man died in the Godswar," he said quietly.

"No. His company died in the Godswar. He walked out. There's a difference, and I suspect it's the difference that keeps you up at night." She turned the file toward him. "Here's my offer, Galloway, and I'll make it once. The Office is sweeping the city. By week's end we'll have warrants out for every hedge-priest and charm-seller in Ashfall, and the tribunals won't ask questions, because the Protector's court has stopped asking questions. You know enough about covenants to be valuable. Tell me what's happening — why the silence is breaking, what wakes first, what it will take to put it back — and I'll see your name stays off every list that crosses my desk. Shielded. Protected. Free to keep selling your dead charms in peace."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then when the sweeps reach the Warrens, I follow orders. I'm told my following of them is quite reliable."

He studied her for a long moment. Lamplight caught the scar along his jaw and the gray coming in early at his temples. When he spoke, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man choosing each word like a footing on scree.

"You want to know what's happening. Fine. Here's what's happening, Captain. For twelve years, nothing answered. Nothing. I sold wood and thread to desperate people and slept fine, because at least the wood and thread didn't lie. Then, nine days ago, something started answering. And I need you to understand what that means, because I don't think anyone in your Office does." He leaned forward. "A god answering a prayer isn't a gift. It's an *invoice*. Every prayer the old covenant covered came with terms — terms written in blood, terms mortals agreed to when they had no better choice. The gods went silent, and everyone celebrated, and nobody asked what happened to the contract. Well, the contract hasn't died. It's been *starving*. And now it's waking up hungry."

"Hungry for what?"

"For what it was always owed." He said it without drama, which made it worse. "Worship. Devotion. Souls. Whatever currency the original terms specified — and I promise you, Captain, the first Lord Protector didn't negotiate those terms kindly, because men who bind gods don't start merciful."

"You're saying the Court did this. Bound them."

"I'm saying someone did. Whether it was the Court, or the Court's masters, or something wearing the Court's face — that I don't know. What I know is that the silence was bought, not given. And debts bought in blood don't lapse. They compound."

Isolde kept her face still, but inside, something shifted — a hairline crack running through masonry she'd believed load-bearing. Because here was the thing she had never said aloud in fifteen years of service: the Office of Purity was founded on the doctrine that outlawed magic was *foreign* magic, cult magic, the poison of enemies beyond the walls. But Galloway was describing something else entirely. Something homegrown. Something signed.

"If that were true," she said, "the archives would show it."

"If the archives showed it, Captain, do you think you'd have been allowed to read them?" He almost smiled. It wasn't a kind smile. "You came here to bargain for information. Ask yourself why you needed to bargain at all. Ask yourself who benefits from you not knowing."

* * *

The Ministry of Ordinances occupied four stories of white granite above the Processional Way, and its upper floors belonged to men whose names appeared on no public roster. Isolde climbed to the third story at the appointed hour, past clerks who did not look up, past doors without handles, to the chamber where Undersecretary Corvel received the officers he found useful.

Corvel was small, neat, and ageless, with the smoothed features of a man long since finished being any particular person. He poured tea himself, which was either courtesy or theater, and gestured her to the chair across from his desk.

"Captain Vane. The sweeps proceed well."

"They proceed. Whether well depends on the measure." She took the tea because refusing it was a statement, and statements in this room cost more than tea. "We've confiscated forty-one items in six days. We've also documented eleven verifiable manifestations. The phenomena are accelerating. I recommend—"

"You recommend nothing yet." Corvel smiled faintly. "You've been busy, Captain. Door-to-door. Thorough. And yet somehow you found time to visit a charm-shop in the Warrens twice in one week."

The tea tasted of bergamot and something underneath it, mineral and cold. Isolde set the cup down with care. "Galloway is a former oath-bearer. He's the single best-informed asset in this city regarding covenant mechanics. My approach of him was operational."

"Of course it was." Corvel folded his hands. "Let me save you the labor of further operations, Captain, since you've proven so diligent. The Office's mandate is changing. Effective immediately, the priority shifts from suppression to containment — and then, within the month, to restoration."

"Restoration," Isolde said.

"The binding of the Nine. The silence has served the realm admirably for twelve years — no plague-gods, no harvest-tithes, no temples taxing the granaries, no wars fought over whose thunder-lord is mightiest. But the mechanism is failing, and the Protector's council has determined that a partial binding invites catastrophe. A god half-wake is a god resentful. So we will not merely restore the old terms." Corvel's voice stayed pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a man reading a laundry list. "We will improve upon them. The new binding will route the covenant through a single anchor. One vessel. One will. The Crown itself."

For a moment the room seemed very far away. "You mean a person."

"I mean the succession. The Protector's line has carried the silence-key for a generation; it was always meant to carry more. Nine thrones, Captain, gathered into one seat. No more starving contracts, no more waking gods squabbling over worshipers. Divinity, administered. Centralized. *Governed.*" He sipped his own tea. "You may imagine it as the ultimate act of the Office of Purity. Not purity from magic, but purity of magic. All of it, in one lawful hand."

"And the oath-bearers? The hedge-priests? The people whose prayers are already being answered?"

"Interim measures apply. Detention where necessary. The tribunals are being expanded." He said it the way he might announce new office hours. "Which brings us to Galloway. He is not an asset, Captain. He is a vector. An oath-bearer is a thread tied to a waking power, and threads get pulled — from both ends. He will be taken before the month turns, and his bond will be examined at length, and what remains of him afterward is not your concern."

"What remains of him," Isolde repeated.

"These things are rarely gentle," Corvel agreed mildly. "But then, neither are gods. You of all people should appreciate that, Captain. Order requires hardness. That's why we chose you." He rose, signaling the audience over, and paused with his hand on the teapot. "One more matter. Your file access has been broadened. You'll find the full covenant histories in the subvault now — the true ones, unredacted. Consider it a reward for diligence. Or a test. We've never much distinguished between the two."

* * *

She read until the lamps burned low.

The subvault was cold, and the documents were older than she'd expected — older than the Protectorate, older than the Godswar, some of them written in a hand that predated the current alphabet and had been transliterated by scholars whose marginal notes disagreed with one another like drunkards. She read the First Concord, and the Tithing of Breath, and the Schedule of Oblations, and by the third hour her rigid, orderly, law-shaped mind had begun doing something it had never done in her career.

It had begun flinching.

Because the covenant was not a treaty. Treaties had clauses for exit, for grievance, for renegotiation. This had none. This was a ledger of extraction dressed in liturgical language: so many breath-prayers per household per season, so much of a dying man's last devotion claimed as tax, children's first spoken prayers entered as state property. Mortal consent was mentioned exactly once, in a clause so buried and so garbled by translation that Isolde read it four times before she understood what it said — and when she understood, she laughed, once, alone in the cold vault, a sound with no humor anywhere in it.

The clause required willing consent. And there was no evidence anywhere in nine centuries of archive that it had ever been freely given.

That was why they stole the silence instead. That was why the binding ran through blood and keys and vessels rather than signatures. You could not forge willingness. You could only take its place.

She thought of Galloway in his lamplit shop, saying *debts bought in blood don't lapse*, and heard it differently now. Not a smuggler's cynicism. A soldier's after-action report.

She thought of Corvel saying *divinity, administered*, and felt something in her chest turn over, slow and heavy, like a ship losing its mooring.

Fifteen years she had served the law because the law was the wall between Ashfall and the dark — because she had seen, as a girl in the harbor quarter, what faithless mobs did, what god-drunk prophets did, what the world became when everyone followed their own little light. The law was imperfect. The law was cruel at the edges. But it was *hers*, and it was *everyone's*, and that had always seemed worth the cruelty.

Now the law wanted to pour nine gods into one man's crown and call it governance. Now the law's own vaults admitted the foundation was theft. And now she sat in the cold holding a warrant draft for Jonah Galloway — detention, examination, indefinite — with her signature block empty at the bottom, and found that her hand would not move.

Not would not. *Could* not. As though the pen had finally learned what she'd spent fifteen years teaching it not to know.

Isolde set the warrant aside, squared the pages of the First Concord, and wrote instead, in her precise captain's hand, a single line in her private journal:

*If the law requires me to build a cage around heaven, the law and I have parted ways.*

Then she closed the book, doused the lamp, and stood a long time in the dark of the subvault, listening to the building breathe above her — and beneath her, far below, through stone and clay and the flooded bones of the old aqueducts, something vast and patient shifting in its sleep, like a hand pressing against the other side of a locked door.

By morning, the whole city would learn that Captain Vane had burned her copy of the Galloway warrant and gone alone, unarmed, down into the Warrens — to a charm-shop whose lights, witnesses would later swear, stayed burning all night, though no one remembered seeing the shopkeeper sleep.
