## 9. The Charm-Seller's War

The first name on my list was a woman I'd buried twice.

Maren Ostwick had run a hedge-shrine out of a tannery loft in the Ropewalk for as long as Ashfall had outlawed magic — which was to say, for as long as there had been an Ashfall worth the name. When the purges came the first time, eleven years back, she'd drowned in the harbor with three other small practitioners, or so the broadsheets said. When I'd gone looking for her two years after that, needing a warding-stitch for a client's roof, I'd found her alive under a new name, selling soap. She'd looked at me like I was a debt come due and told me never to speak of her again.

Now I stood in her doorway again, rain dripping off my coat, and she looked at me the same way. But this time she didn't close the door.

"They say prayers are being answered," she said. "Down by the fish-market. Old woman Kess prayed for her grandson's fever and it broke by morning. The witch-finders took her anyway." Her jaw worked. "Took her *for* it, if you can believe that. Answered prayer is evidence now."

"It was always evidence," I said. "It just used to be worthless."

She stepped aside and let me in.

Her loft smelled of lye and tallow and, underneath it, something I hadn't smelled in twelve years: burnt sage and iron filings, the working-smell of real craft. She still kept her kit. Of course she did. You don't spend thirty years learning to stitch wards into roof-beams and ever really stop being that person. You just go quiet, the way I'd gone quiet, the way the whole city had gone quiet, one practitioner at a time, until silence felt like peace instead of what it actually was.

A held breath.

"You're gathering them," Maren said. It wasn't a question. "The charm-sellers. The hedge-priests. Everyone the Office of Purity has been stepping on for a decade." She crossed her arms. "Why?"

Because eight gods were waking beneath the city with their hands out, and they'd given us one moon's turn to reforge a covenant written in mortal blood, or they'd take worship by force and burn souls for fuel. Because Captain Isolde Vane knew what I was and hadn't arrested me yet, which meant her protection had a shelf life measured in days. Because Sera Ilvane was somewhere in the Clerk's Tower right now, doing gods-knew-what with texts older than the Protectorate, and every hour I spent not acting was an hour the waking gods spent getting hungrier.

"Because nobody else is going to protect us," I said.

"That's not an answer, Galloway. That's a complaint."

I almost smiled. Twelve years ago I'd have liked her. "The covenant that used to shield people like you and me — the small magicians, the street-saints, the ones who never made the histories — it's broken. The big gods want it reforged on their terms. The Crown wants it bound tighter than before. And everyone in between is about to be crushed between those two appetites." I spread my hands. "I'm asking you to help me build a third thing. A network. People who can watch each other's doors, pass warnings, hide the hunted. What we should have had all along."

"And when the Crown comes for the network?"

I thought about Vane's face in my shop, the way she'd said *you're the Galloway*, like reading a name off a grave-marker. "Then we'll know where we stand."

Maren studied me for a long moment. Rain ticked against the loft's skylight. Somewhere below, the tannery vats turned and turned.

"You were a soldier," she said at last. "A mercenary captain, before you were a shopkeeper. The whole Ropewalk knows it, even if nobody says it. You're building an army, Jonah Galloway. Don't insult me by calling it anything smaller."

"The difference between an army and a neighborhood watch," I said, "is whether anyone gets ordered to die. I'm not planning on the first kind."

"No one ever plans on it." But she was already pulling her kit from under the floorboards, blowing dust off a ledger she'd kept hidden through two purges — names, addresses, marks of craft, a whole secret census of the forgotten. "Kess has a sister in Tanner's Row. There's a bone-setter on Cordwainer Street who whispers charms over splints. Half the lamplighters' guild pays tithe to something old under the aqueducts." She slapped the ledger into my palm. It weighed more than any weapon I'd carried in the Godswar. "You'll need this more than I will. I'm too old to run and too stubborn to hide."

"Maren—"

"Don't thank me yet." Her eyes were dry and hard and, underneath, terrified in the way only brave people get to be. "Thank me when your army doesn't get us all burned."

* * *

We worked for six days.

I won't pretend it wasn't good. That's the confession I keep coming back to, the one I'd burn before I'd say aloud in front of Vane or Sera or any god listening: it felt *good*. Walking the Ropewalk with Maren's ledger under my arm, knocking on doors I'd passed a thousand times without seeing, finding behind each one a person who had kept a coal of the old craft alive in a tin box, under a floorboard, inside a psalm they no longer believed in but recited anyway because the words worked.

There was Doss, the bone-setter, whose healing charms had never stopped working entirely — just gone faint, like a voice through a wall — and who wept when I told him why. There was the lamplighters' guildmaster, a squat woman named Petra Hallow with burn-scarred forearms, who showed me the shrine her guild had maintained under the streets for ninety years, candles guttering before a stone that might once have been a god of small fires and had long since forgotten its own name. There were the twins on Cordwainer Street who stitched luck into sailors' boots, and the widow who laid bread out for something in her walls, and a boy of maybe sixteen who could quiet dogs with a hum and didn't know it was magic at all.

Forty-one names in six days. Forty-one people who had spent a decade pretending to be nothing, and who looked at me — at the ledger, at the network taking shape between us — with an expression I remembered from the war. From the mustering grounds, the night before a campaign. Hope trying very hard not to be hope.

That was the part that scared me. Not the witch-finders. Not the waking gods. The look.

I'd led men before. I'd led them into the Godswar's worst years, when the nine thrones still spoke and the battlefields ran red with oaths gone wrong, and I knew exactly how that look ended. Some of the faces wearing it now would wear shrouds later, and they'd wear them because I'd given them something to believe in again, and belief is a debt that always gets collected.

But I knocked on the next door anyway.

* * *

They came for the Ropewalk on the seventh day, an hour past dawn, while fog still lay in the streets like spilled milk.

I heard the bells first — not the chapel bells but the Office of Purity's signal-bells, three quick strikes repeated, the pattern every child in Ashfall learned before they learned their letters. *Purity walks. Hide your wonders.* Then hooves, many of them, and the particular disciplined clatter of a column moving with purpose.

I was at Maren's loft with Petra Hallow and four others, sorting the ledger into copies — one for me, one for the aqueduct cache, one to be sewn into a sail and sent downriver if everything went wrong — when the bells started. We froze around the table like thieves interrupted, which, in the eyes of the law, we were.

"How many?" Petra asked.

I went to the window. Through the fog I could make out torchlight moving along Rope Street, and the gray shapes of wagons. Wagons meant arrests. Arrests meant they had names.

"They hit the fish-market first," I said. "Kess's sister lives there. They're working outward." I turned back to the room. Six faces, and every one of them wearing that look I hated. "Everyone goes out the back, separately, no one runs toward home. Maren—"

"I'm staying," she said flatly. "This is my loft. My name's not in your ledger under anything but the soap-shop. Let them search. I've been searched before."

"Maren—"

"*Go*, Galloway." She was already sweeping the ledger-copies off the table, pressing them into hands, herding with the brisk authority of a woman who had raised six children through two plagues. "And take the boy with you. The dog-charmer. He's too green to run alone."

So I went, with Tam the dog-charmer at my heels, down the back stairs and into the fog-choked alleys of the Ropewalk, and that is how I came to be standing in a butcher's doorway on Cordwainer Street when the skirmish started — though *skirmish* is a generous word for what happened, and *war* is a generous word for what it became. History doesn't care much for precision. It cares for shape.

Here is the true shape of it:

The Office of Purity's sweep reached Cordwainer Street mid-morning. Two wagons, a dozen witch-finders in gray-and-white, and at their head a lieutenant whose name I never learned, young, thorough, carrying a list. Doss the bone-setter was third on that list. I know because I saw the list — saw it fall from his hand later, in the mud, spattered with blood that wasn't his.

Doss didn't run. That was the tragedy of it, the stupid, human heart of it. He stood in his surgery door with his apron on and his instruments clean behind him, and he said, loudly enough for the whole street to hear, "I set broken bones. I have set the bones of your own soldiers, sir, and never asked what side they fell on. Is that your crime now? Mending?"

And the lieutenant, who was following his list and nothing else, said, "Unlicensed practice of the divine arts. Step aside, old man."

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do.

Tam — sixteen, brave in the way that gets boys killed — stepped out of our doorway and *hummed*. I felt it before I understood it: that low note he made, the one that quieted dogs, except this time it didn't quiet dogs. It quieted the witch-finders' horses. All twelve of them screamed and reared at once, and the column dissolved into kicking chaos, and half of Cordwainer Street poured out of doorways to see what the noise was, and among the people pouring out were Petra Hallow and six lamplighters who had been running messages for me since day two.

Petra did not hesitate. That's the other thing history won't record properly — how little hesitation there was, on either side. She put two fingers in her mouth and whistled her guild's fire-alarm, and forty lamplighters came out of the fog with their staves, and the lieutenant drew steel because he was twenty-three and frightened, and someone — I never learned who, and I've stopped trying — threw the first cobblestone.

I waded into it because there was nothing else to do. Twelve years of keeping my head down, and it lasted exactly as long as it took for a boy with a gift he didn't understand to stand alone in front of drawn swords.

I got Tam out. I got Doss out, bleeding from a saber-cut across the scalp that I stitched myself with hands that remembered triage better than they remembered charm-work. I got eleven other people out of the press before the witch-finders regrouped, and then I did the thing I am least proud of and most certain of: I stood in the middle of Cordwainer Street, in front of the whole reformed column, and I let my bond to Veyra show.

Not her power. I don't have her power; I never did, not since the war, not really. Just the *mark* of it — the oath-scar on my forearm, the old covenant-brand that every soldier of the Godswar carried, burning visible through my sleeve like a coal under cloth. The mark of a sworn man. The mark of someone the gods used to answer.

Every witch-finder on that street had grown up on stories of the Godswar. Every one of them knew what that brand meant. And every one of them, to a soul, took one step backward.

"Take your list," I told the lieutenant, "and go."

He went. Whether it was the brand, or the forty lamplighters, or the crowd of three hundred Ropewalkers watching from windows and doorways with stones in their hands — he went. The wagons rolled away empty. The fog closed behind them.

And Cordwainer Street cheered.

That was the worst moment of the day, worse than the fighting. Three hundred people cheering me, and every cheer a weight. I stood there in the mud with blood on my sleeves and a boy shaking against my shoulder, and I understood that whatever I'd meant to build — a neighborhood watch, a warning-network, a *third thing* — was already something larger in their eyes. Something with a name they'd give it whether I approved or not.

By evening they were calling it the Charm-Seller's War. By the next morning, the broadsheets had it: *GALLOWAY RAISES THE ROPEWALK*, though nothing had been raised but voices and a few thrown stones, and one old bone-setter's courage, which cost him his surgery, burned by the Office of Purity in reprisal before the ash of the skirmish had cooled.

* * *

Vane found me two nights later, in the back room of my own shop, counting the wounded.

She came alone, out of uniform, which from Isolde Vane was practically a declaration of love. She stood in the doorway looking at the cots, the bandages, the bucket of bloody water, Doss asleep with his head wrapped, Tam sitting vigil over him with his humming gone quiet — and her face did something complicated.

"Three dead," she said. "Two of mine. One of yours. A lamplighter named Ferro. You know that?"

"I know it."

"There'll be an inquiry. My superiors are using it to push for full purge authority over the lower districts. Martial law by month's end, if they get their way." She stepped inside, closed the door. "I covered for you. I told them the Ropewalk action was unauthorized escalation by an overzealous lieutenant. It bought you time. Not much."

"Why?" I asked. I'd wondered since Cordwainer Street. "You're Purity to the bone, Vane. Why cover for me?"

She looked at Doss's sleeping form for a long moment. "Because I read the old covenant texts last week," she said quietly. "The real ones, from the archive under the Ministry. The ones that say the first Lord Protector signed away mortal consent in blood to buy the gods' silence." Her eyes came up to mine, and they were the eyes of a woman watching her own foundations crack. "Everything I've served for nineteen years, Galloway. Everything I *believed*. And it was built on a theft." She straightened, armor going back up over the wound. "Your network. Your war, or whatever it is. How far along?"

"Forty-one names. More every day. Not enough."

"It won't be enough," she said. "Whatever's coming — and something is coming, the sky over the harbor has been wrong for three nights — a network of hedge-priests won't stop it." She pulled a folded paper from her coat and set it on my table. "Which is why you need to talk to your clerk friend. Sera Ilvane. Because my superiors aren't waiting for the moon to turn anymore. They've moved the timetable up. Whatever the Court is planning to do with the waking gods—" her jaw tightened "—they mean to do it within the week."

I unfolded the paper. A duty roster. A date. And a location that turned my blood cold:

*The Ninefold Court.*

Below the city. Beneath the thrones. Where Sera had gone three nights ago and not come back.

"She's been summoned," Vane said, watching my face. "Ordered to attend the Protector's convocation. As covenant-clerk, she's required to witness whatever ritual they're performing down there." A pause. "If she goes, Galloway, I don't think she comes back out. Neither does anyone else in that chamber."

Outside, over the rooftops of Ashfall, the sky split open with silent white light — no thunder, no storm, just a seam of brightness hanging above the harbor like a wound in the world — and every candle in my shop flared at once, and stayed lit.
