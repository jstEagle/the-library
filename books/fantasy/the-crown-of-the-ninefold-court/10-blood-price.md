## 10. Blood Price

The Clerk's Tower had a room for everything. Sera Ilvane knew them all: the room where confessions were transcribed and burned, the room where the old calendar was kept under triple lock because it counted years by gods instead of Protectors, the room with no name at all that smelled of tallow and cold iron and was used for exactly one purpose.

She went there at the third bell after midnight, carrying a ledger no one else in Ashfall could read.

The door took her blood before it took her key — a needle hidden in the handle, a drop drawn into the lock's throat. Twelve years ago she had helped design that mechanism herself, back when she still believed the Office of Purity was building safeguards instead of cages. The iron tasted of copper and something older beneath it, something that made her tongue go numb. She stepped through into darkness and did not light a lamp.

She wouldn't need one.

"You come alone." The voice filled the room the way water fills a lung. Not loud. Present. It came from everywhere the shadows were deepest, which was everywhere. "You always come alone. I have begun to find it characteristic."

"Korrath." She set the ledger on the bare stone table at the room's center and kept her hand flat on its cover, as if the book might bolt. "I've brought what you asked for."

"I asked for nothing." A shape assembled itself out of the dark across from her — not walking into being but *deciding* to be visible, edges arriving last. Korrath the Justiciar wore the form of a man because men trusted forms, tall and gray-robed, his face smooth as an unstruck coin. No eyes. Where eyes should have been there were only faint impressions, like the memory of a gaze pressed into wax. "I required. There is a difference, clerk, and your whole city is built on forgetting it."

"The difference being?"

"A request can be refused." He settled onto the bench opposite her, and the wood creaked though he weighed nothing; though he weighed everything; though weight was a concept he permitted out of courtesy. "A requirement simply *is*. Like gravity. Like debt."

Sera opened the ledger. Her hands were steady. She had spent twelve years making her hands steady, and she would be damned — possibly literally, given the company — before she let them shake now. "Then let's speak of debt."

* * *

She had prepared for this meeting the way she prepared everything: obsessively, secretly, and with the full expectation of failure. For six nights she had worked past the second bell in her office above the Hall of Records, cross-referencing ledgers that predated the Protectorate, that predated the Godswar, that predated the burning of the first temple district when Sera had been nineteen and had watched hedge-priests dragged into the street by their vestments. Six nights of finding the same thing in every source, written in every dead script the Court had ever suppressed:

*A covenant requires consent.*

Not coercion. Not purchase. Not the clever legal instruments the first Lord Protector had used to bind eight gods into silence while the ninth — gentle Veyra, who had never wanted thrones anyway — simply *stopped*, mid-prayer, mid-battle, mid-breath. Consent. Freely offered, freely renewed, or the whole architecture of the covenant collapsed into what it had always secretly been: theft wearing the robes of law.

That was why the Court had never been able to sign it. That was why they'd had to steal it — siphoning devotion through tax-collectors and tithe-clerks, funneling worship upward like water pumped from a well, feeding on faith the faithful never knew they were giving. You could not force a signature. So the Lord Protector had forged one, in mortal blood, on a page no god had ever agreed to, and called the forgery peace.

And now the ink was fading. And now the gods were waking. And now Jonah Galloway — who did not know yet what she had done, who might never forgive what she had done even if he learned all of it — had been summoned to reforge in one moon's turn what the Court had spent three centuries breaking.

Sera had run the arithmetic every way she knew. One moon was not enough. Not for negotiation, not for the small gods to gather, not for Jonah to understand what he was actually being asked to sign. The waking gods would take worship by force rather than starve, and Ashfall would burn, and the souls of two million people would be the fuel.

Unless someone bought more time.

Unless someone offered collateral the Justiciar could not refuse.

"You are staring at me," Korrath said. "I am told mortals find my face unsettling."

"Your face is fine. It's your patience that unsettles me." She turned the ledger toward him. "Page one. The original terms, transcribed from the Covenant Stone before the Court sealed it away. Read the fourth clause."

"I do not read, clerk. I *know*." But he leaned over the page anyway, and she understood that this too was theater — a god performing humanity so that a human could bear to negotiate. His finger, gray and smooth as riverstone, traced the line of text. "Willing consent of the governed, freely given and freely withdrawn. Yes. I know this clause. I wrote it." Something moved behind the blankness of his face — not expression, exactly. Weather. "It was the clause your Lord Protector found most inconvenient."

"It was the clause he murdered to avoid." Sera kept her voice level. "I've read the transcripts of the signing. Or the non-signing. Nine gods summoned to a table, eight bound before they understood what was happening, the ninth—"

"The ninth chose silence." For the first time, something like heat entered Korrath's voice, and the candle-sconces on the walls guttered though no flame burned in them. "Do not mistake Veyra's absence for victimhood, clerk. She saw the trap coming before any of us. She could have warned us. She could have fought. Instead she went quiet and left us to learn alone what betrayal felt like." The heat banked again, controlled, filed away. "I have had twelve years to consider whether I admire her discipline or despise her cowardice. I have not reached a verdict. Verdicts require evidence, and she has provided none."

"She was protecting us." The words came out of Sera before she could weigh them, and she realized, with a strange distant surprise, that she meant them. "The binding fed on compelled devotion. If she'd fought it openly, the Court would have tightened it until every prayer in Ashfall was a chain. Going silent was the only mercy available to her."

"You defend her."

"I defend the truth. It's my profession."

"It is your *crime*," Korrath said mildly. "Your profession is complicity. You have held the Office of Chief Covenant-Clerk for nine years. Every tithe-levy you countersigned, every devotion-tax you recorded, every forged line of the Protector's great forgery that passed under your seal — those are yours. Do not imagine I summoned you here ignorant of your ledger, Sera Ilvane. I have read it. All of it. I have had twelve years and nothing to read but ledgers."

Sera closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, she made herself meet the place where his eyes should have been.

"Good," she said. "Then we can skip the part where I pretend otherwise. Yes. I'm guilty. I brokered the silence. I drafted the instruments that made it binding. I told myself it was to stop the war — and it *was*, Korrath, the Godswar was killing ten thousand people a week, the streets ran red enough to stain the harbor, and the gods were too busy using mortals as ammunition to notice or care. Someone had to end it. I ended it the only way anyone could. And then I stayed, for twelve years, keeping the machine oiled, telling myself that if I resigned, someone worse would take the chair." She spread her hands flat on the table. "Every word of that is true. None of it absolves me. I know how guilt works. I keep its books."

For a long moment the Justiciar said nothing. In the silence, Sera became aware of the room itself — the way the stones seemed to lean inward, the way the dark between the sconces had texture, like fur, like feathers, like the inside of a throat. She was sitting in a god's mouth and negotiating with its teeth, and she had never in her life felt more awake.

"You interest me," Korrath said at last. "Most mortals who confess to me weep. Some argue. A few lie, which is always instructive. You *itemize*." He tilted the smooth face. "So. Itemize your proposal. You did not come at midnight to recite your sins. Guilt is cheap, clerk. Everyone owns some. What are you selling?"

* * *

"Time," Sera said. "I'm selling you a reason to wait."

"The moon turns in twenty-six days. Your oath-bearer has been summoned. When it turns, we collect what we are owed — the devotion of this city, restored to us, or taken. Those are the terms. Terms are not negotiable. They are the bones of law."

"They're the bones of *your* law," Sera said. "Which brings me to the flaw in your case."

Something shifted in the dark. Not anger. Attention — vast, cold, total, the attention of a court whose jurisdiction was everything. "Flaw."

"You're owed worship. Fine. I don't dispute the debt — the Court incurred it, the Court should pay it, and I'll get to that. But look at *how* you mean to collect." She pulled a second document from her coat — thinner, handwritten, her own cramped clerk's script covering both sides. "Force. You wake, you hunger, you take. Souls burned as fuel. Prayers answered with teeth. You'll get worship, yes — the way a robber gets a purse. Worship extracted under terror isn't devotion, Korrath. It's tribute. And tribute—" she tapped the fourth clause of the original covenant, "—is explicitly worthless under the terms *you wrote*. Willing consent, freely given and freely withdrawn. Take the city by fear and you gain nothing but noise. You'd be starving yourself on stolen bread and calling it a feast."

The temperature in the room dropped. Frost feathered up the stone table between them, delicate as lace, and Sera forced herself not to flinch, forced her hands to stay flat, forced her breath to come slow and white.

"You presume to teach me my own law."

"Someone has to. Your own oath-bearer won't — he'll just refuse you, and he's stubborn enough to die refusing. Your brother-gods are too hungry to think. And the Court certainly won't; the Court wants you bound tighter than before, and the current Lord Protector—" she stopped herself. Careful. Careful. That thread was not hers to pull tonight. "The point stands. Force fails your own test. Which means you need time. Time for the mortals to choose. Real choice takes longer than a moon."

"And you offer to provide this time." The frost crept another inch toward her fingers. "In exchange for what? Mercy? Clemency? I do not deal in clemency. I deal in balance."

"In collateral." Sera reached into her collar and drew out the thin silver chain she had worn for nine years beneath her official robes, where no one — not the Protector's spies, not the witch-finders, not even Jonah, back when there had been a Jonah — had ever seen it. On the chain hung a small vial of black glass, and in the vial, a single drop of blood that had never dried, never faded, drawn from her palm on the night she signed the instruments of silence.

"My soul," she said. "Held in escrow against the term of the extension. You keep it. Literally — this vial, this drop, the essence it carries. If the mortals fail to present new terms within three moons' turn, the vial opens, and what I am becomes yours. Payment on account against the Court's greater debt. If they succeed—" her voice caught, barely, and she hated it, and pushed through, "—if they succeed, you return it, and I stand trial before you for the original betrayal, and accept whatever verdict falls."

The frost stopped growing.

Korrath regarded her for a long, long moment, and Sera understood that behind the borrowed face, calculations were running that would have liquefied a mortal mind — weighing her soul against the city's, measuring the interest on three moons of patience, auditing the honesty of a woman who had spent twelve years perfecting the performance of honesty.

"You would wager your existence," he said slowly, "on the ability of a charm-seller and a traitor to rewrite divine law in ninety days."

"I would wager it on Jonah Galloway specifically. And on myself. And on a few hundred hedge-priests and street-saints nobody important has bothered to count." She slid the vial from the chain and set it on the table between them, and the frost retreated from it as if from a flame. "But yes. That's the shape of it."

"Why?" The question cracked out of him with sudden force, and for an instant the borrowed face flickered — the smooth gray skin rippling like the surface of deep water, something enormous moving underneath. "Twelve years you served the machine that caged us. Nine years you profited from the forgery. Why does a woman like you spend her *soul* to unmake what she built?"

And there it was. The real trial, hidden inside the negotiation, the question she had been avoiding since the night she signed the first instrument and told herself the trembling in her hands was cold.

Sera looked down at the vial. At the drop of her own blood, patient and black, waiting.

"Because I told myself I was saving the city," she said quietly. "And I was. Ten thousand people a week, Korrath. I'd do the arithmetic again tomorrow. But somewhere in the ninth year I stopped checking the arithmetic and started checking the ledgers, and I found entries I didn't remember writing. Tithes levied on grief. Devotion-taxes collected from widows. The Court wasn't preventing worship anymore — it was *harvesting* it, and I was the pen it wrote with." She lifted her head. "You want to know why? Because guilt that stays guilty is just pride wearing sackcloth. Because I built the cage and I will pay anything — *anything* — to see it opened. Because Jonah Galloway spent twelve years selling charms that didn't work to people who prayed to a god I helped gag, and every one of those dead charms is a debt with my name in the margin." Her hands, at last, were shaking. She let them. "Itemized sufficiently?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, through fathoms of stone and office and lawful order, Ashfall slept on, unaware that its fate sat on a bare table in a nameless room, in a vial of black glass.

Then Korrath extended one gray hand, and the vial rose from the table and crossed the air between them, and closed in a grip that had held the scales of creation.

"Three moons' turn," the Justiciar said. "No more. If your oath-bearer presents terms, they will be weighed — truly weighed, clerk, by me, and I am not gentle. If they fail, the vial opens." The smooth face inclined, almost — almost — a bow. "And Sera Ilvane. When you stand trial before me, as you someday must — bring better arguments than you brought tonight. I will expect them."

The dark folded around him like a closing book, and he was gone, and the room was only a room, cold and ordinary and smelling of tallow.

Sera sat alone for a long time, listening to her own heartbeat, feeling the absence of the little weight against her sternum like a missing tooth. Her soul was in escrow. Three months. Ninety days to do the impossible, or lose everything she was.

She picked up her ledgers, straightened her coat, and climbed the stairs toward her office — where, on her desk, a message waited that had not been there when she left. Sealed with wax she recognized instantly, stamped with the sigil of the Office of Purity.

Captain Isolde Vane requested her presence. Regarding the awakening. Regarding, the note said in a hand like knife-cuts, *the charm-seller Galloway*.

Sera read it twice, and began, very calmly, to plan.
