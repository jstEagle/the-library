## 11. Ashfall Burns a Little

The fire started, as fires in Ashfall always did, with a prayer.

Jonah heard it from three streets away — not the words but the *shape* of them, a desperate want hurled at the sky like a stone through a window. By the time he reached the Ropewalk's edge, the harbor quarter was already glowing orange against the dark, and the smoke rolling off the fish-oil stores tasted of something worse than burning fat. It tasted of incense. Of temples. Of a god's breath drawn close over mortal skin.

"Move," he told the crowd pressing backward along the quay, and they moved, because there was still enough of the old mercenary in his voice that strangers obeyed it before they thought to question why.

He shouldered through to the front and stopped.

The Saltwife's Rest — a tavern as old as the seawall, older than the Protectorate — was burning from the inside out, and the flames were *wrong*. They burned green at the heart, and they did not spread the way fire spreads. They gathered. They pooled in doorways like spilled wine, and where they touched people they didn't kill them outright; they *listened*. A dockhand stood in the doorway of the blazing taproom with his arms flung wide and his shirt smoldering, laughing, laughing, saying *it heard me, it finally heard me* — and the fire curled around him like a cat rubbing against its owner's legs, feeding on whatever he wanted so badly it had cracked him open.

Unbound divinity. Leaking into the streets like sewage from a broken pipe.

Jonah had seen miracles for two weeks now. Candles relighting. Fevers breaking. A lame man's leg straightening with a sound like green wood splitting. He'd told himself the small ones were harmless — the gods stretching after a long sleep, brushing against the world without meaning to bruise it.

He'd been lying to himself, and the lie was standing in a doorway wearing a burning man.

"Get back," Jonah said, and grabbed the dockhand's arm.

The man turned. His eyes had gone white as boiled eggs, and when he spoke, his voice came out layered — his own voice underneath, and above it something vast and amused and *hungry*, the way a butcher is hungry looking at a pen of pigs.

"He prayed for warmth," the thing said through the man's smiling mouth. "We are giving him warmth. Why do you take our gift from him, oath-bearer?"

"Because he didn't ask for *this*." Jonah hauled, and the man came loose from the doorway with a sound like tearing cloth, and the fire screamed — actually screamed, a high whistling note that shattered every window down the length of the Ropewalk. The dockhand collapsed in Jonah's arms, alive, weeping, the whites of his eyes fading back to bloodshot blue. "You're supposed to be asleep. You're supposed to be *bound*."

"We were bound," the fire said, from everywhere now, from every flame in the quarter at once. "And then your Court stole our silence, and now the silence is breaking, little merchant, and what leaks while it breaks belongs to no one. Not us. Not you. Pray carefully."

The green heart of the blaze surged up through the tavern's roof and stood for a moment against the night sky like a tree made of light, and then it split, and went looking.

* * *

Isolde Vane had commanded witch-finders through plague riots, grain riots, and once, memorably, a riot of tax clerks, which had been the most dangerous of the three because clerks fought dirty and knew where the records were kept.

She had never commanded them through this.

"Fall back to the cistern line!" she shouted, and her squad — what was left of it, four of twelve, the others scattered or worse — pulled back along the seawall road with their purity-lanterns held high. The lanterns were supposed to burn white in the presence of unlicensed magic. Tonight they burned every color at once, strobing like dying things, useless as decoration.

A woman ran past them carrying a child, both of them untouched by the flames that parted around them like courtiers around a duke. Behind them, a fishmonger knelt in the middle of the street praying aloud — *please, please, my stall, my stall, I'll give anything* — and the fire gave him his stall. Rebuilt it around him in a heartbeat, timber knitting together out of the smoke, whole and gleaming and wrong, the wood grown in shapes no tree had ever made. And the price came due just as fast: the man's shadow peeled off the cobbles and walked into the new stall and did not come back out, and the fishmonger kept praying, kept giving, grinning with black lips, because whatever had answered him had also made sure he couldn't stop wanting.

"That's the fourth one," Sergeant Breck said hoarsely beside her. His lantern had gone dark entirely. "Captain, the prayers aren't being refused anymore. Anything anyone wants badly enough—"

"I can see what happens, Sergeant."

She could see more than that. She could see the shape of the failure, and it was her failure, or her Office's, or the Crown's — twelve years of binding magic tighter and tighter, outlawing charms, purging hedge-priests, driving every small practice of devotion underground until the only prayers left in Ashfall were the desperate kind, the kind with hooks in them. And now the dam had cracked, and all that pent-up wanting was pouring out to meet divinity that had forgotten, in its long sleep, how to be gentle.

Her superiors would call this proof. *See? This is why we bind them.* Isolde looked at the fishmonger kneeling in his impossible stall, hollow-eyed and ecstatic, and felt something crack in her own chest that had nothing to do with fire.

"The children's hospice," she said. "Saint Verity's, on the canal. If the fire reaches the oil barges—"

"It'll jump the water," Breck said. "It jumped the Ropewalk canal twice already."

"Then we get them out before it does. Move."

* * *

Jonah met Isolde Vane at the doors of Saint Verity's, and neither of them pretended to be surprised.

The hospice sat on the canal's far bank, wedged between a rope-walk and a tannery, holding forty-one children whose parents had paid the Church of the Ninefold Court — before the Court went quiet above ground — to keep them fed and prayed-for. Now the building glowed in the firelight like a lamp about to be broken, and the canal between Jonah and it churned with green reflections.

"You brought your people," Isolde said. Her lantern was dead in her hand. Behind her stood six of Jonah's charm-sellers and hedge-folk, faces he knew from the old days — Marta of the Ropewalk with her knot-charms, old Tam Voss who could still read smoke, the twins Ferro and Fenn who'd never told him what exactly they sold and he'd never asked.

"And you brought yours," Jonah said. Four witch-finders, armor scorched, looking like men who'd been ordered to arrest everyone present and had decided, for tonight, not to mention it.

"Temporary arrangement," Isolde said.

"Temporary," Jonah agreed. "The children first. We argue after."

They crossed the canal on the ferry chain, hand over hand above the burning water-light, and the hospice doors opened before they reached them — opened inward with a slow, deliberate creak, and the smell that came out was cold candle-smoke and something older, something like a temple sealed for centuries.

Inside, the forty-one children stood in the dormitory in perfect silence, arranged in rows, facing the altar wall. And on the altar wall, in candle-flame that burned without candles, a face was forming — a long, patient, terrible face, all cheekbone and mercy, the way mercy looks when it has been starving for twelve years.

"They pray here every night," Tam Voss whispered. "Every night for twelve years. To a god who never answered. Do you understand what's been *stored up* in this room?"

The face on the wall turned. It looked at the children the way a man looks at a meal set before him after a famine.

"No," Jonah said, and stepped between the altar and the rows of small bodies, and did the stupidest, bravest thing he had done since the Godswar: he raised his left hand, where the old oath-scar sat white against the palm, and he said, in the formal cadence every soldier of the Godswar had learned, "*In Veyra's name, I claim wardship of these souls.*"

The face stopped.

The whole building stopped — the fire outside, the screaming, the green light on the canal. Everything hung still as the moment between lightning and thunder.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere, from the scar in his palm and the walls and the water under the floor, came a pressure Jonah knew better than his own heartbeat. Not words. Not yet. Just presence — vast, weary, and unmistakably *hers*, spreading through the hospice like cool water through a fevered room, and the face on the altar wall recoiled from it, snarling, dissolving into ordinary flame that guttered and died.

The children woke as if from a dream, blinking, frightened, ordinary. One of them started crying, and the sound was the most beautiful thing Jonah had heard in twelve years.

Isolde stared at him across the dim dormitory. "That was Veyra," she said. "The Silent God. She answered *you*."

"She answers everyone lately," Jonah said. His hand wouldn't stop shaking. "Mostly she just doesn't say anything back. Tonight she—" He looked at his palm. The scar was warm. Warmer than it had been since the war. "Tonight she drew a line."

* * *

Sera Ilvane watched the harbor burn from the Clerk's Tower and understood, with the particular clarity of a woman watching her own arithmetic come due, that they had perhaps nine days left.

Below her, the quarter glowed like a forge-mouth. The Office of Purity's bells rang the alarm pattern she'd helped design fifteen years ago — she'd been proud of that pattern, once — and the tiny figures of witch-finders and charm-sellers moved through the streets together, which should have been impossible, which meant Galloway was down there doing something reckless and half-divine, which meant the plan was fraying faster than she could reweave it.

On the covenant-table beneath the city, eight thrones were emptying themselves into the streets. Every miracle leaked, every prayer answered too literally, was divine essence escaping the binding contract through cracks the Court had never patched, because the Court had never imagined the gods might wake *angry*.

She had spent the evening with Korrath. That was the part she could tell no one — not the Protector's council, not Isolde, certainly not Jonah. The Justiciar had received her in the eighth tier, in a chamber of scales and ledger-light, and had listened to her proposal with the terrible patience of a judge hearing a confession he already possesses.

*More time,* she had asked. *Time to do this properly. Consent, freely given, the way the original covenant required.*

*And what collateral do you offer, clerk,* Korrath had replied, *that your word will hold?*

*Me,* she had said. *My soul. Held in escrow against the debt.*

Korrath had considered her for a long moment, and then had done something worse than accepting or refusing. He had smiled, gently, the way judges smile at defendants who have just revealed they don't understand the charges.

*Clerk Ilvane,* he had said, *do you know why the first Lord Protector had to steal consent rather than ask for it? Because the gods' price was always the same, and honest men have always found it too high. You are offering yourself for time. But time is not ours to sell. When the moon turns, the debt comes due against whoever stands nearest to it. And you, little clerk, have arranged yourself to be standing very near indeed.*

Now she watched the fire, and pressed her forehead against the cold glass, and let herself feel, for exactly one minute, the full weight of what she had done at twenty-six years old to save a city that had never known her name.

One minute. Then she straightened, wiped her face, and went downstairs to find the true texts. There was a clause somewhere in the first covenant — she was certain of it now, certain the way she'd been certain of nothing since the war — a clause about rewriting rather than restoring. About an oath-bearer's willing sacrifice.

She did not let herself think about which oath-bearer.

* * *

By dawn the harbor quarter had stopped burning, mostly because there was nothing left to burn.

Jonah stood on the seawall with Isolde Vane and watched the sun come up gray through the smoke. Between them, unspoken, lay the night's arithmetic: eleven dead, thirty-seven missing, one hospice saved, one god's name spoken in wardship over forty-one children, and one alliance — temporary, both had insisted, and both had lied — forged in a burning street.

"Your people carried children out of the fire alongside my witch-finders," Isolde said. "Marta of the Ropewalk shielded Sergeant Breck with her own body when the flame-thing turned on him. My Office has a list of names for the purge rolls, and hers is on it, and I am going to lose that page."

"Careful, Captain. That almost sounded like doubt."

"It sounded like arithmetic." She turned to face him, and in the gray light her rigid certainty looked tired for the first time since he'd known her. "Galloway. My masters are not trying to understand the awakening. I've seen the orders coming down from the Protector's tower. They're trying to *capture* it. Whatever ritual they're building, it needs the gods awake to work, and everything we saw tonight — the hunger, the leaking, the children lined up in front of that face — is fuel for their argument that mortals must be protected from divinity forever. By any means. Even if the protection is worse than the danger."

"What kind of means?"

"I don't know yet. But the moon turns in nine days, and the gods gave you a deadline, and I don't believe in coincidences of that size." She looked out at the smoking ruins, at the fishmonger's impossible stall still standing on the quay, grown from smoke into shapes no tree ever made, its owner sitting inside it with no shadow, smiling at nothing. "Both extremes are killing this city, Galloway. Lawless gods and lawless men. Someone has to find the middle."

"There is no middle," Jonah said quietly. "I've seen the terms. Restore the covenant, and mortals kneel again — devotion compelled, souls harvested on schedule. Refuse, and the gods take worship by force. Those are the only papers on the table."

"Then write new ones."

He turned to look at her. She said it flatly, like a tactical assessment, but her eyes weren't flat at all.

"You're an oath-bearer," she said. "The last one. The covenant-table recognizes your hand. I'm starting to think that's the entire reason any of us are still alive tonight." She picked up her dead lantern, weighed it, and set it down on the seawall stones, leaving it there. "Nine days. Find the clause. I'll hold off my masters as long as I can — and when I can't, I'll stand where I stood last night. On the side of the children."

She walked away down the seawall, and Jonah stood alone in the smoke-gray dawn with his scarred palm throbbing like a second heart.

Warm. Getting warmer. And underneath the warmth, faint as a voice through a locked door, the Silent God was speaking — not fragments this time, not the half-heard whispers of the past weeks, but words, whole words, gathering like a tide behind the lock.

*Come to the temple,* Veyra said, in the voice that had abandoned him mid-battle twelve years ago and had just spent itself drawing a line around forty-one sleeping children. *Come alone, Jonah. It is time I told you why I fell silent — and what I need you to do.*
