## 13. Midnight in the Clerk's Tower

*Jonah*

The Clerk's Tower rose out of the Ministry district like a needle someone had driven through the city's spine to pin it in place — nine stories of black basalt, windowless on the lower six, and at its crown a single lit room that never went dark. Jonah had walked past it for twelve years without once looking up. Tonight he looked up, and the light in the high window seemed to lean toward him like a face.

Sera was waiting in the shadow of the door.

She wore clerk's grey instead of the crimson of the Court's inner circle, and her hair was down, which it had not been since the war, and which did something to his chest he refused to name. A lantern hung from her hand. In the other she carried a ledger so old its cover had gone the color of old teeth.

"You came," she said.

"You said you'd found the true texts." He kept his voice flat, professional, the voice he used across a bargaining table. "I came for the texts."

"Of course you did." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the scar tissue where a smile used to live. "Come up, then. And Jonah — the stairs are warded against everything except us. I checked twice."

"That's comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be honest." She turned and led him into the dark, and after a moment's stubbornness, he followed.

The stairwell smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of ink — the smell of her, once, when they'd shared a tent outside Vell's Crossing and she'd sat up half the night transcribing casualty rolls by candlelight because somebody had to count the dead properly or the dead would have died for nothing. He had loved her for that. He had hated her for that, later, when counting things properly turned out to be what she did best, including counting the lives she was willing to spend to save a city.

Nine flights. She climbed them without pausing, and he let her set the pace, and neither of them spoke, because there were some silences that were doors and some that were walls, and neither of them knew yet which this one was.

At the top, the room took his breath and held it.

It was round, ringed floor to ceiling with shelves, and every shelf was crammed with books — but not the neat, numbered ledgers of the Office of Purity below. These were older. Hand-copied. Some were scrolls in wax-sealed tubes; some were tablets of fired clay strung on cords like grisly necklaces; one, laid open on the central table, was written on strips of bark that had been stitched together with sinew. The covenant-texts. The real ones, predating the Lord Protector's sanitized editions by centuries.

"This is theft," Jonah said quietly. "This is all theft."

"This is *custody*," Sera said. "There's a difference. The Court burned the originals in the Year of Ash. What survived, survived because clerks like me hid pages inside tax records and hymnals and children's primers. It took me nine years to reassemble what's in this room." She set the lantern down. Its light moved across the table like water. "And last week, cross-referencing a land-grant dispute from the Second Dynasty — a *land grant*, Jonah, gods help me — I found the clause."

He looked at her. Really looked, for the first time since the temple, since she'd stood between him and the eight waking thrones and told him what she'd done. There were new lines around her eyes. Her hands, resting flat on the ancient table, were steady the way hands are steady when they've practiced being steady for years.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

*Sera*

He sat where the lamplight caught him, and she hated herself a little for noticing, for cataloguing the grey at his temples and the new scar splitting his left eyebrow and the way he still read with his whole body leaned toward the page as if the words might bolt. Twelve years, and her body still kept his inventory without asking permission.

"The clause is here." She turned the bark-pages carefully. "The Covenant of Ninefold Accord, original recension, pre-Protectorate. Every scholar for three hundred years has worked from the Ashfall Redaction — the version the first Lord Protector authorized. The Redaction says the covenant can be *restored*: same terms, same signatories' heirs, same blood-price structure. That's what the eight want. That's what Korrath has been citing at me like a debt-collector with a writ."

"And?"

"And the Redaction cut a paragraph." She found it — she had the place memorized, had traced the characters so many times she could see them behind her eyelids — and turned the tablet toward him. "Read it aloud. Please. I need to hear it in someone else's voice before I believe it."

Jonah bent over the bark. His lips moved. Then stopped moving.

"Sera—"

"*Aloud.*"

His voice came out rough. "'Should the Accord be broken — by god or by mortal hand — it may not only be restored but *rewritten*, wholly, its terms remade, provided two conditions are met: that the rewriting be done at the covenant-table by an oath-bearer of standing; and that the new terms be sealed not in blood taken, nor worship compelled, but in a sacrifice *freely given* by that oath-bearer — the whole of their sworn bond, poured out without reservation, unto death if death be asked.'"

The silence afterward had weight. It pressed down on the little round room like deep water.

"A loophole," Jonah said at last. "Three hundred years of scholars, eight waking gods, an entire Crown apparatus hunting the old law — and nobody noticed because the Protectorate edited it out of existence."

"They didn't edit it out because it was dangerous to *them*." Sera heard her own voice go thin and precise, the way it went when she was frightened. "They edited it out because it was dangerous to the *gods*. A restored covenant gives the eight back their thrones. A rewritten one—" She made herself finish. "A rewritten one ends the old order entirely. No compelled worship. No enthronement. Devotion as gift, freely offered or freely withheld. It would unmake everything the pantheon was built on."

"So the eight can never know."

"The eight must never know." She met his eyes. "Korrath reads me like a ledger already. If he suspects—"

"Then we work fast, and we work quiet." He was already pulling the tablet closer, already the mercenary again, mapping terrain. "An oath-bearer of standing. That's me. Last living oath-bearer — the eight said so themselves, the flatterers. And the seal—" He stopped.

She watched him stop. Watched him arrive, in real time, at the sentence she had been living inside for five days now: *the whole of their sworn bond, poured out without reservation, unto death if death be asked.*

"Unto death if death be asked," he repeated softly.

"It says *if*," she said quickly. Too quickly. "It's conditional. The terms could ask less. We don't know until—"

"We know exactly how much the eight will ask." He looked up at her, and his eyes were terrible and calm. "They gave us a moon's turn, Sera. They promised to burn souls as fuel. Do you think men like that negotiate down? The moment I pour my bond into that table, they'll take all of it. Everything I am. Maybe everything I have."

"Then we find another way."

"There isn't another way. You spent nine years finding every other way, and this is what you found." He almost smiled. "Congratulations. Your loophole costs a life."

"*Your* life." The words tore out of her before she could dress them in clerk's grammar. "Only yours. Conveniently. Again. You always—" She pressed her lips shut. The lantern flame shivered.

Jonah stood very still. "Again?" he said.

* * *

*Jonah*

He hadn't meant to say it. He hadn't meant to open that door tonight, with the covenant spread between them like a patient on a table. But the word was out, hanging in the lamplight, and Sera's face had gone white in the particular way of someone who has just been shown their own ledger.

"Vell's Crossing," he said. "You want to talk about Vell's Crossing? Because I do. Twelve years is long enough to rehearse."

"Don't."

"You reported the company's position to the Court liaison. You told me it was to get civilians evacuated before the bombardment. And then the Godswar came to our ridge instead, and forty-one men who trusted me died holding a line for an evacuation that—" He stopped. Breathed. "Was there ever an evacuation order, Sera?"

"Yes." Her voice didn't shake. He'd forgotten how much he hated that about her — no, no, how much he'd *loved* that about her, that she could stand in any fire and speak in complete sentences. "There was. I have the requisition. I have the timestamps. I have the name of the officer who countermanded it after I'd already sent the warning, because the Third Column wanted your ridge as bait." She pulled a folded paper from her sleeve — of course she had it on her person, of course she'd carried it for twelve years — and laid it on the table between them like a card played face-up. "I sold you to save the city, Jonah. That part is true, and I will answer for it before any throne, mortal or otherwise. But I did not sell you to *nothing*. Forty-one was the price of eleven thousand. I've done the arithmetic every day since. Some mornings the arithmetic lets me sleep. Most mornings it doesn't."

He stared at the paper. An old requisition stamp. A dead officer's signature. Proof, after twelve years, that the betrayal had been real and the salvation had also been real, and that both things could live in the same woman the way a blade lives in a sheath.

"I hated you," he said. It came out almost gentle. "For twelve years I built my whole life on hating you. Dead charms, dead god, dead past. And then you stood in front of those thrones and told me the truth, and I couldn't even hate you properly anymore, because you'd done it to stop the slaughter, and I'd have—" His jaw tightened. "I'd have done the same thing. That's the worst of it. Given your seat at that table, I'd have signed the same silence."

"But?"

"But you should have told me. Before. After. *Sometime.* You let me carry forty-one graves believing they were bought with treachery."

"I know." For the first time all night, her composure cracked — a hairline fracture, quickly sealed, but he saw it. "Telling you felt like spending the last thing I had left that was clean. And I am a very good accountant, Jonah. I hoard."

The laugh that came out of him surprised them both — short, cracked, real. "Gods. Yes, you do."

Something shifted in the room then. Not the light. Not the air. Something in the space between their chairs, which had been a battlefield for twelve years and was suddenly, tentatively, only a small round table with a lamp on it.

"Sera." He reached across the bark-pages, slowly, giving her time to withdraw, and covered her hand with his. Her fingers were cold. They curled into his palm like something coming home to a house it had assumed burned down. "Whatever happens at that table — whatever the terms end up costing — I need you to know that I forgave you somewhere around the third flight of stairs tonight. Maybe earlier. Maybe years ago, and I was too proud to file the paperwork."

Her breath caught. "That's a terrible joke."

"I'm a charm-seller. My jokes were never good, they were just *available*."

She laughed — actually laughed, wet-eyed, one hand pressed to her mouth — and then she leaned across the covenant-table, careful of the ancient bark, and kissed him, and it was clumsy and desperate and tasted of ink and cold tea and twelve wasted years, and he held onto her like a man holding onto a rail in a storm, knowing — both of them knowing — that storms end and rails let go.

When they broke apart, her forehead rested against his.

"There's something else," she whispered. "About the clause. About the seal."

"Sera—"

"An oath-bearer of standing," she said. "That's what the text requires. Singular. But it doesn't define *standing*. And I've spent nine years reading this law, and I'm telling you: a chief covenant-clerk who brokered the silence, who holds proxy signatures from four of the nine thrones—" Her hand tightened on his. "Standing can be argued. Standing can be *shared*. If the price is a poured-out bond, then let it be mine too. Let them take the clerk who broke the world. Let it be *just*."

"No." He said it flat and fast, a door slammed. "No. Absolutely not. That's not—"

"Negotiable? Neither is yours." She pulled back far enough to look at him, and her eyes were bright and hard and full of a love so fierce it frightened him. "You see? Now we're both doing it. Now we're both hoarding the clean thing, the sacrificial thing, each of us trying to pay the bill before the other reaches the table." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "So here are my terms, oath-bearer. We have eighteen days. We spend them together — planning, arguing, kissing, whatever keeps us human. And when the moon turns, we go to that table *together*, and neither of us decides alone whose bond pours out. Agreed?"

Every instinct he had — the mercenary's instinct, the survivor's instinct, the instinct that had kept him alive through a war and a betrayal and twelve years of selling dead charms — screamed at him to lie to her. To agree, and slip away, and pay the price alone where she couldn't follow.

Instead he heard himself say: "Agreed."

And the terrible thing, the wonderful thing, was that he meant it — and that some quiet chamber at the bottom of his heart had already begun, without his permission, drafting the argument he would make at the table. *Mine alone. Take mine. She's done enough.*

He looked at her face and understood, with a cold settling certainty, that she was drafting hers.

* * *

*Jonah*

They worked until the lamps burned low — cross-referencing the clause against succession law, against the doctrine of consent that Sera had dug out of Korrath's own trial-records, against a fragmentary commentary by some forgotten hedge-priest who had written, four hundred years ago, in a margin Jonah would remember for the rest of his life: *the gods fear nothing so much as a mortal who owes them nothing.*

Near dawn, Sera fell asleep at the table, her cheek on an open scroll, one hand still resting in his. He sat unmoving so as not to wake her, watching the grey light come up over Ashfall through the tower's single window, and he thought about bonds. About what a man owed, and what a man chose, and the difference between a cage and a door.

Below them, the city stirred. Somewhere in the Ropewalk, his charm-sellers were waking to drill. Somewhere in the ministries, Isolde Vane was burning bridges with the precision of a woman dismantling a bomb. Somewhere beneath the streets, eight hungry gods turned over in their broken court like giants dreaming of breakfast.

And on the table, under his hand, the old bark-page lay open to the clause that could remake the world — *unto death if death be asked* — and beside it, in Sera's neat clerk's hand, a note she must have written days ago and hidden under the scroll, addressed to no one:

*If you are reading this, Jonah, then I was too slow, and you were too noble, and I want it recorded formally, in the proper hand, per regulation: the debt was never yours. It was always mine. Forgive me one last theft.*

Jonah closed his eyes.

Eighteen days. One moon's turn. Two people in love, each secretly writing the other's eulogy.

He slid the note into his coat, over his heart, where he kept the other things he couldn't afford to lose — and began, silently, to plan how to outrun a woman who had spent nine years learning every lock in Ashfall.

The race was on. Neither of them intended to lose. Both of them intended to win the same prize: the right to die for everyone else.

Outside, the sun rose on the ninth day, and somewhere far above the smoke and the spires, the moon — fat, pale, and turning — waited to see who would reach the table first.
