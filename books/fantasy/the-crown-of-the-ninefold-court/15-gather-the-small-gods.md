## 15. Gather the Small Gods

The aqueducts under the Ropewalk had not carried water in two hundred years, but they still remembered it. Jonah felt it in the walls as he descended the maintenance stair behind Old Nan Pell's chandler shop — a coolness, a patience, the sense of stone that had once done something useful and never quite forgiven the city for forgetting. Green-black water stood knee-deep in the lowest channel, fed by some spring no map acknowledged, and it reflected the hundred-odd candles his people had wedged into cracks along the vaulted ceiling until the whole tunnel looked like a river of stars running underground.

He counted them as he came down the last steps. Not the candles. The people.

Charm-sellers. Hedge-priests. A woman who read bones outside the fish market and swore she'd never once lied about what they said. Two brothers who kept a shrine to the Lady of Crossings in a drainpipe and left her offerings of buttons. A one-eyed veteran of the Godswar who sold blessings of safe passage and had never, in twelve years, been able to prove a single one had worked — and had sold them anyway, because hope was also merchandise, and someone had to stock it.

They were the ones the covenant had protected, back when there was a covenant. The small practitioners, the street-saints, the god-touched poor. When the gods went silent, the Crown hadn't just outlawed great magic. It had outlawed *them* — quietly, by degrees, with licensing fees and purity taxes and witch-finders at the door. Twelve years of being squeezed smaller and smaller, until men like Jonah Galloway sold dead charms to desperate buyers and called it survival.

And now they'd come when he called. That frightened him more than anything Isolde Vane could have done.

"You look like a man about to apologize," said Nan Pell, falling into step beside him. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a barrel of nails, and she'd been selling luck-charms since before the silence. She alone among them knew what he had been before he was a charm-seller. "Don't apologize. Apologizing's for people with something to be sorry for."

"I've got a list."

"Save it." She patted his arm with a hand like a boat oar. "Tell them the truth instead. They'll know if you don't. Half of them can smell a lie through wet wool."

The crowd hushed as he waded out onto the raised walkway that ran down the aqueduct's spine — an old inspection path, dry-railed, wide enough for three abreast. Candles guttered in the moving air. Faces turned toward him: brown faces and pale ones, young and old, a few children tucked against parents' sides because nobody could afford to leave them home tonight. He saw fear on most of them. Underneath the fear, worse, he saw hope. They thought he'd come to lead them somewhere safe.

He'd come to ask them to do the most dangerous thing any of them had ever considered.

"My name is Jonah Galloway," he said, and let the name sit there. Let them do the arithmetic. Some of them would remember Galloway of the Ninth Company, oath-bearer to Veyra the Quiet, the mercenary band that held the Saltbridge for eleven days against the Court's own legions while civilians crossed. Some of them would only know him as the man at the corner stall with the copper charms that didn't work. Both were true. He'd stopped pretending otherwise weeks ago.

"For twelve years I sold you charms that couldn't hold a blessing longer than it takes to say one. You knew. Most of you bought anyway, and I want you to understand that I never once thought less of you for it. Hope isn't foolishness. Hope is what we had left after everything else got outlawed."

A murmur ran the length of the channel. The bone-reader crossed herself with an old sign, pre-silence, forbidden.

"Now the gods are waking up." No point softening it. "You've seen it. Candles relighting. Prayers answered. The well-saint in Tanner's Row healing a child's fever last week. And you've heard what else comes with it — the witch-finders burning houses in Copperlane, the purges, the lists. The Crown says the awakening is plague. It isn't. It's debt collection."

He told them. All of it — or enough of it. The Ninefold Court beneath the city. The nine thrones. The contract signed in mortal blood by the first Lord Protector, the one that bought the gods' silence so the Court could hoard divine power for itself. The eight waking gods, hungry, furious, demanding that the last living oath-bearer reforge the covenant within a moon's turn — or come take worship by force, burning souls as fuel. He watched their faces go from disbelief to horror to that flat, grim steadiness that Ashfall's poor wore like armor, because they had never once been surprised by catastrophe. Only by mercy.

"And here's what the gods offered me," Jonah said. "Restore the old covenant. Every mortal kneels. Every prayer owed like a tax. Devotion compelled, collected, enforced. That's the deal on the table. Refill the thrones or burn."

"The old way worked," someone called from the dark — a hedge-priest, one of the brothers with the drainpipe shrine. "Worked a thousand years."

"It worked for the gods," Jonah said. "It worked for the kings who collected the tithes. Ask your grandmother if it worked for the woman who wasn't allowed to pray to anyone but the god her landlord chose. Ask the families who got visited by the tithe-collectors' enforcers when devotion came up short. The old covenant didn't ask love of anyone. It invoiced it."

Silence, except the drip of water and the flutter of candle flames.

"So I'm going to tell you the unthinkable thing now," Jonah said, "and then I'm going to ask you for something impossible, and you're all welcome to go home and pretend you never heard either."

He spread his hands.

"We won't kneel. Not to nine thrones. Not to one. We will write our own terms."

The uproar took a full minute to crest and longer to break. He let it run. Nan Pell stood beside him with her arms folded, radiating the serene confidence of a woman watching a pot she knew would boil.

"You're mad." The bone-reader again, voice cracking across the channel. "You're asking us to negotiate with *gods*. With things that eat prayers like locusts eat wheat. With Korrath the Justiciar sitting up there weighing souls on scales older than the city—"

"Yes."

"With the Protector's witch-finders hunting every one of us by name—"

"Yes."

"With nothing. With charms that don't work and shrines in drainpipes and—" Her voice broke entirely. "With *us*. We're nobody. We're the people the miracles happen *around*, never *to*."

"That's exactly why it has to be us."

Jonah waited until the echo died. Then he crouched, slow and deliberate, and put his palm flat on the wet stone of the walkway, the way an oath-bearer opened formal speech in the old days — a gesture so archaic that half the crowd wouldn't recognize it, but every god under the sky would.

"The great covenant was written by a Lord Protector and nine gods in a palace, and it treated mortals like livestock that happened to talk. Nobody asked a charm-seller. Nobody asked a crossing-woman or a bone-reader or a boy keeping candles lit in a drainpipe. The small gods — the little saints, the local powers, the Lady of Crossings and the well-saint and every humble thing that ever heard a prayer in this city — they weren't even invited to the table. They were furniture."

He stood.

"But here's what I learned from a silent god, and from a woman who broke my heart trying to save a city, and from twelve years of selling dead charms to people who deserved living ones: power doesn't come from thrones. It comes from consent. The original covenant needed willing mortal consent — that's why the Court had to steal it, forge it, buy it in blood. Consent is the coin the gods can't mint themselves. And right now, this room holds more honest consent than the whole Ninefold Court above us."

Old Nan snorted. "Flatterer."

"Truth-teller. There's a difference and it matters." He turned to face the full length of the channel. "Here are the terms I mean to carry to the covenant-table. Hear them and mark them. First: no worship compelled. Not by god, not by king, not by clerk. Devotion becomes a gift or it becomes nothing. Second: no god enthroned above another, and no throne above mortal law — the small gods stand as witnesses, with voice and veto, so that no ninefold court can ever again be closed against the people it governs. Third: the Crown's monopoly on the divine ends. Every soul in Ashfall may bargain, pray, refuse, or walk away, freely, and the small gods keep the doors open."

Murmuring now, different in texture. He pressed on.

"And fourth — the part that costs. The rewrite must be sealed by an oath-bearer's willing sacrifice. That's me. My bond, my sworn power, twelve years of Veyra's gift poured into the contract like blood into ink. I'm not asking anyone to die for this. I'm asking you to help me make sure it's worth what it costs."

The bone-reader pushed forward through the crowd, wading the side-channel without seeming to notice the water soaking her skirts, until she stood below the walkway looking up at him with eyes like wet slate.

"You'd give it up? Your power? The thing that makes you matter?"

"It never made me matter. It made me useful, which is different, and I've been both useless and useful, and neither one is the same as mattering." He smiled, tired. "What makes me matter is standing in a flooded sewer talking four hundred people into treason. What makes *you* matter is that you came."

For a long moment she just looked at him. Then she laughed — a short, hard, disbelieving sound — and turned to the crowd.

"Well? Are we furniture?"

The answer came up out of the channel like water finding its level. Not a roar at first — a rumble, a gathering, hands rising out of the dark by twos and tens, candlelight catching knuckles and cracked palms and the small defiant gestures of people who had spent twelve years being told their faith was contraband. Nan Pell raised her fist like she was hailing a ship. The drainpipe brothers lifted their button-box between them like a relic. Somewhere near the back, a child's voice piped up clear over the grown noise, saying the Lady of Crossings' name the way other children said a friend's.

Jonah stood on the walkway with his heart hammering and let it wash over him, and underneath the noise he felt — faint, far off, like a bell rung in another room — the attention of something vast turn toward the aqueducts and listen.

The small gods had heard too.

*

They worked until the candles burned low, and what they built in those hours was stranger than any army. Nan Pell organized the charm-sellers into circuits — who covered which districts, where the safe-houses were, which chimneys drew and which were watched. The bone-reader, whose name was Maren and whose bones had finally started saying true things again, mapped every active shrine in the city from memory: forty-one sites, from the well-saint of Tanner's Row to a ragged altar to the Mother of Small Mercies behind a tannery, each one a node of consent, each one a door the small gods could speak through. The veteran with one eye — Brannoc, Ninth Company, Jonah realized with a jolt; Brannoc who'd carried the standard at Saltbridge — took the military questions nobody wanted to say aloud. How many could fight. How many should. Where the Crown's patrols ran thinnest between midnight and matins.

"We're not an army," Jonah told them, when the maps were drawn in candle-soot on the aqueduct floor. "Armies get met with armies. We're a petition. Four hundred signatures written in people instead of ink. When the moon turns, we walk up through the Court's tiers openly, unarmed, and we stand at the covenant-table while the terms are spoken. If the Protector wants to slaughter a petition of the city's faithful in front of eight waking gods, that's his choice to make and his soul to answer for."

"And if the gods don't honor it?" Maren asked quietly. "If they hear our terms and laugh?"

"Then I'll have given my power for nothing, and you'll all have wasted a very damp evening." He looked around at the soot-maps, the guttering candles, the faces. "But I don't think they'll laugh. I think they're listening harder than they've listened in a thousand years. Because for the first time since the first Lord Protector signed that contract, somebody's offering them the one thing they can't take."

"What's that?" Brannoc asked.

"A reason not to be monsters."

The meeting broke toward dawn, in twos and threes, along six different stairs, because Nan Pell insisted and Nan Pell was usually right. Jonah stayed last, as the water stilled and the candle-smoke rose and the star-river overhead dimmed to embers. He crouched at the channel's edge and spoke aloud, quietly, to whatever had been listening.

"If any of you small ones are still awake down here — the Lady of Crossings, the well-saint, any who remember being prayed to freely — I'm not asking for a miracle. I'm asking you to witness. When the time comes, stand with us at the table. That's all. Just witness."

The water rippled. Once. In a pattern no current makes.

On the walkway beside his boot, where there had been nothing a moment before, lay a single white feather.

Jonah picked it up and held it a long time, and for the first time since the gods woke, he was not afraid.

Then the far end of the aqueduct lit red — torchlight, many torches, coming fast down the maintenance stairs — and a horn blew three short notes. Witch-finder signals. Someone had talked.
