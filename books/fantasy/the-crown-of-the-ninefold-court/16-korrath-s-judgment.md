## 16. Korrath's Judgment

They came for her at the third bell of the night, and they came politely.

That was the worst of it, Sera thought later — the courtesy of it. Two of Korrath's gray-clad ushers stood in the doorway of her tower office with their hands folded like clerks awaiting a signature, and the taller one said, "The Justiciar requests your presence," as though she were being summoned to review a ledger rather than to surrender her soul.

She had known it was coming since the night she knelt in the flooded dark beneath the Ropewalk and offered herself as collateral. She had signed nothing, spoken no formal oath, but Korrath the Justiciar did not require paper. He required only a debt acknowledged aloud, and she had acknowledged it — *take my years, take my name, take whatever balance remains* — in the flat voice of a woman who had spent eleven years doing arithmetic with other people's lives and had finally run out of columns.

"May I bring my own records?" she asked.

The usher blinked. "The Court provides all documents."

"Of course it does." She closed her ledger, squared its edges out of habit, and set down her pen. The ink was still wet on the last entry: *Terms extended by one moon's turn. Collateral: Ilvane, Sera, chief covenant-clerk.* "Then let's not keep him waiting."

* * *

The Ninefold Court had changed since Jonah first saw it, and Sera felt the change in her teeth before she saw it with her eyes.

The great cavern beneath Ashfall had been dry when she'd brokered the silence here, eleven years ago — a vault of black stone and dead air where nine thrones sat empty under a ceiling carved with the names of every mortal who had ever sworn an oath. Now water ran down the walls in silver threads, moss glowed faintly green along the seams, and the air tasted of rain and iron and something older, something that made the back of her throat ache like the memory of a childhood fever. The gods were waking, and their waking was flooding the foundations of the city above them, and no one up there knew.

Eight thrones were occupied now. Not fully — not the way the old paintings showed them, vast and blazing — but present, each one a pressure, a weight, a shape the eye kept sliding off. She catalogued them the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was how she kept from screaming: Ysmene of the Tides, coiled around her throne like a woman made of river-water; the twins Vash and Vesh, sharing one seat and one hunger; old Halvard the Smith, whose presence smelled of cooling metal; the others dimmer, stranger, half-remembered even by her, who had spent half her life memorizing them.

And at the center of the floor, where the covenant-table should have stood, there was a courtroom.

Korrath had built it in an hour. That was the thing about the Justiciar — he was the least flamboyant of the nine and therefore the most frightening, because he needed no spectacle. Where the others woke hungry and burning, Korrath woke *organized*. He had raised benches from the living stone, a witness box, a stand for documents, and a judgment-seat that was simply his throne moved closer, so that sitting upon it he could look every accused soul in the eye.

He looked like a man. That was the trick of him, or perhaps the truth. Gray robes, gray beard cropped short, gray eyes with the patience of a glacier. In the oldest icons he carried scales; in the flesh he carried only a single sheet of parchment, which he consulted now as Sera was led to the center of the floor.

"Sera Ilvane." His voice was quiet. It filled the cavern anyway. "Chief covenant-clerk of the Protectorate of Ashfall. Eleven years in service to the Crown. Author of four hundred and six binding contracts, including—" he turned the parchment over, though he plainly knew what was written on the back, "—the Silence."

"I prefer its formal title," Sera said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "The Concordat of Divine Abeyance."

"Abeyance." Korrath almost smiled. "Your masters were gifted with words. Eight gods bound into sleep, their worship diverted into the Crown's own vessels, their power hoarded by one mortal line for three generations — and you call it an abeyance. As though we might wake any morning and find it all a misunderstanding."

"That is precisely what happened."

"It is what happened," Korrath agreed. "It is not what was intended. Sit."

There was no chair. Stone rose out of the floor behind her knees, and she sat, because refusing a god's furniture seemed unwise, and because her legs had quietly stopped being reliable somewhere around the word *Silence*.

"The charge," Korrath said, and the cavern itself seemed to lean in. Eight presences sharpened. Somewhere above, through a hundred feet of rock, Sera would have sworn she heard the city hold its breath. "On the ninth day of the month of Embers, in the eleventh year of the Concordat, the accused did knowingly and willingly offer herself as collateral against debts owed by the mortal signatories of said Concordat. This offer was accepted. The debt stands. The payment demanded is—" he paused, and for the first time something moved behind the gray eyes, something that in a man she would have called regret, "—the soul of Sera Ilvane, whole and entire, in perpetuity."

The words landed in her like stones dropped down a well. She heard them fall and fall and never heard them strike bottom.

"You may speak," Korrath said.

She had prepared arguments. Of course she had; she was the best contract-clerk the Protectorate had ever produced, and she had spent the walk down composing rebuttals the way other people composed prayers. But sitting under eight pairs of ancient eyes with her soul priced and posted, all of them burned away except one question, and it was not a legal question at all.

"Did you accept my offer," she asked, "before or after you read my intentions?"

Korrath tilted his head. "Explain."

"I offered myself to buy time. To delay your collection long enough for another solution to be found. You accepted — and then you waited until the moon was nearly turned, until the deadline you granted me has nearly expired, and now you collect. Was that mercy, Justiciar? Or was it bookkeeping? Did you let me spend these weeks believing I'd bought something, knowing all along you'd come to collect the principal anyway?"

The silence stretched. One of the twins laughed — a sound like ice cracking — and Ysmene hushed her with a motion like a wave smoothing itself.

"I read your intentions," Korrath said at last. "I read them the way I read everything: completely. You offered yourself in bad faith, clerk. You hoped to be released. You hoped your charm-seller would rewrite the covenant and render your debt void. You hoped, in short, to have it both ways — the appearance of sacrifice without the substance." He folded the parchment. "I do not punish hope. But I do not honor it as payment, either. A debt acknowledged must be paid. That is the whole of law. Everything else is weather."

"And if the debt was never mine?"

"Then you may prove that. Witnesses will be heard." His gaze lifted past her shoulder, toward the tunnel mouth. "Beginning, I think, with the one who has been standing in the dark for the last quarter hour, deciding whether to interrupt."

Sera closed her eyes. *Jonah.*

* * *

He came out of the tunnel looking terrible, which meant he'd been fighting again — a split lip, a fresh burn along his jaw where a witch-finder's censure-lamp had kissed too close, the new hard muscle of a man who had remembered what his body was for. Twelve years of selling dead charms had softened him. Three weeks of war had re-carved him. And walking beside him, hand resting light on his shoulder like a claim, was the witch-finder captain — Isolde Vane, in a uniform whose insignia she had, Sera noticed, defaced. Burned off. The Office of Purity did not forgive that. Whatever bargain those two had struck, it had cost Isolde more than she'd admitted.

"You're late," Sera said, because she could not say anything else, because everything else was a floodgate.

"You're early," Jonah said. "You've been early for everything your whole life. Eleven years early, this time."

"Jonah Galloway." Korrath's voice dropped the temperature of the room. "Last oath-bearer of the old covenant. The eight of us summoned you days ago. You did not come."

"I came now." Jonah walked forward until he stood between Sera's stone chair and the judgment-seat, which was either very brave or very stupid, and having known him twelve years Sera understood it was both, always both. "And I didn't answer your summons because I've been busy reading. Sera and I found the original covenant texts. Not the Crown's copies — the originals, from before the first Lord Protector got his hands on them. Do you want to know what they say, Justiciar? Because I don't think you know. I don't think any of you know. I think you've been sleeping so long you've forgotten your own marriage contract."

Halvard the Smith rumbled something in a language that predated language. Korrath raised one finger, and the rumble died.

"Speak, then."

"The covenant wasn't a leash." Jonah's voice took on the cadence Sera remembered from a hundred nights around mercenary fires, the cadence he used when he was about to say something true and hated saying it. "It was a treaty. Mutual terms. The gods received devotion — freely given, freely withheld. Mortals received protection, small miracles, the right to petition. Clause after clause after clause, and every clause runs both directions. There is no provision anywhere in the original text for compelling worship. None. The power to compel — the power your brother-signatories are threatening to use on this city, burn souls for fuel, take worship by force — that power doesn't come from the covenant at all. It comes from the Concordat. From the theft. The Crown stole your authority and twisted it, and now you want to use the stolen shape of it back at them, and every soul you burn in the taking gets poured into the same mold."

"We are owed," said Vash, or Vesh — Sera had never learned to tell them apart, and suspected they preferred it that way. Their voice came from everywhere, twin-throated. "Eleven years of devotion, diverted. Three generations of prayer, harvested by mortal hands. The arithmetic is not complicated, oath-bearer."

"No," Jonah said. "It isn't. So let's do it properly." He turned to Korrath, and Sera saw him plant his feet the way he used to before holding a line. "You want a debtor. Fine. Let's audit."

"Audit," Korrath repeated.

"You put Sera Ilvane on trial for the betrayal. But she didn't betray you. Read your own record, Justiciar — you just read it aloud. She brokered the Silence to stop the Godswar. I was there. I fought in it. I watched the nine of you tear the world open over worship nobody was giving freely anymore, because the temples had become tax offices and the prayers had become invoices. The Godswar killed forty thousand people in Ashfall alone. Sera ended it. Yes — by helping the Crown bind you. Yes — by lying to me, by letting me believe my god had abandoned me, by letting me carry that for twelve years while she carried the truth. She owes me. She does not owe you." He drew breath. "The Court owes you. The Lord Protector's line owes you. They signed the Concordat in mortal blood, they harvested your worship, they built a dynasty on stolen divinity — and they are doing it again right now, upstairs, as we speak. The current Protector isn't trying to keep you bound. He's trying to devour you. All eight. Swallow you into the Crown and wear you as a crown. Every hour you spend prosecuting the clerk who tried to stop the first theft is an hour he spends preparing the second one."

The cavern went very still. Even the water on the walls seemed to slow.

Sera stared at Jonah's back and thought, absurdly, of the night he'd found her at the temple entrance, three weeks and a lifetime ago, and the way his face had gone through betrayal and grief and arrived somewhere neither of them had names for. She had expected him to hate her forever. She had built her life, her cold tidy tower life, on the assumption that some debts were simply permanent. And now he stood between her and eight gods, spending his fury like coin, arguing her case better than she could have argued it herself — because he was right, and because he had decided, sometime in the dark of the Clerk's Tower with the covenant texts spread between them, that right mattered more than the wound.

*You fool,* she thought, with a tenderness that frightened her more than the trial did. *You beautiful, impossible fool.*

Korrath regarded Jonah for a long moment. Then, deliberately, he unfolded his parchment and set it face-down on the arm of his throne.

"The oath-bearer makes a legal argument," he said. "Very well. Let us test it legally. Galloway — you assert that the debt arising from the Concordat belongs to the signatory, not to the instrument. That the clerk was a pen, and one does not hang the pen for the hand that held it."

"I assert the pen fought the hand," Jonah said. "Ask her what it cost her. Ask her what she gave up. Go on — you can read intentions. Read hers."

All eight presences turned to Sera at once. It was like being opened. Like a lockpick going through eleven years of her and lifting every tumbler. She felt Korrath move through the night she'd signed the Concordat with her hands shaking and her eyes dry; the decade of ledgers she'd kept double-entry, one column for the Crown's official record and one column for her conscience; the small sabotages no one had ever noticed — misfiled clauses, delayed purges, a witch-finder redirected here, a hedge-priest warned there; the weeks of negotiating with an eighth of a god in the dark, offering the only currency she had left.

When it was done, Korrath sat back. His expression had not changed, but the quality of his attention had, the way a scale changes when the weights shift.

"She obstructed the Concordat from within," he said slowly. "Small obstructions. Insufficient ones. She told herself insufficient was all she could manage, and she hated herself for the arithmetic." He looked at her directly, and Sera discovered that meeting the Justiciar's eyes was like standing at the edge of a very deep, very clear well. "You believed the debt was yours regardless. You believed someone had to pay, and you volunteered, because volunteering was the only agency left to you."

"Yes," Sera said. Her voice cracked on it. "That's the whole confession. That's everything I am."

"And that," said Korrath, "is why the trial cannot end the way you fear — or the way you secretly hoped." He rose from the judgment-seat, and when the Justiciar rose, the whole Court rose with him, stone groaning, eight ancient hungers leaning in. "Because the debt is real, clerk. Not yours — but real. Eleven years of stolen devotion, compounded. Someone must satisfy it, or the law itself fails, and if the law fails then everything your oath-bearer proposes — new terms, free devotion, a covenant rewritten instead of restored — dies with it. I cannot simply forgive. Forgiveness without satisfaction is not mercy. It is rot."

"Then take the debt from the ones who incurred it," Jonah said.

"The signatory line hides behind wards the Protector is raising even now. We cannot reach them — yet. And our patience, oath-bearer, is not infinite. The moon turns in five days." Korrath descended from the dais, gray robes whispering, and stopped before Sera's chair. Up close he smelled of dust and ink, which she supposed was fitting. "Here is my judgment, and hear it carefully, because I will not repeat it. The trial is suspended — not dismissed. Your soul remains posted as collateral, Sera Ilvane, but the debt transfers its priority. If the Concordat's true debtors are brought to account — if the Protectorate's theft is answered, by restitution or ruin — your obligation dissolves as a matter of law. If they are not..." He let the sentence stand unfinished, and the unfinished part was louder than any threat the other gods had made.

"And if we bring them to account?" Jonah asked. "If we storm the Ninefold Court above and break the Protector's ritual — what then? You'll release her?"

"What then," Korrath said, "is that you will owe *me*, oath-bearer. Justice rendered is justice paid. Remember that when you are writing your new covenant. Free devotion cuts both ways." He turned away, then paused. "One more thing. The clerk's obstruction — small, insufficient, real. It weighs. Not enough to cancel. Enough to matter. When the final accounting comes, it will be entered in her favor." Something flickered across the gray face, gone too fast to name. "I am not without mercy, Galloway. I am merely without shortcuts. Learn the difference. It will save your life in five days."

The Court began to dim around them, thrones receding into shadow, the waking gods withdrawing like tide. Sera stayed in her chair a moment longer, breathing, alive, posted like a note pinned to a door — and then Jonah's hand was there, rough and warm, pulling her up.

"Five days," she said.

"Five days," he agreed. Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the first far-off sound of the Protector's ritual rolled like thunder learning its alphabet. "So we'd better go start a war."
