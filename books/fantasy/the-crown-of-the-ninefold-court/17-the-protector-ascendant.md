## 17. The Protector Ascendant

The Lord Protector Aldric Vane had not slept in three days, and did not intend to sleep again until the world was remade.

He stood at the heart of the Covenant Spire — the tower the histories called the Clerk's Tower, though no clerk had worked there in a century — and watched the ritual circle drink light. Nine concentric rings of silver and salt and something darker that had cost him four archivists to transcribe correctly. At the center, suspended in a cradle of chains, hung the First Contract: the original covenant, signed in mortal blood by his ancestor, its vellum black with age and older guilt.

"You understand what you're asking," said the Archivist, a small man whose hands had not stopped shaking since dawn. "The eight are waking. They will feel this. They will *fight* it."

"They will feel it the way a drowning man feels a rope," Aldric said. "They will be grateful before they understand. And by the time they understand, they will be mine."

He unrolled his sleeve. On his forearm, inked in the blood of nine generations of Protectors, the sigil of office pulsed like a second heartbeat. His grandfather had used it to bind one god's whisper. His father had used it to silence a hedge-priest's dying curse. Aldric intended to use it for what it had always been made for — the thing the whole rotten architecture of Ashfall had been built to conceal.

The Crown was not a crown. It was a cage, and he was about to become the thing inside it that could never be overthrown.

"Begin," he said.

The Archivist spoke the first word of the First Language, and every candle in the Spire went out at once — not extinguished, but *swallowed*, their flames drawn upward through the ceiling into the night sky above Ashfall.

Above the city, the clouds began to turn.

* * *

Isolde Vane felt the sky split from half a mile away, through the soles of her boots.

She was running — actually running, commission burned to ash in her breast pocket, her white-and-gold coat traded for a smuggler's grey — when the heavens tore open above the Spire like wet paper. Not thunder. Something worse: a sound like a vast page being turned, or torn, and through the wound in the sky poured light that had no color she had words for. It fell across the rooftops of the Ministry district and everything it touched remembered what it had been before it was stone.

The fountain in Purity Square became, for three full seconds, a fountain again — water leaping silver and glad instead of frozen in the Crown's rationed trickle. The cobbles under her feet softened as if they were earth. Somewhere a bell that had been mute for twelve years rang once, wildly, joyfully, and then screamed as something reached down out of the tear and *pulled*.

"Veyra's mercy," Jonah Galloway breathed beside her. He had stopped running. He was staring up, and his face — that careful, closed merchant's face she'd seen across a raided shop counter — was gone. What was underneath looked younger and much more afraid. "That's not an answered prayer. That's a *harvest*."

"The Protector," Sera Ilvane said. She had a clerk's satchel strapped across her chest and she clutched it like a shield. Inside it were the true covenant texts, and Isolde had decided two nights ago, in a safehouse that smelled of lamp oil and fear, that she would die before letting anyone take them. "He's starting early. We thought we had until the moon turned."

"We have until it finishes turning," Jonah said. "Look." He pointed at the sky, where the tear's edges were creeping outward, slow as rot. "He can't devour them all at once. Eight gods is eight meals, and even he has to eat them one throat at a time. That gives us—" he squinted, counting some arithmetic only oath-bearers could see, "—less than a day. Maybe less than that."

"Then we stop talking and start climbing," Isolde said.

The Ministries of Purity occupied six blocks between the square and the Spire, and they were burning.

Not all of them — not yet. But the Hall of Records was alight from the third floor up, and the Office of Ordinances had its great bronze doors buckled outward as if something inside had wanted out. Her former comrades moved through the smoke in their white hoods, witch-finders with drawn brands, and they were not fighting rioters. There were no rioters left in this quarter. They were hunting *her*.

"There," Sera whispered, and pointed.

Three figures on the steps of the Hall of Records, backlit by flame. Isolde knew them the way you know your own handwriting. Captain Dree Hale, who had taught her the binding-litany when she was nineteen and hungry for certainty. The twins Osk and Merrin Vael, who had carried her on a stretcher for six miles after the Ropewalk skirmish went wrong. And behind them, taller than the rest, wearing the silver trim of a High Finder — Corvan Ashe, who had once told her, drunk at a winter feast, that if the Crown ever asked him to do a thing he couldn't defend at a graveside, he'd burn his own commission too.

He wasn't burning it now. His brand was lit, and it was pointed at her.

"VANE!" Dree's voice cracked across the square like a whip. "You're named! You come quiet or you come in irons!"

Isolde stepped forward, out from behind the fountain's shadow, and let them see her face. Let them see that she wore no hood, no colors, no lie.

"Dree," she called back. "Ask yourself what's happening to the sky. Ask yourself why the Protector needs a harvest tonight, of all nights, if the gods are the enemy he's always claimed." She spread her empty hands. "I'm not named because I broke the law. I'm named because I read the law. All of it. Including the parts he keeps locked."

For one heartbeat — one — Dree Hale's brand dipped.

Then Corvan Ashe put his hand on Dree's shoulder, and the moment died.

"Take them," Ashe said quietly. "All three. Alive, if the gods are kind. Dead, if they aren't."

* * *

Jonah ran.

Twelve years of selling dead charms had kept him soft in the ways that mattered and hard in the ways that didn't, but the old mercenary lived somewhere below the shopkeeper's paunch, and it woke now with a snarl. He took the alley behind the fountain at a sprint, vaulted a stack of ration crates, and heard Isolde's boots hit the ground beside him — the woman ran like a soldier, which of course she was, whatever color coat she wore now.

Sera was slower. Sera had spent the Godswar's aftermath bent over ledgers, not battlefields, and it showed in her ragged breathing, in the way she stumbled on the wet cobbles. Jonah caught her arm without breaking stride and hauled her along, and she didn't protest, and that frightened him more than the witch-finders did. Sera Ilvane protested everything. It was how she loved.

"Left," Isolde snapped. "The Ordinance archive connects to the Spire's undercroft — I signed the survey myself, there's a service stair—"

The wall ahead of them exploded.

Not metaphorically. The archive's flank burst outward in a gout of splintered oak and paper ash, and through the wreckage came something that had been a man. It still wore a finder's whites, mostly. Its eyes were full of the colorless light from the sky, and when it opened its mouth, what came out was not a scream but a *prayer* — a raw, wordless begging, the sound of a soul being pulled upward through a straw.

"God-touched," Isolde said, going pale. "Unbound leakage. The tear's raining divinity and it's getting into people through their prayers—"

The thing lunged.

Jonah did the only thing he knew how to do anymore, the thing he'd done ten thousand times at a market stall for coppers: he answered a prayer. He grabbed the creature's face in both hands, looked into those drowning eyes, and spoke in the old cadence, the charm-seller's cadence, the cadence Veyra had taught him across a battlefield long ago.

"I hear you," he said. "I hear you. You're not forgotten. Hold."

The creature shuddered. For a moment — just a moment — the light in its eyes steadied, and beneath it was a young finder's face, terrified, human, *grateful*. Then the pull from the sky wrenched it away, and it collapsed like an emptied coat, and Jonah stood over it shaking, because he had held a soul in his hands and felt it slip through anyway.

"That's what he's doing," Sera whispered. She had gone the color of the paper ash. "To all of them. To everyone who ever prayed. Jonah — that's what the old covenant *was*. That's what we stopped."

"Not now," Isolde said, and her voice was iron over something bleeding. "Now we climb. Grieve later. Argue theology later. *Move.*"

They moved.

* * *

Aldric Vane tasted the first god at moonrise.

It came to him through the sigil on his arm as a flood of sensation — the Justiciar's cold ledger-light, Korrath's ancient hunger for balance, pouring into the circle like wine into a cup. The Archivist chanted. The rings drank. Above, the tear in the sky widened another handspan, and far below, in the ruined temple beneath the city, Jonah Galloway felt it too: a god screaming as it was swallowed.

But Aldric had prepared for resistance. What he had not prepared for was the taste.

Because Korrath, even devoured, even dragged down the Protector's throat like a draught of liquid judgment, *spoke*. One word, pressed into the marrow of his bones as the god went down:

***Debt.***

Aldric laughed. It echoed strangely in the Spire, off walls that were beginning to remember they had once been a temple themselves.

"Yes," he said to the empty air, to the seven gods still struggling in the sky's wound, to the whole kneeling world. "You'll be paid. In the only coin that matters. Me."

* * *

They reached the undercroft stair as the second bell of the night rang wrong — a cracked, discordant note, as if the metal itself were afraid.

Isolde took the point. Behind her, Jonah and Sera climbed through the dark, and in the dark, Sera finally spoke the thing she'd been carrying since the safehouse.

"If we fail tonight," she said, "the texts die with us, and the clause dies with the texts, and there's never another chance. A thousand years of the Crown, and then nothing. No gods. No gifts. Just a king who owns the sky."

"Then we don't fail," Jonah said.

"That's not a plan, Jonah."

"It's the only plan I've ever had that worked." He caught her hand in the darkness. His grip was warm and calloused and steady. "Twelve years I sold charms that didn't work because I couldn't bear to sell ones that did. Tonight every one of those dead charms gets to mean something. Come on."

Behind them, boots on the lower stairs. Many boots. White hoods in the torchlight below, and Corvan Ashe's voice rising through the dark like smoke:

*"Vane! There's nowhere the named can run that the law won't follow!"*

Isolde looked back down into the torchlight, at the people she had loved for fifteen years, and found — to her own surprise — that she was no longer afraid of them. She drew her blade, and it caught the light falling through the cracks in the sky, and it gleamed not gold and not white but the plain honest grey of a thing sharpened for one purpose.

"The law follows," she agreed, loud enough for all of them to hear. "So does justice. Let's see which climbs faster."

And above them all, the moon — one night from full, one night from dead — began to rise through the tear in the sky, red as a wound, as the Spire's doors opened ahead of them onto the first of the nine tiers, and the war for heaven began on a staircase.
