## 18. Storming the Ninefold Court

The aqueduct mouth breathed cold air into my face, and I thought: *this is where I die.*

Not a dramatic thought. Not a prayer, even — though prayers were cheap coin tonight, with the sky over Ashfall split open like a gutted fish and eight gods bleeding light down onto the harbor. Just a plain merchant's arithmetic. I had run the numbers on my own death a hundred times in twelve years of selling dead charms, and the numbers had never come due. Tonight they would.

"Jonah." Isolde Vane stood at my shoulder in her burned commission coat, ash in her cropped hair, her witch-finder's iron still holstered at her hip because she'd sworn she wouldn't draw it against her own people again and hadn't yet decided whether we counted as her own people. "Say something inspiring."

"I was going to say *don't die*," I told her.

"That'll do."

Behind us, filling the flooded aqueduct from wall to wall, stood everything I'd spent nine chapters — nine weeks, it felt like nine years — scraping together out of the city's forgotten corners. Hedge-priests in their patched vestments. Charm-sellers with satchels full of charms that worked now, gods help us all, actually worked. Street-saints who'd never held anything sharper than a ladle. And scattered among them, in their grey coats with the sigils torn off, forty-odd renegade witch-finders who had looked at the order to arrest me and looked at the Protector's ritual tearing the heavens open above the Ninefold Court, and made what Isolde called *the only honest choice left in Ashfall.*

Marta the candlewoman squeezed my hand as she passed to the front rank. Old Tam, who'd sold me my first dead charm when I was seventeen and starving, nodded like we were meeting at market. A boy named Pip — fourteen, maybe fifteen, charm-seller's apprentice, the kind of kid I used to be — had tied a white feather to his collar and wouldn't meet my eye because he was afraid I'd send him back.

I didn't send him back. That decision is going to sit on my conscience longer than any other I made that night.

Above us, through the aqueduct's broken vaulting, the sky was wrong. The Lord Protector Aldric Veyne had begun his work at moonrise, and the moon itself hung in the wound he'd cut — swollen, red-gold, dripping light like yolk. Eight shapes moved behind that light. Vast. Struggling. I could feel them the way you feel a fight in the next room: not seeing it, just feeling the walls shudder.

He was unraveling them. Pulling their threads into himself, one by one, weaving divinity into a crown of meat.

"Tier by tier," I said, and my voice carried down the aqueduct the way it used to carry across battlefields, before Veyra went silent and took my voice's purpose with her. "Nine tiers. The covenant-table's at the top. Sera reaches the table, I reach Veyne. Nobody stops for glory, nobody stops for grief. We go up until we can't go up anymore, and then—" I faltered. Then what? Then an oath-bearer with no god and a clerk with no army did paperwork while the world ended?

Tam finished it for me. "Then we do what small folk always do. We make do."

I laughed. It came out ragged. "Forward, then. For the small gods."

And the aqueduct roared, and we went up.

* * *

The first tier was easy, which frightened me more than resistance would have.

We came up through the maintenance stair into the Court's outer colonnade — the tier where, in the old days, petitioners had waited with their offerings, where the covenant had touched mortal life most gently. Now it was empty except for the statues. Nine of them, one for each throne, and every statue was weeping. Not metaphor. Actual water, running from marble eyes, pooling on the flagstones, steaming faintly where divine pressure bled through the stone.

"They know," Marta whispered. "The gods know what he's doing up there."

"They know, and they can't stop it," Isolde said grimly. "Because stopping it needs an oath-bearer, and there's exactly one of those in Ashfall, and he's standing here counting statues."

A sound rolled down from above — not thunder, though it shook dust from the vaulting like thunder. A voice. Korrath's, I thought, the Justiciar's grinding bronze tones, but stretched thin, fraying at the edges, a bell cracking as it's struck. Words came through in fragments:

"*—oath-bearer—*"

"*—the table—*"

"*—HURRY—*"

Pip flinched. Old Tam put a hand on the boy's shoulder. "Gods begging now," Tam said softly. "Never thought I'd hear it."

Neither had I. Twelve years ago the gods had been distant majesty, and then silence, and then hunger. Begging was new. Begging meant they knew exactly how the arithmetic ran — that if Veyne finished his weaving, they wouldn't be defeated, they'd be *digested*. Ingredients. The last thing a god becomes is a flavor in someone else's power.

"Move," I said.

The second tier fought us.

Crown regulars — not witch-finders, real soldiers, the Protector's own household guard — had fortified the great stair between tiers, and they'd done it properly: barricades, crossbow lines, lantern-oil ready for burning. A sergeant's voice rang out over the colonnade: "In the name of the Lord Protector, disperse or be put down!"

Isolde stepped forward, and I will give her this: she tried. "This is Captain Isolde Vane! Your orders are unlawful — the Protector is committing heresy against the pantheon itself! Stand down and be counted among the loyal!"

Someone in the barricade laughed. It wasn't a cruel laugh. It was worse — a tired laugh, the laugh of men who'd already decided. "Captain Vane. They said you'd gone mad. Sky's falling and you're telling *us* about loyalty."

"Burn them out," the sergeant said.

They loosed. The first volley took three of ours — a hedge-priest of the River Mother I'd recruited two weeks ago, a Ropewalk rope-maker whose name I never learned, and old Tam.

Old Tam.

He went down with a bolt through the throat, and I felt it like a hook behind my sternum, because Tam had sold me my first charm when I was nothing, had taught me the patter, had been the last living link to who I'd been before the war. He died on the steps of the Ninefold Court holding a charm I'd made him — a luck-charm, a joke between us, dead for twelve years — and as I watched, the charm in his fist lit white and stayed lit.

Even dying, the small gods kept their promises now.

"Grenades!" Isolde roared, and our side answered. Charm-sellers don't fight like soldiers; we fight like shopkeepers, which means we fight dirty and we fight with inventory. Marta's people threw candles packed with blessed tallow that burned the color of prayer. The hedge-priests sang the Ropewalk working-song and the barricade's lantern-oil ignited on its own, without flame, leaping to the Crown's positions like it had remembered an older loyalty than the Crown's coin. Pip — little fool, brave little fool — darted through smoke to drag Tam's body clear, and I covered him with a shield-charm that cost me a year of hoarded power in ten seconds and was worth every drop.

The barricade broke. Some surrendered. Some ran. The sergeant died at his post, and Isolde closed his eyes, and we didn't stop, because stopping was death and grief could wait its turn in line behind survival.

Four tiers to go. Two dozen of ours lost already. The red moon overhead pulsed like a heartbeat, and somewhere above, another god's voice went quiet mid-cry, and I felt the whole city flinch.

* * *

The middle tiers were the Court's heart, and the Protector had turned its heart into a machine.

Tier five was the Hall of Petitions, where mortals had once bargained face-to-face with minor aspects of the Nine. Veyne's wardens had strung it with covenant-anchors — iron frames etched with the original contract's clauses, the ones the first Lord Protector had signed in blood four hundred years ago. Walking under them was like walking under knives. Every charm on my body dimmed. Every prayer in my head went flat.

"It's a filter," Sera would have said. She'd explained it to me a hundred times over the texts in the Clerk's Tower, patient as tide. "The anchors enforce the old terms. Under the old terms, devotion flows one way — up. Nothing returns. That's why your charms died, Jonah. That's why Veyra couldn't speak. The anchors make silence *lawful*."

But the anchors had a flaw, and the flaw was us.

Because the old covenant had been stolen, never freely given — and a stolen contract has no root. When our people walked under those iron frames singing the Ropewalk song, carrying charms given *freely*, charged by gods who'd chosen to answer — the anchors screamed. Iron glowed. One frame cracked clean down its centerline and the pressure in the hall shifted like a door opening in a drowned house.

"The terms don't fit anymore," I breathed, understanding arriving late and all at once. "Veyne's using a dead law to unmake living gods, and the law knows it's dead—"

"Less theology, more walking," Isolde said, but she was smiling, tight and fierce, and her witch-finder's iron finally came out of its holster — not pointed at us, pointed upward, toward the enemy.

Wardens met us on tier six, and these weren't soldiers. These were the Office of Purity's elite, Isolde's former comrades, led by a woman named Hesk whom Isolde had once called the best of us. They fought like witch-finders fight: precise, joyless, terribly efficient. We lost Marta on that tier. I felt her go — felt the candle-light of her go out — and I didn't stop, because she'd have haunted me forever if I'd stopped, and possibly she'll haunt me anyway.

It was Pip who broke the warden line, God forgive me. Hesk had Isolde pinned behind a pillar, blades locked, and the boy simply walked into the gap and held up his white feather and said, in a voice cracking with terror and certainty both: "The small gods are watching. Which side do you want to be on when they get free?"

And Hesk — veteran of twenty purges, ice all the way down — looked at the feather, and at the red moon bleeding through the shattered roof, and at the weeping statues, and lowered her blade.

"We're not fighting for him," she said to her wardens. "Look up. *Look up.* Whatever that thing above us is, it isn't the Protector anymore."

Half her people stood down. Half didn't, and the half that didn't bought our passage with blood on both sides, and tier seven opened before us like a wound.

Two tiers left. Above them, through the broken dome, I could see the covenant-table's silhouette on the ninth tier, and beside it, wrapped in spiraling threads of stolen light, the shape that had been Lord Protector Aldric Veyne.

He was beautiful. That's the horror I need to set down honestly, because history will lie about it otherwise. He was beautiful the way a supernova is beautiful, the way a forest fire seen from a safe distance is beautiful — eight gods' worth of divinity woven into a human frame, crowned in the red moon's light, his eyes gone the color of creation's first morning. And beneath the beauty, fraying at every seam, the man: mouth open in a scream of ecstasy or agony or both, hands knitting threads faster than flesh should move.

One god's voice remained loud enough to reach me. Korrath, the Justiciar, the last to resist, his bronze tones ground down to gravel:

"*OATH-BEARER. THE TABLE. THE TERMS. SHE IS COMING — KEEP HIS EYES FROM HER —*"

*Sera.* On the far stair, climbing alone toward the ninth tier with the true covenant-texts strapped to her back, small and steady and utterly exposed.

And between her and the table, turning from his weaving with the slow inevitability of a lighthouse beam, the Protector's gaze swept down through seven tiers of ruin and found me.

Found me, and *recognized* me.

"**Galloway**," the sky said, in a voice made of eight stolen choruses, and the whole Court shuddered. "**THE LAST OATH-BEARER. COME UP, THEN. I KEPT YOUR SEAT WARM.**"

I looked at Isolde. She looked at me. Around us, what remained of the charm-sellers' war — thirty-some souls, bleeding, singed, terrified, transfigured — waited on my word.

"He's expecting me," I said. "Which means he wants something. Which means whatever he wants, it's the one thing we can't afford to give him."

"Or," Isolde said, reloading her iron with hands that almost didn't shake, "it's a trap he built before you were born, and we walk into it anyway, because the alternative is watching him eat heaven."

"That's the spirit."

I started up the eighth-tier stair, and the small gods of Ashfall rose around me — in the lit charms, in the weeping stone, in the white feather at a boy's throat — and climbed with me.

Above, the Protector smiled with eight mouths, and began to pull the ninth thread.

Veyra's thread.

And every year of silence, every dead charm, every night I'd spent talking to a locked door — it all came due at once, because the moment he touched her, I felt her again. Fully. Terribly. *Jonah,* she said, from inside the unraveling, calm as she'd been the day she went quiet, *when you reach the table — don't restore what was. Write what should be. And whatever it costs —*

The connection tore as he pulled.

I ran.
