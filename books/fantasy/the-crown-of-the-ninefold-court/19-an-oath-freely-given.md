## 19. An Oath Freely Given

The covenant-table was older than the city above it.

Jonah had seen it only once before — twelve years ago, from the far side of a battlefield of light and screaming, when the Ninefold Court still burned with nine gods' presence and he had been too young and too afraid to look at it directly. Now it stood in a chamber beneath the ruined temple, vast as a ship's keel, hewn from black stone that drank the torchlight rather than reflecting it. Its surface was carved with the old terms in a script that predated Ashfall's walls, predates its kings, maybe its gods. The words had been gouged deep by hands that had wanted them permanent.

Blood still stained the grooves. It had gone brown over centuries, but Jonah could smell it — iron and incense and something older, like a storm about to break.

Sera stood on the table's far side, her clerk's robes scorched at the hem, ash in her hair. Behind her, through the shattered doors of the chamber, the sounds of the battle above came down in muffled pulses: charm-sellers and renegade witch-finders fighting tier by tier up through the Court while the Lord Protector unraveled eight waking gods somewhere overhead. The ceiling shuddered. Fine dust sifted down like gray snow.

"Jonah." Sera's voice was hoarse. She'd been shouting orders, or prayers, or both. "The texts are here. Everything we found in the Clerk's Tower. But the seal—" She pressed her hand flat against the table's edge. "The seal has to be an oath-bearer's willing sacrifice. There's no clause around it. No loophole. I've read every line three times."

"I know." He set his satchel on the stone. Inside it were the things he'd carried for twelve years: his dead charms, his company's name-tokens, and the small lead sigil of Veyra that had hung cold around his neck since the day she went silent. "I've known since the aqueducts."

"Then let me—" She started around the table, and he raised a hand, and she stopped.

"No." He said it gently. "You've already paid. You sold yourself to Korrath to buy us time. You brokered the silence that saved a city and hated yourself for twelve years for it. If anyone at this table has earned the right to walk away whole, it's you." He smiled, and it hurt his face. "Besides. Someone has to survive to write this all down properly. You're the only one of us who can spell 'covenant.'"

She laughed — a broken, wet sound — and didn't come around the table. Maybe she knew that if she touched him she wouldn't let him do it.

Above them, something enormous screamed. Not a god. A man. The Protector's voice, stretched thin and wrong across the vaulted dark, chanting syllables that made the blood-stained grooves in the table glow faintly red.

Jonah opened the satchel.

* * *

He had expected the bond to fight him.

Twelve years ago, in the Godswar's last hours, Veyra had poured herself into him — her sworn power, her covenant-gift, the strength that had let a hedge-charmer hold a shield-line against things out of nightmare. Then she had gone silent mid-battle, and the power had stayed in him like a coin clutched in a dead hand: warm, useless, and damning. He had spent twelve years resenting it. Selling charms that didn't work because the real magic sat coiled in his chest where he couldn't bear to touch it.

Now he reached for it deliberately, for the first time since the war.

It came up like a flood.

Not gently. Twelve years of banked divinity did not rise; it *detonated*, filling him shoulder to heel with light he had no vessel for, and he went to one knee on the black stone and heard himself make a sound that wasn't quite a scream. Every dead charm in his satchel lit at once. The candle stubs, the knotted threads, the little tin saints he'd sold to desperate people for coppers — all of them flared like festival lanterns, and for one absurd moment the chamber smelled of every prayer he'd ever failed to answer.

*Jonah.*

Her voice. Not fragments now, not the half-heard pressure behind a locked door. Veyra, whole, speaking directly into the marrow of him.

"You're here," he gasped. "You came."

*I never left. That was the cruelty of it. I stood behind the silence the whole time, watching you sell my name for bread.* A pause, and in it something that might have been grief, or shame, or love worn smooth by long handling. *You kept the tokens. All these years. Even the ones you cursed me for.*

"They were good work," he said. His voice shook. "Even if they were lies."

*They weren't lies. They were promises waiting for me to keep them. And I couldn't. Because keeping them would have meant agreeing to what they built down here.* Her presence turned toward the table, toward the blood-dark script, and Jonah felt her attention like a cold wind off a glacier. *Read the old terms aloud, oath-bearer. So the stone remembers what we're replacing.*

He braced both hands on the covenant-table and read.

"'Let every mortal soul kneel. Let devotion flow as tribute flows to a crown — owed, collected, compelled. Let the nine thrones be fed without end, and let refusal be heresy, and heresy be death.'" His voice cracked on the last line. "That's what you signed. That's what the first Lord Protector signed *for* everyone, in their blood, without asking."

*Yes.* Veyra's voice was very quiet. *And when I understood what I had consented to — that our hunger would be fed forever by souls who never chose us — I chose silence instead. They called it abandonment. It was the only honest thing any of us ever did.* A ripple through her presence, almost wry. *Though I notice you never thanked me for it.*

"I cursed your name for twelve years."

*I know. I listened to every word. Some of them were very creative.*

Despite everything — the shaking ceiling, the screaming above, the light pouring out of his own chest — Jonah laughed. Then he wiped his eyes with the back of his hand, because there was no one left in the world whose opinion of that mattered, and began to speak the new terms.

* * *

He spoke them slowly, the way Sera had taught him clauses should be spoken: plainly, completely, leaving nothing for later argument. The black stone drank each sentence as it fell, and where the old blood-script had glowed red, new lines surfaced pale as bone.

"No worship compelled," Jonah said. "No prayer extracted by fear or law or hunger. Let every god go unheard unless a mortal calls freely. Let every mortal go unanswered unless a god chooses freely. Devotion is a gift, given or withheld, and neither giver nor receiver owns the other for the giving."

The stone shivered. Somewhere far above, the Protector's chant faltered — he felt it, the way a man feels a change in the wind.

"No god enthroned," he continued. "No throne above another throne, no throne above the street. The Ninefold Court stands empty from this hour. Let the small gods sit where they have always sat — in doorways, in kitchens, in the Ropewalk and the harbor quarter and the flooded aqueducts. Let the great gods walk among mortals as guests, not masters, or stay beyond reach entirely, as they choose."

"And the price?" Sera whispered. She had read the texts. She knew there had to be a price.

"The seal," Jonah said, "is paid by the last living oath-bearer, willingly, in full."

He lifted the lead sigil of Veyra over his head. It was warm now for the first time in twelve years — warm as a living hand.

"Twelve years of sworn power," he said to the stone, to the listening dark, to whatever remained of the nine. "Every drop she gave me on that field. Every miracle I hoarded and resented and was too much of a coward to spend. I pour it back. Not into a throne. Into the *terms*. Let it be the first payment on a covenant no one was forced to sign — and let my surrender prove it can be done."

*Jonah.* Veyra's voice, suddenly urgent. *Wait—*

"I'm not waiting," he said. "I've been waiting twelve years. This is the part where I stop."

He pressed the sigil to the table's center, into the deepest groove of ancient blood, and let go.

* * *

It did not feel like dying. It felt like being *emptied* — like a river discovering it had been a dam all along.

The power left him in a single long exhalation. Twelve years of banked divinity roared down through the sigil into the black stone, and the new pale script blazed white, and the old red script — the kneeling-terms, the tribute-terms, the compulsion written in the first Protector's stolen blood — began to burn away like fog off morning water. Jonah felt every year of it going: the shield-strength that had held the line at Cael Ford, the healing warmth he'd never dared use, the small quiet knowing that had told him which buyers lied and which simply despaired. It all poured out of him and into the words, and the words took it greedily, and he sagged against the table feeling suddenly, enormously mortal. Light as paper. Cold as clay.

*Stop,* Veyra said. *Jonah, stop — you're giving more than the bond. You're giving yourself—*

"It's willing," he managed. "That's the whole point. It has to be *willing*."

Above them the ceiling split.

Not collapsed — *split*, along seams no mason had cut, and through the crack poured the Protector's working: a column of grasping darkness studded with eight diminishing lights, the waking gods being reeled in like fish on a hook, their freedom unraveling thread by thread into the Crown's hungry ritual. The darkness reached down into the covenant-chamber, questing, tasting the new terms being written — and recoiled, because the terms were not yet sealed, and unsealed things could still be contested.

The Protector's will came down the column like a landslide.

Jonah felt it hit the half-written covenant and strain against it, ancient law grappling newer law, and he understood with terrible clarity that he had nothing left to hold it with. He was empty. He was a charm-seller with dead stock and a bad knee, kneeling at a table he could no longer fuel, watching the oldest tyranny in the world reach down to snuff the newest hope in it.

Then Veyra stepped out of him.

There was no other way to describe it. She had lived folded inside his silence for twelve years, and now she unfolded — a shape of gray-white light, tall as a doorway, featureless except for the sense of patient, tired, immovable kindness that Jonah had followed through a war and mourned for a decade. She was smaller than he had imagined a god. She was exactly as large as he had always needed her to be.

*Veyra, don't,* he said, though he had no voice left to say it with, so he said it with everything else.

*The terms need a door,* she answered. *Something must stand in the gap between the sealed and the unsealed until the ink dries. Something divine. Something* willing. *Do you see, Jonah? This is why I stayed silent all those years — so that when the choosing came, it would be mine. Freely given. The first gift of the new covenant, made by a god who owes mortals nothing and gives everything anyway.*

"You'll be spent," he said. "Completely. There won't be enough left to—"

*I know what I am buying.* Her light bent toward him, and he felt — impossibly, unmistakably — her hand on his cheek, the way it had been the day he swore his first oath, young and terrified in the mud at Cael Ford. *A door held open long enough for a better world to walk through. Name me one merchant in Ashfall who ever got a better price than that.*

The Protector's darkness struck.

Veyra met it standing. She spread herself across the whole broken ceiling of the chamber — a god become an arch, become a lintel, become a held breath — and the darkness broke against her like surf against a seawall, again and again, and each wave took something from her that did not come back. Her edges frayed. Her light dimmed from silver to pewter to the gray of ash. And through all of it she held, and the new terms burned whiter, sealing line by line, and Jonah knelt beneath his god's sacrifice and pounded the black stone with his fist and wept like he had not wept even for his dead company.

*Sera,* Veyra said, and her voice was thinner now, stretched like cloth. *The final clause. Speak it. An oath-bearer's sacrifice seals the terms — but a witness must name them true. You kept the books for the old lie. Keep them for the new truth.*

Sera was already moving. She climbed onto the covenant-table itself, ash-haired and scorched, and laid her clerk's seal — the Court's own insignia, the mark of the machine that had bought the gods' silence — against the newest line of pale script.

"I, Sera Ilvane, chief covenant-clerk of Ashfall, witnessed and recorded," she said, and her voice did not shake at all. "The terms are spoken. The price is paid. The seal is willing. Let it be *true*."

The table flared.

White light ran the length of the black stone like fire down a fuse, and every groove of ancient blood-script burned away to nothing, and the new terms sank deep into the rock as though they had always been there, waiting to be remembered. The covenant-table of the Ninefold Court — the oldest object in the world, the anchor of nine thrones — became, in the space of a heartbeat, a blank slate bearing one simple sentence in a script any child could read:

*Devotion is a gift.*

And Veyra, holding the door, finally let go.

* * *

She came apart the way snow comes off a roof in spring — gradually, then all at once, drifting down through the shattered ceiling in flakes of gray-white light. The Protector's darkness, finding nothing left to break against, recoiled up the column with something that sounded almost like a scream of frustration. Above, distantly, Jonah heard the eight gods' unraveling stop — heard the Protector's chant stutter, falter, lose its grip on powers that were no longer bound by anything at all.

But Jonah wasn't listening to the sky. He was kneeling in the falling light with his hands cupped, trying to catch her, catching nothing.

"Veyra." His voice was wrecked. "Veyra, please. Twelve years I wanted you silent and twelve years I wanted you back, and now that I have you back you're—"

*Jonah.* Barely a whisper now. Barely a warmth. *Look at what you wrote. Look at what it says. No worship compelled. No god enthroned.* The last of her light gathered near his face, and he would have sworn — he would swear it to his dying day — that she was smiling. *Which means that if I choose to spend myself here, no one commanded it. No covenant demanded it. It is a gift. The first one. Do you understand what that makes me?*

"Don't," he begged. "Don't make me write your eulogy too. I'm not a novelist. I'm a charm-seller. My prose is terrible."

*It always was.* The warmth brushed his cheek one last time. *Bury my name beside your company. Not in a temple. In the ground, where the small things grow. And Jonah — the candle trick. The one the child prayed for, at your stall, the night I woke. I have wanted to tell you for months—*

"What? Tell me what?"

But the light was gone. The chamber was dim and ordinary and full of dust, and the covenant-table gleamed with its single plain sentence, and somewhere above them the Lord Protector was learning — loudly, catastrophically — that eight unbound gods do not forgive the hand that tried to leash them.

Sera slid down the side of the table and landed hard on the floor beside him. For a moment neither of them spoke. Then she put her ash-gray head on his shoulder, and he put his arm around her, and together they watched the last flakes of Veyra settle on the black stone and vanish, the way frost vanishes in the morning — not destroyed, just returned to the air, to the water, to everything.

"She held the door," Sera said finally.

"Yeah." Jonah's throat ached. "She always did. We just never saw the door before."

He looked down at his hands. Empty. No bond, no banked divinity, no sworn power — nothing left in him but a bad knee, a merchant's arithmetic, and a shop full of dead charms that were, as of tonight, no longer dead at all. Every charm in Ashfall was awake now. Every hedge-priest, every street-saint, every knot-tier and candle-burner who had ever prayed to something small and close. The new covenant had opened the door for all of them.

And above them, through the split ceiling, the eight freed gods were turning their attention — vast, wounded, furious, and for the first time in an age *unowned* — toward the mortal who had rewritten their world.

The floor trembled. Sera looked up. "Jonah," she said quietly. "They're coming down here. All of them. To see what you made."

He rose, knees cracking, and faced the stairwell as the first of the nine tiers began to fill with light.

"Well," he said. "Let's hope they read."
