## 1. The Book That Knew Tonight

The dead did not shout. That was the first thing Yara Halloran had learned, back when they were small enough to hide beneath their grandmother's workbench and listen to the offcuts whisper in the shavings bin. The dead murmured. They repeated themselves the way rain repeats itself against a roof—patient, unhurried, certain that eventually someone would open a window and let them in.

Yara had opened that window long ago, and now there was no closing it.

They sat at their bench in the workshop behind the Halloran cottage, lamplight pooling yellow on the whale-ivory blank before them, and listened to Maren Ostwick tell her story for the four hundredth time.

*The cold took him gently,* she said, in the voice Yara had coaxed from her fingerbone three days ago—a thin voice, papery, like wind through reeds. *Tell them the cold took him gently. He was smiling. He was smiling when he went.*

"I know," Yara murmured, turning the bone with a carver's cradle of worn leather. "I'm putting it in."

The memorial piece was nearly finished: a palm-sized tablet of polished ivory, its face worked with a man standing in a doorway of light, his hand raised not in farewell but in welcome. Around the border Yara had cut Maren's own words in the old valley script, the letters small and even as stitches. *He was smiling when he went.* When Maren's daughter came tomorrow to collect it and held it in her two hands, she would hear nothing—people who weren't carved-for heard only silence—but she would feel something. A warmth. A sense of being told a truth kindly. That was all the dead ever wanted, in Yara's experience. To be believed.

Outside, the Valley of Stillness lay under its usual hush. No birds sang here; no wind moved the pines on the crater rim. Magic could not cross into the valley—it pooled at the borders like water against glass and slid away—and so the valley had become, over nine generations, the place people came to be forgotten by the world. Debtors. Deserters. Grief-mad widows. Women fleeing husbands, men fleeing gods. And one bone-carver fleeing nothing in particular, except perhaps the suspicion that they had never quite belonged anywhere, including here.

The stillness was the valley's whole character. Farmers timed their planting by it. Children were taught that if they ever heard a sound with no source—a hum in the stones, a sigh in the well-water—they should come inside and bar the door until an elder pronounced the valley clean again. It had never once been unclean. Not in living memory. The Stillness simply *was*, the way stone was, the way death was.

Yara blew a curl of ivory dust from the finished border and held the memorial up to the lamp.

"There," they told Maren. "Gently. Smiling. Everyone will know."

*Thank you,* said the fingerbone, resting in its cloth on the corner of the bench. *Thank you. Thank you. Thank—*

"Rest now." Yara wrapped the bone in linen and tucked it into the cedar box where client-bones slept between commissions. The murmuring faded to a thread, then to nothing, and the workshop settled into the ordinary quiet of a place where quiet was the law.

It was then, in that settled quiet, that someone knocked on the door.

* * *

Peddlers did not come to the valley often—the pass was treacherous and the customers poor—but when they came, they came in autumn, before the rim roads iced. This one arrived at dusk, which was strange, and alone, which was stranger, leading a mule whose coat was stiff with dried salt.

Salt. Yara noticed it from the doorway and felt the first cold thread go through them, though they couldn't have said why. There was no salt for three hundred miles. The sea was a story the valley told its children along with stories of dragons: something vast and far away that had nothing to do with you.

The peddler was a narrow man, weathered brown, with the watery eyes of someone who'd spent years squinting across distances. His cart smelled of tar and old canvas.

"Bone-carver?" he asked. "They said down in Millbrook there's a Halloran does work with the dead."

"I do work *for* the dead," Yara said. "Or rather, they do it for me. What are you selling?"

The peddler smiled, and there was something wrong with the smile—too eager, too relieved, like a man handing off a weight he'd carried too far. He rummaged in the cart and brought out a book.

Even before he held it out, Yara could smell it: brine and rot and deep cold water. The cover was leather gone green-black with damp, swollen hard as driftwood. Water had gotten into it somewhere and gotten out again, leaving every page rippled and stiff, the ink blurred at the edges of each leaf as though the words themselves had been swimming.

"Found it in a wreck-crate," the peddler said. "Coast goods, third-hand, don't ask me how it traveled this far inland. Can't read half of it myself. But a book-man in Millbrook said—" he lowered his voice, and the eagerness sharpened—"said it's a prophecy book. Old. Written a hundred year past, he reckoned, by some sea-priest gone mad or gone holy, depending who tells it."

"I don't buy prophecies," Yara said. "I bury people. It's quieter work."

"It's copper," the peddler said quickly. "One copper, and it's yours. I'll be honest with you, carver—I've had it a month and I don't sleep right with it in my cart. Things shift in the night. Pages turn. I wake up and it's lying open, always open, like it's trying to show me something." He pushed it toward them. "One copper. Please."

There it was again—that thread of cold. Yara looked at the book and felt, absurdly, that it was looking back. Which was nonsense. Nothing looked back in the Valley of Stillness. Nothing *could*. Magic died at the rim like a moth against a closed window.

But the book had crossed that rim somehow. The book was sitting here, in the magic-dead heart of the world, smelling of drowned ships and refusing to be ordinary.

Yara paid the copper mostly to be rid of the man's eyes.

* * *

That night, with the fire banked and the cottage dark around them, Yara turned the pages by candlelight.

The script was archaic coastal common, the letters tall and slanted, and the water damage made a ruin of every third line. It was exactly what the peddler had claimed: a hundred-year-old prophecy cycle, the kind tide-priests used to chant on headlands before the coast kingdoms burned the last of them. Yara read with the detached interest of someone reading another family's letters. Drowned queens. Sundered crowns. A war beneath the sea that had ended nothing, only paused, the way a held breath pauses.

Then the pages began to keep dates.

The prophet had written the cycle as a calendar of returnings—one entry per generation, each naming a sign and a season. Yara skimmed them. *In the year of the gray harvest, a child born with salt in the blood.* *In the year of two winters, the bells of the deep will ring unheard.* On and on, a century of entries marching forward through the rippled pages, each one further into the future than the last, until—

Until the final entry. The last page that held writing at all.

Yara stopped.

The date at the top of the page was tonight's date. Not the month. Not the season. The day itself, set down a hundred years ago in a dead priest's slanted hand, waiting under the water-damage like a coin at the bottom of a well.

And beneath the date, in lines the water had mercifully failed to blur:

*On this night the Stillness shall break, and out of the valley that cannot hold magic shall come the bearer. Seek the Halloran bone-carver, who hears the dead through their hands. The crown that was sundered shall find its bearer, and the bearer shall choose: the kingdom raised from the deep, or the coastlands given to it. One crown. Two thrones. One throat.*

Yara read it twice. Three times. Then they laughed—a short, ugly sound in the empty room.

"The Halloran bone-carver," they said aloud. "Of course. Every village has a Halloran. Half the coast is Hallorans. My grandmother was a Halloran, her mother was a Halloran—" They stopped, because that wasn't true, actually. Their grandmother had been a Halloran by marriage, and no one had ever said much about where she'd married *from*, and their mother had gone into the sea off the rim rocks before Yara was old enough to remember her face, and—

No. They shut that door firmly. Prophecies worked on fools the way hooks worked on fish: they needed only one soft place. Yara Halloran carved bones for grieving farmers and kept their head down and their name small. Coincidence was cheaper than destiny and twice as reliable.

They closed the book. Set it on the shelf. Banked the fire. Went to bed telling themselves, in the firm tone they used on clients who wanted to believe their dead were angry instead of merely unfinished, that a waterlogged book was a waterlogged book, and a common surname was a common surname, and tomorrow they would deliver Maren's memorial and life would continue being small and safe and silent.

They almost believed it.

* * *

Yara woke at moonset because the silence changed.

Anyone else would have slept through it. But Yara had spent thirty years inside the valley's stillness the way a fish spends its life inside water—they knew its texture the way they knew their own pulse. And at moonset, that texture *shivered*.

It was not a sound. It was the absence of a sound, bending. Like a note held so long it begins to waver. The stillness of the valley—nine generations deep, older than any grave Yara had ever dug—trembled once, twice, and on the third tremble, broke open just wide enough to let one thing through.

A murmur.

Not from the cedar box. Not from any bone Yara had ever carved. It rose out of the ground itself, out of the stones of the cottage foundation, out of the well-water in the yard—a vast, low, many-throated murmur, like a crowd heard through a wall, like the sea heard through the hull of a ship. Voices without bodies, rising through the magic-dead earth that had never once in living memory allowed a single voice to pass.

Yara sat up in the dark, heart slamming, and the murmur resolved—not into words, not yet, but into direction. It came from the north. From beyond the rim, from the far coastlands, from the drowned places the prophecy book named. And underneath the crowd-roar of it, one voice was clearer than the rest, closer, ragged and human and desperate, moving fast toward the valley through the night.

Toward *them*.

On the shelf, the waterlogged book fell open on its own.

Yara did not remember crossing the room. They stood before the shelf with the candle shaking in their hand, staring down at the final page, at the date that was today, at the words that had seemed so laughable six hours ago.

*On this night the Stillness shall break.*

Outside, far off past the pine-dark fields, something cried out—a human voice, wordless, exhausted, coming closer.

Yara reached for their boots.
