## 3. The Priest Who Lost His God

Yara had never run from anything in their life, and it showed.

Three days north of the valley, their boots were ruined, their hands were blistered around the strap of a bag that held everything they owned, and the coral shard rode against their sternum like a second heartbeat. The messenger's voice still lingered in the shard—Yara could feel it when they pressed their palm flat to their chest at night, a faint wet murmur, *carry it north, carry it north*—and they were beginning to suspect that whatever wanted them found did not need to search. It only needed to listen for the thing they carried.

The road climbed out of the farmland into grey pine country. The villages thinned. The people in them watched Yara pass with the flat, incurious stare of folk who had learned that strangers meant trouble, and by the third evening Yara was glad of it, because being invisible had been a skill their whole life and tonight they needed it more than ever.

They made camp in the ruin of a wayshrine, half-swallowed by the hillside, its tide-motifs worn to suggestion: a wave here, a coiled serpent there, a woman's face eroded to a smooth blank oval. Yara sat with their back against the altar stone and ate hard bread and told themselves the prickling along their spine was only cold.

Then the stream below the shrine went silent.

Not slowed. Not frozen. *Silenced*, mid-murmur, as if something had put its hand over the water's mouth.

Yara knew sound. They had spent their life listening to the dead speak through bone and ivory and whale-ear, and they knew what it meant when a living sound died without cause. They were on their feet before they understood why, bag snatched up, and the mist came off the stream like breath off a drowned man's lips.

They were tall, the shapes in the mist. Tall and wrong, draped in weed and brine, faces like things left too long underwater—softened, slumped, the features sliding as if the sea were still trying to wear them. Tide-wraiths. Yara had carved memorial bones for sailors lost to the deep, had heard the dead of the sea through their work all their life, but hearing was not this. This was the dead standing up out of their element and walking into yours.

The nearest one opened a mouth full of dark water and said, in a chorus of voices that scraped like hulls on rock:

***Bearer.***

Yara ran.

They didn't get far. The mist closed ahead of them too, and the wraiths came gliding between the pines without bending a single branch, and Yara's heel caught a root and they went down hard, the bag spilling, the coral shard skittering across the shrine's cracked flagstones—

—and light fell across the clearing. Not lantern-light. A cold blue radiance, sourceless, that made the wraiths recoil like smoke from a flame.

A voice spoke from the treeline. Old, cracked, furious, and utterly unafraid.

"*In the name of the Drowned and the Deep-Borne, I abjure you. You were given rest. Take it.*"

The wraiths screamed—a sound like a whole fleet going down—and came apart into salt spray and drifting weed. The mist collapsed inward, drained away down the hillside, and the stream found its voice again.

Yara lay on the flagstones, heart slamming, and watched an old man walk out of the pines.

He was gaunt and grey-bearded, wrapped in a travel-stained cloak over what had once been fine vestments, now patched with mismatched wool. Around his neck hung a tide-glass pendant on a broken chain—the symbol of the Tidebound priesthood, defaced, the glass deliberately scored with a single deep line. He carried no weapon except a censer on a short chain, still smoking with that cold blue light.

He looked at Yara. Then at the coral shard lying in the dirt. Then he went very still, the way men do when a prayer is answered in a form they didn't ask for.

"Well," he said. "You're real, then."

* * *

Brother Fenwick had been exiled for eleven years, and in eleven years he had learned three things: keep moving, keep praying, and never tell anyone what you know until you're certain they'll believe you.

He broke the third rule within the hour.

It was the shard that did it. He'd seen the shape of it in the wraith-light—that particular white, that particular spiral, cut not by tool but by growth, grown around a secret the way a pearl grows around a wound. He had spent thirty years in the archives of the Tidewatch studying drawings of such things. There were supposed to be seven. All lost. All keys.

And now one was sitting in the palm of a mud-spattered stranger with salt-grey eyes and the look of someone who had never in their life been asked to be important.

"Sit," Fenwick said, and built a fire because his hands needed something holy-adjacent to do. "Eat. And then you're going to tell me how a bone-carver from the Stillness Valley comes to be carrying a key of the Sundered Court, pursued by wraiths that should not be able to cross the tide-line."

"I don't know what any of those words mean," Yara said. But they sat. They always sat when the dead were involved; it was the closest thing to piety they had.

So Fenwick told them. About the Tidebound Court, the parliament of the deep that had ruled the sunken kingdom of Mereth-Nuai before the Sundering. About the drowned queen, Vessaline, whose crown had ended the war and sunk her kingdom in the same act. About the prophecy books, the hundred-year calendars, the passages every priest was made to memorize and every priest above the rank of cantor was made to disbelieve.

"And the heresy?" Yara asked quietly. They'd noticed the scored pendant. They noticed everything; it was the trade.

Fenwick fed the fire a stick. "The Church teaches that Queen Vessaline sacrificed herself. That she took up the crown willingly, sang the Sundering, and dragged her own kingdom beneath the waves to save the coastlands from her court's war." He looked up. "I found the tide-ledgers of her own chamberlain. Sealed records, hidden in the ossuary vaults. She didn't take the crown willingly. Her court drugged her wine, bound her with her own ritual, and put it on her while she screamed. The Sundering wasn't a sacrifice. It was a murder. The founding act of our faith was ten thousand souls being locked in a prison so a handful of traitors could rule what was left above the water."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, the stream talked to itself, indifferent.

"They defrocked me," Fenwick said. "Scored my glass, burned my writings, put me out past the tide-line. Eleven years I've walked the coast telling anyone who'd listen, which is to say no one." He turned the defaced pendant over in his fingers. "And then the valley shakes, the wraiths wake, and a Halloran bone-carver comes north carrying one of the seven keys. So you see why I'm inclined to believe in you whether you believe in yourself or not."

"The book said bearer," Yara said. Their voice had gone flat, careful, the voice they used for clients' grief. "It named tonight's date. It named me. I thought—" They stopped. Started again. "I carve bones. I hear the dead through them. That's all I am. That's all I've ever been allowed to be. And some book a century old wants to make me a *crown-bearer* for a war I didn't fight."

"Yes," Fenwick said. "That's exactly how it feels. I remember."

Yara looked at him sharply, and he held the look, and something passed between them that neither of them would have called kinship yet, because neither of them trusted anything that arrived that easily.

* * *

They walked north together the next morning, because Yara could not fight wraiths and Fenwick could not carry the key himself—it had refused his touch with a shock like icy water, and he'd sworn creatively about it—and because, though neither said it aloud, two cast-out people walking the same road are less alone than one.

Fenwick talked. Yara listened, and carved.

That was the arrangement, discovered by accident on the first night: Yara's gift worked best with their hands busy, and Fenwick had questions that only the dead could answer, and Yara had decided—if deciding was the word for what happened when your fear got larger than your caution—that the dead deserved to be heard even when what they said was terrible.

They stopped at a cliffside ossuary on the fourth day, a hollowed chapel stacked floor to rafters with the bones of the sea-lost, sailors given to the deep and washed back up again, generations of them. Fenwick wanted to consult the old tide-ledgers rumored to be cached there. Yara wanted nothing except to pass through quickly, but the place hummed. The whole chapel hummed, a low chord of waiting, and Yara's hands ached the way they always ached near unspoken grief.

There was a whale vertebra on the altar, ancient, polished by centuries of pilgrim palms. Yara picked it up without thinking. Their knife was in their hand before they'd decided anything.

"What are you doing?" Fenwick asked.

"Listening," Yara said, and cut the first line.

The bone opened like a door.

The sound that filled the ossuary was not one voice but a city's worth—gulls, bells, fishmongers crying the morning catch, children running on wet stone—and beneath it all a woman's voice singing, high and clear and *afraid*, and Fenwick dropped to his knees among the bones of the sea-lost with tears already on his face because he knew that song, every priest knew that song, it was the Lament of the Sundering, and it had never once in history sounded like this. Not triumphant. Not willing.

*They are putting it on me,* the dead queen said through the whalebone, through Yara's carving, through ten thousand throats at once. *My lords, my loves, my council, what are you—I did not consent, I DID NOT—*

The song broke. The city sounds broke. And underneath, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, came a grinding, endless pressure-sound, the sound of ten thousand souls pressed into a ring of iron and coral, each one still awake, each one still *named*, none of them ever spoken to again.

The vertebra went quiet in Yara's hands.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Sea-light shifted through the ossuary's seaward window, laying pale bars across the stacked dead.

"It's not a crown," Yara said finally. Their voice came out very small in all that silence. "Everyone keeps saying *crown*. It's not a crown. It's a prison. Ten thousand souls, Fenwick. It's holding ten thousand souls, and it has been for a hundred years, and I can *hear them*."

Fenwick knelt among the bones with his scored pendant hanging dead and silent against his chest, and felt the last pillar of his old faith come down—not in ruin, but the way a wall comes down when you finally admit there was never anything behind it.

"Then we're not running from the Tidebound Court," he said slowly. "We're running toward the truth they killed a queen to bury. And the key you're carrying—" He looked at the white coral at Yara's throat, and his face went grey. "Yara. The keys don't open doors. The keys open *her*. The seven shards together can unbind the crown—or set it free. And there are people on both sides of the water who have spent a century making sure no one ever gets to choose which."

Outside, far off across the grey water of the strait, a ship's bell rang three times—the signal, though neither of them knew it yet, of a smuggler's runner watching the coast, and of a price about to be named.
