## 4. What the Bones Remember

The ossuary had been a chapel once, before the cliff took half of it and the sea took the rest of the congregation. Fenwick had known it in his old life—had sung vespers here, back when he still believed the Tidefather listened. Now the vaulted ceiling gaped open to a bruised evening sky, and the alcoves that had held the honored dead held only wind, salt, and the long silence of a god who had never answered.

He built the fire small, in the hollow where the altar had been pried out, and told himself the shaking in his hands was the cold.

It was not entirely the cold.

Across the ruin, Yara Halloran sat cross-legged among the bones of strangers, sorting them with the tenderness other people reserved for children or lovers. They had done nothing but sort since they arrived—skull from rib, rib from vertebra, laying each piece on a cloth as if introducing themselves. Fenwick had watched tide-priests handle relics with less reverence, and those relics were supposed to be holy.

"You're staring," Yara said, without looking up.

"I'm observing."

"You're judging." They set a femur down with a soft click. "Everyone who watches me work is either judging or afraid. Sometimes both."

Fenwick fed a stick to the fire. "I was a priest for twenty-two years, Halloran. Judging and fearing are most of the job."

That earned him something almost like a smile. It was strange, he thought, how quickly exile made strangers into companions. Three days north along the cliffs, pursued by things that wore the sea like a shroud, and already they had developed the shorthand of the hunted: few words, shared fire, an unspoken agreement not to discuss the coral shard wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of Yara's pack.

Yara lifted a bone from the pile—a great concave disc, honeycombed and heavy, wide as a serving platter.

"Whale," Fenwick said. "Vertebra. There are herds that pass the strait in autumn. The deep-singers. My order held that their bones were the Tidefather's first scripture."

"Did he write anything good?"

"He wrote," Fenwick said, "in a language we were never permitted to translate."

Yara turned the vertebra over in their hands. Their thumbs found the channels and ridges the way a blind woman finds a familiar doorframe. And Fenwick saw it happen—the thing he had heard whispered about in seminary halls and dismissed as dockside superstition. Yara's breathing slowed. Their eyes lost focus, going soft and inward, and their fingers began to move against the bone's surface as though reading braille written by someone still alive.

The knife came out. Small, curved, worn to a crescent by years of use.

"What are you doing?" Fenwick asked.

"Listening first." Yara's voice had gone distant. "Carving second. You can't carve until you know what wants saying."

"And what does it want saying?"

Yara didn't answer. The knife touched bone.

* * *

Fenwick would spend the rest of his life trying to describe what happened next, and he would fail every time.

The sound began beneath hearing—a pressure in the chest, a fullness in the ears, the sensation of standing too close to a struck bell. Then it resolved, and it was a voice, and it was many voices, layered like sediment, like tide over tide:

*—cold, so cold, the water came up the temple steps and no one would leave the queen, no one would leave her—*

*—they said the crown would save us, they put it on her head while she slept, she never consented, she never—*

*—my daughter was on the eastern terraces. If anyone finds the eastern terraces. Her name was Ilset. Her name was Ilset—*

Fenwick was on his feet without remembering standing. The fire seemed to shrink from the sound. Wind poured through the broken roof and the voices rode it, dozens of them, hundreds, speaking over one another in the accents of a kingdom that had been dead for three hundred years—the drowned capital of Nereth-Maru, the pride of the Sundered Sea, gone beneath the waves in a single night that every priest of his order had been taught to call *the Tidefather's Judgment.*

Yara's hands kept carving. Steady. Unhurried. Tears ran down their face, but their voice, when it came, was calm as a tide-table.

"They're showing me. Fenwick—they're *showing* me. The night it sank. The water didn't come from the sea."

"What do you mean, it didn't come from the—"

"The gates." Yara's knife traced a spiral in the vertebra's crown. "The harbor gates were closed from the inside. Someone sealed the city and then—" Their breath caught. "Oh. Oh, no. Then they brought the crown out of the deep vaults. They put it on her while she slept. She woke up drowning."

The voices swelled. Fenwick pressed his hands over his ears and it did nothing; the sound wasn't traveling through air. It was traveling through him, through the marrow the way it traveled through bone.

*She never consented,* the voices said, in a hundred throats. *She never consented. She never consented.*

"The heresy," Fenwick whispered. He had spoken it once, in the great nave of the Tidemark Cathedral, before the conclave and the inquisitors, and it had cost him everything—his orders, his god, his name in any book worth burning. "I said the queen was murdered. I said the court drowned their own city to hide it. They defrocked me for blasphemy." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "They should have defrocked me for being *late.*"

"Fenwick." Yara's eyes focused, found his. "There's more. There's something about the crown itself. They keep trying to tell me and it keeps—it keeps hurting them to say it. Like the word is too heavy."

"Then don't make them say it."

"They *want* to." Yara bent back over the vertebra. The knife moved faster now, cutting deep, and the whale-bone began to glow faintly along the cut lines, a pale green-gold light like sunlight through shallow water. "They've waited three hundred years for someone who could hear. Do you understand? Three hundred years of priests praying *at* them and no one ever praying *to* them. No one ever asking."

The light brightened. The voices gathered themselves, sorted, aligned—and then spoke together, one voice made of ten thousand, and the ossuary rang like the inside of a bell:

***THE CROWN IS NOT A THRONE.***

Fenwick's knees hit stone.

***THE CROWN IS NOT A SYMBOL. THE CROWN IS NOT A GIFT. THE CROWN IS A LOCK, AND WE ARE WHAT IT LOCKS.***

The vertebra blazed. In its glow Fenwick saw—not imagined, *saw*—the shape of the thing they spoke of: a circlet of white coral and drowned gold, wrought in the image of breaking waves, beautiful the way a snare is beautiful. And threaded through it, packed into it, compressed beyond cruelty into it—

Souls. Thousands upon thousands of souls, folded into the crown's metal like letters into a bottle, each one still awake, each one still screaming silently, for three hundred years.

"When the city drowned," Yara said, and their voice shook now, "the court couldn't let the people go. Dead subjects are still subjects. So the court-mages wove every soul in Nereth-Maru into the crown. Ten thousand. Maybe more. The queen wears it because the queen is the lock, and the lock cannot open while it's worn, and it cannot be set down because—" Yara stopped.

"Because why?" Fenwick breathed.

"Because whoever takes it off becomes the lock instead." Yara looked up at him, and in the bone-light their face was terrible and young. "That's the inheritance, Fenwick. That's what I'm heir to. Not a kingdom. A prison. And everyone who's ever worn it has been both jailer and the jailer's first prisoner."

The light guttered. The voices receded like a tide going out, but they left things behind in the silence—fragments, promises, pleas:

*—find the eastern terraces—*

*—her name was Ilset—*

*—tell the queen we forgive her, tell her we forgave her the first night, tell her—*

And then, last, quietest, in a voice like deep water over a drowned bell:

*—she is coming north to find you, little carver. Our sister. Our jailer's daughter. Be kinder to her than we could be.*

Yara set down the knife. The vertebra went dark. In the sudden ordinary firelight it was only a bone again, scored with spirals, and Yara Halloran was only a tired person sitting in a ruin, wiping their face with a salt-stiff sleeve.

Fenwick realized he was still on his knees. He stayed there a moment longer, because twenty-two years of priesthood had taught him exactly one posture for encountering the divine, and this—this drowned chorus, this unwilling congregation, this heresy confirmed in whale-bone and green-gold light—was the closest he had come to his god since the day they took his vestments.

"It's real," he said finally. "All of it. Everything I was cast out for saying. It was all true."

"Yes."

"And you're the heir to it."

"I didn't ask to be."

"No one asks for anything true." Fenwick rose, joints protesting, and crossed to the fire. His hands had stopped shaking. That frightened him more than the shaking had. "You understand what this means, Halloran. The Tidebound Court isn't hunting you because of a prophecy. They're hunting you because the crown needs a new lock, and their queen is wearing thin. And your sister—"

"Don't." Yara's voice went flat. "Don't say her name tonight."

"She'll say yours soon enough. Admirals announce themselves." Fenwick crouched and fed the fire another stick, watching the flames take it. "We need a ship. We need to cross the Sundered Strait and reach the sunken capital before the Court reaches you, and there is exactly one smuggler on this coast mad enough to make that run this time of year, and she will charge us a price we will hate."

Yara almost smiled again. "You know her?"

"Odessa Vess owes me a debt and hates me for it. It's the firmest foundation I know for a partnership." He glanced back at the darkened vertebra, at the spirals cut into three-hundred-year-old grief. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we buy passage with money we don't have, and after that—" He hesitated.

"After that?"

"After that, little carver, you may need to start practicing." He nodded at the bone. "Ten thousand names is a great many goodbyes to learn."

Out past the broken roof, far across the black water, lightning flickered where no storm should be—in a straight line, in a pattern, like signal fires burning at the bottom of the sea.

Something royal was moving north.
