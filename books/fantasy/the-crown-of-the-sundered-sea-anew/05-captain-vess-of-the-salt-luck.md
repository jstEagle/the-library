## 5. Captain Vess of the Salt-Luck

The Salt-Luck ran before the wind with her rails nearly kissing the water, and Odessa Vess grinned into the salt-spray because there was nothing else to do with a face like hers but grin.

Behind them, three navy cutters cut the grey strait like knives through lard. Ahead, the Sundered Strait opened its throat toward open water and the drowned kingdom beyond it.

"Hard to port!" she bellowed. "Brace for the turn!"

The ship heeled. Cargo shifted below—crates of smuggled brandy, bolts of illegal silk, a coffin's worth of contraband relics—and somewhere in that shifting cargo, two passengers she'd fished out of a burning rowboat an hour ago were clinging to whatever they could find.

She hadn't wanted passengers. She'd wanted coin, quiet, and a clean run to the northern ports. What she'd gotten instead was a bone-carver with haunted eyes and a defrocked priest who prayed to a god Vess was fairly sure was dead.

"Captain!" Fenwick's voice from the deck, ragged. "They're gaining!"

"They're *supposed* to gain! It keeps them interested!" She hauled the wheel hard over. The Salt-Luck groaned, swung, and slid into the shadow of the Gulltooth rocks—a maze of black spires where no navy cutter dared follow at speed.

The first rock scraped past close enough to touch. The second took a strip of paint off the hull. The third—

"Starboard! STARBOARD!"

The ship threaded the needle by a hand's width, and then they were through, into calm water, and the cutters' horns sounded behind them, furious and distant.

Vess let go of the wheel, leaned on it, and laughed until her ribs hurt.

* * *

"You're insane," said the bone-carver.

Yara Halloran sat on a crate in the hold, hands wrapped around a mug of tea they hadn't touched. Up close, Vess could see what the drowning panic had hidden: this one carried weight in their shoulders like a person who'd been carrying something invisible for years. Their eyes kept drifting to the whale vertebra in their lap, turning it over and over like a worry-stone.

"Insane is free," Vess said. "Crossing the Sundered Strait costs money. And you—" she jabbed a finger at Yara, then at Fenwick, who stood by the ladder looking pious and damp, "—have neither money nor anything worth selling except trouble."

"We have gratitude," Fenwick offered.

"Gratitude sinks ships." Vess dropped onto a barrel across from them. "Here's how it works. I ferry you north, past the Strait, to wherever you're going. In exchange, you do one job for me. One."

Yara's hands stilled on the bone. "What kind of job?"

Vess had rehearsed this. On the whole sail down from the wreck site, she'd rehearsed it—cool, businesslike, no cracks showing. But now, with their eyes on her, the words came out rougher than planned.

"My brother went down with the Third Fleet. Eleven years ago. The *Merrow's Daughter*, along with forty other ships, lost in a single night in the middle of the Sundered Sea. No storm. No enemy fleet. Just—" she snapped her fingers, "—gone. The Crown called it a navigational disaster. My family called it bad luck. I call it a lie, because my brother's last letter home said the fleet was carrying something it shouldn't have been, and that if anything happened to him, it wasn't the sea's doing."

The hold was quiet. Water slapped against the hull.

"You want me to raise his voice," Yara said quietly. "From the drowned dead."

"I'm told you hear the dead through your carvings." Vess shrugged, aiming for casual and missing by a mile. "Carve something of his. A name, a likeness, whatever you need. Let me hear his last words. That's my price. That's the whole price."

Fenwick shifted. "Captain, the dead don't always say what we—"

"I know what they don't always say." Her voice came out harder than she meant. "Eleven years, priest. I've been sailing these waters eleven years, diving every wreck I can find, and I've never once found so much as a plank of the *Merrow's Daughter*. You don't get to tell me what the dead might or might not say. They're the only ones left who'll tell me the truth."

Yara looked down at the vertebra in their lap. Then up again, and something in their face made Vess's chest tighten—an understanding, worn smooth like sea glass.

"All right," Yara said. "But I need something of his. Bone, hair, a tooth. Something his body touched."

Vess reached under her coat and pulled out the small oilcloth bundle she'd carried against her ribs for eleven years. Inside: a fingerbone, yellowed, wrapped in a strip of his shirt.

"I dove the shallows where the fleet was last seen," she said. "Found this in a gull's nest, of all places. The birds know where the bodies are even when we don't."

* * *

They did it three nights later, becalmed in the middle of the Sundered Sea, under a sky full of stars that seemed too close.

Vess had expected candles, chanting, maybe some of Fenwick's tide-priest theatrics. Instead Yara simply sat cross-legged on the deck with the fingerbone and a knife and a piece of whale ivory, and began to carve.

At first, nothing. The knife whispered. Chips fell. The stars wheeled.

Then the air changed.

It was like the moment before lightning—the pressure drop, the taste of metal—and then Vess heard it, faint as a voice through a wall:

*...cold... so cold, Dess, why is it so cold...*

The knife stopped. So did Vess's heart.

"Dorian?" she whispered.

The carving in Yara's hands seemed to glow faintly, moonlight caught in the grain. And Dorian Vess's voice came clearer—not echoing, not ghostly, just *present*, the way he'd always been, leaning on her shoulder and talking too fast about nothing.

*Dess. Dess, listen. There's no time—I can feel it letting go of me. Listen.*

"I'm listening," she breathed. "Dory, I'm here. What happened? What sank you?"

*We were carrying it. The Crown—the real one, the one from under the sea. The Court gave it to us, told us to bring it to the coastlands, said it would end the war.* A sound that might have been a laugh, broken in half. *Lies. It was never a gift, Dess. It was a delivery. They wanted it gone from the palace before anyone counted the souls inside it.*

"The souls inside it?" Fenwick's voice, hoarse. "Brother, what souls?"

*Ten thousand of them. The drowned queen's people—they never freed them, they put them IN it, they bound them into the gold to make it strong enough to rule with. And the queen herself—she didn't die in battle, whatever the priests say. Her own chamberlain—* the voice cracked, warped, *—her own blood sold her to the Court, and they drowned her wearing her own crown, and then they couldn't destroy it because it was too full, too heavy with the dead, so they shipped it away and sank our fleet to hide the route—*

"No." Vess was on her feet without remembering standing. "No, that's—that's not—you're wrong. Our family served the Court for six generations. We wouldn't—we wouldn't help drown a *queen*—"

*Not our family, Dess.* His voice gentled, the way it did when he was about to tell her something she didn't want to hear. *The Vesses crewed the ships. But the order came from above us. From the Harrows. Grandmother's line. The Harrow seal was on the manifest. I saw it. I confronted her, the night before we sailed, and she smiled at me and said the sea keeps its secrets, and then—*

The voice frayed like rope under strain.

*Dory—Dory, stay with me—*

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry I didn't write more. I'm sorry I couldn't stop it. Dess—Dess, the cold is coming back—tell Mother—tell her the Harrows knew. Tell her the Harrows always knew—*

And then silence, vast and total, louder than any storm.

* * *

Vess stood at the rail until dawn, watching the water that held her brother.

The others gave her space. Smart of them. When she finally turned, Yara was still sitting where they'd carved, the finished piece resting on their knees—a small figure of a young man, mid-laugh, forever.

"He implicated my own blood," Vess said. Her voice was gravel. "My grandmother's family helped murder a queen and sink four hundred of our own sailors to bury it. And I spent eleven years blaming the sea."

"The sea didn't lie to you," Yara said. "People did. That's worse, but at least it means someone can be held to account."

"Held to account." Vess laughed, ugly. "By whom? The Harrows are landed gentry now. There's probably a portrait of my grandmother hanging in some courthouse."

"There will be ten thousand voices under the sea who remember," Fenwick said quietly. He looked shaken to his foundations, this priest who'd already lost his god once. "If what your brother said is true—if the crown is a prison—then everything I was taught, everything the Tidebound Court teaches, is built on a grave they dug themselves."

"Welcome to the club," Vess said. "We meet on a smuggling ship. Dues are paid in terrible truths."

That got the ghost of a smile out of Yara, tired and crooked. Vess found she liked earning that smile more than she'd expected to. Three days ago these two had been cargo. Now they were something else—something she didn't have a word for yet, except that when she looked at them she felt less alone on her own deck than she had in eleven years.

Bad bargains, she thought. Terrible truths. Shared wounds. That was as good a foundation as any. Better than most families managed.

"So," she said, hauling herself back to captain-mode, hands on hips. "North, then. You two are hunting this crown, aren't you? Don't bother denying it—the priest crosses himself whenever anyone says 'Tidebound,' and you keep carving little crowns in your sleep. I've watched you."

Yara's face closed. "And if we are?"

"Then I'm coming all the way with you." She said it before she could talk herself out of it. "You need a ship. I need answers. And if there's even a chance my brother's soul is locked inside that thing with nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine others—" her jaw tightened, "—then somebody owes those souls a rescue, and apparently the Vess family owes the most."

Fenwick exhaled slowly. "You understand what you're saying. If the crown holds ten thousand souls, whoever claims it claims power over the dead themselves. The Court wants it. Half the coastlands' navy wants it. And now—"

"Now," said a voice from the rigging above, "there's a lantern burning on the horizon, and it's flying no colors I know."

Vess's bosun, Marta, pointed east. Out on the dark water, a light burned green—sea-fire, corpse-light, the kind that didn't come from any lamp—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of singing. Dozens of voices. Hundreds.

Coming closer.

"Well," Vess said, reaching for the wheel. "Either that's the Tidebound Court finally catching up—"

Or, said nobody aloud, because nobody had to: it was the dead, coming to see what the living intended to do about them.

"Get below," Vess snapped. "Both of you. And Yara—bring your knife."
