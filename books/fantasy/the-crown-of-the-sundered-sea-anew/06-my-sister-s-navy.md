## 6. My Sister's Navy

The sails came out of the fog like the ribs of some enormous, patient beast.

Yara saw them first from the bow of the Salt-Luck, where they'd taken to standing at dawn—not because they loved the sea, but because the sea was honest. It had no stillness in it. Every wave spoke, every current muttered, and after a lifetime in the Valley of Stillness, Yara had discovered that noise, even terrible noise, was easier than silence.

"Vess," they said quietly. "We have company."

Captain Odessa Vess came up the companionway with the unhurried stride of a woman who had learned that panic was a luxury she could no longer afford. She squinted into the gray, and Yara watched her face do something complicated.

"That's navy cut," Vess said. "Coastland colors. Three ships." She spat over the rail. "Of course it's three. Never one when you can be humiliated properly."

Fenwick emerged from below, his defrocked priest's collar hidden under a fisherman's sweater, his hands already trembling. "They've been following us since the Strait," he said. "I felt them in the water. The tide-priests sing to their hulls now—the old ways, the ways I was cast out for remembering."

"The old ways being what, exactly?" Vess asked.

"Listening."

The lead ship grew. It was beautiful in the way that instruments of state are beautiful: black hull lacquered like a beetle's back, sails dyed the deep blue of deep water, and along its rail, marines in silver-and-white standing in ranks so perfect they might have been carved. Yara knew carving. These were carvings that breathed.

A voice rolled across the water, amplified by salt-magic until it seemed to come from everywhere at once:

"SALT-LUCK. HEAVE TO AND PREPARE FOR INSPECTION. RESISTANCE WILL BE LOGGED AS TREASON AGAINST THE COASTLANDS."

"Treason," Vess repeated, tasting the word. "Lovely. Fenwick, how much trouble are we in?"

"The admiral commanding this fleet," Fenwick said slowly, "is Serevan Halloran."

Yara's hands went cold on the rail.

The name landed in their chest like a stone dropped down a well—a long time falling, then a long time waiting for the splash. They hadn't heard it spoken aloud in eleven years. They had only ever seen it written, once, in their father's effects after he died: *Serevan, b. winter's end, mother: Captain Ilva Halloran, Royal Coastland Navy.* A half-sister Yara had never met, conceived before their father fled the coast and buried himself in the valley's magic-dead quiet, taking his secrets and his second family with him.

"She can't know who I am," Yara said.

"She read the same book we did," Fenwick said gently. "The prophecy names 'the Halloran bone-carver.' Admiral Serevan is nothing if not thorough. She will have traced every Halloran on every register from here to the Sundered Sea." He paused. "And she will have found your father's name. And yours."

The boarding was almost polite.

Grapnels thudded into the Salt-Luck's rails, and marines swung across in disciplined arcs, boots landing soft on the deck. Their officer was young and tried very hard not to look seasick. He announced the inspection in a voice pitched for ceremony, and Vess surrendered her papers with a smile that could have gutted a fish.

Then the gangway came across from the flagship, and Admiral Serevan Halloran stepped onto the deck of the smuggler's ship, and Yara forgot how to breathe.

She was tall—taller than Yara by half a head—and built like the ships she commanded, all keel and purpose. Her uniform was immaculate, her hair bound back in a knot tight enough to hurt, and her face—

Her face was their father's face. The same heavy brow. The same crooked set to the mouth, as if it were always about to decide whether a thing was funny or unforgivable. Yara had spent thirty years looking at that face in polished bronze and river water, and here it was, walking toward them wearing an admiral's coat.

Serevan stopped three paces away. Her eyes—gray, like theirs, like their father's—moved over Yara's face with the slow, hungry precision of someone reading a document she'd memorized and finally been allowed to see the original.

"You have his jaw," she said.

"Hello to you too," Yara managed.

Something flickered behind the admiral's composure—something young and startled—but she mastered it the way she clearly mastered everything. "Yara Halloran. Bone-carver. Of the Valley of Stillness." She said each fact like she was setting it on a shelf. "I have been looking for you for four months."

"I've been hiding for four months," Yara said. "It's been very restful until recently."

Behind them, Vess made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

* * *

They put Yara in the flagship's great cabin—which was either hospitality or a cell with better furniture, and Serevan made no pretense otherwise. Marines at the door. Fenwick and Vess confined to the Salt-Luck under guard, which meant, Yara suspected, that Serevan wanted this conversation without witnesses.

She poured two cups of tea from a service that had survived more campaigns than most soldiers. Her hands did not shake. Yara's did.

"Do you know what I was doing when Father died?" Serevan asked.

"No."

"Neither did I. For six years. He left the coast when I was nine. Mother told me he'd drowned." She set the pot down with a click. "I lit a memorial candle for a man who was alive the whole time, raising you in a valley where nothing magical can follow. Do you understand what that valley is, Yara? It's a hiding place. He didn't flee there. He *buried* you there."

"He kept me safe."

"He kept you *ignorant.*" For the first time, the composure cracked, just at one edge. "There is a prophecy book a century old that surfaced this spring. I bought it from a peddler in Corrow's Landing for more than my annual salary. It gives tonight's date. It gives your name. And it says—" her voice dropped, went flat and careful, "—that the bearer of the Sundered Crown will decide whether the sunken kingdom rises or the coastlands drown forever."

"And you believe a book."

"I believe the winter." Serevan crossed to the stern windows and looked out at the frozen-gray sea. "Three harvests failed. The herring fleets came back empty two years running. Ice where there has never been ice. My sailors' children are starving, Yara. Whatever is happening beneath the Sundered Sea, it is killing us up here, slowly, politely, the way a fever kills. The crown is the cause or the cure—I don't care which. I intend to take it and end the winter."

"And if ending the winter means drowning someone else?"

Serevan turned. "What?"

"The crown isn't a symbol." Yara's voice came out steadier than they felt. "It's a prison. Ten thousand souls of the drowned kingdom, bound inside it. We've heard them, Serevan. In the bones. The dead remember what was done to them. If you seize it—if you *use* it—you're not ending a war. You're reopening one."

For a long moment the admiral said nothing. Then she sat down across from Yara, and the gesture was so ordinary, so sisterly, that it hurt worse than anything she'd said yet.

"Tell me about the souls," she said. "Tell me everything. And then tell me why the answer isn't simply to take the thing away from whoever's misusing it."

So Yara told her. About the messenger who died on dry land. About Fenwick and the heresy that got him cast out—that the drowned queen was murdered by her own court, that the crown was forged as a cage and called a coronet. About the whale vertebra in the ossuary, and the voices that came through it, and the queen herself now speaking through Yara's carvings, pleading, always pleading.

Serevan listened the way she presumably commanded: completely, without interruption, her gray eyes never leaving Yara's face. When it was done, she was silent for the length of three waves against the hull.

"Our grandmother's grandmother was of the drowned blood," she said at last. "Mother's line. I found the record last month. Which makes us—" she gestured between them, awkward for the first time, "—whatever we are. Heirs to both sides. That's why the prophecy chose a Halloran. Not you specifically. Our name."

"It chose me."

"Because you were the one hiding in a hole where the book couldn't be argued with." She leaned forward. "Yara. Come with me. Not as a prisoner—as my second. My counsel. We'll take the crown together, and we'll decide together what it becomes. A new kingdom, maybe. Or we unmake it carefully, with priests and scholars and years instead of—" she waved a hand, "—whatever it is you and your smuggler and your heretic plan to do."

"We plan to listen to the dead," Yara said. "That's the whole plan. It's the only plan I trust."

"The dead don't feed sailors' children."

"No," Yara agreed. "But they remember why the children started starving. There's a difference between a wound and a symptom, sister, and you're proposing to burn the map because the road hurts."

Serevan flinched at *sister*. Yara saw it land—saw the word hit whatever soft place eleven years of candles-for-a-drowned-father had worn into her—and hated how much satisfaction they took from it, and hated the satisfaction more.

"I'm offering you a family," Serevan said quietly. "The only blood you have. I spent my whole life with no one, and then a book told me I had a sibling, and I sailed three hundred miles through freezing water to find them. Whatever you think of my methods, that offer is real."

And that was the heartbreak of it, Yara thought. That it was real. That somewhere under the admiral's coat and the treason rhetoric was a nine-year-old lighting a candle for a father who wasn't dead, and that child had grown into a woman who would freeze the ocean rather than be alone again.

"I have a family," Yara said. "You met them. They're on the other ship. One of them lost his god and kept his faith anyway; one of them sold her soul in installments and is buying it back one bad bargain at a time. And there's a ghost who refuses to move on, and ten thousand dead who need their names back. That's my family. You could have been in it." Their voice caught. "You still could be. But not like this. Not with chains on my friends and a war in your pocket."

Serevan stood. The composure came back down over her face like a visor, and Yara understood that they were looking at the admiral again, not the sister.

"Then I'm sorry," she said. "Truly. Because I can't let you reach the Sundered Sea ahead of me, and I can't let the Tidebound Court reach you at all. You'll sail with my fleet. Comfortably. As my guest." She moved toward the door, then stopped, her hand on the frame, not turning around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "you have his mouth, too. He used it the same way. To say things that were true and unbearable."

The door closed behind her.

Yara stood alone in the great cabin, in the smell of campaign tea and gun oil, and pressed their palm flat against the stern glass. Below, in the hold of the Salt-Luck, they knew without knowing how, Fenwick was praying and Vess was counting escape routes, and somewhere in the water beneath both ships, the drowned queen's voice was rising through the dark like a bubble seeking air.

Yara whispered to the glass, to the sea, to anyone listening:

"Hold on. I'm coming. Both of us are coming—and gods help us all, only one of us knows yet what she's really sailing toward."

Above, the sky began, very softly, to snow.
