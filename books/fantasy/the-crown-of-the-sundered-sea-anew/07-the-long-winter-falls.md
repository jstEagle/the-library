## 7. The Long Winter Falls

*Vess*

Odessa Vess had run the Salt-Luck through blockades, through reefs that ate ships whole, through a storm off the Teeth that had peeled her mainsail like the rind of an orange. She knew weather. Weather was honest. Weather wanted something from you and told you plainly what it was.

This was not weather.

The cold came out of the north an hour after they lost Serevan's frigate in the fog-banks of the Strait, and it came *sideways*, sliding across the water like something hunting by scent. Vess stood at the wheel and watched the sea change color ahead of them — grey to pewter to a white so dense it looked solid, though nothing yet floated there.

"Captain," called Tam at the bow, his voice already thick with frost. "There's — Captain, the spray's freezing. On the lines."

She looked up. The rigging wore lace. Every rope, every shroud furred white, and the ice grew as she watched, creeping down toward the deck like ivy with intent.

"Fenwick!" she bellowed. "Get up here!"

The priest came at a stumble, his old tide-cassock whipping around his legs, and when he saw the bow-wave he made a sound Vess had never heard from him before. Not prayer. Something smaller than prayer.

"That's not possible," he said.

"Congratulations," Vess said. "You've said it twice today. Yara! Get your bones up here!"

Yara Halloran emerged from below with the coral shard strung at their throat and their arms full of carvings — they slept with them now, Vess had noticed, like a woman sleeping with knives. They stopped at the rail and went very still.

"It's her," Yara said quietly. "The queen. She's not calling us anymore. She's *warning* us."

"Warning's fine," Vess snapped. "Warnings I can sail around. What's she warning us *of*?"

Yara lifted one small carving — a gull, bone-white, no bigger than a thumb — and held it to the wind. The wind answered through it, a voice thin as paper tearing:

"*The sea is closing its eye. Sail, or be kept.*"

Vess put the Salt-Luck hard about and ran south with every stitch of canvas she owned.

It wasn't enough. Nothing was going to be enough. By dusk the sea ahead had gone still as poured glass, and then, with a sound like a struck bell heard through the hull, it began to freeze — not from the edges inward but all at once, everywhere, as if the whole ocean had remembered it was once stone. The ship groaned, lifted, settled into the grip of it, and did not move again.

Vess stood at the wheel long after the wheel could do nothing, because letting go felt like admitting what had happened: that the Salt-Luck, her ship, her freedom, her one unbroken promise to herself, was now a house built on a dead sea.

Behind her, she heard Fenwick say softly, "We are being *kept*."

And she thought, with a bitterness that surprised even her: my brother was kept too.

* * *

*Fenwick*

Brother Fenwick had spent eleven years learning how to be a man without a god, and he had gotten reasonably good at it. He prayed out of habit, the way a sailor whistles in a calm, knowing it changes nothing. But on the fourth day frozen, with the crew eating salt-pork shaved thin and the cold finding new seams in the world to pour itself through, Fenwick knelt in the hold among the water casks and found that the habit had teeth.

He had taught, once, in the seminaries of the coast, that the drowned queen ascended willingly — that she took the crown and the deep upon herself as a bride takes a veil, for love of both kingdoms. It was the first doctrine he had ever doubted, and doubting it had cost him everything: his orders, his name, his place at any altar in the land.

Now the truth sat across the hold from him, carving.

"You should sleep," Yara said. They didn't look up from the vertebra in their hands. "You've been kneeling for an hour. Your knees are cracking louder than the ice."

"I'm praying."

"To whom?"

Fenwick opened his mouth and found the honest answer lodged in it like a fishbone. "I don't know," he said finally. "That's rather the point of me."

Yara set down the knife. In the lantern light their face was hollowed, grey-lipped, older than it had been a week ago. "Then pray to the dead," they said. "They're the only ones answering lately."

"They shouldn't be able to." His voice cracked on it. "Not here. Not through — Yara, the crown is a prison. You told me yourself what the whale-bone showed. Ten thousand souls sewn shut inside it. If the queen can reach you even now, even through your carvings, then either the prison is failing—"

"—or someone is opening it," Yara finished.

The silence between them filled with the sound of the ship settling, ice ticking against the hull like fingers counting.

"The wraiths that attacked you," Fenwick said slowly. "On the coast road. They weren't trying to kill you, were they? They were trying to *stop* you. And the winter—" He stood, joints protesting, and paced the narrow aisle between casks. "A prison doesn't freeze the sea to trap four people in a boat. A prisoner does that. Someone inside is spending everything they have left to keep you here. To keep you from reaching the crown — or to make sure you *do*."

"Which?"

"That," said Fenwick, "is the question I've been praying about. To whom, I couldn't say."

Above them, muffled through planking and ice, someone began to scream. It was Tam, and he was screaming about the figurehead.

They came up the ladder two at a time. The night was clear and starless, the frozen sea glowing faintly with its own pale light, and there, at the prow where the Salt-Luck's painted maiden had ridden for twenty years, stood a woman made of seawater and shadow, her hair moving in a current that did not exist above the ice.

She did not speak. Her mouth moved, and the words came from everywhere else — from the rigging, from the hold, from the small carved gull in Yara's pocket, which had begun to glow like a coal.

"*Carver,*" said the drowned queen. "*Little bone-speaker. Blood of my blood and blood of my murderers both. Hear me, for I am running out of sea to say it in.*"

Fenwick dropped to his knees. This time it wasn't habit.

"He is coming awake," the ghost said. "The thing my court locked beneath the crown when they crowned me with it. I was not their queen. I was their *lid*. And if you do not come — if this winter keeps you — he will wear my kingdom out of the deep like a glove, and he will not stop at the coastlands. Carver. Come and unmake what we made. Come and let us *die properly*, at last."

Her image thinned, streamed, scattered into frost on the air.

In the ringing quiet afterward, Vess said, flatly: "Well. That's torn it."

* * *

*Vess*

By the ninth day the mutiny didn't even bother to hide.

Vess heard it forming the way she heard weather — in the timbre of voices below decks, in who stopped talking when she came down the ladder. Tam and the Corvo brothers, and worst of all little Marta, sixteen and sharp-eyed, who had signed on in a harbor town for honest smuggling wages and had never signed on for this.

"We go back," Marta said, standing in the galley with her arms crossed, frost on her lashes. "South. We turn back and we take our chances with the admiral. A navy cell has bread in it, Captain. Bread and walls and *warmth*."

"There's no going back," Vess said. "You saw the stern wake. We're froze in bow to rudder. There's no back to go to."

"Then we walk." Tam slammed his palm on the table. "Across the ice, south, until we find open water or the coast. Four days' march, maybe five. Better than sitting here eating our boots while ghosts recite poetry."

"And carrying what?" Vess kept her voice level, which took work. "The coral shard stays with Yara. That's the whole reason any of us are in this story. You walk, you walk without it, and whatever the queen's keeping under the sea comes up behind you anyway. There's no away, Tam. There's just *here* and *later*."

"Later for what? For her?" He jerked his chin upward, toward the deck, toward the ghost. "For the priest's murdered queen? Captain, I love this ship, but I never agreed to die in a prophecy."

"No," Vess said. "You agreed to be paid. Half now, half on delivery. Well, the delivery's changed, and I'll tell you what I know: whoever walks off this ice is walking off alone, and I don't split shares with deserters."

The galley went very quiet. Marta looked at Tam. Tam looked at the deck. And Vess understood, with a sick lurch, that she was one wrong word from losing them — and that she could not entirely blame them, because part of her, the part that had buried a brother with no body and no last words, wanted to walk south too, wanted it so badly her teeth ached.

That was when Yara came down the ladder.

They were swaying on their feet. They'd been carving since the second dawn, hardly eating, and their hands were bandaged where the knife had slipped twice. They set a single object on the galley table: a small carving of a young man, unfinished, the face still blank.

"What's that supposed to be?" Tam asked.

"A bargain," Yara said, looking at Vess. "You asked me once why the captain's brother matters to any of this. Vess — sit down. All of you. I can't raise him. Not fully. But the ice — the ice carries sound the way water used to. And he's been trying to reach her for six years." They pulled out their knife. "I think I can give him enough voice to say what he never got to say. And I think you all need to hear what drowned men say about this war, because it isn't what the admiralty teaches."

Vess sat down. So, one by one, did the others.

Yara closed their eyes and began to carve, and the galley filled with the smell of brine and the sound — faint, halting, impossibly far away — of a young man's voice saying his sister's name.

Vess did not cry. Captains don't. But she gripped the table edge hard enough to splinter it, and when it was over she stood, and looked at Tam, and said only: "Still want to walk?"

Tam looked at the little blank-faced carving, and shook his head.

* * *

*Fenwick*

On the twelfth night, the ice spoke with the queen's voice, and Fenwick finally understood what had been done to his faith, and by whom.

He was on watch alone. The frozen sea stretched to every horizon, luminous, empty, and utterly silent — until it wasn't. The voice rose out of the ice itself, everywhere at once, and it was weaker now, threadbare, a candle burned to its last hour.

"*Priest,*" it said. "*You knelt to me tonight. Do not. I am no god. I am a woman who was murdered by her friends and given a crown instead of a grave.*"

"I know," Fenwick whispered. "God forgive me — I know. I taught the lie for twenty years. I built churches on it."

"*Then hear the truth and carry it, since carrying is what you have left. My court feared the deep. They feared dying. So they made a crown to hold ten thousand souls back from passing — ours, theirs, everyone the sundering took — and they set it on my head because I loved them too much to refuse. Every winter since has been the crown dreaming. Every drowning, every fleet swallowed, every coastland child taken by the tide — the lid slipping. And now the thing beneath us all is stirring, and my strength is spent, and the carver sleeps below decks with bleeding hands, and I cannot wake them.*"

Fenwick's breath caught. "Yara? What's wrong with Yara?"

"*The names,*" the ghost said, and there was something in the voice now that Fenwick had heard only once before, at his mother's deathbed: fear. "*Each soul they speak free takes something from them. They will not stop. They will carve themselves hollow before they let that thing rise. Priest — I held ten thousand souls together for a hundred years because I could not bear to let go. Do not let the carver make my mistake twice. When the time comes, someone must be strong enough to tell them to stop.*"

The voice faded. The ice glowed on, patient as a held breath.

Fenwick stood his watch until dawn, and when the others woke he gathered them in the galley — Vess with her red-rimmed eyes, Yara grey and swaying, the crew hollow-cheeked and silent — and told them everything.

When he finished, Yara looked down at their own bandaged hands for a long moment. Then they smiled, small and terrible.

"She's wrong about one thing," they said. "Nobody gets to tell me to stop. But somebody might get to help me finish faster." They raised their eyes, and Fenwick saw they were fixed on the north, where the ice lay darkest and deepest. "The crown's under that. Two days' walk, maybe three, if we cut the ship loose and take sledges. The queen can't guide us anymore — she's nearly gone. But the dead in the ice can. Every soul the winter ever took is frozen into this sea, Fenwick. And I can hear every one of them."

Vess stood. "Then we'd better start cutting."

They worked through the morning, freeing the longboat and dragging the sledges from the hold, and it was Tam — Tam, who had wanted mutiny — who first cried out and pointed north.

At the horizon, where the dark ice met the black sky, something vast had begun, very slowly, to glow. And beneath the glow, faint but unmistakable, carried across miles of frozen sea, came the sound of singing — ten thousand voices, rising.

Yara picked up their sledge-rope and started walking.
