## 8. Beneath the Sundered Sea

The breathing-hole was a scar in the ice, black as an eye, and it breathed.

Yara knelt at its edge and watched the water rise and fall within it—rise, fall, rise—a slow tidal lung exhaling somewhere far below. Around them the frozen sea stretched white to every horizon, and above it the aurora writhed green and bruise-purple, but here at the hole there was only that darkness, patient and deep.

*It remembers me,* the queen's ghost had said through the whale vertebra Yara carried against their chest. *The city. It will open for my blood.*

"You don't have to do this," Fenwick said behind them. His voice was raw; he had not slept since the night the dead began speaking through every carving on the ship, ten thousand murmurs like wind through rigging. He'd tied his broken god-symbol around his neck anyway. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't." Vess crouched by the hole, testing the water with two fingers and flinching from the cold that wasn't cold—it was older than cold, a stillness like the valley's, like death deciding whether to take you. "I've sailed over this spot twenty years. The charts call it the Drowned Choir. Sailors say if you listen at the rail on a quiet night, you hear singing." She looked up at Yara, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "My brother's down there. Whatever you find—" her jaw worked "—say his name right. That's all I ask."

"I will," Yara said. And meant it. That was the terrible thing about their gift: they always meant it.

They stripped to their shift and the oilskin Vess pressed on them, tied the knife at their hip, and held the vertebra one last time. Through the bone, faint as a pulse under snow, the queen's voice: *Follow the lanterns. I lit them before the end. My court let them go dark one by one, but some still burn.*

"Lanterns," Yara repeated aloud. "I follow the lanterns."

Fenwick gripped their shoulder hard enough to hurt. "Whatever she asks of you down there—remember she was murdered by her own people. Grief makes ghosts into liars sometimes. Not because they're cruel. Because they're lonely."

Yara nodded, though the words would not stay with them. Nothing stayed with you after the water took you. They knew that better than anyone; they spent their life listening to what the water kept.

Then they slid into the breathing-hole, and the sea closed over their head like a book shutting.

* * *

Down was a direction that stopped meaning anything.

Yara sank through water so clear it seemed absent, and the cold did not bite—it *recognized*. It moved through them the way their own hands moved through bone, reading, cataloguing, deciding what they were made of. They should have drowned in the first hundred feet. Instead each breath came easy, silver-cold, as though the sea itself were breathing for them, and they understood with a lurch that this was the queen's doing—that royal blood bought air the way coin bought bread, and the price would come due later.

The lanterns appeared at forty fathoms.

They hung in the water like drowned stars: spheres of pale coral-glass, each holding a flame that burned without consuming, green-white and trembling. Most were dark. But ahead, strung along a road of sunken flagstones, perhaps a dozen still burned, and between them Yara began to see the city.

Thalassarine had been beautiful. That was the first horror of it—not ruin but beauty, preserved the way things are preserved in grief. Towers of white stone leaned but did not fall. Arches still stood over streets where silt lay like fallen snow. Fish moved through open windows, through doorways, through the eye sockets of statues whose faces had been carved serene and were now furred with weed. And everywhere, everywhere, the bones. Not scattered—*arranged*. Laid in rows along the walls, folded in niches, stacked in patterns too deliberate for accident. A city that had buried its dead with care even as it died.

Through the vertebra against their chest, the murmur rose. Ten thousand voices, soft, overlapping, the sound of a crowd heard through a wall. Yara had heard the dead their whole life—one voice per carving, maybe two, whispers they could carry in cupped hands. This was an ocean trying to pour itself through a keyhole.

*Hurry,* said the queen. *He is close behind you.*

"Who?"

But the lanterns ahead guttered, and Yara swam.

The altar stood in the heart of the old palace, in a hall whose roof had collapsed outward rather than in, opening the chamber to the distant, filtered light of the surface. It was built of ribs—whale ribs, Yara thought at first, then saw the smaller ones woven among them, human, hundreds, arched together into a dome of ribcage vast enough to swallow a cathedral. And beneath it, on a plinth of black coral, sat the Sundered Crown.

It was small. That was the second horror. A circlet of white metal and bone, no larger than what a child might wear at a naming day, cracked down one side where it had been sundered long ago and rejoined with seams of gold. It looked tired. It looked like something that had been holding its breath for three hundred years.

Yara drifted closer, and the murmur changed. The wall-of-voices became distinct. Became *names*, whispered one after another after another, a litany without end, and Yara realized with a sickness rising in their throat that the crown was not silent. It had never been silent. It was screaming, and had been screaming since before Yara's grandmother's grandmother was born, and the water was thick enough with grief that no one on the surface had ever heard it.

*I could not hold them,* the queen said, very quietly. *When the court bound them into it—to save power, they called it, to save the kingdom—I could not unmake their work. So I brought the crown down here where no hand would reach it, and I let the sea bury me beside it, and I have listened to them scream for three centuries.* A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had frayed to almost nothing. *You carve the dead so they can speak. Tell me, bone-carver. Was I a rescuer or just another jailer who chose a bigger cell?*

Yara reached for the crown—

—and the light above them went out.

Not dimmed. *Blocked.* Yara turned in the water and saw a shape descending through the broken roof, wrapped in a diver's rigging of navy grey, moving with the economy of someone who had trained in cold water her whole life. Lantern-light from her helmet caught the braid, the rank-clasps, the face that was half Yara's own face worn harder.

Serevan landed on the altar dais in a bloom of disturbed silt and stood, sword drawn, in the hall of ribs.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. Two figures in a drowned cathedral, breath hissing through rebreather charms, surrounded by ten thousand arranged bones and one small screaming crown.

"You followed me through the ice," Yara said. Their voice sounded strange in the water, flattened, intimate.

"The breathing-hole froze behind me. I nearly didn't make it." Serevan's voice came tinny through the charm at her throat. She wasn't looking at Yara. She was looking at the crown, and her expression was not greed—Yara had braced for greed, and its absence was worse. Her expression was *hope*, and hope on Serevan's face looked like a wound that had never been allowed to close. "Do you know what the winter is doing on the surface, little sibling? The fishing villages are starving. The coast garrisons are burning their ships for firewood. Children are dying in towns whose names you've never heard, because you carved your little bones in your quiet valley while the world ended." She finally turned. "I read the same book you did. It says the crown can raise the sunken kingdom. It says the bearer holds both thrones. It says—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, Yara could see her hating it "—it says we share a father. I checked the parish records. You're mine. Whatever else you are, you're *mine*, and I am asking you, once, as family: help me lift it. We end the winter tonight. We save everyone."

"And wake what's inside it?" Yara spread their arms, gesturing at the walls, the niches, the dome of ribs. "Serevan. These aren't decorations. These are the prisoners. The crown is a prison. Ten thousand souls were bound into it when the kingdom fell—they weren't saved, they were *taken*, and they have been screaming for three hundred years, and if you raise it, you won't be lifting a kingdom. You'll be tearing open a cage over both our heads."

"You don't know that."

"I hear them." Yara pressed a hand to their sternum, to the vertebra, to everything. "Every name. Every hour. Do you want to know what I've learned about the dead, sister? They don't want thrones. They don't want war. They want their *names back*. That's all any of them ever wanted. Mother included."

Something moved across Serevan's face—the first doubt, quick as a fish shadow—and then she shut it away behind the admiral, behind the rank-clasps, behind thirty years of being the responsible one, the loyal one, the one who carried everything so no one else had to.

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, and reached past Yara for the crown.

"No—"

Her gauntleted fingers closed around the white metal, and she lifted it, and the world broke.

* * *

The scream was not a sound. It was a pressure, a detonation of grief that went through the water and through Yara's bones and through the bones of the dead city all at once. The lanterns that still burned shattered. The silt leapt from the streets in rolling clouds. Above them, three hundred miles of frozen sea groaned like a living thing wounded, and Yara felt it through the water—the ice cracking, fracturing, the long winter *reversing* into something worse, something that would pull the ocean up over the coastlands like a sheet pulled over a face.

The souls were coming out of the crown.

They came as light and as shapes and as voices, tearing free in threads and torrents, and they were not gentle, because nothing that has screamed for three centuries comes out gently. They poured around the sisters in spiraling rivers, and where they passed, the dead bones in the walls began to move, to rattle, to *rise*, and the great dome of ribs overhead flexed like the chest of something inhaling.

"Put it down!" Yara grabbed Serevan's arm. "Put it down, you're killing us, you're killing *everyone*—"

"I'm *saving*—" But Serevan's voice failed, because the souls had found her too, and they were pouring through her, showing her—Yara could feel it through their joined arms, the gift spilling over, shared blood sharing sight—showing her the binding. The ritual. The faces of the courtiers who had done it, calm and robed and certain, singing ten thousand people out of their bodies and into the white metal while the city drowned around them. Showing her the queen, running through flooding halls with the crown in her arms, choosing drowning over surrendering it.

Showing her the children. There had been children.

The crown shuddered in Serevan's grip. The gold seams split. One side of the circlet, the sundered side, began to peel open like a mouth, and the light pouring from it turned from white to a red that had no business existing underwater, and far above, through the broken roof, Yara saw the surface flicker—saw the ice, miles thick, begin to glow from beneath like a lampshade over a rising fire.

Both kingdoms. Both graves. Unless—

"Serevan." Yara took their sister's face in both hands, forced her to look at them, at the one face in the world shaped like her own. "Listen to me. You wanted to save people. So save them. Let go."

"I can't. If I let go, it was all—" Her voice broke entirely. "Father. The fleet. The *winter*. Everything I did—"

"Was done by someone who didn't know. Now you know." Yara's own tears vanished into the sea, weightless, indistinguishable from the water around them. "Let go, and help me. I can't give them back their names alone. There are ten thousand of them, sister. *Ten thousand.*"

And Serevan Halloran—Admiral of the Coastlands, heir of the wrong throne, the responsible one, the one who had carried everything—looked at the crown in her hands, and at the red light climbing toward the surface, and at her sibling's face.

Her fingers opened.

The crown fell toward the altar, turning, splitting wider, and the chorus of the unbound rose to a pitch that shook the ribs of the dome and the ribs of the world—

—and Yara, bone-carver of the Valley of Stillness, dove after it, knife already in hand, reaching for the one thing they had left to give.

Their hands.
