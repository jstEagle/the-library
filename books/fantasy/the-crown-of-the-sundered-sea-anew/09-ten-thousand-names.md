## 9. Ten Thousand Names

The crown sang to me from its altar of ribs.

That was the first thing I understood, kneeling in the drowned capital with the sea pressing against my ears like a mother's palm: it had always been singing. A hundred years of silence in the Valley of Stillness had not been silence at all—it had been this sound, filtered through stone and distance, so faint I'd mistaken it for my own heartbeat. Ten thousand voices braided into one long note, and every soul caught inside it was singing the same word over and over:

*Home. Home. Home.*

They were not singing about the sunken kingdom.

They were singing about the crown itself. It had become the only home they had left.

"Yara." Serevan's voice came distorted through the water, her armor glowing with the cold-light the Tidebound Court wore like jewelry. She stood at the mouth of the great hall, framed by pillars furred with white coral, and even now—even here, among the bones of everything our blood had built—she moved like a woman giving orders to a room that had learned to listen. "Step away from it."

I did not step away. My hand rested on the altar of ribs, and through my palm I could feel them—the dead beneath the dead, the ones whose bodies had been stacked and shaped into a pedestal for their prison. They were not angry. That was the worst of it. They were *tired*, the way water is tired at the bottom of a well.

"You read the same book I did," I said. My voice sounded strange underwater, flattened and close, as if the sea wanted to keep my words for itself. "It didn't name you, Serevan. It named me. Do you wonder why?"

"Because prophecy is a lottery and you won." She waded closer, each step raising a slow ghost of silt. "Because someone has to take that thing before it takes the coastlands. You've seen what the winter is doing. Vess told you. The ice is already at the harbor mouths."

"I've also heard what's *inside* it." I lifted my hand from the altar. The whale-vertebra knife hung at my belt where it had hung since the ossuary, since Fenwick watched me carve a memory into bone by accident and we both learned what I really was. "Ten thousand souls, sister. Not soldiers. Not an army. People. A fisherman who missed his daughter's wedding. A girl who kept snails as pets. A priest who died doubting. They're not powering the crown. They *are* the crown."

Serevan's jaw tightened. In the green half-dark I could see the family in her face—the same sharp Halloran cheekbones, the same stubborn line of mouth that I saw in my own reflection and hated. Our father's face, split between two daughters like an inheritance neither of us asked for.

"You don't know that they suffer," she said. "Maybe they sleep. Maybe it's mercy."

"It's a jar," I said. "And jars are only kind to things that can't remember being free."

She drew her sword.

I want to be honest, because the dead taught me honesty and I owe them that much: for one whole breath, I thought about letting her do it. Let her cut me down, take the crown, raise the drowned kingdom or drown the coastlands or whatever terrible mercy she'd built in her head out of our shared blood. It would have been so simple. I could go back to being no one. I could stop hearing ten thousand people say *home* every time I closed my eyes.

Then the queen's ghost moved through me.

Not into me—through, the way weather moves through an open window—and for a moment I stood in two times at once. I saw this hall alive, lanterns burning, courtiers in kelp-green silk. And I saw the night it ended: the queen walking down these steps alone because she had dismissed her guards, because the man waiting at the altar had sworn he only wanted peace, because love is the oldest lockpick there is. I heard the last thing she said—not a curse, not a plea, just her own true name spoken aloud, as if reminding the dark who it was killing.

*Maerwen of the Ninth Tide.*

The vision broke. Serevan was three paces away, sword rising, salt-water dragging at her arm like regret.

And I did the only thing I have ever known how to do.

I carved.

* * *

There was no time for beauty. There was barely time for anything. I tore the vertebra knife free, dropped to my knees on the rib-altar, and set the blade against the crown itself—the white coral band, crusted with centuries, humming its terrible chord—and began to cut.

Not to break it. Breaking a jar full of souls is still breaking souls. Fenwick had understood it before I did; Vess had said it in her rough way on the frozen deck: *You don't smash a cage, girl, you open the door.* But the door of this crown was made of names. Every soul inside had been sealed in with its name struck out, erased from every record, unremembered by design—because the Tidebound Court of a century ago had known the truth Fenwick preached in exile: that a name is a key, and ten thousand keys together could open anything, even from the inside.

So they had taken the keys away.

Except nothing is ever fully taken. Not from a carver. Not from someone who has spent their whole life listening to bone.

The dead talk through what is carved. And every one of those ten thousand souls had been *in* the crown when it was forged—had felt the chisel-work of its making pass through them like a bell passing through a body. Their names were written in the coral the way footprints are written in snow. Not legible. Not obvious. But there, if you knew how to listen, and I was the only listener left.

I cut the first groove and spoke into it.

"Fen of the Saltmarsh Road," I said. "Fisherman. Father of Mari. Your boat was called *Patience*. Your name is Fen."

The crown screamed.

I cannot describe it any other way. The chord fractured, one thread tearing loose, and somewhere above us the frozen sea shuddered hard enough that cracks raced across the breathing-hole like lightning in reverse. Serevan stumbled. The water went bright around me, then dark, then bright again.

"What are you doing?" she shouted. "*What are you doing?*"

"My job," I said, and cut the second groove.

"Mari of the Saltmarsh Road. Snail-keeper. You were nine years old when they took you, and your mother never stopped setting a place for you at the table. Your name is Mari."

Two threads loose now. Two voices rising out of the chorus, no longer saying *home* but saying *Fen*, saying *Mari*, saying themselves like people waking from a fever calling their own names to prove they still had mouths.

Serevan reached me. Her sword came down—

And stopped. Because between us, rising out of the altar of ribs, the dead queen stood.

Not solid. Not a ghost in the way I had felt her all voyage—a pressure, a pleading. This was memory given shape by the crown itself, dragged up out of the coral as my carving loosened its grip on the past: Maerwen of the Ninth Tide, tall and salt-crowned, looking at my sister with eyes the color of deep water under ice.

Looking at her the way you look at family.

Because that was the secret the book had hinted and the bones had confirmed and Fenwick had been defrocked for daring to preach: the murdered queen had a daughter smuggled to land before the fall. A daughter whose descendants scattered along the coast for a century, thinning like ink in water, until the line ran quiet and small and invisible into a valley where magic could not follow.

Until it ran into me. And into the sister I never knew I had.

"She knows," I whispered, still cutting, still speaking names—*Ondrey the doubting priest. Hessa who loved storms. Brannic, Brannic, your name is Brannic*—"she's always known, Serevan. Why do you think the navy found us so fast? Why do you think the prophecy named both a bone-carver and an admiral? We're not two sides of the war. We're the last two drops of the same wave."

Serevan's sword arm shook. The ghost of Maerwen raised a hand—not commanding, never commanding, only offering—and touched my sister's cheek with fingers made of remembered light, and whatever passed between them, I was not given to see. Some griefs belong to the people grieving them.

But I heard what Serevan said. Underwater, flattened, close. The way she used to talk when we were children, before either of us knew what we were.

"I just wanted it to stop hurting," she said. "The winter. Everything. I thought if I held enough power, nothing could hurt anyone ever again."

"That's how the crown was made," I said gently. "By someone who couldn't stand the grief. Look what it cost."

Her sword fell from her hand and rang against the rib-altar, and the sound was the sweetest bell I have ever heard.

I kept carving.

* * *

I do not know how long it took. Time underwater is a rumor. My hands bled until the sea around me bloomed pink and then cleared, and my voice cracked and healed and cracked again, and still the names came—up out of the coral like fish rising to feed, faster now, the crown helping me at the end, the way a knot helps the hand that finally finds the right pull.

Four hundred names. Nine hundred. Three thousand.

Vess's brother was in there—I found him, *Tomas Vess of the Salt-Luck line, who died shouting a warning he was too late to give*, and I sent his name upward toward the frozen surface like a letter. Fenwick's heresy was in there too, proven ten thousand times over in the flesh-memory of the coral.

At four thousand names the crown split along its first seam. At seven thousand, the altar of ribs began to glow. At nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine, the whole hall filled with light like a sunrise happening in reverse, sinking instead of rising, going down into the roots of the world.

The last name was Maerwen's.

Of course it was. She had saved herself for last, holding the door for everyone else the way queens do, the way mothers do, the way sisters do when one of them finally learns how.

I carved her name into the last unbroken inch of coral and spoke it into the water with everything I had left:

"Maerwen of the Ninth Tide. Murdered, and mourned, and *remembered*. Your name is yours again. Go home—whichever home that turns out to be."

She smiled at me. At both of us.

And the crown came apart—not shattered, not destroyed, but *opened*, unfolding like a flower made of frost, and ten thousand souls rose out of it at once.

The chorus did not cheer. It did not rage. It simply sang—one vast, released chord, ten thousand names sounding together for the first time in a century, joy and grief braided so tightly you could not tell which was which—and the song went up through the drowning water, up through the ice, up through the long winter itself.

Above us, far above, something enormous let go.

The ice began to sing back.

When the light faded, the altar held only bones and broken coral and two exhausted women floating in water gone suddenly, impossibly warm. Serevan was crying without any sound at all, which I suspected was a skill admirals learn early. I reached over and took her hand, because we were the last two drops of the same wave, and waves are meant to touch.

Far overhead, through the breathing-hole, the first crack of spring spread across the frozen strait like a branch blossoming.

And drifting up through the water, soft as snowfall, came a sound I would spend the rest of my life trying to describe: the whole freed sea, whispering ten thousand names, learning them again.

One of them, faint and familiar, was mine.
