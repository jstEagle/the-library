## 10. The Valley Remembers Me

The winter broke on a Tuesday.

Yara learned this the way they had once learned everything—through someone else's mouth. A gull came down out of a sky gone soft and grey and landed on the rail of the Salt-Luck, and Vess said, "Well, that's it, then," as if the bird were an official proclamation. "Gulls don't fly over ice. Never have."

And she was right. The sea was moving again.

It happened in the space between one tide and the next, the way grief does when it finally finishes with you—not all at once but all at once anyway. The pack that had held the Strait for three months sighed, groaned, and split along a fault line older than any kingdom, and the water beneath it was black and patient and alive. The ship rocked. Everyone aboard stumbled, laughed, or wept, depending on how much of the winter they still carried in their chests.

Yara carried the least and felt it most. Three months frozen at anchor, listening to the dead speak through whalebone and coral and the ribs of the ship itself, and every voice they had named in the dark beneath the Sundered Sea still lived somewhere behind their sternum, humming like struck bells that would not quite finish ringing. Ten thousand names. They could recite them now in their sleep. Some nights they did.

"Land by week's end," Vess said, spitting over the rail in the old smuggler's benediction. "Week's end, and I never want to see another icicle as long as my arm."

"You'll miss it," Fenwick said mildly, from his corner of the deck where he'd taken to sitting with his prayer book open on his knees—except the pages were blank now. He had burned the old liturgy in the first thaw-fire, all of it, every hymn to the drowned queen and every curse upon her murderers. "You'll stand on solid ground for two days and start composing ballads about the ice."

"I will not."

"He will," Yara said.

Vess glared at them both, then ruined it by grinning. It was a strange thing, her grin. It fit her face like a coat borrowed from a smaller woman, and every month she wore it a little more easily.

* * *

They made port at Greyharbor, which had spent the war being neither coastland nor deep, and there the found family began, without anyone saying so, to come apart into its separate lives.

Fenwick went first, though he went slowly, the way a man leaves a house he has only just admitted is home. There were others like him now—the defrocked, the doubting, the priests who had prayed into the long winter and heard nothing until the night ten thousand souls sang their own names under the sea. They came to Greyharbor asking for Brother Fenwick, or asking for the man who sailed with him, and Fenwick met them in a rented room above a chandlery and taught them what he was still learning himself: that faith was not a doctrine but a practice of attention. That the dead did not want worship. They wanted witness.

"They're building something," Fenwick told Yara the morning he left, standing on the dock with his blank book under his arm like a brick. "Not a church. I won't call it a church. More like... an archive. Of names." He hesitated, and the old shame crossed his face—the heresy that had unmade him—and then passed, because it wasn't heresy anymore. It was history. "I'd like yours first, if you'll give it. Your name. For the record."

"You've known my name for months."

"Not the name your mother gave you." His eyes were kind and terrible, the way they always were when he was about to be exactly right. "The name the crown knew. The name the dead sang back to you."

Yara looked out at the water, black and gentle now, and gave him the true one, the one they had never spoken aloud because no one alive had earned it. Fenwick wrote it down in careful letters, bowed—actually bowed, which was ridiculous, and which Yara pretended not to be moved by—and walked up the dock road toward whatever he was going to build out of the wreckage of his god.

* * *

Serevan stayed longer.

She had been stripped of rank in the chaotic spring of treaties and reckonings that followed the shattering of the crown—the coastland council had needed someone to blame for the fleet lost in the ice, and an admiral who had drawn steel against the savior of two kingdoms was convenient—but she bore the demotion the way she bore everything, spine straight, jaw set, as if it were a uniform she had chosen.

She came to the Salt-Luck on Yara's last evening in Greyharbor carrying a bottle of wine and the expression of a person about to attempt something more frightening than naval combat.

"Letters," she said, before she'd even sat down.

"Hello to you too, Admiral."

"Don't." Serevan set the bottle down hard. "Don't call me that. I'm not anything now. That's rather the point." She sat, finally, hands flat on the table like a woman steadying herself against weather. "I've been given a choice. Retire to the family estate—which isn't mine, it was never mine, it's Father's, and Father is a portrait and a title and a man who never once asked what I wanted—or take exile. Anywhere. My pick."

"That's not a choice, that's a punishment with scenery."

"Yes." And here Serevan did the thing that Yara had watched her do perhaps four times in their entire acquaintance: she looked away first. "So I'm taking exile. And I thought—" Her jaw worked. Yara watched their half-sister, who had commanded ten thousand sailors and faced down the Tidebound Court without flinching, visibly struggle with the next sentence. "I thought, if the recipient was amenable. Letters. Correspondence. I'm told it's what people do when they—" She gestured vaguely at everything. "Care. About each other. Verbally. At a distance."

Yara laughed. They hadn't meant to. But it came out of them warm and helpless, and to their astonishment Serevan laughed too, short and rusty, like a hinge remembering its purpose.

"You are the worst letter-writer in the history of correspondence," Yara said. "Your dispatches put grown men to sleep. I've read them."

"My dispatches were classified."

"Exactly my point." Yara reached across the table and took the wine bottle, because if they didn't give their hands something to do they were going to do something embarrassing instead. "Write me letters, Serevan. Tell me about wherever you go. Tell me if the fish are good. Don't tell me you're well—I'll know you're lying."

Serevan exhaled, long and slow, some knot in her letting go that had been tied since before either of them was born. "Father would have hated this," she said, almost wonderingly.

"Good," said Yara, and poured.

* * *

Vess took them north herself, because she claimed she was already headed that way, which was a lie, because the Salt-Luck had never once been headed anywhere near the Valley of Stillness and everyone knew it.

They stood together on the last day of sailing, at the rail, watching the coastline change from grey rock to green hill to the pale gold of the valley's wheat fields far inland.

"So this is it," Vess said. "The magic-dead valley. Where nothing supernatural can follow you." She glanced sideways. "Except apparently prophecy books, dying messengers, tide-wraiths, a defrocked priest, a smuggler of legendary charm, a ghost queen, and your own sister."

"The valley was resting," Yara said.

"It was *resting.*"

"Everything hums a little, if you listen long enough. Even silence. Especially silence." Yara touched the rail, and beneath their palm the old wood remembered every storm it had ever survived, faint and sweet as distant music. "It's louder now. Since the crown broke. Everything is. The magic didn't die in that valley—it just went quiet so something terrible couldn't find it. Well. Something terrible found it anyway. So it might as well sing."

Vess was quiet a moment. Then she said, gruffly, "You could stay aboard. Permanent berth. I pay terribly and the food is worse, but the company's begun to grow on me like barnacles."

Yara smiled and meant it and refused it, and Vess nodded as if that were the answer she'd expected, because it was. They embraced awkwardly, captain-fashion, one hard clap on the back each, and then Yara stepped onto the dock at the river-mouth town and watched the Salt-Luck turn for open water, riding lighter than she had since the day her captain lost her brother.

* * *

The walk home took nine days.

On the fourth day Yara noticed the flowers growing out of season. On the sixth, a stream they had always known as silent was making conversation with its own stones. On the eighth they crested the last ridge above the Valley of Stillness and stopped, because the valley was glowing.

Not burning. Glowing—the way a coal glows after the fire seems finished, low and persistent and utterly alive. Every field, every fence post, every grave marker in the little cemetery where Yara had buried the messenger whose name they had learned and spoken and given back to him—all of it shimmering at the very edge of sight, like the valley had been holding its breath for a century and had finally, quietly, let it out.

The neighbors came out of their houses when Yara walked down the main road. That had never happened. For twenty-eight years Yara Halloran had been furniture, background, the strange bone-carver nobody quite looked at. Now old Marta from the bakery pressed a loaf into their arms. Now the cooper's twin boys ran alongside asking questions—*is it true you talked to the dead, is it true the sea froze, is it true you broke a crown*—and their mother hauled them back apologizing, and Yara knelt in the road and told them yes, yes, and yes, and also that the dead mostly talk about bread, and weather, and whether you've been eating properly, and the boys' eyes went wide as moons.

The workshop was exactly as they'd left it: tools on the bench, shavings on the floor, the waterlogged prophecy book still open on its stand where they had thrown it the night everything began. Tonight's date had come and gone a hundred times since. Yara closed the book gently, like closing a door on a guest who had stayed too long, and shelved it.

Then they unlocked the front door, propped it wide, and nailed a sign above the lintel that read:

**CARVINGS COMMISSIONED. THE DEAD SPEAK. THE LIVING LISTEN. ALL GRIEFS WELCOME — NO ONE TURNED AWAY.**

The first client came before the sign's nails were dry: a farmer whose wife had died in the long winter, holding a lock of her hair wrapped in cloth, terrified and hopeful and trying very hard to pretend he wasn't either.

"She never liked the cold," he said. "I just want to know if she's somewhere warmer. If that's—could you even—"

Yara took the hair, and the bone blank, and their knife, and began.

Outside, the valley hummed. Somewhere far south, a sister sharpened a pen. Somewhere east, a priest wrote a first name in a blank book. Somewhere on the water, a captain sang off-key to her ship and meant every word.

And in a small workshop in a valley that remembered them at last, Yara Halloran carved, listened, and gave the dead back their names—one gift, one voice, one goodbye at a time—as if that had been the inheritance all along.
