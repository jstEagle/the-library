## 1. The Taste of Borrowed Lightning

The widow Maren Kell came to the shop at dusk, as she had come every dusk for a week, and stood in the doorway wringing her gloves like a man drowning holds rope.

Astrid Yarrow let her stand there. The shop was small — one room above a chandlery in Greyharrow's Salt Quarter, shelves of glass vials catching the last red light off the harbor, each one glowing faintly with something borrowed. A laugh. A summer. A mother's voice through a door. He kept them sorted by feeling rather than date, because people didn't come looking for dates. They came looking for weather.

"Master Yarrow," Maren said. "You said you'd have it by tonight."

"And I do." He reached beneath the counter and set the vial down between them, but he did not slide it across. Not yet. "Before we do this, we're going to talk again. Sit."

"I don't want to sit. I've been sitting for a year. I sat at his bedside, I sat at the graveside, I—"

"Maren." He said it gently. Gently was a tool like any other, and he kept his tools sharp. "Sit."

She sat.

Here is what Astrid sold: memories. Stolen ones, lifted clean out of a stranger's head with a touch and bottled before they could bruise. It was called charmwork on the street, and in Greyharrow — a city built on smuggled goods where half the customs ledgers were written in two hands and neither of them honest — nobody asked too hard where the merchandise came from. There were maybe a dozen memory-thieves working the Salt Quarter, most of them gutter-quick and careless, snatching whatever glittered. Astrid had rules instead, and the rules had kept him alive and mostly unhaunted for fifteen years.

Never steal love. Never steal grief.

Everything else was fair trade. A first snowfall. The smell of bread from a kitchen that no longer existed. The taste of borrowed lightning — that was what an old fisherman had once called the memory of standing on a cliff when the storm gods still answered, back before magic dried up to a trickle, and Astrid had liked it enough to paint it over his door in letters the rain kept trying to eat.

But love and grief, never. Those belonged to their owners. Take a woman's grief and you took the shape of the hole it left; take her love and you took the reason she'd survived having the hole at all. Astrid had watched another thief do it once, early on, back when he was learning the trade from a man named Corvo who had no rules at all. The customer had walked away lighter and smiling, and three days later she'd jumped from the Widow's Walk with her arms out like she was greeting someone. Astrid had been seventeen. He'd decided his rules that same night, and he'd kept them ever since, and he told himself — most nights successfully — that this was enough.

Which was why Maren Kell's commission had been troubling him all week.

"You want your husband's first kiss," he said. "His first kiss. Which means I'd have to find the boy he was before you, before the marriage, before all of it, and lift it out of him." He turned the vial slowly on the counter so the light moved through it. Inside, something golden shifted like honey remembering the sun. "I found him. Dockworker, retired, lives up Tannery Row. He remembers it fine — a girl behind the fish market, sixty years ago, salt wind and her laughing because he missed her mouth the first time."

Maren's breath went ragged. "That's it. That's exactly — Sera used to tell me about it. How he always said he missed on purpose because she was turning away, and how he never knew if that was true or just a story he'd grown into." She pressed her knuckles to her lips. "Please."

"It's not yours," Astrid said. "That's my problem with it. It belongs to him. And grief—" He stopped himself. Careful. "Grief makes us want things that aren't ours. That's not a sin. But I don't sell other people's beginnings, Maren. I sell moments that are loose. Moments people would give up if they knew how."

"He'll never miss it. He has sixty years of kisses after it. He won't even feel it go."

"That's not mine to decide either."

They looked at each other across the counter, across the little golden sun in its glass prison, and Astrid saw her decide something. She straightened. Widows learn to straighten; it's the whole art of it.

"Then I'm asking him," she said. "Tonight. I'll ask him to sell it to me, freely, and you can buy it fair and bottle it fair and I'll pay whatever you ask." She stood, and her chin did the thing chins do when they refuse to tremble. "Will that satisfy your rules, Master Yarrow?"

It shouldn't have. He could feel the flaw in it, the way a thief feels a bad lock — technically sound, morally rotten, because a grieving woman asking a soft-hearted old man for his treasure wasn't a free trade, it was a siege with polite grammar. But he thought of her face when she'd described the story, the way she held it like a coal cupped in both hands against a long cold year, and he found that his rules had a crack in them he hadn't known about, shaped exactly like her.

"Ask him," Astrid said. "If he says yes with a clear heart, I'll do it. If he hesitates even once, I walk away, and you don't get to hate me for it."

"He'll say yes. He always says yes to me."

"I know," Astrid said quietly. "That's what worries me."

She left. The bell over his door rang its cracked little note, and the shop settled back into its evening hush, forty small glows breathing on their shelves. Astrid stood a while with the vial of the first kiss in his palm. Somewhere below, the harbor slapped at its pilings. Somewhere farther, the tide went out toward the Sundered Sea, which everyone in Greyharrow spoke of the way you speak of a rich, dangerous uncle — with respect, and never after dark.

He put the vial under the counter and told himself he'd sleep on it.

He was lying to himself. He already knew he'd do it. That was the trouble with a carefully small life: eventually the careful part wore through, and you found out what the life was actually made of.

* * *

The priest came an hour past midnight, when the lamps along Rope Street had burned down to blue stubs and even the smugglers were asleep.

Astrid heard him on the stairs — wrong rhythm, dragging, one hand on the wall — and had the knife out of the drawer before the knock came. Fifteen years in Greyharrow teaches you the music of footsteps. These were footsteps at the end of a song.

"Master Yarrow." A voice like wet gravel. Through the door: "I know you keep late hours. I know what you are. I haven't the strength to be dangerous, I promise you."

Astrid opened the door with the knife low and casual, the way you hold a thing you're not holding.

The priest was drowned-looking in every sense — robes crusted white with old salt, beard gone to gray weed, skin the color of something fished up. His eyes were the worst part. They were calm. Men that close to death were usually frantic, bargaining with their bodies, but this one looked at Astrid with the mild patience of someone who had already finished all his arguing, with everyone, including the sea.

"Doran," the priest said. "Brother Doran, of the Tidebound. What's left of them." He swayed on the threshold. "May I come in? Standing up is becoming a negotiation."

Astrid should have shut the door. Inquisitors had been sniffing the Salt Quarter for a month — gray-cloaked, soft-booted, asking their quiet questions — and a priest of the drowned god on his stairs was the kind of trouble that got shops burned and thieves fitted for iron collars. Every instinct he'd sharpened since Corvo's time said *close the door*.

Instead he stepped aside. Later, he'd try to remember why, and the only answer he'd ever find was that the priest's calm frightened him more than the Inquisition did.

Doran made it to the chair by the shelf of childhoods and collapsed into it like a puppet whose strings had been cut one at a time. Up close, Astrid could hear the rattle in his chest, the sea-sound of lungs filling where they shouldn't.

"They told stories about you," the priest said, looking at the glowing shelves with something like wonder. "In the temple, before. A thief in Greyharrow who wouldn't touch grief. Who wouldn't touch love. We thought it was a parable. A nice little story we made up to teach the novices that the old virtues weren't dead, just hiding." His eyes found Astrid's. "You're real."

"What do you want?"

"To give you something. To sell you something. Both." Doran laughed, and the laugh cost him visibly, bending him double until he coughed brine onto the floorboards. "Forgive me. Dying is undignified work." He fumbled at his collar and drew out a chain, and on the chain hung a vial — but not like Astrid's. This one was dark glass, older, sealed with wax stamped by a hand long dead, and inside it something moved that was not light. More like weather. Like a storm deciding.

"The covenant," the priest whispered. "Do you know what sleeps under the Sundered Sea?"

"Stories. Gods asleep under the water. Children's tales."

"Yes," Doran said. "And someone is killing them in their sleep. One flame at a time. Dragon-fire, boy — the fire that fed the gods, that bound them to their bargain — and it's nearly gone, and the drought you've felt in your bones your whole trade, the thinness of the world's magic, the reason your little charms are all anyone can manage anymore — that's the dying, spreading outward from under the sea." He held out the dark vial, and his hand shook, and his eyes didn't. "This is the last true memory of the covenant. What was promised. What was paid. Who broke faith first." A wet smile. "It's heavy. Nothing you've ever stolen weighs anything next to this."

"I don't deal in prophecy. I deal in kisses and summers. Get yourself a—"

"I'm paying you," Doran said, "with my death. You'll understand soon. Take it."

And here is the thing Astrid could never afterward explain, not to Sera, not to himself, not to the fire when it finally asked: he took it. Some part of him that was older than his rules, older than his caution, some shard-shaped thing he'd carried all his life without naming, reached out and closed around the dark vial before his mind caught up.

The moment his skin touched the glass, the seal split like an egg.

The memory poured into him.

He had stolen ten thousand memories. He knew the taste of them — quick, bright, gone down like cold water. This was different. This was a hook behind his sternum. This was a current, and it did not flow in and settle; it *anchored*, driving deep, and when Astrid gasped and tried to push it out — tried the trick that had never failed him, the gentle inward turn that plucked any borrowed thing loose — it would not come. It rooted. It looked around inside him like a traveler entering a house and finding, with recognition, a room it had been searching for.

Doran slumped in the chair, suddenly lighter, suddenly smaller, the way donors looked when the theft was done. Except Astrid had taken nothing. The priest had given. Had *pressed*. And the pressing had gone somewhere it did not intend to leave.

"What did you do to me?" Astrid's voice cracked on it. The vial in his hand was empty now, just dark glass, and cold as harbor-bottom.

"Nothing," Doran murmured. His eyes were closing. "It chose. I only hoped. The fire knows its own, boy. It always—" A breath. A rattle. "There's a woman coming for you. Gray cloak. Soft boots. Vane, her name is. Kill order signed. You have perhaps two days, if the tides favor you. Follow the ash road east. Find Cinderveil. Find the warden. Tell her—"

His chest rose once more and did not fall.

The lamp guttered. On their shelves, forty small borrowed glows flickered — all together, all at once, like the whole shop inhaling — and then steadied.

Astrid Yarrow stood alone in his carefully small life, holding a dead man's empty vial, with a storm anchored behind his sternum that refused to leave, and understood that the smallness was over. Whatever came through that door next — inquisitor, god, or worse — the man who had lived by three neat rules was already gone.

Outside, far out past the harbor lights, the sea went strangely, completely silent.
