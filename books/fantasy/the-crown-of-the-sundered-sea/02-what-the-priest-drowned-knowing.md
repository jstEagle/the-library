## 2. What the Priest Drowned Knowing

The memory woke Astrid an hour before dawn, the way a stone wakes a man when someone throws it through his window.

He had slept with it under his pillow — a stupid instinct, the kind of thing you did with something that frightened you, and he had told himself all evening that it didn't frighten him. It was just another charm. A little heavier than most, warm where charms were usually cool, but charms came in every temperament. He'd once bottled a memory that hummed sea shanties. He'd sold a woman's whole wedding day to her own sister, and no one had called that strange.

But this one pressed back.

He lay in his narrow room above the chandler's shop and felt it against his palm like a second heartbeat, out of rhythm with his own. When he closed his eyes he could *see* through it — not the contents, not yet, but the shape of them, the way you can feel the shape of a letter through unbroken wax. Something vast. Something old. Salt and incense and a voice reciting names the way other men counted coins.

*It won't come out,* he thought, for perhaps the hundredth time since the priest had died in his arms on the chapel steps. Every charm Astrid had ever made could be poured back out again. That was the craft of it: memory went into glass the way water went into glass, and it came out the same way, unchanged, undiluted. He had never once failed to decant a memory he had stolen.

This one sat in its vial like a fist in a glove.

Astrid got up, dressed in the dark, and carried it down to his workroom, where the morning's first grey light came through salt-fogged windows and turned everything the color of old pewter. Greyharrow at dawn smelled of tallow and fish and cold iron — the honest smells of a dishonest city. He set the vial on his workbench between the racks of wares: first kisses, last words, the taste of a mother's cooking, one truly magnificent thunderstorm he'd been saving for a collector in Vail who liked his skies violent.

He picked it up. The warmth pulsed against his fingers.

"Right," he said aloud, because talking to memories was a habit the trade gave you and the trade never took back. "Let's see what you're hiding."

He did what he always did. He breathed slow, found the seam of himself — that place behind the sternum where his gift lived, small and bright as a struck match — and touched it to the glass.

The world tore open.

He was standing on a cliff that did not exist anymore. He knew it did not exist because he knew this coast, had walked every mile of it selling and stealing, and there was no white-stone temple on the headland above Greyharrow, only gulls and sheep-cropped grass. But in the memory the temple stood whole, its columns carved with waves, and below it the sea burned.

Not on fire. *Lit.* Under the water, deep down where the light should have died, great veins of gold-red glow ran along the seabed like the roots of some inverted tree, and where they converged — far out, past the shallows, in the trench the fishermen called the Sundered Deep — something vast and slow turned over in its sleep, and the light pulsed around it like a heart.

*A god,* Astrid understood, with the calm of someone standing inside another man's skull. *That's a god, actually sleeping, actually there.*

And then the priest's younger voice, from somewhere behind him, speaking the words of a rite Astrid had never heard and somehow already knew:

*"By flame the gods sleep sweetly. By flame their dreams keep faith with men. Seven fires burn beneath the world; while seven burn, the covenant holds; while the covenant holds, the drowned ones dream and do not wake."*

The memory shifted, fast now, years sliding by like cards being dealt. The priest — younger, then middle-aged, then old — walking those cliffs in every season. Watching the fires. Counting them. And Astrid watched with him and saw what the priest saw, and the dread came up through him slowly, the way cold comes up through boot leather when you stand too long on winter stone.

Five lights under the sea.

Then four.

Then three, and the sea around the darkened places gone grey and dead as ash, fish floating belly-up for miles, and the tides coming wrong, arriving hours late or not at all, and old women in the harbor villages whispering that their dreams had gone quiet, that the drowned god no longer answered even curses.

Two.

In the last stretch of memory the priest knelt on the empty cliff where the temple had been torn down — and Astrid felt the tearing of it, stone by stone, on orders signed in a hand the priest recognized and feared — and the priest wept, and said to no one, to the sea, to whatever shard of dragon-fire might still be listening:

*"Someone is killing them. Someone is putting out the fires one by one, and when the last goes dark, the gods will wake unfed and furious, and the covenant will be broken not by faithlessness but by murder. I am too old to carry this. God forgive me, I'm giving it to a thief."*

The memory ended.

Astrid came back to his workroom so hard he bit his tongue, and stood gripping the edge of the workbench while the pewter light climbed the walls and his own heartbeat slowed from gallop to trot to walk. His hands were shaking. He looked at them with something like curiosity. Fifteen years in the trade, and he had never once been shaken by merchandise.

*Seven fires,* he thought. *Two left.*

And someone — someone with power enough to pull down temples and sign extinction orders — was hunting the last two.

* * *

By noon he had done the sensible thing, which was to try to sell it.

There was a buyer in Greyharrow for nearly anything. Old Maren Quill bought grief-memories by the pound and resold them to mourners who wanted to feel a loss more deeply than their own lives allowed. The Kestrel brothers bought battle-joy for their pit-fighters. There was a magistrate who collected confessions he'd never earned and a poet who bought nothing but the smell of rain on different streets. Astrid had moved merchandise through all of them for fifteen years without a single question asked, because questions were bad for business and business was how you stayed small and alive in Greyharrow.

He started with Maren, because if anyone would pay for a dying priest's final testament, it was a woman whose whole trade was other people's deaths.

She held the vial to the lamp, and her brow furrowed. "Heavy," she said. "Warm." She uncorked it and held it near her cheek, the way connoisseurs did, letting the surface of the memory lap against her skin. Her eyes widened. She corked it fast and set it down on the counter between them as though it had bitten her.

"What is that?"

"Church goods," Astrid said mildly. "Rites and ruins. Sea views."

"Astrid." Maren leaned across the counter, and her voice dropped to the register she used for prices and threats, which in her shop were often the same conversation. "I looked into that vial and something looked back. I don't deal in things that look back. Take it elsewhere."

He tried the Kestrels, who laughed him out of their counting-house. He tried a scholar down Tallow Lane who bought theological curiosities, and the scholar took one look through the glass and went pale and refused payment, which had never happened before in the history of buying and selling. By late afternoon Astrid had offered the memory to six buyers and gotten six refusals, each faster than the last, and a pattern had begun to assemble itself in his mind with the ugly clarity of a bruise coming up.

Nobody in Greyharrow would touch this.

Not because it was worthless. Because it was dangerous — dangerous in a way that made hardened dealers of stolen souls flinch — and dangerous meant someone had taught them to flinch. Dangerous meant *someone is asking about this.* Dangerous meant —

He stopped in the middle of Rope Walk, a memory-vial seller frozen among the fishwives and rope-makers, and let the thought finish itself.

Dangerous meant the Inquisition.

He'd seen them twice in his life from a safe distance: grey coats, salt-white sashes, faces like locked doors. They didn't come to Greyharrow often. Magic this small, magic like his — bottled moments, harmless thefts, a widow's comfort at two silver — wasn't worth a fire-wagon ride down the coast road. The Inquisition hunted real sorcery. Dragon-work. The old covenant arts. Things that hadn't existed in living memory, which was rather the point of living memory.

So why, Astrid thought, with his pulse beginning to climb, does the fishwife say there's a grey-coat woman at the Salt Gate asking after me by name?

Because there was. He'd heard it an hour ago and filed it away as gossip, the way you file away weather. Now he walked — not fast, never fast, fast drew the eye — toward the harbor, and the pieces clicked together as he went. A priest of the drowned god dies on his doorstep clutching a memory about murdered gods. Within a day, an inquisitor arrives asking for Astrid Yarrow by name. Six buyers refuse the hottest item ever offered on Tallow Lane.

Either the Inquisition had killed the priest and lost what he carried, or they knew exactly what he'd carried and exactly who he'd given it to.

Both options ended badly. One of them, Astrid suspected, ended with him on a pyre, because the priest's memory had used a phrase that was now echoing in him with the dull insistence of a bell that wouldn't stop ringing:

*I'm giving it to a thief.*

Not *a memory-thief.* The priest had looked at him — Astrid had stood inside the man's dying eyes — and seen something else. Seen the shard. *Dragon-fire*, the memory had whispered at its edges, in words the priest hadn't spoken aloud but had known all the same. *The last living piece of it, walking around in a pickpocket's chest.*

Astrid reached the harbor as the fog thickened toward evening. The lamps along the quay were being lit, halos blooming in the murk, and beyond the breakwater the sea lay flat and dark and, he realized now, too dark — two fires left, and one of them guttering, and the god beneath it dreaming thinner dreams.

At the end of the quay, where the lamplight ran out, a woman stood watching the water. Grey coat. Salt-white sash. Sword at her hip worn the way soldiers wore them, like part of the arm. She didn't turn when he stopped twenty paces away, but she spoke, and her voice carried clean across the water-smell and the fog.

"Astrid Yarrow. You're harder to find than your reputation suggests."

"I don't have a reputation," Astrid said. "That's the whole point of me."

"Everyone has a reputation." Now she turned, and her face in the half-light was sharp and tired and entirely unreadable, and her eyes went briefly — deliberately, he was almost sure — to the pocket where the vial sat against his ribs, warm as a coal. "Mine says I'm here to kill you. Let's talk before either of us decides whether that's true."
