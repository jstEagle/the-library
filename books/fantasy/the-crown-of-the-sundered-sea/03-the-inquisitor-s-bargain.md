## 3. The Inquisitor's Bargain

Sera Vane had killed eleven heretics, and she had learned that the twelfth would be no different: a man made of meat and fear, kneeling in whatever light the room allowed, praying to gods who were too busy drowning to listen. That was what she told herself as she climbed the stairs of the tenement on Brine Street, salt-iron key in her fist, three novices at her back with their white hoods drawn low.

The file said *Astrid Yarrow, memory-thief, last of the fire-bearers. Execute on contact.*

She had read it four times on the crossing from Saltreach. It was a thin file. Thin files meant important prisoners, and important prisoners meant someone above her wanted this done quietly — which was its own kind of warning.

The lock surrendered to the key. She went in first, blade already clear, and found the room empty except for a table, two chairs, a cold lamp — and a man sitting in the corner shadow like he'd been expecting her for hours, which, knowing memory-thieves, he probably had.

He didn't look like the last shard of dragon-fire. He looked like a young man who hadn't slept, with soot still ghosting his knuckles and a priest's blood dried brown under one fingernail. He held up both hands, palms out, and said, "Before you do anything permanent — I want to make you an offer."

"Inquisitors don't take offers from heretics." She kept her voice flat. The novices fanned out behind her, chains whispering.

"No," he agreed. "But you haven't called me heretic yet. You've been standing there for six full seconds deciding whether to. That's longer than the others took."

"Kill him," Sera said, because the file told her to, and because the alternative was admitting he'd read something true off her face.

The novices moved. And Astrid Yarrow did not flinch, did not run — he simply looked at the nearest one and said, "You're Marisol. You have a sister in the Saltreach orphanage you send money to through a fishmonger named Teo, and last month the money stopped coming, and you've been afraid to ask why."

The novice froze mid-swing, chain slack in her hand. Her hood tilted.

"I don't steal love or grief," Astrid said quietly. "I never have. But I can see where they're kept. And yours is leaking, girl. Someone's been in your head."

The room went very still. Sera heard Marisol's breathing change, heard the second novice's chain stop moving, and understood in one cold drop that this man had just done more damage with a sentence than she could have done with the whole chain.

"Out," she said to all three. "Wait downstairs."

Marisol hesitated — a heartbeat of loyalty pulling against six years of training — then went. The door closed. The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, Greyharrow's harbor bells counted the hour.

"You could have let them kill me," Astrid said.

"The file says otherwise."

"The file also says I'm the last fire-thief, which means whoever wrote it knows things about me that I only learned three days ago from a dying priest. So either your Grandmother has been watching me my whole life—" he leaned forward, elbows on knees, eyes catching the lamplight like coins at the bottom of a well "—or she's been watching *him*. The priest. Father Ondrey. Which means she knew about the covenant before I did."

Sera's hand tightened on her blade. Not because he was wrong. Because he was right, and she had spent the crossing trying not to think exactly that.

"Here's my offer," Astrid continued. "Three days ago a dying man poured a memory into my hands and it won't come out. It's the Sundered Covenant — the old bargain, the gods asleep under the sea, fed by dragon-fire. And someone has been putting those fires out. One by one. For years. Your Inquisition's whole war on magic? It isn't protection. It's starvation."

"You're a thief telling an inquisitor the law is corrupt. Forgive me if I need more than a bedtime story."

"Then arrest me. Take me to Saltreach in chains. Put me in front of your Grandmother and watch her face when I say the word *covenant*." He spread his hands. "I'm offering you the truth, Inquisitor Vane. All I want is to walk out of this room alive long enough to find who's killing gods."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you execute the last fire-bearer on the orders of the woman who ordered the fires put out, and when the sea finally goes dead and nothing grows anywhere ever again, you'll know whose hand was on the knife."

Sera had faced down smugglers, storm-cults, a possessed lighthouse-keeper. None of them had frightened her the way this tired young man did — because he wasn't lying. Her order trained her to hear lies the way sailors hear weather, and this room was clear skies all the way to the horizon.

*Execute on contact.* She thought of her own induction, the Grandmother's dry hands on her crown, the words *we burn magic so it cannot burn us*. She had been nineteen and grateful. She had never once asked: burn it *where*?

"If I help you," she said slowly, "and we find nothing — no conspiracy, no extinction order, just a mad priest's fever dream — then I put the chains on you myself. No bargains after."

"Fair."

"And if we find what you say we'll find—"

"Then you lose my file," he said. "All of it. Every record, every report, every name I've ever sold to. I vanish. You get to keep believing you're the good guys."

It was madness. It was treason. It was, she suspected, the first honest conversation she'd had since she took the white hood.

"One condition," she said. "I lead. You follow. You speak when spoken to."

Astrid smiled for the first time, and it transformed his whole face into something younger and more reckless. "Inquisitor, in fifteen years of theft, no one has ever caught me. You walked into my room without me hearing you on the stairs. Lead away."

* * *

They went to the harbormaster at dawn, because harbormasters were the Inquisition's left hand in every port city — paid informants, sworn to the salt oath, eyes on every hull and hold. Corrick Mael ran Greyharrow's harbor the way a spider runs a web, and Sera had met his kind before: men whose loyalty was a ledger with two columns.

She brought Astrid in as her prisoner, wrists bound with ceremonial cord, hood half-drawn. It was theater, and they both knew it, and Corrick's office smelled of ink and old fish while he smirked at them across a desk stacked with manifests he'd clearly never read.

"Fire-thief, is it?" Corrick said, sucking air through his teeth. "Grand work. The Grandmother herself will want confirmation before transport. Standard procedure — I'll need to send word ahead. Salt hawk takes a day."

"There's no time," Sera said. "The prisoner is unstable. Memory-magic. He's been tampering with my novices' minds."

Corrick's eyes flicked to Astrid, and Sera watched something pass behind them — recognition, yes, but also *calculation*. This man had known. Had known about Astrid, perhaps about the priest, and had sat on it, waiting to see which way the wind blew.

"Tampering," Corrick repeated. "Nasty business. Of course, if the prisoner were to be *damaged* during transport — drowned, say, very common in Greyharrow — certain interested parties might rest easier. There are those who'd pay handsomely for that service."

"Name them," Sera said.

Corrick laughed. "Inquisitor. Come now. We're all servants of the same white hood."

"We are," Sera agreed pleasantly, and drew her blade, and laid it flat across his desk between the manifest and the inkwell. "Which is why you're going to tell me who offered to buy a dead fire-thief, and why your last three salt-hawk dispatches went to Saltreach addressed not to the Order but to a private seal. A seal I saw on the Grandmother's own correspondence last winter."

The laugh died. The office went quiet enough that Sera could hear the tide against the pilings below.

"You've seen it," Corrick said, and his voice had changed entirely — gone small, gone careful, the voice of a man stepping off solid ground. "The black wave on grey wax."

"I've seen it. Now tell me what it buys."

Corrick looked at the blade. At her face. At Astrid, bound and silent, watching him with the patient hunger of a man who could see exactly where grief was kept.

"He came to me eight months ago," Corrick said finally. "An envoy. White robes, but no Order colors — wrong cut, older. Said the Grandmother required the ports watched for anyone carrying *unlicensed flame*. Said when the last bearer surfaced, I was to delay any Order response until private arrangements could be made." He swallowed. "There's a ship, Inquisitor. The *Quiet Fathom*. It doesn't appear in any registry. It comes to the far anchorage at night, takes on cargo, leaves before dawn. And three weeks ago it carried out crates marked with the black wave, and the crates were *warm*. Warm enough to steam in the night air. I helped load one myself, and inside—" his voice dropped to almost nothing "—inside was a brazier. Iron, old, older than anything I've seen. And it was going out. Like watching a man die. There was still a coal in it glowing, just barely, and the envoy looked at it and said, *only seven left now*, and laughed."

"Seven what?" Astrid asked softly.

"Fires," Corrick whispered. "God-fires. He called them god-fires."

Sera felt the floor tilt beneath everything she had believed. Eleven heretics. Six years of the white hood. A lifetime of *we burn magic so it cannot burn us* — and all along, the burning had been the point. Not protection. Extinction. Signed, sealed, dispatched under a private seal by the woman whose hands had crowned her.

"When did the *Quiet Fathom* last dock?" she asked.

"Two nights ago. It sails again tomorrow, midnight tide, back to wherever these braziers go." Corrick wiped sweat from his lip. "That's all I know, I swear it on the salt. But Inquisitor — the envoy. You should know what he said when I asked why. Why starve the gods. He said it wasn't the Grandmother's idea originally. Said the order came down *to* her, years ago, from someone older than the Order itself. Someone who remembered what the gods demanded the last time they were awake."

Outside, the harbor bells rang the half-hour. Tomorrow, midnight. Less than a day.

Sera looked at Astrid. He was already looking at her, and there was no triumph in his face, only a grim kind of confirmation — and underneath it, she realized, something that looked almost like pity. Pity for her. For the hood, the oaths, the Grandmother's dry hands on her nineteen-year-old crown.

"Well, Inquisitor?" he said quietly. "Do we have a deal?"

She cut his bonds with one stroke of the blade that had killed eleven heretics, and answered the only honest way left to her.

"We sail tonight. And Astrid Yarrow — pray your priest was wrong about one thing only. Pray to every drowned god you like that whoever gave my Grandmother her orders isn't who I think it is. Because if it is—" she sheathed the steel, and her hand did not shake, though everything else in her wanted to "—then the thing we're chasing isn't a conspiracy. It's a debt. And old debts, my grandmother used to say, are collected in children."

Above the harbor, the first gulls of morning wheeled over water that had gone, in the past year, visibly colder — and somewhere beneath the grey swell, in the dark where the last seven fires guttered, something ancient turned over in its sleep, and began, slowly, to dream of waking.
