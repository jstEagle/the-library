## 4. Salt and Ash Road

### Astrid

Greyharrow burned behind them by the time the road gave out.

Not the whole city — just the harbor district, the harbormaster's gilded counting house, the archive where Sera had pulled her own file and set it alight in a brazier of salt. A small fire, a message fire. By morning the Inquisition would know exactly which way its lost daughter had gone, and by evening they would be following.

The ash-lands began where Greyharrow's stink ended. Astrid felt the change before he saw it: a silence in his chest, like a room someone had just left. For fifteen years he had carried his charms against his skin, little warm coins of other people's lives, and now, crossing the border stone into the old province of Vael — where magic had once been so thick that children learned to whistle storms — every charm sat cold as river stones.

"Feel that?" Sera asked. She rode half a length ahead, her gray cloak pulled tight, her sword riding easy on her hip in the way of someone who had worn it so long it was less a weapon than a limb.

"The charms went quiet."

"That's what killed this place." She said it flatly, the way you'd report weather. "Vael was the garden of the coast, once. Vineyards to the hills. My order's histories call it the Flourishing. Then the flames started going out, one by one, and the land went with them." She gestured with her chin at the country around them: black soil, gray grass gone to wire, orchards of trees that had died standing, their branches like fingers pointing at a sky that never quite committed to rain.

"You sound like you believe the histories."

"I was raised on them." A pause. "I believed them until three days ago."

They rode on. The road beneath them was old imperial paving, cracked and shouldered apart by nothing at all — even weeds wouldn't grow here. Astrid had stolen memories from farmers in the border towns, small bright things: a wedding feast, a first frost, a child's laugh at a dog chasing gulls. He found himself turning them over in his mind like a miser counting coin, needing to be sure they were still there. They were. But they were cold, and cold was new, and new frightened him more than he let show.

At dusk they made camp in the ribs of something enormous — a temple, maybe, or a granary, its walls fanned open to the sky like the carcass of a whale. Sera built no fire. "Smoke reads for miles out here," she said, and shared her dried fish and hard bread without ceremony, dividing it with the precision of a woman who had provisioned herself for two since the moment she decided not to kill him.

Astrid ate, and watched her, and tried to reconcile the woman across the ashes with the file he'd glimpsed in the harbormaster's cellar before they torched it. *Sera Vane. Salt Inquisitor, Third Veil. Seventeen executions authorized, nine confirmed.* Nine people she had killed for doing, in essence, what he did for a living.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm deciding whether to be afraid of you."

"And?"

"I haven't decided."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, visiting from a life she'd apparently lived before the Inquisition got her. "Good," she said. "Stay undecided. It'll keep you sharp." She wrapped herself in her cloak facing outward, toward the dark plain. "Sleep. I'll take first watch."

He slept, and dreamed of the priest's memory — the covenant, the sleeping gods, the long slow murder of the flames — and woke twice with the taste of brine in his mouth.

### Sera

On the third day of the ash-lands, the wraiths found them.

Sera smelled them before she saw them — a smell like the inside of a shell, like tide pools left too long in the sun. She reined up and put out an arm across Astrid's chest, and he stopped, obediently, which told her he'd felt it too. The air had gone wrong. Thick. Listening.

"What is it?" he whispered.

"Wraiths. Memory-wraiths. The histories say—" She stopped. The histories were written by people who had never seen one, because the wraiths had starved out with everything else when the magic died. The histories said they were extinct. The histories, Sera was beginning to understand, were mostly lies told by an order that needed them true.

They came out of the ground itself, or seemed to — pale shapes rising through the gray grass like steam given ambition. Three of them. Then five. They had no faces, only a smoothness where faces went, and they moved without legs or urgency, gliding, patient, the way water is patient.

"They're drawn to memory," Astrid said. His voice had gone thin. "That's — the priest's gift. It's still in me. It's *loud*, Sera. It's been loud since we crossed the border, I thought it was my imagination—"

"How do we kill them?"

"We don't. You don't burn smoke." He was backing his horse, eyes fixed on the nearest shape, which had begun to drift toward him with terrible unhurried interest. "You outrun them, or you feed them something else. A charm. A cheap one, a distraction—"

His hand went to the pouch at his belt and came away empty. Sera saw it happen — saw the moment he understood that the strap had been cut clean somewhere in the last mile, that his charms lay scattered behind them along the road like bread crumbs, that whatever had cut them had wanted exactly this: a fire-thief alone with nothing to give but himself.

"Astrid," she said quietly. "Your pouch."

He looked down. The nearest wraith made a sound then, and it was the worst thing Sera had heard in seventeen years of the Inquisition's worst sounds — a wet, yearning keening, the sound of hunger remembering what it used to eat.

It came for him fast after that. Faster than anything without a body should move. And Sera discovered something about herself in the space between one heartbeat and the next, something that would have gotten her executed in Greyharrow square: she did not hesitate.

She put her horse between the wraith and Astrid and drew her blade — not the salt-iron executioner's edge, which she'd surrendered with her oath, but the plain steel underneath — and she cut. Steel shouldn't bite wraith; steel is nothing to smoke. But the blade had been quenched, years ago, in blessed brine, back when she still believed in blessings, and the wraith came apart around her edge with a shriek like a gull scalded in boiling water. Cold washed over her, a cold full of someone else's dying summer, and then it was gone, and there were four more coming, and Sera Vane stood in the ash of the Flourishing and fought like a woman who had finally found a fight worth having.

She killed two. The third got past her — she heard Astrid cry out, heard the wet keening turn triumphant — and she turned in time to see it pouring itself into his mouth, his nose, his ears, smoke drowning a man from the inside.

What the order taught, in such cases, was simple: stand clear, mark the time of death, report.

Sera grabbed him by the collar instead, hauled him off his horse, and drove both of them into the dead fountain at the center of the ruin — dry for a century, choked with ash — because the histories said one more true thing about wraiths: they could not cross running water, and water remembered how to run if you *insisted*. She pressed her palm flat against the fountain's cracked basin and thought, with everything she had, *flow* — and something answered. Not much. A seep. A trickle of brown water rising through the ash-choked basin like blood through a bandage. Enough.

The wraith recoiled out of Astrid's mouth like a hook leaving flesh, shrieked, and came apart against the trickle of water as though it had struck a waterfall.

Then it was quiet, and they were lying tangled in a dry fountain in the middle of a dead country, breathing hard, her hand still fisted in his collar, his heart hammering under her forearm, close enough that she could count the ash in his eyelashes.

"That," Astrid said hoarsely, "was not in your file."

"No," Sera said. "It wasn't."

She let go of him. She did it slowly, which surprised her, and she didn't move away afterward, which surprised her more. They stayed in the fountain like two shipwreck survivors on a raft, and the water — their small stubborn miracle of water — kept seeping up between them, cold and alive.

"They cut your pouch," she said at last. "Someone followed us out of Greyharrow. Someone who wanted the wraiths to have you."

"The Inquisition doesn't use wraiths."

"The Inquisition doesn't admit wraiths exist." She stared out at the plain, where the remaining shapes had withdrawn, circling at a distance, patient as tide. "Whoever's killing the gods has tools my order pretends it never forged. Which means whoever's killing the gods isn't outside my order, Astrid. They're *inside* it."

He was quiet a while. When he spoke, his voice had changed — stripped of the smuggler's silk, down to something plainer and younger. "You broke every rule you have today. For me."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Sera looked at him — really looked, the way she'd been trained to look at suspects, and hated herself for noticing that the training had slid into something else. "Because I spent seventeen years executing thieves," she said, "and I've just learned the thefts were the honest part. Because Grandmother signed the extinction order and called it protection. Because if I'm going to burn my life down, I'd rather it be for something than against something." She paused. "And because you looked afraid. And I couldn't stand it. How's that? Honest enough?"

"Honest enough," he said softly.

* * *

### Astrid

They walked after that, leading the horses, because the wraiths circled and horses feared them and fear travels down the reins. For two days they moved through the ash-lands together, and Astrid noticed things he would have charged money to steal, if anyone could have sold him his own seeing.

He noticed that Sera hummed when she thought he was asleep — three notes, over and over, some lullaby from a childhood she'd never mentioned. He noticed that she counted her steps in fours, always fours, a habit drilled so deep it had become breath. He noticed that when she talked about the order, her hands talked too, shaping the words, and that her hands went still and careful whenever she talked about her family, the way people go still around a wound they've decided not to look at.

And he noticed — this on the second night, sitting watch together in another dead ruin, shoulders almost touching — that he had stopped keeping track of what she owed him and what he owed her. With everyone else in fifteen years, Astrid Yarrow had run a ledger. Debts and payments, charms and coin, everything balanced, nothing carried over. With Sera the ledger had quietly closed itself, and he hadn't noticed the exact page.

"The priest's memory," she said, into the silence. "It's still loud?"

"Louder. Every day we come south, it's like... someone turning a lamp up. Like it knows where it's going." He rubbed his sternum, where the memory lived now, warm as a coal. "Sometimes I think it's not a memory at all. Sometimes I think it's a compass."

"To Cinderveil."

"To whatever's left." He glanced at her. "There's something else. Since the wraith. When it was inside me, I saw — pieces. Of the thing that sent it. Or made it. A harbor. A fleet under sail. A very old woman's hands signing something." He hesitated. "Sera. The hands wore your order's rings."

She didn't answer for a long time. Out on the plain, the last light was failing, and the gray grass bent all one direction, as though bowing to something far off in the south — something below the horizon, below the sea, waiting.

"My grandmother trained me," Sera said finally. "Not Grandmother-the-title. My actual grandmother, before the order took me formal. She taught me the lullaby I hum. She taught me the counting." Her hands had gone still. "When I get to Cinderveil, Astrid, I'm going to find out which grandmother signed that order. And God help me — whichever one it is —"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Far to the south, beyond the edge of the world, the horizon flickered — once, twice — a red pulse against the dark, like a coal breathing under ash.

Cinderveil. Still burning. Barely.

"There," Astrid whispered, and the priest's memory flared in his chest so hot and so glad that for one moment he forgot to be afraid —

—and in the grass behind them, silent as falling snow, the remaining wraiths began, at last, to close in.
