## 5. The God Who Answers Collect

The ash-lands had a way of making a man count what he carried.

Astrid did it every night now, the way other travelers counted coins or arrows. He'd sit by whatever fire Sera allowed — she rationed flame like a miser, convinced the light drew Inquisition eyes even out here where there was nothing left to burn — and he'd open his coat's inner lining and touch each charm in turn. Glass beads no bigger than a thumbnail, stoppered with wax and prayer. Fifteen years of theft, sorted by weight.

*The taste of rain on the Harrow rooftops.* Light as a seed.

*His mother's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrist.* Heavier. Grief always made memories dense.

*The priest's covenant.* That one he kept separate, wrapped in wool at the bottom of the satchel, because it was not for sale and never had been, and because some nights he swore it grew warmer against his ribs, like a coal refusing to die.

Sera slept badly on the road. He could tell by her breathing — the sharp little catches when a dream went wrong, the long controlled exhales when she forced herself back under. An inquisitor's sleep, drilled into her by people who taught that rest was just another vulnerability to be managed. He found himself listening for it. He told himself it was tactical. A man should know when his guard was awake.

That night, the fire burned low and blue, which should have been his first warning. Ash-wood didn't burn blue. Nothing burned blue.

He reached into the lining to check the charms — habit, ritual, prayer of a godless trade — and his fingers closed around one bead that was *wrong*.

It was warm. Not priest-warm. Warm like skin. Warm like a hand held too long over a candle, and when he lifted it to the dying light, the memory inside wasn't still. Memories sat quiet in their glass; that was the whole point of them, the thing that made them sellable. This one moved. It swirled like ink dropped in water, dark and turning, and through the glass he heard — faint, wet, patient — a voice.

*"You kept so many. Surely you can spare an old man one conversation."*

Astrid dropped the bead.

It didn't shatter. It landed in the ash and lay there glowing softly, and the voice came again, no longer needing the glass, rising out of the ground itself, out of the dead soil, out of the air that hadn't held rain in a hundred years.

*"Don't be frightened, thief. Frightened men make poor decisions, and I have come a very long way to negotiate."*

"Sera," Astrid started to say, and then stopped, because saying her name aloud felt like handing something over. The voice noticed everything. He understood that immediately, the way you understand a knife is sharp — not through argument but through the body.

"Who are you?" he said instead, low.

*"You know. The priest knew. You've been carrying my brother's covenant against your heart for three weeks and telling yourself it's just merchandise."* A sound like water receding over stones — laughter, maybe. *"I am Nereus. I am the tide that comes back. I am the debt your gods have been avoiding since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to fear the sea."*

The bead pulsed in the ash. And Astrid — fool, professional, man who had built a life on knowing exactly what a memory was worth — looked at it, and felt it look back.

"I don't make deals with things I can't see."

*"Then see."*

The world folded.

Not violently — that was the horror of it. It folded the way a page turns, gently, and Astrid was standing in a kitchen that no longer existed: low ceiling, copper pots, the smell of yeast and woodsmoke. His mother's kitchen. The one in Greyharrow, before Greyharrow ate itself, before the rent and the sickness and the winter his father didn't come home from the fishing boats.

A boy sat at the table, seven years old, legs swinging, watching bread rise. He had Astrid's crooked nose and a gap in his smile that the adult Astrid had forgotten he'd ever had.

Astrid's throat closed. Because this was the memory. Not a reconstruction, not a god's conjuring trick — it was *the* memory, the original, the one he had sold at fourteen to a broker named Quill for enough coin to bury his mother and eat through spring. He remembered selling it. He remembered the hollow it left, the shape of a childhood missing from inside him, the way you feel a pulled tooth with your tongue for the rest of your life.

"You can't have this," he whispered. "It's sold. It's gone. Someone else owns it."

*"Someone else owned it,"* Nereus said, and the kitchen rippled like the surface of deep water. *"Memories change hands, thief. You of all people know that. I bought it from a broker in a drowned city. Do you know what men throw away, down where the pressure makes them honest? Everything. I am rich beyond your imagining in things people chose to forget."*

The boy at the table looked up. Looked at him. Smiled with the gap-toothed grin and said, in a child's voice pitched exactly right, "You came back."

Astrid put his hand flat on the table to steady himself. The wood was warm. Real. His mother turned from the hearth with flour on her wrists and said, "You're letting the cold in, love. Shut the door."

"There is no door," Astrid said. "There's no house. There's no — " His voice cracked. "This is a sales pitch."

*"Of course it is."* No shame in it. The sea doesn't apologize for being deep. *"I am offering you a bargain, and I wanted you to understand the merchandise. So. Let us speak plainly, man who trades in plain things."*

The kitchen dimmed. The boy faded first, then the bread, then his mother's hands — last, always the hands — until Astrid stood alone in a grey nowhere that smelled of salt.

*"Your old gods sleep beneath the Sundered Sea. They do not dream, they do not wake, because the covenant binds them — fed on dragon-fire, chained by their own promise to stay their hands from the world of men. For two thousand years they kept it. And for two thousand years, men like your Grandmother Vane have been snuffing the flames, one by one, calling it piety. Calling it protection."* The voice curled closer. *"There is one fire left. You are carrying its last living shard in your coat, and you have been sleeping beside it like a man napping on a lit fuse."*

"It stays bound," Astrid said. "Whatever the gods promised, they promised. That's what a covenant *is*."

*"Is it?"* Something vast shifted in the grey, the suggestion of shoulders, of scales, of a mouth. *"Come to the water, Astrid Yarrow, and I will show you what your covenant actually says. What the fine print holds. What the gods took as their fee the first time — and what they'll take when they wake hungry. Your inquisitor's own family knows the price of a god's attention. Ask her why her order really exists."*

"That's not my problem."

*"No. Your problem is smaller and crueler."* The grey parted, and there — floating between them like a charm the size of a cathedral — hung the memory of the kitchen, whole and glowing. *"Here is my offer. Shatter the covenant. Give me the shard, unspent. And I return this to you — not a copy, not a comfort. The original. Your childhood, restored, seamless, yours forever. Every year of it. Your mother's voice. Your father coming up the harbor road. The boy with the crooked nose, grown into you like a root into soil. You would be a whole man, Astrid. You have been a rented room your entire adult life, and you know it. I am offering you the deed."*

Astrid stood very still.

That was the trap of it — that the offer was true. He could taste the truth of it the way he tasted truth in any memory he handled: the grain of it, the weight. Nereus had his childhood. It existed. It could come home.

And underneath the wanting, cold and clear, the trader in him ran the numbers. *Nothing is free. Especially not from something that speaks in offers.*

"What does it cost me," he said, "beyond the shard?"

*"Beyond the shard? Nothing you haven't already spent."* The voice softened, which was worse. *"You think I'm asking you to betray someone. I'm not. Think, thief. If the gods wake bound to a broken covenant, magic returns to the world — real magic, wild magic, the kind that made the ash-lands green. The kind your charms are only an echo of. You would not lose anything. You would gain everything you sold, and the world would gain everything it lost. The only ones who suffer are the ones who built the drought. The Grandmothers. The Inquisition. The woman you're walking beside, whose order murdered the last warden's family and called it holy work."*

"She's not — " Astrid stopped.

Too late. The silence stretched, and Astrid could feel the god smiling the way you feel weather changing.

*"Ah,"* said Nereus. *"Yes. Her. I wondered how long you'd take."*

"It's not like that."

*"It is always like that. It has been like that since the first man lied to himself beside a fire."* The grey began to close, the kitchen-memory sinking back into the dark like a lantern going down into deep water. *"I won't press tonight. I am the tide, Astrid Yarrow — I do not need to hurry. But know this: the covenant demands a price too, and its price is steeper than mine. Ask the fire what it eats before you feed it. Ask what fifteen years of stolen moments weigh, and what else burns besides."*

"The shard is not for sale," Astrid said. "To anyone."

*"Everything is for sale,"* said the drowned god, gentle as drowning. *"You taught the world that. I'm only learning from the master."*

* * *

He woke face-down in the ash with Sera's boot in his ribs and her blade drawn, kneeling over him, eyes wide and furious in the pre-dawn grey.

"—third time I've shaken you," she was saying. "You were talking. To nobody. Your lips were moving and there was nobody there, Astrid."

He pushed himself up. His coat was open. The charms were scattered around him in a ring, every single one of them out of the lining, arranged in a perfect circle — and every one of them empty. Clear glass. Wax stoppers intact. Fifteen years of theft, gone like breath off a mirror.

Except two. The priest's covenant, clutched in his fist so hard the glass had cut his palm. And one more, sitting apart from the circle, warm as a held hand: a kitchen. Copper pots. Flour to the wrist. A boy with a gap in his smile.

Nereus hadn't taken it. He'd left it. A down payment. A hook.

"Astrid." Sera's voice, sharp. "What talked to you?"

He looked at the empty charms, at fifteen years of small salvations evaporated, and heard the god's last words settling into him like water into a hull: *ask what else burns besides.*

"The sea," he said. "And it made me an offer I'm afraid I understood perfectly."

Sera followed his gaze to the one charm left in the ring — the childhood, glowing faintly, waiting — and when she looked back at him, he saw she already knew it wasn't his anymore.

Because nothing was. Not anymore. The question was who it belonged to now — and what it would cost to keep it.

"We move," Sera said, standing, sheathing the blade. "Now. Whatever bought you a dream, it knows exactly where we are."

She was right. That was the worst part. Somewhere past the ash-hills, past the drowned roads, the tide was coming in — and it had given him until the water reached Cinderveil to decide.

Astrid closed his hand around the kitchen, around his mother's flour-dusted wrists, around the boy who believed he'd come back, and got up to follow the inquisitor toward the last fire in the world.

*Ask what else burns besides.*

He intended to. He was afraid the answer was *her*.
