## 6. Grandmother's Confession

They took her irons off in the antechamber, which was the first wrong thing.

Sera Vane had been a prisoner before — twice, in her novitiate years, for infractions she'd never regretted — and she knew the choreography of it. Prisoners kept their chains until the last possible moment. Prisoners were marched, not escorted. Prisoners were made to wait in cold rooms with their own sweat going stale on them while someone upstairs decided how much of their life they would be permitted to keep.

Instead, two Salt Brothers walked her down a corridor that smelled of beeswax and old stone, and a third opened the door at its end and stood aside, head bowed, like a servant admitting family.

The chamber beyond was small. That surprised her too. She had imagined Grandmother's audience hall as something vast — a cathedral of salt-stained glass, ranks of kneeling inquisitors, the weight of the whole order pressing down. But this was just a room above the harbor at Vespergate, with one window, one lamp, and one woman sitting at a plain table with a cup of tea going cold beside her.

"Sit down, child," said Grandmother. "You look half-drowned."

Sera did not sit. "Where is Astrid Yarrow?"

"Gone. The fleet went to Cinderveil; he wasn't on it." Grandmother turned her teacup a quarter-rotation, a habit Sera had seen a thousand times across a thousand briefing tables, back when Grandmother had merely been Mother Superior Corvane and Sera had been eleven years old and certain of everything. "He'll come to the fire. They always come to the fire. Sit."

"You know I can't."

"I know you already have." The old woman looked up then, and her eyes were the same gray as the winter sea off Greyharrow, and tired in a way Sera had never let herself see before. "You broke a memory-wraith apart with your bare will to save a fire-thief. You burned your writ of execution in an ash-land gully. You've been sitting in my chair of judgment since the moment you met him, girl. The only question left is what we do with you now. So sit, and I will tell you why there was ever an order to kill him at all."

It was the *why* that did it. Not the command, not the threat. Inquisitors were trained against commands and threats the way swimmers are trained against waves. But Sera had spent twenty years serving an order whose reasons she had never once been permitted to ask about, and the word *why* slipped under all her armor the way water slips under a door.

She sat.

Grandmother poured a second cup. The tea was black and bitter, cut with salt — the inquisitor's draught, meant to sharpen the mind against charm-work. A courtesy, or a message: *I expect you to remember every word of this.*

"The order teaches that magic was outlawed to protect people," Grandmother began. "That charms breed dependence, that memory-theft unmakes the thief, that gods demand sacrifice and men are better off praying to nothing. All true, as far as it goes. Every lie worth telling is built out of true stones." She drank. "But the law came first, Sera. Before the abuses. Before the accidents. Before any of the stories we use to justify it. Do you know what happened in the year the Covenant was signed?"

"There was a drought of magic. The ash-lands—"

"There was a *bargain*." The old woman set down her cup with a click that sounded, in the quiet room, like a lock turning. "The old gods slept beneath the Sundered Sea, and dragon-fire fed them, and while they slept the world had wild magic — floods of it, uncontrolled, wonderful and monstrous. And the gods dreamed, and in their dreaming they *asked*. That is the nature of a god, child. It does not command. It asks, and asks, and asks, and eventually the asking becomes the shape of the world around you."

Sera's hands had gone still in her lap. She was aware of her heartbeat, slow and heavy, like something walking up stairs.

"One god asked more clearly than the rest," Grandmother said. "Nereus. The Drowned King. He asked for payment for his protection of the coasts — a tithe, he called it. Ships, sometimes. Cities, occasionally. And once—" she paused, and for the first time her voice lost its evenness, frayed at one edge, "—once, a family. A fisherman's family from the Vespergate shore. Seven souls. The god said the sea had counted them and found the arithmetic pleasing."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"My grandmother was that fisherman's wife," Grandmother said. "My mother was pulled from the water at age six, the only one who surfaced. She grew up and told me the story, and I grew up and did something about it. I joined the order when the order was twelve heretics in a cellar with a theory: that the gods are fed by fire, that fire can be starved, and that a world with no gods in it is a world where no one gets counted into anyone's arithmetic." She folded her hands on the table, and Sera saw that they shook — slightly, barely, the tremor of very old anger banked but never extinguished. "We didn't outlaw magic to protect people, Sera. We outlawed magic to starve a god. Everything else — the doctrine, the pyres, the file on your Astrid Yarrow — everything else is scaffolding around that single beam."

"The extinction order," Sera said. Her voice came out flat. "The harbormaster's ledger. Your signature."

"Mine. And my predecessor's. And hers before her, back two hundred years, because the work is not finished. There were seven flames feeding the covenant. Six are out. One remains, on Cinderveil, and when it dies the gods sleep dreamless forever and no one is ever asked for anything again." Grandmother leaned forward. "Yarrow carries the last living shard of dragon-fire in his body. He doesn't know it yet — or he suspects, which is worse. If he reaches Cinderveil and gives himself to that flame, the covenant reforges, the gods wake, and Nereus opens his mouth and starts *asking* again. My fleet is burning the isle as we speak. But fire finds fire, and I am an old woman, and so I am sitting here drinking bad tea with the one person who might stop him another way."

"What way?"

"Tell him the truth," Grandmother said simply. "You're the only one he'll believe."

Sera laughed — a short, ugly sound with nothing in it. "You want me to defect to save your extinction. Is that it? Burn my writ, break my oaths, and then carry out your order anyway by other means?"

"No." For a moment Grandmother looked her full in the face, and behind the gray eyes Sera saw the cellar-heretic, the daughter of the drowned, the woman who had spent a lifetime building a machine whose teeth had finally reached her own hand. "I want you to understand that you were right to burn the writ. That's the confession, child. That's the whole of it. I built a church on a true stone, and the church became a lie, and I knew, and I kept signing. Because stopping felt like letting my family drown twice." Her hands were shaking openly now. "You asked once, in your novitiate year — you asked Mother Corvane why we burned a hedge-witch who'd done nothing but ease a dying man's pain. Do you remember what I told you?"

"*The law is the shield between the sea and the shore.*"

"And I believed it. And it was true for a hundred years, maybe. And now there is a man walking toward Cinderveil carrying the last flame of the old world inside him, and a god beneath the sea offering him his childhood back if he'll break the world open, and an order I built that answers both with fire and iron and has forgotten there was ever anything underneath." She rose, slowly, joints cracking like ship's timbers, and crossed to the window overlooking the black harbor. "I am done signing, Sera. The question is whether you are."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the fleet was provisioning — Sera could hear the hawser-creak, the shouts, the bell marking the watch. Going out to burn an isle. To put out the last light rather than let a god wake hungry.

"Loyalty isn't to the lie," Grandmother said, without turning. "It's to the truth the lie was protecting. Choose which one you serve."

* * *

They gave her a cell anyway, because ritual demanded it — a clean room with a salt line under the door and no lock on the inside, and everyone involved understanding it was theater. Sera sat on the cot through the middle watch and turned the confession over the way she'd turn a suspect's knife, looking for the false edge.

She didn't find one. That was the horror of it. Twenty years of training to detect deception, and the only thing she detected was grief, worn smooth as sea-glass from two hundred years of handling.

Her mother had been inquisitor too. Her mother had taught her the catechism before she could write: *magic steals, gods devour, the law stands between.* Her mother had died believing it. Sera thought of the ash-lands, of the wraith coming apart under her will, of Astrid's face lit by charm-light — startled, grateful, alive — and understood with terrible clarity that she had stopped believing somewhere on that road and had simply declined to inform herself.

A hedge-witch easing a dying man's pain. A widow buying a stolen first kiss. A priest paying with the last secret of a drowning faith. None of it was devouring. None of it was arithmetic. The devouring had been done by the order itself, burning the world's wonder down to ash to spite one hungry god — and the god was waking anyway, and the order's answer was *more fire*.

She thought of Astrid's rule, told to her across a smuggler's campfire with a crooked grin: *never steal love, never steal grief.* Even the thief had lines. Her order had none left.

Near dawn, boots in the corridor. Not the Salt Brothers' soft shuffle — hard heels, military cadence. The door swung open on a junior inquisitor Sera half-recognized, pale as tallow, holding a sealed dispatch.

"Inquisitor Vane. Grandmother's orders. You're to be conveyed to Cinderveil aboard the *Ossuary*." His throat worked. "There's — there was a message first. From the isle. The wardens' beacon changed color at moonrise. It's burning green again, and the diviners say—" he swallowed, "—they say the flame has found a vessel. Something's coming to it across the water. Grandmother says you'll know who."

Sera stood. Her wrists remembered the weight of irons that weren't there anymore.

"He'll give everything," she said quietly, mostly to herself, and the junior inquisitor blinked at her as though she'd spoken in tongues. "Everything he ever stole. He'll think it's a fair price, because he's spent fifteen years believing his own past was currency." She picked up her coat from the hook, and beneath it, where the Salt Brothers had missed it, the charred corner of a burned writ still rode in the inner pocket like a coal that wouldn't cool.

On her way past, she paused at the door.

"Tell Grandmother the answer is yes," she said. "Both questions. I'll stop him reaching the fire — and I'm done being her instrument while I do it."

The *Ossuary* cleared the harbor at first light, running east into a rising wind that tasted, faintly and impossibly, of woodsmoke and summer. Sera stood at the rail and watched Vespergate shrink behind her, and did not pray to anything, because she no longer knew what was listening — only that whatever waited at Cinderveil, she meant to reach it before the man she'd burned her life down for fed it the one thing she couldn't bear to lose.

Him.
