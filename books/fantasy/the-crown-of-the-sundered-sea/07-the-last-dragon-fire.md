## 7. The Last Dragon-Fire

*Astrid*

The sea turned to glass the moment they crossed into Cinderveil's shadow, as if even the water knew better than to make noise here.

Astrid stood at the prow of Sera's stolen skiff and watched the island grow out of the mist — a black tooth of volcanic rock wrapped in smoke that rose in slow, deliberate coils, like breath from something sleeping. No gulls. No fish breaking the surface. Even the salt spray seemed reluctant.

"It's dying," he said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words came up his throat like a taste.

Sera rowed without answering. She had barely slept in three days; ash-lands did not forgive rest, and neither did she. But her hands on the oars were steady, and her eyes never left the shoreline where black sand met water in a long, silent curve.

"You can feel it," she said finally. Not a question. "The flame."

"I can feel everything." Astrid pressed a hand flat against his own chest. Since Greyharrow, since the priest's gift had rooted itself inside him and refused to be sold or given away, he had felt it growing — a heat behind his ribs that no charm could explain away. On the road, when the memory-wraith had come screaming out of the fog with its hundred borrowed faces, the wraith hadn't wanted the priest's covenant. It had wanted *him*. It had reached for the shard the way a starving man reaches for bread.

He was carrying fire. He had been carrying fire his whole life, and selling it off in thimbles because he thought it was nothing but a thief's small talent.

The keel ground against black sand. Sera stepped out first, boots sinking, one hand on the hilt of her inquisitor's blade — though what good salt-steel would do against whatever waited on this island, Astrid couldn't say. She'd stopped pretending otherwise somewhere past the third day of ash.

"Stay close," she said. "And if anything offers you your childhood back—"

"I'll ask for a receipt."

Her mouth twitched. It was almost a smile. He hoarded those the way he used to hoard charms — small, stolen things he had no right to keep.

They climbed through fields of cooled lava, black glass crunching underfoot, until the smoke parted and Astrid saw the flame.

It sat in a crater like an egg in a nest of stone — small, absurdly small, no bigger than a hearth-fire, burning a color he had no name for. Not orange. Not gold. A white so deep it bent toward blue, and around it the air shimmered and *hurt*, the way looking at the sun hurts. This was the last dragon-fire in the world. The thing that fed sleeping gods. The thing fifteen generations of Inquisitors had spent their lives strangling.

And it was guttering. Fading between one heartbeat and the next, flaring, fading again — a candle in a drafty room with no one left to cup a hand around it.

"There's someone there," Sera breathed.

There was. A woman stood beside the flame, very still, dressed in robes gone gray as ash, her hair white and loose to her waist. She did not turn as they approached. She had heard them coming, Astrid realized, for hours. Perhaps days.

"You took your time," the woman said. Her voice was dry wind over stone. "I have kept this fire alive for two hundred years. I could spare you another week, but I confess my patience has burned down with everything else."

* * *

*Sera*

Two hundred years. Sera's training said impossible. Her training also said magic was theft, gods were parasites, and the Inquisition existed to protect the innocent — and she had watched Grandmother sign an extinction order with the same pen she used to sign festival permits.

She kept her hand on her blade and walked beside Astrid up the crater's rim, and every step made her more certain of two contradictory things: that this woman was telling the truth, and that they were both already dead unless something changed.

"You're a warden," Sera said. "Of the Sundered Covenant."

"I am the last one. There were nine, once. Then three. Now me." The woman turned, and Sera saw her eyes — pale as sea-glass, ancient and amused and terribly sad. "You wear the order's steel, child. Tell me, does Grandmother still tell you the flames were dangerous? That the gods were cruel? That the drowning of the world's magic was mercy?"

"She told me it was protection."

"Mm. And if a wolf tells the sheep it is guarding them, is that protection?" The warden's gaze slid past Sera, to Astrid, and changed entirely — sharpened, brightened, went almost hungry. "Oh," she said softly. "*Oh.* Come here, boy."

Astrid didn't move. Two hundred years of keeping a dying flame alone tends to make a person forget how to be gentle, and Sera saw him register that. But he came anyway, stepping across the black glass until he stood at the crater's edge, and the warden raised one weathered hand — not touching him, hovering an inch from his chest, above the place where the heat lived.

"The shard," she whispered. "I felt it light up three weeks ago, halfway across the sea, like a shout in a tomb. The priest. Old Maren — he found it, didn't he? Found *you*. And gave you the covenant instead of taking it to his grave."

"He paid me with it," Astrid said. "For passage. He was dying."

"He paid you with the world." The warden laughed, a cracked, rusted sound. "Good. Good. That stubborn old fool. I told him the shard would choose its own vessel or burn out, and he swore he'd find it a thief, because thieves know how to hold things lightly—" She caught herself. Drew breath. And when she spoke again, all the amusement had drained out of her voice, leaving something flat and heavy as lead.

"Sit down, both of you. What I have to tell you, you will want to be sitting for."

* * *

*Astrid*

He sat on warm black stone, close enough to the flame to feel its failing rhythm against his skin — flare, fade, flare, fade — like a second heartbeat trying to sync with his own and losing.

"The covenant is simple," the warden said, pacing now, restless as the fire. "The old gods are vast, and vastness sleeps badly. So we bound them beneath the sea with dragon-fire — fed the binding continuously, flame into oath, oath into sleep. In exchange, the gods agreed to let magic flow freely through the world. Rain for the crops. Healing for the sick. Small wonders for small people. That was the bargain. Sleep, and give; wake, and take."

"And someone's been killing the fires," Sera said.

"For two centuries. One by one. The Inquisition called it holy work. Eight flames extinguished. Each death made the drought worse, made magic scarcer, meaner — and made the Inquisition more necessary. A tidy machine." The warden spat into the crater. "Grandmother's order. Signed before your grandmother was born, girl. They don't outlaw magic because they fear it. They starve the gods so the binding weakens, so that when the last flame dies, the covenant breaks *clean* — no rage, no war. Just gods waking into a world that forgot how to fight them."

"Except Nereus," Astrid said quietly.

The warden stopped pacing. "You've spoken with him."

"He answers collect." He told her — the voice in the stolen memories, the offer of his childhood back, the drowned god's patience, his courtesy, the way he made ruin sound like rescue. The warden listened with her eyes closed, and when he finished she looked older than her two hundred years.

"Nereus doesn't want freedom," she said. "Freedom is small to him. He wants *worship*. He wants the world kneeling again, tides in its veins, temples full of screaming. If the last flame dies unfed, the covenant shatters, and Nereus wakes unbound. Every god wakes unbound. And the first debt any waking god collects is the bloodline that bound it." Her sea-glass eyes found Sera. "Ask your Grandmother about the Vane family sometime, child. Ask her why she really signed."

Sera went white. Said nothing. Astrid reached over and put his hand over hers on the black glass, and she didn't pull away, and he understood that this was worth more than any charm he had ever bottled.

"So we feed the flame," he said. "That's why the priest sent me. I'm the shard. I walk in, the fire takes me, the covenant reforges itself. Simple."

"No," the warden said. "Not simple. Listen to me, boy, because this is the part Maren died before he could tell you." She crouched in front of him, gripping his wrists, her hands strong as rope. "Dragon-fire is not fuel. It is not wood to be thrown on. It is a *hearth*. And a hearth does not burn stone. A living shard can rekindle the flame, yes — but only if the flame is given something to burn that is truly alive. Not flesh. Fire doesn't want meat. It wants *memory*. Living memory, freely surrendered, held in the heart when it goes in. That is the price. That has always been the price. The first wardens fed it their deaths — the whole of their lives, remembered, so the gods would sleep gently. It is why wardens die nameless. Why no one remembers us."

The crater was very quiet. Flare, fade. Flare, fade.

"How much?" Astrid asked. His voice sounded far away to him. "How much memory?"

"All of it," the warden said gently. "Everything you carry. Fifteen years of stolen charms. The priest's gift. Your childhood, such as remains of it. Every face, every name, every moment of warmth you have ever held." She paused. "And love most of all. The flame will take the strongest thing first. It always takes the strongest thing first."

* * *

*Sera*

Sera heard the words and understood them the way you understand a wave is going to break — too late to run, exactly in time to drown.

*Love most of all.*

She thought of ash roads and shared watches. Of the wraith's claws and Astrid's blood on her hands, and the moment after, when he'd laughed — actually laughed — with her fingers pressed to his split lip. She thought of the file on her desk with his name on it, and the night she'd burned it page by page in a harbor tavern's stove, and how lighting it had felt like a vow.

If he walked into that fire carrying what he carried now, he would come out the other side a stranger. Alive. Whole. *Empty of her.* The flame would eat his love for her first, because it was the largest thing in him — larger than the grief, larger than the stolen years, larger than the shard itself.

"That's not a price," she said, and her voice came out wrong, scraped raw. "That's just murder with extra steps. You're asking him to kill himself and leave a body that walks around."

"I'm asking nothing," the warden said. "The covenant asks. I only translate." She rose, joints cracking like cooling stone, and looked from Sera to Astrid and back. "There is another way. There is always another way, and it is always worse. A life freely given — not memories, but the life itself. One soul, wholly surrendered, feeds the flame for a thousand years. One of you. Only one is required."

"Don't," Astrid said. He was looking at Sera, and he already knew — she could see him knowing — what she was about to say.

"Somebody has to pay," Sera said. "That's the whole shape of it. It's been the shape since Greyharrow. You're the shard — you're needed *alive*, whole, carrying fire into the binding. I'm an inquisitor. I've spent my career taking things from people. Let me finally give something back."

"You don't get to make that trade."

"Neither do you."

They stared at each other across the guttering flame, two people who had spent a thousand miles learning each other's armor and finding, now, at the exact center of the map, that the armor came off at the worst possible moment.

The warden sighed — a sound like the tide giving up on a shore.

"Decide by dawn," she said. "The fire won't last past tomorrow's dusk. And I would decide quickly, children — because the sea has been watching this crater for three days now, and Nereus knows precisely where you've landed."

Far below, at the edge of the black sand, the glass-flat ocean began — very slowly, very politely — to boil.
