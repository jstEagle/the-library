## 8. Everything He Stole, Everything He Kept

The shard in Astrid's chest had begun to sing.

Not with sound — nothing so kind. It sang the way a kettle sings before it screams, a thin pressure building behind his ribs, and all night on Cinderveil's black shore he had felt it tuning itself to something vast and patient beneath the waves. The volcanic isle breathed under him, warm through his boots, and above him the last dragon-fire guttered in its cradle of stone like a candle someone kept forgetting to shield from the wind.

He had come down from the warden's tower alone. Sera was still up there — he'd watched her silhouette against the flame's red light, arguing with the old woman in low voices that the wind tore apart before they reached him. Arguing about him. He knew how those arguments went. He'd been the subject of them his whole life.

The tide was out. The sea lay flat as hammered pewter, and in it Astrid could see the stars doubled, as if the sky had drowned and didn't know it yet.

*There you are,* said Nereus.

The voice came not through his ears but through the charms — every stolen moment he carried lit up at once, a constellation of other people's memories flaring inside him like lamps in windows. The widow's first kiss. The fisherman's last morning with his son. A hundred small warmths, all burning at once, all bending toward the water.

"You don't give up," Astrid said aloud. His voice sounded very small on so much empty beach.

*I am patient the way the sea is patient. I have waited nine hundred years for this shore to erode.* The water stirred, though no wind touched it. Far out, something moved beneath the surface — a shape too large for honesty. *I have watched you, little thief. Watched you sell pieces of yourself to stay alive. Do you know what I admire about you? You never pretended it wasn't a wound.*

Astrid's hand went, without thinking, to the satchel at his hip. Fifteen years of charms, glass vials and bone stoppers and one iron locket he had never been able to sell. Everything he'd taken from other lives to buy his own.

"What do you want, Nereus?"

*What I have always wanted. The covenant broken. The gods unchained.* The voice wrapped around him like cold water closing over his head. *And I am prepared, at last, to pay fairly. Hear my offer, fire-thief, and then refuse me if you can.*

The sea rose — not in waves but in *images*, spilling up onto the sand like light spilled from a dropped lantern.

Astrid saw a kitchen. A crooked house in Greyharrow's lowest quarter, rain on the shutters, a woman singing off-key while she stirred something on the stove. He knew her face. He would know it anywhere, though he had sold the memory of it when he was fourteen years old — sold his mother's face to a flesh-broker for three months of food and a roof that didn't leak. He had told himself for fifteen years that he couldn't remember what she looked like. Now here she was, whole and laughing, flour on her hands, calling a boy's name —

"Astrid! Supper!"

Him. Small and whole and loved. The childhood he had cut out of himself with a borrowed knife of magic because hunger is a debt collector worse than any god.

*All of it back,* Nereus murmured. *Every charm returned to your own keeping. Your mother's kitchen. Her voice. The years you sold in the ash-lands just to eat. And more —* the images shifted, and now Sera stood in them, alive and unburned, standing beside him on this shore with her hand in his and no fire waiting to drink her memories away. *Give me the shard. Let the covenant die. And Sera Vane walks away from this island owing nothing. No sacrifice. No flame. No forgetting. She keeps everything, including you.*

The vision held. It was cruel in its precision — Nereus had clearly learned, over centuries of drowning sailors, exactly where the soft places were. Astrid stood in the middle of his own restored life and felt it pull at him like an undertow.

His mother's voice. Gods, her *voice*. He hadn't known he still missed it until it filled his ears again, and now he couldn't imagine living the rest of his life without it, which was, of course, the point.

"No," Astrid said.

The vision flickered. *You haven't heard the terms.*

"I heard them fine." His hands were shaking. He made himself look away from the kitchen, from her, from the boy who got to keep being happy. "You're offering to pay me with things that were never yours to give. My childhood isn't a coin in your pocket, Nereus. It's a debt I already paid. I sold it fair. Bad price, fair sale."

*You would take it back if you could.*

"Maybe." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "That's the whole trick of being a thief, isn't it? We always think we can steal it back later. But some debts should stay paid. That's what makes them mean anything." He pressed his palm flat against his chest, against the singing shard. "My mother fed me with what she had. When it ran out, I found another way to feed myself. If I take her memory back now — bought with a world where the gods come up out of the sea and take whatever they want, the way they did before the covenant — then it isn't hers anymore. It's yours. A bribe. I'd rather be empty than owned."

The sea went very still.

*You are a strange creature,* Nereus said at last, and there was something in the voice that might have been respect or might have been hunger wearing respect's face. *The priest who gave you the covenant fought me with prayers. The wardens fight me with walls. You fight me with bookkeeping.*

"Somebody has to. Gods are terrible with accounts."

*Then let us speak plainly of accounts.* The water pulled back, and the tenderness went out of the god's voice like warmth going out of a corpse. *The flame demands a living memory of love. You will feed it your love for the inquisitor, and she will stand there watching you forget her, and she will carry that wound the rest of her life. Or —* and now the images changed, and Astrid saw Sera walking into the fire alone, her dark hair catching the light like a banner, *— she offers herself instead, and loses everything: you, her order, her name, her whole remembered self, to spare you. Either way, one of you burns. Unless.*

"Unless," Astrid said, "you get the shard. And nobody burns."

*Nobody burns,* Nereus agreed silkily. *The gods wake free. The drought ends — I am generous; I will even end the drought. Magic returns to the world like a tide, and men may use it freely, and only occasionally will we ask for small things. A village here. A family there. Nothing anyone would miss.*

"There it is." Astrid nodded slowly, almost grateful. "Thank you. I was starting to feel tempted, and I hate feeling tempted. It makes me sloppy."

*You refuse?*

"I'm a thief, Nereus. I know a rigged scale when I'm standing on one." He turned toward the tower, toward the red glow at its crown. "You want the shard? Come take it. But I warn you — I've never once given up anything without making the buyer overpay."

Behind him, the sea gathered itself like a fist.

* * *

He was halfway up the switchback trail to the warden's tower when he heard her boots on the rock above him, coming down fast.

Sera Vane descended the way she did everything — like the mountain had personally wronged her and she intended to file a complaint. Her inquisitor's coat was scorched at one shoulder from the ash-road, her braid half-collapsed, salt in the lines around her eyes. Two weeks ago he'd thought she was the most dangerous thing hunting him. Now the sight of her did something to his chest that had nothing to do with dragon-fire.

"You're supposed to be resting," she said. "Warden's orders."

"Wardens give orders like priests give blessings. Nobody listens." He stopped on the step below hers, so they were nearly eye level, which she hated. "Where's the old woman?"

"Asleep. Two hundred years buys you the right to sleep through the end of the world." Sera studied his face, and he watched the inquisitor in her read it — the flush, the sweat, the residue of a god's attention. Her jaw tightened. "He talked to you."

"He made me an offer. I declined. There were visions of my mother. It got emotional." He tried for light and heard it land somewhere short. "Sera—"

"Don't." Her hand came up, sharp as a drawn blade. "Don't soften it. Whatever he offered, whatever the price actually is — tell me straight, because I've spent the last hour listening to that warden talk in circles around it, and I am done being managed by people who think grief is a courtesy they can spare me."

So he told her. All of it. The living memory of love. The fire that drinks what feeds it. The choice between his remembering and hers.

He watched her arrive at it — watched the understanding move through her like a blade sliding home, her breath going shallow, her eyes going bright and terrible.

"No," she said.

"That was my answer too."

"No, Astrid." She stepped down onto his step, crowding him against the rock, close enough that he could see the pulse jumping in her throat. "You don't understand. I've been doing the arithmetic since the warden named the price, and yours doesn't balance. You've already lost your childhood. Your mother. Every year of yourself you ever sold. You're a man held together by other people's leftovers — and I—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it like weather, "—I have twenty-eight years of memories and most of them are worthless. Drill halls. Sermons. My grandmother teaching me to write my letters, fine, keep that one, burn the rest. One good life for one half-lived one. It's not even a bargain. It's charity."

"Sera."

"Let me finish—"

"It's not charity," he said quietly, "because you're not paying alone. Don't you see? If you walk into that fire, I spend the rest of my life as the man who let the woman he loves erase herself to save him. That's not a debt I survive. I've carried debts my whole life, Sera. Sold pieces of myself to cover them. This one I'd carry sober, awake, remembering — and it would hollow me worse than any flame. You'd be sparing me the fire and giving me something crueler."

"Then we're at an impasse."

"We're always at an impasse. It's practically our courtship."

She laughed — one sharp, wet note, gone immediately — and then her hand closed on his collar and she kissed him, fierce and brief, like sealing a letter she meant him to open after she was gone.

When she pulled back, her eyes were shining and absolutely resolute, and Astrid felt the bottom drop out of his stomach, because he knew that look. He'd seen it on buyers realizing too late what they'd traded away.

"Sera. What did you just do?"

Her hand drifted, almost absently, to her own temple — and came away holding a curl of pale light, soft as seafoam, trembling like a moth. A memory, freshly drawn, still warm. Still *hers*. The kiss. Their first. Already taken, already held, ready to feed.

"The fire wants a living memory of love," Sera Vane said, in the flat voice she used for verdicts. "It never said whose."

She turned toward the tower, and the last dragon-fire roared up to meet her — and far below, the sea began to rise.
