## 9. The Sundering Reforged

**SERA**

The fleet came out of the fog at dawn, and it came like a verdict.

Forty ships, black-hulled, salt-scoured, their sails marked with the Inquisition's grey wheel. Sera stood on Cinderveil's shattered causeway and counted them twice, because the first count felt like a lie. Behind her, the mountain breathed. The dragon-fire's glow pulsed up through the cracks in the rock like a heartbeat under skin, slow and guttering and dying.

"They'll land within the hour," she said.

Astrid stood beside her, his coat whipping in the hot wind off the caldera. He looked calm. That was the worst part. She had watched this man flinch from raised voices, from debts, from the memory of a mother he'd sold to buy bread — and now, with an armada coming to burn him off the face of the sea, he looked like a man waiting for a kettle to boil.

"You could still run," she said. "The warden knows the sea-caves. I could hold them at the causeway long enough—"

"Sera." He said her name the way other people said prayers. "You've known me for two months. You know exactly how fast I run."

She almost laughed. It came out as something broken instead.

Below them, the first signal-fires bloomed on the flagship's deck. Grandmother was aboard — Sera could feel it, the way you feel a storm behind a hill. Somewhere in that grey-walled cabin, the woman who had raised her, trained her, sharpened her into a blade, was reading the extinction order one more time and calling it mercy.

"Whatever happens," Astrid said quietly, "I need you to hear me now, while you still can."

"Don't."

"Because after—" His hand went to his chest, where fifteen years of stolen moments sat bottled beneath his ribs like swallowed stars. "After, there may not be an *after* that remembers you."

"I said don't." She grabbed his collar, dragged him close enough that she could see the firelight reflected in his eyes — the shard of dragon-fire that had lived in him since before he knew its name. "You don't get to say goodbye in advance. That's cowardice dressed up as kindness, and I have executed men for less."

He smiled, and it wrecked her. "Then I won't say goodbye. I'll say: when you find me again, be patient with me. I'm told I'm charming but I talk too much about prices."

The first landing boats hit the water.

* * *

**ASTRID**

They came up the causeway in grey waves of their own, forty crossbows deep, and the warden met them alone.

Astrid watched from the caldera's rim as the two-hundred-year-old woman walked down the black stone with a lantern in one hand and nothing else, and the front rank of inquisitors actually hesitated — because some part of every soldier still believed in wardens, in fires kept burning past all reason. For three heartbeats, nobody moved.

Then Grandmother's voice carried across the water, amplified by some engine of office or old cruelty: *"By order of the Salt Inquisition, extinguish the flame. Kill the thief. Spare the inquisitor Vane if she surrenders."*

*Spare.* As if Sera were a hostage instead of a traitor. As if love were a crime someone else had committed against her.

The warden set down her lantern, and the lantern-flame leapt free of the glass and became a wall.

Astrid didn't stay to watch the fight. Every second the warden bought was a second bought with two centuries of vigilance, and he would not waste them standing in awe. He turned and ran up into the mountain's throat, along the path of cracked obsidian toward the final flame, and the heat rose around him like a tide until his shadow burned on the rocks ahead of him.

And the sea spoke.

*Little thief,* Nereus said, and the voice came up through the soles of his feet, through the stone, through the water table itself. *The fleet is mine to drown. One word from you. One loosening of your grip, and every ship out there becomes driftwood and every inquisitor becomes kelp. You need only stop fighting me.*

Astrid kept running. "You'd drown them anyway."

*I would free them. There is a difference only the drowned understand.*

He reached the flame.

It was small. That was the cruelty of it — the last living shard of the fire that had woken the world, and it was no bigger than a candle, crouched in a basin of fused glass, guttering like everything else. Two hundred years the warden had fed it, drop by drop, memory by memory, and it had burned down to this: a blue-white tongue of light that leaned toward him the moment he entered the chamber, the way a starving thing leans toward warmth.

Or toward kin.

Astrid knelt. He opened his chest — not flesh; will — and brought up the charms.

Fifteen years of thefts rose in him like silt in disturbed water. The widow's first kiss, returned to her months ago but still warm in his keeping. A fisherman's last sight of his son's sail. A child's birthday, sold twice by a desperate father and bought back once by Astrid out of guilt. His mother's hands, kneading bread in a kitchen that no longer existed. His childhood entire, the years he'd sold at fourteen to survive a Greyharrow winter — Nereus had promised them back, promised them whole, promised him the boy he'd been with the hunger finally gone from his belly.

*All of it restored,* the god murmured, closer now, the water rising in the caldera's throat behind him, black and patient. *Every moment you ever gave away, returned. And the inquisitor lives, untouched, unburned. You lose nothing but a duty. Refuse, and you lose everything you are.*

"That's the offer?" Astrid whispered. "That's the best a god can do?"

*It is the only honest trade left in the world.*

"Yeah," Astrid said. "It is."

He began to feed the fire.

* * *

**SERA**

She fought her way up the causeway through her own former brothers and sisters, and it was not a battle, it was a grief with weapons. She used the flat of her blade. She broke wrists, not necks. And all the while the mountain shook beneath her, and the light pouring from the caldera changed — deepened — went from guttering candle-yellow to something older, bluer, a color that made her eyes water and her teeth ache and some buried animal part of her want to kneel.

The warden fell at the causeway's midpoint. Sera saw it happen — saw the old woman take three bolts she should have dodged, saw her go down laughing, actually laughing, her lantern rolling free and its flame crawling back up the slope toward the mountain like a loyal dog.

*"Vane!"* Grandmother's voice, furious now, cracking. *"Stand down or be struck down!"*

Sera stood on the black stone between the fleet and the flame, and she understood, suddenly and completely, what she had been her whole life: a lock. A well-made lock on a door that led to a starving god's prison, and locks exist to be loyal to doors, not to keys.

"No," she said, and raised her blade against the fleet.

The first volley of ballista-fire came out of the fog, and the sea rose to meet it.

Not waves. *Nereus.* A shape the size of a cathedral district, unfolding out of the deep with the slowness of something that has waited nine hundred years and intends to enjoy the waiting. Water poured off him in cataracts. Where his shadow crossed the ships, masts groaned and hulls screamed, and men leapt into water that was suddenly, terribly awake.

Sera ran. Up the slope, through heat that peeled the varnish off her scabbard, into the caldera's throat where the light was now a pillar —

— and stopped.

Astrid stood in the heart of it, and he was *coming apart*. Not bleeding, not burning — unraveling. With each memory he fed the flame, a piece of him went with it. She watched him feed it a summer and his hair went white at the temples. She watched him feed it a face — his mother's, she realized, she'd seen it in a stolen glimpse once — and he swayed like a man losing blood. And he was smiling. Smiling and weeping and feeding the fire everything, and the fire was growing, climbing the caldera walls, writing covenant-script across the sky in letters of blue-white light.

"Astrid!"

He turned. And she saw the exact moment he reached the last charm — the one he'd never named, the one he'd carried closest, the one that was hers. Their first night on the ash road, her laughter, the taste of rain on rust. She saw him hold it. She saw him look at her with fifteen years of stolen life draining out of his eyes, and she saw him choose.

"No—" She lunged forward. "Take mine! Take my memories, I'm offering, the fire takes love, any love, it doesn't have to be—"

"It has to be freely given," he said, and his voice was already going strange, going distant, going *clean*. "Yours wouldn't be a theft, Sera. Mine always were. That's why they burn so bright."

"Astrid—"

"Hey." He caught her hand as she reached him, and his grip was steady, and his eyes were already forgetting her, and he smiled like a man setting down a weight he'd carried since childhood. "When you find me again — be patient with me. I'm told I'm charming."

"*Don't you dare—*"

He let it go.

The memory left him like a bird leaving a cage, and the fire took it, and the pillar of light went up through the clouds and *through* the sky, and the whole Sundered Sea lit blue from horizon to horizon.

* * *

**ASTRID**

He did not remember doing it. That was the point. That was the price.

But somewhere below thought, below the clean white emptiness where fifteen years had been, something witnessed its own ending. He felt the priest's gift leave him — the covenant, the sleeping gods, the names of things older than salt — and felt the fire read it aloud to the world. He felt the flames reach down through the mountain, through the seabed, into the dark places where vast shapes had lain dormant and dim for nine hundred years, and *touch* them.

The gods woke.

He felt them wake the way you feel dawn through a wall — not seeing, knowing. And bound, all of them, bound to the bargain written in his stolen years: fed by willing flame, barred from dominion, held to the terms of a covenant reforged by a thief who paid with everything he'd ever taken.

Nereus came last.

The drowned god rose against the pillar of light and found it a chain. Astrid — the empty, the new, the man with no past — felt the god strain against the covenant and felt the covenant hold, and heard, distantly, from very far above the water, a sound like rage and like relief tangled together, and then the sea closed over Nereus like a book, and the god went down into the deep places, bound, bound, bound, and the ocean lay flat and quiet as a settled grave.

The fire dimmed to something bearable. Something *kept*.

Astrid opened his eyes. He was kneeling in a glass basin's shadow, and a woman was holding his hand, and she was crying, and he did not know her.

He knew — distantly, the way you know the shape of a room you've never entered — that he was supposed to know her. That the absence where she should be had a shape, and the shape was specific, and it hurt in a way that had no story attached to it.

"Hi," he said, because it seemed important to say something. "I'm Astrid. I think I owe you... something. I don't remember what."

The woman laughed through her tears, and the sound went through him like a hook through cloth, catching on something deep and nameless.

"My name," she said carefully, "is Sera. And you're going to buy me a drink, stranger. You owe me that much."

Down the mountain, the fleet drifted mastless and silent, and the sea glittered with a light that had not touched it in nine hundred years, and far away in Greyharrow harbor, in a city just beginning to learn that magic was legal and small again, a bottle on a shelf flickered with borrowed lightning — and stayed lit.
