## 10. A Stranger at the Harbor

The tide came in over Greyharrow like an apology.

Sera Vane stood on the harbor wall where the Inquisition's gallows had once been — the same gallows she had climbed a hundred times to read sentences aloud — and watched the water turn gold in the late light. The gallows were gone now. In their place stood a market stall with a striped awning and a hand-painted sign: *Yarrow — Memories Bought & Sold, Fair Rates.* Beneath it, smaller, almost shy: *No love. No grief.*

Some rules survived even when the man who made them could not remember making them.

She had argued the case herself, in the end. Not before Grandmother — Grandmother was ash and rumor now, her fleet scattered, her name struck from every ledger Sera could reach — but before the new Conclave of Salt, the reformed order she'd helped build from the wreckage. *Magic is legal again,* she'd told them, *so let it be small. Let it be honest. Let a man sell a widow the sound of her husband laughing, and let no one burn for it.* They had agreed, mostly because Sera Vane was the only inquisitor alive who had stood on Cinderveil and watched a god rise out of the sea, and terror makes for very persuasive testimony.

So Greyharrow changed. Slowly, the way harbors change — one plank, one rope, one season at a time. Charm-sellers hung their shingles beside the fishmongers. A hedge-witch opened a shop that smelled of rosemary and rain. Children who would once have been dragged to the Salt Cells for humming charms under their breath now traded bottled sunsets in the alleys like marbles.

And Astrid Yarrow sold memories from a stall on the harbor wall, and did not know her.

That was the arithmetic of it, the price that had been paid, and Sera had learned to carry it the way sailors carry old scars: not proudly, not bitterly, just always. He had fed fifteen years into the dragon-fire — every charm, every theft, the priest's dying gift, and last of all the months on the salt-and-ash road, the wraith, the firelight, her mouth against his in the lee of a ruined chapel while the world ended quietly around them. He had given all of it so that the gods would wake bound instead of free, so that Nereus would stay drowned, so that magic would come back to the world like rain instead of like fire.

He had kept nothing. That had been the covenant's cruelty or its mercy; she still couldn't decide. To reforge it, he'd had to surrender a living memory of love — and because his whole life was built of stolen things, the only memory that was truly, wholly his, never bought or bartered, was her.

*You'll forget me,* she had said, holding his face, ash in his hair, the volcano roaring behind them.

*I know,* he'd said. And then, with that crooked half-smile she had hated and loved in equal measure: *But you won't. So one of us will have to do the remembering for both of us. Try to make me sound better than I am.*

She had tried. Gods knew she had tried. She had gone to him in the first weeks, when he woke in a cot in the temple hospice with clean eyes and no past, and she had told him everything — Cinderveil, the road, the wraith, the kiss. And he had listened politely, the way you listen to a story about strangers, and thanked her, and asked if she was the one who'd pulled him from the water.

It wasn't a lie, exactly. It just wasn't the truth either.

So she had stopped telling. And started buying.

* * *

"Back again?" Astrid said, without looking up from the tray of vials he was arranging. "You know, most people wait more than three days between purchases. At this rate I'll have sold you my entire inventory by midwinter."

"Your prices are fair," Sera said.

"My prices are extortionate. Widow Marsh told me so just this morning." He looked up then, and smiled, and it went through her the way it always went through her — like a hook behind the ribs, gentle and merciless. "What is it today, Inquisitor? Another sunset? You own six."

"Seven," she said. "I lost one."

"Liar. You keep them lined up on a windowsill. I've seen your house." He said it lightly, teasing, and then something flickered across his face — a hesitation, a narrowing, as if a door had blown open somewhere inside him onto a room he didn't recognize. It happened sometimes. Little drafts from a burned house. "How do I know that? About the windowsill?"

"You've delivered to my street," she said smoothly. It was the answer she always gave. It was never quite enough.

He studied her a moment longer than was comfortable. Then he shrugged and held up two vials, turning them so the light caught them: one amber, one deep sea-green. "Amber is a storm seen from a lighthouse. Green is the moment a diver breaks the surface and takes her first breath. Both very popular. Neither," he added, tapping the green one, "is what you want."

"No?"

"No. You always buy the quiet ones. Sunsets, snowfall, a cat sleeping in a patch of sun. Never anything with weather in it." He set the green vial down and picked out a small stoppered phial of pale gold, hardly bigger than his thumbnail. "This one. A woman teaching her daughter to whistle. That's the one you'll stand there holding for ten minutes and then buy anyway, and then tell me you don't know why."

Sera looked at it. Her throat was tight. On the salt-and-ash road, in the ruins of a waystation with rain coming through the roof, she had taught him to whistle — badly, both of them laughing, his fingers clumsy on hers until she'd taken his hands and shown him. He had never gotten it right. He'd said he'd practice. There had been no time to practice.

"I don't know why," she said.

"I know," he said softly. "You never do."

She paid him. He refused to take full price — he always took less than full price, some instinct of generosity he couldn't source — and she stood there at the stall as the harbor sounds washed around them, gulls and rigging and the slap of water on pilings, and she thought, not for the first time: *this is enough. This has to be enough. He is alive and unhaunted and the world is mended, and wanting more is greed.*

But it wasn't greed. That was the thing she'd finally understood, watching him across the market month after month. Love without memory wasn't a debt owed. It was just a door, standing open, waiting for someone to walk through it again.

* * *

He closed the stall at dusk, as he always did, and she lingered, as she always did, and this time — this time — he didn't say goodnight.

"The strangest thing happened today," he said, coiling the awning rope with quick, practiced hands. "A woman came by. Old, half-blind. Bought a memory of the sea from me, and when she paid, she grabbed my wrist and said, 'You've got kind hands, boy. Whoever taught you to hold things gently, thank her for me.'" The rope stilled in his grip. "And I said, 'No one taught me.' But that's not true, is it? Because the second she said it, I thought of rain coming through a roof. Of someone taking my hands in hers. I don't know who. I don't know when. It's like a word on the tip of my tongue that isn't a word at all."

Sera said nothing. The tide breathed against the stones.

"The priest's memory," he went on. "Before Cinderveil. Everyone tells me I gave up everything I'd stolen. But that gift was never mine to give, was it? It passed through me. Maybe some things pass through and leave a shape behind. Like water through sand." He turned to look at her fully, and the last light caught his eyes, and they were the same eyes she had loved across a burning island, and they did not know her at all, and they were trying to. "You keep buying memories you don't need. You keep looking at me like you're waiting for something. So I'm going to ask you plainly, Inquisitor Vane, and you can refuse to answer, and I'll never ask again."

"Ask," she said.

"What did we lose?"

The question hung between them over the gold-dark water. She could lie. She had lied well for months. But he had burned his whole self down rather than lie to the world, and he deserved better than her caution.

"Everything," she said. "We lost everything. And then the world got it back, except us."

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, the way he seemed to absorb hard truths — without flinching, without drama, the steadiness that had carried him up a volcano to unmake himself. "Did we lose it well?"

"Yes," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "Better than anyone."

"Good." He stepped closer. Close enough that she could smell salt and lamp-oil and, beneath it, something warm that was only him, unchanged, irreducible. "Then here's what I know — and it isn't much, but it's mine, all of it, the first things I've owned since I woke up. I know I look forward to market days and I told myself it was the custom. I know your voice does something to my chest that I can't account for. I know that when you laugh — which is rare, Inquisitor, you should work on that — I feel like I've gotten away with something." His mouth curved. "And I know that I have never once, in all these months, wanted to steal a single memory from you. Which for a man like me must mean something."

"It means you have rules," she managed.

"It means," he said, "that whatever we were, I'd like to find out what we could be. From the beginning. No debts. No covenants. No gods keeping score." He held out his hand, palm up, empty — the gesture of a thief offering nothing, which was the most precious thing he had. "They say the first charm ever worked was a stolen kiss. Bottled lightning, the old sellers called it. I hear it's worth a fortune."

Sera looked at his open hand, and at his face — patient, hopeful, entirely unhaunted — and she understood at last what the fire had left him. Not nothing. A clean page. And a clean page was not an ending. It was the wildest kind of beginning, the kind you could write anything on.

She put her hand in his.

"Astrid Yarrow," she said, "you are about to commit your first crime."

"Am I?" His smile went crooked, familiar, inevitable, a thing her body remembered even if her mind had never met it. "What's the charge?"

"Theft," she said, leaning in. "Of a first kiss."

"Free of charge," he murmured, and kissed her — soft at first, then certain, the harbor lights blooming behind her eyelids like bottled storms — and somewhere far below the Sundered Sea, in the green dark where old gods slept their bound and dreaming sleep, something that had once been a drowned god felt the covenant hum with new warmth, and turned over, and let them be.

When they broke apart, breathless, the tide was coming in, and Astrid laughed — low and wondering, like a man discovering an old scar that doesn't hurt.

"Well," he said. "I don't remember you. But I think I could learn."

"You will," Sera Vane said, and laced her fingers through his, and walked him home along the water. "One day at a time. I've got time. We both do."

And the sea, which keeps all memories, kept that too.
