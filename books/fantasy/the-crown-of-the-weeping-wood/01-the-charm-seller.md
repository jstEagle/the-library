## 1. The Charm Seller

The night market of Vell woke the way a wounded animal wakes: slowly, warily, one eye open for danger.

Felix Galloway knew every hour of its waking. They had learned them the way other children learned prayers—by repetition, by necessity, by the low candlelight of survival. The market began at dusk with the fishwives and the rag-pickers, those honest poor who traded in things that smelled. By full dark came the second wave: the fortune-tellers with their painted cards, the hedge-healers who sold willow-bark and lies, the cutpurses drifting like smoke between the stalls. And last, in the deepest hour before the lamplighters made their rounds, came the ones like Felix—the sellers of small mercies that could not be named aloud.

Felix's stall was a folding table no wider than a coffin lid, set in the lee of a shuttered chandler's shop on Ropewalk Lane. On it, arranged with the care of a jeweler, sat three dozen little glass vials on beds of faded velvet. Each was stoppered with wax, sealed with a drop of tallow, and wrapped at the neck with a twist of colored thread. Red thread for luck in love. Green for luck in coin. Blue for luck in safe passage. Simple colors. Honest-seeming colors.

Nothing about them said *memory*. Nothing about them said *magic*. In Vell, where the Edict of Ash had made such words into gallows-words a generation past, a charm-seller lived or died by what their goods did *not* say.

"Charms," Felix called softly, to the trickle of passersby hooded against the river damp. "Bottled luck. Fair prices, fairer fortunes."

Their voice was a market voice, worn smooth as a river stone. It gave nothing away. Fifteen years of practice had taught Felix Galloway how to be nobody—a person shaped like an absence, a face the wardens' eyes slid over like rain off slate. They were twenty-six years old and had been invisible since they were eleven, which was, if you thought about it too long, its own kind of grief.

So Felix did not think about it. Felix counted coins instead.

* * *

The first sale came an hour after moonrise: a dockhand with knuckles like knots, wanting green thread for a card game he had no business winning. He held the vial up to the lantern light, squinting at the faint shimmer inside—not quite liquid, not quite light, something between, the way breath looks on a cold morning.

"What's in it?" he asked, though he didn't really want to know. Nobody ever really wanted to know. That was the beauty of it. That was the whole rotten architecture of Felix's life: people wanted the gift without the giving, the cure without the cause.

"Dew from the Founder's statue," Felix said. "Gathered at solstice. Very lucky."

It was a lie so old it had gone smooth in their mouth, like a pebble sucked for years. The truth was less poetic. The vial held a memory—one of Felix's own, or one bought or bartered or lifted from some sleeping stranger in the years before the hunt made such harvests a hanging matter. This particular one belonged to a man Felix had never met, a memory of winning a hand of cards in a tavern long since burned down. Felix had taken it from a dead man's house, from a dead man's keepsake-box, back when the dead still left their memories lying about for the gifted to find. A memory of joy, bottled. It would seep into the dockhand's blood through his skin, warm him from the wrist up, and for an hour or two he would feel the way that dead stranger had felt when the queen of hearts fell his way: invincible, golden, certain.

Then it would fade, and he'd come back next week for another.

"That'll be four coppers," Felix said.

He paid six, because he was feeling lucky already, and shuffled off into the dark grinning at nothing.

Felix watched him go and felt the familiar arithmetic turn over in their chest. Four coppers was bread for two days. Six was bread and a candle stub and a little set aside against winter. And somewhere behind all that counting, quieter than the counting, the other ledger: *you sold a dead man's happiness to a stranger and called it dew.*

They had gotten good at not hearing that ledger. Mostly.

* * *

The trouble with the night market—and Felix loved it, and hated it, and could not have told you where the love ended and the hate began—was that it was full of people pretending. The fortune-tellers pretended to see futures. The healers pretended their teas were more than boiled weeds and confidence. The whole lane was a cathedral of pretense, everyone huddled together under the same blanket of lies for warmth, and in that respect Felix Galloway fit perfectly. They were the best liar in a city of liars, because Felix lied with other people's truths.

A charm-seller's stock-in-trade was simple: memories, bottled while fresh. There had once been dozens of the gifted in Vell—memory-thieves, dream-readers, soul-menders, folk who could pull a feeling out of the air like a thread from a spool. Before the Edict, they'd worked openly. There had been shops. There had been a guild hall on Candlemaker Street with a sign of an open hand. Felix's mentor, old Mother Corvane, used to say she remembered when the gifted walked in daylight, and she'd say it with a laugh that had no floor under it.

Now there were perhaps a dozen left in Vell, scattered like sparks in wet wool, each convinced they alone had survived by their own cleverness. Each wrong, probably. Cleverness kept you alive; it just didn't keep you *living*. Felix had understood that distinction early, in the bone-deep way children understand things they cannot name.

Because here was the secret under all the secrets, the one Felix carried like a stone sewn into the lining of their coat: the memories they sold were real. Every vial on that folding table held an hour of somebody's actual life. The red-thread charms held weddings, first kisses, the moment a mother first saw her child's face. The green held windfalls and winnings and the taste of a meal eaten in safety. The blue held the feeling of standing on a ship's deck with the whole sea open before you, or walking out of prison, or surviving a fever. People bought them because people were starving—not for bread, in most cases, but for the sense that life had been, once, or could be, briefly, more than this.

And Felix took their coppers and felt purpose and shame in equal measure, stirred together like paint, indistinguishable.

* * *

She came near midnight, when the market had thinned to ghosts and gutter-runners. Felix noticed her shoes first—that was the training, notice the shoes, the shoes tell you everything. They were good shoes, once. Now cracked at the seams, resoled twice, the heels worn down to the nail. A woman falling slowly out of the middle class, clinging by her fingernails to respectability. Her dress was mended with thread that almost matched. Her hands were folded tight, white-knuckled, the way hands fold when they've been arguing with themselves for days and have finally lost.

She stopped at the table. She looked at the vials for a long time without touching anything.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. Not a question. Word traveled in the night market the way water traveled through walls—slowly, invisibly, finding every crack.

"I'm a charm-seller," Felix said carefully. "There are others."

"There are none others like you." Her eyes came up. They were gray eyes, the color of weather, and they had the look of someone who had cried herself empty weeks ago and now lived in the dry country beyond. "The others sell feelings. Warmth. Little shivers. You sell *days*. I'm told you can sell a whole day."

Felix kept their face pleasant and empty, the market face. "I sell luck, mistress. Bottled and sealed. If you're needing more than luck—"

"My son is dead."

The words dropped between them like a coin down a well. Felix heard it land, far below, in the place where they kept the things they didn't think about.

"He was nine," the woman went on, in the flat reciting voice of the newly bereaved, the voice that has said the words so many times they've worn grooves. "Fever, in the spring. The healers couldn't—I paid everything, and they couldn't." Her hands unfolded, refolded. "I have one memory left of him that isn't worn through. One day I can still see clear. His birthday, last autumn. We went to the river and he—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Every time I take it out and look at it, it gets smaller. You understand? It's wearing out. Like a coin passed hand to hand until the face wears smooth. Soon I won't be able to see his face at all."

Felix said nothing. There was nothing lawful to say.

"They say you keep memories," she whispered. "That you have hundreds. Thousands. That somewhere in your collection there might be—" Her voice broke and reformed. "His name was Tam. Tam Garrow. He had a gap in his teeth and he whistled when he laughed and he was afraid of eels and he wanted to be a bell-ringer when he grew up because he liked the idea of being high up in the dark making something beautiful."

The lantern flame leaned in the river wind. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Mistress," Felix said at last, and their voice came out lower than intended, stripped of the market polish. "What you're asking for—if I even had such a thing—it wouldn't be him. It would be a stranger's memory of a boy. An hour, maybe two of borrowed grief. It fades. It always fades."

"I know what it is." She said it fiercely, leaning across the table, and for a moment Felix saw the woman she must have been before the fever took her son and then, piece by piece, everything else. "You think I don't know what I'm buying? I know it won't bring him back. I know it's a lie I'll pay for. But I stood in the churchyard today and I tried to remember the sound of his laugh and I *couldn't*, do you hear me? Three months, and it's going. It's going the way everything goes. And you—you sit here selling other people's yesterdays to strangers like it's nothing, like it's *dew*—"

"Keep your voice down." It came out sharp, instinctive, the warden-fear speaking. They both glanced down the lane. Nothing moved but the lantern shadows.

The woman pressed her lips together. When she spoke again it was barely above breath. "Please. I have eight coppers and a silver ring that was my grandmother's. I know it isn't enough. I know whatever price you ask, it isn't enough. But I'm asking anyway, because I don't have anywhere else to ask."

And there it was. The moment Felix Galloway had built their whole slippery life to avoid: a person in need, looking directly at them, seeing them. Not the stall. Not the charms. *Them.* It was the one thing invisibility could not protect against, and Felix felt it land like a hook behind the breastbone.

Because here was the truth they never said aloud, not even to themselves, not usually: Felix kept a collection. Of course they kept a collection. Every memory-thief did; you couldn't work the trade without keeping back a tithe, a sample, a thing to study your craft on. And among the hundreds of vials in the hidden compartment beneath the floorboards of their rented room—weddings and shipwrecks and first snows and last words—there were a few that Felix had never sold and never would. A few that were theirs alone.

Including one, harvested years ago from a grieving father's keepsake-box in the Marsh End, of a boy of nine with a gap in his teeth, laughing on a riverbank while his mother baited a hook and pretended not to cry with happiness.

Felix had kept it because it was beautiful. That was the excuse. Because it was the finest thing in the collection, a perfect hour, sunlight and water-sound and a child's laughter caught mid-air like a mayfly in amber. They had told themselves it would fetch a fortune someday, from the right buyer. They had told themselves many things.

"I'll need to check my stock," Felix heard themselves say. "For anything... matching your description. Come back tomorrow night. Same hour."

Hope hit the woman's face like sunrise hitting a ruin—illuminating all the damage, changing none of it. "Tomorrow," she repeated, as if the word were in a foreign language she was trying on. "Thank you. Thank you. What do I owe you for—"

"Nothing tonight. Tomorrow we'll settle it." Felix was already packing the vials away, fingers quick, not looking at her. "Go home, mistress. Don't walk the lanes after the lamps go out. Not these nights."

"No," she agreed, rising. "Not these nights." And then, pausing, with the terrible simplicity of the desperate: "They say the crown is calling the gifted out. That there's to be a hunting. Is that true?"

The question went through Felix like a splinter finding its depth. "People say a great many things," they said lightly. "Mostly nonsense. Goodnight, mistress."

She went. Felix watched her until the dark took her, those cracked good shoes carrying her home to a house with a chair no one sat in.

* * *

Afterward, Felix packed the stall with mechanical care. Velvet roll, vials nested, table folded, lantern pinched out. The routine was a litany, a prayer without a god: *velvet, glass, wax, thread.* Their hands knew it so well their mind could wander, and wander it did—to the hidden compartment under the floorboards, to the amber hour waiting there, to the shape of tomorrow night and the strange, terrible transaction it would be.

Because here was the thing Felix would not let themselves finish thinking: they weren't going to sell that memory to the woman. They couldn't. Selling it would make it commerce, and commerce was the armor Felix wore, and if they took off the armor for her—for a stranger, for a grieving mother with worn-out shoes—then the whole careful edifice wobbled. No. They would give it. Give it free, wrap it in some fiction about surplus stock, watch her drink in her son's laughter one more hour, and then—

And then what? Then she'd come back in a month, hollow again, and there'd be nothing left to give, and Felix would have spent their finest treasure for one stranger's single night of grace. It was bad craft. It was worse business. Old Mother Corvane would have boxed their ears. *Never give the gift, child. The gift given is the thief unmasked.*

But the ledger in Felix's chest—the quiet one, the one under the coin-counting—had already written the entry. *One hour of a boy laughing. Paid out. Purpose: so that a mother remembers why she stays alive.* And against it, in the shame column, in small cramped letters: *and so that you can feel, for one night, like a person who belongs to something.*

Because that was the ache, wasn't it? The one that lived under everything, patient as rot. Felix Galloway belonged to the night market the way a ghost belongs to a house: present, useful, unseen. They belonged to no one and nothing else. No family—they told people their family was dead, and it was close enough to true that it never cost them anything to say it. No guild, since the guild hall on Candlemaker Street had been burned with half its members inside it, the year Felix turned eleven. No name spoken warmly anywhere in Vell.

Just a folding table, three dozen vials, and a talent that was, by law, worth a pyre.

Felix slung the pack over their shoulder and slipped into the dark, taking the long way home along the canal, as always, checking the reflections in the black water for followers, as always. The city breathed around them—smoke and river and distant bells marking the hour. Somewhere a watchman coughed. Somewhere a warden's lantern swung its slow arc across an intersection, and Felix melted into a doorway until it passed, heart steady, breath shallow, practiced.

At the mouth of Tanner's Row they paused. Across the square, washed pale by the moon, stood the Founder's statue—old Aldric Vell himself in bronze, arm raised toward the mountain that loomed northward over the rooftops, the mountain everyone knew and no one spoke of. The mountain where, according to the oldest stories, the fae kept their court, trading in what mortals surrendered. Children's tales. Nursemaid nonsense.

Felix looked at it a moment longer than usual. They couldn't have said why. Something in the air tonight—some change in the wind off the peaks, some smell like ash that wasn't smoke—made the old bronze figure seem less like a monument and more like a warning.

Then they shook it off, pulled their hood down, and went home to count their treasures and dread tomorrow's generosity.

Behind them, far to the north, past the city wall, past the fields, the mountain stood under the stars with its patience of stone—and in the streets of Vell, in the houses of the powerful, orders were being drafted that would reach the night market within the week, and burn it clean.

But that was tomorrow's grief. Tonight, Felix Galloway unlocked their door, knelt, pried up the loose board, and lifted out a single vial of amber light—a boy laughing on a riverbank, forever—and held it up to the candle, and wept, quietly, the way they did everything: alone, efficiently, and where no one could see.
