## 2. Ash on the Wind

The proclamation came on a Tuesday, nailed to every door in Vell by soldiers who did not knock.

Felix heard it before they saw it—the flat percussion of hammer on wood, repeated down the lane like a slow heartbeat, and beneath it the particular silence of neighbors reading through windows rather than gathering in doorways. In Vell, bad news traveled fastest by absence: the baker who didn't call out his morning loaves, the fishwife who wasn't singing, the children kept indoors. By the time Felix pushed open their shutters, the street already knew.

The broadsheet was printed in red ink, which cost more than black and was chosen for exactly that reason. *By order of His Majesty's Grace,* it began, *and by counsel of the Office of Purity, let it be known that the practice of sorcery, hedge-craft, memory-working, or any art of the unlicensed gift is hereby declared treason against crown and covenant.* Below that, smaller: *All persons so gifted shall present themselves for assessment within fourteen days. Those who conceal their gift do so at peril of soul and body.*

*Assessment.* The word had a clean, bureaucratic shape, like a ledger entry. Everyone in Vell knew what assessment meant. It meant a cell, a question asked over and over in different voices until you answered wrong, and then—lately, in the provinces to the south—a square, a stake, and a crowd told to bring their children, because witnessing was educational.

Felix read it twice, memorized it, and burned it in the stove with a twist of dried lavender to sweeten the smoke. Then they went down to check on Mrs. Halloway next door, because Mrs. Halloway was ninety-one years old and had been pulling fevers out of sick children since before the outlawry, quietly, for eggs and firewood, and she was exactly the sort of person who would try to walk to the assessor's office out of some stubborn idea of honesty.

She was fine. She was sitting in her kitchen shelling peas as though the world hadn't tilted, and when Felix came in she looked up with eyes gone milky at the edges but still sharp underneath.

"You burned yours," she said. Not a question. She could smell lavender smoke the way other people smelled rain coming.

"Of course."

"Good." She dropped a pod into the bowl. "They came for the Tamms boy this morning."

Felix went very still. "Tammy? He's sixteen."

"He's gifted, is what he is. Lights candles with his breath when he forgets himself. His mother signed the confession herself, I heard—thought if she gave him up gentle, they'd be gentle back." Mrs. Halloway's hands kept moving, pea after pea, a small metronome of ordinary work. "They took him from the tannery. He was elbow-deep in a hide."

Felix thought of Tammy Tamm: a big, awkward boy who blushed when praised, who once spent an entire summer secretly mending the shoes of poorer children because he'd worked out how to coax leather soft again with a touch. That was the whole of his crime. A boy who fixed shoes.

"Where are they holding him?"

"Founder's Square. Tonight." Mrs. Halloway finally looked up, and her old eyes were wet and furious. "There's to be a burning. First one in Vell in twenty years. They want everyone to come, Felix. They're calling it a *civic occasion*."

*

The rest of the day passed the way days pass before executions: too fast and too loud. The market stayed open—of course it did; hunger doesn't pause for spectacle—but the noise of it had changed pitch. Bargaining happened in whispers. People didn't meet each other's eyes. Felix sold three charms before noon and hated every one of them, because each bottle they pressed into a stranger's palm was a small act of the exact treason the red broadsheet named, and every buyer knew it, and every buyer bought anyway, and that shared secret used to feel like warmth and now felt like a rope they were all holding together over a drop.

One woman bought a charm of summer rain on a tin roof and clutched it to her chest without haggling, and Felix understood: she wasn't buying luck. She was buying proof that beauty could be kept, bottled, saved—that not everything lovely got taken.

Felix closed the stall early. There was inventory to hide, and hiding it properly took hours: the good bottles buried in the false bottom of the flour chest, the ledger of names—who owed what, who had bought what, who might talk—fed page by page into the stove. The ledger hurt worst. Fifteen years of careful work, of knowing everyone in the night market by their wants and griefs, reduced to ash because knowledge itself had become evidence.

By the time the light went amber over the rooftops, there was nothing left in Felix's rooms to convict them. Nothing except themselves.

*

Founder's Square had been built for markets and proclamations and, once a generation, coronation feasts. It had never been built for what they put in its center that evening: a scaffold of black timber, a stake, a ring of oil-soaked faggots, and around it all, a cordon of witch-hunters in grey-and-silver livery, their faces hidden behind visors polished to mirrors.

The mirrors were the cruelest part, Felix thought, wedged into the crowd three rows deep between a cooper and a woman holding a sleeping child. You couldn't look at a hunter without looking at yourself.

The square filled past capacity. The crown had made attendance near-compulsory—wardens at every approach turning back only the bedridden—and so Vell stood shoulder to shoulder in the cold, six thousand people pretending they wanted to be there, breathing steam into the darkening air. Somewhere a priest was intoning the Litany of Purity, his voice flattened by the wind. Torches guttered along the colonnade. Above it all, on the balcony of the Assize Hall, sat the magistrates in their fur collars, warm and distant as painted figures.

They brought Tammy out at full dark.

He walked between two hunters, and Felix's heart broke in a new way, because Tammy wasn't dragged—he *walked*, straight-backed, chin up, sixteen years old and determined to give them nothing. Someone had washed his face. Someone had combed his hair. His mother stood near the front of the crowd, held upright by two sisters, making a sound that wasn't screaming, that was worse than screaming, a low continuous note like a wire under strain.

The hunter captain read the charges. Felix barely heard them. *Unlicensed gift. Concealment. Contempt of assessment.* Words that would fit any one of a hundred people in this square. Words that would fit—

A bottle in Felix's pocket seemed suddenly very heavy. They'd brought it without thinking, an old habit: always carry something to sell, something to trade, something to barter your way out of trouble. Inside it was a memory of the sea, bought from a drowned sailor's widow years ago. Evidence. On their body. At a burning.

Felix's hand closed around it through the wool of their coat, and for one absurd moment they considered simply dropping it, letting it shatter on the cobblestones, letting the sea loose among the crowd—a flash of salt wind, gull-cries, a summer beach, confusion enough to slip away. But the hunters would know instantly what it was. And there were children here. Let them have one horror tonight, not two.

The captain finished reading. Tammy was bound to the stake. He said something then—Felix couldn't catch the words, but the front rows flinched, and later the story would go around the night market in fragments: that he'd called out *I'm sorry, Mam*, or possibly *the candles light themselves, I swear it*, depending on who needed to have heard what.

They lit the pyre.

Felix had imagined, in the gray hours of the afternoon, that they would look away. Everyone imagines they'll look away. But the crowd does something to you—it holds your face forward like a hand under your chin—and besides, some stubborn animal part of Felix refused to grant the magistrates the mercy of being unseen. If this was done in Vell's name, then Vell would watch, and remember, and the remembering would be a debt.

So Felix watched. Watched the first smoke rise white and then black. Watched Tammy Tamm hold his silence for a long, impossible minute, and then not. Watched the mirrored visors of the hunters reflect six thousand faces and none of them look away either, and understood that the mirrors cut both ways: the hunters were forced to watch too, every one of them, their own faces superimposed on the flame.

Beside Felix, the cooper whispered, "God forgive us," and the woman with the child said, "Hush," though the child slept on, and somewhere in the crowd a single voice began—very softly, half-swallowed—to hum.

It took Felix a moment to place the tune. Then their stomach dropped through the cobblestones.

It was a lullaby. An old one, older than the outlawry, the kind mothers sang before anyone decided that certain gifts were crimes. *Sleep, my sparrow, the wood is weeping...* Harmless. Unprovable. Ungovernable. One voice, then another joining, then a third—a thread of melody running through the crowd like water finding cracks, wordless deniable grief set to music, and the hunters couldn't arrest a tune, couldn't burn a lullaby, and the magistrates on their balcony must have known it, because none of them moved to stop it.

The song rose as the fire roared. By the end, half of Founder's Square was humming, and Felix was humming too, tears freezing on their cheeks, and the sound was the most frightening and beautiful thing they had ever been part of.

*

They walked home the long way, through streets slick with ash that had drifted on the wind like dirty snow. Ash in the gutters. Ash on the sills. Ash settling on the awnings of the shuttered stalls, and Felix thought: *this is what we'll breathe for years. This is what the city will taste of.*

Their invisibility had always been a strategy, carefully maintained—unremarkable face, unremarkable stall, charms disguised as trinkets, gift folded small and hidden. It had worked for fifteen years. But Tammy Tamm had been invisible too, right up until someone remembered him. The hunt wouldn't stay general forever. Broadsheets named categories; informers named people. And Felix Galloway had a chest full of contraband memories, a head full of stolen years, and a neighbor who knew about the lavender smoke.

At their door, Mrs. Halloway was waiting in the dark, wrapped in two shawls.

"I couldn't watch," she said. "Did he—"

"At the end, he was brave."

She nodded slowly. "Then they failed, whatever else they think they've done." Her milky eyes fixed on Felix with uncomfortable precision. "You'll be leaving, won't you. Or fighting. One of the two. I've known you since you were a scrap stealing apples off my sill, child, and you've got the look now. The look people get before they stop hiding."

Felix started to deny it. The words died. Fifteen years of practiced blankness, and one old woman took it apart with a look.

"There's something I have to find," Felix said instead. "In my mentor's things. I keep putting it off."

"Then stop putting it off." Mrs. Halloway pressed something into their hand—peas, absurdly, a handful of shelled peas, food for a journey or a vigil, the currency of her whole life. "Whatever you're carrying, carry it soon. The wind's changed. You can smell it."

Felix could. Under the ash, under the cold, the city smelled different tonight—like a struck match held too long, like the moment before a room ignites.

Inside, they lit no lamp. They sat in the dark amid the clean emptiness of their hidden life, the flour chest silent, the stove cold, and turned the handful of peas over and over in their palm like a rosary.

Tomorrow, they would open Aldous's effects. Tomorrow, the ledger. Tonight, Felix sat with the fear until it stopped trembling and grew still, and then harder, and then something with edges—something that could be gripped.

They had watched a boy burn for lighting candles with his breath.

Felix Galloway was done being invisible.
