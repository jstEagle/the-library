## 3. What the Ledger Keeps

The rain came down on the Galloway house like a confession no one wanted to hear.

Felix had been avoiding the attic for eleven days. They had counted, the way they counted everything now—days since Old Maren Halloway's funeral, since they'd stood in the mud of the paupers' row with rain soaking through their coat and felt nothing at all, which was its own kind of lie, because they had felt everything, all at once, and simply refused to let it through. Eleven days. The mentor was dead; the shop above the tannery was shuttered; and somewhere in the attic of the house Felix had inherited by default—no one else had ever wanted it—sat whatever remained of a life that had taught Felix everything worth knowing about survival.

Survival. That word again. It sat in Felix's chest like a stone swallowed whole.

They climbed the ladder with a lamp in one hand, the other gripping the rail until their knuckles went white. The attic smelled of dust and camphor and something older underneath, something like dried flowers and candle smoke—the ghost-smell of magic worked carefully, quietly, for decades. Below the floorboards, below the whole city of Vell, the wardens were still sweeping. The hunt had begun nine days ago with a proclamation nailed to every door: *By order of the Crown, all practitioners of unsanctioned arts shall surrender themselves for assessment.* Assessment. The crown had always been good at words that meant the opposite of what they said.

Felix set the lamp on a crate and looked at the room.

Maren's things were everywhere, the way they had always been—chaotic, deliberate, a kind of organized hoarding that only made sense to the woman who'd made it. Bundles of dried herbs hung from the rafters like small hanged men. Jars of river water caught moonlight from the single round window. A dressmaker's mannequin stood in the corner wearing nothing but a shawl, and Felix had to look away from it, because in the lamplight it looked too much like a person waiting.

"Right," Felix said aloud, just to hear a voice. Their own voice sounded wrong in here. Smaller. "Right."

They started with the crates nearest the window, because that was where Maren had always kept the things she used most. Bottles, mostly. Empty bottles in straw, each one labeled in her cramped hand: *grief, secondhand. courage, borrowed. first snow, 40 years old.* Felix ran their fingers over the labels without reading them properly, the way you might touch the face of someone sleeping. She had taught Felix that a memory could be drawn out of a person like a splinter, if you knew where the skin was thin. She had taught Felix how to bottle it, how to seal it with wax and a whispered word, how to sell it as a charm to people who would never know what warmth they carried against their breastbones.

She had never taught Felix why she'd stopped teaching. Why, in the last years, she had grown strange and watchful, flinching at wardens' boots on cobblestones, burning letters unopened. Why she had died alone in this house with the door barred and the shutters drawn, as if she'd known something was coming for her and had decided, in the end, to simply stop running.

Felix worked through the crates methodically. Herb bundles. A mortar cracked down the middle. Three bottles of memory so old the light inside had gone the color of weak tea. A child's shoe, single, inexplicable. Letters tied with string—Felix burned those without reading them, because some habits of protection outlive their purpose.

It was under the floorboard beneath the mannequin that they found the ledger.

They found it because the board sounded wrong when they stepped on it—a hollow note, a liar's note—and Felix had spent their whole life learning to hear lies. The board came up with a groan. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay a book bound in grey leather, unmarked, heavy as a paving stone.

Felix knew before they opened it. Some part of them knew, the way you know a storm is coming from the smell of the air. Their hands were steady—that was the terrible thing, the trained thing, the thing Maren had built into them without either of them ever naming it. Their hands were steady as they unwrapped the cloth, steady as they broke the little wax seal on the cover, steady as they turned to the first page.

*Accounts,* said the heading, in Maren's handwriting, younger and sharper than Felix remembered it. *In the matter of what is owed, and to whom, and what has been paid.*

It was a ledger of sales.

Not charms. Not bottled memories sold in the night market for coppers. Sales. The pages recorded transactions in Maren's neat, merciless script: dates going back forty years, names, descriptions of goods, prices paid, and in the margin of every entry, a single recurring notation in red ink:

*Paid to the Court Beneath.*

Felix turned the pages slowly. The lamp hissed. Rain drummed on the roof like fingers.

*Sold: the memory of a wedding, thirty-one years held. Buyer: the Court Beneath. Price: the seller's debt forgiven.*

*Sold: ten years of a soldier's courage. Buyer: the Court Beneath. Price: passage for two across the border.*

*Sold: a mother's love for her living child, redirected to the dead. Buyer: the Court Beneath. Price: —*

That last one had no price listed. Just a dash, and beside it, in red: *she asked twice. I refused to record what she offered the second time.*

Felix's mouth had gone dry. They kept turning pages. They told themselves they were looking for something specific, though they couldn't have said what—confirmation, maybe, that Maren had been a monster, or proof that she hadn't, or simply an ending, a last page, a place to stop.

Then they reached the entry dated fifteen years ago, in the autumn, and their hands finally stopped being steady.

The page was different from the others. The writing was larger, pressed hard enough into the paper to tear it in places, as if Maren's hand had shaken while she wrote—or as if she had written it over and over in her mind before committing it once, brutally, to ink.

*Sold: one soul entire, female child, aged six years. Condition: intact, unspoiled, unclaimed by any grief. Buyer: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Price: the life of the seller's remaining child, guaranteed safe passage through the coming purge, and silence everlasting.*

And beneath that, in red ink, pressed so deep the nib had nearly gone through the paper:

*I am sorry. I am sorry. I am sorry. Wren Galloway. Her name is Wren Galloway. Let it be written somewhere that her name is Wren Galloway.*

The lamp fell from Felix's hand.

They didn't remember dropping it. They remembered the sound it made—glass and tin on floorboards, the flame guttering but not dying, throwing shadows up the walls like drowning figures—and they remembered being on their knees in the dust with the ledger open in front of them, reading the entry again, and again, and again, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something survivable if given enough chances.

Wren.

Their sister. Their sister who had died—no. Who had *been taken*, fifteen years ago, in the fever-season, when Felix was eight and Wren was six. Taken in the night, Maren had said. Fever takes the small ones fastest, Maren had said, and Felix had believed her because believing her was easier than the alternative, and because Maren had held them afterward, and fed them, and taught them, and loved them in her flinty, transactional way, and what kind of child looks at the woman who saved them and sees a monster?

But Wren hadn't died. Wren had been *sold*. Sold by the woman who raised Felix, sold to buy Felix's safety during the purge—the purge, the great witch-hunt of fifteen years ago, the one that had emptied half the tannery district, the one whose fires Felix had been told had nearly claimed them both. Maren had bought Felix's life with Wren's soul. Had traded a six-year-old girl, *intact, unspoiled, unclaimed by any grief*, to a court beneath the mountain that ate what mortals surrendered.

And the court beneath the mountain was real. Felix had always filed it away with the weeping wood and the founder's ghost and all the other stories Vell told itself—nursery tales, warnings, the folklore of a city built on top of something older. But here it was in Maren's ledger, entry after entry after entry, forty years of transactions. The court was real. It bought. It paid. And it had bought Wren.

Felix didn't know how long they knelt there. The rain changed its rhythm. The lamp righted itself somehow, or Felix righted it, or neither. At some point they became aware that they were speaking aloud, saying *no* over and over in a voice they didn't recognize, flat and mechanical, a denial with no feeling in it because the feeling was too large to fit through any door the body owned.

Grief came first, then—not for Wren, strangely, but for the child Felix had been. The eight-year-old who had cried for three days and then stopped crying because Maren had said, gently, *the dead don't come back, little fox, and the living must eat.* The child who had built a whole self out of that sentence. Out of *the living must eat.* Every charm sold in the dark, every memory stolen and bottled, every careful invisibility—it had all been built on the foundation of a lie told to a grieving child by the only adult left to tell him anything.

Because that was the guilt, when it finally arrived. It arrived not as a wave but as a slow flood, finding every crack. *You lived because she died.* Not died. Was consumed. Was being consumed still, perhaps, year after year, in whatever dark glittering hall the fae kept their purchases—if souls could be eaten slowly, the way Maren's ledger implied, the way the entries about "installments" implied, then Wren had been dying for fifteen years, a little more each day, and every day Felix had spent haggling in the night market, every day Felix had spent perfecting the craft of taking memories from strangers, had been a day purchased with their sister's ongoing annihilation.

And worse—worse, because guilt is inventive—was the thought that arrived last, quiet as a knife between ribs: *You are her apprentice. You do what she did. You take from people who cannot afford to lose what you take.* Felix had told themselves for years that it was different, that the memories they stole were surplus, that the desperate mother in the market had *wanted* to buy back her son's laugh, that everyone walked away richer. But Maren had probably told herself things too. Maren had probably stood over this very ledger and told herself that selling one soul to save another was arithmetic, was mercy, was the best available price. And Felix had learned everything from her. Everything except where she'd hidden the parts of herself she couldn't bear.

Felix closed the ledger.

They sat in the dust with the book in their lap and the dead woman's herbs hanging overhead like witnesses, and they did not cry. Crying was for people with time. Instead they did what Maren had trained into them, what survival demanded: they made a list.

One. The court beneath the mountain was real, and it traded in souls, and it had bought Wren fifteen years ago.

Two. The ledger recorded prices. Which meant the court honored bargains. Which meant bargains could be struck, renegotiated, broken, or beaten.

Three. Fifteen years was a long installment plan. If Wren's soul was being consumed gradually, then some portion of her remained. Something could be saved. Maybe not all. Something.

Four. The turning of the year was six weeks away. Felix didn't know why that date mattered—there was nothing in the ledger about it—but the word *solstice* appeared in the margins of the last dozen entries, underlined, again and again, in red, like a warning Maren had left too late or too frightened to explain. Whatever happened at the solstice, it mattered to the court. Deadlines mattered to buyers.

Five. Felix could not do this alone. The thought sat in their stomach like a swallowed coal. They had spent twenty-three years learning to need no one. But the mountain gate would be watched, the passes patrolled, the court itself a labyrinth of bargains designed to strip travelers of everything they carried. And Felix had exactly one skill, and it was theft, and theft alone would not be enough.

They needed help. They needed, absurdly, impossibly, help from the very machinery that hunted people like them—which meant, somewhere in the ranks of the witch-hunters, there might be a crack. A hesitation. A hunter who wept.

Felix laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, closer to a sob than they intended. A memory-thief planning to rob the fae, guided by a dead traitor's ledger, needing the mercy of a witch-hunter. Maren would have called it suicide. Maren had probably called it exactly that, once, alone in this attic, before sealing the ledger under the floorboards and going downstairs to make supper for the child whose life she'd bought with another child's soul.

"I'm going to get her back," Felix said to the empty attic. To the hanging herbs. To the mannequin in its shawl. To Maren, wherever dead traitors went. "Whatever it costs. Whoever it costs. Do you hear me? I'm going to get her back."

The attic said nothing. Attics are honest that way.

Felix wrapped the ledger in its oilcloth and tucked it inside their coat, against their chest, where the weight of it sat like a second heartbeat. Then they climbed down the ladder, out of the dead woman's house, into streets washed clean by rain and full of people who didn't know yet that the world had changed.

They were three steps into the alley behind the house when the lantern at the corner flickered—not with wind, but with presence—and a voice spoke from the shadow beneath it, low and level and carrying the particular authority of someone accustomed to being obeyed.

"Felix Galloway. Don't run. I've been looking for you—and I think you're going to want to hear what I have to say."

The figure stepped into the light. Grey coat. Silver badge. The eyes of someone who had seen too many pyres and would see more.

A witch-hunter. Standing in the rain, saying Felix's name like she already knew everything.

* * *

Later—much later, in the years when Felix told this story to people who needed to hear it—they would say that the strangest part was not the ledger, nor the sale, nor even the witch-hunter waiting in the alley. The strangest part was that when Felix read the words *her name is Wren Galloway, let it be written somewhere*, they had felt, for one unbearable moment, grateful. Grateful to the woman who sold their sister. Because someone, at least, had written the name down. Because in a city determined to forget its gifted, in a kingdom that erased what it burned, someone had taken the trouble to make sure that Wren Galloway had existed at all.

Love and horror, Felix learned that night, live in the same house. Sometimes they share a bed. Sometimes one of them writes the ledger, and the other one hides it under the floorboards, and both of them call it protection.

The rain kept falling. The witch-hunter waited. And Felix Galloway, orphaned twice in one night—once by truth, once by the woman who'd told the truth only in secret, in red ink, in a book buried beneath a dead woman's floor—stepped forward into the lantern light to meet the enemy who might, improbably, terribly, become the only ally they had left.
