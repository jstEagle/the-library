## 4. The Hunter Who Wept

The house on Candlemaker's Row belonged to a dead man, and Felix Galloway was robbing it.

Not the way ordinary thieves robbed—no crowbar in the doorjamb, no silver spoons rattling into a sack. The dead man's valuables were all still here: his furniture under dust sheets, his clock stopped at some hour that no longer mattered to anyone. What Felix had come for hung in the air itself, invisible as breath on glass.

They sat cross-legged on the floor of the study with their palms flat against the floorboards, eyes closed, and *listened*.

Every room kept what had happened in it. Most people never noticed—they walked through their lives shedding memories like loose hair, and the rooms drank them up. But a memory-thief could feel them: warm patches in the cold of an empty house. This one glowed near the hearth, dense and golden as honeycomb. An old happiness. Someone laughing until they wheezed. The smell of brandy and pipe smoke. A woman's voice saying, *"You ridiculous old bear,"* with such fondness that even now, years after her voice had gone silent in the world, it still lived here like a banked coal.

Felix drew it out slowly, carefully, the way you'd lift a thread from a tapestry so the picture wouldn't notice its own unraveling. The warmth pooled in their cupped hands, shimmered, and slid into the little glass vial waiting between their knees. The stopper went in. The vial warmed against their palm, and inside it something swirled like firelight seen through amber.

A good haul. A *kind* one, too—the widow would sell the house soon, empty and echoing, and she'd never know that the best part of her marriage had been carried out through the kitchen window. Better it live in someone's keeping than evaporate with the wallpaper when the plasterers came.

Felix told themselves that every time. It didn't always work.

* * *

Ilsa Verrow had been standing in the doorway for four minutes before she moved.

She'd learned patience in the hunt. You didn't kick doors anymore—not since the reforms, not since Captain Rennick decided arrests made better theater than raids. You watched. You waited. You let the gifted show you the shape of their sin before you named it, because the crowds in Founder's Square liked a confession, and a confession needed witnesses.

So she stood in the shadow of the hallway with her hand resting easy on her baton and watched a slip of a person in a patched grey coat pull something out of a dead man's floorboards, bottle it, and smile—a small, tired, private smile—and something in Ilsa's chest turned over wrong.

Four minutes. She could have taken them in two.

She stepped forward instead, boot heel clicking deliberate on the boards, and said: "Don't run."

The thief froze. Turned. And Ilsa saw the exact moment recognition landed—the widening of dark eyes, the quick arithmetic behind them, weighing the window versus the door versus the lie. Younger than she'd expected. Thin as a winter branch. Hands that looked more used to needlework than violence, though she knew better than to trust hands.

"Captain Verrow." The voice was low, careful, pitched somewhere between apology and defiance. "I was just—"

"Don't." Ilsa held up a hand. "Whatever it is. I've heard every version. I was looking for my aunt's ring. I was watering the plants. I'm the dead man's ghost." She almost smiled. Almost. "Sit down."

The thief sat. Slowly, theatrically, all innocence—but Ilsa saw the vial vanish into a sleeve with a speed that spoke of long practice, and noted it, and said nothing about it yet.

"You're Galloway," she said. It wasn't a question. Three weeks of quiet inquiries after the night market rumors, three weeks of a name passed from mouth to mouth like contraband—*the charm-seller on Tallow Street, the one whose luck-charms actually work.* "Felix Galloway."

"And you're the witch-hunter who burned the Cobbler last Tuesday." No fear in it now—or none showing. That was its own kind of skill. "Funny how we both know things we shouldn't."

"The cobbler raised the dead."

"He healed a horse. Badly. It limped." Felix's chin came up. "You want to tell me again which of us is the criminal?"

And there it was—that turn in Ilsa's chest again, worse this time. Because she had stood in Founder's Square with the wind carrying ash into her hair, and she had read the charges off the scroll in her clearest parade-ground voice, and the cobbler—a round, gentle man who'd once fixed her bootlace without charging her—had looked at her the whole time with an expression she couldn't name until much later, alone, staring at her ceiling. Not hatred. Pity. As if *she* were the one being destroyed.

"Stand up," she said, rougher than she meant to. "Hands where I can see them."

* * *

Felix stood. Their heart was going like a rabbit's, but their face stayed smooth, because panic was a luxury and they'd trained themselves out of luxuries young. Warden Verrow—*Captain*, the sash said now, they must have promoted her after the burning—circled them slowly, baton loose in her grip, and Felix catalogued her the way they catalogued everyone: tall, mid-thirties, uniform pressed but cuffs worn shiny from use, a face built for sternness that kept trying to be something else and failing.

There were shadows under her eyes like bruises. And there—Felix's gift brushed against her whether they willed it or not, the way their gift always did around strong feeling—there was grief on her, thick and old, soaked into her like smoke into wool.

Grief, and underneath it, guilt. Guilt with teeth.

"You're going to arrest me," Felix said. Not asking. Watching her face do its failing.

"I should." She stopped in front of them. Close enough that Felix could see her hands weren't quite steady. "You know what happens to me if I don't? There are quotas now. Reports. If I let a documented gift-wielder walk out of this house, I'm next on the list. They don't care that I've served eleven years. They'll burn me with the rest."

"Then burn me too," Felix said, "and save yourself the paperwork."

Something cracked across her face then—quick, involuntary, gone. But Felix had spent a lifetime reading faces across market stalls, and they caught it: not anger. *Exhaustion.* The look of someone who has been holding a door shut against a flood and has just realized the water is already at her waist.

"You don't understand," Verrow said quietly. "What it's like. Standing there while—" She stopped. Started again. "Do you know what the gifted look like? When they're on the pyre?"

"No," Felix said. "I've been busy not being on one."

"They look *relieved*." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Some of them. The old woman two weeks ago—before the flames caught, she looked right at me and she smiled, like I was doing her a kindness, like anything was better than hiding anymore. And I keep thinking—" Her jaw tightened. "I keep thinking that's the real crime. That we've made a whole city of people who'd rather burn than spend one more day afraid."

The study was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past on the cobbles, and life in Vell went on pretending nothing was wrong.

Felix found, to their surprise, that they couldn't hold the sneer. They tried. It wouldn't stay up.

"Why are you telling me this?" they asked.

"Because you're the first one I've caught in months who isn't cursing me or begging." She looked at them directly, and her eyes were wet, and she seemed furious about it. "You just stand there like you've already grieved for me. Like you know something I don't."

"I know you're going to have to choose," Felix said softly. "Soon. Everyone does, eventually. The crown or your conscience. And I know which one keeps you warmer at night, Captain, because I can *feel* it on you—it's all over you like frost. You haven't slept a full night since the first pyre."

"Shut up."

"It's true, though, isn't it? You lie awake counting them. The ones you've burned. You know their names. You probably know their *faces* better than their families did, because you're the last thing most of them ever saw—"

"*I said shut up.*"

The baton came up. Felix flinched—couldn't help it—and Verrow froze with it half-raised, breathing hard, staring at them, and then at her own arm as if it belonged to someone else.

Slowly, shaking, she lowered it.

"Gods' teeth," she whispered. "Look at me. Eleven years. I joined because I believed it, do you understand? When I was nineteen, a hedge-witch in my village cursed my brother's crops out of spite over a fence line, and he watched his children go hungry for a winter, and I thought—I *knew*—that magic was a knife and someone had to take it away from people. That was all. That was everything. Take away the knives." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "Nobody told me the knives would multiply. Nobody told me that by the time you've confiscated every knife in Vell, you're standing in a square holding the only one left, wondering when you became the thing you were afraid of."

Felix said nothing. There was nothing clever to say. For the first time in longer than they could remember, they simply listened.

* * *

Ilsa set the baton down on the dead man's desk. It felt like setting down a bone she'd been gripping so long it had grown into her hand.

"Here is what's going to happen," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "In about ten minutes, I'm going to walk out of this house and report that I investigated a report of unlawful activity on Candlemaker's Row and found nothing but squatters. The property's empty; it'll hold for a week, maybe two. After that, someone else gets the rumor about the charm-seller on Tallow Street, and I can't protect you from whoever comes."

Felix stared at her. "Why?"

"Because—" And here was the thing she hadn't planned to say, the thing rising out of her like water through a crack. "Because three nights ago I dreamed about the cobbler. He was sitting at my kitchen table, mending my boots, and he looked up at me and asked me to hand him the awl, and I woke up reaching for it. Reaching to help him. Do you understand what that means?" Her voice shook. "It means some part of me has known for a long time that I'm on the wrong side of this. It means I need—I *need*—there to be one person I didn't put on the pyre. Just one. So that when they finally come for me, I can say I was something other than what they made me. Once. At least once."

The silence stretched. Felix Galloway looked at her for a long moment, and Ilsa had the strange sensation of being *read*—not searched for weapons or lies, but read, the way you'd read a letter from someone you'd wronged.

"That's not mercy," Felix said at last. "That's just guilt with better posture."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "Probably."

"But it's a start."

Felix reached into their sleeve and set the vial on the desk between them. Inside, the captured warmth swirled gold.

"What's in it?" Ilsa asked.

"A memory. From this house. The dead man and his wife, laughing by the fire." Felix's thumb traced the glass. "I sell them disguised as charms. Luck, health, safe travel. People buy them because they feel *good* and they don't ask why, and honestly, most of them don't want to know. Knowing makes it heavy." A pause. "The mother on Tallow Street bought one last month. Her son died in the fever year. I sold her back a memory of him learning to whistle. She thinks it's a charm against sorrow." Felix's jaw worked. "Maybe it is. Maybe that's all any of it is."

Ilsa picked up the vial. It was warm, absurdly warm, like a living thing. "And if I open it?"

"You get a dead stranger's happiest afternoon in your head forever. And he loses it. Well—he's dead. Nothing left to lose it but the room." Felix shrugged, and the shrug didn't quite land; it was too carefully casual. "Everything I do is theft, Captain. I've just gotten very good at finding people who'll thank me for it."

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Ilsa set the vial down and pushed it back across the desk.

"Keep it," she said. "Sell it. Feed yourself." She pulled a folded paper from her coat instead—a page torn from an official ledger, covered in her own cramped handwriting—and laid it flat. "But listen to me now, because I'm only saying this once, and then I'm going to forget I said it, and so are you.

"There's a list. Being compiled at the Provost's office. Names gathered from informants, tax records, parish gossip—all of it. Every suspected gift-wielder in Vell, ranked by threat and by *convenience*. The convenient ones go first. The poor, the friendless, the ones nobody will miss." She tapped the paper. "This is a copy of the first forty names. Forty-one, if we count the blank space at the bottom where mine will go eventually, because I've started asking questions I shouldn't."

Felix took the page with careful fingers. Scanned it. And went very still.

Halfway down the list, in the column marked *Notes*, beside a name that was not theirs: *Galloway, Wren (decd. 15 yrs). Family connection to subject F.G. Investigate sale records — court beneath mountain.*

"Wren," Felix said. The word came out strange, flattened, like a coin worn smooth. "Where did you get this name?"

"An archivist's note. Fifteen-year-old case file, flagged for follow-up." Ilsa frowned at their face—at whatever was happening in it. "Galloway. Do you know her?"

"She was my sister." Felix's voice was very quiet and very controlled, which was somehow worse than screaming. "She died when we were children."

"Then why is the crown investigating a fifteen-year-old death *now*, in the middle of a purge, with a note about the court beneath the mountain attached to it?"

Felix didn't answer. They were staring at the page, and Ilsa watched something move behind their eyes—some door opening onto a room they'd kept locked a very long time.

"I have to go," Felix said.

"Galloway—"

"Thank you. For the list. For—for whatever this was." They pocketed the paper, and paused at the window, silhouetted against the grey light. "Captain. You asked why I looked at you like I'd already grieved for you." A beat. "Because people who fight their own side always end up on mine eventually. It's the oldest story there is. I'll leave the window open."

Then they were gone, quick and quiet as smoke, and Ilsa Verrow stood alone in a dead man's study with an unspent arrest and a heart pounding like a fist on a door.

On the desk, the vial still glowed faintly gold. She didn't touch it. But she looked at it for a long time—longer than she could explain—before she straightened her sash, composed her official face, and went out to lie to the crown.

* * *

Felix ran the whole way home.

Not from the wardens. From the word. *Wren.*

They bolted the door of their garret, lit no lamp, and sat in the dark with the stolen page trembling in their hands, reading the archivist's note again and again until the letters swam. *Sale records. Court beneath the mountain.* Fifteen years ago, Felix had been ten, and Wren eight, and their parents had sold something to pay a debt—something they'd been told was a birthright, a blessing, a *burden lifted*. Felix had cried for a week without knowing why. Their mother had held them and said, *you'll forget, little fox, forgetting is a mercy,* and by spring Felix had forgotten, and only a hollow ache remained where a sister used to be.

Their gift had woken that same winter. The first thing they'd ever stolen was their own memory of losing her—bottled it themselves, hid it in the floorboards, and told everyone, including themselves, that they'd never had a sister at all.

The vial was still under the third floorboard. It had always been there. Felix had never once dared to open it.

Now they knelt on the cold boards with it in their hands, small and heavy as a heart, and understood at last what they were holding: not a memory. A map. Fifteen years old, addressed to a kingdom beneath the mountain, where their sister's soul had been sold and—according to a witch-hunter's dry bureaucratic note—was apparently still *on account*.

Outside, the bells of Vell rang the ninth hour. Somewhere in the city, another pyre was being stacked.

Felix Galloway pressed the vial to their lips, like a kiss, like an oath, and began to plan how to steal a sister back from the dead.
