## 5. A Lady Without Yesterdays

The invitation came on paper worth more than everything Felix owned combined.

It arrived at the charm stall in the night market—delivered by a footman in livery so fine that three pickpockets circled him for an hour before giving up in despair. Cream-colored vellum, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and addressed not to *Felix Galloway* but to *the Memory-Seller of Lantern Row*. Someone knew. Someone had known for a while.

*Mademoiselle Odile Marchand requests your services regarding a matter of... retrieval. Come at dusk. The Marchand house keeps its own gate on Verrin Street. Do not come by the front door; the front door is watched.*

Felix read it twice, then a third time, holding it near the stall's hooded lamp until the letters seemed to swim. Retrieval. Not charms, not luck, not the little bottled comforts that kept bread on their table. *Retrieval.* The word sat in Felix's chest like a swallowed stone.

Because there was only one thing in all of Vell that anyone would hire a memory-thief to retrieve.

And because the name on the letter was one Felix had heard before—in the ledger's margins, in the mentor's cramped hand: *M—debt settled through the Marchand line.* He hadn't understood it then. He'd told himself he would puzzle it out later. Later had turned out to be a footman with tired eyes and a letter that smelled faintly of cold candle smoke.

"You're staring at that like it might bite," said Maret, the pie-seller two stalls down, without looking up from her crusts.

"Everything bites eventually."

"Mm." She shaped dough with flour-white fingers. "If you're going to die, do it after market. I've got a delivery coming."

It was meant as a joke. It landed somewhere nearer to prophecy. Felix folded the letter into their coat, over the breast, where it lay against the heartbeat like a second, colder heart, and began packing up the stall early—for the first time in six years, leaving coins unearned on the table rather than risk being late to whatever waited behind the Marchands' side gate.

* * *

Verrin Street belonged to the part of the city where money had gone to die gracefully.

The houses were old nobility, built when Vell still remembered why it existed—tall, narrow-fronted, their stone faces carved with saints whose features the reformers had chiseled smooth a generation ago. Empty-eyed saints. The whole street had that look: wealth preserved in amber, worship scraped away, everyone pretending the blankness had always been there. Felix moved through it like oil through water, invisible by habit, head down, hands in pockets, one thumb hooked through the strap of their satchel where eleven bottles of other people's yesterdays clinked softly with each step.

The Marchand gate was iron, older than the street around it, and it opened before Felix could knock.

"The garden path," said the footman. Up close he looked less like a servant and more like a soldier who had misplaced his war. His eyes did a quick, professional inventory of Felix—the coat too good for a gutter thief and too bad for a tradesman, the satchel, the way Felix stood with weight balanced on both feet as if expecting to run. "She's waiting under the pear tree. She'll know if you lie to her, so I'd advise against it."

"She can't possibly—"

"I don't know what she can do." The footman's voice went quiet and strange. "That's rather the point. This way."

The garden behind the house was a ruin pretending to be intentional. Roses grown feral over collapsed trellises. A dry fountain with a saintless basin. And beneath a pear tree heavy with unpicked fruit, wrapped in a shawl despite the mild evening, sat Odile Marchand.

Felix had heard the name spoken in markets and drawing rooms alike, always with the same careful hush reserved for tragedy among the rich: *poor Odile Marchand, she sold everything, you know, every scrap of her past, to pay her father's debts—she doesn't even remember her own mother's face.* People said it the way they said *what a lovely sunset*, comfortably, from a great distance.

In person she was younger than the rumors suggested. Twenty-four, perhaps twenty-five. Dark hair cut short and practical, as though she had woken one morning and simply decided hair wasn't worth remembering either. Her face was the kind painters loved—high cheekbones, a mouth made for some expression it never quite reached—and her eyes were the problem. Felix had seen empty eyes before. On the burned-out. On the grief-mad. These were different. These weren't emptied by loss. They had been *emptied*, scoured clean, and someone had hung curtains in them anyway: a pleasant half-smile, a tilt of courteous attention, all of it worn like clothing over nothing at all.

"You're smaller than I expected," she said. Her voice was low and unhurried, an instrument tuned to a single note. "I imagined someone taller. Thieves are always taller in the imagining."

"And you're exactly as they describe," Felix said, then winced. "Forgive me. That was—"

"Honest? Don't apologize for honesty. It's the only currency I can't counterfeit anymore." She gestured to the bench across from her. "Sit. Would you like wine? I'm told it's very good. I wouldn't know. Everything tastes like the first time, which sounds delightful and is actually a form of torture."

Felix sat. The bench was stone and cold through their coat. "You know what I am."

"I know what you are the way I know what hunger is. Abstractly, and with increasing urgency." She poured two glasses from a bottle sweating on the table between them, though her hand trembled slightly on the second pour—not fear, Felix decided, but effort, like someone lifting something heavier than it appeared. "I've spent four years trying to buy back a single memory of my own life. Four years. Every dealer in this city has taken my money and given me nothing, or worse, given me *fabrications*—little sugar-plum pasts stitched together from what I told them I wanted to remember. One man sold me a childhood in a house I have never lived in, with a dog I have never owned, and I wept over it for a week because it was the closest thing I had to a mother's love." She set the bottle down with exaggerated care. "Then I heard about the charm-seller on Lantern Row. Who doesn't sell fabrications. Whose bottles are real. Who sells memories that were actually lived."

"They're not mine to sell twice," Felix said quietly. "What's bought from me is bought outright. The original goes into the bottle. What stays in the seller's head afterward is—" they searched for the word, "—an echo. Fainter each year. That's how it works. That's how it *has* to work, or the wardens—"

"I don't care how it works." For the first time the curtain slipped. Something raw moved behind her eyes and was gone. "I want to hire you for a theft, Master Galloway. Not a purchase. A theft. From the place where my past is kept."

The stone in Felix's chest grew teeth. "There's only one such place."

"Yes." Odile smiled the pleasant, terrible smile. "The court beneath the mountain."

The garden seemed to lean in. Somewhere beyond the wall, a watchman called the hour—two bells past dusk—and the sound fell into the silence like a coin down a well.

"How do you know about the court?" Felix asked. "Most people in Vell think it's a nursery story. Something to frighten children out of the deep roads."

"My father knew. Before they took his mind along with his fortune, he knew a great many things." She drew something from beneath her shawl: a small key, black iron, unremarkable except that it hummed. Not audibly. Felix felt it in their teeth, in the fillings of their gift, the way a struck tuning fork makes nearby strings shiver. "He left me this. He left me almost nothing else—his debts ate everything, including, eventually, the deed to our own history—but he left me this, and a sentence written on the back of a receipt: *They keep what we surrender. Nothing surrendered is ever destroyed. Find the weeping wood.*"

"Weeping wood," Felix repeated. The words stirred something—a half-remembered fragment from the mentor's ledger, a phrase in the margin beside Wren's entry that Felix had assumed was poetry. Their mouth went dry.

"That's what he wrote. I assume it's a place. Or a person. Or a door." Odile set the key on the table between them, and Felix noticed now that her hands had stopped trembling—that the effort had been in *holding steady*, all this time, the way a drowning man expends more strength staying calm than thrashing. "Here is what I know about myself, Master Galloway. I know that I wake every morning in a body that flinches from things I cannot name. I know that I cannot bear the smell of snowmelt. I know that when I hear a particular melody—I couldn't hum it for you, I only know it when I hear it—I sit down wherever I am and cry for an hour and haven't the faintest idea why. I am a locked house walking around in fine clothes. And I am done asking politely to be let back in."

She leaned forward. The lamplight from the house caught her face and for a moment Felix saw past the curtains entirely, saw the vast clean emptiness behind them, and recognized it—horribly, intimately—as a cousin of their own.

"So," Odile said. "Name your price. Whatever it is. Money, silence, a favor from a name that still opens doors in this city—name it, and then steal me back one memory. Any memory. Even a small one. Even the taste of something I used to eat." Her voice cracked, just once, and she pressed it flat again like smoothing a wrinkle from silk. "I would give you everything I have left for ten minutes of being a person with a past."

Felix should have refused. Some part of them, the part that had survived fifteen years in Vell by keeping small, by selling luck-charms and lying low, was screaming the old litany: *the mountain isn't yours to touch, the court eats thieves, the last person who went looking came back wrong or didn't come back.* But that part had been dying since the night Felix found the ledger. Since the entry in the mentor's cramped hand: *Wren. Sold. Fifteen years' term. The debt comes due at the turning of the year.*

Two kinds of emptiness, sitting across a garden table. And between them, a road under the roots that led to everything Felix had lost and everything Odile had never been allowed to keep.

"Your price," Felix said slowly, "is that you come with me."

Odile blinked. Of all the responses she had rehearsed for, Felix suspected, this was not among them.

"Not just hire me. Come. Because here's the truth you need before you spend another coin chasing sugar-plums: no thief alive can steal from the vaults beneath the mountain alone. I've seen what's written about them. The court doesn't trade in coin—it trades in surrender. You want a memory back? Fine. But the price of walking through the gate is a memory paid on entry, and the price of leaving—" Felix held her gaze, refusing to soften it, because softening it would be a lie, and this woman had been lied to enough by dealers with sweet bottles, "—the price of leaving may be more than any of us can pay. I'm going to the mountain regardless. I have my own account to settle there. Someone sold to that court fifteen years ago who shouldn't have been, and the debt comes due soon. If you travel with me, you won't be a customer. You'll be a conspirator. The crown burns people for less. The fae do worse."

For a long moment Odile Marchand said nothing at all. The pear tree dropped a fruit onto the grass with a soft, final thud.

Then she laughed—a startling sound, rusty, as if laughter were also a memory she'd had to buy back secondhand.

"A conspiracy," she said. "Good. I've tried being a victim for four years. It's terribly dull and pays nothing." She rose, wrapping the shawl tighter, and Felix saw how thin she was beneath it, how much of her had been sold off along with the past—meat and bone included, apparently, when the debts ran deep enough. "I'll need three days. There's a man in the Low Quarter who holds my father's papers, and I'll want the footman armed, and I intend to learn which of my relatives will try to stop me from leaving, so I can enjoy disappointing them properly." She paused at the garden's edge, silhouetted against the light spilling from the house, and turned back. "Master Galloway. The person you're going to save. Is it someone you loved?"

The question went through Felix like a needle finding the exact center of a bruise.

"Yes," they said.

"Then we understand each other completely." Odile smiled—and this time, for just a heartbeat, the smile was not a curtain over the emptiness. It was a crack in a wall, and something alive looked out through it. "Three days. Be ready to leave by the north road, and tell no one—no one—where we're going. My father's notes say the mountain watches for names spoken aloud." She lifted the black key from the table and pressed it into Felix's palm, closing their fingers over it. It was warm. Keys shouldn't be warm. "Hold this. I don't trust myself with it. I keep having dreams about doors, and I'm afraid that one night I'll walk through one and simply... not come back. Better it belongs to someone with something left to lose."

She walked toward the house, straight-backed, a lady without yesterdays going home to a mansion full of rooms she couldn't remember living in.

Felix stood alone under the pear tree, the key humming against their palm like a trapped moth, and looked down at it—and stopped breathing.

Because in the fading light, on the key's bow, worn nearly smooth but unmistakable to eyes trained since childhood on hidden marks, was a maker's stamp. A tiny stamped device Felix had seen once before, pressed into the wax seal of a ledger page, beside an entry dated fifteen years ago.

Beside the entry that read: *Wren.*

The Marchand family had not merely known about the sale.

Their seal was on it.

* * *

Felix walked back through Vell in a daze, the city sliding past like scenery behind glass, and did not truly see any of it until they reached the bridge over the Sere and stopped, hands on the cold rail, watching the black water carry lamplight downstream.

Odile's family had sold Wren.

Or Odile's father had brokered it. Or the Marchand debts—the debts that had eaten their fortune, their past, everything—had been *paid* with a child's soul, and Odile herself had been handed over as collateral without ever knowing what she'd lost or why. Two children, sold by the same hand, fifteen years apart or fifteen years together; Felix couldn't yet see the shape of it, only that the shapes interlocked, tooth to groove, like a lock and the key now burning a hole in their pocket.

*M—debt settled through the Marchand line.* The mentor's margin note. It had never been a mystery. It had been an accusation, waiting all these years for someone brave enough to read it aloud.

Below the bridge, the river murmured its one wordless syllable, over and over, the way rivers do. Above, the stars were coming out—thin, cold, indifferent, the same stars that must have watched over the mountain's gate on the night a child named Wren Galloway was led down into the dark.

Felix thought of Odile crying at melodies she couldn't name, flinching from snowmelt, waking in a body full of ghosts that had been legally repossessed. Thought of Wren, somewhere beneath the earth, wearing fifteen stolen years like jewelry on someone else's throat.

*Two kinds of emptiness,* Felix had thought in the garden, pitying her.

Now, gripping the rail until knuckles whitened, they understood the truth with terrible clarity: there was only one kind. The court beneath the mountain made sure of that. It fed on surrender, and it never, ever let a debt go unpaid—and somewhere in the dark below, the interest on the Marchand family's bargain was still accruing, measured in the only currency that mattered.

Three days. Then the north road, the passes, the gate—and whatever waited beneath the roots, wearing a queen's smile.

Felix pushed off from the rail and walked on, and if their shadow on the cobbles seemed to move a half-step ahead of them, eager, hungry, already climbing toward the mountain—well. In Vell, that was the sort of thing a person learned not to look at directly.

The turning of the year was coming. And it was bringing all its debts with it.
