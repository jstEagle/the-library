## 6. The Road Under the Roots

Ilsa Verrow had ridden the mountain passes a hundred times in her service to the crown, and she had never once been afraid of them.

She was afraid of them now.

Not of the passes themselves—the Grey Teeth, the shepherds called them, that ragged spine of stone where Vell's northern border gnawed at the sky—but of what she was doing in them. Three days out from the city, riding hard through country that grew wilder with every mile, and every mile put more distance between Ilsa Verrow, Captain of the Witch-Hunters, and everything she had ever been.

Behind her, Felix Galloway kept their horse moving with the grim competence of someone who had learned to survive by never being noticed. Beside them rode Odile Marchand, wrapped in a traveling cloak worth more than most warden pensions, her face turned toward the peaks as though she might find something there—though Ilsa knew better now. Odile Marchand had nothing to find anywhere. She had sold it all.

And in Felix's coat pocket, wrapped in wool against the cold, sat a small glass vial that glowed faintly when the light was wrong: a bottled memory of the old roads, bought or stolen—Ilsa had stopped asking which—from some shepherd dead these twenty years, and it was the only map they had to a gate that officially did not exist.

*If my superiors could see me*, Ilsa thought, and then corrected herself. *When my superiors see me.* Because they would. There was no version of this that ended with her riding back into Vell and pinning her commission back on her breast as though nothing had happened. She had understood that the moment she'd opened the garrison stable and led out three horses instead of reporting a theft.

She had been a hunter for eleven years. She knew how stories like hers ended.

The road climbed through pine forest so old and dense that noon came down to the ground as a green twilight. Frost furred the branches. Somewhere above them, wind moved through stone with a sound like slow breathing, and Ilsa found herself listening to it the way she had once listened for the crackle of pyres—a sound you learned to hear even in your sleep, if you stayed long enough in the trade.

"You're doing it again," Felix said.

They had drawn level with her without her noticing, which annoyed her. "Doing what."

"Listening for us." Felix's voice was light, but their eyes weren't. They had a way of looking at her that felt like being read—not the crude reading of a confession under pressure, but something gentler and far more invasive. "You keep checking where we are. Half-turning in the saddle. You've done it since dawn."

"I'm checking the road."

"The road is behind me," Felix said. "You keep looking at me and Odile."

Ilsa said nothing. The horses' breath steamed. Ahead, Odile laughed softly at something invisible—she did that sometimes, Ilsa had noticed, laughed at nothing, or perhaps at the ghost of whatever joke she'd once been—and urged her mare forward through a drift of last year's leaves.

"Captain Verrow," Felix said quietly, "if you're going to arrest us, do it before we reach the pass. Not after. It'll be crueler after."

"I'm not going to arrest you."

"You said that in Vell, too. Before you broke me out of your own cells and stole three government horses."

"That wasn't stealing. It was requisitioning."

Something flickered across Felix's face—surprise, then the careful suppression of a smile. "Requisitioning."

"For an ongoing investigation." Ilsa kept her eyes on the trail. "Which is what this is. Officially. Unofficially—" She stopped. The truth sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. *Unofficially, I signed the order that destroyed your family, and I have not slept a whole night since you showed me the ledger.* She wasn't ready to say that. Perhaps she would never be ready. "Unofficially, I don't know what this is yet. But I know the crown burns children in Founder's Square now, and I know I lit the first torch, and I know that if there's a court under the mountain that buys souls, then someone in Vell has been selling to it for longer than I've been alive, and I want to know who gave the orders. That's all. For now."

Felix studied her for a long moment. Then: "That's not all. But it's a start."

They rode on.

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow beneath a fallen oak whose roots had torn up a wall of black earth taller than a man. Odile built the fire—she was startlingly good at it, quick and economical, as if fire-building were one of the few skills that survived the loss of everything else—and Felix unwrapped the vial and held it up to the flames.

The memory inside woke. Light moved in the glass like water finding its level, and shapes swam there: a road, white with frost, winding between two standing stones; a cairn shaped like a sleeping man; a gate of living root grown into an arch, beyond which the dark went down instead of up.

"Again?" Odile asked.

"Again," Felix said. "Watch the left side. Where the path forks past the cairn."

They watched together, three heads bent over a dead shepherd's last journey, and Ilsa felt the strangeness of it settle over her like snow. Eleven years she had hunted this exact scene: gifted folk huddled over stolen remembrance, trading in the crown's forbidden currency. She had broken up memory-rings in cellar chapels. She had arrested a hedge-healer, a woman of sixty, for selling bottled comfort to grieving mothers, and Ilsa had stood in the square and watched the pyre take her because the law was the law and the law said magic was the wound in the world.

Now here she sat, warming her hands at a fire she hadn't built, memorizing a smuggler's map out of a thief's glass, and the strangest thing—the thing that made her throat tight—was that it didn't feel like betrayal.

It felt like coming off duty.

"Here." Felix tilted the vial toward her. "You've got the best eye for terrain. Tell me if this fork matches anything on the warden charts."

Ilsa took the vial carefully, as one takes something holy or explosive. Up close, the memory hummed faintly against her palm, a sensation like a word remembered on the tip of the tongue. Inside, the frost-white road split around the cairn—one branch climbing toward a saddle of rock, the other diving down into shadow between the roots of something enormous.

"There's a chart," she said slowly. "Pre-Interdiction. In the archive under the Hall of Judgement. The old survey maps, from before the mountain roads were sealed. They were supposed to be burned with everything else." She frowned. "There was a fork marked near a boundary cairn on the north survey. The cartographer wrote a note in the margin. I remember it because it was odd—he wrote *the mountain eats the road here*."

"Eats it," Odile repeated.

"His words. And below it, in different ink, someone else had written: *do not survey past the third marker*. No signature. No reason given." Ilsa handed the vial back. "We burned the maps the following year. All but one set, kept under seal. I filed the order myself."

Felix looked at her. "You file a lot of orders."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "I do."

The fire cracked. Above the black wall of torn earth, stars came out in their cold multitudes, and somewhere far off a wolf spoke once and fell silent.

"We should sleep in watches," Ilsa said, standing. "Wardens patrol the high passes at night this time of year. Solstice patrols. If they have lanterns lit, you'll see them a mile off against the snow. If you see lanterns, you wake me. Not each other. Me." She met Felix's eyes. "Whatever happens on this road, no one speaks to a warden but me. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Felix said.

Odile nodded, then hesitated. "And if they won't listen to you, Captain? If your authority has already traveled faster than we have?"

Ilsa looked north, toward the pass, where the mountains stood against the stars like the shoulders of something waiting.

"Then we'll find out how much of me is still a captain," she said, "and how much is just a woman on a stolen horse."

* * *

The wardens found them at midday on the second day, and there were nine of them.

Ilsa heard the horses before she saw them—the particular disciplined rhythm of a patrol moving at canter through broken country—and she was off her own horse and walking to meet them before Felix had finished swearing. Behind her she heard Odile say something low and urgent, and Felix hiss back, *"No—let her,"* and then the trees opened onto a shelf of bare granite and there they were: nine riders in the grey-and-iron of the Warden Corps, spread across the trail like a hand closing.

Their captain spurred forward. Ilsa knew him. Dain Correc, ten years her junior, promoted on the strength of a winter spent burning hedge-witches along the coast. He had a face like a closed ledger and he wore his new authority the way thin men wear borrowed coats.

"Captain Verrow." He reined in six feet away. His eyes went past her—to Felix, to Odile, to the third horse with its travel-stained saddle—and came back with a different weight in them. "You're a long way from your jurisdiction."

"So are you, Correc. The solstice patrols run the western passes. This is the north road."

"Orders changed." He smiled without warmth. "Yours must have, too. We had word from the Hall two nights ago. A warrant." He drew a folded paper from his breastplate, and Ilsa's stomach went cold and quiet, the way it always did before violence. "Desertion of post. Theft of crown property. And—" his eyes flicked behind her again, and this time they stayed, hungry, on Felix and Odile—"harboring unregistered gifted persons. Two of them, by the description. One a memory-worker. One a noblewoman who sold her past, which makes her property of the crown by statute, if you can believe the legal phrasing. I couldn't, at first."

The wind moved through the pines. On the granite shelf, the silence had the specific texture of a held breath.

"Read it to me," Ilsa said.

"I don't need to read it to you. You know the form."

"*Read it to me, Captain.*" She put the full weight of her rank into it, eleven years of it, the voice that had emptied taverns and stopped riots. "Because if that warrant bears the seal of the Hall of Judgement, then it was issued by Provost Ellery Vance, and Provost Vance does not issue warrants for desertion without a countersignature from the Lord Marshal, and the Lord Marshal is in the eastern provinces until the thaw. So either that paper is genuine, in which case you'll read it aloud like a man following procedure—or it's a piece of paper Correc wrote himself to cover an unauthorized arrest, in which case you'll want to think very carefully about the next thirty seconds."

She saw it land. She saw doubt move behind his eyes like a fish under ice—not doubt of her, exactly, but doubt of himself, of the neat story he'd ridden out with. Nine men watched their captain weigh a piece of paper against a legend, and legends are heavy things.

"It's genuine," Correc said, but slower now.

"Then read it."

He unfolded it. His eyes moved down the page, and Ilsa watched him read, and she watched the reading change him. She had seen that happen before—in courtrooms, in interrogation rooms, the moment a man discovers the gap between what he was told a document says and what it actually says. Provost Vance was many things, but careless with paper was not among them, and Ilsa had spent three sleepless hours in Vell before leaving reconstructing, line by line, exactly what a warrant for her own arrest would look like and what traps it might hold.

"What does it say, Captain?" one of the wardens asked.

Correc didn't answer. His jaw worked. At last he lowered the paper and looked at Ilsa with something that was almost respect and entirely hatred.

"It says," he said, "that Captain Verrow is to be detained and returned to Vell for questioning." A pause. "It says nothing about the other two."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "It wouldn't. Read the last clause."

He read it silently. His face went through several weathers. "*...detention to be conducted with discretion,*" he recited flatly, "*as the subject may be operating under orders not recorded in the public register.*"

"Under orders," the youngest warden breathed.

"Under orders," Ilsa confirmed, and let the silence do its work. It was true, in the way that mattered: she *was* operating under orders. Her own. The only authority left to her. "Provost Vance suspects I'm hunting a larger quarry than the street-level workers we've been burning. He's right. I am. And if you take me back to Vell in chains, Correc—if you drag a captain of the witch-hunters through the gates in irons on a warrant that half-expects her to be guilty—you will be the man who ended the only investigation that might reach the top of what's happening to this city. Is that the story you want told about you? In the taverns? In the Hall?"

Correc sat very still. Snow began to fall, fine and dry, out of a sky the color of unpolished pewter.

"Your companions," he said finally. "The gifted ones. They come back with you. That part isn't negotiable."

"They come with me," Ilsa said, "because they're my informants. Registered under my authority, under the same discretionary clause. Turn to the second page, Captain. Go on."

There was no second page. There never is. But Correc looked, because a man who has begun to doubt his paper will doubt it all the way down, and in the three seconds his eyes dropped, Ilsa stepped forward into the space between the horses and said, quietly, for him alone:

"Dain. Ten years ago you were a corporal who cried when we burned the healer at Marrow Cross. I held your shoulder while you were sick afterward. I don't know what happened to that man, and I'm not asking you to find him today. I'm asking you to ride back to Vell and tell Vance you found nothing on the north road. Nothing. Can you do that?"

He looked at her for a long time. Snow gathered on his shoulders, on the iron of his breastplate, on the folded warrant in his fist.

"Three days," he said at last. "Then I report what I report."

"Three days," Ilsa said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." He wheeled his horse. "I'm not doing it for you." But as the patrol turned and clattered back down the trail, he glanced once—only once—at Felix Galloway, and there was something in the glance that Ilsa recognized, because she had worn it herself not a month ago: the look of a hunter who has begun to suspect that the people he hunts are the only ones telling the truth.

* * *

They rode until full dark, putting distance behind them, and made a cold camp in the lee of the first true peak, where the trees gave out and the world became stone and starlight. No fire. They ate hard bread and salt pork and drank snowmelt, and nobody spoke for a long while.

It was Odile who broke the silence, in her drifting, unhurried way, as though continuing a conversation none of them remembered starting.

"You lied to him beautifully," she said. "The countersignature. The second page. How did you know he wouldn't simply arrest you anyway and sort out the paperwork later?"

"Because he wanted to be lied to," Ilsa said. "Men like Correc need permission to do the right thing. Give them a document—even a false one—and they'll cling to it like a rail in deep water." She tore her bread. "I've used it before. It doesn't get easier to be proud of."

"And the crying corporal," Felix said. "Was that true?"

"Every word." Ilsa looked up at the pass, a black notch in the star-field where the road went over and down into country no warden chart admitted to. "That's the worst part. Everything I told him was true except the parts I invented, and he couldn't tell which were which, and neither, anymore, can I." She laughed, short and without humor. "Eleven years I built myself into a tool of the law. Clean edges. Now I lie as easily as breathing and mean most of what I say, and I'm leading a memory-thief and a woman with no past to a door under a mountain, and do you know what frightens me? It's not the wardens. It's not the fae. It's that this—" she gestured at the two of them, at the cold camp, at the whole absurd expedition— "is the first time in eleven years I've felt like I'm doing my actual job. Finding the truth. Protecting people from the thing that's eating them." She shook her head. "I just never expected the thing eating them to be wearing my own seal."

Felix was quiet for a moment. When they spoke, their voice had lost its market-street polish.

"When we get Wren back," they said, "whatever's left of this—of us—we should burn the Hall of Judgement's archives. Every ledger. Every sale. Let everyone they bought and sold just... stop being property."

"You'd free the souls?" Odile asked.

"I'd free the *names*. The rest takes longer." Felix lay back against their pack and looked at the stars. "One thing at a time. Tomorrow we cross the pass. Tomorrow we find the third marker."

Ilsa took the first watch, because she was still, in whatever remained of the old grammar, the captain. She sat with her sword across her knees and listened to her companions breathe, and she thought about the young corporal who had cried at Marrow Cross, and how she had told him then that the sickness passed, that duty was a muscle and it strengthened with use, and how she had believed it, and how she had built a whole life on believing it.

Above the pass, clouds were gathering out of the clear night, thickening around the summit like smoke around a wick, though no wind stirred anywhere else in the sky. As Ilsa watched, the notch of the pass seemed to darken further—not with cloud, she realized, but with shape: a suggestion of arches, of root-work, of a gate standing open where no gate should be, visible for one heartbeat and gone.

On the granite beside her, faint as breath on glass, frost was forming in patterns. Not crystals.

Letters.

Ilsa leaned close, her heart going slow and hard, and read the single line the mountain had written in the language of the pre-Interdiction surveys, the language of the sealed maps she had once filed away:

*THE THIRD MARKER REMEMBERS YOU, CAPTAIN.*

Behind her, in her sleep, Odile Marchand began, very softly, to sing a song she could not possibly have known—the same song, though Ilsa had no way of knowing it, that a hollow girl beneath the mountain hummed while she worked.
