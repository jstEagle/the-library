## 7. Toll of Small Things

The mountain had been growing in the window of the world for three days before they reached it.

Felix had watched it happen the way one watches a storm come up a valley—slowly enough to pretend it wasn't happening, then all at once too large to deny. From the lowlands it had been a gray suggestion on the horizon, a bruise against the sky. By the second day it had shoulders. By the third it filled half the heaven, black-bellied with snow, its peak lost in cloud that did not move the way clouds were meant to move. It hung over them now like a held breath, and the road beneath their feet had changed from packed earth to something older: flagstones fitted without mortar, each one worn smooth in the center as though a river of feet had poured through here once, long ago, and left only this polished stone behind.

No feet came this way now. The villages they'd passed through had shuttered windows and dogs that wouldn't bark. Ilsa said it was because word traveled faster than riders in country like this. Odile said nothing at all, but she rode with her hand resting on the satchel where her recovered memory-fragment slept in its glass, and her silence had the particular weight of someone listening for something underground.

Felix walked beside their horse because walking kept the fear from pooling in their boots. That was a trick learned young: keep moving, keep your hands busy, keep counting things. Fence posts. Crows. Heartbeats. They had been counting since dawn, and the number that kept returning, unbidden, was fifteen years—the age of Wren's absence, the age of the debt, the number of winters their sister's soul had been down there, in the dark, being eaten by degrees.

*Before the turning of the year,* the ledger had said. *When the account comes due.*

Nine days remained.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said, reining her mare alongside. "Where you count."

"I don't do a thing."

"Your lips move. Very slightly. Like a mouse chewing on something." She smiled the smile she'd taught herself in place of all the smiles she couldn't remember—a courtly construction, elegant and load-bearing. "What are you counting?"

"Fence posts."

"There haven't been fence posts for an hour."

"Then I'm counting how wrong you are."

Odile laughed, brief and bright, and Felix felt the strange warmth of it settle somewhere under the ribs. Two kinds of emptiness, they had thought, when they first met her—the noblewoman who had sold every yesterday she owned to pay her family's debts, and the charm-seller who had never belonged anywhere enough to be emptied of anything. But emptiness recognized emptiness the way two notes an octave apart recognize each other. There was a harmony in it that Felix hadn't expected and didn't entirely trust.

Ilsa Verrow brought up the rear, as she had for two days, riding with the rigid straightness of a woman who had taken off her captain's insignia and could still feel the ghost of its weight on her shoulder. She wore plain wool now, her hunter's blade wrapped in oilcloth and lashed to the saddle rather than carried at her hip. A disguise so thin it was nearly a confession. Everyone in these hills knew what a witch-hunter looked like even stripped of her colors; it lived in the way she sat a horse, in the way her eyes went to rooftops before faces.

"The gate's close," she called forward. "Half a mile. Maybe less."

Felix stopped counting fence posts and started counting heartbeats.

* *

They heard the threshold before they saw it.

It was not a sound exactly—or it was many sounds folded into one, the way a chord is many notes. A low resonance came up through the flagstones and into the bones of their feet, into their teeth, into the small hollows behind the eyes, and it sang a note that was almost familiar, almost the hum of a struck wineglass or a wet finger circling a bowl's rim, except that no instrument made it and no throat could hold it. The horses refused it. All three animals planted themselves and would not go forward, ears flat, nostrils wide, and finally there was nothing to do but tie them to a lightning-split pine and continue on foot.

Around the last shoulder of the mountainside, the road ended.

Not faded. Not crumbled. *Ended*—abruptly, cleanly, at a wall of living rock that rose sheer out of the earth like the prow of a buried ship. And in that wall stood the gate.

Felix had expected doors. Iron or oak or stone, some hinge and some bar, something that could be forced or picked or bribed. But there were no doors. There was an archway cut into the mountain's flank, tall as a cathedral's mouth, and within the archway there was simply *darkness*—not shadow, not the ordinary dark of a cave mouth, but a darkness with texture, with depth, a darkness that seemed less like an absence of light than a presence of everything else. Standing before it, Felix felt the way they felt holding a bottled memory up to the sun: aware of something vast compressed behind a very thin wall.

The archway was carved. As their eyes adjusted, figures emerged from the stone—thousands of them, generations of them, spiraling up both pillars and across the lintel. People giving things away. That was the whole story told in granite: a figure offering up a lock of hair; a figure pressing a ring into another hand; a figure with its chest opened like a locket, something small and shining lifted out. Higher up the carvings grew stranger—figures surrendering shadows, names, reflections, whole days—and higher still, near the crown of the arch, the figures had no faces at all, as if the mountain had run out of patience with detail.

"It's true, then," Ilsa said quietly. She had gone pale beneath her wind-chapped tan. "I always thought the old roads were stories we told to frighten recruits. Pay the toll and pass beneath." Her jaw worked. "There's a version in the hunters' primer. We were taught it was allegory."

"What does the primer say the toll is?" Felix asked.

"Something you'll miss." Ilsa's laugh had no humor in it. "Which tells you nothing until it takes it."

Odile had dismounted and was standing closest, closer than any of them, her face tilted up toward the carved figures with an expression Felix had never seen on her—not the constructed serenity, not the sharp wit, but something naked and yearning. "My family came through here once," she said. "Generations back. It's in the fragments I've managed to buy back—scraps. My great-great-grandmother's diary mentions *the singing stone*. She paid it something and could never remember what." Her voice dropped. "She spent forty years trying to find out what she'd lost, and died not knowing. She wrote that the not-knowing was worse than any grief, because grief at least has a shape."

"Then let's know what we're losing," Felix said, "before it's taken from us. Is that possible? Can you choose?"

As if in answer, the mountain spoke.

* *

It did not speak in words. Or—it spoke in words the way the sea speaks in sentences, meaning arriving whole inside the skull without bothering with the ear. Felix staggered. Beside them, Odile made a small sound; Ilsa's hand went to the empty place at her hip where her sword should have been.

*WHAT WILL YOU GIVE,* said the mountain, in a voice like the deep note of a bell felt through bedrock, like the groan of a glacier calving, like a mother's voice heard from inside the womb—old, vast, and terrible in its patience.

And then, gently, horribly, it began to *show* them.

Felix felt their own memories stir like sediment in disturbed water. Not stolen—nothing was taken yet—but *offered*, laid out, the way a merchant lays out wares for a customer who has not yet decided. Here: the smell of rain on hot cobblestones in Vell's tanners' quarter, the summer they were nine. Here: the taste of burnt honey-cakes, the first ones they ever sold, the buyer's surprised delight. Here: Wren's laugh, gap-toothed at six, at ten, at thirteen, the last laugh before—

Felix wrenched their mind sideways, gasping. "Stop," they said aloud, to the air, to the stone. "Stop showing me. Let me choose."

The pressure receded, courteous as a withdrawn hand. The darkness in the archway seemed to consider them.

*CHOOSE THEN, LITTLE THIEF,* said the mountain, and Felix had the appalling sense of being known—of every bottled charm in their coat, every stolen evening and borrowed joy, inventoried in a glance. *YOU OF ALL WHO COME HERE UNDERSTAND THAT A MEMORY IS NOT AIR. IT HAS WEIGHT. IT HAS PRICE. THE ROAD BENEATH IS OLD AND HUNGRY AND IT KEEPS ITS OWN LEDGER. EACH WHO ENTERS PAYS ONE MEMORY. NOT THE CHEAPEST. NOT WHAT YOU WOULD GLADLY SPARE. THE MOUNTAIN KNOWS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN COINS AND TREASURE.*

"And if we refuse?" Ilsa asked. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles were white.

*THEN THE ROAD REFUSES YOU, AND YOU MAY WALK BACK DOWN AND COUNT YOUR DAYS UNTIL THEY RUN OUT.* A pause, vast and unhurried. *THE GIRL'S SOUL IS ALREADY HALF EATEN. YOU HAVE NINE DAYS. I DO NOT ADVISE REFUSAL.*

"How do you—" Felix started, and stopped. Of course it knew. The ledger had passed through hands that passed through hands; the mentor's effects had been bought and sold and read aloud in rooms. Perhaps the mountain heard everything spoken on its slopes. Perhaps it had heard Felix whispering Wren's name into their collar every night since Founder's Square.

*I HEAR MY MOUNTAIN,* the voice said, which was not quite an answer, which was entirely an answer. *NOW. CHOOSE.*

* *

Ilsa went first. She insisted on it—captain to the last, unwilling to ask of others what she would not pay herself. She walked to the threshold with her spine straight and her chin level, the posture of a woman mounting a scaffold, and stood a long moment in the archway with her eyes closed.

Felix watched her face while the mountain took its price.

He watched the moment it happened. It was not dramatic. There was no flash, no sound, no tearing. Ilsa simply went still—stiller than stillness—and when she opened her eyes again there was a new look in them, a flinch, the look of a person who has reached for a stair that isn't there. She swayed. Odile caught her elbow.

"What did it take?" Felix asked.

"I don't—" Ilsa pressed her palm flat against her own sternum, as if checking that the rest of her was still inside. "There's a hole. Right here. I can feel the edges of it." Her voice frayed. "I know I've lost something. I know it mattered. I know it was—" She stopped, and Felix saw her try three times to reach the memory and three times find the door bricked over. "That's the cruelty of it, isn't it. I can feel the shape of the missing thing. Like a tooth. Like an amputation. I just can't—" Her jaw set, hunter-hard. "It doesn't matter. I'm through. Come on."

But she looked back at the daylight, at the valley, at the world, with an expression Felix couldn't name, and stepped through the dark, and was swallowed, and did not scream, and did not return.

Odile went next.

She approached the threshold differently—not like a scaffold but like an altar. She had prepared, Felix realized. Somewhere in the last two nights she had decided what she would pay, and there was a terrible calm in her as she stood before the arch.

"I have so little to choose from," she said, conversationally, to Felix, to the mountain, to no one. "Do you understand? Everything before four years ago is already gone—sold, sealed, stacked in some vault like yours, like mine, waiting." She laughed softly. "So the joke is that the mountain must take from the little I've rebuilt. Four years of yesterdays. That's my whole estate." Her chin lifted. "Take it, then. Take whatever you like. I'll grieve it later, if I can find it."

*THAT,* said the mountain, with something that in a smaller voice might have been pity, *IS THE FIRST HONEST ANSWER I HAVE HEARD IN A CENTURY.*

The dark folded around her like water around a dropped stone, and she was gone.

* *

Felix stood alone before the gate.

The wind came down the mountainside carrying snow-smell and pine and the faint charred ghost of Vell's pyres, and Felix wrapped their arms around themselves and made the mistake of trying to inventory what they had. It was like trying to count coins while drowning. Every memory rose at once, clamoring: the night market, the lanterns, the first charm ever sold. Their mentor's smoke-and-paper study. Wren's hand in theirs, small and sticky, crossing Founder's Square when the fountain still ran. Their mother—

Their mother.

The thought arrived and Felix tried to turn from it and could not, because the mountain had turned them toward it, gently, the way you turn a child's face toward something they need to see.

Their mother had died when Felix was eleven—fever, the bad winter, the year the wardens first came to Vell and everyone learned what the word *gifted* would cost. Fifteen years, and the memory of her should have worn soft as an old coat. Instead Felix had guarded it. Had *curated* it. In a life built entirely of other people's memories—bottled, sold, traded, worn like borrowed clothes—one memory had remained sacrosanct, never sold, never shared, never even opened alone after midnight except on the worst nights: their mother's face.

The tilt of her head when she laughed at something Father said. The way her brow creased when she counted coins at the kitchen table, lips moving—*the way my lips move*, Felix thought, with a jolt, *the mouse chewing something—I count the way she counted*. The exact brown of her eyes, warm as tea, as fresh-turned soil, as the wood of the table she leaned on. The scar through her left eyebrow from a childhood fall she'd turned into a joke. Her face, saying *Felix* the special way she said it, two syllables stretched into three, into music.

Fifteen years, and Felix had never sold it, though there had been months—bad months—when a single memory of a mother's love would have bought food for a season. They had eaten hunger instead. They had told themselves it was sentiment. Now, standing at the mouth of the hungry dark, Felix understood it had never been sentiment. It had been ballast. It had been the single stone at the bottom of the well that made all the other water make sense. Everything Felix was—the thief, the liar, the charm-seller with the quick smile and the locked heart—had been built *around* that face, the way a pearl forms around a grain of grit. Take it out, and what remained?

*YOU KNOW,* said the mountain, not unkindly. *YOU HAVE ALWAYS KNOWN. THAT IS WHY YOU NEVER SOLD IT. AND THAT IS WHY IT IS THE ONLY COIN I WILL ACCEPT.*

"No," Felix said. Their voice cracked in the middle. "No. Take something else. Take Vell—all of Vell, take the market, take the pyre, take every customer I ever—"

*THE MARKET IS NOT YOURS. THE PYRE IS NOT YOURS. I DO NOT ACCEPT WHAT MEN MADE. ONLY WHAT LOVE MADE.* The vastness paused, and the pause had a gentleness in it that was worse than any threat. *LITTLE THIEF. YOU HAVE PAID IN OTHER PEOPLE'S MEMORIES ALL YOUR LIFE. YOU KNOW BETTER THAN ANYONE WHAT THEY ARE WORTH. PAY YOURSELF FOR ONCE.*

Felix stood at the threshold and wept, briefly, furiously, the way one weeps at a graveside—with anger in it, with refusal, and underneath the refusal, acceptance already blooming like frost.

Because the mountain was right. That was the unbearable thing. Felix had spent fifteen years selling other people's treasures and hoarding their own, and the hoard had one jewel, and the jewel was a dead woman's face, and Wren was down in the dark with her soul being eaten bite by bite, and Wren was *alive*, Wren could be *saved*, Wren could someday stand in the sun again and squint and swear and laugh—and a face was a beautiful thing but it was a thing belonging to the past tense, and Felix had never once in their life chosen the past over the living.

Had they?

Their hands shook. They raised them anyway, cupped, the way they cupped them over a bottle when sealing a charm, and they did the thing they had done ten thousand times for strangers: they gathered the memory, found its edges, its weight, its warmth—and instead of pouring it into glass, they held it out into the dark.

*Her name was Maren.* Wait—no. No, her name was—Felix reached for it and the mountain was already drinking, already folding the memory away like a letter sealed, and Felix clawed at the edges of it—*her name, what was her name, her face, the scar, the eyebrow, the tea-brown, the way she said my name, the way she said—*

Gone.

The silence afterward was enormous. Felix stood in the wind with tears freezing on their cheeks and felt the hole open in them, felt its edges, exactly as Ilsa had described—an amputation, a missing tooth, a stair that wasn't there. They knew they had loved her. They knew the love was still there, vast and directionless, a river whose banks had been pulled out from under it. They knew there had been a face, a specific face, a face they would have known anywhere, a face that was—

Nothing. Warm nothing. A shape of missing.

"That's the cost," Felix whispered to the dark, to no one, to their sister fifteen years lost. "That's the literal cost of love. You get to keep the loving. You just lose the *her*."

The mountain said nothing more. It had taken what it came for. The darkness in the archway parted like a curtain, and beyond it Felix saw the beginning of the road under the roots: stairs descending in long spirals into the warm dark, lit by veins of blue luminescence in the living rock, going down farther than sight could follow, going down the way roots go down, patient and inevitable.

Felix wiped their face with the heel of their hand, and stepped through.

* *

The dark closed behind them like water, and the world below was not what Felix expected—which was, perhaps, the first honest surprise of their entire life. They had imagined cold. Instead there was warmth, a deep mineral warmth rising from the stone, and the sound of water moving somewhere far off, and the blue light threading the walls like luminous marrow. The stairs gave onto galleries, the galleries onto halls, the halls onto vistas that made Odile stop mid-stride with her hand at her throat: caverns vast as inverted cathedrals, hung with gardens of pale root-flowers that glowed softly, feeding on nothing, on memory itself perhaps, on the seep of surrendered years.

They walked for hours. Or minutes. Time moved strangely down here, pooled and eddied. Ilsa led with a hunter's instinct for terrain, marking turns, counting passages. Odile drifted with her fingers trailing along the glowing walls, reading them somehow, her lost past humming in the stone like a tuning fork.

And Felix walked with the hole in their chest and their mother's name dissolving on their tongue, trying to hold the shape of the missing thing, trying at least to keep the *love*, and finding—as they descended deeper, as the air grew sweeter and stranger—that even the shape was softening, wearing away like a chalk mark in rain, until all that remained was a single certainty, small and hard as a seed:

*Someone was loved here once. Someone is loved still. Go and get her back.*

Then the passage opened, all at once, onto light—and Felix stopped breathing.

Below them, spread through a cavern the size of a buried sky, glittered a city. Towers of glass and bone-white stone. Bridges like spun sugar. Ten thousand lamps burning with a light that was not firelight, not lamplight, but something warmer and more personal, something that flickered the way candleflames flicker when a soul is near. Figures moved along the terraces in silks the color of dusk, and about their necks and wrists and crowns they wore jewels that pulsed—softly, rhythmically, like heartbeats. Like captured light. Like souls in glass.

And descending the grand staircase at the city's heart, attended by silent servants, came a girl of perhaps nineteen, dark-haired, slight, carrying a silver ewer with the careful grace of someone who has long ago stopped wondering why she obeys.

Felix knew her walk before they saw her face. Fifteen years, and the body remembers; the body had learned that walk beside them, chasing them through Vell's alleys, matching them step for step.

"Wren," Felix breathed—and the word fell into the vastness below like a coin into a well, and somewhere far beneath them, something ancient heard it, and turned its attention, slow and delighted, upward.
