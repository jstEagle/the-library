## 8. The Court Beneath

The gate did not open so much as admit them, the way a throat admits a swallow.

Felix had expected darkness. What they got instead was light—light without source, rising up from below like warmth through floorboards, gold shot through with green, the color of sunlight drowned in deep water. It poured over the threshold of the mountain's inner door and painted their faces as they stood blinking on the last step of the stair.

"Stay close," Ilsa murmured. Her hand rested on the hilt of her sword out of habit rather than hope; they had all three agreed that steel would mean nothing down there, and she had agreed too easily, which frightened Felix more than any blade could have. "And whatever you see—whatever you're offered—don't say *thank you*. Don't say *please*. Don't promise anything."

"I know how bargains work," Felix said.

"You know how *charms* work," Odile said softly. She stood at the rear, her hands folded, her face composed in that way she had—the composure of someone who had practiced having no expression because she had no memories to make one from. "Bargains are older. Bargains have teeth."

The stair ended. The air changed.

It was warm below the mountain—not the damp cold Felix had braced for but a dry, honeyed warmth, scented with something like beeswax and something like ripe pears and underneath it all, faintly, unbearably, the smell of woodsmoke. Of home fires. Of Vell in autumn, before the hunts began. Someone down here had bottled that smell, Felix thought. Someone had sold it.

They stepped out of the stairwell and onto a road of white stone that curved away into impossible distance, and Felix forgot, for three full heartbeats, every warning they had ever given themselves.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was built inside a cavern so vast the eye failed before it found the walls. Stalactites hung from a ceiling lost in golden haze, each one draped with chains of tiny lights—lanterns? stars? captured fireflies the size of fists?—and between them cascaded waterfalls that fell upward, silver threads climbing back into the dark. The road itself was paved with slabs of moon-white stone veined with silver, and the silver moved. It flowed, slow as honey, from slab to slab, carrying its light along channels that branched like the roots of a tree.

And the city—if it was a city—rose on either side of the road: towers of glass and living wood, spirals of pale coral-like stone, bridges that arched between spires with no visible means of support. Figures moved along those bridges. Tall figures, slender, dressed in colors that hurt to look at directly—colors Felix had no names for, hues that existed only down here where the sun had never reached.

"They're beautiful," Odile whispered.

"That's the trap," Ilsa said. But her voice had gone rough, and her hand had tightened on her sword hilt until the knuckles whitened, and Felix understood that she was not looking at the towers or the lights. She was looking at the faces of the people on the bridges. Some of them were not tall. Some of them were not slender. Some of them walked with the stooped shuffle of old women and the uncertain gait of children, and their eyes—

Their eyes were wrong. Not cruel-wrong. *Empty*-wrong. The look of someone walking in their sleep, smiling at something only they could remember.

"Mortals," Felix breathed. "They took mortals."

"The court trades in what mortals surrender," Odile recited, like a lesson learned by rote—which, Felix realized, it probably was; some fragment of her past must have known this place. "Years. Memories. Names." She paused. "Souls."

A carriage passed them on the road—a thing of gilded bone and smoked glass, drawn by two creatures that might once have been deer and might still be, if deer had too many joints and eyes like chips of obsidian. Through the smoked glass Felix caught a glimpse of the passenger: a fae lord, perhaps, elegant and terrible, wearing a coat that shimmered.

And then the carriage turned, and the light caught the coat properly, and Felix stopped breathing.

The shimmer was not fabric. It was *memories*.

Felix knew the texture the way a blind man knows Braille, the way a thief knows the weight of a purse. Years of them. Decades. Strung together and woven into cloth, first-days and wedding-nights and last-words rendered visible, a whole human life cut into panels and sewn into a garment that fit a creature who had never been born and would never die. And across the back of the coat, stitched in thread that glowed like embers, was a scene Felix recognized with sickening certainty: a market street. Awnings. A fountain. Founder's Square, in Vell, on a market day fifty years gone, worn like a cape by a thing that smiled at nothing.

"Felix." Ilsa's voice, low and urgent. "Keep walking."

"They're *wearing* them." Felix's voice came out cracked. "Ilsa, they're wearing people's lives like—like *clothes*."

"Yes."

"And that one—" Felix pointed, helpless, furious, at a lady crossing a bridge above them, her gown a cascade of what could only be childhoods, dozens of them, layered like petals, each one a different child's first snowfall or first lie or first love, "—that one is wearing *children*."

"Yes," Ilsa said again, and this time her voice broke on the word, and she did not apologize for it.

Odile said nothing at all. She was staring at the gown of childhoods with an expression Felix had never seen on her face before—hunger, naked and terrible, the hunger of a woman looking at a table full of food she could not eat. One of those childhoods might have been hers. Any of them. All of them. Sold, spent, worn by strangers while she walked the surface world hollow as a bell.

"We keep moving," Odile said finally, and her voice was steady, and Felix loved her a little for it, and hated the court a great deal more.

* * *

The road descended in long, gentle curves toward the heart of the cavern, and as they walked, the traffic thickened. Fae nobility passed them in twos and threes, and none of them looked twice at three mortals on foot—why would they? Mortals came down here all the time. Mortals came down here to sell.

That thought settled into Felix's stomach like a swallowed stone.

They passed a fountain—not water but light, liquid gold bubbling up from a basin of black stone—and around its rim sat fae children, if such things could be called children, playing a game with pieces that gleamed. Dice? No. Felix looked closer and wished they hadn't. The game-pieces were small vials, hundreds of them, and the fae children rolled them and laughed and snatched them from one another, and inside each vial swirled a little storm of color that Felix's gift read as easily as a printed page: *a mother's lullaby. A soldier's homecoming. A baker's pride in his bread. A girl's first kiss behind the tannery.*

Trinkets. Playthings. The currency of children.

One of the fae children dropped a vial. It rolled to Felix's feet.

They picked it up before they could think better of it—and the moment their fingers closed around the glass, the memory inside reached for them, hungry, recognizing kin. It flooded up through their palm:

*A kitchen. Rain on the window. A woman humming, flour on her hands, turning to smile at—*

Felix wrenched their hand open and let the vial fall. It shattered on the white stone, and the memory spilled out like wine, a brief bright puddle of a stranger's mother, and the fae children shrieked with laughter, and one of them said, in a voice like bells being strangled, "*Clumsy! You've wasted a good one, mortal. That was sixty years aged.*"

"I'm sorry," Felix heard themselves say, and Ilsa's elbow drove into their ribs hard enough to bruise.

"*Don't apologize,*" she hissed. "Don't speak. Don't owe them anything."

But it was too late; the child was already grinning, already filing the debt away somewhere behind its beautiful face, and Felix walked faster, heart hammering, feeling the ledger of this place opening beside them, a new line inked under their name.

* * *

The palace, when they finally reached it, was not a building. It was a tree.

Or it had been a tree, once—a trunk wide as a cathedral, silver-barked, rising from the center of the cavern all the way up into the golden haze until it vanished. But it had been grown *into*, shaped, hollowed and windowed and crowned with galleries, and from its highest branches hung lanterns that burned with a light Felix recognized and could not name until they did, and wished they hadn't:

Soul-light. The same glow that lived in Wren's vial, if the ledger's description could be trusted. The palace was lit by souls the way Vell was lit by oil.

At the tree's base, doors of amber stood open, and flanking them stood guards in armor of woven shadow, and beyond the doors Felix could see the throne room beginning—a hall of mirrored floors and hanging gardens, and at the far end, on a dais of living root, a figure seated in state.

Queen Marenthe.

Even at this distance, even through the press of petitioners and courtiers, she drew the eye the way a flame draws moths. She was beautiful the way the court was beautiful—too much, too perfect, beauty like a blade laid flat against the skin. Her gown was grey and silver, the color of morning mist, and her hair was the white of birch bark, and about her brow she wore a circlet of dark wood, twisted branches, bare of leaves.

Weeping wood. Felix knew it from the charmsellers' cant: *weeper-wood, grief-timber, the tree that bleeds when cut.* It grew only in one place in the world, the old stories said—in the founder's garden, in the founder's time, a hundred years and more ago. There was no weeping wood left in Vell. There was none left anywhere.

So where had the queen gotten hers?

"Names," a herald cried, in a voice like frost forming. "The court receives all who come. Give your names and your business, and be received."

Ilsa stepped forward, and Felix saw her gather her crown authority around her like a cloak—Captain of the Hunt, voice of the law of Vell—and saw, too, the flicker of something pass through the assembled courtiers when they recognized what she was. Hatred? Amusement? Hunger?

"Ilsa Verrow," she said. "Captain of the Witch-Hunters of Vell. I come under parley, seeking audience with Her Majesty on a matter of trade."

"A huntress," the herald said, tasting the word. "How novel. And your companions?"

"My retainers."

The herald's gaze slid to Felix, and Felix felt it like a fishhook behind the eyes—felt the thing *reading* them, skimming the shelves of their mind the way Felix skimmed other people's. They slammed their mental shutters down, the way their mentor had taught them, thought of nothing, thought of gray stone, thought of—

The herald smiled. "Retainers," it repeated, in a tone that suggested it had seen exactly what Felix was and found it amusing. "Very well. The court receives you. Kneel when you enter the presence. Speak only when spoken to. And mortals—" the smile widened, showing teeth that were slightly, subtly, too many, "—mind your hands. Things picked up in this court are rarely returned."

They filed through the amber doors into a coolness that smelled of sap and night-blooming flowers, and the mirrored floor showed Felix their own reflection, multiplied to infinity, and in every reflection their face looked paler, thinner, more afraid.

* * *

The throne room was a garden grown indoors, or a forest trained to behave like architecture. Ferns the size of oaks. Flowers that turned, slowly, to follow the courtiers as they passed, the way sunflowers follow the sun. And everywhere, everywhere, the light of souls—hanging in lanterns, threaded through the branches, pooled in basins like luminous water, so that the whole hall glowed with the harvested brightness of ten thousand surrendered lives.

Felix kept their eyes down. Kept counting steps. Kept breathing.

And then, near the foot of the dais, among a cluster of serving girls refilling the soul-lanterns' oil—or whatever fed them—Felix saw her.

She was small. Fifteen years old now, though the last time Felix had seen her she'd been six, waving from a doorway, shouting *come find me, Lix, come find me!* She wore the court's livery—grey and silver, mist-colored—and her hair had been braided in a style Felix didn't recognize, with beads of amber worked into it, and she moved with a strange, floating grace, like someone half-asleep.

But when she turned to pass a lantern to another servant, the light caught her face full-on.

Wren's eyes. Their mother's eyes. The same warm brown, the same slight downward tilt at the outer corners that had made their mother look perpetually amused, the same eyes that had watched Felix over the rim of a soup bowl, that had cried when their father died, that had—

The tray slipped in the serving girl's hands. Just a fraction. Just enough that one lantern tilted, and its soul-light sloshed against the glass, and the girl frowned at it, puzzled, the way you frown at a word that's almost on the tip of your tongue.

Then she righted the tray, and smoothed her face into blankness, and carried on.

She hadn't seen him. Felix wasn't sure she could see anything anymore, really—wasn't sure there was anyone behind those eyes to do the seeing. Fifteen years of service to this court. Fifteen years of her soul siphoned away vial by vial, year by year, and what remained was a body that hummed and poured and curtsied, a lamp with the flame nearly guttered.

Something in Felix's chest tore loose and started bleeding.

"Lix."

Odile's hand on their arm. When had she learned that name? Had Felix told her, on the road, in some unguarded hour? It didn't matter. Her grip was iron, and her whisper was barely breath: "Not here. Not now. *Not yet.*"

"She's right there." Felix's own voice sounded foreign to them, scraped raw. "Odile, she's *right there*, I could—"

"You could get us all killed and her soul spent in punishment, yes." Odile's eyes, when Felix met them, were fierce. "You told me why we came. We came to take her *whole*. Patience isn't surrender, Felix. Patience is the shape love takes underground."

The words landed somewhere deep, and held Felix upright when nothing else could have.

Ahead, the queen stirred upon her throne of roots. Her gaze swept the hall—lazy, radiant, absolute—and snagged, for just a moment, on the three mortals standing among the petitioners. On Felix.

She smiled.

It was not a cruel smile. That was the worst of it. It was warm. It was the smile of someone greeting a guest they had been expecting for a very long time—a smile with recognition in it, and welcome, and beneath both, so faint that Felix might have imagined it, something that looked almost like *pity*.

"Come forward," Queen Marenthe said, and her voice filled the hall the way light fills a room, gentle and total. "The thief of Vell. I have been waiting for you since before you were born."

Beside Felix, Ilsa's hand found theirs and gripped hard. Ahead, among the servants, the girl with Wren's eyes went on pouring light, humming something under her breath—something tuneless, half-remembered, that Felix's traitor heart identified anyway, in one lurching instant, as the first four notes of a song their mother used to sing.

The queen beckoned.
