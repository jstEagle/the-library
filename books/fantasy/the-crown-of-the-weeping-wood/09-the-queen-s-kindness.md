## 9. The Queen's Kindness

The audience chamber had no ceiling that Felix could find.

Above the chandeliers—each one a cage of silver holding a slow-turning knot of light that might once have been someone's summer—the dark simply went on, soft as velvet and deep as well water. It was not the darkness of a cave. Caves ended. This one seemed to lean in and listen.

Felix stood on a floor of black stone veined with gold so fine it looked like writing, like a letter written by someone with too much time and too much grief, addressed to no one who would ever read it. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks: tall figures in colors Felix had no names for, their faces beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful, precise and patterned and utterly without warmth. Around their throats, their wrists, threaded through their hair, they wore small glinting things. Years, Odile had whispered on the road down. They wear mortals' surrendered years like jewelry. Some of those years, Felix thought, looking closer at a courtier whose collar glittered like frost-bitten wheat, might have belonged to people from Vell. People Felix had sold to, or stolen from, or passed in the market without ever knowing what they'd lost.

Do not look at their jewels. Do not give them your name freely. Do not eat. Do not thank anyone. Odile's instructions, recited like scripture, and now Felix understood why her voice had gone flat saying them. Rules were all that kept a person shaped like a person down here.

And then the doors opened—or rather, the wall ahead of them simply agreed to be a doorway—and Queen Marenthe came into her court.

Felix had prepared for a monster. They had spent three days underground rehearsing the shape of her in their head: vast, terrible, crowned in bone or fire, something out of the cautionary tales the wardens told to keep children from the mountain passes. What came instead was a woman of middle years, gray-streaked and plain-faced, dressed in a gown the color of wet slate. She walked without ceremony, hands folded, and the entire glittering court bent toward her like grass under wind. She looked tired in a way that was almost human, almost familiar—the tiredness of someone who has carried something heavy for a very long time and has forgotten how to set it down.

But her crown—

Felix's breath caught. The crown was alive. A circlet of slender branches, silver-gray and weeping, trailing leaves that dripped light instead of water, drop by luminous drop onto the queen's shoulders, where the drops sank in and vanished. A wood Felix knew only from the city's name for it—the weeping wood that had grown over Vell's founder's grave before the first stones were laid—and here it was, worn like a wound, like a memory the queen could not take off.

*Steady,* Felix told themselves. *You have lied to wardens. You have lied to buyers weeping over bottled sons. You can lie to a queen.*

Queen Marenthe stopped ten paces away and looked at Felix for a long, unhurried moment. Her eyes were brown. Ordinary brown. That was somehow the worst thing yet.

"You are the Galloway child," she said. Her voice was warm. Hearth-warm. Mother-warm. "Wren's brother."

"I didn't say my name," Felix said, because Odile had said never to confirm anything for free, and because the sound of Wren's name in this stranger's mouth made something in Felix's chest twist like a wet cloth being wrung.

"No," the queen agreed pleasantly. "You didn't." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, which was worse than if it hadn't. "Come and walk with me. Courts are for courtiers. I find that honest conversations happen better in gardens."

She turned, and the court parted, and Felix—who had planned to be clever, guarded, a locked box walking—found their feet moving after her, because what else was there to do? Behind them they felt Ilsa's hand hover near the hilt she wasn't permitted to draw, and Odile's silence, which was its own kind of blade. But the queen had said *walk with me*, singular, and the fae nobility had closed ranks behind Felix like water closing over a dropped stone, cutting them off from their allies entirely.

*Don't be afraid,* Felix told themselves, and failed, and walked anyway.

* * *

The garden grew in a cavern so large its far walls were rumor. Pale trees stood in rows along streams that ran with faint silver light, and the flowers were all the colors of things Felix had seen in memories but never in life—a blue like the moment of waking safe, a red like the last look between lovers parting at a door. The air smelled of rain on hot stone. Somewhere, water fell from a height with a sound like slow applause.

"Your sister tends the roses," the queen said conversationally, as though they were two neighbors leaning over a fence. "There, past the third stream. She is very good with them. She doesn't remember learning how."

Felix's whole body went rigid. "Where is she?"

"Patience. We will see her. That is rather the point of today." Marenthe paused beside a tree whose bark had split and healed and split again, scarred into patterns almost like letters. She touched it gently, the way one touches an old dog. "I want you to understand something, Felix Galloway, before we speak of bargains. Everyone who comes down here thinks they know what I am. The crown above calls me a devourer. The gifted call me a thief. Your witch-hunter friend—" Felix started; the queen waved a hand, unbothered—"yes, I know who travels with you, I know everything that walks through my gates, it is the nature of gates—I'm sure she calls me worse. But consider: every soul in my keeping was *sold*. Not taken. Sold. By parents. By creditors. By kings and captains and kind mentors with debts of their own. I have never once had to break into a human life. Your people line up at my door with their most precious things held out in cupped hands, begging me to take them."

"The ledger said fifteen years," Felix said. Their voice came out flat. "A child's soul. My sister. She was seven."

"She was," the queen said softly. "She sang when she was frightened. Did you know that? She has not sung in some time now." She turned those ordinary brown eyes on Felix, full of a kindness that made Felix's skin crawl precisely because it seemed genuine. "I did not buy her to hurt her, child. I bought her because souls are the only currency down here, and I am running out of time faster than any of us would like. But that is a longer story than you have patience for, and I suspect you have already heard pieces of it from the Marchand girl, who hears more than she remembers."

Felix thought of Odile's face in the torchlight of the deep roads, saying *the queen's bargains always hide a second price*, and planted their feet in that certainty like roots. "Say what you want from me."

"Straight to business." Marenthe laughed, low and real. "You are very like her, you know. Wren. She bartered with my seneschal for extra candlelight once, aged nine, and got it, too." Something moved across her face then—quick, buried, gone—that Felix could not read and did not trust themselves to try. "Very well. Here is what I want, and here is what I offer, plainly, so you may weigh them."

They had come, without Felix noticing, to the garden's heart: a still pool ringed in white stone, its surface so perfectly clear that it showed not the cavern above but the sky—Vell's sky, gray and cold, snow beginning. And kneeling at the pool's edge with her hands in the dark water, tending pale roses that bloomed underwater, was a girl.

Seventeen now, where seven had been the last time Felix saw her. Taller. Thinner. Hair the color of river mud instead of the bright chestnut Felix remembered. She moved like sleepwalkers move, graceful and absent, and when she lifted a rose from the water and shook it gently dry, she hummed—three notes of a song Felix knew, a nonsense tune about a fox and a baker, and then the humming trailed off into nothing, unfinished, the way a house unfinished is not a home.

Felix's knees nearly went. They gripped the white rim of the pool and held on.

"Wren," they said.

The girl did not turn. The humming drifted off again into that patient, hollow quiet, and she lowered another rose into the water as though Felix were wind, weather, a thing beneath the notice of the working dead.

"She cannot hear you yet," the queen said, and there was no cruelty in it, which was the cruelest possible way to say it. "Her soul sits in my vault, half-spent. What walks here is what remains attached to it—enough to tend roses, not enough to know your face." She let that land. She was good at letting things land. "At the turning of the year, the remainder finishes ripening, and it becomes mine entirely, woven into the court's foundation, and the girl you are staring at becomes nothing at all. Unless."

"Unless," Felix repeated. The word tasted like iron.

"Unless I release her. Which I am prepared to do." The queen folded her hands, and the weeping crown dripped its slow light down her back. "I ask one service in return. A theft, Felix Galloway—one theft, the kind you are famous for in the night markets you believe we don't watch. There is a man in Vell. Aldous Renwick. He keeps a counting-house near Founder's Square, and he owns a memory I need: a single afternoon, forty years ago, spent in the weeping wood above this mountain, in the company of a woman he has long since convinced himself he dreamed. I cannot take it myself. Souls cross my threshold; memories must be *carried* down willingly, borne by mortal hands, and his would never come willingly. But yours—" she smiled again, warm as banked coals— "yours go anywhere, don't they? Steal it. Bring it to me before the solstice. Do this, and Wren walks out of this mountain whole, free, owing nothing. I will even sweeten the pot, as merchants say." She gestured, and a servant glided forward bearing a small glass vial on a silver tray: inside it, light swirled like dawn caught mid-argument. "One year of her childhood. Her seventh birthday, the day before the sale. Consider it a taste of what returning her soul will restore. Proof of good faith."

Felix stared at the vial. Inside it, faintly, they could see a kitchen. A table with flour on it. Small hands pressing shapes into dough. And a voice—they couldn't hear it, but they knew it anyway—their mother's voice, laughing, telling someone to mind the oven.

Their eyes burned. They made themselves look back up at the queen.

"And the second price?" Felix asked quietly. "My friends say your bargains always carry one."

For just a moment—less than a heartbeat—the warmth in Queen Marenthe's face flickered, the way candlelight flickers when something passes close to it. And beneath it Felix saw, or thought they saw, something enormous and old and unbearably sad, a weight behind her eyes like the mountain itself wearing a woman's face.

"Clever child," she said. "The second price is simply this: Renwick will miss the afternoon. He has kept it forty years precisely because he does not know he has it, and losing it will change him in ways neither of us can chart. Perhaps he becomes crueler. Perhaps kinder. That is true of every theft you have ever committed—you simply never stayed to watch. This time you'll know. That's all. That's the whole of it." She spread her hands, reasonable, wounded, honest-seeming. "I do not lie, Felix Galloway. It is against my nature, quite literally. Everything I offer you is exactly what it appears to be. Whether it is therefore *good*—" her smile turned strange at the edges, "—is a question for humans to answer. You are so much better at it."

The vial sat on its tray between them, glowing, patient. Beyond it, at the pool's edge, Wren pressed another pale rose into the dark water and hummed three notes of a fox-and-baker song, and did not finish it, and did not turn around.

Felix thought: *it's one memory. One afternoon, forty years old, belonging to a moneylender. Against a whole soul. Against her.* The arithmetic was so easy it insulted them, and that easiness was exactly what Odile had warned about, and Felix found—to their horror—that they did not care. Want sat in their chest like a swallowed coal, burning holes through every caution.

"I need until tomorrow to tell my companions," Felix said. Their voice only shook a little. "They'll want—"

"Of course. Take all the time the solstice allows, which is less each hour." The queen inclined her head, gracious as a hostess seeing guests to the door, and turned back toward her garden of pale roses. Then she paused, without looking around, and added—lightly, almost as an afterthought, in the tone of one mentioning weather:

"Oh, and Felix? When you speak with them—your hunter and the lady with no yesterdays—do think carefully about what you share. Lady Odile, after all, sold her past once already. People who sell things once," Queen Marenthe said, resuming her walk among the flowers, "have been known to sell other people's secrets twice."

Behind Felix, in the quiet of the garden, Wren began to hum again.
