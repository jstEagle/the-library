## 10. Bargains and Blind Spots

Odile Marchand had learned, in the year since she sold her yesterdays, to navigate the world by its edges rather than its centers. She did not know her own name until she read it on documents. She did not know her family until servants told her their faces were familiar. She had built herself a life of margins and careful inferences, a woman walking through fog with her hands outstretched—and she had become very good at reading what other people concealed, because she had nothing of her own to get in the way.

So when Queen Marenthe's herald had pressed the folded contract into Felix Galloway's hands—*bring me one memory from the surface world, and the girl goes free*—Odile had watched the paper the way other people watched knives.

Now, three days into their descent through the mountain's outer galleries, with the queen's hospitality humming around them like a struck bell that would not finish ringing, she finally said it aloud.

"You're going to take the bargain."

It was not a question, and Felix flinched as if it had been an accusation. They sat in a guest chamber the court had given them—a chamber too beautiful by half, its walls veined with luminous stone, its table laid with fruits that never rotted and bread that never staled. Everything here was generous the way a snare is generous: soft, inviting, shaped exactly to hold you.

Felix sat hunched over the contract, which they had not signed but had not put down either. In the lamplight—the light came from no lamp at all; it simply was—they looked young. Younger than Odile had first guessed. Their fingers kept moving across the vellum's edge, tracing the seal of interwoven branches, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

"I'm going to *consider* the bargain," Felix said.

"You've considered it for three days. You sleep holding it." She nodded at the satchel beside their cot, where the corner of the folded contract protruded like a splinter from skin. "I don't sleep. I have nothing to dream about, so I watch instead. You talk to it when you think Ilsa is awake. And last night you asked it a question—I heard you. You said, *what memory?*"

The silence that followed had texture. Odile felt it against her skin like velvet dragged the wrong direction.

"The queen said she'd choose," Felix said quietly. "She said any memory I bring her, freely stolen from the surface, freely given—she'd take it as payment."

"And you believed that?"

"I believed she *meant* it. That's worse, isn't it? That's the trap everyone warns about with fae—that they lie. But she doesn't lie. That's the horrible part. She says exactly what she means, and what she means is a door with another door behind it."

Odile rose and crossed to the window—or what served for a window here, an arch of glassy stone looking out onto a cavern where pale trees grew upside down from the ceiling, their roots trailing like hair, weeping slow drops of light. The Weeping Wood, the servants called it, with the reverence of people who had forgotten there was anything else to revere. Even now, even knowing what she knew of this place, some traitorous part of her found it beautiful. That was the second price beginning already, she thought. Beauty was how it started.

"Felix," she said. "Tell me what you know about my family's debt."

Felix looked up. "You told me. Your father borrowed against your past to cover his losses. Sold all of it—every memory—to the court."

"All of it, in one transaction, fifteen years ago. Do you know why a single payment? Why not sell a decade here, a childhood there, spread the cost?" She turned from the window. "Because the court doesn't offer installments to mortals it wants whole. My father went to them desperate, and they gave him terms he couldn't refuse: everything, or nothing. And because he was desperate, and because desperation makes arithmetic out of people, he took everything. And do you know what I discovered afterward, once I'd woken up empty and had years to study the ledger fragments that survived?"

Felix shook their head slowly.

"The sum they paid him. I found it. It was a third of what my memories should have fetched on any open market—even the illegal ones, even the charm-sellers' black trade in Vell's night markets pays better than what they gave him." She let that sit. "They weren't buying my past, Felix. They were acquiring it. There is a difference between purchase and acquisition, and the difference is always a second price—one that never appears on the contract, because the contract is only the visible edge of the thing. The rest is underwater."

The door opened. Captain Ilsa Verrow came in with the particular economy of motion she carried everywhere, shaking frost from the deep roads off her grey cloak—though whether the mountain's cold was real or theatrical, Odile could not say. The witch-hunter's eyes went from Odile's face to Felix's hands still resting on the contract, and something in her jaw tightened.

"Am I interrupting a funeral?" Ilsa asked.

"A negotiation," Odile said.

"Then I'm glad I arrived before anyone agreed to anything." She unbuckled her sword belt and set it on the table—not a surrender, Odile noted, but a gesture, a coin placed down to buy a seat at the conversation. "Because I've spent the day doing what our hosts suggested and touring the lower galleries. And I've seen what happens to the souls they consume."

Felix's head came up sharply. "You saw Wren's?"

"No. Not hers." Ilsa's voice stayed level, but Odile saw the effort of the levelness, the way a hand stays steady on a rope bridge through sheer will. "I saw others. Souls at various stages. There's a gallery where they keep the ones nearly finished—the court calls them the *Ripe*. Glass vessels, each with a light inside, and the lights are..." She stopped. Started again. "When you look at them long enough, they look back. And they're grateful. That's the worst of it. Whatever the consumption does, near the end, it feels like peace. I stood in front of one for—I don't know how long—and part of me wanted what it had."

"That's the queen's kindness reaching for you," Odile said.

"Then it reaches well." Ilsa pulled out a chair and sat, uninvited, the soldier's habit of taking ground and holding it. "Here is what I know. Wren Galloway's soul has been down here fifteen years. It is more than half consumed—we've confirmed that. At the turning of the year, it finishes, and whatever is left of your sister stops being recoverable forever. Not difficult. Not costly. *Forever.*" She looked at Felix directly. "And in exchange for one theft—one job, the kind you do every night in Vell without losing sleep—the queen will hand her back. Now. Whole. Before the solstice."

"*Whole*," Odile repeated softly. "You believe that word?"

"I believe it's the only offer on any table."

"It isn't an offer. It's bait, and the hook is invisible, and you—" Odile caught herself, breathed. She had to be careful here. Anger made her clumsy; anger was a luxury of people who owned their own tempers, and she had sold hers along with everything else, or so it sometimes felt. She started again, slower. "Captain. You've spent your career hunting the gifted. Tell me what you learned about fae bargains in your training. The crown must teach its hunters something."

Ilsa's mouth twisted. "We're taught they cheat. We're taught never to sign, never to accept food, never to speak our true names. A catechism of refusals." Her eyes flicked to the fruit on the table, and Odile saw her register, perhaps for the first time, that all three of them had eaten. "But we're taught nothing about *why*, because the why is uncomfortable. The crown doesn't want hunters who understand the courts. Understanding breeds sympathy."

"And the why is this," Odile said. "They don't cheat. That's the catechism's great error. Every story you grew up on—the trickster fae, the false coin, the words that turn to leaves—those are stories mortals tell to make themselves feel less foolish. But the courts beneath the mountain keep their word the way a jeweler keeps a stone: perfectly, precisely, and always with an eye to what it will be worth later. When they promise you freedom, you receive freedom. Exactly as much as they defined. No more. The second price lives in the definitions."

Felix had gone very still. "The contract says 'a memory from the surface world.'"

"Yes."

"It doesn't say whose."

"No," Odile said. "It doesn't."

The lamplight hummed. Somewhere beyond the walls, faint and sweet, someone was singing—one of the court's servants, a mortal voice gone smooth and strange from years below. Felix stared at the contract as though the vellum might confess.

"They want me to steal a specific memory," Felix said. "That's what she meant, isn't it. She said she'd choose after. She'll send me to steal from someone specific. Someone whose memory serves her purpose on the surface. And whatever that purpose is—" 

"—you will be the instrument of it," Odile finished. "With your gift, which she has now measured. With your access to Vell, which she has now mapped through you. And with your sister's soul as the leash." She let herself say the next part gently, because it needed saying and gentleness was the only way it would be heard. "You wouldn't be paying one memory, Felix. You'd be selling her a key to the city, held by the one thief talented enough to use it. Fifteen years ago she acquired a child's soul. This solstice she stands to acquire the city that soul came from. That is not mercy. That is *acquisition.*"

For a moment—for one bare moment—Odile thought she had reached them. Felix's face opened the way a fist opens, all the cunning falling away, leaving only the frightened person underneath, the person who had walked into a fairy court to save a sister and discovered the exit was priced in everything.

Then Ilsa spoke.

"She's right," the witch-hunter said, and Odile blinked. "About all of it. About the second price, about the key, about the queen's precision. I've read the old case files; I've seen how these bargains end. Odile Marchand is telling you the truth." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and her grey eyes were terrible with honesty. "Take it anyway."

"*Ilsa*—"

"Let me finish." She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; that was what made her frightening. "You want to protect Felix from the trap. I understand. But you're protecting them from the wrong death. Here are the choices as they actually stand. One: Felix refuses the bargain, and we spend the next weeks trying to breach a vault guarded by things that wear centuries as jewelry, and we fail, and at the turning of the year Wren Galloway ends—not dies, *ends*, becomes a warm light in a noblewoman's bracelet, smiling out of someone else's hand forever. Two: Felix steals the queen's memory, pays the hidden price, whatever it is, and Wren walks out of here alive. Maybe the price is cruel. Maybe it costs Vell something. But it costs something *that still has time to fight back.*" She tapped the table, once, like a gavel. "A debt can be contested. An ending cannot."

"That's not strategy," Odile said. "That's triage."

"That's exactly what it is. I'm a hunter, Lady Marchand. Triage is the only honest arithmetic I know." Ilsa turned to Felix, and her voice dropped into something almost private. "I signed the order that sent your family to ruin. You know that now. You know what I owe you. So hear me: if the price of your sister is a piece of your future, pay it. Pay it and hate yourself after, the way I've hated myself, and live with it, the way I've lived. Because the alternative is standing in Founder's Square someday, looking at ash on the wind, knowing you had a door and chose principle instead of your blood."

The room held its breath. Upside-down trees wept their slow light beyond the glass. And Odile watched Felix Galloway stand between them like a rope in a tug-of-war, pulled toward two different kinds of ruin, and understood—with the terrible clarity of a woman who had no memories to bias her—that both of them were right.

That was the cruelty of it. That was the thing no ballad ever sang. Sometimes the trap and the open road lead to the same place, and wisdom is only the luxury of those who can afford to wait.

"I need to walk," Felix said at last, hoarsely. They gathered the contract, folded it away against their chest like a wound being bound. "Don't follow me."

"Felix—" Ilsa began.

"Don't follow me." And they were gone, out the door and into the corridors of living stone, footsteps fading into the mountain's endless soft echo.

* * *

Odile and Ilsa did not speak for a long time.

The witch-hunter sat with her sword across her knees now, cleaning it though it did not need cleaning, the small repetitive work of a woman keeping her hands from shaking. Odile returned to the window and watched the inverted wood drip its light, and thought about the fragment of herself that lived somewhere in this mountain—shelved in a vault, labeled, waiting. Somewhere below them, in glass, was the girl who had danced at her own presentation ball, who had loved her father enough to forgive him, who had hated him enough to run. Odile knew these facts the way she knew her name: from documents. She felt nothing where the memories should be, a socket without a tooth, and yet she had come all this way, and she was beginning to understand why. Not to reclaim. To *witness.* To stand in front of what she had been and confirm it had existed.

"Do you truly mean it?" she asked finally. "What you said to Felix. Take the deal, pay the hidden price, hate yourself after?"

Ilsa set down the whetstone. "Every word."

"Even if the price is Vell itself?"

The captain was quiet for a moment. "Ask me again when I've seen the price," she said. "That's the honest answer. I've burned people on guesses, Lady Marchand. I've condemned families on evidence I didn't examine closely enough because examining it would have slowed the pyres. I won't do it again—not to Felix, not to anyone. But I'll tell you what I *do* know." She stood, buckling the sword back on, and crossed to the window beside Odile. Below, in the cavern of roots and light, a procession of fae nobles moved among the trees, wearing rings that glowed with swallowed years. "Down here, I keep thinking about the executions. All of them. Every name on every order I signed. And for the first time in my life I'm asking whether the crown that commanded them is worth the uniform." Her reflection in the dark glass looked old, older than her years. "Maybe that's the queen's doing too. Maybe doubt is also a kind of bait. I don't know anymore. That's what this place does—it takes your certainties and sells them back to you as questions."

Odile almost smiled. "Welcome to my entire life."

To her surprise, Ilsa laughed—a short, rough sound, startled out of her. Then it died, and the captain's gaze sharpened on something far below, among the trees.

"Lady Marchand," she said slowly. "Who is that?"

Odile followed her eyes. Down in the Weeping Wood, apart from the glittering procession, a figure in servant's grey stood beneath the largest tree, utterly still. Small. Female. The posture of someone listening to music no one else could hear. As they watched, the figure raised one hand and touched the weeping trunk, and the tree's light shivered around her fingers like water around a stone.

Something in Odile's chest contracted—an ache without an origin, grief arriving ahead of its own reason, the body remembering what the mind had sold.

"I don't know," she said. "But she's mortal. Look at how she stands. Fae don't stand like that—like they're waiting to be dismissed."

Below, the girl in grey lifted her face toward their window—toward them, impossibly, across all that distance—and Odile saw, even at that range, even in that strange light, a pair of eyes she had seen before. Eyes that belonged to a portrait in a ledger. Eyes that Felix Galloway had described on the road under the roots, over and over, the way the devout describe saints.

Wren turned away and walked into the dark between the trees, and the weeping light closed behind her like water over a drowned thing.

Odile's hands had gone cold. "Fetch Felix," she said. "Now. Before they do something alone and irreversible. Their sister just looked straight at us—and, Captain, she looked *afraid*."
