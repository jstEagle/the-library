## 11. The Memory Vault

The vault did not want to be found.

Felix understood that the way they understood a locked door, a shuttered window, a lie told smoothly enough to pass for truth. The palace of Queen Marenthe was built on misdirection as surely as it was built on stone—corridors that doubled back on themselves, staircases that descended into rooms higher than the ones they'd left, windows of thick glass showing gardens that could not possibly exist a mile beneath the mountain's roots. The whole court was a memory of a palace, worn smooth by centuries of forgetting, and Felix had spent fifteen years learning how memories worked.

They moved through it now wearing someone else's face.

It had cost them three hours and a stolen memory—a minor one, plucked from a fae footman dozing in an alcove near the servants' stair: his own reflection, so that he would not notice its absence when Felix borrowed it. The face sat over Felix's features like a mask of warm wax, gray-skinned and elegant, with eyes like chips of slate. It was not comfortable. Wearing another creature's selfhood was like wearing boots two sizes wrong; every step reminded you.

*Left at the singing arch,* Odile had whispered before they parted, her hand pressed flat against Felix's chest as though she could hold their heart steady by force. *The vault lies below the throne room, down where the music comes from. And Felix—if the light starts weeping, run.*

*Why would light weep?*

She had looked at Felix with those terrible blank eyes that held nothing and somehow everything. *Because mine did, once. I saw it in a dream I don't remember having.*

Now, past the singing arch—which sang in a voice like glass rubbed with a wet finger, a sound that set Felix's teeth aching—past the guards who wore armor of blackened silver and did not blink, past a corridor where the walls were veined with something luminous and slow, like sap, like blood—now Felix stood before a door that was not a door.

It was a wall. That was all. Pale stone, seamless, unmarked. But the air before it hummed against Felix's skin the way a bottled memory hummed through its glass, and when they closed their eyes they could *feel* what lay beyond: thousands upon thousands of small bright things, packed together like fireflies in a jar, each one flickering with the rhythm of a heartbeat that no longer existed.

Souls. Stored as light in glass.

Felix pressed their palm flat against the false wall and did the thing they had never dared do in Vell, the thing that would get them burned if any warden ever learned of it: they opened their gift fully, let it pour out of them like water from a cracked pitcher, and *listened*.

Memories had textures. Felix had known this since childhood—their mother's laugh had been velvet, rough-side-out; the day Wren was born had tasted like honey left too long in the sun. And the wall before them, when touched with the gift, resolved into a texture so vast it made Felix's knees soften: grief, mostly. Grief layered on grief layered on longing, centuries deep, the accumulated surrender of every mortal who had ever paid the mountain's toll or sold a piece of themselves to survive. The vault was not a treasury. It was a wound the size of a cathedral.

*I'm coming, Wren,* Felix thought, and pushed.

The stone did not open so much as *agree* to be elsewhere—a ripple passing through it like wind over wheat—and Felix stepped through into cold and darkness and light beyond counting.

* * *

Shelves.

That was the first absurdity: after all the dread, all the whispered warnings, the vault of souls was arranged on *shelves*, rank upon rank of them, rising into darkness overhead, receding into darkness ahead, carved from the same pale stone as the palace but furred with a soft gray mold that ate the sound of footsteps. And on every shelf, in rows as neat as a chandler's stockroom, stood glass vials of every size—some no larger than a thimble, some as long as Felix's forearm—and inside each one burned a light.

No two lights were alike. That was the second absurdity, and it undid Felix more than the first. One vial glowed the deep gold of late afternoon through a kitchen window. Another flickered blue-white, restless, like lightning deciding whether to strike. Another pulsed slow and rose-colored, patient as a sleeping child. Each one was a life—or a piece of one—and each one shone with the particular color of the person it had been torn from.

Felix walked the aisle with their breath held high in their chest, reading labels.

The fae wrote labels. That was the third absurdity, and it made Felix want to laugh, or scream, or both: neat little tags of parchment, inked in a flowing hand, cataloguing the merchandise. *A FIRST KISS, FREELY SOLD. THE SMELL OF BREAD AT DAWN. TWENTY YEARS OF MARRIAGE, PARTIAL — HUSBAND RETAINED HIS HALF.* Some of the vials were dim, their contents faded to embers. Others blazed so fiercely they cast shadows. And here and there, in a place of honor at the ends of shelves, stood vials of black glass that gave off no light at all—but when Felix passed them, the hairs on their arms rose, because those vials were not empty. Those vials were *watching*.

Whole souls. Felix understood suddenly, with a lurch, what the black glass meant. Everything else in this room was a fragment—a sold kiss, a surrendered year. But the black vials held people entire, kept the way one keeps a vintage too fine to drink.

Odile's soul was somewhere on these shelves. Felix looked for it without meaning to—searching for a light with nothing inside it, a glow with no color, a flame that remembered no one—and had to stop themselves, hands trembling, because if they started looking for Odile they would start looking for Wren and if they found Wren—

But they had come to find Wren. That was the whole of it. That was the only reason they had crossed half of Vell and most of a mountain and given up their mother's face at the threshold. So Felix stopped walking, closed their eyes, and did the hardest listening of their life.

They tuned their gift the way a musician tunes a string, past the golden kitchen-lights and the blue lightning, past the rose pulse of sleeping children, searching downward, inward, for the smallest sound in the world: a girl humming. A song about a fox who wanted to be the moon. Their own name, spoken in a child's voice, fifteen years old and buried alive.

There.

Not far. Two aisles over, low shelf, near the floor. Faint—so faint Felix's heart stuttered with terror that it was already gone, already drunk down to the dregs—but *there*, flickering like a candle in a doorway, stubborn as a weed through cobblestone.

Felix ran. They did not care anymore about gray-faced disguise or silent feet; they ran down the aisle with the mold swallowing their steps, and dropped to their knees before a shelf near the vault's far wall, and there it was.

A vial of green glass, no longer than a finger.

Inside it, a light the color of new leaves struggled against a darkness that filled the lower half of the vessel like sediment, like ash settling in snow. Half. It was half gone. Fifteen years of a queen's slow sipping, and Wren's soul—her actual soul, her laughing, stubborn, fox-song soul—had been eaten down to its last ember, and the ember was *fading even as Felix watched*, the green dimming by degrees too small to measure and too large to bear.

"Wren." Felix's voice came out cracked, a stranger's voice. They lifted the vial with both hands, cupping it the way they'd once cupped a sparrow fallen from the nest, and the little light stirred toward their warmth like a plant toward sun. "I'm here. Do you hear me? I'm here, and I'm not leaving without you."

The light flared—just barely. Just enough. A pulse, a recognition, a heartbeat answering a heartbeat across fifteen years of theft.

And then Felix saw the label.

They had been braced for it. Braced for *WREN GALLOWAY, SOLD BY CONTRACT*, or worse, *SOLD BY HER BROTHER*—because that was the guilt that had gnawed Felix since the ledger, since the night they'd read their mentor's handwriting and understood that the coin from Wren's soul might have fed them, clothed them, kept them alive through the lean years. They had rehearsed their own damnation on the road under the roots until it fit like an old coat.

But the label said none of that.

The label said, in that same flowing hand:

*MARENTHE — RESERVE. TO BE CONSUMED AT TURNING OF YEAR. NOTE: SUBJECT'S SOUL INSUFFICIENT ALONE. SUPPLEMENT WITH GALLOWAY CHILD (W.) UNTIL WHOLE.*

Felix read it three times. The words refused to arrange themselves into sense, and then, horribly, they arranged themselves all at once.

*Subject's soul insufficient alone.* Not Wren's vial. This was—Felix turned the tag over with a shaking thumb—this was the queen's own reserve, the portion of Marenthe that the court kept against her turning-of-year renewal. And Wren—Wren was not the prize. Wren was the *supplement*. An ingredient. A fortifying measure, listed on the label like cream added to thin milk.

Why would a queen need supplementing? What was wrong with her soul that fifteen years of a child should be required to make it whole?

Felix knelt there in the cold, holding their sister's fading light, and the dread that had carried them this far sharpened, honed itself, turned its edge inward and became a question. Questions. A whole shelf of questions.

They set Wren down gently—gently, though every instinct screamed to run, to smash the glass, to swallow the light themselves if they must—and looked up at the shelf above. Black glass vials stood in a row there, whole souls in dark bottles, and beside them, apart, on a small stand of its own like a relic on an altar, stood another vial. Old. The glass clouded with age. The light inside it was unlike any other in the vault: not gold, not blue, not rose, but white—white shot through with veins of green, shifting, restless, *weeping*, trailing wisps of itself down through the stopper like tears down a cheek.

The label had faded almost to nothing. But the parchment was heavier than the others, older, sealed at the edge with a stamp Felix knew from every history book in Vell, from the statue in Founder's Square, from the coins in their pocket:

A crown above a tree.

The seal of Aurelian Vance, founder of Vell. The man who had led the first settlers into the valley four hundred years ago. The man whose statue's eyes, it was said, followed you across the square. The man whose city had outlawed magic a generation ago, whose crown's witch-hunters—Ilsa's hunters—burned the gifted in his name.

His name was still legible beneath the seal, ink gone brown with centuries: *VANCE, A. — DO NOT CONSUME. QUEEN'S ORDER.*

*Do not consume.* The founder's soul, kept in the dark like a poison, like a secret, forbidden even to a court that drank souls the way Vell drank wine. And weeping. Four hundred years dead, and the light was still weeping.

Felix's mind, trained by fifteen years of reading what people hid inside them, began—unwillingly—to assemble the shape of the truth the way a thief assembles the shape of a lock by feel. A queen whose soul was insufficient alone. A founder's soul marked *do not consume* by the queen's own order. A city founded four hundred years ago by a man who had, according to every history, died peacefully in his bed.

Had he?

Or had he come beneath the mountain, once, and traded something? Had he surrendered something so vast that the mountain had grown fat on it, that a court had risen here wearing his surrender like jewelry? Was Queen Marenthe—was she—

The light in the founder's vial surged against its glass. Not gently, not the way Wren's ember had reached for warmth. Violently. Frantically. The white-and-green light threw itself against the clouded sides again and again, and though no sound crossed the glass, Felix felt it in their teeth, in their sternum, in the gift wide-open behind their ribs: a voice, wordless, screaming a warning four hundred years old.

*Run*, the founder's soul seemed to say. Or perhaps: *Listen*. Or perhaps, in the oldest language of all, the language of light and loss: *I know what she is, and you do not, and God help you, child, you have brought the last piece of her to her door.*

Somewhere far above, muffled by miles of stone and shelf and silence, the singing arch changed its note.

Footsteps. Not the mold-eaten silence of Felix's own passage—real footsteps, deliberate, unhurried, accompanied by the soft ring of blackened silver. Coming down the aisle. Coming toward the shelf where Felix knelt between their sister's dying light and a dead founder's weeping one, holding evidence that would unmake a queen.

Felix's hand closed around Wren's vial. There was nowhere left to hide.

The footsteps stopped at the end of the aisle, and a voice like cool water over stones said, "You are not the first mortal to find this place, little thief. But you may be the first to leave it."
