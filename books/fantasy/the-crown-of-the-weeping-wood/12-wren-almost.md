## 12. Wren, Almost

The servant's corridor smelled of cold stone and something sweeter beneath it—honey, perhaps, or the ghost of honey, the way the whole court smelled of sweetness laid over something older and darker, like flowers planted in a grave.

Felix moved through it the way they had learned to move through every dangerous place in their life: quietly, apologetically, as though they were furniture that had learned to walk. The fae servants glided past without seeing them. That was the mercy of a borrowed livery and a face kept carefully forgettable—a skill Felix had practiced since childhood, though never with stakes like these.

*Third turning past the Hall of Glass,* Odile's note had said, in her precise, elegant hand. *There is a door that is not a door. It opens for grief.*

Felix had not asked what she meant. They were beginning to understand that in this court, riddles were not decoration. They were architecture.

The corridor turned, and turned again, and then there it was: an archway of pale wood set into black stone, so seamlessly fitted that Felix would have walked past it if the wood hadn't been weeping. Thin threads of sap ran down its grain like tears down a face, pooling at the threshold in small amber beads that caught the light of the phosphorescent moss overhead.

A door that is not a door. It opens for grief.

Felix stood before it and let themselves remember—not Wren alive, not Wren laughing, but Wren the last time they'd seen her, fifteen years ago, small and furious in the doorway of their mother's house, shouting that Felix always got to stay, that Felix was the favorite, that it wasn't fair. They had been too angry to say goodbye properly. They had been fifteen years carrying that.

The sap-beads trembled. The door swung inward without a sound.

* * *

The room beyond was small and round and wrong.

It was wrong the way dreams are wrong—the proportions slightly off, the ceiling a little too high, the light coming from no source Felix could name, a soft grey luminescence like the inside of a pearl. There was a bed. There was a window, though they were deep beneath a mountain, and through the window Felix could see a garden that did not exist: a wood of slender pale trees, all of them weeping sap, all of them bending in a wind that touched nothing else in the room.

And in the bed, sitting up, hands folded in her lap like a good child waiting to be called on, was Wren.

She was seventeen. She should have been twenty-two. The court had kept her the way one keeps fruit in ice—preserved, unripened, stopped at the moment of her taking. Her hair was the same dark copper Felix remembered. Her face was rounder than it should have been, the face of a girl who had never grown into it, and her eyes—

Her eyes were open, and they were Wren's eyes exactly, the warm brown with the flecks of green like moss on stone, and they looked directly at Felix with perfect, pleasant blankness.

"Hello," she said. Her voice was soft and unhurried. "Are you my new keeper?"

Felix's throat closed. They had rehearsed this. In the long climb down from the gate, in the hours of hiding among the court's glittering corridors, they had rehearsed it a hundred times: *Wren, it's me, it's Felix, I've come to take you home.* They had imagined her leaping up, imagined her crying, imagined her angry even—angry would have been fine, angry would have been *Wren*—imagined all of it except this: being looked at by their sister as though by a stranger who had been told to be polite.

"I—" Their voice cracked. They tried again. "I'm not your keeper."

"Oh." She considered this with a small tilt of her head, birdlike, patient. "Then are you lost? People get lost here sometimes. The corridors move when the Queen is dreaming."

"The corridors move," Felix repeated faintly.

"When she's dreaming badly, mostly." Wren—this almost-Wren—smiled, and the smile was right too, the crooked one, the one where the left corner rose higher, and it was unbearable, it was like watching someone wear your mother's voice. "But I don't mind. It's peaceful here. I have my work."

"What work?"

"I keep the songs." She said it simply, proudly, the way a child reports a duty. "The ones people give away. When someone sells a memory, the song goes out of it first—it's the part that can't be bottled, so it comes here, and someone has to hold it until it fades. I hold them." She patted the blanket beside her, an invitation. "Would you like to hear one? It's about a woman who planted a tree over her son's grave. It's nearly gone. Only three notes left."

Felix did not sit. Sitting felt like surrender, like settling in, like becoming one more thing in this room that belonged to the court. "Wren," they said. "My name is Felix."

"Felix," she repeated, tasting it. "That's a nice name. Was it expensive?"

"What?"

"Your name." She frowned slightly, the first shadow across her placid face. "Everything here costs something. Names cost the most. Mine isn't mine anymore—I gave it away, or it was given, I don't remember which, and I don't remember what it was, which is strange, because you'd think you'd remember *that*, wouldn't you? What you used to be called." She looked at her own hands as if they might belong to someone else. "Sometimes I think I hear it. Under the songs. Like a bell rung underwater."

"Wren," Felix said, and her head came up sharply.

"What did you say?"

"Wren. Your name is Wren."

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind her eyes. Something vast and drowning, a shape pressing up against the underside of ice. Her breath caught. Her fingers curled against the blanket. And Felix stepped forward without deciding to, heart hammering, hand outstretched—

—and then it passed, whatever it was, sliding back down into the deep water of her, and she blinked and smiled her crooked smile and said, kindly, "That's a pretty sound. Is it from a song?"

* * *

Felix stayed.

They told themselves it was reconnaissance. They told themselves they needed to understand the room, the wards, the rhythms of the keepers who came and went—a gaunt fae man who brought food that steamed but had no smell, who checked the silver band around Wren's wrist the way a farmer checks a tether, murmuring numbers under his breath. *Seventy-one parts remaining,* he said once, and wrote it in a little book, and Felix understood with a nausea that reached all the way down to their bones that he was counting soul. That somewhere in the Hall of Glass, the vial with Wren's name on it was a little emptier each day, and that at the turning of the year it would be empty entirely, and this girl in the bed would be nothing but a well-shaped absence wearing their sister's face.

So they stayed, and watched, and memorized, and hated themselves for how easy it was to stay. Because Wren talked to them. Because she was lonely—of course she was lonely, fifteen years in a pearl-grey room holding dying songs—and because every hour she talked, Felix learned another piece of what had been taken.

Not everything was gone. That was the cruelty of it, the exquisite engineering of it. The court had stripped her memories the way one strips a house for sale—fixtures, fittings, everything of value carried off—but the walls remained. She remembered *how* to be a person without remembering being one. She knew what bread was. She knew that mothers existed, though she couldn't say whether she'd had one. She knew, when Felix flinched at a sound in the corridor, to say "You're safe," in a tone so precisely gentle that Felix had to turn away and pretend great interest in the impossible garden.

And she sang.

She didn't seem to know she was doing it. It came out between sentences, while her hands folded cloth or smoothed the blanket—little fragments, half-melodies, the residue of other people's sold lives. A lullaby in a language Felix didn't know. A marching tune gone ragged with grief. A love song with only its middle left, beginning and ending nowhere, circling on itself like a moth.

And then, on the second night—or what the court called night, when the pearl-light dimmed and the weeping trees outside the false window seemed to lean closer—she hummed a song that stopped Felix's blood.

Four notes. Rising, falling, rising again, then a pause, like a held breath.

Their mother had made it up. Made it up for two children afraid of thunder, tapping it out on their backs in the dark: *da-da-da-dum.* It meant *I am here, I am here, I am here, sleep.* It had never been sung outside their house. It had never been sung anywhere at all. It lived nowhere in the world except in two heads in a garret above the tannery in Vell—one of which belonged to Felix, and one of which had been sold.

"You know that one," Felix said. Their voice came out strange, scraped thin.

Wren's hands stilled. "Do I?" She hummed it again, slower, frowning at it as though it were a puzzle box. "I do. Isn't that odd. It doesn't feel like the others. The others feel like—they're in my mouth, but this one feels like it's in my chest." She pressed her palm flat against her sternum. "Like it grew there. Do you know it?"

"Yes," Felix said. "Our mother made it up. For us. For you and me. When there were storms."

Wren looked at them for a long time. The pearl-light made her face unreadable, soft-edged, dreamlike. "We had storms together?" she asked at last, wistfully, terribly. "That sounds nice. I hope I wasn't frightened."

"You were," Felix said. "You always climbed into my bed. You said my room was closer to the ground and therefore safer from lightning. You were very serious about it. You'd worked it out."

Something flickered—the shape under the ice again, pressing upward, mouths opening silently—and Wren laughed, a short bright laugh that broke in the middle like a dropped plate, and pressed both hands over her face. "Why does that hurt?" she whispered through her fingers. "Why would a nice story hurt?"

Because it's yours, Felix thought. Because they took everything and left the shape of everything, and the shape remembers even when you can't. Because you are a wound walking around in a dress, and no one in this glittering hell has ever once asked if it hurts.

They crossed the room and sat on the edge of the bed, and because they did not know what else to do, because words had failed in every language they owned, they tapped the rhythm on her back. Four notes. *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

Wren went utterly still.

Then she leaned into them, forehead against their shoulder, a child's gesture from a body that had been kept a child, and shook. Not crying—there were no tears, and Felix wondered dimly if those had been sold too—but shaking, some deep structural trembling, like a house in an earthquake that does not fall.

"I don't know you," she said into their shoulder. "I want you to know that. I don't know you, and I don't want you to be sad about it, but I don't. You're kind, and you smell like smoke and rain, and when you tap that song I feel like a cup being filled, and I don't know you." She pulled back and looked at them, and her eyes were wet after all, or something like wet, catching the pearl-light. "Is that terrible?"

"No," Felix said, and found it was true. "No, Wren. It's not terrible. It's just what they did."

* * *

They could have run that night.

Felix lay awake on the floor beside her bed—she had insisted they stay, had arranged a blanket for them with housewifely gravity, another instinct outliving its memory—and went over it the way they went over a lock before touching it. The keeper came at dawn with his book and his numbers. He was alone. He was slight, and Felix had spent a lifetime learning where soft bodies kept their weaknesses. The corridor beyond the weeping door had three turns and no guards; the servants' ways were unwatched; Odile's note had promised a stair, and Odile's notes had not yet lied.

Take her. Carry her. Run.

It would take eleven minutes. Maybe twelve. They had done harder things for less.

Felix closed their eyes and tried to see it: Wren on their back, light as a bundle of sticks because the court had starved something out of her that food couldn't restore; the stair; the gate; the mountain demanding its tolls; the surface; Vell; a room above the tannery; a life. Wren blinking in real sunlight. Wren eating bread. Wren asking, gently, forever, *who are you? why are you crying?*

And then what?

The thought arrived without drama, the way the truest thoughts do. It simply sat down beside them in the dark.

She would wake tomorrow and not know why she was in a strange city. She would ask for the queen—wouldn't she? Some part of her tethered, some thread still running back down into the mountain, the silver band on her wrist humming its quiet numbers. And even if the band came off, even if the thread cut clean—even then. Seventy-one parts. Felix had seen the vault. They had seen the vials, the light in glass, the slow machinery of consumption. Taking the body without the soul was not rescue. It was kidnapping a hollow. It was bringing home an empty house and calling it a sister.

And worse—some part of Felix that was honest in the way only 3 a.m. is honest—worse: the court would notice. The queen, who had received Felix with warmth like a fire in a winter parlor, whose kindness had been the most frightening thing Felix had ever seen. The hunt would come. Not the crown's clumsy hunt, with its pyres and its informants, but *this* hunt, patient and total, and it would find them, and it would take her back, and it would take the price out of Felix besides, and Wren would end up in this room again with fewer parts remaining and no memory even of the stranger who tried.

Running was a lock that didn't open. Felix knew locks. This one was painted on.

They opened their eyes. The pearl-light was returning, dawnless and grey. Wren was awake, watching them from her pillow, humming the four notes softly, filling the room with their mother.

"It's still there," she said, wonderingly. "The song in my chest. It didn't fade overnight. The others always fade." She touched her sternum again. "Do you think some songs don't fade? Do you think some of them just wait?"

Felix sat up. Their whole body ached with a fatigue that had nothing to do with sleeping on stone.

"Wren. Listen to me. I have to go away for a little while. Not forever. I have to go and get something that belongs to you, and then I'll come back, and when I come back I'm going to take you out of here—all of you, every part, the ones in jars and the ones in your chest—and I need you to do one thing until then."

She nodded solemnly, ready for any task, this faithful hollow girl.

"Hold the song," Felix said. "Don't let it fade. However they empty you, however much they take—hold that one. Can you do that?"

"I can hold things," she said. "It's my work."

"I know," Felix said. "It always was."

At the weeping door they paused and looked back. Wren had drawn her knees up and wrapped her arms around them, and she was humming—four notes, rising, falling, rising, a held breath—and outside the false window the pale trees bent in their windless wind, and the sap ran down their grain like tears down a face, and Felix understood suddenly, completely, why the door had opened for grief.

They walked out. They did not look back again, because looking back was a luxury priced beyond anything they had left to pay.

Somewhere below them, in the Hall of Glass, seventy-one parts of their sister waited in the dark. Somewhere above, a hunter and a lady without yesterdays were keeping a rendezvous that Felix was now very late for. And somewhere in the queen's warm parlors, a bargain was ripening toward its second price.

Felix Galloway went to find Odile Marchand, and to ask her, finally, what she had seen in the vault—what was written on the vial beside Wren's, in letters old enough to predate the mountain itself.

The founder's name. They needed to know why it mattered.

They needed to know before the year turned.
