## 13. The Hunter's Confession

The deep roads had a way of holding sound the way a cupped hand holds water, and in the hours after their flight from the vault, every drip and distant groan of stone sounded to Ilsa Verrow like a knock at her door.

She sat apart from the others at the edge of a chamber where an underground river ran black and slow, its surface so still it mirrored the faint phosphorescence clinging to the ceiling. Somewhere above them—she could not guess how far—lay Vell, with its wardens and its ledgers and its square where she had lit fires that would not go out behind her eyes. She had unbuckled her sword belt an hour ago and set it across her knees, and she found she could not decide whether she was guarding it or preparing to surrender it.

Felix was asleep. Truly asleep, for the first time since she'd known them, curled against Odile's shoulder near the riverbank with the founder's vial wrapped in cloth and tucked beneath their arm like a child's doll. Odile did not sleep—Odile never seemed to sleep—but she watched Felix with an expression Ilsa had no name for, something between tenderness and envy, as though the sleeping thief were holding something Odile had once owned and lost.

Wren's body lay on the far side of the chamber, wrapped in Odile's traveling cloak. The girl breathed. That was the horror of it—the slow, even breathing of someone merely resting, when everything that made breathing worth doing had been siphoned away into glass and gold. Fifteen years of breaths spent in service to a queen who wore stolen centuries like rings on her fingers.

Ilsa knew how that felt from the other side of the glass. She had been the one who poured.

"You're going to wear a groove in that stone," Odile said quietly, without turning her head. "Pacing."

"I'm not pacing."

"You're about to. You have the look of a woman rehearsing a speech." Odile's eyes came around at last, pale and unbothered. "I've watched noblemen rehearse confessions for forty years of court life. They always pace first."

Ilsa looked down at her hands. They were a hunter's hands, scarred at the knuckles, steady on a crossbow, steady on a torch. Steady, always steady. That had been the whole of her reputation: Captain Verrow does not flinch. Captain Verrow follows orders. Captain Verrow wept only once, they said, and no one agreed on when.

"I signed the order," she said.

The words came out flat, stripped of anything that might have softened them. Across the chamber, Odile went very still.

"Fifteen years ago. The Galloway family. Father, mother, two children flagged as gifted by an informant whose name I never learned because I didn't ask. The order came down from the Lord Warden under crown seal. All I had to do was sign my name at the bottom and pass it to the enforcement detail." Her thumb moved along the pommel of the sword across her knees, back and forth, back and forth. "I signed it. I passed it. And then I requested reassignment to the northern districts, which everyone praised as humility, and which was actually just me running."

The river said nothing. Rivers were good at that.

"The parents escaped," Ilsa went on, because now that the dam had cracked she could not stop the water. "That was the one mercy in it, if you can call it mercy. They got the children out ahead of the raid. But the order stood. The children were named in it. Wren Galloway, age six, gifted. There was a standing warrant for fifteen years, and every hunter who worked Vell inherited it, and none of us ever closed it because none of us ever found her." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, like a blade drawn at the wrong angle. "I hunted her. Do you understand? For fifteen years I carried a paper with her name on it, and I told myself I hoped I'd never find her, and I never once burned the paper. Because burning it would have meant admitting what I'd done, and I wasn't brave enough for that. I was only disciplined enough to keep being a coward on schedule."

* * *

She had been twenty-four years old, and hungry—not for food, though there had been years of that too, but for permanence. A warden's daughter from the tannery quarter who had clawed her way into the hunters' academy on marks alone, because marks were the one currency a poor girl could mint without capital. She had believed, then, in the doctrine they taught between drills: that magic was a fire uncontained, that the gifted were kindling scattered through a wooden city, that a hunter's coldness was a form of love for everyone she'd never have to burn.

The order had come to her desk on a morning of gray rain. The Lord Warden himself had brought it, which should have been the first warning—he did not carry papers; papers were carried to him. He had set it down in front of her and stood there with his hands clasped behind his back while she read it, and she had felt his attention on her like a hand on the back of her neck.

"A sensitive matter," he'd said. "The family has connections. Old ones. The crown wants this handled cleanly and quickly, and it wants a name on the authorization that carries weight going forward. Yours is rising, Verrow. This is what rising looks like."

She had read the names. Galloway. Four of them. The youngest was six.

"There's an informant's claim here," she'd said. "Unverified. Protocol requires—"

"Protocol," the Lord Warden had said, in the tone a man uses for a dog he is fond of but will not be ruled by, "is for matters that are not sensitive."

And she had thought—Ilsa remembered this with a clarity that felt like a wound reopened nightly—she had thought, *if I refuse, he will find another name, and the children will burn anyway, and I will have spent my one piece of leverage for nothing.* It had even been true, probably. It was the sort of arithmetic cowards do well. She had dipped her pen. Her hand had been perfectly steady. She had been proud of that steadiness, afterward, in the worst hours of the night, when pride curdled into its opposite: *I signed the death of a six-year-old without even trembling.*

The raid had gone wrong in exactly one respect. The parents had run ahead of it, gotten the children out, vanished into the rookeries where hunters' boots were heavy and informants' eyes were everywhere. The father had died within the year—fever, the records said, though records in Vell said whatever kept their writers comfortable. The mother had disappeared entirely. And the children—

The boy had grown up to bottle other people's memories and sell them as luck-charms in the night market, and the girl had been sold to the court beneath the mountain, and neither fact had Ilsa known until three weeks ago, when a charm-seller with familiar eyes had looked up at her across a market stall and she had felt fifteen years fall on her all at once like a collapsing roof.

* * *

"They'll want to know," Odile said at last. Her voice was careful, the voice of a woman handling glass. "When Felix wakes. You understand that."

"I know."

"And you're going to tell them yourself. Not let it come out sideways."

"That's why I'm sitting over here rehearsing a speech instead of pacing." Ilsa almost smiled. It didn't take. "There's a version of this where I say nothing. Where we finish the job, save the girl, and I ride north and request another reassignment and spend the rest of my life being disciplined on schedule. I've been drafting that version for fifteen years. It's very polished. It has excellent footnotes."

"But?"

"But I watched her today." Ilsa nodded toward the still shape in the cloak. "In the vault. Felix put their hand on the glass and Wren's soul dimmed toward it, like a candle leaning into a draft. Like it recognized something. And I stood there thinking: I did that. Every year of that dimming traces back to a signature. My name, in ink, on a rainy Tuesday, because a man with clean boots told me it was what rising looked like." She finally lifted her gaze to meet Odile's. "You sold your past to pay a debt. I've wondered since the road whether you regret it."

"Every day," Odile said simply. "And I'd do it again. Both things are true. That's the trick nobody warns you about—that guilt and necessity can share a bed for decades and both sleep soundly."

"Then you understand."

"I understand better than you'd like." Odile rose, unhurried, and crossed to the river's edge. She crouched there, studying her reflection—a woman with no yesterdays, looking at a face she couldn't account for. "But hear me, Captain, because I've watched men offer forfeits before, in courts far older than yours. Guilt wants absolution. It will dress itself up as sacrifice and call the dressing justice. Offering your life is easy. It's a single payment, dramatic, final, and it lets you stop carrying the thing." She turned her head, and in the phosphorescent light her eyes looked like chips of ice over deep water. "Responsibility is harder. Responsibility means living with your name on that order and doing the work anyway. Which one are you actually offering?"

Ilsa had no answer ready. She sat with the silence and let it be honest.

* * *

Felix woke near what passed for morning in the deep roads—a shifting of the air, a brightening of the ceiling-glow—and the first thing they did, before speaking, before stretching, was touch the vial at their side. Confirming it was real. Confirming the founder's soul still slept in its glass, the key they had stolen from the queen's own vault.

Then they saw Ilsa, apart, sword across her knees, and Ilsa saw them see her, and understood that the moment had arrived whether she had finished rehearsing or not.

"Captain," Felix said. Their voice was rough with sleep. "You look like a woman about to resign."

"In a sense." Ilsa stood. Her legs ached; everything ached; she welcomed it. "Sit down, Galloway. What I have to say doesn't improve with either of us standing."

Felix sat. Odile drifted back to her place nearby, watchful, saying nothing, giving Ilsa the room she'd asked for and the witness she hadn't.

"Fifteen years ago," Ilsa began, "an order crossed my desk. Crown-sealed. It named four people as gifted and condemned them to seizure and pyre. The Galloway family." She watched Felix's face as the name landed—watched the flinch, the quick calculation, the dawning. "The order required an authorizing signature from a ranking officer. The Lord Warden wanted a rising name on it. He stood over me while I read it. I raised protocol objections. He dismissed them. And I signed."

The silence that followed was total. Even the river seemed to hold its course.

"You signed," Felix repeated. Flatly. The way one repeats a number in a ledger to be sure one has heard it right.

"Wren was six years old. Your parents got you out ahead of the raid—I don't know how, I've never learned how—but the warrant stayed open. Your names stayed in the rolls. And I—" Her voice caught, and she let it, because smoothing it over would have been one more lie. "I carried that warrant for fifteen years. I told myself I hoped never to find you. I never once destroyed the paper. That's the size of my courage, Galloway. That's the whole measure of it."

Felix had gone very still in the way Ilsa had learned to read as dangerous—the stillness of a person deciding, rapidly and completely, what they are willing to become. When they spoke, their voice was quiet enough to be worse than shouting.

"My mother died two years after we ran. In a cellar, coughing blood, because she wouldn't risk a hedge-healer and I was too young to steal medicine worth having. My father took work that broke him and drank what it paid. And Wren—" Felix's hand found the vial at their side and gripped it. "Wren was sold. Do you know what a child's soul fetches, Captain? Neither do I. Our mentor handled the sale. He kept the ledger. I found it in his things after he died, and I read the entry eleven times hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something else. *One soul, juvenile, unspoiled. To the court beneath.* Fifteen years she's been down there, being drunk slowly, and the queen wears her years like jewelry, and I am going to get her back or die in the attempt." They stood. They were smaller than Ilsa, thinner, a thief's build, but in that moment they filled the chamber. "So tell me why I shouldn't put your own sword through you right now. Give me a reason that isn't about your guilt. I've seen what your guilt costs other people."

It was a fair question. It deserved more than the answer she wanted to give.

"Because my guilt won't help her," Ilsa said. "And neither will my death—not yet. Here's what I am that you need: I know the enforcement rotations in Vell. I know which wardens can be bought and which only pretend they can't. I know the solstice security schedules because I helped write them. And I know the mountain gate protocols from the inside, because I've sealed three of them in my career." She unbuckled the sword belt fully, held it a moment, and then—deliberately, letting Felix watch—laid it on the stone between them. "And because I'm not asking you to forgive me. Forgiveness is a currency I haven't earned and won't insult you by requesting. I'm telling you the debt exists, and that I intend to pay it in the only coin I have left, which is use. If, at the end of this, you still want my life—you can have it. I won't resist. But spend me first. Spend me on her."

Felix stared at the sword on the ground between them for a long time. Then at Ilsa. Something moved behind their eyes—hate, yes, and grief, and underneath both, unwelcome and undeniable, the recognition of a fellow creature shaped by the same machinery. Two kinds of damage, meeting.

"You'll carry the warrant," Felix said at last. "You'll carry it until she's free. And if she wakes and wants justice—"

"Then she'll have it. From me. However she defines it."

"And if she wants nothing? If there's nothing left in her that remembers enough to want?"

Ilsa had prepared for every question except that one. She stood in it, bare, and found that the honest answer was also the only one.

"Then I'll carry it anyway," she said. "Some debts don't require the creditor's consent. That's what makes them debts."

Felix picked up the sword. For one suspended heartbeat Ilsa genuinely did not know whether it would come for her throat—and discovered, with a strange calm, that she had made her peace with either outcome. But Felix only weighed it, turned it, and held it back out to her, hilt-first.

"We leave within the hour," they said. "The solstice is nine days off, and the queen knows we stole from her vault. Get us to Vell, Captain. Get us through your own security lines. After that—" their jaw tightened, and grief showed through the crack like light through a shutter, "—after that we'll see what my sister's freedom costs, and who pays it."

Ilsa took the sword. Buckled it back on. It sat differently at her hip now, heavier, the way truth weighs.

Behind Felix, Odile caught Ilsa's eye and gave the smallest nod—the nod of one woman who had asked her hard question and received, at last, an answer she could work with.

Nine days, Ilsa thought, as the company gathered itself in the phosphorescent dark. Nine days until the turning of the year, until Wren's soul was consumed past saving and the queen became a thing no vial could unmake. Nine days to cross the passes, evade the huntsmen already surely combing the deep roads behind them, breach a city on high alert, and split a plan down the middle between a pyre and a mountain.

And somewhere behind them in the dark, patient as water finding stone, the queen's huntsmen were already following the trail of three thieves and a stolen soul.

Ilsa checked her blade, checked the shadows, and took the first watch.
