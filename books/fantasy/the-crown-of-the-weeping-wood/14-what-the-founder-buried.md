## 14. What the Founder Buried

The fragment came to her in the dark, the way all true things seemed to come to Odile Marchand now—uninvited, unwrapped, and sharp at both ends.

She had not meant to open it. That was the truth she would carry afterward, the small absolution she clung to: that she had not meant to. The vial had simply been there among Ilsa's confiscated papers, tucked into a folio stamped with the crown's seal, and it had been humming.

Not singing. Humming. A low, patient note like a struck bell that had forgotten how to stop ringing, felt more in the teeth than the ears. She had carried the folio for three days since their flight from the court, since the deep roads had tried to swallow them and spat them out half-buried in snow above the tree line, and for three days she had told herself the hum was nothing—the mountain still speaking through its stolen goods, the way a house settles after its owners leave.

But on the third night, in the ruined shepherd's hut where they'd made camp, with Felix asleep sitting upright against the wall and Wren's hollow body breathing its slow dream-breath under a wool blanket, and Ilsa standing watch at the door with her hand never far from her sword—on that third night, Odile took out the vial and held it to the firelight, and the humming stopped.

It stopped because it recognized her.

* * *

She knew what it was before she knew what was in it. That was the cruelty of being memoryless: her mind kept no inventory, but her body did. Her hands remembered things her head could not. When she held certain objects—a key, a letter sealed with green wax, a child's shoe—her fingers would tighten around them with a tenderness or a dread that arrived without explanation, like weather blowing in from a country she'd never visited.

This vial made her hands shake.

It was smaller than the soul-vials of the court beneath the mountain, plainer, its glass clouded rather than clear, as though whatever light it held had been poured in long ago and had slowly gone overcast. There was no label. There was only a scrap of parchment tied around its neck with wire, and on the parchment, in a hand she did not recognize but which made her stomach drop as though she were falling upstairs, two words:

*Marchand debt.*

She should have called for Ilsa. She knew that too. They had agreed—after the vault, after Wren, after the queen's honeyed voice offering bargains wrapped inside bargains—that no one would touch anything from the court alone. They had agreed it the way people agree to rules they intend to break, nodding solemnly while already composing their exceptions.

Odile sat down on the cold hearthstone, turned the vial twice in her fingers, and pulled the wire free.

The stopper came away with a sound like a sigh.

What rose from it was not light, exactly. It was the memory of light—the way a room remembers a window after the shutters close. It gathered in front of her, silver-grey and slow, and shaped itself into something almost like a figure, almost like a face, and then, mercifully or cruelly, it stopped trying to be a person and became instead a place.

A wood. Trees with pale bark and leaves that hung heavy and wet even in high summer, dripping though no rain fell. A path worn soft into the moss. And at the path's end, a clearing, and in the clearing—

*Her.*

Odile saw herself. Younger—not by years, for her face was much the same, but by weight. This other Odile stood straighter. Her eyes were not the polite, unfocused calm that strangers mistook for serenity; they were fierce, and quick, and full of a fury so old it had gone smooth as river stone. She wore travelling clothes stained with road dust, and she was arguing with someone just outside the memory's frame, and her voice, when it came, was the first sound of her own past Odile had heard in eleven years.

"—because he sold it, Mother. He sold the whole wood. Every tree, every grave, every name carved into the stones. Do you understand what that means? You cannot sell a place where the dead are *kept*. It isn't property. It's a promise."

The scene shifted, the way memories do when they are old and have been handled by many hands. Now she was older in the vision—or the same age, but harder—and she stood in a marble hall Odile did not know, before a desk, before a man whose face was blurred by the vial's decay but whose signet ring was sharp as a knife: a crown above a mountain. The crown's own seal. The crown's own man.

"The debt stands," the blurred mouth said. "Your father pledged collateral. The family name survives only if the pledge is honored."

"Then take the estate. Take the ships. Take my title."

"We have taken those." A pause, and in the pause, Odile felt the temperature of the memory change, felt the young woman's heart begin to hammer against her ribs as though the ribs were her own. "What remains is you."

"I don't understand."

"You will," said the man with the ring, "for about a minute."

And then the memory tore.

* * *

Odile came back to herself on the floor of the hut with her cheek against the stone and the empty vial rolling gently beside her hand, and she was screaming, or had been screaming, or was about to scream again—she could not tell which—and Ilsa was kneeling over her with one hand braced on her sword hilt and the other hovering an inch from Odile's shoulder, afraid to touch her.

"Lady Marchand. Odile. Look at me. What did you take?"

"It was mine." Her voice came out scraped raw, as if she had been using it for hours. Perhaps she had. "Ilsa—it was *mine*. My father sold me. Not my fortune. *Me.* To pay a debt to—" She stopped. Her mind, reaching for the end of the sentence, found the same blank wall it always found, the wall where eleven years used to be. But now the wall had a door in it, and through the door she could see the weeping wood, and the marble hall, and the ring.

"To whom?" Ilsa asked, and her voice had gone very quiet, very flat, the voice of a hunter who has caught a scent.

"That's the thing." Odile pushed herself up. Her hands would not stop trembling, so she pressed them flat against her knees. "That's the thing, Captain. I don't remember who bought me. The memory doesn't say. It tears right at the—" She mimed it, the rip, the white gap. "Eleven years. Everyone assumed—I assumed—that I sold my past to some moneylender, some mortal debt, some ugly transaction with ugly men. That's what the family said afterward. That's what I built my life on top of. A hole with a boring story in it."

"And now?"

"Now I know the story isn't boring." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Now I know the hole has a shape, and the shape is a mountain."

Felix stirred against the wall. In sleep or near it, they murmured something—Wren's name, half-formed—and Odile looked at the girl under the blanket, at the beautiful vacant face, at the chest rising and falling with breath that belonged to no one anymore, and something in Odile's chest went hard and bright as a coal.

"Felix," she said. "Wake up. I need your eyes."

* * *

They woke him properly, or as properly as anyone could be woken who had slept sitting up in a freezing hut after fleeing a fae court. Felix blinked at the firelight, at the empty vial in Odile's palm, and his expression moved quickly—from confusion to recognition to a kind of dread that Odile was learning to read on him the way sailors read weather.

"Where did you get that?"

"Among Ilsa's papers. From the vault antechamber, the night we ran. It must have been swept up with the documents." She watched his face carefully. "I opened it. I know we said—"

"You opened it." Felix rubbed his eyes. "Of course you did. Of course." But there was no anger in it, only exhaustion, and underneath the exhaustion something that might have been envy. "What did it show you?"

So she told them. All of it—the weeping wood, the argument with her mother, the marble hall, the man with the ring, the terrible little joke at the end, *you will, for about a minute*. She told it plainly, because she had learned in the last weeks that plainness was the only armor left to her, and when she finished, the silence in the hut was so complete she could hear the snow settling on the roof.

Ilsa spoke first, and her voice was strange. "Describe the ring again."

"A crown above a mountain. Gold. Old-fashioned workmanship—the band was worn thin on one side, like it had been turned on the finger for years."

"Whose finger?"

"I couldn't see his face. The memory's decayed. But the ring—"

"Not his face." Ilsa had gone to the doorway, to the grey pre-dawn light, and she stood with her back to them, and Odile saw that her shoulders had locked. "The founder's seal. Crown above mountain. Every witch-hunter carries it stamped on their commission papers. Every execution order in Vell bears it. We're taught it's symbolic—the founder watching over the city's purity." She turned. Her face in the firelight looked ten years older than it had a month ago. "We're also taught the founder died childless, four hundred years ago, and that the weeping wood was cut down for timber within a generation of her death. Cleared. Gone. There's a granite monument where it stood."

"There's a wood," Odile said. "I've seen it. It's alive, and it drips, and it grows at the heart of a court beneath the mountain, and the queen wears it as a crown."

"I know what she wears." Ilsa's jaw worked. "I've knelt in front of that crown. Twice. Formal presentations, when I received my commission. Every hunter does. You kneel, and she touches your shoulder with a branch of it, and the sap is cold, and everyone says it's an honor." Her hand had drifted to her sword again, unconsciously, the way a man touches a wound. "It didn't feel like an honor. It felt like being *measured*."

Felix had been quiet through all of this, turning the empty vial over and over, and now they set it down on the hearthstone with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might go off.

"Say it," Felix said. "One of us has to say it out loud or we'll all keep circling it until the solstice eats Wren whole."

No one said it. So Felix said it.

"The founder didn't die. The founder was *sold*. Or she sold herself—there's always a bargain, there's always a ledger—and whatever was left of her went under the mountain, and it's been down there so long it forgot it was ever a woman, and now it calls itself Queen Marenthe, and it wears her grief as a crown, and it stays alive the only way a stolen thing can stay alive." Felix's voice cracked, and steadied. "By eating. By taking souls the way she herself was taken. Wren. Odile. Hundreds of years of us. She's not a queen who buys souls. She's a soul that was bought, making the world match her wound."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the dead pines with a sound like distant weeping, and Odile thought: *even the mountain mocks us.*

"No," Ilsa said—but it was the *no* of a woman objecting to a verdict, not to the evidence. "The founder's name was Marenthe. Marenthe the First. It's in every history of Vell. The queen's name is—"

"Marenthe." Odile heard her own voice come out level and cold and wondered briefly whose voice it had been before it was hers. "You said it yourself, Captain. Four hundred years, and the founder's name is still on the lips of every soldier, every schoolchild, every burning square. Names are power. Especially here. Especially *down there*. What better disguise for a stolen soul than to hide in plain sight—to wear the one name no one would ever question?" She looked at the empty vial. "My father's creditors weren't moneylenders. They were collectors. Agents. The court has been buying pieces of Vell for centuries, and the crown has been the broker, and neither the buyers nor the sellers ever look each other in the eye because the paperwork is excellent and the seal is sacred."

"The executions," Ilsa whispered. "The hunts. Fifteen years ago, when Wren's family was condemned—when I signed—" She stopped, swallowed. Started again, flatter. "The gifted aren't purged. They're *harvested*. The pyres are theater. The ones who burn are the ones who refuse to sell. The ones who sell are sent quietly, with good prices and good terms, down the mountain. And the hunt keeps the supply frightened enough to negotiate."

"Wren didn't sell," Felix said softly. "Our mentor sold her. But she was on the ledger all the same. Everything flows to the same account." They looked up, and their eyes were wet and furious. "She's been drinking my sister for fifteen years."

* * *

They talked until the sun came up, laying the pieces out on the hearthstone between them like cards, and the picture they made was worse in its completeness than any single piece had been alone.

Odile contributed what the fragment had given her, and it was more than she had said at first—more than she had dared. Because the memory had not ended at the tearing. There had been a last image, a single frame clinging to the edge of the rip like a splinter, and she had held it back through the long night the way one holds back the worst news until the others have eaten.

In the final frame, she was in a carriage. Dark windows. Her hands bound not with rope but with ribbon—green ribbon, silk, absurdly gentle—and across from her sat a figure in grey whose face she still could not see, but who leaned forward and said, in a voice like water finding its level:

*"Don't be afraid. The queen is very kind to girls who remember things. She likes to collect what they remember. You'll be useful for years."*

"She wanted my memories," Odile said. "Not my soul. My soul was the payment. My memories were the *merchandise*. Eleven years of a noblewoman's past—births and deaths and debts and scandals—who knows what my family buried in my own head. And the queen collects memories the way magpies collect silver." Her hands had gone still at last, folded in her lap, white-knuckled. "Which means somewhere in that court, in that vault, in some glass and gold reliquary, there is a shelf with *me* on it. Eleven years of Odile Marchand, catalogued and corked."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. "You could get them back."

"I could die getting them back," Odile corrected. "There's a difference, and I intend to keep noticing it." But even as she said it, she felt the pull of it, the awful gravity of that imagined shelf, and she understood at last why the queen's bargains worked—not because mortals were greedy, but because the court sold back to you the exact shape of your own emptiness, priced to bleed.

It was Ilsa who broke the circle, finally, standing and buckling on her sword with the brisk violence of a woman refusing to think.

"Four days to the solstice. Two days' march to Vell, if the roads hold and the wardens don't hold them against us. We can't fight the court—we've seen what fights it costs. But the queen has one weakness she doesn't know we've found." She tapped the empty vial with one gauntleted finger. "She thinks her origin is buried. Buried things have a way of coming up. If the founder's soul can be freed—if there's anything left of the woman under four hundred years of hunger—then Marenthe can be killed, and Wren can be cut loose before the year turns."

"And if there's nothing left of her?" Felix asked. "If it's all hunger now, all the way down?"

Ilsa met their eyes. "Then we learn what the founder learned," she said, "about the price of selling yourself. And we pay it differently."

Outside, the dawn came up the colour of ash, and Odile sat with her empty vial in her lap and thought about shelves, and ribbons, and the eleven years of herself waiting in the dark under the mountain like letters that had never been delivered.

The past, she understood now, did not stay buried. It waited. It hummed. It recognized you across a crowded vault and stopped singing the moment you picked it up.

And four hundred years of buried past was, at this very moment, wearing a crown of weeping wood and counting the days until the year turned—and it had no idea that three broken people in a shepherd's hut had just dug up its grave.

Or so they believed.

For when Odile looked down at the vial one last time before wrapping it away, she saw—faint, fading, unmistakable—fresh frost blooming on the inside of the glass, in the shape of a small, patient smile.
