## 15. Flight Through the Deep Roads

The vault's alarm was not a sound so much as a presence—a pressure that bloomed through the stone like blood through water, and Felix felt it in their teeth before they heard it in the air.

*Run,* said every instinct they had ever honed in the night markets of Vell, and for once Felix obeyed without argument.

They burst from the Memory Vault with Wren's half-consumed soul still burning behind their eyes—that small, guttering light in its glass cradle, too fragile to carry, too precious to leave. Odile was already moving, her pale gown dark with dust, her hand trailing along the wall as though she could read the mountain's moods through her fingertips. Ilsa brought up the rear, sword drawn, her witch-hunter's coat swapped for the grey livery of a court servant that fooled no one and slowed no one either.

"The deep roads," Odile said. It was not a suggestion. "The servants' ways. The queen's huntsmen will take the grand galleries—they'll want to be *seen* coming."

"And you know this how?" Ilsa demanded.

"Because I have spent three years buying back pieces of myself," Odile said, "and one of them remembered these halls." She did not explain further. She never explained further. That was the shape of her: a woman built entirely of forward motion, with nothing behind her but purchased fragments.

Felix followed her into a corridor barely wider than their shoulders, where the walls wept mineral light and the air tasted of iron and old winters. Behind them, the pressure of the alarm swelled. Ahead, the dark opened like a throat.

* * *

Ilsa Verrow had spent fifteen years learning how to hunt people through stone.

She knew the grammar of pursuit: the way sound carried differently when pursuers split up; the discipline of the quarry who resisted looking back; the fatal comfort of believing a turn taken was distance gained. She had taught these lessons to recruits in the warden yards of Vell, standing over maps of streets she would later walk with warrants in her fist.

Now she was the lesson.

"They've split," she murmured. The echo came wrong—doubled, layered, one set of footsteps running true and another ghosting through some parallel gallery beyond the wall. "Two parties at least. They're herding us."

"Herding us where?" Felix whispered.

"Toward somewhere open." Ilsa hated how easily the words came. Fifteen years of service did not wash out; it only changed sides. "A court always has an amphitheater. A place where things are done *formally*."

Odile stopped so suddenly that Felix nearly collided with her back. She stood very still, head tilted, listening to something none of them could hear—or perhaps to something only she could, some scrap of a self she'd bought back from a glass vial and swallowed whole.

"Not the amphitheater," she said softly. "There's a junction ahead. The Crossroads of Small Mercies. My memory named it that." Her voice caught on the strangeness of citing herself as a source. "Four passages. Three lead deeper into the court. One leads up."

"Up is what we want," Felix said.

"Up is what they'll expect us to want." Odile turned, and in the mineral gloom her face was a study in composure stretched thin. "But my memory also says the fourth passage is trapped—not by the fae. By the mountain itself. It demands a toll, even here, even now. And the toll is *held*."

"Held?" Ilsa asked.

"Open. Someone has to hold the way while the others pass. The passage stays shut until the holder releases it or—" She did not finish. She didn't need to. In Vell they hanged people; everyone knew how sentences ended.

Silence pooled between them, cold as the water seeping down the walls.

"I'll hold it," Ilsa said.

"No," Felix said immediately.

"You don't get to argue. You're the one who can't be caught—you're the thief, you're the whole plan, you're the reason any of this—" Ilsa's voice cracked, and she mastered it the way she mastered everything, with violence turned inward. "I signed the order, Galloway. Fifteen years ago. I put your sister's name on a list because the crown told me the list was mercy, that the gifted would be *safer* below. I owe this road more than you do."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly how debts work." She sheathed her sword and rolled her shoulders like a woman preparing for a long watch. "Go. Both of you. When you're through, count to a hundred, and don't look back."

"No," Felix said again, and this time it came out steady. "We don't leave people. That's the whole point. That's what I'm learning, anyway, late and badly." They looked at Odile. "Is there another way?"

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Then, slowly, she reached into the bodice of her ruined gown and drew out a slender vial, stoppered with silver, glowing faintly with a light like late afternoon through curtains.

"One," she said. "But it's mine."

* * *

The vial held the oldest memory Odile had ever bought back: the day she was seven years old, and her mother braided her hair by a window, and told her she was loved.

It was, in truth, nearly all she had of herself. Everything else she'd recovered had been practical—the roads, the names, the shapes of doors. This one she had kept sealed for three years, unworn, unopened, because she had been afraid that if she took it into herself and it fit badly—if the woman she'd become could not hold the child she'd been—then there would be nothing left of Odile Marchand at all, just a well-dressed absence walking around in expensive shoes.

She had never told anyone this. There had been no one to tell.

Now there were two people looking at her in the weeping dark of the deep roads, waiting for her to say the thing she had already decided.

"It's a key," she lied smoothly, because some habits were load-bearing. "Memories open doors here. The court runs on surrendered things. A memory freely given should persuade the mountain to hold itself open—for a while."

"For a while," Ilsa repeated.

"A while is all we need." Odile pressed the vial into Felix's hands before either of them could see her hands shaking. "When we reach the surface passage, pour it into the seam of the door. Don't drink it. Don't look at it. Just pour."

"What does it—"

"It doesn't matter what it holds. What matters is that it's *mine*, given freely, and the mountain respects ownership." She smiled, and it was almost her real smile, the one she'd practiced in mirrors for a face that had no past to practice against. "Go."

* * *

They ran.

The deep roads of the court beneath the mountain were not carved but grown, Felix realized—veins of crystal and root-amber threading through living rock, pulsing faintly with a rhythm too slow to be a heartbeat and too regular to be anything else. The mountain was asleep, or dreaming, or digesting. Felix tried not to think about which.

Behind them, the pursuit resolved from echo into certainty: the ringing of metal-shod feet, the high thin music of fae horns that made the walls flex and shiver. Once, rounding a bend, Felix glimpsed them—huntsmen in antlered helms, faces like beautiful knives, moving with the terrible economy of things that had never once doubted themselves.

An arrow of green glass buried itself in the wall an inch from Felix's ear and dissolved into perfume.

"Faster!" Ilsa barked, and shoved them both through a low archway as a second volley shattered against the stone behind them.

The Crossroads of Small Mercies opened around them like the inside of a bell: four passages radiating from a chamber floored in silver sand that whispered underfoot. And there—exactly as Odile's stolen memory had promised—the fourth passage stood sealed behind a door of woven roots, dark and dense as a closed eye.

Above the door, in letters of dripping amber, words surfaced and sank like drowned bodies:

*WHAT IS HELD MAY PASS. WHAT PASSES MUST BE HELD.*

"There's no time," Ilsa said, drawing her sword again as the horns swelled closer. "Odile, the vial—"

But Odile was already stepping backward, away from the door, toward the sound of the hunt.

"No," Felix said, understanding arriving like ice water. "No, no, that's not the plan—"

"The plan was always going to break," Odile said. She sounded almost serene. "Plans are scaffolding. You build them knowing you'll tear them down. But someone has to hold the door from this side, Felix, and it can't be you—you're carrying Wren's hope in your head and Ilsa's guilt in your pocket and apparently the entire future of magic in Vell, which is a great deal of luggage for one charm-seller." The horns were very close now. She began to walk toward them, unhurried, a noblewoman strolling her own grounds. "I'll hold the passage. When you're through, seal it behind you. The mountain will keep me safe—it keeps everything safe, that's the horror of this place. Nothing here is lost. Nothing here is ever allowed to be lost."

"That's not safety, that's a vault!"

"Yes," Odile said, and her smile cracked clean through, and underneath it was the seven-year-old girl by the window, terrified and beloved and about to be left alone in the dark. "Isn't it wonderful? For once, I'll be the treasure instead of the thief."

She raised both hands, palms out, toward the woven door—and *pushed*, not with muscle but with something older, something the mountain recognized, and the roots groaned apart like a mouth opening.

"Through," she commanded. "*Now.*"

* * *

Ilsa went first, because Odile ordered it and because orders were the only language her guilt spoke fluently. Then Felix, clutching the vial, pausing at the threshold—

"Odile—"

"—is a fool's name for gratitude," Odile called back, already bracing against the doorframe as the first huntsman rounded the corner, antlered and gleaming. "Go home, charm-seller. Sell someone a happy memory. Come back for me when you've learned to steal bigger things."

Then the huntsmen were on her, and the door began to close, and the last thing Felix saw was Odile Marchand standing straight as a candle in the wind, holding the passage open with her whole purchased, patched-together self—

—and then the roots snapped shut, and the dark took her, and Felix was running again, up and up, through passages that narrowed and warmed, that began to smell of rain and pine and the far-off smoke of human fires.

* * *

Ilsa caught Felix at the top of the last stair, hauling them bodily through a seam in the mountainside that spilled them out into night air so cold and clean it felt like a slap.

They lay on wet grass beneath a sky salted with stars, gasping. Below and behind them, the mountain hummed its slow digestion. Somewhere inside it, sealed in silver sand and amber light, a lady without yesterdays was holding a door against the dark.

"She bought us the time," Ilsa managed at last. "With the only thing she had left."

"No." Felix sat up. Their hands were still clenched around the vial—Odile's vial, the one she'd pressed into their grip and never taken back, the one that glowed now like a small stubborn sunset against their palm. "She gave us the time. There's a difference, and I'm going to spend the rest of my life learning it."

Ilsa looked at the vial, then at Felix, and something passed across her face that was not quite hope but lived next door to it.

"The solstice," she said. "How many days?"

Felix counted against the stars, the way their mentor had taught them, and felt the number land in their chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"Three," they said. "We have three days to raise an army of outlaws, break into a kingdom, and steal back two souls—one of them mine, one of hers." They rose, legs shaking, and looked east, where the distant lights of Vell glittered like a handful of scattered embers. "And if we're going to do it, we start tonight. Because I know every hidden gifted soul in that city—and it's time they knew each other too."

Far below, deep in the mountain, something ancient shifted in its sleep—and far above, in Founder's Square, the first pyre of the season was already being stacked.
