## 16. The Turning of the Year

The light that met them at the mountain's mouth was wrong.

Felix had expected dawn—had dreamed of it, in the last hours beneath the earth, when the deep roads groaned and shifted and Odile's blood marked their passage like a trail of red thread. They had dreamed of gray morning light, of wind, of the smell of pine and cold stone. What they got instead was a sky the color of a bruise, low and swollen, and an air so still it felt held, the way a room holds its breath before bad news.

They came up out of the roots in single file: Ilsa first, sword drawn though there was nothing yet to fight; then Felix with Wren's empty body carried against their chest like something breakable and precious, which she was both and neither; then Odile, leaning on the hunter's shoulder, her side bound in strips torn from her own fine ruined gown, each step a small controlled act of will.

Behind them, the mountain sealed itself. There was no door to close—it had never been a door, only a fold in the world shaped like one—but Felix heard it anyway, a sound like a great book shutting, and felt the weight of everything they'd left inside settle over the land like frost.

Wren did not stir. She breathed. Her heart beat, slow and even as a clock in an empty house. But her eyes stayed closed, and when Felix spoke her name—and they had spoken it perhaps forty times on the road down, and forty more on the desperate climb back—she only smiled in her sleep, faintly, the way she'd smiled as a small child dreaming of sugar.

*Four days,* Felix thought, laying her down on a bed of moss just inside the treeline. *We have four days.*

The solstice would fall on the night of the fourth. And somewhere beneath all that stone, in a vault of glass and light, half of Wren's soul sat in its vial, thinning by the hour, being drunk down sip by sip by a queen who wore centuries like jewelry. When the turning of the year came, whatever remained of Wren would be gone—not dead, not even consumed, simply *incorporated*, poured into Marenthe until no boundary remained between the girl and the crown. And the queen would be unkillable. Whole. Eternal. Fed on a child from Copperlane whose only crime had been a brother who could steal memories and a family too poor to refuse what the ledger offered.

Felix knelt in the moss and brushed the hair back from Wren's face. Fifteen years since they'd seen her awake. Fifteen years, and now here she was, warm and breathing and utterly absent, humming sometimes in her sleep—the childhood song, always the childhood song, the one fragment of herself the court hadn't thought worth taking.

"Her body's failing," Odile said quietly. She had lowered herself onto a root nearby, gray-faced, one hand pressed to her bandaged side. "You've noticed. The sleep is getting deeper."

"I've noticed."

"It's how the court keeps its vessels. The soul thins, the body follows. If we don't return what's hers before the solstice—" Odile did not finish. She didn't need to. Felix had spent three weeks learning the grammar of fae bargains, and the sentence completed itself in their mind with terrible fluency.

Ilsa stood apart, facing downslope, watching the valley. She had not spoken since the flight began. Felix had seen her take two huntsmen's blades meant for Odile's throat, had seen her cut down a former brother-in-arms in the dark of the deep roads without hesitation and without joy, and now she stood very straight in her scorched uniform with her sword point-down in the earth like a man at a graveside, and said nothing at all.

"They'll know we're coming," Ilsa said finally. "Word travels faster than we do. By now every warden post between here and Vell has our names." A pause. "My name most of all."

"The passes," Felix said. "Can you get us through?"

Ilsa turned. In the bruised light her face looked older than it had a month ago, carved by things Felix didn't want to imagine her carrying. "There are no passes anymore, Galloway. There's a cordon. Captain Rennick has been building it since autumn—chain posts across every road, every ford, every goat track he can find. He calls it the Winter Net." Her mouth twisted. "He's been waiting for someone worth catching."

* * *

They traveled by night and hid by day, which was backwards from every sensible rule of the road, but the wardens patrolled the daylight hours in confident columns while the nights belonged to the desperate. Odile knew old hunting lodges, forgotten foresters' huts, a shrine to a god whose name had been outlawed alongside magic, where the roof still held and the well still gave sweet water. She moved through the wilderness like a woman reading a book she'd written and then sold—a fact she acknowledged once, bitterly, wrapping her hands around a fireless brazier of coals: "I paid for this knowledge too. Apparently I bought my way out of my past and into a map of it."

On the second night, huddled in the shrine while sleet ticked against the shutters, Felix took stock of what remained to them, the way they'd once taken stock of stolen goods laid out on a market blanket.

What they had: Wren, sleeping. The founder's vial, wrapped in wool at the bottom of Felix's pack—heavy beyond its size, cold beyond reason, humming faintly when Felix slept too near it, as if it dreamed. Odile's fragment of recovered memory, which had named the queen for what she was. Ilsa's knowledge of the cordon. And four days, shrinking.

What they did not have: any plan worth the name.

"We go back into the city," Felix said. "That's the start of it. Everything else follows."

Ilsa looked up sharply. "Vell is a trap. You know what's happened there since we left."

"I know what the wind brought us," Felix said. "Tell me the rest."

So Ilsa told it, in the flat voice of a soldier delivering a report about her own disgrace. The hunt had intensified—that was the polite word for it. While the three of them had been underground trading memories with monsters, the crown had doubled the pyres. Founder's Square burned twice a week now. The wardens had stopped pretending to distinguish between the gifted and the merely strange; a hedge-healer in Tanner's Row had been taken for curing a fever, a charm-seller from the night market for selling luck. Informants were paid in grain allotments. Neighbors watched neighbors. Children were taught rhymes about reporting whispers.

"And Rennick knows I'm turned," Ilsa finished. "There's a price on me now. Five hundred crowns." She almost smiled. "More than they ever offered for you, Galloway. I'm finally worth something."

"You were always worth something," Odile said, unexpectedly, and the silence that followed was stranger than any argument.

Felix stared into the dead brazier and felt the sand running. Four days. Three, now, by the time the sleet stopped. A city full of informants between them and the mountain gate beneath Vell. A sister sleeping toward dissolution. A vial that might be salvation or might be the queen's final snare—they still didn't know which, and there was no one left alive who did.

And underneath it all, quieter than the rest, a truth Felix kept turning over like a coin worn smooth: the plan required people. It required the hidden ones—the charm-sellers and hedge-healers and memory-thieves who had survived a generation of burnings by staying scattered, silent, alone. Felix had spent their whole life believing that invisibility was safety. Now they needed every invisible person in Vell to become visible at once, on the longest night of the year, in the shadow of the pyres.

It was the worst idea Felix had ever had, and they loved it the way you love anything that might save your sister.

* * *

They reached the outskirts of Vell on the third evening, and the city had changed.

Felix had grown up learning Vell the way other children learned prayers: every alley, every wall with a loose stone, every rooftop that ran clean from Copperlane to the river. They knew the city's moods the way sailors know weather. And the city's mood now was fear—not the sharp passing fear of a raid or a roundup, but the settled, structural fear of a place that had rearranged itself around terror and called the arrangement order.

Lanterns burned at every corner, fed by the city's oil ration, because darkness was where the gifted moved. Wardens walked in pairs, then in fours. On the walls above Founder's Square, Felix could see the scaffold of a new pyre already built, stacked and tarred and waiting, and beside it a second scaffold, and beside that a third. The crown was expecting a harvest.

Wren slept through her homecoming. Felix carried her through the drainage culvert below Tanner's Row—the same culvert they'd used at eleven years old to smuggle their first stolen memory out of a nobleman's house—and felt her breath against their neck, shallow and slow, slower than it had been in the mountains. Odile was right. The body was following the soul down.

"There's a cellar," Felix said, when they emerged into the tannery district's chemical dark. "Under the old chandlery. Marisol used it, before—" Before Marisol had been dragged through Founder's Square in chains while Felix watched from a crowd and did nothing. That neighbor. That pyre. The memory of it had been the coal under everything since.

The cellar was cold and smelled of tallow and years, but it was dry, and the door locked from the inside, and it was, for the moment, unwatched. They laid Wren down on a pallet of sacking. Felix lit one candle, no more, and sat beside her and held her hand, which was cool, cooler than it should have been, and counted her breaths the way you count coins you're afraid are short.

Thirty-two breaths to the minute, when she'd been forty in the mountains.

"Felix." Odile's voice at the foot of the stairs, soft. "Someone's been asking after you. Down at the night market. A boy—Tobin, the one who sells the dream-vials. He says a woman's been to three sellers looking for the Galloway charm. Describing it exactly. The blue wax, the pressed flower under the seal."

Felix went still. "A buyer?"

"A hunter," Odile said. "Or a baiter. Tobin wouldn't say who sent her. He wouldn't sell her the information, but he wouldn't swear he was the only one she asked." She paused. "The gifted are frightened, Felix. Frightened people sell things. You know that better than anyone—you made a living off it."

The words landed where they were meant to, and Felix let them, because flinching would cost time they didn't have. "Then we move faster than she does. Tonight. I need to see the faces I'm asking to risk their necks—all of them, before word spreads further. Can you walk?"

"I can walk."

"Good. Ilsa stays with Wren."

From the shadows by the pallet, Ilsa spoke for the first time in hours. "You're leaving your sister guarded by the woman whose signature put her family on a list fifteen years ago."

"Yes," Felix said.

"Why?"

Felix looked at Wren's sleeping face, at the candle flame doubling itself in her closed eyes, and found the answer had been sitting in them for days, waiting to be said aloud. "Because you came down into the dark to get her back. Because you bled for a woman you'd never met. Because guilt that only wants absolution runs away, but guilt that wants responsibility stays." They stood, and pulled their hood up, and became, in the space of a breath, the invisible thing they had spent a lifetime perfecting. "Stay. Watch her. Count her breaths. If they slow past twenty-five, wake me wherever I am, and I don't care what bargain breaks doing it."

* * *

The night market was a ghost of itself.

Felix remembered it loud—lantern-strung, bargaining, alive with the low commerce of small magics disguised as trinkets. Now the stalls huddled in the lee of the chandlery wall with their shutters half-closed, and the sellers worked in near-darkness, and conversation died in ripples as Felix passed, because everyone knew the Galloway silhouette, and everyone knew what it meant that the Galloway silhouette had come back from the dead with a hunted captain's cloak folded under one arm.

They gathered in the back of Tobin's stall, seven of them: Tobin himself, nineteen and terrified and trying to hide it behind his seller's grin; Mother Ashka, the hedge-healer, whose hands shook now when they hadn't a year ago; the Ferro twins, who read fortunes and had stopped reading their own; old Bramwen, who bottled sleep the way Felix bottled memory; and two others whose gifts Felix only guessed at. Seven. Out of dozens who had once worked these stalls. The rest had fled, or burned, or gone quiet in the way of people hoping to be overlooked.

Felix told them everything. Not the safe version—the whole of it. The court beneath the mountain. The souls in glass. Wren. The queen who was the founder, wearing centuries like a crown of weeping wood. The solstice, and what it would make her if they failed. And then the ask, which was the maddest part: that on solstice eve, while the whole city watched the pyres, the hidden ones would move—create diversions, open ways, hold doors, be seen for the first time in a generation.

Silence, afterward. Sleet ticked on the awning. Somewhere across the city, faint as a heartbeat, a bell rang the hour.

Mother Ashka spoke first. "If we're caught, we burn."

"If we do nothing," Felix said, "we burn anyway. Slower. One of us a month, one of us a season, until there's no one left to be invisible. I've watched it my whole life. So have you. I'm offering the only exit anyone's offered us in fifteen years, and yes, it goes through fire."

Bramwen laughed—a dry, awful sound. "The charm-seller leads a revolution. The wardens will die laughing."

"The wardens can wait to laugh," said one of the Ferro twins, and her sister nodded, and something moved through the little group like warmth through cold hands—not agreement, not yet, but the possibility of it, which was more than Felix had dared carry in.

It was Tobin who broke it. He had gone pale in a new way, and when he spoke his seller's grin was nowhere. "Felix. The woman. Looking for your charms. I lied to her—I said I'd never heard the name. But Denny Corve didn't lie." He swallowed. "Denny took her coin. Denny told her everything."

Felix felt the night tilt. Denny Corve. A hedge-charmer, minor talent, big debts, a man who'd wept at Marisol's burning and sworn he'd rather die than inform. "When?"

"Yesterday. Maybe the day before. He was drinking when she found him, and she paid in grain scrip, and everyone saw the scrip, Felix. Everyone knows what grain scrip means now."

Felix was already moving, mind running ahead down the streets, mapping routes, counting hours. Denny knew the stalls. Denny knew faces. Denny knew, if he'd talked long enough, about the chandlery cellar—because Denny had slept in it himself one frozen week last winter, when his landlord threw him out.

Twenty-five breaths. Twenty-five, and wake me wherever I am.

Felix ran.
