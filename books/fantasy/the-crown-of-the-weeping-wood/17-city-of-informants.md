## 17. City of Informants

Vell smelled of woodsmoke and fear.

Felix had been back in the city for two days, and both days the smell had grown stronger. It came from Founder's Square, where a second pyre had burned while they were still under the mountain, and from the tanneries, where the wardens had begun burning confiscated charms along with the hides—bottled memories popping like fat in the fire, little ghosts of other people's summers rising off the smoke. The city had always been a place of secrets. Now it was a place of sieges, and every secret was a weapon someone might use against you.

Felix moved through it all with their hood up and their hands in their pockets, feeling the weight of what they carried: the founder's vial, wrapped in wool and tucked against their ribs like a second heart; Odile's list of names, copied out in her precise hand; and beneath everything, the hollow place where the memory of their mother's face used to live. They had learned to stop reaching for it. Mostly.

The safehouse was a candlemaker's cellar off Tanner's Row, lent to them by a woman named Bess who sold luck-charms by day and hedge-blessings by night and had never once asked Felix what they bottled or where they got it. That was the old courtesy of Vell's hidden gifted: don't ask, don't tell, don't look too long at anyone's hands. For fifteen years that courtesy had kept them alive. Felix meant to break it tonight.

They had spent those two days doing the thing they were best at—stealing into lives, but gently now, leaving something behind instead of taking. A word in the ear of the herbwoman on Cordwainer Street. A note slipped beneath the door of the lamplighter who could make his flames burn blue when no one watched. A charm left on the sill of the midwife's window, one that held not a memory but an invitation, folded into light: *Tanner's Row, three bells after dark. If you want to stop hiding, come.*

* * *

By the third bell, eleven people had come.

Felix counted them from the top of the cellar stairs, and the counting hurt more than they expected. Eleven. In a city of forty thousand souls, eleven people willing to climb down into a candle-smelling dark because a stranger's charm told them it might be safe. And yet each face was a small miracle of courage—the herbwoman Magrit with her stained fingers and her chin up like she was daring the room to flinch; the lamplighter Oskar, young and freckled, still smelling of lamp oil; the midwife Sera, gray-haired, who had delivered half the wardens' children and knew exactly which of them would grow up to burn her. There was a ferryman whose charms kept the river ice from cracking under his clients' boats. A clerk who could find any lost document in the archive by asking the paper itself. Two brothers who mended shoes and mended hearts, though they'd deny the second trade to their graves. Others Felix knew only by reputation, faces from the edges of the market, the kind of people who had spent their whole lives being careful.

Eleven. Plus Bess, who stood by her own cold hearth with her arms crossed, plus Odile Marchand, who sat slightly apart in a plain gray dress with her hands folded, watching everyone with the particular attention of a woman who had no memories of her own and so studied everyone else's as if they might be hers. Ilsa Verrow was not there. She was keeping watch above, in the candle shop, wearing a cloak with her captain's insignia torn off, and Felix did not let themselves think about how much safer the room felt knowing she was there.

"Thank you for coming," Felix said, and their voice cracked on the second word, which was not how they had planned to begin. They started again. "Thank you for coming. Most of you don't know me. Some of you bought my charms and didn't know what you were buying. I'm not going to apologize for that, because apologizing is a luxury we've stopped being able to afford." They took a breath. "I'm going to tell you why you're here, and then some of you are going to leave, and that's fine. Leaving is allowed. What isn't allowed anymore is pretending."

They told them. Not everything—not the court beneath the mountain, not yet—but enough. The hunt. The pyres. The ledger their mentor had kept, the sales, the souls traded below like coin. Wren. The turning of the year, four days away now, and what would happen to Wren's soul—and what the queen beneath the mountain would become if it finished consuming her. And then the plan: that the gifted of Vell had hidden long enough, and that hiding was a slow way of dying, and that on solstice eve they would act—some of them above, to pull a condemned witch-hunter from a fire, and one of them below, to end a queen.

Silence followed, the thick kind, the kind with weight.

It was Magrit who broke it, arms folded now to match Bess. "You're asking us to burn so one girl can be saved."

"I'm asking you to decide whether you'd rather burn slowly or stand," Felix said. "Those are the choices. The crown has stopped distinguishing between charm-sellers and murderers. You've all seen the square."

"The square isn't the question." Magrit's voice was steady, but her hands weren't; Felix saw her thumb worrying a stain on her apron. "The question is whether one girl's soul is worth eleven lives. And I'll tell you what I told the last fool who came recruiting hope through my neighborhood: I have patients. People who will die this winter without me, quietly, illegally, in their beds, alive. If I stand up, they lie down. That's my arithmetic."

"And it's good arithmetic," Felix said. "I won't pretend otherwise. But there's another column you're not counting. After the solstice, if the queen gets what she wants, the hunts won't stop. They'll get worse, because the queen will need more souls, and the crown will need more favor, and the price of hiding will go up until none of us can pay it. You're not choosing between your patients and a stranger. You're choosing between your patients and everyone."

"That's a recruiter's arithmetic," said one of the cobblers, the elder brother, Tam. "Recruiters always find a bigger number to hide behind."

"Yes," Felix said simply. "Because the numbers are real. I'm not here to make you feel brave. I'm here because in four days, either we do this or we spend the rest of our short lives listening to the fires."

Odile spoke then, softly, from her corner. "There is a third option nobody likes. Do nothing, and hope the mountain forgets about Vell." She looked around the room. "I sold my entire past to escape a debt, and I can tell you from experience: the things you sell do not forget you. They wait. They compound interest. The court beneath the mountain has been patient for centuries. It will not stop being patient now."

A few heads turned toward her—a noblewoman, even disgraced, even memoryless, was still a creature from another country to most of them. But no one argued. Something in her flat, certain voice made argument seem beside the point.

Then Sera the midwife stood, slowly, one hand pressed to the small of her back. "I delivered a baby last month," she said, "in a room where the mother made me put out all the candles so no one would see me working. She was terrified of her own labor. Of me. Because I know things. Because I've always known things." She looked at Felix. "I'm sixty-one years old. I have been careful for fifty years, and careful has bought me a cellar practice and a life of putting out candles. I'll come. Not for your girl—I'm sorry, child, but grief for strangers is a rich person's luxury. I'll come because I am done delivering babies in the dark."

One by one after that, the answers came. Oskar the lamplighter, immediately, almost before Sera finished speaking—"I'm in, obviously, I've been waiting my whole stupid life for someone to ask"—which startled a broken laugh out of the ferryman, who then also said yes, quieter. The younger cobbler, Joss, yes; his brother Tam, a long silence and then a grudging, "Someone has to keep him alive," which everyone understood to mean yes. The clerk, yes. A quiet woman named Pell who read fortunes with cards that sometimes answered questions she hadn't asked, yes. Bess, who owned the cellar, said only, "You're standing in my answer."

In the end, seven said yes. Four said no.

Magrit was the first to leave, gathering her basket with dignity. At the foot of the stairs she paused. "If you fail," she said, without turning around, "they'll come for everyone whose name was in that room. Think about that. Think about whose names you're spending." Then she was gone, and the others who refused went after her in ones and twos, and the cellar felt larger and colder with each departure.

But seven remained, plus Odile, plus Bess, plus Felix, and Felix stood in the middle of the candle-warm dark and felt something they had no bottled equivalent for: not joy, exactly, and not safety, but the strange vertigo of belonging. Fifteen years of working alone, of being a ghost with excellent penmanship, and now here was a circle of people who knew what they were and had chosen, with open eyes, to stand near them anyway. It was terrifying. It was the best thing that had ever happened to them. Both things were true at once, and Felix understood, finally, why people risked so much for love—it wasn't despite the terror. It was through it.

They spent the next hour on details. Oskar would map the lantern routes around Founder's Square; the clerk, whose name was Anselm, would pull the execution order and learn the exact hour and the guard rotations; Pell would read the cards for timing, which she did right there, laying them out on a flour-dusted barrel, and frowned at what she found and said only, "There's a card missing from this spread. Someone in this room isn't telling us something." Everyone laughed a little too quickly, and the moment passed, and Felix thought no more about it, which later would feel like the great failure of their life.

* * *

They met again the following evening, and again the night after, and the plan grew flesh: which streets, which signals, who would hold which door. On the third night, two bells after dark, Felix climbed the stairs from the cellar to fetch more candles and found the shop empty.

Bess was upstairs in her rooms, unconscious, her tea untouched. Ilsa was gone.

There was no sign of struggle. That was the worst part—that Ilsa, who had disarmed three men in an alley once without spilling her wine, had apparently simply opened the door and walked out, or been walked out, quietly, between one bell and the next. On the table lay a single sheet of paper in a warden's neat bureaucratic hand:

*Captain Ilsa Verrow is detained on charges of treason against the Crown of Vell. Her execution is set for the solstice, Founder's Square, at the turning of the year.*

The same hour. The same square. Below the mountain, Wren's soul would be consumed at the very moment the fire caught above it, and whoever had arranged it had known exactly what they were doing—or had simply been cruel in the ordinary, efficient way of bureaucracies, which amounted to the same thing.

Felix stood holding the paper while the others came up the stairs behind them, drawn by the silence, and watched the news move through their faces one by one: Oskar's confusion, Sera's grim understanding, Anselm going pale and saying, "Only someone who knew the time and place could have—only someone *inside*—"

And then everyone looked at Pell, who was already looking at her cards, laid out on the barrel, the same spread as before, and her face had gone the color of tallow.

"The missing card," she whispered. "I found it. It was face-down under the table leg. It's the informant's card. It always was." She turned it over with one finger, and Felix looked at the painted figure and felt the bottom drop out of the night—because it was a card they knew, a card from their own stock, a fortune-teller's deck they had sold a version of years ago, and printed on its face, in the style of the old woodcuts, was a man with a basket of wares and his eyes closed.

The peddler. One of the four who had refused. One of the four who had heard every name, every street, every hour, and walked out into the dark with it all folded in his pocket like a charm he intended to sell.

Felix set the paper down very carefully, as if it might go off.

"They have Ilsa," they said. "And in two days, they'll have her on the pyre at the exact hour Wren dies. So we change nothing. We do everything. Both fires, one night." They looked around the circle of faces, frightened and resolute, and felt the belonging in their chest like a brand. "Starting now—we assume every wall in Vell has ears. From this moment, we speak in signs, we move separately, and we trust no one we haven't bled with. I'm sorry. I wanted to offer you something better than that."

Sera snorted. "Child, you offered us the first honest night in fifty years. Don't ruin it with apologies."

Outside, the wind came down off the mountain carrying ash, and somewhere across the dark city a bell began to ring—not the alarm, just the hour, ordinary and indifferent—and Felix Galloway stood in a candlemaker's shop surrounded by the first family they had ever chosen, and began, quietly, to plan a rescue and a descent and a war, all at once, in the space of two days.
