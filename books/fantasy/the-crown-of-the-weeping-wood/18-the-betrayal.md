## 18. The Betrayal

They came for her at dawn, and Ilsa Verrow was almost grateful.

She had been awake since the fourth bell, sitting at the plain table in the garrison quarters she'd occupied for nineteen years, dressed in her uniform with every button fastened, her sword laid across her knees. She had not slept. She had spent the night writing a confession she would never send—eleven pages detailing every order she had signed, every pyre she had kindled, every name she could still remember, which was all of them, because guilt kept its own meticulous ledger—and when the boots came pounding up the stairs, she set down the pen with something like relief.

The door burst inward. Four men in crown livery, not garrison grey. The Inquisitor's own. Behind them, pale and sweating in the corridor light, stood Warden Corvane's clerk, Tam Hesk, clutching a writ like it might bite him.

"Captain Verrow." Hesk's voice cracked on the honorific. He had been a boy when she'd first pinned on the silver badge; she remembered teaching him to ride. "By order of Lord Inquisitor Corvane, you are under arrest for high treason against the crown of Vell."

Ilsa looked at the four men. Young. Nervous. Their hands too near their hilts.

"You'll want to take the sword," she said. "I won't give you a reason to use it."

One of them stepped forward anyway, as if he hadn't heard, and wrenched it from her lap so roughly that his ears went red. Another bound her wrists—not tightly. She noticed that. She noticed everything now, cataloguing small mercies the way she had once catalogued small cruelties.

"The charge," Hesk said, reading from the writ because he could not bear to look at her face. "Consorting with known practitioners of forbidden arts. Aiding their flight from lawful authority. The informant has given testimony under seal."

"Name him," Ilsa said quietly.

Hesk flinched. "You know I can't—"

"Then say nothing at all, Tam. You've said enough." She rose. Her knees ached; she was forty-one years old and she felt every one of them standing up. "Lead on."

As they marched her through the garrison yard, past the training posts where she had drilled recruits for two decades, past the armory where her name was still stenciled on a equipment locker, the soldiers stopped what they were doing to watch. None of them spoke. One—a girl barely seventeen, whom Ilsa had pulled from a beating behind the tanneries two winters ago—began to cry, and turned away to hide it.

*This is what remains,* Ilsa thought. *Not the executions. Not the orders signed in another woman's hand while mine shook above the ink. This. A child weeping into her sleeve because the monster who was kind to her turned out to be a traitor after all.*

She almost laughed. It would have been an ugly sound.

* * *

They held her in the cells beneath Founder's Square—the same cells, she realized with a cold settling in her stomach, where the gifted waited before their burnings. The irony was not lost on her. Corvane, if anything, appreciated symmetry.

He came at midday, alone, dismissing the guards with a flick of two fingers. He looked older than he had a month ago. The hunt was eating him alive, though whether from conscience or appetite, Ilsa could no longer tell.

"Ilsa." He sat across from her cell, on the stool the guards used, and folded his hands. For a long moment neither spoke. Through the barred window, high and narrow, she could see a slice of sky going white with cloud. "Give me the names."

"There are no names to give."

"Fourteen practitioners sheltered in the Tanner's Row warrens. A memory-thief who calls himself Galloway. Give them to me, and this becomes what it should have been all along—an administrative matter. A captain misled by a clever criminal. You'd lose your commission. Nothing more."

"And if I refuse?"

Corvane sighed, and it was almost tender, and that tenderness frightened her more than any threat. "Then at the turning of the year, you burn in Founder's Square beside whoever else we've gathered by then. And I will have to write the proclamation myself, and put your name in it, and read it aloud, because that is my office now. That is what I am." His hands tightened in his lap. "Ilsa. Fifteen years ago I watched you sign an order you didn't believe in, because they told you the alternative was worse. I stood next to you. I let you carry it. Don't make me watch you do it twice."

The words landed exactly where he meant them to. She let him see that they had.

"Here is what I remember about that day," she said at last. "I signed, and the family was taken, and there was a little girl. Wren, her name was. She had a ribbon in her hair, blue, and she waved at me from the cart because she thought the silver badge meant I was there to help her." Her voice did not shake. She had practiced this steadiness for fifteen years; it was the only craft she had left. "I have been paying for that ribbon ever since, Corvane. Every pyre. Every name. And it has never once been enough, because it was never meant to be payment. It was meant to be forgetting. And I find, sitting here, that I don't want to forget anymore."

Corvane studied her for a long time. When he stood, he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with age.

"At the solstice," he said. "Dawn. Same square. The crown announces it tonight." At the door he paused, his back to her. "For what it's worth—I'm sorry it came to this."

"For what it's worth," Ilsa said, "so am I. But not for the reason you think."

* * *

The proclamation went up at dusk, nailed to the doors of every guildhall and cried at every corner by criers whose voices cracked on the cold. Ilsa heard it through her window, faint and formal:

*Let it be known that Captain Ilsa Verrow, having consorted with practitioners of forbidden arts against the peace of the crown, is stripped of rank and title, and shall suffer death by fire at the turning of the year, at dawn, in Founder's Square, that all may witness the fate of those who betray their city.*

At dawn. The same hour the mountain would finish consuming Wren Galloway's soul. The same hour the queen beneath the world would become unkillable, crowned and complete, forever.

Ilsa sat on the cell floor with her back to the stone and worked it through, the way she'd once worked through battle plans, coolly, completely, hating every step of the arithmetic.

Felix Galloway could not be in two places. That was the whole of it. The gifted of Tanner's Row were brave but untrained, soft-hearted, half of them terrified of their own shadows' tricks. They could not storm a pyre guarded by forty crown soldiers without Felix to lead them, and Felix would not lead them anywhere except toward her, because Felix was the sort of fool who repaid betrayal with rescue. Ilsa had arrested them once, held a blade to their throat, bargained with their freedom like a fishwife—and still they had walked beside her under the roots of the world.

So the choice would come to Felix, and it would be no choice at all, unless Ilsa made it one.

*If you come for me,* she thought, pressing her forehead against the cold bars, *Wren dies, and the queen wins, and everything we bled for under the mountain is ash. If you save the girl, I die, and I die clean—cleaner than I've been in fifteen years.* She closed her eyes. *Don't you dare waste your grief on me, Galloway. Spend it where it buys something.*

And yet—even now, even resolved—some small and shameful part of her listened for footsteps on the stairs. Some part of her, the part that had wept over the ribbon and the cart and the little girl's waving hand, wanted desperately to be saved, wanted someone to choose her, just once, just this once, after a lifetime of being the one who chose who lived.

She found the feeling, examined it, and set it down like a weapon surrendered at a checkpoint.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty dark. "You don't get to want that. You gave that up. You gave it up the day you signed."

But wanting, she discovered, does not obey orders. It never had. That was the whole disease of it—that was why the crown burned witches, maybe: not because magic was lawless, but because wanting things was, and the gifted were simply people who had stopped pretending otherwise.

Somewhere above her, Vell was lighting its lamps. Somewhere across the city, Felix Galloway was hearing the criers, doing the same terrible arithmetic she had just finished. Somewhere beneath the mountain, a girl who remembered nothing hummed a song to a queen wearing a stolen crown.

Ilsa lay back on the bare boards, folded her hands over her breast the way the dead were laid out, and closed her eyes.

"Choose the girl," she whispered, to no one, to everyone, to the slice of white sky beyond the window. "Choose her, and live, and plant something in the ground where I burned."

The lock on the stairwell door rattled. Boots—two pairs, moving quietly, wrong for guards, who never bothered to be quiet down here.

Ilsa opened her eyes and sat up slowly, heart hammering despite everything, despite the resolution she'd built brick by brick through the long dusk.

Footsteps stopped outside her cell. A shadow fell across the floor, thin and familiar, smelling faintly of lamp oil and bottled memories.

"Felix," she breathed. "No. *No.* What are you—"

A key scraped in the lock. The door swung open, and Felix Galloway stood in the torchlight, hollow-eyed, snow in their hair, holding a second key and a terrible expression Ilsa knew already, with dread, was the beginning of an argument she could not afford to win.

"I heard the proclamation," Felix said. "We need to talk about the solstice. Because I've figured out how to do both—and it's going to cost us everything."
