## 19. Two Fires, One Night

The solstice came down on Vell like a lid.

Felix felt it in the hours before dawn, standing at the window of the safehouse above the tannery row, watching snow fall through the light of a hundred bonfires. The city had never burned so bright on the longest night. Every square had its pyre stacked and waiting; every street its wardens with their iron lanterns; every window a family pretending not to watch. The crown wanted a spectacle. The crown wanted the gifted to see what the turning of the year meant now — that when the old sun died and the new one was born, so would they.

Below, in the tannery's reeking yard, the hidden gifted of Vell were gathering for the last time.

Felix counted them as they came. Old Maren Kost, the hedge-healer whose hands smelled of yarrow and whose cures had saved half the dockside children from fever. The twins, Anselm and Petra, who could whisper to locks until the locks whispered back. A boy of maybe fourteen called Sparrow, who could make himself forgotten mid-sentence, a gift so small it had kept him alive precisely because no one remembered he had it. Forty-one souls in all. Felix had spent three weeks finding them, knocking on doors that had stayed shut for fifteen years, speaking the old cant in market alleys, leaving bottled memories on windowsills like calling cards. Some had refused. One — a charm-seller named Doss, who had once been a friend of their mentor — had smiled too warmly and asked too many questions, and Felix had known, the way you know a counterfeit coin by its weight, that Doss would sell them all before the week was out.

They had been right. That was how Ilsa was in a cell beneath the Warden's Hall now, and how her execution was posted on every corner: *Captain Ilsa Verrow, traitor to the crown, to burn at Founder's Square at the hour of the solstice dawn.*

Two fires. One night. And only enough of them to save one.

* * *

The argument had gone on until the candles drowned in their own wax.

"We split," Odile had said, and she said it the way she said everything now — flat, certain, a woman who had learned that hesitation was just another word for loss. She stood at the head of the trestle table with the founder's vial before her, cradled in a nest of black wool. Inside it, a light moved like a trapped moth: the soul of Aldous Vane, first lord of Vell, stolen from his own monument four hundred years ago and carried down into the dark. "The queen expects us to come for Wren. So we come for Wren. But she also expects us to be together, because together is how we've survived everything else. So we are not together."

"And Ilsa?" Sparrow had asked, small voice in the big room. "She burns at dawn."

Everyone had looked at Felix then. They always did now, and Felix still hadn't gotten used to it — had spent a whole life being invisible and had become, in the space of a season, the axis everything turned on. It sat wrong, like a coat cut for someone else.

"Ilsa knew the risk," Felix had said, and hated the words even as they left their mouth, because they were true and true things can still be cruel. "She told me herself. If it comes to choosing—"

"She offered her life," Odile said. "That doesn't mean we spend it."

"No." Felix had closed their hand around the vial, felt the light inside flutter against their palm like a heartbeat. "It means she gave us a choice, and we have to make it. Marenthe consumes Wren's soul at the turning — the exact hour the new year turns, when the veil between what is given and what is taken wears thin. If we're not there, if we're not *in time*, then Wren is gone forever and the queen becomes something no one can kill. Ever. Not next year. Not in a hundred years. Ilsa burns at dawn. Wren burns at midnight. We cannot be in both places."

"So we split," Odile said again. "Some go to the square. Some go under the mountain."

"I go alone."

"Felix—"

"Alone." They had said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "The mountain gate takes payment. It took my mother's face. It will take something else from anyone who passes, and I won't ask any of you to pay that twice. And the vial—" they lifted it, let the light show through the glass, "—this has to reach the vault. The vault answers to memory, and memory is mine. You'd be carrying a lantern into a place that eats lanterns."

Maren Kost had spoken up then, her voice like dry leaves. "And who frees the hunter?"

Felix had looked around the room at forty-one faces, most of them frightened, all of them present, and understood that this — not the mountain, not the queen — was the miracle. Fifteen years of hiding, and here they were, in a tannery yard, about to do the one thing the crown had made unthinkable: act together.

"Whoever wants to," Felix had said. "That's all it's ever going to take. Whoever wants to."

* * *

They divided in the yard as the snow thickened.

Twenty-two chose the square. Maren Kost led them, because healers know fire better than anyone — how it takes, how it can be cheated. Petra and Anselm went with them, and Sparrow, though Felix tried to talk him out of it; he only grinned with the gap in his teeth and said, "No one remembers me anyway. Best man for walking into a crowd that's trying to forget." Nineteen stayed behind to hold the safehouse, to keep the roads open, to be the door the others could come home through.

Odile stood apart, wrapped in a plain gray cloak that made her look like what she was: a woman with nothing but her name and her nerve. She had argued, at the end, to come with Felix. She had lost the argument, and she took it the way she took everything, without complaint and without forgiveness.

"You'll need this more than I will," she said, pressing something into Felix's hand. A small bottle, stoppered with wax, holding a curl of gray mist. "My fragment. The founder's memory — the piece I recovered. When you face her, break it near her. Let her remember being human. It may buy you the moment you need."

"It might kill you to give it up."

"I've got nothing left to kill." Her mouth twitched, almost a smile. "Come back, Galloway. Come back and tell me what I was."

Then she hugged them — quick, fierce, over before Felix could respond — and walked away into the snow toward Founder's Square, toward the fire she intended to stand in front of.

Felix watched her go. Then they shouldered their pack — the vial, the last of their bottled charms, a knife, bread, the childhood song folded up inside them where no one could steal it — and turned toward the mountain.

Behind them, the bells of Vell began to ring the old year down.

* * *

The road under the roots was harder in winter.

Felix climbed through the pass with the wind tearing at them sideways, snow filling their tracks almost as fast as they made them, and the whole way down the far side they could feel the mountain noticing them — a pressure like a hand pressed flat between the shoulder blades, patient and vast. The gate stood where it had always stood, a seam of darkness in the cliff face framed by roots older than the city, and when Felix reached it, the darkness spoke.

*Payment.*

"I know." Felix knelt in the snow and opened themselves the way they'd been taught, the way they'd taught others: hold a memory up to the threshold and let it go. Last time it had taken their mother's face, and Felix had grieved for her twice, once when she died and once at this door.

This time the mountain reached deeper. It sifted through them like fingers through a coin purse, considering, discarding — the taste of the night market, the smell of the tannery yard, Odile's fierce brief hug — and then it found what it wanted and pulled.

Felix gasped. Something went out of them that they couldn't name, couldn't picture, could only feel as an absence shaped exactly like joy. They knelt there in the snow for a long moment, cataloguing their own self like a merchant taking stock after a robbery, and found one thing missing among many present: they could no longer remember why the night market had ever mattered to them. Not the selling, not the charms, not the small warm commerce of strangers' happiness. Only that it had. Only that some version of them had loved it, and that version was now a stranger reported secondhand.

"Thief," Felix told the mountain, and their voice cracked in the middle of the word.

The gate opened anyway.

* * *

The court beneath had dressed itself for the solstice.

The caverns blazed. Chandeliers of living crystal dripped light like slow honey onto floors of polished obsidian, and everywhere the fae nobility glittered in their jewelry of surrendered years — necklaces of a fisherman's last summer, rings set with a child's first snowfall, brooches that held whole decades and threw off little colored ghosts of feeling as their wearers passed. Music played from instruments that had no players. Servants glided with trays of wine that tasted, Felix knew, of whatever the drinker most regretted.

Felix moved through it wearing a stolen face — a minor gift, quickly bought from a dying hedge-witch years ago and hoarded ever since for exactly one night such as this — and a servant's livery lifted from a laundry line in the outer galleries. No one looked at servants. Invisibility, Felix thought, was the one magic the crown had never managed to outlaw.

The vault lay below the throne hall, past guards whose armor was grown, not forged, and Felix had planned for the guards: a bottle of borrowed sleep, decanted into the brazier smoke that drifted through the lower corridors. What Felix had not planned for was the queen.

She was waiting at the vault door.

Queen Marenthe stood alone in the corridor, unguarded, unarmored, wearing a gown the color of deep water and the crown that was not a crown at all — a circlet of living wood, silver-barked, hung with buds that wept slow beads of amber sap. The weeping wood. The founder's grief, worn as ornament for four hundred years. Her face was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, and her eyes, when they found Felix's stolen face, saw straight through it.

"There you are," she said warmly. "I wondered which door you'd choose. Come. The vault is cold, and you've had such a long climb."

Every instinct screamed. Felix's hand found the knife inside their coat, and the queen laughed — a real laugh, delighted, terrible.

"Oh, child. Keep it. You'll want it in a moment, I think." She turned and laid her long white hand on the vault door, and it swung open onto darkness full of drifting lights. "But first, let me show you what you came to steal."

* * *

The vault was a cathedral of glass.

Ten thousand vials on ten thousand shelves, each holding a soul rendered down to light, and the light breathed. The whole room pulsed faintly, slowly, like the chest of a sleeping giant, and Felix stood in the doorway drowning in the enormity of it — every vial a person, every shelf a neighborhood, centuries of Vell's surrendered dead and sold living glowing in the dark.

Wren's vial sat on a pedestal at the room's center, alone, honored, half its light already drunk away. Beside it, exactly where the ledger had promised, a smaller vial labeled in faded gold: *ALDOUS VANE, WHO FOUNDED AND WAS FORSAKEN.*

And beside *that*, waiting on a velvet cushion as if it had always been meant for Felix's hand, lay a knife of black glass.

"The bargain stands," the queen said from the doorway, her voice soft as sap dripping. "You brought me the founder's light. Give it here, willingly, and walk out with your sister's soul entire. Or—" she gestured, graceful, indifferent, at the black glass knife, "—take her by force, and learn what four hundred years of practice does to a thief who thinks theft is a weapon. Choose quickly, little Galloway. The year is turning, and I am *so hungry*."

Above them, miles up, through miles of stone and snow, Felix imagined they could hear the bells reaching the final hour — and beneath the bells, fainter, a different sound: shouting, and the roar of a crowd, and the crackle of a fire catching in Founder's Square.

Two fires. One night. And Felix Galloway standing in the dark between them, with a vial of a dead king's soul in one pocket, a dead woman's memory in the other, and no plan left but the truth.

Their hand closed around Odile's little bottle of gray mist.

"Well," Felix said, to the queen, to the vault, to the whole hungry dark. "Since you asked so nicely."

They threw the bottle down and broke the founder's memory at her feet.
