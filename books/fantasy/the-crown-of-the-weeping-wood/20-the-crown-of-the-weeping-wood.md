## 20. The Crown of the Weeping Wood

The doors at the end of the last corridor were made of living trees.

Felix had walked through the deep roads for what felt like a season—through halls of salt and halls of glass, through chambers where the walls breathed in slow rhythms like sleeping lungs—but nothing had prepared them for this. The doors were white birch, slender trunks grown side by side, their bark peeling in curling ribbons, and from every branch hung small drops of clear liquid that fell without ever striking the ground. They simply rose again, weeping upward, as though the wood grieved in reverse.

Felix stood before them a long time, one hand pressed to their ribs where Odile's blood still stained their shirt. Behind them, far down the corridor, the sounds of pursuit had faded—or been called off. That was worse. The queen's huntsmen did not give up; they were *summoned away*, which meant she wanted Felix to come to her.

"Of course you do," Felix whispered to the weeping doors. "You've wanted that since I set foot under this mountain."

They thought of Wren, hollow-eyed in her chamber of moss and candlelight, humming a song she no longer knew the words to. They thought of Ilsa, somewhere above or below—the court's geography refused to hold still—held in whatever gentle captivity Marenthe kept for useful enemies. They thought of the vial in their coat pocket, cold as a held breath: a sliver of glass labeled with a name five hundred years dead. *Aurelian Vell.* The founder. The first stolen soul.

The doors opened without being touched.

* * *

Beyond them was a grove.

That was the only word for it—a forest growing in the heart of the mountain, lit from nowhere and everywhere by a pale light like the hour just before dawn. Birch and rowan and blackthorn stood in ranks, and between their roots ran streams so clear they seemed not to exist, water moving over stone without sound. And in the center of it all, on a throne grown from a single tree that had been bent and braided into the shape of a seat over centuries, sat Queen Marenthe.

She wore a crown of the same weeping wood. Thin branches circled her brow, and the tears rose from them endlessly, hovering around her head like a constellation of small griefs. She was beautiful in the way old things are beautiful—not smooth, but *deep*. Her face was lined like riverbeds. Her eyes were gray as the sky over Vell in November, and Felix recognized that gray. It was the color of Founder's Square at morning, when the market stalls were being raised and the city smelled of bread and coal smoke and rain on old stone.

"You came the long way," the queen said. Her voice was warm. That was the horror of it—it was genuinely warm, the way a hearth is warm, with no performance in it at all. "Most who come to me arrive by invitation. You broke in, bled your friends, and burned half my vault." She tilted her head. "I find I admire it. It takes a certain kind of thief to steal from a queen."

"I'm not here to steal from you." Felix's voice came out steadier than they felt. They walked forward across the moss, boots silent, the grove watching them with a thousand wooden faces. "I'm here to take something back."

"Wren." The queen said the name softly, tasting it. "Yes. I know why you've come. I have known since the night you read your mentor's ledger and wept into his coat." She smiled, and it was a mother's smile, and Felix hated how much of them wanted to believe it. "Sit, Felix Galloway. Not on the throne—I would not insult you so. Here."

She gestured, and a low seat of woven branches rose from the moss beside her dais. An audience seat. A supplicant's seat. Felix did not sit. They stopped three paces away instead, close enough to see that the queen's gown was embroidered with tiny figures—mortals, hundreds of them, stitched in thread the color of dawn, each one caught mid-motion. Dancing. Kneeling. Burning.

"Do you know what I am?" Marenthe asked.

"A thief," Felix said. "An old one. Older than me."

"I am a memory." The tears rose around her crown, catching the sourceless light. "Five hundred years ago there was a woman named Aurelian Vell, who built a city on a river because she dreamed of a place where strange children would be safe. Do you know what happened to her dream? Within two generations of her death, her city outlawed the very people it was built for. Her name was put on coins and proclamations and witch-hunter commissions. They made her into a *brand*, Felix. A stamp of authority. Everything she loved, wearing her face like a mask." Her hand rose slowly to touch the weeping crown. "And then, a century ago, someone found the last remaining record of what she actually looked like, what she actually *felt*—a memory, preserved in glass, sold by her own descendant to pay a gambling debt—and the fae court bought it. And poured it into a vessel. And woke it up."

"And it became you."

"It became me." She said it without shame. "I am everything Aurelian was—the love, the grief, the fury, the hope—all of it, alive again. But a soul cannot live on itself alone. A flame needs fuel. And the only fuel a soul burns is..." She spread her hands, and Felix understood, with a lurch, what the whole glittering court was. Every jewel on every noble's throat. Every vial in the vault. The surrendered years of mortals, harvested and worn.

"Wren," Felix said again. Their throat was tight. "You're consuming her. Fifteen years, and she's half gone already. By solstice she'll be nothing but light in a bottle, and you'll be—" They stopped.

"Unkillable," Marenthe finished gently. "Whole. Eternal. Yes."

* * *

"Why her?" The question tore out of Felix before they could dress it up. "There are thousands of souls down here. Thousands. Why did you need my sister?"

The queen regarded them for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—something old and tired and terribly human.

"Because of what she is," she said at last. "Your sister was born gifted, Felix, as you were. But her gift—do you even know what it was? You were six when she was taken. You barely remember."

"She could make things grow," Felix said. "Mama said flowers turned toward her."

"Flowers turned toward her because she *gave*." Marenthe leaned forward, and the tears of her crown trembled. "Every gift costs the giver something, Felix. Yours takes memories from others and leaves you hungry. Your friend the huntress pays in sleepless nights. The Lady Marchand paid with her entire past. But your sister—your sister's gift gave *hope*. Living, breathing hope, drawn out of herself like honey from a comb. Do you understand how rare that is? How *potent*? One year of Wren Galloway's surrendered hope is worth fifty years of any ordinary mortal's fear or longing." Her voice softened. "I have been feeding on her for fifteen years, and she is still half-alive. No other soul in this court has lasted four."

Felix's hands had curled into fists at their sides. "So you'll drain her dry at solstice."

"At the turning of the year, the veil thins and the consumption completes cleanly—no residue, no waste. It is not cruelty, Felix. It is *harvest*. The farmer does not hate the wheat." She rose from her throne, and up close she was taller than Felix expected, and older, and the lines of her face rearranged themselves into expressions that flickered like weather. "But."

"But."

"But I am offering you a choice." She descended the three steps of the dais and stood before Felix, close enough that they could smell the grove on her—rain, sap, woodsmoke, the smell of the Weeping Wood before the city paved over its edge. "I did not summon my huntsmen off your trail by accident. I let you reach the vault. I let you find the founder's vial. I let you find *her*. Because I wanted you to see everything clearly before I made my offer. I am not my nobles, Felix. I do not deal in tricks. My bargains are honest—which makes them more dangerous than any trick ever forged."

"The offer," Felix said flatly.

"Wren's freedom. Whole. Untouched. Her fifteen years restored, her body and soul rejoined, and both of you carried to the surface tonight, past every warden, past every pyre, safe forever." The queen's gray eyes held theirs. "In exchange, you surrender her willingly. You sign the release. You tell me—truly, freely—that you give your sister to the court."

The grove was silent except for the rising tears.

"That's not a bargain," Felix managed. "That's just taking her."

"No." Marenthe shook her head, and the crown wept faster. "A taken soul curdles, Felix. Resists. Burns bitter and gives little light. But a soul given *willingly*, by the one person whose surrender means the most to her—oh." Her breath caught, genuine, awful. "Oh, what she'd burn like then. Hope freely yielded by her own beloved brother. She would sustain me for another century at least. Perhaps two. Long enough to finish what Aurelian started. Long enough to go back up that mountain and remake Vell into the city she dreamed—no huntsmen, no pyres, magic lawful, strange children safe." Her voice dropped to something almost tender. "Everything you have wanted since you were six years old and watched soldiers carry your sister away while you hid in a cupboard and did *nothing*. I will give it all to you. The city saved. The hunts ended. Your sister's sacrifice turned into a kingdom's redemption. All you must do is say the words."

* * *

And the terrible thing—the thing Felix would never be able to explain to anyone, not to Ilsa, not to Odile, not even to themselves—was how much of them *wanted to say them*.

It wasn't even the bargain. It was the permission. All these years, Felix had carried the cupboard like a stone in the chest: the six-year-old who heard the door break and stayed hidden, who survived because they hid, who built a whole self out of hiding—out of bottling other people's brightest moments and selling them cheap, out of being nobody, out of *invisibility*. And here was a queen offering to transmute that shame into meaning. Wren wouldn't have died for nothing. Felix hadn't failed her; they'd been waiting, all along, for this moment of cosmic bookkeeping where the loss would finally balance.

*Mama's face,* Felix thought, touching the empty place in their own memory where the toll of the gate had taken it. *I already paid once. What's one more payment?*

"You're crying," the queen observed, not unkindly.

"Am I." Felix wiped their cheek with the back of their wrist. Their hand, they noticed distantly, had drifted toward their coat pocket. Toward the vial. "Let's say I believed you. Let's say I said the words. What happens to the city after a century of you? You feed on surrendered souls, Majesty. Where does Vell come up with a century's worth of surrenders? Does everyone get a fair price? Or does the court beneath the mountain just... keep the ledger running?"

For the first time, the queen hesitated.

It was a small hesitation—a blink, a shift of weight—but Felix had spent their whole life reading the moment a mark realized the con had a second layer, and they saw it now, plain as a crack in glass.

"There would be adjustments," Marenthe said. "Trade would need to continue. Memory, years, small surrenders—voluntary ones, fairly compensated. The city would prosper. Most would never feel the cost."

"*Most.*"

"Felix." She reached out and took their chin in cool fingers, tilting their face up, and her eyes were so gray and so full of Aurelian's ancient, exhausted love that Felix's knees nearly buckled. "Child. I have worn this crown for a hundred years. Do you know what I weep for?" The tears around her head swirled closer, and Felix saw, suddenly, that each drop held an image—a street, a face, a festival day, the founding of the city, all of it, all of her, rising and falling and never landing. "I weep for everyone I have ever consumed to stay alive. Every one. I feel them going. I remember them better than they remembered themselves. And I have decided, over a hundred years of weeping, that the alternative—letting the dream die, letting the city rot under hunters and crowns—is *worse*. Someone must be the firewood. Better I carry the guilt than the dark win."

"That's not a queen talking," Felix whispered. "That's an addiction talking."

The fingers on their chin tightened, very slightly. "Careful."

"No." Felix's heart was slamming against their ribs, but underneath the fear was something clean and cold and finally, *finally* certain. "You want my surrender because a willing soul burns brighter. Fine. Then hear me say true things, Majesty, since you claim to deal honestly. You are not Aurelian Vell. You're what's left when a city eats its founder and calls the meal 'heritage.' She built a refuge for children like me and my sister. You run a vault full of them in bottles. And if I gave you Wren—if I signed her away so you could wear her hope like a crown—you wouldn't save Vell. You'd just be the most comfortable monster in it, and I'd be the one who fed you."

The grove went still. Even the tears stopped rising.

For a heartbeat Felix was sure the huntsmen would come pouring from between the birches, and they gripped the vial in their pocket and braced to spend their life buying three seconds of escape—

But Marenthe only stepped back. And laughed.

It was a low, wondering laugh, and it cracked something open in her face—the mask of the merciful mother slipping, showing briefly the thing beneath: vast, weary, hungry, *old*.

"Ohh," she breathed. "There it is. There's the spine. Do you know how long it's been since anyone told me the truth to my face? A hundred years of nobles bowing and mortals begging and every soul coming to me pre-broken." Her eyes gleamed. "You would have been magnificent, Felix Galloway. Which is exactly why I cannot let you leave with her."

"Then we do this the hard way."

"We do it the only way it was always going to go." The queen lifted one hand, and the grove answered—roots shifting underfoot, branches lowering, the whole living cathedral of the Weeping Wood turning its attention upon Felix like a single enormous eye. "You have the founder's vial. Shatter it, and you unmake me—release the original soul, scatter everything I am, collapse this court to bedrock. But a released soul does not simply *vanish*, child. Five hundred years of Aurelian, and fifteen years of your sister tangled in me like a root through stone. Break the vessel carelessly and Wren goes with me. Break it without something to anchor her—" she smiled, and it was almost pitying, "—and you lose her twice. So. Go on, little thief. Show me what you're willing to pay."

Felix drew out the vial.

It caught the pale light and threw it back in fragments, and inside it something stirred—something that had waited a hundred years in the dark, dreaming of a city on a river.

Behind the queen, far down among the birches, a girl in servant's gray stood motionless, watching, her empty eyes fixed on Felix with the faintest flicker of recognition. Her lips were moving. Humming.

Felix closed their hand around the glass until it bit.

"All right," they said quietly. "Here's my price."
