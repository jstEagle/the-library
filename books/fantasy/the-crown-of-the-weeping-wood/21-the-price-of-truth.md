## 21. The Price of Truth

The court's heart was a cavern no mason had ever touched. The walls were root and stone braided together, and from the ceiling hung the weeping wood—a tree grown upside down, its pale branches trailing downward like a woman's hair underwater, and from every branch fell slow silver drops that never reached the floor. They fell into the queen's crown instead. Marenthe wore the tree itself, or it wore her; the living wood had grown through her hair, through her scalp, down along her jaw, so that she seemed less a woman wearing a crown than a tree that had learned to smile.

Felix stood at the center of the cavern with the founder's vial cupped in both hands.

It was small. That was the terrible thing about it. Fifteen years of Wren's soul had been poured into a vessel the size of a thumb, and beside it in the vault had sat this other vial—older, colder, its glass fogged from within as though something inside were still breathing—and on its wax seal, pressed deep by hands long dead, the mark of Vell: the mountain, the river, the seven stars of the founding.

*Alaric Vane,* the ledger had said. *Founder. First payment.*

The first soul the court beneath the mountain had ever taken.

"You hold my beginning," Marenthe said. Her voice was warm the way a hearth is warm when the house around it is burning. She did not approach. She didn't need to; the roots moved for her, the whole cavern leaning in to listen. "Three hundred years ago I was a man who loved a city too much. Do you know what that costs, little thief? To love something so completely that you would sell anything to keep it safe? I sold myself. And they kept me. They have kept me ever since, drop by drop, year by year, until there was so little of Alaric Vane left that what remained forgot it had ever been a man at all." She smiled, and the weeping wood shivered, shedding silver. "I could have been bitter. Instead I became kind. You've seen my kindness."

"I've seen your cages," Felix said.

"I prefer *vessels*. Nothing is wasted here, Felix Galloway. Not grief, not memory, not love. Above ground your kind throws away everything precious because it hurts to carry. Down here we keep it all." Her eyes went past Felix, to where Wren stood at the edge of the light—hollow-eyed, humming, her hands folded like a good servant's. "Your sister has been a comfort to me. She sings when she thinks no one listens. Songs from a childhood I never gave her. Isn't that remarkable? The body remembers what the soul was made to forget."

Felix's hands shook. The vial trembled against their palms, and inside it the fog swirled, restless, like a sleeper turning toward a voice.

Behind them, somewhere up the ruined stair, Odile was holding the passage open with bloodied fingers and a will that had outlasted her own history. Somewhere above Vell, Ilsa Verrow was standing on a pyre built for two fires. There was no time. There had never been time; there had only ever been the choosing.

"What do you want," Felix said. Not a question. A door held open.

Marenthe descended from her throne of roots, and the tree came with her, dragging its silver rain across the floor. Up close she smelled of cold iron and honey. "I want what I have always wanted," she said gently. "To be finished. Your sister's soul completes me at the turning of the year—one more season of her, distilled, and I will be beyond unmaking. But I find—" and here her voice softened into something almost human, almost wounded— "I find I am tired of being almost. So. An offer, thief, and I offer it once."

She raised one hand, and the air between them filled with light.

Felix saw it all at once. Saw Wren waking whole, laughing, older than she should be but alive, alive, alive. Saw themselves back in Vell with coin enough to buy the whole night market, respected, untouchable, the wardens' ledgers burned, the pyres cold forever. Saw their mother's face—the one they'd paid at the gate—restored, smiling down at them the way she had before the fever, before everything. Saw a life with no holes in it. Every stolen thing returned. Every debt forgiven.

"All of it," Marenthe whispered. "Yours. Hers. Everything the court has ever taken from your family, given back with interest. You need only do what you have always done best." Her smile widened, and the tree wept faster. "Give me Wren willingly. Sign her over with open eyes and an open hand. A willing surrender is worth ten thousand taken ones. It will finish me tonight, and you will walk out rich in every way that matters."

The vision held. Felix let themselves look at it—really look, the way they'd once looked through other people's memories, greedy and grieving at once. Their mother's face. Wren's laugh. A city that didn't burn its children.

And underneath the vision, quiet as a coin dropped in a well, they heard the truth they had spent fifteen years refusing to hear:

*This is what you are. This is what you've always been. Someone who takes the beautiful thing and keeps it, because keeping is easier than losing.*

They had bottled strangers' joy and called it survival. They had hidden their gift while neighbors burned. They had crossed a mountain and paid a mother's face and told themselves it was all for Wren—but some small honest corner of them had known, since the vault, since the moment they saw the vial labeled with the founder's name, how this would end. Because there was only one thing in all the world that Marenthe feared, and it wasn't a thief with a knife.

It was the first soul. The original. The woman-shaped wound at the center of her crown.

"Here is my counter-offer," Felix said.

Their voice didn't shake. That surprised them. Fifteen years of shaking, and now, at the bottom of everything, stillness.

"You want a willing surrender," they said. "Fine. Here's mine." They lifted the vial higher, into the silver rain, where the light caught it and turned it to a small captive star. "Not Wren. *You.*"

Marenthe went very still. Even the weeping wood stopped weeping.

"That vessel," Felix said, "holds the soul you were before you became this. Alaric Vane. The man who loved a city. He's still in there—I felt him move when I spoke his name in the vault. And a soul can't be destroyed, can it? That's the whole rotten engine of this place. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. Which means he can be *returned*." They looked her full in the face, at the tree growing through the queen, at the three hundred years of borrowed kindness stretched thin over something ancient and starving. "I'm going to give him back to you. All of him. And when he wakes up inside you, you're going to remember exactly what it feels like to be human—and this court, this beautiful cage, is going to come apart at the seams."

"No," Marenthe said. For the first time, her voice was not warm. "You cannot. The seal—"

"—is wax," Felix said, "and I have cracked harder things than wax with softer hands than these."

They had learned, over years of bottling other people's lives, that a memory was not glass. It was a living thing, and living things wanted to return to themselves. All theft was only a delay. All bottles, eventually, broke.

But the vial alone would not be enough. They understood that now, standing in the heart of the court with the turning of the year hours away. A released soul needed an anchor—something true, something freely given, a weight to hold it steady while it remembered its shape. The fae knew this law better than anyone; it was why they traded only in surrenders. Light without anchor scattered. Love without cost was only a word.

Felix knew which memory they would pay. They had known since the gate, when they'd given up their mother's face and discovered that some part of them had been saving something else all along—something deeper, something they'd guarded even from themselves.

The day they learned their gift.

They were nine years old. The kitchen smelled of burnt sugar because Wren—six years old, gap-toothed, fearless—had tried to make their mother's honey-cakes alone and set the pan smoking, and Felix had run to her, and grabbed her hand to pull her back from the stove, and the world had *opened*. They had fallen into their sister the way you fall into water: her terror, her fierce little pride, the taste of smoke, the thought *I'll fix it, I'll fix it before Mama sees*—all of it pouring through Felix like light through a cracked window. They had pulled back gasping, and Wren had stared at them and laughed, breathless, soot on her nose, and said, *"Your eyes went funny, Lix. Like the sky doing something."*

That was the day. That was the treasured thing. Not the power—the power had been a curse and a livelihood and a hiding place. The treasure was Wren's hand in theirs, and the laughter, and the knowledge that they were *something*, that the strange child who belonged nowhere belonged, at least, to her.

If they gave it up, they would not know why they loved her. They would simply love her, the way you love a scar you can't explain. They would stand in the morning light beside a sister made whole and feel a devotion with no story attached to it, a love with its root cut off, blooming anyway.

Marenthe understood a heartbeat before they moved. Perhaps she had always understood; perhaps that was why she had offered so much.

"You would be nothing," the queen said, and for one instant the kindness fell away entirely and something old looked out through her face, something that remembered being buried under a mountain while its city grew up without it. "A hollow girl with a stranger's heart—"

"A loving one," Felix said.

They crushed the vial against the weeping wood.

Glass burst. Light did not—it *bloomed*, white and total, and out of it came a sound Felix had never heard in all their years among souls: a voice saying its own name. *Alaric. Alaric. Alaric.* The founder's soul rose like a man surfacing from deep water, three hundred years drowning ending in a single breath, and it streamed toward the queen—not to attack her, but to *come home*, the way a river comes home to the sea, inevitable and gentle and absolutely unstoppable.

Marenthe screamed. Or perhaps she sang. From her mouth came both sounds at once, tangled, as the original soul poured into her, into the tree, into the crown of weeping wood—and the silver rain turned gold, and the roots shuddered, and everywhere in the vast cavern the mortals' surrendered years flared like lamps remembering fire.

"Now," Felix whispered, and opened their hands, and gave away the kitchen, the smoke, the small warm hand, the sky doing something behind their eyes.

It left them the way all their stolen goods had left: cleanly, completely, without negotiation. One moment the memory was theirs; the next it was a thread of light anchoring another soul's return, and Felix Galloway stood in the collapsing heart of the fae court unable to say why their chest ached, only that it ached toward Wren, only that it ached *for* her, enormously, without reason, without history, without end.

The court began to crumble.

Not violently—that was the horror and mercy of it. The walls simply forgot how to be walls. The roots loosened their grip on the stone. The vaults upstairs, Felix knew, were spilling their light, thousands of bottled souls rising through cracking rock like bubbles through dark water, going home. The nobility shrieked as their jewelry of stolen years dimmed and died. The whole glittering kingdom, built on what mortals surrendered, was being reclaimed by the simple law Felix had learned over a market stall: *everything returns.*

Wren was standing very straight, blinking, her hands opening and closing as if testing whether they belonged to her.

Felix ran to her. Had no memory of deciding to run—only the ache, only the direction. They caught her arms, and she looked down at them with eyes that were filling, slowly, like wells refilling after drought, with herself.

"Lix?" she said.

The name landed in Felix's chest and found no room prepared for it. They didn't know why she called them that. They didn't know why the sound of it made them want to kneel in the ruins and weep. They only knew that they loved her the way the mountain loved the river—without origin, without explanation, entirely.

"Yeah," Felix said. "Yeah, Wren. I've got you."

"The queen—" Wren's voice cracked, rusted from disuse. "There was a queen, she—where—"

Gone. Where Marenthe had stood there was now only the weeping wood, uprooted at last, lying across the broken floor with its silver rain gone quiet—and curled within its branches, small as a sleeping child, a figure of light that might have been a man, or might have been a city, breathing at last without pain.

The cavern groaned. Dust sifted gold through the failing light. Somewhere above, faint as a heartbeat, Felix heard Odile shouting their names down the crumbling stair, her voice raw, her passage still open, still *hers*, held open by a woman with no past and more courage than any history could have taught her.

"Wren. Can you run?"

She looked at Felix—at this stranger who loved her inexplicably—and something in her, some animal wisdom older than memory, decided to trust it. She nodded.

Hand in hand—small warm hand, and Felix would never know why that felt like coming home—they ran for the light.

* * *

The deep roads came apart around them as they climbed. Stone sighed and sloughed. Souls in their thousands streamed upward past Felix's shoulders, warm as birds, and where they passed the rock healed instead of breaking, as if the mountain itself were being forgiven. Twice the passage ahead sealed itself and twice Odile's voice tore it open again—she had found, in the vault, one bottle bearing her family's name, and had shattered it unopened, spending whatever fragment of herself it held to keep the road alive. Felix did not learn this until later. In the climbing there was only Wren's hand, and the ache that was somehow also a wholeness, and the gold dust falling like the first snow of a kinder year.

Then grass. Then night air. Then the mountain gate behind them folding shut with a sound like a book closing, and the stars of the founding wheeling overhead, and Vell below them in its valley—dark, but not entirely. On the far side of the city, past the rooftops, a square glowed orange.

Firelight. A crowd. And rising above the pyre-wood, lashed and waiting, a figure in a witch-hunter's coat that no longer fit the crown that condemned her.

Wren squeezed Felix's hand. "Who is that?" she whispered.

Felix looked at the flames being lit below, at the woman who had signed a family's ruin and crossed a mountain to unsign it, and felt the answer arrive without any memory to carry it—just the truth, bare and burning, the only thing they had left to give.

"Someone we save," Felix said, and started down the mountain.
