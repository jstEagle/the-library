## 22. Morning in Vell

The morning after the turning of the year came down over Vell like a sheet being shaken out—gray at first, then gold at the edges, the kind of light that made even the smoke-stained rooftops of the Tanner's Quarter look briefly holy. Felix Galloway stood in the doorway of a borrowed room above a hedge-healer's shop on Candlewick Lane and watched it happen, because they had not been certain there would be a morning to watch.

Their hands were empty. That was the strangest part. For fifteen years their hands had always held something—a vial, a coin, a lie—and now they hung at their sides like tools belonging to someone else. The memory was gone. The day they had learned what they were: gone, scooped out and spent like a coin tossed into a well, and where it had lived there was now a smooth-walled absence that ached when they pressed their tongue against it, the way a missing tooth aches. They knew *that* they had once known. They could not know *what*.

It was, they decided, a fair price. It was, some mornings, almost bearable.

Below, in the shop's back room, Wren was sleeping.

* * *

Felix went down the stairs quietly, out of habit, though quiet no longer mattered here. The hedge-healer, a stooped woman named Maren Colm who had spent thirty years pretending her poultices were only herbs, had given them the whole upper floor without asking why three strangers and one hollow girl needed it. The gifted of Vell were learning, slowly, painfully, to open their doors to each other. It was like watching frost thaw off a window—one pane at a time, and never all at once.

Wren lay in the narrow bed with her hands folded on the blanket, and for a moment, watching her breathe, Felix let themselves pretend. Let themselves imagine that when she opened her eyes she would squint against the light and say, *Felix, you're standing in it again, move*, the way she used to when they blocked the sun over the washbasin they'd shared as children. Let themselves imagine the song rising up between them, finished this time instead of half-remembered.

Then her eyes opened, gray as river stones, and looked at him without recognition, and the pretense folded itself away with the gentleness of long practice.

"You're the one who brought me back," she said. Her voice was hoarse from disuse, from years of use by something else. "They told me. The woman with the sword, and the lady who doesn't remember things. They said you went into the mountain and gave something up."

"I did."

"What did you give?"

Felix sat on the edge of the chair across from her. "I don't remember," he said, and smiled, and found that the smile was mostly true. "That was rather the point."

Wren studied him for a long moment. There was nothing of the court in her face anymore—the glassy patience, the queen's reflected calm had drained out of her the moment her soul slid home—but neither was there anything of the sister he had lost. She was new. She was fifteen years old inside a body that had kept growing without her, a stranger wearing a beloved face, and Felix understood, with a clarity that felt like the missing memory's ghost, that this was the true cost. Not the day of his gift. This. He had bought her life and lost her love, and both transactions were final.

"I don't know you," Wren said. Not cruelly. Carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "But I dreamed about you. In the mountain. All those years—I wasn't all gone, I think. Some part of me was in the glass, watching. And there was a boy who used to hum outside my window." Her chin trembled, just slightly. "Was that you?"

The song. Their mother's song, the one she'd sung over the washbasin, the one Wren had carried through fifteen years of consumption like an ember cupped in two hands. Felix's throat closed. He nodded.

"Teach it to me," Wren said. "Properly, this time. From the beginning."

So Felix sat beside the bed in the gold-gray light and sang, and Wren's voice came in ragged and wrong on the second verse and right again by the fourth, and it was not the past restored—it was something else, something being built rather than recovered—and Felix thought: *this is what belonging costs, and this is what it buys,* and the ache in the place where the memory had been shifted and settled and became, if not peace, then its near neighbor.

* * *

Ilsa Verrow resigned her commission on the third day of the new year, in writing, delivered to the Office of the Hunt by a courier who took one look at her seal and ran.

She told them about it that evening, in Maren Colm's kitchen, where the beginnings of something unprecedented were taking shape around a scarred table: charm-sellers and hedge-healers and a memory-thief and a disgraced captain, passing bread and small beer and, for the first time in a generation, speaking openly of what they were.

"They wouldn't take the letter from my hand," Ilsa said. She turned her cup in her fingers. She was out of uniform for the first time since Felix had known her, in a plain wool coat, and she looked strange with it—unarmored, unfinished. "So I read it aloud in the square. To whoever was there. Which, after the solstice, was quite a lot of people."

Felix remembered the square after the solstice. Remembered the pyre that had burned empty because the gifted of Vell had come out of their hiding places—forty of them, fifty, more than anyone had guessed—to pull Captain Ilsa Verrow down off the scaffold while Felix was beneath the mountain shattering a queen. Remembered the crowd watching, and not stopping them, and the silence afterward that had not been afraid.

"What did the letter say?" Odile asked. She sat with her hands wrapped around her cup, and Felix still caught himself sometimes looking at her hands, remembering how they had bled holding the deep road open.

"It said," Ilsa replied, "that I had ordered executions under false pretenses, signed condemnations under pressure, and burned people whose only crime was the blood in their veins. It said I would spend whatever remained of my life making it right. And it said—" she paused, and Felix saw her gather herself the way she once gathered for a charge, "—it said the hunt ends with me. That magic is not the crown's to burn out of its people, and that any warden who comes to Candlewick Lane will have to go through me first. Unarmed, if necessary. I'm rather hoping it won't be necessary."

Silence around the table. Then old Tam Wicke, who sold luck-charms two stalls down from where Felix had once worked the night market, raised his small beer and said, "To going through you," and the cups came up, and Ilsa's eyes went bright and hard and she drank like a soldier accepting a toast she intended to earn.

Afterward, on the step outside, she stood beside Felix in the cold and said, quietly, "Your sister. How is she?"

"Learning me," Felix said. "Like a language. She's getting the vowels."

"And you? What did it take from you, in the end? You've never said."

Felix considered lying, out of habit, and found the habit had worn through. "A day," they said. "An important one. I can feel the shape of the hole but not what filled it." They looked at her. "You signed the order that sold my sister. I know that. I've known it since the deep roads. Do you want to know the strange thing?"

"Tell me."

"I find I don't hate you for it. I keep waiting for the hate to arrive and it doesn't. Maybe that's the memory I gave up—maybe hating you was in it somewhere." A breath, fogging silver in the cold. "Or maybe I finally understand that the queen was made of exactly that. Centuries of what people surrendered. Hate would have fed something. I'm done feeding things."

Ilsa looked out at the lane, at the lamplighters beginning their rounds, at the city that had tried to erase her and that she had chosen anyway. "Then we build," she said. "One law at a time. One door at a time." And she went back inside to the warmth and the argument about whether hedge-healers should register with the guild or defy it, and Felix stayed on the step a while longer, lighter and stranger, watching Vell glitter.

* * *

Odile Marchand chose not to take her past back.

The choice arrived, as such things do, without ceremony. In the ruins beneath the mountain, before the collapse, Felix had gathered up what they could carry: Wren's vial, the founder's shattered vessel, and a handful of the vault's surviving glass—souls and memories orphaned by the queen's fall, including, labeled in the court's elegant hand, *Marchand, O.: the whole of it, purchased in full.* Odile had carried it out of the deep roads pressed against her wounded side, and for weeks it had sat on her mantel like a cat she hadn't decided to keep.

On a bright, bitter afternoon in the second week of the year, she brought it to Felix.

"You're the only one who could open it safely," she said. "If I wanted it opened."

"Do you?"

She walked to the window of Felix's rented room—his own rooms now, above the candle-shop, paid for with honest coin from honest charms, though he still could not entirely shake the thief's instinct to stand where the light didn't reach. Below, Wren was in the street teaching the cobbler's daughters a clapping rhyme, laughing at her own mistakes, building a self out of nothing but the present tense.

"When I was in the mountain," Odile said, "bleeding, holding that passage—I knew exactly who I was. Isn't that absurd? I'd sold every yesterday I owned, and in the worst moment of my life I was *complete*. Because completeness isn't memory, Felix. It's choosing. Every hour, choosing who you are next." She turned the vial in her hands; the light inside it stirred like a sleeping thing. "If I drink this, I get back a girl who owed a debt so large she sold herself to pay it. A family that treated her as currency. I'd remember loving them. I might even remember why." She set the vial down on the table between them, decisively, the way one sets down a card. "I don't want her back. I want to keep becoming."

"And the vial?"

"Keep it. Or break it. Or give it to someone who needs a past more than I need mine." She smiled then—not the polished society smile Felix had first seen in her townhouse, all surface and strategy, but the newer one, the one that had been forged underground. "Besides. Someone in this company should be allowed to forget things on purpose. You've had enough forgetting done *to* you."

After she left, Felix held the vial a long time. Inside, faintly, a young woman laughed at something no one would ever hear again. He wrapped it in cloth and put it in the drawer with his few treasures, and did not decide, and found that not deciding was its own kind of freedom.

* * *

On the last day of the month, Felix went back to Founder's Square.

Not to the center, where the pyres had stood and where, now, someone had planted a ring of winter roses no one claimed credit for. To the edge, where the weeping wood grew—the last tree in Vell older than the city itself, the one the founder had loved, the one whose image Queen Marenthe had worn as her crown of silver branches and falling leaves. The fire of the solstice had never touched it. But the night the court crumbled, every branch of it had wept sap like tears, and in the morning the ground beneath it had been scattered with seeds.

The wardens' notices were still nailed to the posts nearby—HUNT PROCLAIMED, GIFTED TO SURRENDER—but the paper had gone soft with weather, and no one had replaced them, and a child had drawn a chalk sun over the crown's seal. Small rebellions. The city learning a new shape.

Felix knelt in the frozen ground at the wood's edge with a trowel stolen—purchased, he corrected himself, *purchased*—from the ironmonger's, and dug six small holes in a line where the morning sun would find them.

He thought about what to say, and decided against saying anything. Words had been the queen's medium, words and bargains and beautiful traps. He simply worked. Six seeds from the weeping wood, pressed into dark earth, covered, watered from the flask at his belt with snowmelt he'd carried three streets for no reason he could name except that it felt right.

A gift given freely. No bargain attached. He wondered if the tree would know the difference. He suspected it would.

"Felix!" Wren's voice, from across the square, breathless. She came running with her scarf flying, the cobbler's girls behind her, and skidded to a stop at the edge of the turned earth. "There you are. Ilsa's looking for you—there's a man at the shop, a warden, but not to arrest anyone, he says—he says the Magistrate wants to talk about *repealing* the Edict, actually repealing it, and Ilsa said bring Felix, and I said I'd find you because—" she stopped, caught her breath, grinned at him with a grin that was becoming familiar the way a new house becomes familiar, room by room, "—because that's what sisters do, isn't it? Find people?"

"That's what they do," Felix said, and stood, and brushed the earth from his knees.

Behind them the weeping wood stood bare against the winter sky, its branches dripping slow bright drops that froze as they fell, and beneath the frozen ground six seeds waited in the dark for a spring that Felix Galloway—who had sold other people's yesterdays to survive, and given away his own to save what he loved—was finally, finally going to be there to see.
