## 1. The Covenant of Hollowmere

The crows arrived before the frost did, which was how Sebastian knew the season had turned.

They came down from the border stones in a ragged black ribbon, six of them, and settled along the fence rail of her goat pen like a delegation with grievances. She was elbow-deep in the water trough, breaking the skin of ice with her knuckles, and she did not look up. Looking up was the first mistake. Looking up too long was the second.

"You're late," she said, low, under the sound of the wind through the bare alders. "I've been feeding you since the first hard frost for nine years running. You could manage some gratitude."

*Gratitude,* said the eldest crow, the one she called Maggot because he'd earned it, *is for the dead. We brought you news instead.*

"News is better than gratitude anyway."

*The witch-hunters passed the north road at dusk yesterday.* Maggot hopped twice along the rail, head cocked, one eye fixed on her with that wet black attention that never failed to make her spine prickle. *Three of them. Grey cloaks. They stopped at the wardline and one of them spat.*

Sebastian kept her hands moving in the freezing water. The covenant of Hollowmere was simple, older than anyone living, and absolute: no magic entered the valley, and none left it. Whatever gift a soul carried was stripped silent at the border stones, pressed flat like flowers in a book. The valley had been magic-dead for two hundred years, and its people were proud of it, or pretended to be, and King Vaelor's witch-hunters respected the wardline the way wolves respect a fire — not out of reverence, but because crossing it cost them something they couldn't afford to lose.

Which meant the valley was safe. Which meant Sebastian Marchetti, who had been able to speak to beasts since before she could speak properly to people, was invisible here. A farm woman with a limp mule and a crooked fence and nothing inside her worth burning.

"Did they see anything?" she asked. "Anyone?"

*They asked the toll-keeper about a courier. A man on a lathered horse, riding south, riding fast. The toll-keeper said he'd seen no one. He lied well, for a man who can't hear us.* Maggot ruffled his feathers. *But the hunters didn't believe him. They turned back north. For now.*

"For now," Sebastian repeated. She pulled her hands from the trough and dried them on her apron, and only then did she look up, briefly, letting herself meet the crow's eye. It was the closest thing to intimacy her life contained, and she rationed it like salt. "Thank you."

*Don't thank me. Feed me.*

She scattered barley along the fence rail, all six birds descending in a clatter of wings, and went inside to heat the kettle, and told herself the cold feeling in her stomach was just the weather.

It wasn't the weather. It hadn't been the weather since the courier's name had first crossed the crows' gossip three days ago — a stranger riding toward Hollowmere as if the valley were a destination rather than a place people fled to. Nobody came to Hollowmere. People were born here, married here, died here, and thanked the covenant for keeping the world's teeth out. The last person to arrive from outside had been a tinker, eleven years ago, and he'd wept at the border stones like a man coming home from war.

Sebastian put the kettle on and tried very hard not to think about what kind of man rode toward a magic-dead valley with witch-hunters at his back.

* * *

Marta Kessel caught her at the well the next morning, which meant Marta Kessel had been waiting for her.

"Sebastian." Marta's smile was wide and her eyes were narrow, and between those two expressions lived thirty years of neighborliness stretched thin as ice over deep water. "You're up early."

"Crows woke me."

"Mm." Marta leaned against the well housing, arms folded, breath steaming. She was a broad woman gone soft at the middle, with flour permanently under her fingernails, and she had known Sebastian since Sebastian was a girl climbing her apple trees. That history should have counted for something. Lately it felt more like a debt being called in. "Funny thing. My boy saw lights past the stones two nights back. Up by the north wardline. Lights, and voices."

"Hunters camp there sometimes," Sebastian said. "Checking the line."

"Do they?" Marta tilted her head. "In twenty years I never once saw them camp. And here's another funny thing — Tam says he heard your voice out by the pen that same evening. Talking to somebody. But when he looked, there was nobody there but you and the birds."

The well rope creaked in the wind. Somewhere behind Marta's shoulder, a crow sat on the barn roof, watching, and Sebastian wished fervently that it would leave, because its presence made everything worse — made it look like exactly what it was.

"I talk to myself," she said. "My mother did it too. Bad habit. You talk enough to yourself, you start answering."

"Your mother." Marta said the name the way you'd set down a heavy stone. "Your mother was strange, everyone said so. And your grandmother before her, they say, though she left before most folks remember. Strange women, the Marchettis. Sweet, but strange." She pushed off from the well and patted Sebastian's arm, and her hand lingered half a heartbeat too long. "Well. Strange isn't a crime in Hollowmere. Nothing is, so long as it stays inside the line. That's the whole point of the line, isn't it? You keep well, Sebastian."

She walked away up the lane, unhurried, and Sebastian stood with the bucket going cold in her hand and understood that she had just been warned. Not accused — warned. Marta Kessel would not go to the hunters. Not yet. But she would watch, and she would count, and if the arithmetic ever came out wrong, she would decide that loving a neighbor for thirty years was cheaper than burning beside her.

That was the covenant's true cruelty, Sebastian thought, hauling the bucket up. It didn't just strip the magic out of you. It taught everyone around you to do the stripping too.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon because work was the only prayer she trusted. Mended the south fence where Bracken had leaned on it. Split wood until her shoulders burned. Boiled turnips into submission. Bracken stood in his stall watching her with his long patient face, and she let herself speak to him only once, quietly, while she curried him: *You'd tell me if someone came up the road, wouldn't you?*

*I always have,* Bracken said. His voice in her mind was like slow water over stones. *No one has come.*

No one came. She cooked supper, ate it standing at the window, watched the light die over the western ridge where the border stones stood grey and patient as old teeth. The valley lay quiet around her — smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys, a dog barking twice in the village and falling silent, the first stars pricking through. Ordinary. Safe. Invisible.

She was washing the pot when Bracken screamed.

Not a word — a scream, raw animal terror tearing through the link they shared, and Sebastian was out the door with the poker in her hand before her mind caught up to her feet. Frost glittered on the yard. The pen gate banged empty. And beyond it, up the slope toward the western ridge, a figure was stumbling down through the alders — a man, lurching, one arm wrapped around his ribs, leaving a dark stuttering trail across the frosted grass.

He crossed the wardline.

Sebastian's whole body flinched back from it, the way you flinch from a flame. Nothing magical crossed into Hollowmere. Nothing magical *could*. The covenant drank it at the stones; hunters tested it every generation and always turned away, hollowed, diminished. It was the one law of the world she had never once seen broken.

And yet the man came on, down through the trees, and as he lurched into the open she saw why the impossible was possible: there was almost nothing left of him to strip. He was spent. Emptied. Whatever he had carried, he had carried it here, and the wardline took its tithe from a man already bleeding out, and let the rest through.

"No," Sebastian heard herself say. "No, no, go back—"

He couldn't hear her. He fell twice between the fence and her door, and the second time he didn't get up so much as crawl, dragging himself the last few yards across her frozen yard, and by the time she reached him he had collapsed against her doorstep with his back to the wall and his legs splayed in the frost, and his eyes found hers with terrible clarity.

He was young. Younger than her, maybe twenty-five, with a courier's travel-worn face and blood sheeting down his side from a wound she could see was far past any help she owned. His lips moved.

"They're coming," he rasped. "Behind me. Maybe a day. Maybe less. Listen — listen — I was told to find a Marchetti. Are you — are you a Marchetti?"

The name went through her like a hook. Her grandmother's name. The strange woman who'd left before anyone remembered her, whom Marta had spoken of that morning like a stone dropped down a well.

"Yes," she said, and hated herself for saying it, and could not have done otherwise.

Something like relief broke across his face, and with it came the end of him; she watched the relief spend the last of whatever was holding him together. He fumbled at his coat, fingers clumsy and dark with blood, and dragged out a folded square of oilskin, stained through, and pressed it into her hands with both of his, gripping her wrists with shocking strength.

"The court beneath the mountain," he whispered. "The choir. Your family — they'll know what it means. The name in the margin. She said — she promised—" A cough took him, wet and terrible. "Don't let them have it. Don't let them sing her again."

"Whose name? Who promised? *Who?*"

But his grip was loosening, and his eyes had gone from her face to the sky above the ridge, where the first stars stood in their ancient indifferent order, and whatever he saw there, he did not come back from it. The strength went out of his hands all at once, like a tide withdrawing, and Sebastian knelt in her own frozen yard holding a dead man's blood between her fingers and an oilskin packet against her chest, and above her, from the barn roof, the crows began to scream.

She looked up. They weren't screaming at the body.

They were screaming at the road.

Far down the valley, past the dark fields, past the sleeping village, a light was moving. Then another. Then a third, strung out along the north road like lanterns carried in careful procession, coming on steadily, unhurried, the way men walk when they are certain of where they are going and certain of what they will find when they arrive.

Sebastian got up. Her legs shook. She looked at the dead courier, at the packet in her hands, at the lights, and she understood — with a clarity that felt like falling — that the invisibility she had spent her whole life building was over. Not threatened. Over. Marta had seen the lights too, by now. The whole valley would know a stranger had died at the Marchetti woman's door, carrying something, hunted by the king's own grey cloaks. There was no version of tomorrow in which she stood in her kitchen and split wood and fed her crows and remained nobody.

Thirty years of hiding, ended in ten minutes by a dying man's last promise.

She tucked the packet inside her coat, against her heart, where the bloodstain soaked warm through the cloth, and went to saddle Bracken in the dark.
