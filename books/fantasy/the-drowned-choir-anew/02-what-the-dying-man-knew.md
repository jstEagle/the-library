## 2. What the Dying Man Knew

The crows woke her before dawn, and they were not gossiping.

Sebastian had spent eleven years learning the difference between crow-talk and crow-alarm. Gossip was a loose, rolling sound, feathers and insults, the small politics of the fence line. Alarm was shorter. Sharper. It came down like a dropped knife.

*Two-legs coming,* said the eldest crow, the one with the torn left wing who roosted on the border stones and considered himself her senior in all things. *Not valley two-legs. Smelling ones. Iron and smoke.*

She was at the window before she'd finished tying her braid.

Three riders on the far ridge, where the wardline ran—a seam of grey stones older than any map of Hollowmere, older than the usurper's grandfather, older than the covenant that had pressed this valley flat and magicless for as long as anyone could remember. The riders wore the long coats of witch-hunters, and their horses moved wrong for the terrain, too smooth, too obedient, like things being *held* rather than ridden.

Her hands went cold on the sill.

They couldn't be here for her. No one came to Hollowmere for anything but grain disputes and funerals. They were here for the courier—the man who had died against her door three nights ago with blood in his mouth and a rolled leather tube clutched in his fist like a rosary.

*Hide it,* said the crow. *Hide your face. Hide the dead man's leavings.*

"I don't have his leavings," she whispered, which was true only if you didn't count the map now sewn into the lining of her winter coat, or the ring he'd pressed into her palm with his last strength, or the word he'd mouthed when his eyes had found hers—impossibly, knowingly—and he'd understood what she was before she'd said a single thing aloud: *Marchetti.*

Her grandmother's name. A name no one in Hollowmere had spoken in thirty years.

She buried the courier properly that morning, quickly, in the soft ground behind the goat shed, while the riders picked their way down toward the village proper. She said no words over him because words over graves were a kind of magic and magic did not live here—but she thought them, loudly, furiously, in the privacy of her own skull, which even Vaelor's hunters had never learned to read.

*I don't know who you were. I don't know why you carried my grandmother's name across half the world to die on a stranger's doorstep. But I'm going to find out.*

Behind the goat shed, Bracken stamped once, twice, and blew a long breath through his nose. The mule had been her mother's before it was hers, and he was the only creature in the valley who had ever heard her speak beast-speech and lived to remember it—not because the gift didn't work here, but because it worked so faintly, so watered-down, that she'd convinced herself for years it was imagination. A girl talking to her mule. Nothing strange in that.

Except that Bracken answered. And mules, as a rule, do not answer.

*Hunters,* Bracken said, which was all he ever said about anything, because Bracken held opinions the way a stone holds water—deeply, and only under pressure. *Bad ones. Going away now?*

"Not yet," Sebastian murmured, filling his trough. "But soon."

***

The map took her three days to read, and she read it the way you'd defuse something, in stolen hours, by candle stub, with the shutters barred.

It was not drawn on paper but on cured hide, thin as onion skin, and the ink had been mixed with something that shimmered faintly when the light crossed it at an angle—something that should not have shimmered inside the covenant's reach, and did anyway, stubbornly, like a coal refusing the rain. The geography was wrong on purpose. Rivers bent where rivers didn't bend. Mountains wore names she'd only heard in her mother's rare, bitter stories: the Weeping Pass. The Tithe Road. And at the center, rendered in careful cross-section as though the cartographer had cut the mountain open with a pen, a shape beneath Mount Cerenne that made her breath catch every time she looked at it.

A cathedral. Inverted. Sunk into the mountain's roots and flooded to the vaults, its spires pointing *down* into black water, its nave a drowned hall where hundreds of small marks clustered like minnows—each mark a tiny spiral, each spiral annotated in a cramped clerical hand.

Souls. She understood that by the second day, and understanding it felt like swallowing ice.

The margins were full of notes in at least three hands. One was the courier's—hurried, slanting, written in a code of numbers and saints' days. One was older, formal, bureaucratic: tithe schedules, delivery dates, sums paid in *years* rather than coin. And one was different from both. Older still. Looped and patient, written by someone who had owned her hand completely, and beside the inverted cathedral, in that looping hand, a single line:

*The choir keeps what we sell it. God forgive me, it sings so beautifully.*

Below that, a signature.

*M. Marchetti.*

Sebastian sat back from the hide until the chair knocked the wall. Her heart was doing something arrhythmic and awful.

Her grandmother. The woman her mother had refused to name, whose portrait had hung turned-to-the-wall in the attic for Sebastian's entire childhood, whose absence had shaped the family more surely than any presence. She had assumed—everyone assumed—that the old woman had simply died badly somewhere, or worse, run off. Not this. Not *sold souls to a flooded cathedral and signed her name to it.*

And then there was the other thing. The thing she kept circling back to at night, when sleep wouldn't take.

On the third evening, working by feel more than sight, she found it: a fold in the hide, so fine it was nearly invisible, stitched flat with thread the color of the parchment itself. Inside the fold, in the courier's slanting hand, four words:

*Your soul is owed.*

She read it perhaps forty times. Each time it meant something slightly different and all of the meanings were unbearable. Your soul is owed—as in, someone had taken it. As in, someone had sold it. As in, there was a ledger under a mountain with her name—or a name adjacent to her name—in it, and the arithmetic of her own self did not balance.

That was the night she stopped sleeping well.

***

The hunters came to her door on the fourth day, and they were polite, which was worse than if they hadn't been.

There were two of them inside the kitchen; the third waited with the horses. The tall one had a voice like oiled hinges and a warrant case slung across his back, and he smiled the whole time he asked his questions, which gave his face the look of a mask worn slightly askew.

"A courier," he said. "Riding a bay mare, grey about the muzzle. Carrying dispatches for the court. He went to ground somewhere in this valley—we've tracked him to the wardline." He turned his smile on her like a lamp. "You're the last holding before the high farms. You'd have seen him."

"Travelers don't stop here much," Sebastian said. She was pleased at how steady her voice came out. "We're poor hosts. Nothing worth stealing."

"Nothing worth *anything*, I'd wager." The hunter laughed at his own joke. His companion didn't. The companion was looking around the kitchen slowly, at the herbs drying from the beams, at the iron pot, at the window where a crow sat watching them with what Sebastian sincerely hoped was ordinary bird indifference and not the frankly judgmental stare it was actually giving.

"The man was hurt," the tall one said, dropping the smile entirely, the way you'd drop a tool you were finished with. "Wounded crossing the wards—which shouldn't be possible, should it? Nothing wounded gets *in*. The covenant keeps things out. Keeps things *flat*." His eyes came back to hers and stayed. "Unless something inside let him through. Unless something inside has a crack in it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, Bracken stamped.

"He never came here," Sebastian said.

"Mm." The hunter straightened. He looked at her a moment longer, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to satisfy some private calculus, because he nodded, almost kindly. "No. I don't suppose he did. But we'll ride the valley another day or two. Talk to your neighbors. You understand. Procedure."

She understood procedure. She understood that by tomorrow evening, old Marta would mention the girl up the hill who talked to animals, and the shepherd's boy would mention the crows that followed her like a retinue, and none of it would mean anything to anyone—except to men who hunted cracks in the world for a living.

They left. She watched them from the loft window until the ridge swallowed them, and then she packed.

She packed the way grief packs: fast, numb, missing things. The map went into the coat lining. The courier's ring went on a cord around her neck. She took her mother's good knife, a week of food, flint, thread, the small hoard of coin from the loose floorboard. She stood a long time in the middle of the room with her grandmother's portrait finally turned face-out, studying the painted woman's mouth, trying to see the mouth that had written *it sings so beautifully.*

Then she went down and saddled Bracken in the dark.

*Going,* Bracken said. Not a question. Mules are precise.

"Yes."

*Bad idea.*

"Almost certainly."

He considered this with the gravity of his kind, ears swiveling through every compass point, and then he lowered his shoulder so she could swing up more easily, which was, from Bracken, a speech.

They went north along the drover's path in the blue hour before sunrise, hooves quiet on the turf, past the border stones where the eldest crow waited with three others lined behind him like an honor guard.

*We'll watch the road,* the crow said. *Word travels faster than hunters. When you pass beyond the stones, little speaker, listen for us. The world out there is loud, but we are louder where it matters.*

"I know," Sebastian said. "Thank you."

*Don't thank me. Bring back news. It's been a dull decade.*

And then the stones were behind her, and the wardline passed over her skin like the surface tension of a pond breaking, and the world changed.

She felt it immediately—felt everything immediately. The air thickened with presences she'd spent a lifetime hearing through wool and water: grass thinking its slow green thoughts, wind carrying messages between hills, a hawk's cold attention sweeping the slope above. Sound upon sound upon sound, a flood where there had been a trickle. She gripped Bracken's mane and breathed through it, and Bracken—steady, unimpressed Bracken—said only:

*Louder now.*

"Louder now," she agreed, laughing shakily, wiping her eyes with the back of one hand, because it wasn't grief exactly and it wasn't joy either, it was both braided into one rope and pulling her forward.

Beyond the stones, the road bent toward the free cities and the mountains beyond them, toward a drowned cathedral and a ledger with her name in it. Behind her, Hollowmere lay flat and silent and safe, and would stay safe only as long as no one looked too closely at the girl who had just left it.

Somewhere on the road ahead, a crow's voice came down the wind, thin and clear:

*Riders behind you. Moving fast. Run, little speaker.*

Bracken ran.
