## 3. The Free Cities Have No Law for Me

The road out of Hollowmere ended at a river, and the river ended at Vell.

Sebastian had never seen so many people in one place. The free city spilled down its hills like spilled grain—ten thousand rooftops, smoke rising from a hundred chimneys, bells ringing from towers she could not name. Bracken's ears swiveled at every sound. The mule had been quiet since the pass, quiet in the way of beasts who have decided their human is carrying something heavy and will not be asked to carry more.

*Too many,* Bracken said, which was as close as he came to complaint.

"I know," Sebastian murmured, low enough that only the mule heard. "Keep your thoughts small."

That was the rule now. She'd learned it on the second day out, when a farmer's dog trotted alongside them for a mile and she made the mistake of answering his cheerful commentary about rabbits. The dog had gone still, head cocked, and looked at her the way animals look at things they cannot explain—and Sebastian had felt, for the first time in her life, what it was to be *seen* by something that might tell.

In Hollowmere, her gift had been a secret kept by silence. Out here, it was a secret kept by discipline. It was not the same thing at all.

Vell's gates stood open—free cities kept no walls against their own—but the gatehouse held a different kind of watch. A woman in grey sat at a table beneath the arch, and before her lay a ledger bound in black leather, and beside the ledger lay a tuning fork of pale silver that hummed faintly even in still air.

"Name," said the woman, not looking up.

"Sebastian Marchetti."

"Trade?"

"Mule-trader." It was close enough to true; Bracken snorted.

The woman reached for the fork, and Sebastian's stomach dropped. She had read enough of the courier's map margins, enough of Wren's name scrawled there like a warning, to know what came next: every soul entering a free city was sounded, the way a bell is sounded for cracks. The fork would touch her skin, and if anything in her rang—

*Anything in her rang.* That was the whole of her. That was the secret twenty-three years of silence had been built around.

But the woman only pressed the fork briefly to Bracken's neck, then to Sebastian's wrist, and frowned at it. "Dead tone," she muttered. "Valley folk." She waved them through with something between pity and distaste. "Hollowmere. No wonder you look half-starved. Move along."

*Dead tone.* Sebastian walked through the gate with her heart hammering and understood, slowly, what had happened. The covenant of her valley didn't just strip magic from those who entered. It clung. It soaked into the bone like river water into wool, and to anyone listening with silver and skill, she sounded hollow. Sounded safe.

Sounded like nothing.

She should have felt relief. Instead, walking down Vell's crowded high street with the map burning against her ribs, she felt something closer to grief. All those years hiding from witch-hunters who would never have come—the valley had hidden her not because anyone loved her, but because someone, long ago, had decided she was easier forgotten than protected.

Her grandmother's name was written in the margin of the map. *Mirelle Marchetti, sold to the choir, year of the Red Tithe.* And beside it, in the same cramped hand: *the other one lives.*

The other one. Her whole life, she had thought herself singular. Now she knew she was a remainder.

* * *

Vell smelled of fish and tar and frying onions, and Sebastian spent her first coin in years on a bowl of all three. She ate in the shadow of a chandler's awning, the map open on her knees under the table, tracing the route it drew up through the Weeping Pass to the mountain called Cael Duren, where the court beneath waited with its drowned choir and its ledgers of souls.

A cat sat watching her from atop a barrel. Not staring the way cats stare—staring the way cats stare when they are being paid.

"You're far from home," the cat said.

Sebastian went cold from the crown of her head down. "I don't know what you mean."

"Neither do I, mostly," the cat admitted, washing one paw. "But the grey-cloaks pay three coppers for word of anything that talks back to beasts, and I've got kittens, and you've got a mule you keep murmuring to like a lover. So. Three coppers, or I yowl."

It was absurd. It was terrifying. It was, she realized with a lurch, exactly the world the dying courier had died trying to warn her about—a world where her gift wasn't a secret but a *commodity*, catalogued and priced, hunted by professionals.

She did the only thing she could think of. She picked up the bowl and threw the contents—not at the cat, at the space behind the cat—and while the animal sprang away in outrage she grabbed Bracken's halter and walked, fast, into the thickest part of the market crowd.

She made it four streets.

They came out of an alley in pairs, two men and two women in undyed wool, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who own the ground they walk on. Not witch-hunters—no silver forks, no grey cloaks. Something worse: they moved like locals. One carried a net of fine chain that chimed when it shifted, each link tuned to a different note.

"Beast-speaker," the tallest said, not unkindly, which somehow made it worse. "Under the Edict of Bells, your voice is crown property. You'll come quiet, or you'll come rung."

Bracken screamed and reared, and Sebastian grabbed for the halter rope and missed, and the net-man swung—

And stopped. Stopped mid-swing, arm frozen, mouth open, as though someone had plucked a string inside him and found it broken.

From the doorway of a dyer's shop stepped a broad woman of perhaps fifty, grey-streaked hair cropped short, hands stained indigo to the wrist. She held a small brass instrument to her lips like a whistle, and though no sound came from it, the air around the four bounty-takers shivered like heat off a road.

"Rung," she repeated, contemptuous. "You lot couldn't ring a dinner bell." She lowered the instrument. "Go on. This one's claimed. Guild dispute."

The tall one hesitated. "Songsmithe Ilse," he said, and spat the name like a curse. "You're struck off."

"I'm *disgraced*, dear, which means I still know where all the bodies are buried and which notes wake them." She smiled without warmth. "Tell the Guildmaster his nets sing flat."

They went. They went quickly, and the crowd closed over them as though nothing had happened, because in Vell, apparently, nothing ever did.

Ilse turned to Sebastian. Up close her eyes were the pale grey of old ice, and they missed nothing.

"You," she said, "are going to buy me a drink, and then you are going to show me the map in your coat, because I saw the seal on it when you opened it at the food stall, and I have been looking for that seal for eleven years."

* * *

The tavern was called the Drowned Rat, which Ilse said was either very funny or very sad depending on how much you knew, and she bought the drink herself because Sebastian's coins were gone into the cat's economy.

"The seal," Ilse said, turning her cup slowly. "Show me."

Sebastian hesitated. Every instinct honed in Hollowmere said hide it, deny it, smile and lie. But the net-men would regroup, and the cat had her scent, and this indigo-handed woman had just done something impossible in a doorway without breaking a sweat.

She laid the map on the table.

Ilse looked at it for a long time. Her thumb traced the wax seal at its corner—a circle broken by seven vertical lines, like a cage seen from above—and when she spoke again her voice had changed, gone quieter, older.

"Cael Duren. The court beneath the mountain." She looked up. "Where did a Hollowmere mule-trader get a court-seal map?"

"A dying man gave it to me at my door."

"Dying men give maps to no one. Dying men clutch maps and take them into the ground." Ilse leaned forward. "Unless someone told him to give it. Unless someone chose you." Her eyes narrowed. "Who are you, girl? Truly."

"Sebastian Marchetti."

Something flickered across the songsmithe's face—recognition, then a strange, careful blankness, the look of a woman setting down a weight she has carried too long.

"Marchetti," Ilse repeated. "I knew a Marchetti. Court archivist, before the purges. Mirelle. She sang the binding-records—someone had to write down what they took, and she volunteered, gods help her, because she thought if she wrote it gently enough it wouldn't count." Ilse drained her cup. "They say she sold her own granddaughter's twin to the choir. Year of the Red Tithe. The babe never cried once, they said. Already half song."

The tavern noise seemed to recede. Sebastian heard her own pulse instead.

"That was my grandmother," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "The map has her name in the margin. And a note that says *the other one lives.*"

Ilse set the cup down very carefully, as though the table might otherwise crack.

"So," she said softly. "You're the one who lived." She rubbed her stained hands together, and for the first time she looked less like a rescuer and more like a gambler about to show her cards. "Then hear my price, girl, because I don't work for gratitude. Eleven years ago the court took my voice-license, my guild-ring, and my sister—all three for the crime of writing one honest ballad. My sister's soul is in that choir. I know the mountain's service tunnels, the priests' rotations, the keeper's weaknesses, and the exact pitch that cracks a binding-lock. All of it yours—for a price."

"Which is?"

"You take me with you. And when you stand in that flooded cathedral, whatever bargain you strike—" Ilse's pale eyes held hers, unblinking, "—you find my sister first. Before your twin. Before your vengeance. Before anything."

Outside, somewhere in the darkening streets, a chain-net chimed, and a cat yowled three times, and boots began to gather.

"They've called the grey-cloaks," Ilse said, standing, pulling a hood over her cropped hair. "Back door, now. You can decide on the road whether my price is robbery or mercy—but decide fast, beast-speaker, because in Vell, under the Edict of Bells, they're already writing you into the ledger."

She flipped the map closed and slid it back across the table.

"And once you're in a ledger," she said, "you never come out clean."
