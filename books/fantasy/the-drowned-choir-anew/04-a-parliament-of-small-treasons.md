## 4. A Parliament of Small Treasons

Ilse Varn had sung for kings, and she had learned this: the difference between a court and a den of thieves was only the quality of the candlelight.

The smuggler's den beneath Vell's tannery quarter was lit by whale-oil lamps gone amber with smoke, and their light made everything look like a confession. Crates of untaxed salt. Bolts of silk stamped with a dead duke's crest. A wall of ledgers that would have made a magistrate weep. And in the middle of it all, on an upturned ale cask, a map sealed in blood-wax, and a girl from Hollowmere who watched Ilse study it the way a hawk watches a snare.

"You knew the seal," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question. She'd been circling it for two days, ever since Ilse had touched the courier's map and gone white as tallow.

"I sang at the mountain," Ilse said. "Before I fell."

"Before you *fell*." Corvo snorted from his corner, where he sat sharpening a knife he never seemed to finish sharpening. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, desertion sitting on him like wet wool. "She means before they cut her name off the rolls and her voice out of the choir registry. Ask her what she did. Go on. It's a good story."

"It is not a good story," Ilse said.

Sebastian waited. She had the patience of someone raised among animals who only spoke when speaking cost something, and Ilse found she could not bear that patience. It was worse than shouting.

"I unmade a song," she said. "A binding-song. One verse of it. The Verse of Keeping, they call it—the thing that holds a soul quiet once it's been sold into the choir. I loosened one line. Just one. For six hours, one soul in the drowned cathedral remembered its own name." She turned the map over in her hands, not looking at anyone. "They couldn't punish me by killing me. Killing a songsmithe wastes the training. So they took my voice instead—my *registered* voice, my right to sing anything but common work-chants—and they sent me down to Vell to teach merchant daughters how to hold a harp without looking bored."

"And the soul you freed?" Sebastian asked quietly.

"Rebound within the day. But for six hours it screamed, girl. Six hours, and every priest in the cathedral heard a stolen soul remember being a person." Ilse finally looked up. "So when I saw that seal, I didn't see a map. I saw the inside of a place I swore I'd never think about again. That's what I knew."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, the tannery vats groaned through the floorboards, and the smell of lime and hide soaked down into everything, so that Ilse suspected she would smell of it forever now, the way she already smelled of disgrace.

Sebastian reached out and took the map back. Not snatching—taking, deliberate, the way you'd take a knife from a child. "Then you'll tell me how to get in."

"I'll tell you how to get in," Ilse said, "because if I don't, you'll go alone and die stupidly, and I'll have freed exactly nothing. But understand what you're asking. You want to walk into the one place in the world where your gift isn't just illegal. It's *inventory*."

* * *

Corvo had guarded the mountain road for eleven years, and in that time he had counted four hundred and twelve convoys go up into the dark and none come down.

He told them this on the third night, when the fugitives had stopped pretending they were strangers. The den had grown crowded in ways no ledger accounted for. Sebastian's beasts had begun arriving—not summoned, Corvo understood that much now, but *informed*, the way word travels through a city's poor. Two crows first, then a limping fox with a torn ear that slept against Sebastian's boot like a dog, then, absurdly, a river otter that came up through the drainage grate and took up residence in a barrel of rainwater, watching everything with small black judgment.

"They're telling her things," Wren said. The clerk had arrived two nights past with a satchel of copied pages pressed to his chest like a nursing infant, and he hadn't fully put it down since. He was young, ink-stained, and looked permanently surprised to be alive. "The birds. They've been flying the whole valley and the pass roads, and she just—listens. I watched her stand in the yard for an hour doing nothing, and afterward she drew me a map of every hunter patrol between here and the Weeping Pass. From *birds*."

"That's witchcraft," Corvo said flatly.

"That's beast-speech," Sebastian said, not looking up from the fox she was feeding salt pork. "It's older than Vaelor's edicts. Older than his grandmother. The valley stripped it out of everyone who crossed the wardline—that's why I hid. In there, I'm just a woman who talks to herself. Out here—" she gestured at the room, at the map, at all of them, "—out here it's a hanging offense. So yes. Call it what you like."

Corvo should have hated her for it. He had spent eleven years guarding the road that fed souls into the mountain, telling himself it was just soldiering, just pay, just orders, and here was a farm girl whose crime was born into her bones. Instead he found himself saying the thing he'd carried up from the lowlands like a stone in his boot.

"There's a tithe-convoy leaves Vell for the mountain every new moon," he said. "Grain, salt, wine for the priests. And other cargo. Sealed wagons, cold ones, that the drivers aren't allowed to look at." He met Sebastian's eyes. "I escorted nine of them. I never opened one. I want you to know that I *never let myself* open one, because I knew what I'd find, and I wanted to keep not knowing."

"Knowing is the whole war," Wren said softly, hugging his satchel.

"Then I'm done losing," Corvo said.

* * *

It was the otter, in the end, who brought the proof.

Ilse woke near dawn to a wet slap against her cheek and found the creature standing on her chest holding a strip of oilcloth in its paws, insistent as a tax collector. By the time she'd lit a lamp, Sebastian was already awake, kneeling by the drainage grate with her head cocked, listening to something Ilse would never hear.

"She says there are men in the culvert tunnels," Sebastian reported. "Below the tannery. Four of them. They've been asking after a mule and a girl from Hollowmere. And—" a pause, her jaw tightening, "—they're carrying a soul-net. Iron and wire, blessed twice. Someone told them what I am."

"The hunters followed us out of Vell," Corvo said, already moving, pulling his old garrison cloak from under the floorboards. "Or someone sold us."

"No one sold us," Ilse said. "No one had to. Wren's copies, the map, the seal—Ilse Varn asking questions in the low quarters? The city has ears older than any of us." She stood, and the old discipline of the court settled over her like armor she'd hated wearing and missed anyway. "Which means we stop being a parliament and start being an army, or we stop being anything at all."

But Sebastian wasn't moving. She was crouched by the grate, both hands flat on the stone, and her face had gone strange.

"What," Ilse said. "What did it say?"

"Not the otter." Sebastian's voice had dropped to almost nothing. "A crow. Old crow, comes down from the pass country. It flew three days to find me." She looked up, and Ilse saw that her eyes were wet and furious at being wet. "It nests on the mountain. On the outer galleries of the court itself. And it says—" she swallowed. "It says there's a voice in the flooded cathedral that sings wrong. Off-key. Every night, same hour. And the other voices bend toward it. Like iron filings toward a lodestone."

"So?" Corvo asked.

"So the crow says the priests call it the Anchor Voice. And it says—" Sebastian pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth for a moment. "It says when it sings, the water rises half a finger's width. All by itself. Against the bindings."

Wren went pale. "That's impossible. Bound souls don't move water. They don't move *anything*. That's the entire point of the binding—"

"Unless the binding doesn't fit," Ilse said slowly, and the thought arrived in her like a bell struck in an empty hall. She had spent thirty years inside songs, inside the architecture of them, and she knew what a mismatched note did to a chord. She knew what a soul out of true with its binding could do, because she had spent six hours once letting one remember its own name. "Girl. Your family. Your grandmother's name on the map. Tell me again what the courier gave you. Exactly."

"A name. My grandmother's—Maren Marchetti. And a debt. He said the family owed the choir a soul, and the soul was—" Sebastian stopped. Started again, quieter. "He said the soul was mine. Or half of mine. He died before he could say more."

"Twins," Ilse said. "You were born a twin."

Sebastian stared at her. "How could you possibly—"

"Because bound souls don't move water," Ilse said, "but a soul that was *split* does. Half a soul in the choir, half walking around Hollowmere talking to crows—one piece always pulling at the other. That's your Anchor Voice. That's why the water rises. It's not fighting the binding, girl. It's reaching for you." She picked up the map, the blood-seal warm under her thumb, and felt the whole shape of the thing settle into place, terrible and clean. "Vaelor feeds the choir stolen souls to power his armies—we've known that since Wren's ledgers. But your twin isn't fuel. She's a keystone. Pull her free and the whole arch comes down."

The den was silent except for the drip of the culverts and the otter's slow breathing in its barrel.

"That's the bargain, then," Corvo said at last, heavily, the way men speak when they've decided something their bodies haven't agreed to yet. "New moon's in five days. Tithe-convoy leaves from the Salt Gate. I know the countersigns; I wrote half of them." He looked around the lamplit room—at the disgraced songsmithe, the thieving clerk, the witch-girl and her parliament of beasts—and shook his head like a man laughing at his own funeral. "We ride up as grain merchants, and we come down as the end of the world."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, boots were already moving through the tannery yard. The hunters had found the grate.

Ilse snuffed the lamp.
