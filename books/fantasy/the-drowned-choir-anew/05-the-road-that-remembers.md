## 5. The Road That Remembers

The Weeping Pass began where the road gave up pretending to be a road.

Sebastian felt the change before she saw it—a pressure in her teeth, a hum beneath the hooves, like standing too close to a struck bell. Bracken's ears swiveled forward, then back, then flat against his skull, and when he spoke, his voice came low and uneasy through the bond they'd built over weeks of shared oats and shared fear.

*The ground remembers something here,* he said. *It doesn't like remembering.*

"I know," Sebastian murmured, keeping her voice under the wind. Ilse rode behind her on the pack mule, hood up, hands buried in her sleeves. Corvo walked at the head of the column with his soldier's stride, as if walking were a discipline he could still believe in.

Above them, the pass walls rose sheer and gray, streaked with mineral tears that ran down the stone in long silver lines—the weeping the pass was named for, though the drovers' songs told another story. The water wasn't rain-fed. It rose from springs deep in the rock, and it carried taste: iron, ash, salt. The taste of old blood, Corvo had said, though he'd said it quietly, looking away, as if confessing to a crime no one had accused him of yet.

The crows had refused to come this far. Sebastian had asked them—asked properly, with the courtesy her grandmother's margins taught her—and they'd answered with a single word she'd turned over for two days before understanding it.

*Unwilling.*

Not afraid. Unwilling. Crows were not cowards; they simply declined, the way one declines an invitation to a funeral where one knew all the mourners. Whatever had happened in the Weeping Pass, the birds had already grieved it.

They climbed through the morning, and the land grew louder. Not sound, exactly—Sebastian had no gift for stone, no way to hear what the mountain heard—but memory has weight, and weight presses. By midday she felt it in her joints like weather coming. The others felt nothing, or claimed to. Only Wren, the clerk who'd fled the court with ledgers strapped to his chest like armor, kept glancing at the cliff faces as though expecting them to open.

"They brought them through here," Corvo said suddenly, stopping. His voice cracked the silence like a branch. "The tithe-convoy road. Before the tunnel was cut, before the funiculars—they marched them over the pass."

"Marched who?" Ilse asked, though Sebastian suspected she already knew. Songsmithes always knew more than they admitted.

"The souls." Corvo turned to face them, and the light caught the scar that split his jaw. "The living ones. The ones Vaelor's priests took whole—not dead souls, not ghosts. Living people, emptied. They walked them up this road in chains, singing the binding-songs as they went, because the choir had to be fed while the vessels still breathed. And the mountain—" He stopped. Swallowed. "The mountain drank it in. Every scream. Every prayer. It soaked into the stone, and now the stone can't stop bleeding it back out."

Sebastian looked at the silver streaks running down the cliffs and understood, finally, why the water tasted of iron. The pass wept because it had been made to watch.

"You know this how?" Ilse demanded. Her voice had the edge it always took when she smelled a lie—or a truth someone was carrying like a wound they hadn't shown anyone.

Corvo didn't answer right away. He stood facing the cliff wall, and Sebastian watched his shoulders, watched the way they set themselves, the way a man squares up to take a blow he knows is coming.

"Because I drove the wagons," he said. "Eleven years. Tithe-convoy, second rank. I told you I guarded the mountain road. I didn't tell you what the road was *for*."

The silence that followed was total. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

Ilse's face went hard. Wren flinched as though struck. Sebastian felt something cold move through her—not anger, not yet, but its precursor, the way frost precedes snow.

"You delivered them," she said. "Living people. To be emptied."

"Yes."

"And you're telling us now, halfway up a mountain, because—"

"Because the pass remembers," Corvo said, "and I'm tired of being the only thing here that won't." He pulled off his glove and pressed his bare hand flat against the wet stone. Held it there. Sebastian watched his arm tremble, watched him stand in whatever the stone gave back to him, and when he stepped away his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking you to know who's walking with you. If you want me gone, say it now, and I'll go down the mountain alone and let the hunters have me. That's penance enough, maybe. Maybe not. But you should choose knowing."

Sebastian thought of the courier dying at her door. Thought of the map, the name in the margin—her grandmother's name, written in a hand that shook. Everyone on this mountain had sold something. Everyone had been sold. The difference between Corvo and the priests was only that Corvo had come back down and couldn't stop hearing the pass weep.

"We keep you," she said at last. "But you walk at the front. You look at every mile of this road, and you don't look away again."

Corvo bowed his head. It wasn't gratitude, exactly. It was the posture of a man accepting a sentence he'd requested.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, and the beasts found them.

That was the strange mercy of the high country: where crows would not go, other things did. A marmot came first, bold and fat, and sat at the edge of the firelight regarding Sebastian with the frank appraisal of a creature deciding whether she was worth the trouble. Then a pair of choughs, black-winged and red-beaked, wheeling down from the crags to land on a boulder and tilt their heads at her.

She spoke to them. Of course she spoke to them—it was the only thing she'd ever been able to do, the one gift the covenant hadn't stripped from her, the one thing that had made her life in Hollowmere both bearable and impossible. But out here, past the wardline, past the valley's dampening shroud, the speaking came easier. Fuller. The marmot didn't just answer; it *conversed*, with opinions about weather and grievances about eagles and a detailed report on everything it had seen along the eastern scree.

And what it had seen was soldiers.

"There's a column coming up the south fork," Sebastian told the others, relaying the marmot's account. "Twenty, maybe thirty. Hunter-livery, black and bone-white. They're moving slow, sweeping the switchbacks. They'll reach the saddle by tomorrow noon."

Ilse went pale. "Hunters. This far up?"

"They're following us." Wren hugged the ledgers tighter. "The convoy disguise bought us days, not distance. Someone at the outer court flagged the missing tithe-wagons."

Corvo spread the map across his knees, weighing options aloud, and Sebastian let him talk while she listened to the choughs. Birds saw the world in patterns humans missed—thermals and ridgelines, the geometry of pursuit. One of them, the older bird, the one with the notch in its beak, kept returning to a single image: a goat track, barely a track at all, threading the north face above the main road. Steep. Exposed. Narrow enough that a misstep meant a long fall onto the rocks below.

Narrow enough that twenty-nine soldiers could not follow in column.

"It wants us to climb," Sebastian said, interrupting Corvo's debate. "There's a path over the shoulder of the peak. The birds use it. The hunters won't expect it, because men don't ask birds for directions."

Ilse stared at her. "You trust a chough's sense of a safe route?"

"I trust that it flies these cliffs every day of its life and has never once fallen off them. Can you say the same of any guide you've hired?"

A muscle worked in Ilse's jaw. Then, grudgingly: "Fair."

They broke camp before dawn, in the blue-black hour when the stars still ruled. Bracken carried Ilse and the packs; Sebastian climbed on foot, one hand on the mule's neck, listening to the mountain through the soles of her boots. The choughs flew ahead, two dark commas against the paling sky, marking the line of the track with their shadows.

For six hours it held. The track was terrible—loose shale, ledges wide enough for one boot, drops that made Wren walk pressed to the wall with his eyes shut—but it was *theirs*, a secret stitched into the mountain by hooves and wings, and the hunters below shrank to a line of ants crawling the wrong road.

Then the ambush found them anyway.

Not on the track. Above it. Sebastian never learned how many—six, maybe eight, hunters who'd split from the column and scaled the scree in the night, patient as lichen, waiting where the path bent around a spur. She heard the crossbow before she understood it: a wet mechanical snap, and a bolt hissing past her ear close enough to part her hair.

"DOWN!" Corvo roared, and threw himself sideways into the hunters' line like a landslide given legs.

What followed happened too fast for memory to sort into order. Steel in the narrow light. Ilse screaming words that were half song and half curse—her voice, the disgraced voice, the voice she'd traded pieces of, doing what it still could. Wren flattening himself over the ledgers like a mother over a child. Sebastian on her knees in the shale, heart slamming, reaching without thinking for the only weapon she had.

She called.

Not a word. Not a command. Something older—a cry pitched to the frequency of wild things, the sound she'd used once, years ago, to turn a boar from a neighbor's child. The cliffs answered. From the crags above came a shrieking descent of choughs, dozens of them, diving at the hunters' eyes and hands, and from somewhere below, from the goat-track's lower reaches, a bellowing that Sebastian recognized with a horror that arrived before the sight did.

Bracken.

He shouldn't have been able to climb that fast. He shouldn't have been able to climb at all, loaded as he was, but love does not consult physics. He came up the track like a rockfall with a purpose, and when a hunter broke from the melee and leveled his crossbow at Sebastian—at her, at the girl who could speak to beasts, the prize the edicts priced higher than gold—Bracken put his body between the bolt and her chest.

The bolt took him high in the shoulder, angling down.

Sebastian heard the sound he made, and some part of her that had lived through her parents' deaths, through the covenant's silences, through every loss Hollowmere had dealt her, simply closed like a door.

She didn't remember killing the hunter. The others swore she never touched him—that the choughs drove him off the ledge, that the mountain itself seemed to shrug him loose. What she remembered was kneeling in the shale beside Bracken with her hands pressed to the wound, feeling the heat of his blood push up between her fingers, hearing him try to speak and getting only a thin, ragged thread of voice:

*Worth it,* he managed. *Told you. Worth it.*

"Don't you dare," she whispered. "Don't you dare make this a bargain. You're not payment. You're not a cost. You're Bracken."

He breathed. Shallowly. The bolt had missed the lung by a finger's width, but the shoulder was ruined, and the pass was another day from any help, and there was no help on this mountain that wasn't owned by the thing they were climbing to fight.

Ilse knelt across from her, sleeves bloody to the elbow, doing what a songsmithe could do—which was not flesh-mending but something adjacent, humming a binding-note into the wound that slowed the bleeding, that told the torn edges of him to hold. "He'll live," she said, "if we get him off this rock and keep the wound clean. But he doesn't carry anything again. Not ever. And he walks slow, and we walk with him."

Sebastian sat back on her heels and looked at her hands, red to the wrist, and understood the shape of the day. The first cost. Not the last. She had known, since the courier died at her door, that this road would charge tolls—but knowing is a thin garment against the cold of actually paying.

Corvo dragged the hunters' bodies off the track and stripped them of anything useful. Wren counted the ledgers twice, hands shaking. The choughs settled on the rocks around Bracken like a guard of honor, and the marmot returned, and Sebastian spoke to all of them, thanking them in the formal cadences her grandmother's margins had taught her, and the oldest chough cocked its head and answered with something that stayed with her long after the fire was lit and the camp made small and hidden in the hollow below the spur.

*The mountain saw,* the bird said. *The mountain keeps what it sees. When you go down into the dark place, girl—it will remember you kindly.*

Below them, far down the south fork, the hunter column had stopped. Through Ilse's spyglass they watched the officers gather around something—a messenger, a fresh rider, a scrap of paper. Even at this distance Sebastian saw the moment the column's posture changed: the weary trudge becoming purpose, the sweep becoming a hunt with a fixed point.

"They've had orders," Corvo said grimly. "New ones. They're not chasing us anymore. They're heading somewhere."

The column wheeled. It did not continue up the pass toward the saddle. It turned east, toward the high road that ringed the mountain's shoulder—the road that led, Wren realized aloud, staring at his own stolen maps with dawning dread, directly to the outer court. Directly to the gates they had planned to slip through disguised as a tithe-convoy.

Directly to the one place, in all the world, where Sebastian Marchetti had to arrive before they did.

"Why?" she asked. "Why would they abandon the chase?"

Wren smoothed the map flat and traced the roads with a grimy finger, and when he spoke, his voice was quiet and terrible. "Because someone told them we're coming. Because someone at the court knows exactly what we're carrying—" he tapped the ledgers, then tapped the space beside them, the empty air where a bloodstained map had ridden for a hundred miles, "—and who we are. And they want us to arrive. They want the choir to meet the twin."

The fire crackled. Bracken breathed, shallow and stubborn. And somewhere beneath their feet, deeper than any mine, the drowned choir was singing—and Sebastian, lying awake in the dark, could almost hear which voice among the hundreds had just changed its tune.
