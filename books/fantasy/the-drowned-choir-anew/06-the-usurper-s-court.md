## 6. The Usurper's Court

Corvo had walked this road before, and the road remembered him.

That was the trouble with the mountain road to Vaelor's court—it was paved with the boots of everyone who had ever marched it, and stone kept what flesh forgot. He felt it through his soles as he led the tithe-convoy around the first switchback: a faint wrongness, like walking over a grave that hadn't finished settling. Three years ago he had marched this same grade in Vaelor's gray, one of forty soldiers escorting a wagon of chained men and women up toward the outer gates, and he had told himself then what all of them told themselves: *it's only bookkeeping. It's only the tithe.*

Now he was the one driving the wagon, and there was no one left to lie to.

"Keep your eyes down," he murmured to the others without turning his head. "Convoy drivers don't look at the gates. Convoy drivers look at their mules."

Sebastian, walking beside Bracken under a borrowed drover's hood, gave the smallest nod. Ilse rode in the wagon bed itself, wrapped in a choir-priest's stolen vestments, her face hidden beneath a hood of white wool—the one disguise Corvo had sworn wouldn't work until she'd put it on and become, instantly, someone he flinched away from. She wore the priesthood the way a knife wears its edge. Beside her sat Wren's cage—or rather, the birdcage they'd bought in the last market town, empty, which Ilse claimed every priest traveling down-mountain was required to carry. "For the soul that misbehaves," she'd said, and laughed in a way that made even Corvo glad she was on their side.

The outer court opened before them at midday: a shelf of black granite carved out of the mountainside and walled with iron, holding barracks, storehouses, a chapel whose spire had been sheared off and capped with lead, and—above everything, cut into the cliff face itself—the Gate of Ledgers.

It was not a gate for people. Corvo knew that now, though he hadn't understood it the first time he'd passed beneath it. The archway was lined with thousands of bronze plates, each one stamped with a name, a date, and a price. Names of the sold. Dates of the selling. Prices paid by the crown. The wind moved through the arch and made a sound almost like whispering, almost like reading aloud.

He heard Sebastian stop breathing when she saw it.

"Eyes on your mule," he said again, gentler this time.

They passed under the names. Corvo counted his own footsteps the way he always did, an old soldier's habit, and somewhere around step two hundred he realized he was counting them as penance—one for each soul he'd helped deliver, though there had been more souls than steps, more than he would ever let himself total up.

* * *

The tithe-yard stank of tallow and iron. They unloaded under the eyes of yard-sergeants who barely glanced at them—convoy crews were furniture here, interchangeable, invisible—and Corvo learned in the first hour that invisibility had rules. Drivers ate apart from guards. Guards ate apart from priests. Priests ate alone. And above all of it, above the barracks and the yards and the sheared chapel, stood the Records House, a long low building of pale stone with windows too narrow for escape and too wide, Corvo thought, for anything honest to happen inside.

It was in the Records House that the ledger-keepers worked, and it was to the Records House that Ilse, in her stolen vestments, announced she must go to verify the convoy's tithe-seals. The yard-sergeant waved her through with a bored salute. Corvo went as her porter, carrying her document case, head bowed, a big dumb ox of a man who existed nowhere at all.

Inside, the horror was not blood. That was the thing he needed Sebastian to understand later, and couldn't find words for yet. The horror was ink. Rows of desks. Rows of clerks. The soft scratch of nibs from morning to dusk. On the walls hung schedules—the choir feeding schedule, rotation of the binding-cells, quarterly quotas by province, all written in a fine, patient hand. Everything orderly. Everything accounted for. Somewhere below their feet, hundreds of stolen souls sang Vaelor's power into being, and up here the only evidence was the smell of lamp-oil and the tidy click of an abacus.

One clerk worked at the desk nearest the archive door. Young—younger than Corvo, barely more than a boy—with ink-stained fingers and the hollow-eyed diligence of someone who had done nothing else for years. His name-plate read WREN. He copied without pause, page after page, each entry identical in format:

*Name. Province. Date received. Price paid. Voice-quality (per assessor). Disposition.*

Disposition. Corvo had once asked a sergeant what disposition meant, back when asking questions still felt safe. The sergeant had smiled. *Where they end up, son. The choir keeps what it keeps.*

Wren never looked up. But as Ilse presented her seals at the counter, Corvo—waiting, watching, a porter with nothing to do but see—noticed what three years of soldiering had trained him never to notice: that the boy's copying hand changed whenever the supervisor crossed the room. In public view, Wren wrote fast and loose. When the supervisor's back turned, his letters shrank, tightened, became something else—a compressed, private shorthand, squeezed into margins, into interlinear gaps, into the ruled space beneath the official line where no auditor ever looked.

He wasn't copying the records. He was *copying the copies.* Every name. Every price. Every voice-quality, every disposition. Hiding a second true ledger inside the first false one, stroke by stroke, day after day, in a room full of people who would kill him slowly if they ever read his margins.

Ilse saw it too. Corvo watched her see it—watched her gaze snag on the boy's hands and stay there, the way a songsmithe's ear snags on a note sung a quarter-tone flat. She said nothing. But when they left the Records House, she walked slower than she needed to, and her eyes were wet with something that wasn't fear.

* * *

That night, in the drivers' quarters, the plan bent.

"The clerk," Ilse said. She sat on the floor with her back against Bracken's stall, still in the priest's vestments because taking them off felt, to all of them, like a small surrender. "The young one. Wren."

"He's nobody," Corvo said. "A functionary."

"A functionary who has spent years building us a weapon we could never have built ourselves." Her voice dropped. "Every soul sold under Vaelor, named and priced, in his own hand. Do you know what that is? That's testimony. That's a trial waiting to happen. We don't need to steal proof from this place—we need to walk out with the man who *is* the proof."

"And how," Sebastian asked quietly, from the shadows near Bracken's head, "do you propose we convince him to leave? He has a desk. A bed. A life that doesn't end on a rope."

Ilse looked at her for a long moment. "Because I heard his pen," she said. "I'm a songsmithe, girl, or I was. Every hand has a rhythm, and his breaks—just slightly, just once per page—at every entry marked *disposition: choir.* He grieves them. One hesitation each. Hundreds a month." She folded her hands. "A man like that doesn't need convincing. He needs a door."

* * *

The door came sooner than any of them expected, and it opened itself.

Two nights later, Corvo stood the late watch in the tithe-yard and saw the Records House lamp burn past midnight, past the hour when clerks were locked to their dormitory, past the hour when anything lawful happened in that building. Then a shadow slipped along the wall—small, quick, clutching a satchel to its chest like a wounded animal—and stopped dead when it came within arm's reach of him.

Wren. Ink to the elbows, eyes enormous, breathing like a runner.

"I know what you are," the boy whispered. "All of you. I've known since the convoy came in—I checked the seal against the registry, it was retired eleven years ago, and priests don't travel with empty cages unless the cage matters more than the bird." His voice cracked and steadied. "I have four years of entries. Every name. Every price. Every disposition. Including—" he swallowed, fumbled the satchel open with shaking hands, including pages copied so many times they'd gone soft as cloth, "—including the provincial births. The ones taken at birth, before registration. Sold by family consent, sealed under grandmother's mark."

Corvo went very still. Above them the Gate of Ledgers whispered in the wind, reading its ten thousand names to the dark.

"There's a woman in the valley provinces," Wren said. "Old record. Twin birth. One child registered, healthy. One child entered as *disposition: choir, price waived—kin's contribution.* The seller's mark in the margin—" he turned the page toward Corvo, toward where Sebastian stood frozen in the doorway behind him, and read the name aloud into the cold night air:

"Marchetti. Serafina Marchetti. Grandmother. Seller."

The wind rose through the bronze arch, and for one moment the whole outer court seemed to murmur it back.

Behind Corvo, he heard Sebastian make a sound he had never heard a person make—not grief, exactly, and not rage, but both arriving at once at the same closed door. And Wren clutched his satchel of four years of patient, orderly, damning truth and asked the only question left to ask:

"Which way is down?"
