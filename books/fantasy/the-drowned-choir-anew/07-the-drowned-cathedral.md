## 7. The Drowned Cathedral

The stair went down farther than any stair had a right to.

Sebastian counted the steps at first—two hundred, then three hundred, then she lost the count when the air changed. It grew heavy, wet, tasting of stone and something older beneath the stone, something that had been drowning for a very long time and had made its peace with it. The walls wept. Not water, exactly—water would have been kinder—but a slow seepage that gleamed in Ilse's lantern-light like sap, like resin, like the amber tears of trees that remembered being forests.

"Mind where you put your hands," Corvo said behind her, his voice pitched low in the way soldiers learned to speak underground. "The old courts liked their decorations sharp."

She didn't need to be told. The galleries flanking the stair were lined with statues, and every one of them was singing.

Not aloud. Not with mouths, though some of them had mouths, frozen open mid-note by sculptors who must have heard the choir through the mountain's bones. The singing came from everywhere and nowhere, from the water itself, from the seams between the stones. It was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, Sebastian thought—vast and patient and utterly indifferent to whether you survived it.

Hundreds of voices. She could feel them now, not hear them so much as *know* them, the way she knew Bracken's breathing or the crows' gossip at the border stones. But these were no beasts. These were souls, hundreds upon hundreds of them, woven into a single sustained chord that never ended because it was never allowed to end. And threaded through the chord, holding it taut, was a power she recognized from the witch-hunters' amulets, from Vaelor's coin, from every edict nailed to every door in the valley: the usurper's magic, sung into being, note by stolen note.

*He builds his armies on this,* she thought. *Every soldier who marches under him walks on the backs of singers.*

Beside her, Wren clutched the copied ledgers to her chest as though they might warm her. The young clerk had gone pale somewhere around the two-hundredth step and had not recovered her color since. "I filed these," she whispered. "For eleven years I copied these names and told myself it was just... record-keeping. That someone, someday, would read them and make it right." Her voice cracked on the last word. "I never listened. Gods forgive me, I never once stopped to listen."

"You're listening now," Sebastian said.

Wren nodded, wiped her face with the back of her hand, and kept walking.

* * *

The stair opened onto the first flooded gallery, and Sebastian's breath left her all at once.

Water stretched before them—black, still, mirror-flat—filling what had clearly once been a cathedral nave of impossible size. Pillars rose out of it like the legs of drowned giants, their capitals lost in shadow far above, where the vaulted ceiling hung draped in stalactites that glittered when the lantern swung. Along both walls, arched windows that should have held glass instead held waterfalls, thin silver curtains falling forever into the flood without raising so much as a ripple.

And floating on the water—no. Not floating. *Standing* in it, waist-deep, arranged in tiers that descended toward a distant altar-island—were figures.

They did not move. They did not breathe, or if they breathed, it was in unison, one great shared inhalation that lifted the surface of the flood in a single gentle swell and let it fall. Their faces were turned upward, eyes closed or open or absent; at this distance Sebastian couldn't tell which, and found she didn't want to know. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, a pale underwater luminescence rising off the singers themselves, and in it their skin shimmered faintly, like moonlight on fish scales, like the inside of shells.

"The choir," Ilse breathed. The songsmithe stood at the gallery's edge with an expression Sebastian had never seen on her sharp face—reverence and horror fighting for the same ground. "Gods below. I trained six years at the court conservatory and they never told us. They taught us harmonics for *blessing*, for binding wards, and never—" She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "Every hymn I ever wrote fed this. Every song I sang in that court gave them the tuning."

"Then you can give us the detuning," Corvo said flatly. He had his hand on his sword hilt, though against this, Sebastian thought, a sword was about as useful as a whisper.

Ilse didn't answer. She was staring at the water with the look of a woman doing arithmetic she despised.

Sebastian stepped closer to the edge.

The moment her boot touched the flooded gallery's first submerged flagstone, everything changed.

The chord faltered.

It was small—a hitch, a skipped beat, the way a flock of starlings stutters mid-turn—and then it *found* her. Sebastian felt it happen, felt the attention of several hundred stolen souls swing toward her like a compass needle finding north, and the sound that had been everywhere became intimate, directional, *aimed*. The water trembled. The lantern-flame leaned toward the flood as if bowing.

And out of the vast, layered, centuries-deep chord, one voice separated.

It was high and clear and young—younger than Sebastian herself, though it carried an ache that no young voice should own—and it rose above the others the way a single lark rises out of a morning full of crows. It did not sing words. It sang her name.

Not *Sebastian*. No one down here could know *Sebastian*.

"*Sefa.*"

The name hit her like cold water to the sternum. Only two people alive had ever called her that—one was dead, the other had sold a soul before Sebastian was old enough to hold a spoon. It was the name her grandmother used in the rare soft hours, the name stitched inside Sebastian's second-best cloak where no witch-hunter would think to search. A family name. A *hidden* name.

Her knees nearly went.

"Sebastian?" Corvo's hand landed on her shoulder. "What is it? What did it say?"

"She said my name." Sebastian's voice came out strange, scraped thin. "My true name."

"That's impossible. The ledgers don't carry true names, I'd have seen—"

"They wouldn't need to." Ilse had gone very still. "True names aren't written, girl. They're *sung*, at a christening, into the child's blood. If that voice knows yours..." She trailed off, and the silence finished the sentence for her: *then it shares your blood.*

Bracken whickered softly behind them—the mule had refused to descend past the last dry landing, standing now at the top of the stair like a sentinel—and even from here Sebastian felt his worry come down the stones to her like warmth through a wall. *Steady, old man,* she sent back. *I'm steady.* She wasn't sure it was true.

She walked to the water's edge.

Corvo started after her and Ilse caught his arm. Later, Sebastian would remember that—remember how they let her go, the two most guarded people she'd ever met, letting her walk alone toward the thing they'd crossed half a kingdom to find, because some part of them understood that this was not a moment that admitted witnesses.

* * *

Up close, the nearest singers resolved into terrible detail.

A woman with grey in her hair, her lips parted around a note she had been holding, by the look of her, for decades. A boy no older than twelve whose feet had never touched mud or grass, only this water, only this dark. An old man whose face was carved into an expression of such perfect peace that Sebastian understood, with a lurch, that it was not peace at all—it was surrender worn smooth by time, the way riverstones go smooth, until only the shape of giving-up remained.

Their eyes were open. All of them. And none of them saw.

Except—

At the third tier, half-drowned in the pale light, a figure turned her head.

She was Sebastian's age, or would have been. Her hair was the same dark, unruly dark, floating in the water like ink poured into ink. Her face was Sebastian's face—not similar, not suggestive, but *the same*, the way the two halves of a broken seal are the same, each carrying the mark the other lacks. When their eyes met, the whole cathedral seemed to inhale.

"*Sefa,*" the soul said again, and this time it was not a song. It was a voice. A sister's voice, rusty from disuse, wondering at itself. "You came. You actually came. Grandmother said you wouldn't. Grandmother said you'd never even *know*."

"I didn't know." The words tore coming out. "I didn't know you existed until four weeks ago. I got a map from a dying man and your name was in the margins and I thought—I thought I was chasing a debt. I didn't know I was chasing *you*."

"I know." The twin smiled, and the smile was Sebastian's smile, and it broke her heart in a place she hadn't known she had. "I've been listening to you anyway. All your life. Did you never wonder why the beasts came so easily to you? Why the covenant never quite took? You were never wholly in the valley, Sefa. Half of you has been down here the whole time, singing. We leaked into each other. We're a cracked bell—we ring wrong wherever we hang."

Sebastian sank to her knees at the waterline. The flood touched her fingertips, cool and impossibly smooth, and through it she felt the choir's chord vibrate up her arm and into her chest, and beneath the chord, faint as a heartbeat under blankets, the pulse of the one who wore her face.

"What's your name?" she whispered. "Your true one?"

"Sable." The twin's eyes glistened. "Grandmother named us both the same night. Named me into the mountain's ledger and you into hiding. Two names, one night. Do you understand what that makes us, Sefa? Not twins. *Halves.* She cut the coin in two before either of us could spend ourselves."

"Why?" It came out as almost a scream, swallowed by the nave. "Why sell you? Why hide me? Why any of it?"

"Because the mountain takes a tithe from every bloodline that ever bargained with it, and ours bargained deep. One soul per generation, chosen by the choir itself. And the choir chose me—chose the louder half, the singing half. So Grandmother did the only merciful thing left. She hid you where magic cannot follow. She made you small and quiet and invisible, because invisible things don't get tithed." Sable's voice gentled. "She didn't sell me, Sefa. She *traded* me for you. And she died believing you'd hate her for it."

"I could never—"

"You might. You might yet." The twin drifted closer, close enough that the water between them thinned to nothing, close enough that Sebastian could see the ledger-brand on Sable's collarbone: a small sigil, a mountain with a mouth. "Listen to me. We don't have long. The keeper feels you—he's felt you since the stair. When he comes, there will be a bargain offered, and it will be dressed in mercy, and it will be a trap, and Ilse will see it first but no one will listen to her, because no one ever listens to the disgraced." Sable's fingers found Sebastian's through the water—cold, cold, so cold, and yet gripping with desperate human strength. "There will be a choice. There's always a choice, that's how the bindings work—they need consent, freely given. Remember that. Whatever he offers, whatever shape the choice wears—*it needs your consent.* Refusal is a weapon down here. It may be the only one."

"Sable—"

"He's coming." The twin's face went taut. Around them, tier by tier, the choir's heads were turning—all of them now, hundreds of faces rotating with glacial slowness toward the far archway, where a new light was kindling, gold and greasy, nothing like the pale glow of the singers. The chord bent itself around the approaching presence the way grass bends around a descending boot. "He's coming, and Sefa—whatever you feel when you see what they've built him into—don't pity him. Pity is consent wearing a kind face."

The gold light swelled. Somewhere above, Corvo shouted a warning, and Bracken screamed from the top of the stair—a sound Sebastian had never heard the mule make, a sound like a door tearing off its hinges.

Sable's grip tightened one last time.

"Sing loud, little sister," she said. "Even if it's the last thing you do. *Especially* if it's the last thing you do."

And the keeper of the drowned choir came walking across the water.
