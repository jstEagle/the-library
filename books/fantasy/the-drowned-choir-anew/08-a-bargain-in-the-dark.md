## 8. A Bargain in the Dark

Ilse had spent eleven years learning what a voice was worth, and the choir-priests taught her the rest of it in a single night.

They took her in the flooded gallery below the third landing, where the water ran black and still as poured glass. She had been walking point with her lantern hooded, humming under her breath the way she always did—old habit, old comfort—and the hum had carried. Of course it had carried. Sound traveled strangely down here, along the water and the stone, and everything in this mountain was listening. Everything in this mountain had been listening for years.

The priests came out of the dark without splashing. That was the first horror: men wading through knee-deep water in silence, their white robes dry as paper, their faces smooth and patient as eggs. They did not speak. One of them simply opened his hands, and Ilse's voice left her.

Not her breath—her *voice*. She felt it go like a hook drawn out of flesh, a warm wet thread pulled up through her throat and into his palm, where it coiled, glowing faintly gold, singing her own three-note hum back at her in a stranger's timbre. She had crafted voices for the court once. She had bound songs into steel and stone and soldier's blood. She had never once considered what it would be to have hers held in someone else's hand.

They walked her down through galleries that grew older as they descended, the architecture shifting from Vaelor's clean imperial lines to something far, far older—arches like ribs, walls furred with pale moss that glowed when she passed, as if the mountain itself were turning in its sleep. The water rose. Her boots filled. Somewhere below them, always below, the choir sang.

She had heard choirs before. She had trained them. This was not that. This was hundreds of voices braided into a single sustained tone that never ended, never breathed, and underneath the beauty of it—the unbearable, aching beauty of it—Ilse heard the structure, because she could not help hearing structure the way a mason cannot help seeing cracks. It was a binding. A vast, patient, perfectly maintained binding, note laid upon note upon note, and every stolen soul in the drowned cathedral was a load-bearing wall.

*Vaelor's power has a foundation,* she thought, *and I am standing on it.*

They brought her to a chamber where the ceiling opened into blackness and the water was chest-deep and warm as blood. A figure stood on a dais at its center—a priest taller than the others, robed in white gone grey at the hem, as though he had been standing in this water for a very long time. His face was kind. That was the worst part. He had the mild, reasonable face of a man about to discuss terms.

"You are a songsmithe," he said. His voice was the choir's voice, all of them at once, gently out of phase with itself. "We tasted your craft in your humming. Court-trained. They do not make your kind anymore."

"They killed us instead," Ilse said. Her own voice sounded thin and far away. "I was told you'd know that. You keep the ledgers."

"We keep everything." He smiled without warmth or malice, the way tide smiles. "You came here following a map drawn by a dead courier, carrying a girl who hears beasts, toward a soul that answers to her blood. Do not look surprised. The water tells us things. The water has been telling us about Sebastian Marchetti since before she was born."

Ilse's heart went cold and quick. "Then you know why we've come."

"I know what you intend," the keeper said. "And I know what you will cost me." He stepped down from the dais, and the water parted around him with a courtesy that made her skin crawl. "So let us not waste the night. I have an offer, songsmithe, and you are the only one of your company positioned to consider it."

"Consider it for whom?"

"For everyone." He raised his open hand, and above his palm her stolen voice hovered, coiled and gleaming, humming her mother's lullaby—the one she had never sung for anyone, the one she thought had drowned with Vell. "Your companions live. The girl reaches the cathedral. The clerk keeps his stolen pages. The soldier walks out of my mountain. In exchange—" his fingers closed slowly around the light, "—a songsmithe's voice. Freely given. Fully given. Not a piece of it, not a bargain-cut fragment. All of it. Every song you will ever make, surrendered to the choir, to be woven into the binding forever."

The chamber was silent except for the choir below, that endless sustained tone, and Ilse understood suddenly what she was looking at. The binding needed maintenance. It needed *craft*. The priests were custodians, not creators—they could hold the notes but they could no longer make them, and somewhere in the last century the making had died out, and the great binding beneath the mountain had been running on patchwork ever since. Running thin. Running tired.

That was why Vaelor's magic worked at all, and that was why it frayed at the edges. And that was why they wanted her.

"You're dying," she said quietly. "All of you. The whole arrangement. You need a new mason."

"We need a willing one," the keeper said. "A coerced voice sours the work. We learned that lesson across four hundred years of other people's coercion." His kind face tilted. "Freely given, songsmithe. That is the price of everything you want."

*Everything you want.* She thought of Corvo, who flinched at bells. Of Wren, who clutched her copied pages like a child clutching a knife. Of Bracken, bleeding somewhere above them in the dark, if the mule even lived. Of Sebastian Marchetti, who had crossed half the world on the strength of a name in a margin, wanting her twin.

Wanting everything. They all wanted everything. It was the disease of the desperate.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the water takes the girl tonight, before she reaches the heart. Gently. She will simply walk into the deep gallery and keep walking, and the choir will gain a voice that answers to her twin, and the binding will hold another decade, and Vaelor will raze the free cities with a power you might have ended." He said it without threat, the way a physician reads a prognosis. "I do not want that. But I am a keeper, not a merciful man. There is a difference, and you of all people should appreciate it."

Ilse looked at her voice hanging in his fist. Eleven years ago she had stood in Vaelor's court and watched a nine-year-old girl be sold into this same water while the court applauded the harmony, and Ilse had written the transposition herself, had made the sale *sound beautiful*, and had told herself for a decade that she'd had no choice.

There was always a choice. That was the whole obscenity of it.

"Yes," she said.

* * *

Sebastian hit the chamber before the keeper finished the rite.

Later, Ilse would learn how—Wren's maps, Corvo's knowledge of the guard rotations, a rat who had run word down through the flooded galleries faster than any human messenger—but in the moment there was only the door bursting inward and lantern-light slashing across the black water, and Sebastian Marchetti standing hip-deep with her sword still sheathed and her fury entirely undrawn, which was somehow more frightening than steel.

"No," Sebastian said.

The keeper turned, unhurried, Ilse's voice still cupped in his palm like a candle flame. "Daughter of Hollowmere. The water has been so eager to meet you."

"You don't get her voice." Sebastian waded forward, and the beasts came with her—a marsh-ferret dripping off her shoulder, two river-rats paddling at her hips like pilot fish, and above, in the high dark, the sudden pressure of wings. "Whatever deal you struck, it's void. She doesn't trade. None of us trade. That's the entire reason any of us are still alive."

"The alternative," the keeper said mildly, "is that none of you leave."

"Then say that plainly," Sebastian said, "instead of dressing it up as mercy. You want her voice because your binding is failing and you're too proud to admit the choir doesn't need a keeper anymore—it needs to be *freed*, and you can't bear it, because freeing it means admitting everything you've done was for nothing."

The water shivered. The choir below faltered—one note, half a beat, a stutter in that eternal tone—and Ilse saw the keeper's composure crack like ice under a boot.

"You do not understand what unbinding releases," he said.

"Show me," Sebastian said. "Or get out of our way."

It was Corvo who broke the moment—not the priest, not the girl. The soldier came through the doorway behind Sebastian, gray-faced, sword drawn and shaking, and said the words Ilse had been dreading since Vell:

"We should take the offer. Both offers. Ilse gives her voice, we walk out, we come back with an army—"

"With whose army?" Wren's voice, sharp, from the shadows. The clerk pushed forward, hugging her satchel of copied ledgers to her chest like a shield. "Vaelor owns every army. We have *proof*. Names, dates, prices paid—we have the record of every soul sold into this water, and if we die down here the record dies with us, and then it never happened, and then it happens again. The ledgers matter more than—"

"More than my sister?" Sebastian turned on her, and the lanterns guttered. "More than Ilse's voice? More than any of us? Wren, you've been reading those pages so long you've forgotten they're people."

"They're *dead* people!" Wren's composure tore straight down the middle. "They're dead and documented and the only thing they can still do is testify, and you want to spend that on a rescue that gets us all killed! Your twin is a note in a chord! I'm sorry—I'm sorry—but she is, and the chord holds up a tyrant, and the ledger is the only weapon that survives either way!"

The silence after that was total. Even the choir seemed to lean in.

Corvo lowered his sword halfway. "We flee," he said, quieter. "Tonight, while they're talking. I've run this road ten years. There's a service gallery behind the third cistern, it goes up past the tithe-yard, we can be out by dawn. Leave the map-burden. Leave the dead. Live, and fight later." His jaw worked. "I delivered souls to this place. I know exactly what living with leaving looks like. I wouldn't wish it on any of you."

"So that's us," Ilse said. Her throat ached around the hole where her voice had been, and she found she was furious—at the keeper, at Corvo, at Wren, at herself most of all, for saying *yes* in that black water, for being so ready, again, to pay with something precious so that nothing would have to change. "One of us wants to run. One wants the paperwork. One wants to sell herself. And one wants a ghost." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "We were never a company. We were four wants wearing one cloak."

"Don't," Sebastian said.

"Someone has to say it." Ilse turned to the keeper. "Give me back my voice."

"Ilse—" Sebastian started.

"Give it back. Now." When he hesitated, she stepped closer, chin up, close enough to see the fine grey rot at the hem of his robe, the truth of him. "Because I'm not giving it to you. I'm going to use it. Right here, right now, against your binding—and you're going to let me, keeper, because you heard my craft and you know I can find the seam. Or you can keep my voice in your fist and watch a beast-speaker and a deserter and a clerk tear your cathedral apart without me. Choose."

For a long moment the only sound was the water moving against their bodies, patient as centuries.

Then the keeper opened his hand.

Her voice came home like a swallowed ember, and Ilse gasped, staggered, caught herself on the dais—and began, before anyone could stop her, before she could stop herself, to sing.

She did not sing the binding. She sang *at* it—one long searching line thrown against that vast sustained chord below, hunting for the flaw, the patchwork, the decade-old repair where some lesser smith had spliced a stolen voice into the weave. The choir shuddered. Water climbed the walls. The keeper cried out—a real cry, an old man's cry—and the moss-light died in the galleries above, and somewhere far overhead, faint as memory, the mountain itself groaned.

And beneath everything, beneath the chord, beneath the water, one small clear voice that was not Ilse's called up through the flood:

*"Lala?"*

Sebastian went white. "That's—that's what my grandmother called—"

The choir broke key.

Every soul in the drowned cathedral stopped singing at once—for the first time in four hundred years, the binding fell silent—and in the ringing dark, Vaelor's armies on the far side of the world stumbled mid-march as their borrowed power cut out beneath them.

The keeper whispered, "What have you done," and the water began to rise.
