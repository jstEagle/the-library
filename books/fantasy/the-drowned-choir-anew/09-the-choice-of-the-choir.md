## 9. The Choice of the Choir

The water remembered her name before she did.

Sebastian waded into the flooded nave with her boots in one hand and her heart hammering against her ribs like a bird that had forgotten the shape of its cage. The cathedral stretched above and below her at once—pillars rising out of black water to vanish into a darkness that was not empty but *listening*. Somewhere far overhead, through miles of stone, Vaelor's armies were marching on the free cities, and every step of their march was paid for here, in this water, by these voices.

She could feel them now. All of them.

They were not ghosts. Ghosts, she thought, would have been kinder. These were souls—hundreds of them, thousands—suspended in the flooded dark like lanterns drowned against their will, each one singing a note it had not chosen. Together they made the sound she had been hearing since the Weeping Pass: a chord so vast and patient it had seemed like silence until she learned to listen underneath it. It was Vaelor's power. It was his armies' strength, his witch-hunters' sight, the binding that kept the mountain's court fat and the free cities afraid.

And beneath all of it, one voice was calling her.

*Sebastian.* Not her name, exactly. Something older, shaped like her name the way a river is shaped like the rain that feeds it. *Little sister. You came.*

Her breath caught. She had known—Wren's stolen ledgers had told her in ink and dates, in the terrible neatness of bureaucracy—but knowing was a ledger and this was a voice. Her twin. Sold at birth by their own grandmother's hand, entered into the choir's keeping before she had drawn her first full breath.

"I came," Sebastian said aloud, and the water carried the words in rings of pale light. "I'm sorry I'm late."

*Twenty-two years,* the voice said, and there was laughter in it, broken and bright as light on drowned glass. *You're very punctual for someone who didn't know I existed.*

Ilse's hand found hers in the dark—the songsmithe had insisted on coming this far, though her throat still bore the bruise-shadow of the keeper's bargain refused. Corvo stood at the gallery mouth above, sword drawn out of habit rather than use, watching the flooded stair they'd come down. Wren was somewhere behind with the ledgers clutched to her chest like a child. But here, in the water, it was only Sebastian and the voice.

"You know why I'm here," Sebastian whispered.

*I know what you were told you'd find.* A pause, long enough for the choir's chord to swell and bend around them. *A soul in a cage. Take it back, be whole, run. That's what the map promised, isn't it? What the dying man died carrying?*

"Yes."

*And do you know what taking me would mean?*

Sebastian looked down. The water was clear here, impossibly clear, and below her feet the floor of the cathedral showed through: a mosaic, ancient and vast, of a thousand figures with their mouths open, singing. She understood suddenly that the mosaic was not decoration. It was a diagram. The choir was the keystone. Every stolen soul sang a note of the binding, and the binding held Vaelor's magic together the way a keystone holds an arch—and if you pulled one stone—

"One note," Sebastian breathed. "If I take you, the chord breaks anyway. Just messily. Just cruelly."

*Clever little sister.* The voice gentled. *Or you can give me back. Give me to the choir—not to the keepers, never to the keepers—to the song itself. Freely. And a freely given soul is a note no binding can hold. It would crack the whole chord open. Every soul in this water would wake up singing their own name instead of his.*

"And Vaelor's power drowns," Sebastian said. "Mid-march. Mid-battlefield."

*Yes.*

"And you drown with it." Her voice cracked on the words. "That's the part everyone keeps leaving out. If the binding breaks while you're inside it—"

The voice was quiet a moment. When it spoke again, it sounded, for the first time, tired. Young. Twenty-two years old and tired.

*I have been singing someone else's song since before I was born. Do you understand what you're offering me? Not death. An ending. My own note, sung once, my way, and then—rest. Or whatever comes after rest.* A ripple moved across the water, touching Sebastian's knees like a small cold hand. *You would be giving me the only thing our family ever gave anyone in this place: a choice.*

* * *

Above them, faint through miles of stone, the mountain shuddered.

Corvo's voice came down the stair, tight as wire: "Sebastian! The whole peak just moved—Vaelor knows we're here. The keepers are coming down, and they are *not* walking."

Ilse gripped Sebastian's arm. "Whatever you two are deciding, decide faster."

But Sebastian wasn't listening anymore. She was kneeling in the water, both hands flat on its surface, and the choir was rising around her—not in body, for they had none, but in attention. Hundreds of presences turning toward her like flowers toward a window. She felt them the way she felt crows at the border stones, the way she felt Bracken's warm mind even now, miles away and bleeding, still stubbornly alive because she had told him to stay alive and he was too loyal to disobey.

All her life she had hidden her gift. All her life the covenant of Hollowmere had pressed down on her like a lid, and she had thought the hiding was mercy—hers to give, hers to receive. Now she knelt in the drowning-place of hundreds of souls and understood that invisibility had never been mercy at all. It had been a smaller cage, politely furnished.

"Tell me your name," she said to the water. "Your real one. Not the one the ledgers gave you."

The voice trembled.

*Maren,* it said. *My name is Maren.*

"Maren." Saying it aloud felt like setting a lamp in a window after years of darkness. "Our grandmother sold you. Our grandmother sold us both, in different ways—I was raised in a valley where nothing could hear me, and you were raised in a place where everything could, and none of it was ours to choose." She swallowed. "So here's the only inheritance I have to give you, and it isn't gold or land or even a life lived side by side. It's this: *you choose.*"

*Sebastian—*

"If you want to go home with me—if some part of you can be untangled from this chord and carried out whole—I will fight every keeper in this mountain to do it, and Ilse will write a ballad about it whether I survive or not." Behind her, Ilse made a sound that was half sob, half laugh. "And if what you want is to end it—if you want your own note, sung once, your way—then I give you back. Freely. No bargain. No price. A gift goes where it wills."

The choir went silent.

Not quiet—*silent*, the way the world goes silent when something enormous holds its breath. For the first time in twenty-two years, the drowned cathedral held no sound but water dripping from galleries no one had walked in centuries, and Sebastian's own ragged breathing, and the distant thunder of Vaelor's army rolling over the world above like a storm that believed itself eternal.

Then Maren laughed.

It was a small sound, human, unbound—a girl's laugh, twenty-two years old, hearing her own voice echo off stone for the first time.

*Freely given,* Maren said. *Freely returned. Watch, little sister. Listen. Sing with me—one verse. One verse of our own.*

Sebastian opened her mouth and gave back the only thing she had ever owned completely: her voice, her gift, the beast-speech that had made her strange and hunted and invisible. She did not speak to animals now. She spoke to her sister, in the old tongue under all tongues, the one every living thing knows and only the lonely remember.

Together they sang one verse.

It was nothing like the choir's vast engineered chord. It was rough and small and imperfect, a duet between a woman knee-deep in black water and a soul learning the shape of freedom, and it was the most beautiful thing the cathedral had ever heard, because it was *chosen*.

The binding cracked like river ice in spring.

* * *

Sound came back wrong—too much of it, all at once, from everywhere.

Every soul in the flooded dark woke singing its own name. Not the chorus Vaelor had built; a parliament, a riot, a storm of voices each gloriously distinct, and the binding that had woven them into his power unraveled thread by golden thread. Sebastian felt it go the way one feels a great weight lift from a long-bent back: the pressure that had filled the mountain, the hum in the stones, the sick sweet pull on the water—all of it draining away, running downhill, dissolving.

Far above, she knew without knowing how she knew, Vaelor's armies stopped mid-march. She felt it through the freed souls streaming past her like migrating birds: the witch-hunters' sight going dark, the war-beasts' bindings snapping, the great engine of stolen magic stalling on the battlefield with a sound like a cathedral bell dropped from the sky. Somewhere on the plains before the free cities, a usurper who had never lost anything reached for his power and found only his own two hands.

In the water around Sebastian, lights were rising. Hundreds of them, thousands, drifting up through the flooded galleries like lanterns finally allowed to float home. Some paused near her—brief, curious, grateful—and she felt touches like fingertips, like foreheads pressed to hers, like thanks in a hundred languages including several that belonged to beasts.

Maren rose last.

She gathered herself out of the water into a shape of light and outline—a young woman with Sebastian's jaw and their grandmother's stubborn chin, smiling with an expression Sebastian recognized from her own mirror on good days.

"You'll tell them," Maren said—aloud now, in air, in a real voice, because the binding was dead and dead things make no rules. "The ones who were sold. You'll say their names. Someone has to remember them out loud."

"That's Wren's job," Sebastian managed. "She has the ledgers. She has *all* of them."

"Good clerk." Maren's light flickered, softening, thinning, becoming less a shape and more a warmth. "Good sister. You were worth twenty-two years of waiting, and I never once got bored of calling your name into the dark and wondering if you'd answer."

"I answered."

"You always would have." The warmth leaned close, and Sebastian felt a kiss pressed to her brow like sunlight through water. "Now go up. Go loudly. You've been invisible long enough."

And she was gone—not extinguished, Sebastian would swear to the end of her days, but *finished*, a note sung whole, complete, at rest.

The water went still and ordinary. The cathedral was only a ruin now, beautiful and empty, its mosaic of open mouths just carvings, just art, just grief made permanent by hands that had meant well a thousand years ago.

Ilse was crying openly, which Sebastian suspected no one in the free cities would ever believe. Corvo had sheathed his sword and was staring at his own hands as if checking they were still his. Wren stood on the stair with the ledgers, and her face was wet, and she said, in the small precise voice of a girl who had spent her life copying other people's crimes:

"The records are going to need correcting."

Sebastian climbed out of the water, put on her boots, and started up the stair toward the shouting and the breaking mountain and the world remaking itself above.

Behind her, in the empty cathedral, the first drops of ordinary rain began to fall through the shattered roof—falling, for the first time in living memory, on nothing that was singing.
