## 10. Anew

The bells of Vell rang wrong for three days after the mountain drowned.

Sebastian heard them from the harbor road, where the company had gathered to watch the sea give back what it had taken. The bells had been cast under Vaelor's edict—soul-touched bronze, Ilse said, tuned to the choir's frequency so that every strike carried a thread of stolen song into the city's bones. Now that thread was cut, and the bronze rang hollow, honest, ugly. A bell with nothing to hide.

"Listen," Ilse said. She had her hood down for the first time since Sebastian had known her, her songsmithe's brand bare on her throat where anyone might see it. She looked older without the hood. She looked like someone who had decided to stop apologizing for her face. "That's what music sounds like when no one's holding a knife to it."

"It sounds terrible," Corvo said.

"Yes," said Ilse, and she was smiling.

The city was rebuilding in the way cities do—not all at once, not grandly, but in the small stubborn grammar of hammers and market stalls and children chalk-marking walls that had been forbidden surfaces a month ago. The soul-ledgers had come out of the mountain with Wren and forty other clerks who had spent years copying records by candlelight, waiting for someone to ask them what they knew. The ledgers were stacked now in the open square beneath a canvas awning, and every morning the freed souls came to read them.

That was the part Sebastian could not get used to: the testimony. No tribunal had been convened. No court sat in judgment, because every court in the free cities had been built on Vaelor's coin and Vaelor's song, and no one trusted a judge whose chair still remembered being paid for. Instead, each dawn, the souls of the drowned choir rose out of the harbor mist—thin as breath, bright as frost—and stood among the living, and named their sellers.

Not accused. Named. That was different. An accusation could be argued with. A name was simply a name, spoken in a voice that had once belonged to a person, and everyone in the square went quiet as it fell.

*Magistrate Aldren of Vell, who sold his brother's daughter for a river charter.*

*Captain Sera Ives, who took tithe-convoy wages for eleven years.*

*Grandmother Maren Marchetti of Hollowmere, who sold a child at birth and kept the other one blind.*

Sebastian had been standing near the awning when that last one came. The mist had shaped itself into something small—a girl, perhaps seven years old, wearing the face Sebastian had only seen once, underwater, in the flooded cathedral's green light. Her twin. Not whole, not returned to any body, but *free*, and free things do not vanish; they linger until their business is done.

"You gave me back," the soul had said. "So I get to say the names. That's how it works now. You made it work."

"I'm sorry," Sebastian had whispered, because there was nothing else, because sorry was too small and too large at once.

"Don't be." The little ghost had smiled with Sebastian's own mouth. "You're the best thing she ever did, and she never even met you. Go home, sister. Go be *seen*."

* * *

She left Vell on Bracken, because Bracken refused to be left behind, and because the mule's wound had healed into a broad pale scar across his shoulder that he displayed to strangers with the pride of a veteran. Corvo rode with them as far as the crossroads at Tarn Ford, where he meant to turn himself in—not to a court, which no longer existed in any form he trusted, but to the families of the convoy towns he had guarded on the road to the mountain. "They should hear it from me," he said, "before they hear it from a ledger." He shook Sebastian's hand like a man closing a door gently. Wren stayed in Vell with the archives, and Ilse—

Ilse walked her to the city gate and stopped there, as if the threshold were a line even she would not cross.

"You could come," Sebastian said. "Hollowmere has good grass. Terrible gossip. The crows will love you."

Ilse laughed—a real laugh, unpracticed, the first Sebastian had ever heard from her. "I spent twenty years learning to write songs that wouldn't get people killed. Now nobody gets killed for singing. Do you know what that means? It means I have to learn everything again." She touched her brand, almost fondly. "I'll come find you when I've written something worth carrying over those hills. Until then—" she tapped the map case at Sebastian's hip, the dead courier's bloodstained map, its edges soft as cloth now from handling, "—keep that somewhere dry. History's going to want it."

"The history can have a copy."

"No," Ilse said. "History should have to visit."

* * *

The Weeping Pass had stopped weeping.

Sebastian noticed it from the ridge above Tarn Ford—the pass where the land held memory, where stone faces had bled grief-mist for as long as anyone remembered. The massacres were still written there; the land did not forget. But the mist that rose from the old battlefields now rose *clear*, and where it drifted, wildflowers came up through the scree in reckless purple drifts, blooming months out of season, as if the ground had been waiting a hundred years for permission.

Bracken stopped at the crest and blew out his lips, which was his way of commentary.

"I know," Sebastian said. "I feel it too."

Because she did feel it—that was the change no one had warned her about. Before the cathedral, her gift had been a whisper she kept behind her teeth, a thing that lived in the valley's silence like water under stone. Now the world spoke to her whether she asked or not. The wind off the pass carried fragments of old marching songs. The stones murmured coordinates to one another. Somewhere below, a fox was complaining about the weather in terms she chose not to repeat to Bracken.

And underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, a hum. Rising from the earth itself. From Hollowmere.

The covenant valley. The magic-dead place. Humming.

* * *

She crossed the border stones at dusk, alone except for Bracken and the crows, who found her a mile out and escorted her the rest of the way with the shrieking self-importance of an honor guard.

The wardline passed over her like cool water. She felt it try to do what it had always done—strip, silence, smooth her flat—and felt it fail. Whatever the choir had unbound in the mountain, it had loosened the covenant's grip everywhere its song had reached, and Vaelor's binding-songs had reached into every valley his armies had ever marched through. Hollowmere's silence was not broken. It was simply no longer enforced.

The neighbor was waiting at the lane's end. Old Marta, who had watched Sebastian for nineteen years with narrow eyes and reported nothing, because reporting required certainty and Marta had only ever had suspicion. She stood with her hands folded, and beside her, perched on her fence rail with shocking impropriety, sat three crows.

"They've been talking," Marta said. "All week. Saying you were coming. Saying—" her voice wavered, steadied, "—saying your grandmother's name. Saying it like an apology."

"They talk a great deal," Sebastian said. "Most of it's complaints about the weather and grudges against hawks. But yes. They were right."

Marta looked at her for a long moment—at the scarred mule, the map case, the woman who had left as a rumor and returned as a headline. Then she did something Sebastian had never seen her do. She bowed her head, slightly, the way one does to a person whose weight one has finally measured correctly.

"Your mother's cottage is still standing," Marta said. "I kept the roof patched. I never knew why." She turned back toward her house, then paused. "The crows say the valley's going to sing. Is that true?"

Sebastian listened. Beneath the soil, beneath the roots, beneath the old border stones themselves, the hum was rising—soft, many-voiced, patient. The choir had not gone silent when it broke Vaelor. Freed things travel. Freed things look for places that were made quiet against their will, and settle there, and fill them.

"Not sing," Sebastian said. "Hum. Like a house where someone's finally home."

Marta nodded slowly, and went inside, and left her gate unlatched—which, in Hollowmere, was a proclamation.

* * *

Sebastian spoke with the crows openly at the border stones that evening, as the light went long and gold across the valley floor. She told them everything—the mountain, the water, the choice, the sister. They listened with their heads cocked, and when she finished, the eldest crow, the one with the torn ear she'd known since childhood, hopped onto the wardstone itself and said:

"Took you long enough."

Then the whole murder of them lifted off the stones at once, dozens of wings, and flew low across the valley in a long dark ribbon, and everywhere they passed, the hum rose to meet them—out of the fields, out of the wells, out of the mouths of caves that had been sealed since before the covenant. The valley of Hollowmere, magic-dead for four generations, began to glow along its streams with a light like moonrise happening underground.

Bracken nudged her shoulder. *Well?* he said, in the wordless way of mules, which is somehow entirely articulate. *Aren't you going to answer them?*

Sebastian Marchetti stood at the border stones of the valley that had hidden her, in the last light of the day that had unhidden her, and she opened her mouth, and she answered them.

* * *

Wren kept the night hours at the archive in Vell, because the souls preferred to testify after dark, and because Wren had discovered that they liked having someone awake to listen.

On the first anniversary of the drowning, a soul came to the archive window that Wren did not have a ledger page for. It was small. It wore the shape of a child. It did not give a seller's name, though heaven knew Wren had copied enough of them.

Instead it pressed one translucent hand flat against the glass, and in a voice like water finding its level, it said:

"She answered them. Did you hear? All the way from Hollowmere—the crows carried it, and the wind carried it, and every freed soul between here and the sea heard her answer them." The little ghost smiled. "My sister says the valley's humming. My sister says it isn't finished."

Wren, who had spent a lifetime believing that records were how the dead spoke, picked up a fresh page and wrote at the top of it, in a careful clerk's hand: *Testimony continues.*

Outside, far to the north, the mountains were humming too.
