## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There are three rules to keeping the ledgers of the Guild of Rare Exchanges, and I have followed all of them for nineteen years without so much as a smudge of ink out of place.

One: record everything. Two: question nothing. Three: wash your hands before supper, because the ink we use is not entirely ink.

I know how that sounds. I knew how it sounded when Master Hollis first said it to me, a boy of eleven with ink already under his fingernails, and I have had nineteen years to decide he was being whimsical. He was not. But more on that later, or possibly never, depending on how tonight goes.

My name is Wendell Amberdale, and I am the keeper of the Guild's ledgers, which is a grander way of saying that I write down impossible things in a book that remembers them better than I do. The Guild trades in rare exchanges — that is our polite phrase for it. A widow's grief for a season of good harvests. A soldier's courage for twenty years of untroubled sleep. A name, occasionally, though until this evening I would have told you — did tell myself, at any rate — that names were only ever traded in the old stories, the ones we tell apprentices to make them sit up straight.

I am also, when the ledgers allow me an hour to myself, a bone-carver. It is a quiet craft. You take a piece of riverbone, you look at it until it tells you what it wants to be, and then you argue with it for six hours until it agrees. I carve tokens mostly: small things, charms, keepsakes. The Guild sells them to clients who want something to hold while they wait for whatever they've purchased to arrive. I never asked what the tokens were *for*, precisely. Rule two.

The workshop sits at the back of the guildhouse in Lowmarsh, above the canal, because everything in Lowmarsh sits above the canal; the town is built on stilts and stubbornness over water that was here long before us and will be here long after. The water has a sound. If you live here long enough you stop hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. I stopped hearing it around my fourteenth year.

Tonight I heard it again.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and a keeper of ledgers should know better than anyone that order matters.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes do, with paperwork.

The evening entry was routine. A client from upriver — a miller with damp in his walls and ambition in his heart — had exchanged seven years of his second son's luck for a guarantee against flood. Standard terms. I wrote the entry in the day-ledger in my best hand, blotted it, sanded it, and set the book aside, and that was when I noticed the eighth entry on the page.

I had written seven entries that afternoon. I counted twice, because counting is the first thing they teach you and the last thing you forget.

The eighth entry was in my handwriting. That was the first wrong thing. The second wrong thing was the date, which was tomorrow's. The third wrong thing was what it said:

*Received into escrow: one (1) name, whole and unencumbered. Payer: the canal itself. Terms: perpetual. Note — the debt of names now stands at four thousand, one hundred and twelve.*

I read it three times. Then I did what any sensible keeper does with an entry that shouldn't exist: I checked my pen for leaks, checked the blotting sand for damp, checked the window for drafts that might have flipped pages, and checked, finally, my own memory, which returned a firm and confident *nothing*. I had written no such line. I had certainly not written it in ink that hadn't dried yet.

The ink was still wet.

Gentle reader, if you have never watched wet ink on a dry page — ink you did not write, dated to a day that hasn't happened, describing a transaction between a client who is a body of water and no counterparty at all — then you have never known the particular loneliness of being the only person in a room with something that shouldn't be there. The lamp guttered. The canal slapped its slow hands against the pilings below. And somewhere under the floor, under the stilts, under the black water, something turned over in its sleep.

I did what rule one demanded. I recorded everything.

In the margin beside the impossible entry, in small letters, I wrote: *Found thus upon closing, hand unknown, date future, payer impossible.* My own note looked absurdly small next to it, like a child's chalk scrawl on a cathedral wall. But it was mine, and it was true, and true things are the only currency I have ever trusted.

Then I closed the ledger, and the cover settled with a sound like a held breath released, and I went to find Master Hollis, because some entries are too heavy to carry alone.

* * *

Master Hollis received me in his study, where he always sat beneath the portrait of the First Keeper, a stern woman named Aldith Vane whose eyes follow you around the room no matter which way you arrange the furniture. He was mending a quill, which he did whenever he wanted to look busy enough that people wouldn't tell him things.

"An entry appeared," I said. "In the day-ledger. Unwritten."

He did not look up. "Entries don't appear, Amberdale. They're written. By someone. Find out who."

"The ink was wet. The date was tomorrow's. And the payer—" I hesitated, because saying it aloud would make it real, and real things accrue interest. "The payer was the canal."

Now he looked up. And here is the thing I have turned over perhaps four hundred times since: he was not surprised. He was not confused, or alarmed, or skeptical. He looked at me the way a man looks at a clock that has finally struck an hour he has been waiting for.

"Show me," he said.

I showed him. He read the entry twice, ran his thumb along the margin where my small true words sat, and then — and I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left — he took out a knife and began scraping the page.

Not erasing. Scraping. There is a difference, and the difference is that erasing removes a mistake, while scraping removes a truth. The impossible entry came away in pale curls, and Master Hollis swept them into the fire, and the fire made a sound I have never heard a fire make before or since — a low, chorded hum, like a room full of people clearing their throats to sing.

"What is the debt of names?" I asked him.

He didn't answer. He closed the ledger, and he put his hand flat on the cover, and he said, "Wendell. How many years have you kept these books?"

"Nineteen."

"And in nineteen years, have I ever once lied to you?"

"No," I said. "You've once scraped a page in front of me, though, so I'll thank you not to test the record."

Something moved behind his face — something that might, in a younger man, have been regret. "Go home, Wendell. Carve something. Sleep. Tomorrow the auditors come down from the capital, and after they're gone, if you still have questions, I'll answer every one of them."

"You'll answer them tomorrow," I said, "or the page-scraping will have been for nothing, because I'll already have written them down."

For the first time in nineteen years, Master Hollis smiled at me, and it was the worst thing I saw all night, and I saw quite a lot.

"I know," he said. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

I went home. Or rather, I went to my rooms above the workshop, because home and work have been the same address for me since I was eleven years old, which I mention now because it will matter later, in the way that everything I thought was harmless is going to matter later.

I carved. It's what I do when the world misbehaves — you can't argue with bone and a knife; the argument is the point. I took a length of riverbone and worked it while the lamp burned low, and I did not think about wet ink, and I did not think about fires that hummed, and I did not think about the word *escrow*, which I had written ten thousand times and never once wondered about, because escrow means *held in trust*, and I had never asked: held by whom? Trusted with what?

Around midnight, the carving told me what it wanted to be. They usually want to be something simple — a bird, a knot, a face. This one wanted to be a mouth. Small, open, mid-song. I carved it without deciding to, the way you walk a road you've walked a thousand times, and when I held it up to the lamp I found I had given it a throat, and the throat had lines in it, fine as script, and the lines were—

Well. They were entries. Tiny ones, in a hand I recognized, because it was mine.

I set the token down. I wiped my hands on my apron. I told myself, in the reasonable voice I keep for exactly these occasions, that a carver's hands sometimes work ahead of his mind, that muscle memory is a kind of sleepwalking, that there was nothing in the world stranger than what I'd already seen tonight and therefore nothing left to fear.

And then the singing started.

It came up through the floor. Through the stilts. Through the black water that I had not heard in five years and now could not stop hearing, every slap of it against the pilings suddenly rhythmic, suddenly deliberate, suddenly *counted*. And underneath the water-sound, woven through it like a thread through cloth, there were voices.

Not one voice. Many. Hundreds. Thousands, maybe — I couldn't count them, and counting is the thing I do, and the fact that I couldn't frightened me more than the singing itself. They weren't singing words, or not words I knew. It was more like the memory of words, the shape language leaves behind when the language is taken out of it. A choir of mouths with nothing left to say except that they had once been able to say things.

They sang in my workshop. Under my floor. In the bones of the building, in the bones of the town, in the water that everyone in Lowmarsh had agreed, by mutual unspoken treaty, to stop hearing.

And then, for one moment — one bar, if a song like that can be said to have bars — the voices resolved, and I heard, clear as a bell over a grave, a single word rise out of the choir:

*"Keeper."*

I dropped the bone-carving. It hit the floor and rolled, and came to rest face-up, and the little carved mouth sang silently at the ceiling, and the lines in its throat had rearranged themselves into a single new entry, in my hand, dated tomorrow:

*Note appended to debt of names: the keeper hears.*

I sat down on my stool and I did not move for a long time. The lamp burned. The water counted. The choir sang on, patient as tide, and I understood — the way you understand a thing when it is far too late for understanding to be useful — that rule two, question nothing, had never been a kindness to me. It had been a kindness to the books.

Because here is what I know now, writing this by a guttering lamp with the singing rising gentle and terrible through my floorboards: someone has been paying the Guild in names. Four thousand, one hundred and twelve of them. Someone — something — has been holding them in escrow. And the ledger, my ledger, the book I have kept faithfully for nineteen years, has just informed me that the payment is due.

Tomorrow the auditors come from the capital. Tomorrow Master Hollis will smile his terrible smile and answer my questions, or scrape them off the page of the world. And tomorrow night, if the entry in the bone is honest, the keeper will hear.

I am the keeper.

The choir has just learned my name.

And I have the sudden, sickening suspicion that I am listed somewhere in those books too — not as the writer of the entries, but as one of them.

* * *

I did not sleep. I want that on the record, since records may soon be all that remains of me: Wendell Amberdale, keeper of ledgers, slept not at all on the night the impossible entry appeared, but sat instead with the day-ledger open on his knees, reading backward through nineteen years of his own careful hand, looking for the word *name*.

I found it forty-one times.

Forty-one entries across nineteen years, each one innocent alone, each one buried in the ordinary commerce of luck and grief and courage and sleep. *Received into escrow: one (1) name.* Always one. Never two. Never a round number that might catch the eye. Forty-one small stones slipped into nineteen years of river, and I stepped past every single one of them, because rule two had worn a groove in me smooth as a canal bank.

And here is the detail that finally made me put the book down and press both hands flat against the floorboards, as if I could feel the singers through the wood, as if I could apologize through it: every one of the forty-one entries was written in my hand. Not forged. Not slipped in by another. Mine — the slight rightward lean, the doubled crossbar on my t's, the little hook I put on the numeral four that no other keeper shares. Entries I had no memory of writing, in a hand that could belong to no one else, describing transactions I had witnessed with no part of my mind that survives as memory.

Which means either I have been writing them in my sleep, or something has been borrowing my hand, or — and this is the possibility I set down last, the way you set down the heaviest stone — the memories were taken along with the names, and I paid for each entry with the very act of forgetting it.

Rule one: record everything. Perhaps I have. Perhaps the ledger simply doesn't show me the cost.

The sky outside my window was going grey at the edges, the particular bruised grey that comes before dawn in Lowmarsh, when the fog comes up off the canal and makes the town look like a sketch of itself. The singing had softened toward morning, the way tide softens, but it had not stopped. It would not stop. I understood that now the way you understand a debt: completely, and with dread.

Below me, in the workshop, the little bone mouth lay where it had fallen, still singing its silent entry. *The keeper hears.* And beneath that, added sometime in the night while I sat reading one floor above it, one final line, small as a watermark:

*Auditors come at dusk. Burn nothing.*

Burn nothing. As instructions go, it was oddly specific, and I have since learned that specific instructions from impossible sources are the only ones worth following. Because when I walked down through the guildhouse that morning, past the clerks yawning over their breakfast tea, past Master Hollis's study where the First Keeper's painted eyes tracked me across the threshold — I smelled smoke.

Someone, somewhere in that building, had already begun.

Master Hollis stood in the archive doorway with a brazier and a face like a man doing arithmetic he hates, and the oldest volume in the Guild's keeping — the one older than Aldith Vane, older than the guildhouse, older, I had always assumed, than the Guild itself — lay open on a trolley beside him, waiting.

"Wendell," he said pleasantly. "Good morning. I'll need your help with a disposal."

And the choir, faint and patient under the floor, under the town, under everything, sang one word up through my boots:

*Soon.*
