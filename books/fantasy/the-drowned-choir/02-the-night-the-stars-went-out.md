## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

I want to say I noticed the wards falling the way a man notices a change in the weather — some animal part of him prickling before his mind catches up. That would be tidy. That would make me sound like the sort of keeper who understands what he keeps.

The truth is I was arguing with an inkwell.

The guild's inkwells were carved from river-stone and enchanted to never run dry, which sounds like a blessing until you've spent eleven years watching one bleed black onto your sleeve because it has opinions about how fast you write. This particular evening it had decided my hand was too slow for the entry at hand, and we were having words about it.

"Patience," I told it, "is a virtue recorded in no ledger I keep."

It spat a blot at me. I blotted the blot. Somewhere below, in the counting hall, Master Halloway was locking the strongroom for the night, and the guildhouse settled into its usual creaks — the building equivalent of an old man muttering himself to sleep. The Guild of Rare Exchanges had stood on its hill above Corrowmere for four hundred years, and it had earned every one of those creaks.

I was finishing the day's entries when I came to the one that shouldn't exist.

You'll want me to describe it precisely, and I will, because precision is the only prayer I know. Entry 4,417, dated that evening, in my own hand, which was the first wrong thing. I hadn't written it. The second wrong thing was the column heading: *Paid in names.* The third wrong thing was the amount, which was not a number at all but a list — forty-one names, written in a script like water deciding to be letters. And the fourth wrong thing, the one that made me set down the pen and sit very still while the inkwell watched me with what I swear was sympathy, was the counterparty line.

It read: *Received by: the drowned.*

I did what any sensible ledger-keeper does with an impossible entry. I checked the arithmetic. There wasn't any. Names don't sum. Then I checked the binding — whether someone had slipped a page in among mine — but the page grew from the spine like all the others, same vellum, same grain, and when I held it to the lamp the watermark showed the guild's scales-and-key, faint as a scar.

The book itself had written it. Or something had written it *through* the book.

"Right," I said aloud, to the workshop, to the inkwell, to nobody. "That's fine. That's completely fine."

The inkwell did not believe me either.

I should have gone straight to Master Halloway. He'd taken me off the streets of Corrowmere when I was nine, all elbows and hunger, and taught me that a record honestly kept is the closest thing mortals get to justice. He'd taught me bone-carving too — the name-tokens, little slips of river-bone we inscribed for clients who wanted their debts witnessed by something more durable than paper. Eleven years of his gruff kindness sat in my chest like a stone as I climbed toward his study, and I told myself the stone was loyalty.

But I didn't go to his study. I went to the window instead, because that was when the sky did it.

* * *

Here is what happened, and I have rehearsed telling it so many times since that the telling comes out smooth as river glass, though nothing about it was smooth at the time.

The stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. Not swallowed by fog, though fog was coming — I'll get to the fog. They went out the way a candle goes out when pinched between wet fingers: all of them, everywhere, in the same instant, with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard. A soft *tch*, like the universe licking its thumb.

And then the horizon stopped existing.

I don't mean it got dark. Dark is a thing; dark has texture; you can be brave in the dark. What happened was worse. The borderlands north of Corrowmere simply *ended*. There was a line — I could see it even from the guildhouse window, a seam hanging in the air like the edge of a stage curtain — and beyond the line there was no distance. No hills fading into dusk. No suggestion of more world waiting politely to be seen. Just a flat, absolute absence, as if some enormous hand had closed the kingdom of Vell like a book and left us holding the cover.

My mind did what minds do. It offered explanations, each stupider than the last. An eclipse. A trick of the glass. I had fallen asleep over the ledgers and this was the dream where you can't scream. But the inkwell had followed me to the window — they do that, when they're worried — and it was trembling against my palm, and inkwells are not dreamers.

Below me, Corrowmere began to notice. First one voice, then a dozen, then the whole town waking up at once like a hive stung. Dogs first, always dogs. Then bells — the temple of the Tidemother ringing alarm, then the temple of the Hearthfather ringing back, two faiths agreeing for the first time in living memory that something was deeply wrong.

And under it all, faint, rising through the floorboards and the soles of my feet and the bones the guild had taught me to carve:

Singing.

The same singing I'd heard the night before in my workshop, except now it wasn't confined to one room. It came from everywhere the fog touched, and the fog was touching everything past that seam. Hundreds of voices. Thousands. They weren't menacing. That's the detail I couldn't shake, the one that kept me at the window when every instinct said run. They sounded *sad*, yes, but also patient, the way creditors are patient. Like they were singing a bill of collection.

"*Keeper,*" said one voice, clear as a struck bell, and it was not outside. It was inside the room with me, inside my skull, wearing my own echo like a borrowed coat. "*You hear us. You have always heard us. Come and count.*"

I am not proud of what I did next. I closed the shutters. I sat down at my desk in the dark, hands flat on the ledger, and I pretended to be a man who had seen nothing, in a guild that had recorded nothing, in a country that was being quietly deleted at its edges.

It lasted maybe ten minutes. Then Master Halloway's bell rang three times — the guild's oldest signal, older than the guild itself, meaning *all hands, all records, emergency* — and pretending stopped being available.

* * *

The counting hall was lit with dragon-fire lanterns, the expensive kind, the kind the guild only lit when it wanted light that couldn't be argued with. Every keeper of the Rare Exchanges was there — thirty-one of us, blinking, half-dressed, arranged along the long tables like specimens. Master Halloway stood at the head, and I had never seen him look old before. He was seventy if he was a day, built like the door he'd barred behind us, and now he looked like a door someone had already broken down once and repaired badly.

He held the newest ledger volume in both hands, and I thought, absurdly, *he's brought it to show us, he'll explain, there's an explanation, there's always an explanation, that's what the guild is for.*

"Gentlemen," he said. "Ladies. Colleagues." A pause, the length of a man deciding whether to lie to people he loves. "As of tonight, the Guild of Rare Exchanges is dissolving its charter."

Nobody breathed.

"The border wards have failed," he went on, in the flat voice he used for reading unfavorable terms to clients. "You will have seen the sky. What you saw was not weather, not war, not the wrath of any god currently licensed in Vell. It was a default." He set the ledger down on the table before him, gently, the way you'd set down something sleeping. "We are in default. The guild has been in default for some time. And the creditor—" here his voice did something I had never heard it do, something small and human and afraid— "the creditor has come to collect."

Old Merribel, who kept the salt-taxes and had buried three husbands and outlived every one of their opinions, raised her hand like we were back in lessons. "Master. Collect *what?*"

Halloway looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at me — just for a heartbeat, just long enough — and whatever he saw in my face made his decision for him.

"That," he said, "is no longer any of yours to know. Which brings me to the difficult order." He straightened. "All ledgers are to be sealed and burned. Tonight. Every volume, every loose page, every token in the archive vaults. The guild's records die with the guild. This is not negotiable, and it is not a request, and any keeper found concealing so much as a receipt will be dealt with as the charter directs."

"And the charter?" asked young Pellwick, whose voice cracked on the word. "Does the charter still direct anything?"

"The charter," said Halloway, "burned an hour ago."

I remember thinking, with the strange clarity terror sometimes lends: *he's lying about something. Not about the burning. About the reason.* Because I had watched that man grieve — when his daughter took ship south, when Merribel's second husband died, when the flood took the lower counting-house. He grieved like a landslide, openly, terribly. And the face he wore now was not grief. It was *guilt*, worn carefully, like a coat buttoned to hide the stain underneath.

Thirty keepers rose to obey. Thirty honest, dull, decent record-keepers went to fetch the history of four hundred years and feed it to the fire, because the man who raised them said so.

I stayed seated.

"Wendell." Halloway's eyes found me again, and this time there was a message in them, urgent and unreadable. "Your ledgers too."

"My ledgers," I said, "are current."

"All the more reason."

"Master, with respect — burning a ledger doesn't erase a debt. Any fool who's kept books knows that. Burning a ledger just means nobody can prove what was owed." I heard my own voice come out steady and was vaguely impressed by it. "If we're in default, sir, then somebody, somewhere, is owed something enormous. And you're ordering us to destroy the only evidence of what."

The hall went quiet enough that I could hear the fog pressing against the windows. Halloway crossed the floor to me slowly, and up close I could see his hands shaking, these hands that had taught mine to hold a chisel steady at nine years old.

"Wendell Amberdale," he said softly, "you were the best student I ever had, and that is exactly why I am telling you: some accounts, once opened, can only be closed by forgetting them. Do your work. Burn your books. Live." His grip on my shoulder was almost painful. "*Live.*"

Then he turned and gave the order again, louder, and the guild moved like the machine it had always been, and I moved with it, because that is what machines do.

* * *

Here is the confession portion of the evening, which I offer without excuse: I stole the oldest volume in the guild's keeping, and I am glad, and I would do it again, and some nights that gladness frightens me more than the fog does.

The Archive Deep was below the counting hall, below the strongroom, below the wells — a vault cut into the hill's living rock, where the guild kept the volumes too old and too dangerous for the open shelves. Keepers weren't supposed to enter alone. Keepers weren't supposed to enter at all, really; the Deep was Halloway's and the charter's, and the charter was ash. But I had been carrying keys I didn't ask questions about for six years — Halloway's gift, or Halloway's burden, handed over one rain-soaked afternoon with the words *"someone young should hold spares"* — and tonight the questions finally arrived, all of them, late and furious, like relatives after a funeral.

The stair down was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. The choir was louder here. Of course it was. Whatever the drowned were, whatever they'd been sold to buy, they had been singing at this guildhill longer than the guild had been listening.

The oldest volume sat on its lectern in the center of the Deep, alone, under its own dragon-fire lantern that had burned — I realized as I approached — without refilling for as long as anyone could remember. It was bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, and it had no title, and when I put my hands on it, the singing stopped.

Every voice. All at once. The silence was so total I could hear my own heart doing its stupid panicky work, and beneath it, fainter than breath, a single voice saying, clearly, in a child's accent centuries out of fashion:

"*At last.*"

Gentle reader, whoever you turn out to be: I have been called careful my whole life. Careful Wendell. Steady Wendell. The boy who counts twice. And I want it on the record that I stood in that vault for a long moment, weighing duty against truth, guild against dead, the man who raised me against the voices under the hill, and that my decision, when it finally came, was not noble.

It was simple curiosity. The deadliest thing I own.

I wrapped the volume in my coat. I took the lantern, because it seemed to belong with the book the way a dog belongs with its master. I walked up the stairs past the smoke already seeping down from the burning shelves above — they worked fast, my colleagues, honest to the last ember — and I let myself out the postern gate into a night with no stars, while behind me the Guild of Rare Exchanges burned its memory and called it mercy.

* * *

The fog reached Corrowmere before dawn.

I watched it come from the top of Gullwatch Hill, the stolen book heavy against my ribs like a second heart, and I will try to describe it accurately because accuracy is the only inheritance I have left. It did not roll in like weather. It *advanced* like a decision — a wall of pale grey moving at walking pace, utterly silent, and where it passed, things did not vanish so much as become *uninsisted upon*. A fencepost didn't disappear; it stopped mattering, and then it stopped being there, and those are different, and I wish to God I didn't know the difference.

Behind me, the guildhouse still smoldered. Ahead, the fog ate the northern road, the mill, the temple bell mid-peal. And in my arms, warm as a sleeping cat, the oldest ledger hummed against my chest, and the choir sang inside it, patient as tide, and one voice — the child's, the one that had said *at last* — sang a name over and over, soft and hopeful, a name I somehow knew was mine.

"Wendell Amberdale," it sang. "Wendell Amberdale. Keeper. Keeper. We have paid so much, and you are the last coin, and we have waited so long to be counted."

I stood on that hill with the whole country going out around me like a lamp, and I understood, with the terrible clarity that arrives only after every other option has burned, three things.

First: the guild had not raised me. It had *invested* in me.

Second: the debt was real, and enormous, and I was holding the only proof of it left in the world.

Third: whatever came out of that fog to collect — I was going to have to meet it with the books balanced, or not meet it at all.

The fog reached the foot of the hill and paused, courteous as a caller at a door.

Somewhere in it, something cleared its throat.

I picked up my lantern, clutched a dead guild's oldest sin to my chest, and started walking — not away from the fog, as any sensible man would have, but along its edge, toward the sunken capital and the answers drowning there.

Behind me, unhurried, it began to follow.
