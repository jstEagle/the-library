## 3. A Country Unmade

I have crossed the border of Vell eleven times in my life — twice for guild business, once for a funeral I still owe someone a eulogy for, and eight times because Sable Quill's predecessor smugglers charged less than honest coachmen. Every crossing required papers. Every paper required a name. Every name required a stamp from the Guild of Rare Exchanges, because apparently even the act of being somewhere had to be notarized.

So it was with some professional interest that I noticed, three miles out of Harrowfen, that the border had stopped existing.

Not fallen. Not broken. *Stopped.* The road simply ran out of conviction. One moment there was gravel under my boots and hedgerows on either side; the next, the hedges were suggestions, and the gravel was remembering, faintly, that it had once been stone. Beyond it, where the horizon should have been, there was nothing so dramatic as darkness. There was an absence of distance itself — a flatness, like the world had been pressed between the pages of a very large book.

The stars, when night came, did not come with it.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was my trade before it became my crime. It wasn't cloud cover. Clouds have edges, weight, the decency to move. This was as if the sky above Vell had been asked a question it couldn't answer and had decided, politely, to stop existing rather than embarrass itself. Where the constellation of the Ploughwife hung over the eastern hills every night of my thirty-one years, there was now a smooth, starless nothing, and my eyes kept sliding off it the way a tongue finds a missing tooth.

In my satchel, wrapped in oilcloth against my spine, the oldest ledger of the Guild of Rare Exchanges sat like a coal that hadn't decided whether to burn me or warm me. Every few hundred yards I caught myself checking it was still there, which was absurd — you don't misplace a book that heavy, or a guilt that heavy either.

*Keeper,* said something behind my left ear.

There was nothing behind my left ear. I checked. This is the sort of thing that becomes necessary, in my line of work — or my former line of work, since I had, as of midnight, become the kind of man who steals from his own guild and runs.

*Keeper,* it said again, patient as tide.

"Keeper of what?" I asked the empty road, and immediately regretted it, because asking questions of voices is how you end up in the guild sanatorium counting your own teeth. But the voice didn't answer, and neither did the second voice that joined it, or the third, low and layered, like a choir rehearsing in a flooded church. They weren't singing words yet. They were singing *toward* words. The way water sings toward a boil.

I walked faster.

* * *

Harrowfen had been, the last time I passed through, a market village of perhaps two hundred souls, famous for eel pies and a bell tower that leaned like a drunk telling a secret. I arrived at dusk, if you could call it dusk — the light failed early now, as though the days themselves were being shortened by someone trimming the wick — and I stopped at the top of the hill because what lay below was Harrowfen the way a portrait lies below its sitter.

It was all there. That was the horrible part. The bell tower leaned its familiar lean. The market square held its stalls. Smoke rose from chimneys in the gray evening air. And yet everything had the quality of a thing seen through wet glass — colors slightly too soft, edges slightly too willing to blur. A cart stood by the well, and I swear on whatever gods are still taking appointments that the cart's wheels were turning without the cart moving, slowly, like a memory trying to remember rolling.

Then a woman stepped out of the baker's, and I watched her forget her own errand mid-stride.

I don't mean she paused. I mean I watched it happen — watched the purpose drain out of her face like water out of a cracked bowl. She'd been carrying a basket. She looked at the basket. She looked at her hands holding the basket. Her mouth opened around a word that never arrived, and for one long moment she stood in the middle of her own street, a stranger to herself, while the fog — which had crept up from the fields without any wind to bring it — curled gently around her ankles like a cat greeting someone it has decided to keep.

"Bread," she said at last, uncertainly, to no one. "Was it... bread?"

"It was bread," said a man from the doorway across the lane, and then he frowned, because he had answered confidently and could not say why. "I think. Do we bake?"

"We bake," the woman said, with the desperate firmness of someone holding onto a rope over a drop. "We've always baked."

They stood there together, two people holding their lives by the handles, and the fog purred around them, and I understood with a lurch that I was watching a village die in the gentlest possible way — not burned, not sacked, just *misplaced*, the way the guild misplaced things it owed people. Houses softened at their corners. The leaning bell tower leaned a little further, then reconsidered, then forgot which way it had been leaning in the first place. Somewhere a dog barked, and the bark came out wrong, muffled, as if the dog were barking underwater, or barking from several years ago.

And underneath it all, rising like damp through plaster, the choir.

This time they had words. Or near enough. A hundred voices, maybe a thousand, men and women and children, singing in a harmony that had never been taught to human throats because it wasn't meant for them. And threaded through the music, clear as a needle through cloth:

*Keeper.*

"Stop calling me that," I whispered.

*You carry the book,* they sang. *You carry the book, keeper. You carry our names.*

"I carry an inventory," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle of the word, which rather undermined the point. "Ledgers record transactions. Grain. Wool. Bone-carvings. Nobody's *names*—"

But even as I said it, I was remembering. Remembering the entries I'd transcribed over fifteen years, the ones written in the old clerk's hand, the ones Master Aldery had told me were archaic formalities: *Received: forty names, condition varied. Delivered to the Wardens of the March. Consideration rendered per schedule.* I'd assumed *names* meant signatures. Signatories. Some defunct merchant house.

I had carved bone tokens for fifteen years and never once asked why each token needed a name scratched into it, or why the guild kept those tokens in a locked vault instead of sending them out with the goods.

The woman with the basket turned and looked directly at me.

Her eyes had gone pale — not blind-pale, but washed-out, like a page left too long in the sun. Yet she saw me. More than saw me. She looked at me the way you look at the last lit window in a dark street.

"You're the ledger-man," she said. Her voice was fraying at the edges, threads coming loose. "Aren't you? You're the one who writes it down."

"I—"

"Write us down." She took a step toward the hill, toward me, and the fog moved with her, tenderly, the way you'd move with someone ill. "Whatever's happening. Write it down so it happened. My name is—" She stopped. Her face went through something terrible and quick, a whole storm in a teacup. "My name is..."

She couldn't find it. I watched her reach for it and watch it not be there, and I realized, with a cold that started in my teeth, that I knew her name. Marta Colloway. Eel pie. Two sons. I'd stayed at her inn four years ago and she'd overcharged me for the room and undercharged me for the pie and told me about her sons until midnight, and I remembered because remembering things was my job, and here was the joke, the great cosmic jest of it: the only man who could write her down was the man whose guild had written her *out*.

"Marta Colloway," I said. "You run the Drowned Eel. Your sons are Tam and Osric. Tam wants to be a bell-ringer even though the tower leans. You make the best pie between here and the capital and you know it, and you charge for it accordingly."

Something came back into her face. Not much. A candle-end relit in a drafty room. "Colloway," she breathed. "Yes. Yes, that's — that's mine, isn't it?"

"It's yours," I said. "Hold onto it."

"For how long?" she asked, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing I'd seen that day, and I had watched the stars go out the night before. Behind her, the fog had reached the well. The cart by the well was fading — not moving, not dissolving, just becoming *less agreed-upon*, the way a word repeated too many times stops meaning anything.

"As long as you can," I said, which was a coward's answer, and we both knew it.

She nodded, satisfied, and turned back toward her bakery, and I stood on the hill like a thief at a funeral, because that is more or less what I was.

* * *

I went down into Harrowfen anyway. I've thought about that since — whether it was courage or compulsion or just the particular stupidity of a man who has spent his life believing that records matter, that writing a thing down is the same as saving it. I went street by street, and I spoke to everyone the fog had half-erased, and I gave them back what pieces I had. The farrier was called Bren. The leaning tower leaned east. The eels came up from the fen ditches in autumn, fat and silver. Each fact I handed over bought its owner another minute of being somebody, and I handed them out like a miser forced at knifepoint to spend his hoard, and the choir sang overhead, louder now, and I finally understood they weren't threatening me.

They were *thanking* me. That was worse.

*Keeper,* they sang, *keeper, you remember, you remember, keeper of the book of names—*

"I don't have your names," I told them, standing in the emptying square, shouting at the air like a madman, because the alternative was admitting I might. "If I had them, I'd give them back. Do you hear me? I'd read every one aloud."

The singing changed. Just slightly. The way a room changes when someone in it decides something.

*Then open the book, keeper.*

"The book is sealed. Guild seal. Wax and iron and—" I stopped. Because I was lying, wasn't I? Not about the seal. About the rest. The oldest ledger had come away from its shelf too easily. The lock had opened too willingly. And there had been a moment, one moment, in the guildhouse dark, when I'd put my hand flat on the cover and felt — I had no other word for it then, and I don't have a better one now — *recognition*. The book had known my hand. The way a lock knows its key. The way, I understood suddenly, looking at the softening houses of Harrowfen, a debt knows its debtor.

The fog thickened. Down the lane, the bakery was going — the walls first losing their color, then their corners, then their insistence on being walls. Through the dissolving doorway I could see Marta Colloway kneading dough that was forgetting to be dough, and she was humming, and the tune she hummed was the choir's tune, and she didn't know it, and I understood that the song wasn't coming for her. It was *in* everything already. It had always been in everything. The wards had only ever been the lid on the pot.

"Come with me," I said through the doorway. "Marta. Come north with me, there are places—"

She looked up. For a heartbeat her eyes were clear and brown and entirely her own. "Where would I be going, love?" she said gently, the way you speak to someone grieving. "Everything I am is right here."

And then it wasn't, and she was still smiling, and the smile was made of fog, and the bakery was a shape the fog was wearing, and I stood in the street of a village that had never quite finished happening, and I did the only thing left to a keeper with nothing left to keep.

I wrote it down. Right there, kneeling in the road, ledger open on my knees, pen shaking. *Harrowfen. Market village. Eel pies. Bell tower leans east. Marta Colloway, sons Tam and Osric. Here, and then not here. Witnessed.*

The ink sank into the page strangely — eagerly, the way dry ground takes rain. And the choir, all around me, in the fog and under the ground and inside my own skull, went quiet for exactly as long as it took me to finish the entry.

Then, softly, in a hundred drowned voices, in perfect unison, they said:

*That's one.*

I looked up. The fog was parting ahead of me — just barely, just enough — opening a corridor northward, toward the low hills, toward the drowned capital I'd sworn since childhood I'd never visit, toward whatever the guild had spent five centuries refusing to pay for.

And walking up that corridor, out of the mist, came a figure with a crossbow slung casual across her back and words inked down both bare arms like a woman who had run out of paper and refused, on principle, to run out of memory.

She looked at the ledger on my knees. Then at me. Then she grinned like a knife being introduced to a purse.

"Well," she said. "You must be the keeper."
