## 4. The Smuggler Who Refused to Forget

I want it on the record — my record, the one inked down my left forearm in a hand that stopped being legible to anyone but me around the time I turned twenty — that I did not rob Wendell Amberdale.

I caught him, yes. I held a knife to his throat and relieved him of his pack while he stood there vibrating like a struck bell, yes. But robbing implies he had anything worth taking, and what he had was a book that wanted him dead, a face like a man who'd watched his own funeral from the back row, and the peculiar stink of guild-ink that gets into everything those bastards touch. I took the book because taking it was the only way to find out whether I'd have to kill him. That's not robbery. That's due diligence.

Let me start where it started, which was the ford at Marrowbridge, three days after the stars went out, in fog so thick I could taste it — old pennies and pond water and something underneath that I can only describe as *apology*.

* * *

You need to understand what the fog does before you understand me.

It doesn't kill you. Killing is honest work; killing leaves a body, and a body is a fact, and facts are the one thing the fog cannot abide. No — the fog *unwrites*. It comes in soft as a lover's breath and takes your edges first. Your mother's face goes vague. The name of your village sits on your tongue like a stone you can't swallow. Then the house you grew up in stops being a house and becomes a suggestion of a house, then a rumor, then nothing, and you stand in the road where your whole life happened and feel a grief with no object, like mourning a dream you can't quite remember having.

Most people let it happen. That's not cowardice. Memory hurts, and the fog offers anesthesia, and I have never once blamed a soul for taking the easy exit.

I'm just built wrong for it.

The first thing the fog ever tried to take from me was my sister's name — Bree, who drowned when we were nine, in the canal behind the tannery, and whose name our parents started losing within the year, saying *the girl* and *you know who I mean*, until one day our mother looked straight at me and asked if I'd ever had a sibling. And something in me went *no. Absolutely not. Not this.* So I took the charcoal stub from beside the hearth and wrote BREE on the inside of my wrist, small, where a sleeve would hide it, and when the letters faded off my skin I rewrote them, and when my skin ran out of room I got clever about it.

That was twenty-two years ago. I am, as far as I know, the only fully furnished woman left between the mountains and the sea. Every road I've walked since the wards fell, I've walked past villages dissolving like sugar in rain, and I've stopped in each one and written down what was going under. Names. Faces. The recipe for Nana Ostrey's plum cordial, gods rest her, whoever she was by the time I got there. My body is an archive. It itches. It aches in cold weather. It has made me unwelcome in every bathhouse from here to the coast, because people take one look at a woman covered in writing and decide she's either mad or cursed, and honestly they're not wrong on either count.

But here's the thing nobody tells you: memory is cargo, and cargo has value, and a woman who can carry what everyone else is dropping will never starve. Smuggling used to mean brandy and bolt-cloth past the toll-wards. Now it means carrying the name of a dead baker's daughter to a town that forgot her, and charging what the market will bear, which is usually a meal and a dry corner. I've made peace with the pay cut. There are worse trades than being the last person who remembers.

Which is why, when I saw smoke rising off the ford at Marrowbridge — real smoke, woodsmoke, someone stubborn enough to keep a fire lit in fog-country — I went toward it instead of away.

And there he was. A thin man in a bone-carver's apron, sitting on an overturned cart, feeding sticks to a fire like a man performing the idea of calm, with a ledger open on his knees and his lips moving over the words. And around him, faint as heat-shimmer, the air was *singing*.

I don't hear the choir. Let's be clear about that. I've crossed fog-country for two years and the dead have never once given me a note. Whatever they sing, they sing for him. But I could see it working on him — see the way his shoulders flinched at harmonies I couldn't catch, the way he kept pressing his palm flat against the ledger like it might bolt.

So I did what any sensible businesswoman does with a man who is simultaneously haunted and valuable.

I put my knife at his throat and said, "Don't move, keeper."

He moved. They always move. But he moved *slowly*, hands up, and he said, "You can see them too?" in a voice cracked clean through, and I swear on every word written on my skin, the disappointment I felt — that he was just another haunted fool and not, say, a guild treasurer with pockets full of unmarked silver — lasted exactly as long as it took me to look down at the ledger in his lap.

I know guild-ink. I've stolen enough of their documents to know the watermark, the thread in the binding, the particular black of their ink that never fades no matter how long the fog chews on everything else. Guild paper doesn't forget. That's not poetry; that's manufacture. And this book was old — older than the guildhouse seals, older than the current master's signature style, bound in something that wasn't leather and didn't want to be touched.

"Well," I said. "Aren't you the interesting piece of contraband."

"That's mine," he said.

"Everything's mine. That's what a knife means." I flipped it open one-handed, keeping the blade where it belonged, and read the first entry my eye fell on, and I'll tell you now, I've read ransom notes and death warrants and a guild warrant for my own arrest — flattering, badly spelled — and none of them turned my stomach like that page.

It was a list of names. Dated. Priced. *Received in payment:* and then names, dozens of them, with the cold little notation after each: *source: drowned.*

"Where did you get this?"

"I keep the ledgers," he said. "Kept. Past tense as of four days ago." He swallowed, careful, against my knife. "My name is Wendell Amberdale. And I think I'm the only person alive who knows why the stars went out."

Now. A lesser smuggler would have taken the book and left him kneeling in the fog. A crueler one would have sold him to the Reclamationists, who were paying in solid coin for anyone connected to the ledgers, and been rich by week's end. But I read that page again — *source: drowned* — and I thought about Bree, and about every name I carry that the fog has orphaned, and I thought: here, finally, is the account book. Here is where all my cargo came from.

You don't rob the man holding the other half of your own history. You make a deal.

* * *

We talked by the fire while the fog leaned in around us like a creditor at a funeral. He told me everything — the impossible entry, the night the stars died, the guild master ordering the ledgers burned, the theft, the village coming apart around him mid-sentence. He told it badly, in the way honest men do, doubling back and apologizing and staring at his own hands like they belonged to a stranger. I let him talk. Meanwhile I counted what he wasn't saying, which was considerable, and watched the way the singing bent toward him whenever he touched the book.

"You hear them," I said. "The dead in there."

"I hear them." His laugh had no floor under it. "They call me keeper. I don't know if it's an honor or an accusation."

"It's both. Everything worth being is both." I sheathed my knife, mostly because my arm was tired, partly to watch him relax and then realize he shouldn't. "Here's what I know, bone-carver. Two years I've been writing down what the fog eats. Two years of carrying other people's dead around on my skin because nobody else would hold them. And every mile east, it gets worse, and I could never figure out *why* — why the wards held for six hundred years and then quit all at once, like a purse cut from its string." I tapped the ledger. "And now this sad little accountant walks out of the guildhouse with the answer strapped to his back."

"It's not an answer. It's a debt."

"Debts are answers. Debts are the most honest kind." I pulled back my sleeve and showed him — not Bree, not yet, that's private even now — but the outer rows, the recent work, the villages. "See this? Harrow Fen, population forty-one, gone Tuesday. This one, the miller's twins, born and forgotten inside a week. I am the only record these people existed. Do you understand what that costs? Do you understand what it means that some clerk in a warm room *sold* them?"

"They weren't my sales," he said, too fast.

"No," I agreed. "But they were your handwriting."

He went white. Which told me two things: that he'd already worked it out himself, and that he hadn't yet learned to stop hoping no one would say it aloud. Good. Guilt is fuel. I run on it myself.

"So here's my price," I said. "Not the book. I want passage — you and me and that cursed choir of yours, east, to the sunken capital, to whatever's under the water that all this debt drains into. You're going there anyway. I can see it in the way you look at the river. Nobody flees west when the west is already gone."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take the book and sell it to the highest bidder, and the highest bidder will be the people who burned the stars, and you can spend the rest of your short life being forgotten in a ditch." I smiled at him, and I felt it go sharp, the way it does. "But you won't say no. Because you heard them singing before you ever opened this book, and men who hear the dead don't sleep well until the dead are settled. Am I wrong?"

He was quiet a long moment. The fog pressed close. Somewhere in it, a house that had stood for two hundred years finished forgetting itself with a sound like a sigh.

"The capital's flooded," he said finally. "Half-drowned. Nothing left but—"

"Dragon-fire," I said. "Stones soaked in it. The one place in Vell the fog can't chew through, because you can't unwrite a city that remembers itself." I shrugged. "I smuggle memories, Amberdale. You think I haven't mapped the route to the only vault that never loses its contents?"

Something shifted in his face then — not hope exactly, hope is cheaper than that, but the recognition of a fellow professional. Two people who understood that information is the only currency that holds its value at the end of the world.

"You want the truth," he said slowly, "and I want... I don't even know what I want. To pay it. Whatever it is. Honestly. Outside the guild, outside the law, outside—" He gestured at everything, the fog, the dark sky, the ash of his old life. "All of it."

"Honestly," I repeated, and laughed, and it came out rougher than I meant. "Do you know what I charge for honesty? Everything. Honesty is the one cargo that always costs the carrier." I stuck out my hand, ink-stained, scarred, every finger a paragraph. "Sable Quill. Smuggler, scribe, self-appointed archivist of the end of Vell. Your guide, your guard, and from this moment forward, the only reason you're still alive. In exchange: you share the ledger. I read what I want. And when we find out who sold my dead — because some of these names are mine, carver, I can feel it — I get to be there when they answer for it."

He took my hand. His grip was a clerk's grip, soft, but he didn't flinch from the ink, and that's more courtesy than I've had in years.

"Sable Quill," he said. "Like the bird?"

"Like the pen, genius. My mother had a sense of humor and a grudge against subtlety." I shouldered my pack. "Come on. We've got light for maybe two hours and the ford won't hold its shape past dusk. And bring the book close — if the fog wants it, better it learn early that it'll have to go through me."

* * *

We made three miles before dark, which in fog-country is a triumph, and camped in the shell of a chapel that had forgotten its god but kept its walls, which struck me as about right for Vell these days. I built the fire. He sat turning the ledger over in his hands like a man holding a sleeping animal that might wake angry.

"One question," he said, as I was banking the coals. "Your arms. All of it. Does it hurt? Keeping everything?"

I considered lying, for form's sake. Decided against it. First day of a partnership and all.

"Every day," I said. "Like carrying water in your hands across a desert. You can't set it down, you can't hurry, and your arms burn the whole way. But at the end of it, somebody drinks." I looked at him across the fire. "Why do you ask?"

"Because I think I've been doing the opposite," he said quietly. "Carrying nothing. Writing down what other people paid and never once asking what it cost them." His thumb moved along the ledger's spine. "And I think the cure is going to look a lot like what you do. Hurting on purpose, so it doesn't spread."

Well. There are worse things a man can say on his first night out from under the guild's roof.

I didn't tell him that, though. Instead I said, "Sleep, carver. Dawn's in six hours and the fog doesn't negotiate." And I sat up keeping watch, as I always do, running my fingers over the oldest lines on my wrist where the ink has sunk so deep it's part of the skin now.

Bree. Still there. Still mine.

Sometime past midnight, Wendell sat bolt upright in his bedroll, eyes wide, listening to something I couldn't hear. And when he spoke, his voice had gone strange and careful, the way voices do when they're repeating words that aren't theirs:

"She says — the young one, the one with the river in her voice — she says to ask you what you charged my mother. For remembering her." He blinked, coming back to himself, looking shaken. "Sable, I don't understand. She says she knows you. She says you wrote her name once, years ago, on a canal bridge near the tanneries. She says..." He stopped. Swallowed. "She says thank you. And that she's been waiting a long time to meet the other keeper."

The fire popped. The fog breathed against the chapel walls. And I sat very still with my sleeve fallen back and one name showing, twenty-two years old and worn smooth as river glass, and understood that my cargo had just found its owner.

"All right," I said, when I could speak. "Then we'd better get to the capital. I have questions too."
