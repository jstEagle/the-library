## 5. What the Guild Owes

The lantern was the first honest thing the guild ever gave me.

It had been in my family of apprentices longer than I'd been alive — a squat iron thing with a cage of blackened filigree, and inside it a bead of dragon-fire no bigger than a currant. It never needed oil. It never went out. When I was nine and afraid of the dark under the guildhouse stairs, Master Oren had set it on my workbench and said, "There. Now you can see what you're doing."

He hadn't meant anything by it, I'm sure. That was the trouble with the Guild of Rare Exchanges: everything terrible we did looked, from up close, like ordinary kindness done at scale.

I sat now in the wreck of Sable Quill's smuggler's hold — which is to say, a beached river-barge she insisted on calling *the Vow*, though she refused to say what the vow was — with that lantern burning steady beside me and the oldest ledger of the Guild of Rare Exchanges open on my knees.

Outside, the fog pressed against the portholes like a face trying to remember how faces worked. Somewhere above, stars were supposed to be. There weren't. The choir hummed through the hull, low and patient, the way tide hums against a pier it has already decided to take.

Sable sat across from me sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. She'd been sharpening it for an hour. It was, I suspected, less about the knife than about watching whether I would flinch when I read whatever came next.

"Page one," I said. "Entry one. Dated four hundred and eleven years ago." I turned it so the light caught the ink. "You want to hear this?"

"I stole nothing," she said. "I just didn't stop you stealing. Different crimes. But yes. Read."

* * *

Here is what I knew going in — or thought I knew. Every apprentice learns the Catechism of Exchange before they learn to carve their first bone button:

*A thing given must be met with a thing given.*
*A thing taken must be met with a thing returned.*
*No exchange is closed until both sides say it is.*

We recited it every morning, kneeling, before breakfast. It sounded holy. It was meant to. And for four hundred years, apparently, it had also been a lie of omission so large that the entire kingdom of Vell fit inside it comfortably, along with its wards, its stars, and its borders.

Because entry one wasn't a trade record. It was a confession written in the language of bookkeeping, which is the only language powerful men trust.

*Received of the Founding Choir, drowned at the Weeping Ford: two hundred and six names.*

That was all the first line said. Received of. As if the dead had walked up to a counter and handed them over.

But below it, in a different hand — older, shakier, pressed hard enough into the vellum to tear — someone had added: *Taken. Not received. Let no successor soften this word.*

I read it aloud anyway. My voice did something embarrassing around the word *taken*. Sable's knife stopped moving.

"Go on," she said, softer.

The next entries were worse, because they were tidier.

The founders of my guild had not been merchants who drifted into wickedness. They had been desperate men standing on the banks of a flooded valley — the same flood, I realized, that made the sunken capital sunken — with the border wards failing and the old gods' contracts expiring, and they had needed something to anchor the wards to. Wards need anchors. Everyone knows that. Stone, blood, oath, name.

They took the names of the drowned. Two hundred and six souls who had died in the Weeping Ford disaster, who could not object because the dead are famously bad at filing complaints. They wove those names into the ward-stones, and the wards held, and the stars stayed lit, and Vell was saved.

And then — here is where my stomach turned over, here is where I put the ledger down and looked at the ceiling of the barge for a while — then they kept the debt.

Because a name woven into stone isn't gone. It's *held*. Held things accrue interest. The ledger's second page says so in plain figures: every year the wards burned brighter, the names wore thinner. Every year the guild owed the dead more of themselves back, and paid none of it, and recorded the shortfall with beautiful penmanship.

"They refinanced," I said.

"What?"

I turned the book toward her. "Look. Every sixty years or so, there's a new section. A new hand. They call them *Renewals*. The original two hundred and six names start fraying — you can see it, the entries literally fade, the ink goes grey — and so instead of paying them back, the guild takes new names to patch the old ones. Fresh collateral. New collateral." I swallowed. "Drowned people, mostly. River folk. Sailors. Anyone the water got, or anyone the guild could arrange to have gotten. Their names go onto the rolls as *received*, and get stitched into the ward-stones alongside the originals, and the debt gets bigger, and the wards keep holding, and everyone in Vell gets to keep their sky."

Sable was quiet a long moment. Then: "How many renewals?"

I counted. It didn't take long. The sections marched down the centuries like fence posts. "Seven. The last one's dated ninety-one years ago. Signed—" and there it was, the seal I'd pressed ten thousand times without once wondering what it sealed, the twin rivers and the closed circle, "—signed by Guildmaster Corvus Hale. Who trained the man who trained me."

"And the newest entries? Your era?"

I flipped forward. Past the seventh renewal, past the careful columns of names in hands I recognized — some of them were handwriting I had *seen*, had sat across tables from, had drunk with. And then I reached the pages I had helped make, and I stopped being able to pretend I was reading about other people.

Because the recent entries weren't names taken from the drowned.

They were tokens. Bone tokens, carved with a name apiece, filed under *Exchanges, Rare, Pending*. I had carved hundreds of them. I'd assumed they were memorial pieces — grief-work, the kind of thing rich families commissioned. Someone dies; the guild carves their name into bone so the family can hold something solid while they grieve. It had seemed tender. It had seemed like the gentlest corner of the guild's business.

The ledger disagreed. Each token was logged as *collateral, held against renewal eight*. Renewal eight. The next refinancing. Which meant the guild had been gathering names for years, waiting, stockpiling souls against the day the wards needed patching again.

Against the night the stars went out.

Which was three nights ago.

"Oh," I said. It was not a sufficient word. Nothing was going to be a sufficient word for a while.

* * *

I need to tell you what carving those tokens felt like, because I've been telling myself it felt like nothing, and that's its own kind of lie.

It felt good. That's the truth. Bone takes a name beautifully if you cut it right — there's a moment when the graver bites and the letter opens up out of the white like a seed splitting, and the whole workshop smells faintly of chalk and tallow candles, and I would sit there feeling useful. Feeling *necessary*. Keeper of impossible things. Every apprentice dreams of the ledger desk. I got it young, and I was proud.

Nobody ever told me where the names came from. But nobody had to, did they? That's the architecture of complicity. You don't need to be lied to if you're willing to do the lying yourself, quietly, in the part of your head where questions go to drown.

Questions go to drown. I used that phrasing without thinking, and then I heard the choir outside the hull shift key — lower, attentive — and understood they'd heard it too.

"I carved them," I said to Sable. My voice came out flat as a coin left on a rail. "Fourteen years. I never asked."

She set down the knife. For once she didn't have a quip loaded, and I learned something about her from that.

"You know what the fog does to a place?" she said finally. "It doesn't erase all at once. It erases the edges first. The far field goes, then the fence, then the gate, then the road. And the people standing in the middle keep saying *it's fine, it's fine*, right up until the middle starts softening too." She tapped the ledger. "Your guild is the middle. Has been for four hundred years. Somebody in every generation stood exactly where you're standing, read exactly these pages, and decided the middle was worth defending."

"And you think I'll decide the same."

"I think," Sable said, "that you haven't run yet, and that's more interesting than what you decide."

* * *

I kept reading. What else does a keeper do?

By the third hour the lantern's little flame had become the whole world — a currant of dragon-fire and a pool of gold and the rest darkness — and the ledger had become a country, and I was walking through it, and every mile was worse.

The renewals had a pattern, once I looked. Renewal three, after the ferry disaster at Coldharbour — a ferry whose chains, the marginalia noted with bureaucratic tenderness, *had been loosened per arrangement*. Renewal five, after the fishing fleets of the southern coast lost two hundred souls in a single storm season — storms that, per the margin, *arrived on schedule*. Renewal seven, ninety-one years ago, and the margin note in Guildmaster Hale's own hand: *Supply secured ahead of need. Well done, all.*

They hadn't been scavengers of tragedy. They'd been farmers. They had sown drownings and harvested names, generation after generation, and called it stewardship, and taught children to kneel and recite *a thing taken must be met with a thing returned* while taking and returning precisely nothing.

And the interest — the interest was the worst of it, because interest is where the arithmetic stops being evil in the abstract and becomes evil with a schedule. Page after page of accrued debt, compounding. The dead were owed not just their names back but the years their names had spent in servitude, the songs unsung, the graves ungrieved because no one living knew the names to grieve. The figure at the bottom of the current page ran to eleven digits. I counted twice.

"No one can pay that," Sable said, when I showed her.

"No," I agreed. "Not all at once. Not honestly." I sat back. The lantern flame leaned, though there was no draft. "But the catechism doesn't say pay it fast. It says pay it true. One name at a time, starting with the oldest, starting with—"

"With what?" she said. "Starting with what, Wendell? Even if you read all two hundred and six originals their names back tomorrow, the wards still fall. The fog's already eating the borders. You close the book, the country closes with it."

"Yes."

"That's your plan? *Yes?*"

"It's the beginning of one." I turned pages backward, toward the founding, toward the shaky hand that had written *taken, not received*. "Whoever wrote that note wanted successors to know the truth and flinched from it anyway — he wrote it in the margin, small, like a man whispering in church. Four hundred years of whispers." I looked at the fire. "Somebody has to say it out loud."

The choir, through the hull, went very quiet. Not absent — *attentive*. The way a room goes quiet when the person everyone has been talking about walks in.

And then I heard it, threaded under the silence, clear as a struck glass: a girl's voice, singing a single line of melody I didn't recognize and somehow knew, and beneath it, almost words, almost my name — except not *Wendell*, not the name on the guild's rolls, but *keeper*, the word they'd been using since the first village dissolved, and this time it didn't sound like an accusation.

It sounded like a summons.

Sable heard nothing, of course. But she saw my face change, and she reached over and closed the ledger with one finger before I could stop her.

"Not tonight," she said. "You've been reading for three hours and you look like a man who's aged a decade per hour, which puts you somewhere around two hundred and six, and I don't smuggle antiques." She stood, stretched, retrieved her knife for no reason whatsoever. "Sleep. Tomorrow we talk about what a payment even looks like. And Wendell—" she paused at the hatch, silhouetted against fog, "—when we get wherever we're going, and the dead ask you for their names back? Don't negotiate. Bookkeepers always want to negotiate. Just give them what's theirs."

She went up. I sat alone with the lantern and the closed book and the humming dark.

Eleven digits of debt. Two hundred and six names at the root, and hundreds more grown from them like rot rings in a tree, and somewhere out in the fog, a choir that had waited four hundred years for an honest man to pick up an honest pen.

I opened the ledger again, to the last blank page, and I wrote at the top of it, in my best hand — the hand they'd praised at my graduation, the hand that had carved a thousand stolen names:

*Debt acknowledged in full. Payment to begin immediately. No further renewals will be entertained.*

Below it, smaller: *Signed, Wendell Amberdale, keeper, formerly of the Guild of Rare Exchanges.*

The lantern flared — actually flared, one bright pulse, like a held breath released — and out in the dark the choir began, very softly, to sing.

And far off across the water, thin and wrong against the silence, I heard another sound answering it: hoofbeats, many, riding the surface of the fog itself, coming the way we'd come.

Someone had noticed the oldest ledger was missing.
