## 6. The Choir Names Its Dead

I have been dead for one hundred and forty years, and in all that time, no living person had ever asked me a question.

They talked *at* us, of course. The guild men with their brass tokens and their ledgers, reading out the words that were ours — *Ivetta Marshwater, daughter of the eel-catchers* — as though reciting an inventory. The priests who came to the shore to preach at the water, which is like preaching at rain. And once, memorably, a tax assessor who stood on the quay and read our names off a list to determine whether drowned persons owed back taxes.

We sang at him until he wet himself. Dead girls take their amusements where they can find them.

But asked. No. Never asked. So you will forgive me if, when Wendell Amberdale finally heard us — truly heard us, his hands shaking around that stolen ledger like a man holding a live eel by the tail — I did not greet him gently.

"You," I said, and every voice in the choir said it with me, because we are nothing if not harmonious. "You're the one who carved them."

He flinched. Good. A flinch means the conscience still has feeling in it. I have watched too many guild men smile through worse.

* * *

Let me tell you what it is like to be sold.

Not drowned — drowned was only the door. Sold, after. You feel your name lifted out of you the way a splinter comes out: a small bright pain, then an absence shaped exactly like yourself. You keep everything else. Your memories, your voice, your terrible singing. But when someone calls across a room, you don't turn, because there's nothing left in you that answers to anything. When your mother — if she lived long enough — spoke your name over the water, the word passed through you like wind through a broken window.

The guild took our names and put them into the border wards, and the wards held the fog out of Vell, and Vell called this peace. Peace. As if a country could stand on the backs of the drowned and call itself dry land.

For a century and a half we drifted where the tide took us, which was nowhere, because the tide doesn't want us either. We learned each other. That's what a choir is, really — people who have nothing left but voices, teaching those voices to hold one another up. Old Brammel, who'd been a ferryman and still poled us through the dark water out of habit. The twins, Sela and Senne, who died holding hands and refuse to stop even now, which makes harmony difficult and love easy. And me. I was eleven when the flood took Corren's Landing. I have been eleven for a very long time. It has its inconveniences — nobody takes a dead child seriously, even among the dead, even when the dead child is the only one of us who remembers the words to all the old songs — but it has its uses. People underestimate you. Then you sing the fog into their bones.

So when Wendell Amberdale sat down in his own workshop, ledger open, lantern lit, and said, "I hear you. Tell me what you want," I confess my first instinct was to be cruel about it.

"What we *want*," I said, drifting close enough that he saw me — properly saw me, sea-wet hair and salt-bloated hem and the particular gray that drowning leaves in the cheeks, "is a very long list, bone-carver, and it begins with an apology from everyone who ever profited."

To his credit, he didn't run. He went pale, which for a man whose face is mostly freckles is quite a transformation, and he gripped the ledger like it might anchor him, and he said:

"Then start at the beginning of the list. Please."

*Please.* One hundred and forty years, and the first word anyone offered us freely was *please.*

I decided, provisionally, not to drown him. Provisionally. I'm dead, not soft.

* * *

Here is what I did not expect: he already knew some of it. Not the shape of it — not yet — but the smell of it. He'd found the entry in the ledger, the impossible payment made in names, and he had spent his sleepless night doing arithmetic with his soul.

"The tokens," he said quietly. "The name-tokens I carve. Bone, with a name inscribed." His thumb moved along the edge of the table, back and forth, a carver's restless habit. "They're ours. Yours. They're made from — "

"From us," I finished for him. "Every token you've carved in twenty years carries a name we lost. The guild harvests names the way other men harvest timber. There's always more forest, they tell themselves. Until the forest learns to sing."

He looked at his hands then. I have seen many living people look at their hands — guild men especially, when the truth catches up with them — and most of them look away quickly, or find something else to blame. Wendell Amberdale looked at his hands the way a man looks at tools that have been used against him without his knowledge. Confused. Wounded. And underneath it, beginning to kindle, something I had almost forgotten the living could make:

Anger.

"Who?" he asked. "Whose names? Read me one. Just — just so I know it's real."

So I gave him mine. I stood before him in my drowned dress and I said, "Ivetta Marshwater. Daughter of Maren and Osser Marshwater of Corren's Landing. Eleven years old. I liked eel pie and I hated church and I once pushed my brother into the harbor for calling my singing 'frog-work,' and I never got to apologize for the pushing, because three days later the sea came for all of us, and then the guild came after, and they took the name off my body like picking a pocket."

The workshop was silent except for the lantern's little flame-talk and, beneath everything, the choir, holding its breath. We do breathe. It's a courtesy.

Wendell opened the ledger.

I will say this for guild bookkeeping: it is meticulous. Evil usually is. Sloppy evil gets caught; tidy evil gets *incorporated*. He turned pages with fingers that trembled less than they had any right to, and he found the section, and his eyes went down the column, and I watched him find me.

"Ivetta Marshwater," he read aloud.

And oh — oh, I cannot describe it, I have tried for a day and a night and I give up trying. It was the splinter going back *in*, but sweetly, the way warmth goes into cold hands. My name settled back into me and fit, because it had never stopped fitting, because it was mine, it had always been mine, it had merely been *elsewhere*. Somewhere in the choir, Sela gasped. Old Brammel's pole-hand tightened on nothing.

"Say it again," I said, and my voice cracked, which is a strange sensation when you're mostly water and grievance.

"Ivetta Marshwater," Wendell said again, and then he understood — I watched it land in him like a stone dropped down a well — and he turned back to the beginning of the page and started at the top.

"Brammel Ostwick. Ferryman, Greyharbor. Drowned in the Year of the Long Frost."

Brammel made a sound like a rope under strain.

"Sela Tideborn. Senne Tideborn. Twins, Corren's Landing."

Both at once, both voices braided, the twins laughing and sobbing in the same breath.

He read. Name after name after name. Fishermen and washerwomen, a lighthouse keeper, two smugglers, a whole family named Dunmore — seven entries, seven lines, and when he reached the seventh, a baby, *Mira Dunmore, aged four months*, Wendell Amberdale's voice broke clean through.

I should tell you that I had planned, insofar as the dead plan anything, to stay hard through all of it. To watch him suffer properly. Justice, I thought, should taste like salt. But there is a thing that happens when your name comes home to you while a stranger reads it aloud with tears running into the creases of his guilt — a thing I have no better word for than *dignity*. For a hundred and forty years we had been inventory. Line items. Collateral. And here was a living man, a guild man, a *carver of the tokens themselves*, sitting in a pool of lamplight and giving each of us back our personhood one syllable at a time, weeping over the four-month-old as though she were his own.

It was the first act of justice anyone had ever offered us. It was small. It was one evening, one lantern, one borrowed book. But justice is like that. It doesn't arrive whole. It arrives the way dawn arrives — grudgingly, in installments, until suddenly there's enough light to see by.

* * *

He read until his voice failed, and then he kept reading in a whisper, and when the lantern burned low he fed it oil without stopping, and I drifted through the choir and listened to my people become themselves again, one restored name at a time. Sela and Senne were arguing about whose death had been more graceful. Brammel was telling anyone who'd listen about the ferry he'd nearly bought in Greyharbor, the one with the green trim. Mira Dunmore — well. Some things return as pure light, and I won't cheapen them by describing them.

Near midnight, Wendell stopped. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes.

"There are thousands," he said hoarsely. "Thousands of entries. This volume alone — and it says here the debt was *refinanced*. Renewed. Over and over. Which means—" He looked up at me, and his face had gone from grief to something harder and colder and, frankly, much more promising. "Which means the guild didn't just steal these names. They've been *spending* them. For centuries. To keep the wards up. To keep Vell comfortable."

"Now you see it," I said.

"And the wards fell anyway." He laughed — a short, ugly, wonderful sound. "All those years, all those names, and it wasn't enough. Or it was never meant to last. Either way—" He closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin you intend to reopen. "Someone owes a debt. A real one. Paid honestly, outside whatever law let this happen."

"That," I told him, "is the first intelligent sentence a living man has spoken in my hearing since the tax assessor."

He almost smiled. Then the smile died, because he is quicker than he looks, and he said: "The guild knows I took the ledger. They'll come for it. For me." He glanced toward his shuttered windows, toward the fog pressing gray against the glass. "And whatever they send won't be men. Will it."

"No," I said. "It will be wardens. Stitched with stolen names, the same as the wards. Reclamationists, they call themselves." I let the word sit in the room like a dead fish. "They'll come for the book, keeper. And when they come — " I drew the whole choir in close around his little workshop, every restored voice, every returned name, a hundred and forty years of the unpaid gathering in the dark like weather — "you will not face them alone."

He looked at us. All of us. The drowned, the sold, the forgotten, standing shoulder to shoulder in his shop among the bone shavings and the half-carved tokens, and I watched him understand that his life had ended somewhere between the ledger's first page and its thousandth, and something new had begun instead.

"Then we'd best get moving," he said. "There's a smuggler south of here. If anyone can get us to the capital—"

"*Us,*" I repeated.

"You've waited long enough for your names," Wendell Amberdale said. "You shouldn't have to wait longer for the rest of what you're owed."

Old Brammel laughed then, a great rusty bellows of a sound, and the twins whooped, and the choir — my choir, my beautiful ruined family — began to sing. Not the mourning-song. Not the fog-song. Something older, from before the flood, from before the guild: a road-song, a going-song, the sort of thing eel-catchers' daughters learn at their mothers' knees.

Outside, far off across the dark fields, I felt them coming — the Reclamationists, stitched and wrong, moving fast through the forgetting-fog with guild orders in their mouths.

They were a day behind us. They would be a day behind us forever, if I had anything to say about it.

And I did. I am Ivetta Marshwater. I have my name back.

Now I mean to collect.
