## 7. Hunters in the Fog

I've been chased before. It's practically a job requirement in my line of work, right up there with lying to customs officials and knowing which rivers freeze last. But there's a difference between being chased by a man who wants your cargo and being chased by something that wants *you* — specifically, the parts of you that make you you.

The Reclamationists came out of the fog three days after we crossed into the Thornwood, and they came wrong. Not riding, not marching. Walking like men who'd been assembled rather than born, each step placed with the same mechanical care Wendell used when he was shelving his bone-tokens. I counted six of them through the trees, and every one of them wore a coat stitched from what looked like a hundred different fabrics, none of them matching, all of them humming faintly with borrowed names.

"They're wardens," Corvane had told us, back when he still flinched at his own reflection. "Or what's left of them. The guild takes a warden who's served too long, strips him down, and sews the confiscated names into the seams. Makes them obedient. Makes them *durable*." He'd said it the way you'd describe a medical procedure you'd undergone yourself, which, given the scars on his forearms, maybe he had.

Six of them. One ledger between us. And a forest that couldn't remember its own geography long enough for us to use it against them.

That was the real problem with the Thornwood. Most forests, you can lose a pursuer — double back, cross streams, let the trees do the work. But this wood had been half-eaten by the same forgetting that took the stars. Whole stretches of it had gone soft, paths that existed in the morning gone by noon, oaks standing where birches had been an hour before. My maps were worse than useless here. They were *dangerous*, because I kept trusting them, and the forest kept disagreeing.

"You're sulking at that map again," Wendell said. He'd come up beside me without a sound, which was a habit of his I was starting to find less charming and more alarming. Bone-carvers, apparently, learn to move quiet. It's the hands, he told me once. You spend enough years working small and precise, the rest of you follows.

"I'm not sulking. I'm strategizing at a document that has betrayed me."

"Ah. Strategizing."

"Don't use that tone with me, Amberdale. You've never been lost in your life. You lived above your workshop. Your entire world was forty feet across."

"And now my entire world is a stolen book, a ghost choir only I can hear, and a smuggler who talks to her maps like ex-lovers." He almost smiled. Almost. There wasn't much smiling in him these days, and I understood why, but I missed it anyway. "What does she say?"

"She says we should take the river road east, except the river road east stopped existing around dawn, so instead she says we should take the river road east, which is either courage or dementia."

Behind us, deeper in the trees, the choir started up.

Wendell went still the way a deer goes still — everything at once, total commitment. His head tilted, listening to whatever the dead were telling him, and I watched the color leave his face by degrees.

"What are they saying?"

"That the hunters have stopped following our trail." He swallowed. "They've stopped because they don't need it anymore. The guild master gave them a piece of me."

"A piece of—"

"My name-token. The one I carved when I entered the guild's service. Every member carries one; it's how the ledgers track us." His hand went to his chest, to the place where the token used to hang, and I realized with a cold slide in my stomach that his shirt was open at the collar and the cord was gone. Had been gone since the guildhouse. Since the night he stole the ledger, and someone — or something — had lifted it off him first.

"They can find you through it."

"They can find the token. And the token is..." He made a small, helpless gesture. "Me. As far as the guild's magic is concerned, the token is the truest version of me there is. More true than the flesh wearing it."

"So they're not tracking a man. They're tracking a receipt."

"If you want to be crude about it."

"I always want to be crude about it. It's my best quality."

But my hands weren't steady when I rolled up the map, and Wendell saw, and neither of us said anything about it, because that's the arrangement we'd fallen into: I keep the fear moving so it doesn't pool, and he keeps pretending not to notice, and between us we maintain the fiction that this is all very manageable.

It was not manageable. Six Reclamationists were walking through a dissolving forest toward a man whose name was a homing beacon, and the only people standing between them were a disgraced warden with a hole where his certainties used to be, a child who'd never seen a star, and me — Sable Quill, who had spent thirty years making sure nothing got forgotten, and who was beginning to suspect that the universe had taken that personally.

* * *

We ran.

Not immediately — first there was the argument, because Wendell wanted to stand and talk to them, actually *talk*, on the theory that anything made of stolen names might listen to reason from the man trying to give those names back. Corvane shot that down with a single sentence: "I've seen what they do to people who try. They take the mouth first." After that even Wendell agreed running was the more eloquent position.

So we ran, and the forest ran with us or against us depending on its mood. Trees swapped places when we weren't looking. A stream we'd forded that morning appeared ahead of us going the other direction. The child — Pip, though I suspected that wasn't the name she'd been born with, since she'd been born after the stars went out and nobody remembered what naming things was supposed to feel like — rode on Corvane's shoulders and narrated our flight with the merciless honesty of the very young.

"The trees are scared," she announced. "Can't you hear them? They're all holding their breath."

"Pip," Corvane said, "not now."

"They're scared of the sewn men. The trees remember when the sewn men came before, a long time ago, and took the words out of the ground."

Nobody answered that. There wasn't anything to answer it *with*.

The choir, meanwhile, had become our early-warning system, which meant it had become Wendell's burden, which meant he was doing the thing he did where he carried triple weight and pretended it weighed the same as everyone else's share. He'd stop mid-stride, tilt his head, and call directions like a man reading weather: "Left here — no, hard left, they've split into pairs—" and then we'd run some more, and the fog would press in, and somewhere behind us the Reclamationists would walk their terrible patient walk, unhurried, because they knew something we didn't want to know: that the forest itself was erasing our options faster than we could spend them.

By late afternoon we'd lost two hours of ground to a valley that had forgotten it was a valley and filled itself in. By dusk, Pip was asleep on Corvane's back and Wendell was gray-faced from listening, and I made the call I'd been dreading.

"We need to stop. We need a fire, food, sleep. Whatever's chasing us walks slow; if we drop dead on our feet, slow will be plenty."

"We can't risk a fire," Corvane said.

"We can't risk *nothing*, is what we can't risk. I've seen what happens to people who forget to eat in this country. They forget they have mouths, and then it's over." I was already gathering wood as I talked, because in my experience the best way to win an argument is to start doing the thing before anyone agrees to it. "Small fire. Green wood, low smoke. Two hours, then we move."

Wendell sat down heavily against a stone that may or may not have been there yesterday. For a moment he looked every year of his age and several he hadn't earned yet.

"Sable," he said quietly. "If they catch us — when they catch us — they'll go for the ledger first. Then me. Then anyone who helped." He looked at me across the little fire, and the light did something honest to his face. "You could still walk away. Take your boat, your roads, your skin full of names, and be gone before they arrive. No one would call you a coward. I wouldn't."

I threw a stick at him. Missed, which somehow made the point better.

"Amberdale. Listen carefully, because I'm only saying this once and I'll deny it forever after. Thirty years ago I watched the guild erase my brother. Not kill — *erase*. One day he owed them money, the next day nobody in our village remembered he'd ever existed, including our mother, including me until I found his name written in my own hand in one of my old notebooks and couldn't remember writing it." I fed the fire another branch, harder than necessary. "Everything I am — every tattoo, every scar, every word inked into my hide — exists because I decided no one gets taken out of the record while I'm alive to hold the pen. So no. I'm not walking away. I'm the opposite of walking away. I'm the woman who stays and writes it all down."

He didn't say anything. He just looked at me for a long moment with an expression I'd learn later was the closest he came to gratitude — a kind of stunned, wounded softness, like a man being handed something he'd stopped believing existed.

Then the choir screamed.

All of it, every drowned voice at once, a sound only Wendell could hear but so violent that he folded forward as if struck, hands over ears, and Corvane was up with a blade drawn and I was killing the fire with dirt, and through the trees — through the fog that had crept close while we talked — came the Reclamationists.

All six. No hurry in them at all.

* * *

Here is what I know about fighting things that are mostly memory: you cannot beat them the way you beat men. Corvane learned it first. He met the nearest one head-on, proper warden technique, blade angled for the seam under the arm — and the blade went through cloth and came out the other side trailing threads, and the Reclamationist didn't bleed, didn't stagger, didn't do anything a body ought to do when opened. It just turned its head, slow as a door, and looked at him with eyes that had once belonged to somebody else.

"Corvane," it said, in a voice like a hymn played underwater. "You were Seventh Chair. You signed the transfer orders for the Harrow parish names." It smiled with someone else's mouth. "We remember you."

"That's very touching," Corvane said, and hit it with a tree.

To be fair to him, it worked better than the sword. The trunk caught the creature across the shoulders and drove it into the ground, and for a moment the stitches along its back split and something poured out of the gap — not blood, *names*, actual syllables spilling into the air like startled birds, and every one of them made the fog recoil. I filed that away. Everything in this country is afraid of something, and I had just watched the fog flinch from loose names.

"Wendell!" I shouted. "The seams! Cut the seams!"

He was already moving, ledger clutched to his chest like a shield, which was exactly wrong — the ledger wasn't for shielding, it was for *reading* — and I grabbed his collar and hauled him behind the stone. "Listen to me. You hear the choir. The choir knows these creatures' names — every name sewn into them, the whole inventory. If we cut them open, the names get out. Can the choir catch them? Catch them and *keep* them?"

His eyes went wide, then fierce, and I watched fear turn into something with edges.

"Yes," he breathed. "Yes — Ivetta says — she says bring them down and hold them still, and she'll do the rest—"

"Then we hunt."

I want it recorded that what followed was not a battle so much as a burglary conducted at speed. We couldn't destroy the Reclamationists; we could only rob them. Corvane and I became the blunt instruments — driving them into trees, into stones, into each other, anything to split those humming seams — while Wendell stood in the middle of the chaos with his arms raised and his eyes rolled back, singing in a voice that wasn't entirely his, calling out names like a man calling cattle home. And every time a seam tore and the syllables spilled free, the fog *took* them — no. Not the fog. The choir took them, out of the air, out of the creatures' backs, gathering the stolen dead home like a shepherd who has finally, finally found the fence broken.

The Reclamationists fought the way fog fights: patiently, everywhere at once, wearing us down. One of them caught me by the throat and lifted, and its grip was cold in a way cold isn't supposed to work, cold that reached past skin into the record of me, and I felt it *tugging* — felt it trying to unpick the oldest tattoo, the first name I ever wrote on myself, my brother's name over my heart—

I did the only reasonable thing. I bit through its glove and spat the leather out, and drove my belt-knife into the seam at its shoulder, and ripped.

The name that came out was a woman's. It hung in the air shining for half a heartbeat, and then the choir had it, and the Reclamationist dropped me and simply... deflated, coat collapsing around nothing, because apparently it had been that name holding the shape together all along.

Three of them we emptied that way. A fourth Corvane put down with a boulder and considerable personal satisfaction. The fifth and sixth broke off — stepped backward into the fog in unison, unhurried even in retreat, and the last thing the nearer one said was addressed to me, in that drowned-hymn voice:

"Keeper of skins. The guild master knows what you carry on your body. He knows what your maps cost. When we come again, we will take the paper first."

Then the fog closed, and they were gone, and the clearing was silent except for Pip crying quietly and Wendell on his knees in the ash of our fire, shaking, listening to a choir that was suddenly, vastly fuller than it had been that morning.

* * *

We took stock by moonlight that may or may not have been the moon, since nobody had seen stars in weeks and we were all guessing.

The count was bad. Corvane had a gash down his arm that needed stitching and refused to discuss how he'd gotten it. Pip had stopped crying but had also stopped talking, which in a child her age was the worse injury. Wendell hadn't spoken since the fight; he sat turning his hands over and over, looking at them like tools that had done unexpected work.

And I — I unrolled my maps, all eleven of them, the record of thirty years of roads and rivers and safehouses and graves, every inch annotated in my own hand, and found them blank.

Not destroyed. Not burned. *Blank.* The Reclamationists hadn't touched them. They hadn't needed to. Somewhere in that fight, in the cold grip at my throat, in the tugging I'd felt — they had reached into the paper and taken the ink out of it. Every route, every mark, every name of every contact I'd ever trusted, gone, leaving eleven sheets of clean white nothing.

I sat down. Hard. I'll admit it. Thirty years of memory, and I'd kept it on paper like a fool, paper that could be *unwritten*, and the guild had just demonstrated that they considered my life's work a line item.

"Hey." Wendell's voice, rough from the singing. He crouched next to me. "Sable. Look at me."

"They took my maps, Amberdale."

"I know."

"Thirty years. Every road I ever found. Every person who ever hid me. Gone like — like chalk in rain."

"I know." He hesitated, and then he did the bravest thing I saw him do in the whole sorry history of Vell, braver than the altar, braver than the end of it all, though I didn't know it yet. He held out the ledger. The stolen one. The reason six sewn men had walked through a dying forest after us.

"Read yourself," he said.

"What?"

"In the ledger. The guild recorded everything — that's the whole horror of it, they wrote down every theft, every name, every exchange. Which means—" he tapped the cover, "—if they tracked you, if they catalogued your brother, if they noted your routes because you were smuggling people *out* of their reach for years — it's in here. The guild's own book. Their crime, turned into your backup."

My hands shook opening it. I won't pretend otherwise. I found the index with fingers that wouldn't behave, ran down the columns, and there — under Q, because the guild had filed my brother's erasure under Quill, first initial T., disposition: complete — was Tomas. My brother. His name, his debt, the date they took him out of the world, all of it in a clerk's tidy hand.

And below it, in the margin, in smaller script, a note: *subject's sister (S.Q.) intercepted correspondence re: parish routes, 3 entries, see folio 112.*

Folio 112 had my roads. Not all of them. Three of them. But three roads I'd thought lost forever, written down by the enemy, preserved by their own monstrous thoroughness.

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and Pip looked up at the sound, and Corvane paused in his stitching, and I laughed anyway, because the alternative was screaming and I've always been a professional.

"They documented their own crimes," I said. "Arrogant bastards kept receipts."

"The guild's one virtue," Wendell said, "is that it never throws anything away."

I closed the ledger and held it against my chest, over my brother's name, over my own initials, and felt something settle in me that I recognized, eventually, as resolve — not the hot kind that burns out, but the cold kind that builds. They could unwrite my maps. They could unwrite me, maybe, if they caught me. But they had written it all down first, in their precious book, and that book was mine now, and every page of it was ammunition.

"Well then," I said, standing, wiping my face with the sleeve that had Tomas's name inked beneath it. "New plan. We stop running east like rabbits and start moving like people with a destination. Because I know a road now — a real one, out of their own records — and it leads to the flooded capital, and I intend to walk it while reading their dirty laundry aloud."

Wendell smiled then. Actually smiled, tired and crooked and real.

And that was when the choir changed key.

He stiffened, head tilting, and I watched his expression go from warmth to wonder to something I didn't have a word for — awe with fear inside it, like a man watching a door open where no door had been.

"What is it?" I asked. "What are they saying?"

He looked at me, and then past me, at the dark trees, at the fog, at the whole unraveling country, and his voice when it came was barely above a whisper.

"They're singing the road. All of it — every mile to the capital. Sable, they've been carrying it in their mouths this whole time, waiting for us to ask." He swallowed. "The dead are offering to lead us home."

The fog parted ahead of us, just a little. Just enough to show a path.

And down that path, faint as breath, we heard singing.
