## 9. Ivetta's Verdict

The dead do not sleep, which is fortunate, because we had a trial to hold.

I had been dead for two hundred and eleven years, and in that time I had learned three things about being a ghost. First: you cannot touch anything, no matter how much you want to. Second: time goes strange, like water going strange, so that an hour can be a season and a season can be a sneeze. Third: forgiveness is a living person's luxury. The dead can offer many things — memory, warning, the cold weight of witness — but forgiveness requires a future, and we had none. We had only what was taken from us, and the shape of its absence.

So when the bone-carver knelt in the mud of the half-forgotten forest with his hands open and empty, and Sable Quill stood over him with her arms crossed and her skin full of other people's forgotten mornings, and the whole choir of us hung in the fog like breath on glass — when he said, *I carved them. Every token. For nineteen years I carved names into bone and never once asked whose they were* — none of us offered him forgiveness.

We offered him silence instead.

You have never heard silence until you have heard four hundred drowned voices stop singing at once. The fog itself seemed embarrassed. Even the trees, those soft gray suggestions of trees that couldn't quite remember being trees, leaned in.

Wendell Amberdale stayed kneeling. I will give him that. A lesser man would have filled the silence with excuses, and excuses are the currency of the living, always minted too fast and spent too cheaply. He just knelt there in the mud with his ruined hands open, and waited for whatever we decided he deserved.

Sable broke first. She always breaks first; it is her best quality.

"Well?" she said, to me, because apparently I was the judge now. "He confessed. Don't ghosts get to vote or something?"

"We're not a jury," I said. "A jury decides guilt. His guilt isn't in question. He confessed before witnesses." I drifted closer, close enough to see the fog move through my own hands, which even after two centuries I found rude. "The question is what happens next."

"Then decide," she said. "Because if you lot stand here humming at each other all night, the Reclamationists will find us, and I'd rather not be stitched into some warden's coat."

"She's right," Wendell said quietly. "Whatever you choose, choose it fast."

"No," I said. "That's exactly wrong. That's how the guild thinks. Fast decisions, sealed ledgers, entries made in haste and paid for by other people for centuries." I turned to face him properly. He flinched — the living always flinch when a ghost looks directly at them; it's like being read by your own conscience with better posture. "No. You've spent nineteen years doing your work quickly and carelessly. You'll wait while we do ours slowly."

And we did. We took until dawn.

* * *

Here is what the choir did while the bone-carver knelt in the mud: we remembered him.

This is our one power, and it is not nothing. The guild sold our names, but they could not sell what happened to us, because nobody wants to buy drowning. So we kept it. Every one of us keeps her own last hour, polished smooth as river stone from handling. And now we pooled them, all four hundred and eleven of our final hours, and passed them among ourselves looking for him — for any trace of him, any moment where his hands had touched our ending.

Maren found it first. Maren was a fisherwoman out of Corrow Bay, drowned in the year the guild refinanced the northern wards, and her last hour held this: a workshop smelling of tallow and bone dust, and a young man with ink-stained fingers taking a token from a tray and reading it with a small frown, the frown of a man checking arithmetic, not the frown of a man wondering who the name belonged to. Then he set it in a box marked *NORTH WARD — RENEWAL*, and went back to his tea.

That was all. That was everything. Nineteen years of tokens, and in every drowning, somewhere in the machinery of it, there was Wendell Amberdale's careful, incurious handwriting.

"He didn't drown us," said old Tam, who had been a ferryman and had strong opinions about water. "The guild drowned us long before he was born. He just... kept the gears oiled."

"He carved us," said little Nell, who had been seven. She had been seven for two hundred years and would be seven forever, and when she spoke, even the fog listened. "Every one of us, he carved. My name came off his knife. I felt it go."

"That's not fair, Nell," Maren said gently. "You were already gone."

"I know," said Nell. "But it's still true."

And that, more than anything, is why the dead do not forgive. It isn't cruelty. It's precision. Forgiveness blurs; the dead cannot afford to blur. We are made entirely of what actually happened.

When dawn came up gray through the fog, I went back to the clearing where the bone-carver still knelt — he hadn't moved, though I could see the tremble in his shoulders, the way cold and fear and shame braid themselves into muscle — and I gave him our verdict.

* * *

"Wendell Amberdale," I said, and he shuddered at the sound of his own name, which interested me. Most men preen at it. "The choir has considered you."

"I'm ready," he said. "For whatever—"

"You're not," I said. "Nobody is ever ready. Be quiet."

He was quiet.

"Here is what we found when we looked for you in our deaths. We found your handwriting. In every drowning the guild has engineered since you took up the knife, there you are: precise, punctual, and blind. You checked the tokens for cracks. You checked them for forgery. You checked everything except whether they should exist at all." I let that sit. Fog sat on his shoulders like a second man. "You want to know if we forgive you. We don't. We can't. Forgiveness is for people with futures, and ours was sold. But hear me, bone-carver: we do not forgive you, and we do not cast you out either. There is a third thing, and it is harder than both."

"What third thing?" Sable asked, suspicious. She stood apart with her arms wrapped around herself, and I noticed — because the dead notice everything, it's practically our job — that she had positioned herself between Wendell and the treeline. Watching for wardens even now. Loyalty, in Sable Quill, looked exactly like irritation.

"Use," I said.

"Use," Wendell repeated.

"The guild used you for nineteen years. They used your hands, your patience, your talent, and your willingness not to ask questions. Fine. Now we use you. All of it. Your hands, your patience, your talent — and this time, your questions too." I drifted down until my face was level with his, close enough that he could see the water still moving in my hair, though there had been no water in that forest for a hundred years. "You built the machine that eats names. You will spend the rest of your life unbuilding it, token by token, name by name, and the choir will carry you while you do. Not forgive you. Carry you. Do you understand the difference?"

His eyes were wet. Living eyes, always leaking; it's undignified and I confess I envied it. "I think so," he whispered.

"Say it plainly. The dead deal in plain speech. It's one of the few perks."

"If I give you my work," he said slowly, feeling each word like a man testing ice, "you'll... help me finish it. Help me return the names. All of them. Even though—" His voice cracked. "Even though I'm the reason most of them need returning."

"And when the ledger is balanced," I said, "when every stolen name sits back in the mouth that lost it — then we'll talk about what you are to us. Not before. Penance first. Verdict after. That is the order of things."

Sable snorted. "That's not mercy, that's indentured servitude."

"Yes," I said pleasantly. "We learned the arrangement from the guild. We're told imitation is sincere flattery."

Wendell laughed. It was a broken, wet, astonished sound, and it did something to the fog around us — loosened it, the way a chord loosens a locked door. "Then I accept," he said. "On one condition of my own."

Ghosts cannot raise eyebrows, but we manage the effect anyway. "Go on."

"When I carve the first honest token — the first one that gives a name back instead of taking it away — I want it to be yours, Nell." He turned toward where the small dead girl hung in the mist. "You said your name came off my knife. Let it come back the same way."

Nell was silent for a long moment. Two hundred years of silence, folded into a moment.

"All right," she said finally. "But I get to watch. Close."

"You'd have watched anyway," said Tam fondly.

"I know," said Nell. "I like watching him suffer. Is that allowed? Being dead and petty?"

"It's practically mandatory," I said.

* * *

He worked by dragon-fire lantern light, there in the mud of the dying forest, with the fog pressing close and four hundred and eleven ghosts gathered round like a congregation that had finally gotten the sermon it was owed.

First he had to make the token. He had no prepared bone — his stock had gone into the river with the rest of his old life — so he sacrificed something of his own: a carving from his pocket, a little songbird he'd whittled years ago, the kind of idle gift a man makes for someone he means to give it to and never does. I saw Sable see it. I saw her decide not to ask. Some mercies are just good timing.

He scraped the bird smooth with his knife, murmuring apologies to it, and then he took out the ledger — the old one, the true one, the one he'd stolen from the burning guildhouse while his masters' world ended behind him — and found Nell's entry.

It took him a while. The ledger was written in the guild's cipher, columns of refinancing dates and ward-anchors and interest accrued in souls, and finding one girl's name inside all that bookkeeping was like finding one specific tear in a flood. But he found it. *Nellis Marsh, b. 1491, d. 1502, Corrow district. Collateralized: Northern Ward, third refinance. Value assessed: negligible.*

*Negligible.* I heard the choir read that word together, four hundred and eleven mouths, and the temperature dropped hard enough to frost the ferns.

"They wrote 'negligible,'" Sable said flatly. "About a child."

"They wrote it about all of us," I said. "Get used to the word. We're going to spend it back at them with interest."

Wendell's jaw was tight. "The entry has to be amended in the proper form or the ledger won't accept it," he said. "I know the forms. God help me, I know all the forms." He wet his pen. Beneath the guild's cold column of figures, in a hand that shook only a little, he wrote: *Returned. Freely given. Witnessed by the creditor entire.*

Then he set knife to bone.

I have watched men carve before. Butchers, shipwrights, sculptors — the living love to cut things into shapes. But I had never watched a man carve the way Wendell Amberdale carved that morning, slow as a funeral, each stroke deliberate, each curl of bone dust falling like snow onto his knees. He carved her name — her true name, *Nellis*, not the diminutive the ledger preferred — and beneath it the date of her birth, which the ledger had recorded and nobody had ever celebrated, and beneath that a single word: *remembered*.

When he finished, he held the token up in both hands, the way you'd hold water in a drought.

"Nellis Marsh," he said aloud. "Born in spring. Died at seven. Loved — " he glanced at the ledger, then closed it, refusing it. "Loved. That's enough. Your name is yours. Take it home."

Nell drifted forward.

I will tell you what happened, and you may believe me or not; the dead have stopped minding. When her small ghost-hands closed around the token, the bone did not pass through her. It *held*. For two hundred years nothing in this world had held against her, and now something did, and she made a sound I had not heard since the day I drowned — a sound like a laugh and a sob sharing a coat.

"It's warm," she said. "Why is it warm?"

"Dragon-fire lantern," Wendell said hoarsely. "It's been near the flame for weeks. It holds heat."

"Liar," said Nell, glowing. "It's warm because it's mine."

And then she sang.

One voice, thin and clear, a child's voice out of a drowned century — and the choir caught it, the way a congregation catches a hymn, and for the first time since the stars went out we sang a note that was not grief. I cannot describe it without lying, so I will say only this: the fog stopped moving. The trees remembered themselves for a full minute. Somewhere far off, a fox began to cry, which foxes do when the world does something holy and they feel left out.

Sable Quill was crying and pretending she wasn't, scrubbing at her face with a sleeve covered in other people's memories. "Right," she said, voice cracking. "Fine. Good. Now that we've all had our feelings — " she pointed east, where the fog was beginning to churn in a way fog shouldn't, in long deliberate ropes, like something breathing through a straw — "we should probably run, because that's a working, and workings mean wardens, and I count at least six knots of it coming up the old drover's road."

Wendell got to his feet, joints cracking, the ledger clutched to his chest like a newborn. "How far?"

"Half a night, if we're lucky. Less, if they've got a stitched tracker." She looked at me. "Well, choir? You've got your penance-project. Can you carry him or not?"

I looked at Wendell Amberdale — kneeling man, rising man, keeper, carver, complicit, contrite, ours now whether he liked it or not — and I gave him the only verdict the dead have left to give.

"We carry you," I said. "Run."

Behind us, Nell's single warm note rose and multiplied, four hundred and eleven voices catching fire from it, and ahead of us the fog parted around the drover's road like a crowd making room for a funeral — which, depending on how the next few miles went, is precisely what it was.
