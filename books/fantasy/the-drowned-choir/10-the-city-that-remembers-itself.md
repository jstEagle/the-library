## 10. The City That Remembers Itself

I've smuggled a lot of things in my life — contraband salt, stolen relics, one very indignant archduke rolled in a carpet — but I'd never smuggled hope before, and let me tell you, it's the heaviest cargo there is. It doesn't pack well. It leaks.

We came down out of the hills on the ninth day after the forest, when the fog finally thinned enough that we could see what lay ahead of us: the capital of Vell, drowned to its rooftops, glittering under a sky with no stars in it like a table set for a dinner party where every guest had died.

"Cheerful," said Corvane, who had joined us three days prior and whose conversational contributions so far consisted mostly of grim observations delivered at inconvenient moments. "The old quarter's still above waterline. The Spire district. Dragon-stone."

"Dragon-stone?" Wendell clutched his ledger to his chest the way other men clutch their mothers. He'd been doing that since the Reclamationists nearly took it off him. I couldn't blame him. Half my body was covered in notes that said essentially the same thing: *don't lose this.*

"The palace foundations," Corvane said. "Old kings liked to build on dragon-fire. Said it made their rule eternal." His mouth did something bitter. "It made the stones remember, anyway."

I squinted across the flooded plain. And there it was — I saw what he meant. The fog lay over everything like a wet blanket, softening edges, eating outlines, the same hungry grey soup that had swallowed half a village behind us while its residents stood in it mid-sentence and forgot they'd ever had faces. But over the city center, the fog *broke*. Bent around something. The towers stood sharp-edged and stubborn, their reflections sharp in the floodwater below them, as if the water itself refused to blur them.

"It's not forgetting," I said slowly. "The city. It's holding on."

"Hold on to your own head while you're at it," Corvane said. "Reclamationists patrol the causeways. If they're not here yet, they're coming."

"They're always coming," I said. "That's the trouble with people who think they own the past. They never stop collecting."

* *

Getting into a drowned city is easier than getting out of one, which is the kind of wisdom nobody pays smugglers enough for.

The streets ran underwater from the second ring inward, clear and green-gold, lit from beneath by veins of dull ember-light running through the masonry like blood through marble. Dragon-fire, soaked into the stone centuries ago, still burning down slow as tree rings. Fish moved through doorways that used to be front doors. A market square spread out forty feet down, awnings gone to lace, stalls furred with weed, and standing in the middle of it all — I swear on my mother's grave, which I dug myself, which is a longer story — a fountain still playing. Water rising out of water, endlessly, pointlessly, beautifully.

Wendell stopped on the causeway rail and just stared, ledger hugged tight, mouth open like a landed fish.

"You're going to want to close that," I told him. "Fog gets in."

"How is it still *running*?"

"Dragon-stone doesn't know how to quit," Corvane said. "Everything built on it keeps doing what it was doing. Forever. There are ovens down there that have been baking nothing for two hundred years."

"That's the saddest sentence anyone's ever said to me."

"Wait until he tells you about the clock tower," I said.

And then I heard it. Faint, threading up through the floodwater, through the stone, through the soles of my boots and into my teeth: singing. Not the choir — Wendell's ghosts kept their distance from me mostly, out of some ghostly etiquette I chose not to question. This was different. Thinner. Human voices, many of them, echoing up through drowned streets, singing something wordless and round, the way you sing when you're trying to keep time without a clock.

Wendell's face changed. "You hear it too?"

"I hear *something*."

"That's not the choir," he said, and there was wonder in it, and fear, because Wendell wore those two expressions on the same face so often they'd started to share a wrinkle. "That's living voices."

* *

We found them by following the song down a stair that became a canal that became a stair again, into the heart of the old merchant quarter, where the dragon-veins burned brightest and the fog outside the walls pressed against the windows like a beggar at glass.

The tavern was called The Drowned Rat, which struck me as either very honest or very optimistic naming, and it sat half-submerged in a sunken plaza, its upper floor dry, its lower floor an aquarium, its cellar apparently a mermaid convention. Someone had strung lanterns — real fire, tallow, cheap — along the balcony rail, and the light fell gold on tables scarred by a hundred years of knives and tankards, and around the biggest table sat perhaps twenty people, singing.

They weren't refugees, exactly. Refugees have somewhere they fled *from* and somewhere they're fleeing *to*. These were people who had simply decided the world could end without their permission being required. An old woman mending nets that would never hold fish again, because mending was her prayer. Two brothers gutting eels from the flooded street below, hauling them up through the window with a bucket on a rope. A man teaching a dog to balance a cup. Children — actual children — chasing each other between the tables while the adults sang over their heads.

Every one of them looked up when we came through the door. Twenty faces. And here's the thing about faces in Vell these days: most places, if the fog has been working on you, people start going vague. Features soften. You can't quite say later whether the innkeeper had a beard or a scar or a name. I've watched whole conversations dissolve into warm noise.

These faces were *sharp*. Every line carved deep. Eyes like coins fresh from the mint.

"Well met," said a voice near the hearth, "and mind the third step, it bites."

The speaker rose from a corner table where he'd been sitting apart, drinking alone, which is how I knew him before he even turned around. Wardens drink alone. Even disgraced ones — *especially* disgraced ones. He was big through the shoulders in the way of men who've carried armor too long, grey at the temples, and stitched down the length of his left forearm, visible where his sleeve was pushed back, was a column of names in black ink. Dozens of them. Small, neat, careful letters, each one different in a hand that wasn't his.

Corvane went rigid beside me. "Vessa."

She turned. And I watched two people recognize each other across a gap that had nothing to do with distance. Her face did three things in one second — shock, fury, and underneath both, horribly, relief.

"*Corvane.*" She crossed the room fast. Up close she was younger than her voice, maybe thirty-five, with a burn scar along her jaw and eyes that had done arithmetic no one should have to do. "They told the garrisons you died at Harrow Ford."

"I did, more or less." He glanced at the names on her arm. "Those aren't yours."

"No." Her jaw set. "They're ones I pulled back. Reclaimed from the reclaimers. Fourteen names I got out of the archive before they sealed it." She touched the ink almost gently. "Someone has to keep them somewhere honest."

"And they struck you off for it."

"They struck me off for asking who gave the order to start writing *living* people into the ledgers." She said it flat, but the flatness cost her; I saw the price paid in the tendons of her neck. Then she saw Wendell. Saw the ledger under his arm. Her hand went to a knife that wasn't there — wardens, honestly — and Corvane put his own hand over her wrist, easy as breathing.

"It's the true ledger, Vessa. The first one. We're taking it to the fire-chamber to pay the debt. All of it."

For a long moment the only sound was the fountain far below, still playing its endless pointless beautiful water, and the tavern's singers, faltering into silence one by one as they realized something important was happening at the door.

Then Vessa laughed. One short, cracked note, like ice giving way. "All of it." She looked at Wendell the way you look at a man who says he'll empty the sea with a spoon. "Keeper. You have any idea how many names are in that book?"

"No," Wendell said quietly. "But I'm going to read every one."

* *

They gave us a table. Of course they gave us a table — that's what the Drowned Rat was *for*, though nobody could have told you its purpose in words. Places like this don't survive on commerce. They survive on gravity. Somebody starts a table and the lost things roll toward it, and before anyone's decided anything, there's a family, and everyone insists it's temporary right up until it isn't.

The eel brothers fed us. The net-mender — Maren, ninety if she was a day, hands like knotted rope — put a bowl of something hot in front of the smallest member of our party before anyone had even noticed the child was crying.

Oh. Yes. I should mention the child.

She'd been following us since morning. I'd spotted her twice in the fog — a small shape keeping exactly forty paces back, ducking behind ruins with the tactical subtlety of a cat behind a curtain. Corvane clocked her too and, to his credit, didn't reach for a weapon, which for Corvane is practically a warm embrace. When we came down the stair into the plaza, she'd simply fallen in behind us like a duckling that had imprinted on the least maternal object available, which, given the choices, was me.

She was maybe eight years old. Small for it. Barefoot, wrapped in an adult coat cut down badly, hair chopped short with what looked like shears wielded in the dark. And when Maren set the bowl in front of her and said, "There you go, little love, what do they call you?" —

the child looked up with the strangest expression. Not shy. Not scared. *Puzzled*, like Maren had asked her a riddle in a foreign language.

"I don't have one," she said. "I was born after the stars went out. Mam said names come from the stars." She said it without self-pity, the way you report weather. "So I'm waiting."

The whole table went quiet. Even the dog stopped balancing its cup.

I felt something twist in my chest that I did not appreciate. I've spent my whole life making sure I couldn't be erased — inked myself full of names and dates and debts, every memory nailed to my skin like shingles against the rain. And here was a child who'd never been written down anywhere at all. No ledger. No record. No guild entry, no tax roll, no christening. The fog couldn't take what was never inscribed — but neither could anyone give it back.

"Mam said when the stars came back, I'd get to pick," she went on, spooning eel stew with the focus of the genuinely hungry. "So I'm waiting to pick."

"What if they never come back?" asked one of the eel brothers, gently, the way you ask a hard thing of a child when lying would be worse.

"Then I'll pick anyway," she said, matter-of-fact, "when I find something worth naming."

I caught Wendell looking at her. Then looking at his ledger. Then looking at her again with an expression I recognized, because I'd been wearing versions of it since the night the stars went out: the look of a man doing arithmetic he hates.

"Don't," I said, low, under the table noise.

"I wasn't—"

"You were absolutely about to offer her a name out of that book. Don't. Names in there aren't gifts, Wendell. They're invoices."

He closed his mouth. To his credit — and I was keeping a running tally of his credits lately, it was becoming an alarming habit — he understood. Instead he slid his bowl toward the girl, trading her his eels for her potatoes, solemn as a treaty, and she considered the terms and accepted, and that, more than any speech, seemed to decide the room's opinion of us.

* *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the lanterns had burned to stubs and the singers had sung themselves hoarse and gone to bedrolls among the tables — I sat at the big table with the core of it: Wendell bent over the ledger by candlelight, lips moving, cataloguing. Corvane sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. Vessa across from him, the two wardens circling each other verbally like cats deciding whether the truce holds, and I gathered from the wreckage of their conversation that it had been Vessa who'd trained Corvane, once, back when they were both believers. That left marks on both sides.

And the child, asleep in a nest of nets by the hearth, one fist curled around Maren's rope.

I sat there with my ale going warm and thought: *this is the thing the fog can't touch.*

Not dragon-stone. Not wards, not ledgers, not ink. All the machinery of remembering — I'd trusted it my whole life, stitched it into my own hide — and it had failed everywhere it mattered, because machinery only remembers what someone chooses to feed it. The guild had chosen to feed it debt. The fog was only the bill collector.

What the fog couldn't touch was a table. Eel stew passed to a stranger. A bowl traded for potatoes. A woman mending useless nets because her hands needed the prayer. A warden striking fourteen stolen names onto her own arm and calling it justice, unpaid and unlicensed and utterly outside any law that had ever been written in Vell.

Nobody had granted any of this. Nobody had authorized the Drowned Rat. It existed because people kept choosing it, nightly, one bowl at a time. Belonging chosen, not bestowed — I'd smuggled a lot of philosophies in my day and discarded most of them at the border, but this one I decided, sitting there in the tallow-light, to declare at customs and keep.

"You're smiling," Wendell said, not looking up from the ledger. "It's unsettling."

"I'm drunk."

"You've had half an ale."

"I'm *economically* drunk. It goes further." I turned my cup. "Wendell. Ask them."

He looked up. "Ask who what?"

"The Rat-folk. Vessa. Ask if they'll stand with you. At the fire-chamber." I nodded at the sleeping child, at Maren, at the whole improbable warm weight of the room. "Because whatever happens down there — and I've seen enough guild operations to know it won't be tidy — you're going to need more than a bone-carver, a smuggler, and two ex-wardens with trust issues."

"We're not ex-wardens," Corvane said. "We're current wardens of a jurisdiction that no longer exists. It's different."

"It's worse," Vessa said, and almost smiled.

Wendell looked around the tavern — at the nets, the eels, the fountain playing its heart out forty feet below, at the child asleep among the knots of rope — and something in his face settled, like a keel finding water.

"All right," he said. "Tomorrow. I'll ask."

"There won't be a tomorrow to ask in," said Vessa, and the warmth went out of the room like a snuffed lamp. She was standing, head tilted, listening to something none of the rest of us had caught yet — and then I caught it too, faint, wrong, coming up through the floodwater from the direction of the eastern causeway. Not the choir. Not the singing of the drowned.

Boots. Hundreds of them. In step.

"Lanterns out," Vessa breathed. "Now. They've found the city — and keeper—" her eyes fixed on the ledger in Wendell's arms, and on the small sleeping shape of the only person in Vell born free of it, "—they've come for the child."
