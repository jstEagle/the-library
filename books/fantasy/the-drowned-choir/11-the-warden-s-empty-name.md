## 11. The Warden's Empty Name

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep now, scratched into oar-blades and barrel staves because paper is a luxury of countries that still exist — that I did not defect for noble reasons.

There was no thunderclap of conscience. No midnight epiphany in which I saw the drowned dead and wept. What happened was smaller and stupider: I was given an order, and I read it, and reading it made me sick, and I have never in my life been able to be sick quietly.

The order came sealed in grey wax, which is the wax they use when they don't want a thing remembered by anyone but its recipient. It said, in the guild master's own cramped hand:

*The debt requires collateral of sufficient mass. The child will serve. Bring her to the Reclamation Hall by the dark of the moon.*

The child. Pell. Six years old, born under no stars, who had spent the entire voyage to the drowned capital asking me whether fish got lonely, and whether I'd ever eaten anything worse than boot leather, and whether the fog could get inside you if you swallowed some by accident. She had decided, somewhere along our march, that I was hers to look after, in the way that children decide things — absolutely, without paperwork.

And the guild master had written *the child will serve* the way he might write *the ink is running low*.

So yes. I deserted. I stole a boat, I burned my stitching-scars with lye until the stolen names in them went quiet, and I rowed into a city that is mostly underwater, to find the man whose name was in every ledger entry I'd ever enforced.

Not my finest plan. But plans are for people whose institutions haven't rotted from the inside, and mine had.

* * *

They nearly killed me at the gate, which I deserved, and then Sable Quill stopped them, which I didn't.

"Corvane," she said, standing between me and Wendell's bone-knife with her arms crossed and her skin full of handwriting. "He's one of theirs."

"I was," I said. My voice came out wrong — too flat, like a coin dropped on stone. "I'm not anymore. As of four days ago."

"That's not how being one of theirs works," Sable said. "You don't stop. You just run out of orders."

She wasn't wrong. That's the thing about Sable Quill; she's rarely wrong, and she makes sure you know it, and somehow you end up grateful, because someone has to say the true ugly thing out loud or it festers.

Wendell lowered the knife slowly. He looked older than his ledger entries suggested — thinner, hollowed around the eyes, the way men get when they've been reading names aloud for weeks. Behind him, in the flooded tavern where they'd made their camp, the air hummed. Not sound exactly. More like the memory of sound, pressed flat and stored.

"You heard it too," he said quietly. "The order."

"I carried it." I reached into my coat and produced the grey-sealed letter, and watched him flinch from it as if it were wet. "The guild master means to refinance the debt. The old way's failing — the wards are dead, the ledger's in your hands, and the drowned won't take substitutes anymore. So he's found new collateral." I looked at the letter, then couldn't look at it anymore. "Living collateral. Starting with the girl."

The humming in the air stopped. All of it, all at once, like a room full of people holding one breath.

Then the choir arrived.

I had heard Reclamationist stories about the Drowned Choir — that they were a weapon, a haunting, a fog with teeth. Stories are how wardens keep from thinking. What actually manifested in that tavern was nothing so simple. They came up through the water-stained floorboards like light coming through a keyhole, dozens of them, translucent and patient, and their leader was a girl no older than sixteen with riverweed in her hair and eyes the color of deep water over deep stone.

Ivetta. I knew the name from the oldest pages of the oldest ledger. Entry one. The first theft.

She drifted close enough that the cold of her settled into my bones, and she studied me the way an appraiser studies a coin suspected of being clipped.

"A warden," she said. Her voice was the sound a bell makes after it stops ringing. "Stitched with stolen goods."

"Were," I said. "I burned them out. It hurt more than I expected, which I suppose tells you something about what I was carrying."

"It tells us you carried them carelessly enough to feel the loss," she said. "That's not virtue, warden. That's inventory."

Behind her, one of the choir laughed — a small, bright, terrible sound, like ice cracking in spring. I liked them immediately, in the way you like people who refuse to let you lie to yourself. It's a rare service. Most folk charge extra for it.

"The child," Ivetta said, turning to Wendell. "Where is she?"

"Asleep," Wendell said. "Sable's watching her."

"She sleeps in a drowned city guarded by ghosts and a smuggler with a knife," Ivetta said, "and the guild master wants her body for collateral. Keeper — we need to talk about what your ledger can do before his moon goes dark."

* * *

We talked in the tavern's back room, which had once been a counting-house, which felt appropriate, because everything in Vell was eventually revealed to have been a counting-house all along.

Wendell spread the stolen ledger across a table furred with salt-bloom, and I looked at the book I had spent eleven years killing to protect, and felt — nothing. That's the strange part. You expect revelation to burn. Mostly it just sits there, damp and ordinary, like everything else.

"Explain it to me again," I said. "Slowly. I'm a warden; we're trained to hit things, not understand them."

"The wards were anchored by a debt," Wendell said. He had the tone of a man reciting something he'd repeated to himself so many times it had worn smooth. "Names taken from the drowned, centuries ago, held in trust — except there was never any trust. The guild refinanced it, generation after generation, rolling the payment forward, taking new names to cover the interest. The country stood because the debt stood. The stars went out because—" he paused, and his jaw tightened, "—because I helped carve the tokens. Because we all kept the books balanced instead of paid."

"And now?"

"Now the debt's called due. Fully. And the guild master doesn't intend to pay it. He intends to refinance it one last time — with living names. Names still attached to breathing people." Wendell's hand flattened on the page. "A living name isn't just a marker, Corvane. It's a whole life still accruing. One child's name, freely surrendered — or taken — would cover the principal for another century. Maybe two."

"He'd damn a six-year-old to buy the kingdom time," I said.

"He'd call it mercy," Wendell said. "That's the worst part. He'd mean it. He showed me a vision once — Vell restored, perfect, whole, everyone safe. All it costs is that nobody asks who paid. That's always been the price. We've just never had to look at the payer before."

I sat with that. Around us, the choir had gathered in the doorway and along the walls, silent, listening the way only the dead listen — completely, without impatience, because they have nothing left to spend time on.

"All right," I said finally. "So we run. Take the girl, take the boat, get out past the fog line before the dark of the moon."

"Run where?" Sable said from the doorway. She'd appeared the way she does, silently, arms crossed, one eyebrow already loaded. "The fog's eating the map faster than I can rewrite it. There's no outside anymore, warden. There's inside, and there's less inside every day."

"There has to be an outside."

"Why?" she asked, genuinely curious, which was worse than if she'd mocked me. "Because you need one? The world doesn't arrange itself around your exits, Corvane. Believe me. I've checked."

It was Wendell who answered, and when he spoke, his voice had changed — quieter, heavier, the voice of a man setting down something he'd been carrying alone.

"Because the ledger says so," he said. "Look."

He turned to the final pages of the volume — pages I had never seen, because wardens weren't permitted past the seal, because the seal existed precisely so that men like me would enforce a book we'd never read. The script there was older than the guild, older than the kingdom, written in a brown-black ink that I chose not to think too hard about.

"When the debt was first taken," Wendell said, "there was a provision. A release clause. If the ledger is ever paid honestly — every name returned to its owner, every entry closed, nothing rolled forward, nothing forged — then the anchor dissolves. And the dragon-fire that holds what's left of Vell together can be spent on one last working." He looked up at me. "A door. Out. Not back — forward. Somewhere the fog hasn't written yet. Under new stars."

The room was very quiet. Even the water dripping through the ceiling seemed embarrassed to interrupt.

"But it has to be paid," he said. "Honestly. Completely. A paid ledger opens the door. A forged one — a refinanced one, a child traded for a century — keeps Vell exactly as it is. Rotting politely. Dying by installments." His fist closed on the table edge. "Those are the options. There's no third page. I've looked."

"So the deadline isn't just the girl," I said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water. "The deadline is everything. Either we pay the whole debt before the guild master takes her — or he pays his version, and the door closes forever, and Vell gets another hundred years of borrowed time bought with a life that hasn't started yet."

"Now you're getting it," Sable said. "Welcome to the family. We cry on Thursdays."

* * *

I found Pell asleep in the loft above the tavern, wrapped in a sailcloth blanket, with Sable sitting beside her sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening. Guarding. Always guarding. On Sable's forearm, fresh ink: *Pell sleeps. Pell is real. Pell asked me today why the sea tastes like tears, and I told her salt, and she said those might be the same thing, and I wrote it down so I'd never lose it.*

"How is she?" I asked.

"Six," Sable said. "Which is its own kind of armor. She thinks the ghosts are shy." She looked up at me, and her eyes were hard and tired and honest. "You're going to tell them, aren't you. That you carried the order. That you rowed here fast enough that maybe the guild master's other teams are only days behind you."

"Yes."

"Good." She went back to the knife. "Tell Wendell first, though. He'll take it better from you than from the fog. And Corvane—" she didn't look up, "—when this is done, whatever 'done' turns out to mean, you and I are going to have a long conversation about every name you ever stitched into a warden. And you're going to remember all of them for me. Out loud. In order."

"That could take days."

"I've got ink," she said, "and you've got guilt. Between us we'll manage."

Downstairs, Wendell was standing at the window, looking out at the black water where the drowned city's spires broke the surface like ribs. The choir hung around him in the gloom, and Ivetta's cold attention rested on him like a hand.

I gave him the rest of it — the numbers, the timetables, the names of the Reclamationist companies mustering at the fog-line, the fact that the guild master himself would come, because he always came when the collateral was precious enough. Wendell listened without blinking. When I finished, he was quiet for a long moment.

"Dark of the moon," he said. "How long?"

"Nine days," I said. "Maybe eight. The fog's moving faster than their maps account for. Everyone's schedules are dissolving."

"Eight days to return several thousand stolen names to their owners," Wendell said. He laughed — a small, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man doing arithmetic with his own life. "At the rate I can read aloud, that's impossible."

"Then it's fortunate," said Ivetta, from the shadows, in her bell-after-ringing voice, "that you have a choir, keeper. The dead are very good at singing many names at once." Her eyes turned to me, and the temperature of the room dropped. "And it is fortunate that the warden knows the guild master's mind. Warden — you will tell us everything. Every route, every habit, every fear. Beginning with the one thing he loves."

I opened my mouth to say *he loves nothing*, and then I stopped, because that was the answer of a man who had served him for eleven years and never once been permitted to look at him plainly.

"He loves the ledger," I said slowly. "The original. The idea of it. He keeps a copy of the first page framed above his desk. He touches it before every signing." I felt something turn over in my chest — not hope exactly, but the place where hope would go if anyone were still issuing it. "He thinks the debt is his. He thinks he owns it."

"Oh," said Ivetta, softly, terribly, "he does not."

Outside, far off across the black water, something began to glow — faint, rhythmic, pulsing like a heartbeat under the waves. The dragon-fire, guttering. Counting down.

Eight days. Less, if the fog had opinions.

I went to find a barrel stave and my knife. Someone had to start writing all of this down, and as of four days ago, that someone was me.
