## 12. Reclamation

The bells of the drowned capital rang wrong.

They'd been ringing all evening, but I only noticed when the rhythm changed — the slow, mournful toll the city kept out of habit giving way to something quick and sharp, like a heart remembering it could stop. Corvane heard it first. Wardens always hear their own kind coming.

"Reclamationists," he said, standing in the doorway of the tavern with water dripping off him and his face gone the color of old candle wax. "Three companies. Maybe four. They're walking in over the shallows."

"Over the *shallows*," I repeated. The flooded city sat in forty feet of black water at its deepest, thirty at its edges. Nobody walked over that. Nobody except people stitched together with stolen names, who had never much bothered with what bodies were supposed to do.

"They don't swim," Corvane said. "The names hold them up."

I looked at Wendell, who was sitting by the fire with a name-token half-carved in his lap, and watched the color leave him the way tide leaves a flat.

"The child," he said.

Pell was asleep upstairs, which tells you everything about children born after the stars went out — the sky eats itself and they sleep right through it. We got her awake anyway, and she came down rubbing her eyes with her sleeves pulled over her hands, and she looked at all our faces and understood faster than any adult would have. Children do that. They haven't learned yet to hope a thing is smaller than it looks.

"They're coming for me," she said. Not a question.

"No," said Wendell, in the voice he used for lies he wanted to believe. "No, love, they're—"

"I'm not stupid." She said it without heat, the way you'd note the weather. "I'm the first entry in the new ledger. You said so."

He had said so. He'd carved her a token two nights past, her name freely given back to her, the first honest entry in three hundred years, and he'd said it plain: *you are where the debt turns around.* Which meant, to anyone with ledgers in their blood, that she was also where the debt could be strangled. Kill the first honest payment and every payment after stays impossible.

I put my hand on my knives and found, to my complete lack of surprise, that my hands were steady. Fear does that to me. It's the quiet after fear that shakes me.

"All right," I said. "Then we don't let them have her. Everyone agree? Good. Now somebody tell me how we fight an army that walks on water, because I've smuggled past navies, tariffs, one very determined customs goose, and I have never once fought the sea itself."

The choir answered.

* * *

You should understand something about the drowned dead before I tell you this next part: they are not sad. That's the lie everyone tells about ghosts, that they're grief given legs. The Drowned Choir was fury given harmony. Three centuries of having their names sold out from under them had refined them into something with edges, and when Ivetta rose through the tavern floor that night — pale as paper money, hair drifting like ink deciding where to go — she wasn't there to mourn with us.

She was there to conduct.

"They will come across the water," she said, and her voice did the thing it does, arriving inside your skull rather than your ears, "because the water has forgotten how to be deep. It forgot because they made it forget. But I remember." Her smile was the worst and best thing I saw that whole night. "And I am owed a great many things, and tonight I collect."

Here is what the fog is, if you've never lived in a country being unmade from its edges: it's memory with nowhere to go. Every village softened into mist, every resident faded mid-sentence, every road unwritten — it all goes into the fog. It doesn't vanish. It just stops belonging to anyone. And a fog full of unbelonged things, the choir knew, can be *given* somewhere.

Ivetta sang, and the other voices took it up, and the drowned capital exhaled.

I stood at a window and watched the sea around the city turn from water into something older and angrier. Shapes moved in it. Streets that no longer existed surfaced like whales. Faces — I won't describe the faces, I've decided against describing the faces, some things stay between me and my skin — drifted just below the surface, looking up at the Reclamationists' advance with the patience of creditors.

Corvane made a sound beside me. Not quite a word.

"You knew they could do this?" he asked.

"I suspected," I lied. "I'm a smuggler. Suspecting things is most of the job."

What I actually felt was awe, and under the awe, something colder: the understanding that we were standing next to a power that had been waiting three hundred years for permission, and permission had been granted by a bone-carver with soft hands, and powers like that do not stay leashed by gratitude forever. But that was a problem for a night that wasn't this one.

The first company of Reclamationists reached the old harbor steps at moonrise and the sea remembered how to be deep.

I'll give the guild this: they didn't scream much. Men stitched with forty stolen names each come apart differently than normal men — the names go first, peeling off them like wet paper, rising as small bright ghosts that the choir caught and held, and then whatever was underneath just... folded. Went back to being nobody. It's the only mercy in this whole rotten business, that the Reclamationists mostly stopped existing rather than died. Though I suppose someone loved whoever they'd been before the stitching. Someone, somewhere, is missing them without knowing why.

Two companies broke. The third didn't.

* * *

The third company came at the archive wing, because the guild master — and I want it on the record that I called this, I called it at supper, ask anyone — the guild master doesn't want Pell. Pell is a symptom. He wants the ledger, and the ledger lives where Wendell works, surrounded by six hundred years of exchange records, and the archive wing sits on the dry ridge above the harbor with exactly one gate worth defending.

Which is why I was standing on it.

Let me explain what I did, because afterward everyone kept looking at me like I'd done something noble, and nobility implies choice, and I want the choice understood properly. On my skin — arms, ribs, back, the inside of my left thigh where nobody asks — I have written everything the fog tried to erase. Every village, every face, every conversation the country forgot. I started at fourteen, when my home town went soft overnight and I woke up the only person in Vell who still remembered it existed. Ink and salt-water, renewed weekly. I am, in the most literal sense available, a walking archive. It's why Wendell trusted me. Two keepers of memory recognizing each other, he said. I thought it was romantic nonsense then. I believe it now, which is worse.

A gate holds if what stands on it cannot be forgotten. That's warden-craft, Corvane explained it while we ran — a threshold anchored in memory resists erasure, and the Reclamationists' whole art is erasure. A forgotten gate is just architecture. A *remembered* gate is a wall.

But the fog had eaten so much of Vell that my skin alone couldn't anchor anything that size. The gate needed more memory than I carried in ink.

It needed the memory in the ink itself. The written record. And a written record burns.

So here is the choice, laid out honestly: stand aside, let them take the archive, let them take the ledger, let them take Pell and refinance the debt against the living until every child in whatever Vell becomes is born already sold — or burn my life's work, page by page, tattoo by tattoo, feeding my own past into the gate to make it too heavy to move.

People talk about sacrifice like it's a big clean gesture. It isn't. It's kneeling on cold stone with a lamp in one hand and a knife in the other, cutting the ink out of your arm in strips because burning the skin alone wastes time, and reading each piece aloud as it burns because memory has to be *spent*, deliberately, named, or it just goes to fog like everything else. It is watching the map of Hallowmere go up — the town where I was born, which exists nowhere else now, nowhere, I checked, I checked for twenty years — and saying its streets out loud into the fire so the gate can hold them, and feeling the town leave me even as I give it away. You don't forget it all at once. You forget it the way the country did. Edges first. Then faces. Then why you ever cared.

I burned Hallowmere. I burned the customs goose, gods rest her vicious soul. I burned my mother's kitchen, my first run, the face of the boy who taught me letters, the smell of my own childhood, the reason I started keeping records at all — that one hurt worst, because once it was ash I couldn't remember why any of this mattered, only that I had decided it did, and decision without memory is a strange bare way to live.

The gate held.

The Reclamationists hit it again and again and it held, because a gate fed with true spent memory is heavier than anything the guild can forge, and behind me the choir sang the fog into blades and Corvane — traitor, warden, friend, I've stopped sorting those — fought like a man paying down his own debt, and Wendell stood in the archive door with a lamp of dragon-fire in his hand and tears running freely down his face, watching me spend myself, doing nothing, because there was nothing he could do and we both knew it and he stayed anyway, which is its own kind of holding a gate.

Dawn came gray and starless as always. They withdrew. The water gave back its silence. We counted ourselves and found us all present, more or less, though "present" had developed some new definitions where I was concerned.

* * *

We lost the archive wing anyway. I want that understood too, because victory reports are liars and I refuse to be one.

They couldn't take the gate, so at the very end — as the last company fell back into the shallows — they did the thing I should have predicted, being a smuggler, being a professional of exactly this kind of spite. They fired it. Dragon-fire, guild-issue, the hot stuff that doesn't care about stone or water or six hundred years of careful shelving. By the time we beat it back, the archive wing was a beautiful glowing skeleton, and the rain of ash was falling soft as snow onto the harbor, and Wendell stood in the doorway of a building that had raised him and made a sound I have only heard once since.

Not everything burned. Ledgers are built to survive their owners; that's the whole point of them. Wendell went in through smoke with his apron over his mouth and came out with an armload: the oldest volume, the debt-ledger itself, and perhaps forty others chosen blind in the dark. Forty books out of six hundred years. The rest is ash floating on the bay.

He laid them out in the tavern among the mugs and the bandages and counted them twice, and his hands shook, and Pell put hers over his, and nobody said the obvious thing, which is that the knowledge in those ashes might have told us how to end this instead of merely surviving it.

I sat by the fire with new blank patches on my arms — the skin heals smooth, empty, like pages torn out — trying to remember my mother's face and getting instead the fact that I had once known it. Corvane brought me tea. Ivetta drifted up through the floorboards and looked at me a long time with those ledger-dark eyes.

"You paid," she said. "Freely. No one made you."

"Don't make it holy," I said. "I did the arithmetic. I was worth less than the gate."

"You were worth more than the gate," she said, "and you spent yourself like you were worth less. That is the part that isn't arithmetic." And she began, softly, to sing — not the war-song, something older, and the other voices joined, and I realized with a jolt that they were singing *Hallowmere*. The streets of it. The bell. My mother's kitchen window facing east.

They had caught it as it burned. Everything I read aloud into the fire, the choir had gathered, and now they gave it back to me in fragments — not whole, never whole, memory spent doesn't refund — but enough. A street name. A face, blurred. The goose.

I cried into the tea, which I maintain was mostly steam.

Wendell sat down beside me eventually, ash still in his hair, the debt-ledger heavy on his knees. For a while neither of us spoke. Firelight, ghost-harmony, the smell of a library drowning.

"There's a gap," he said finally. "In the oldest volume. The binding's intact but the pages are cut out — cut recently, knife-work, careful. Someone removed entries before the book ever left the vault." He turned it toward me. The stubs of pages lined the gutter like teeth after a bad winter. "Sable. The archive held every record the guild kept of its own refinancing. Names bought, names resold, interest accrued in souls. If we're going to pay this debt honestly, we need to know its full shape. And someone didn't want us to know."

I looked at the cut pages, and then at Corvane across the room, talking low with Pell, wearing the face of a man who'd defected clean — and I thought about wardens, and what gets stitched into them, and what a clever guild master might send ahead of his armies, walking over the water, carrying nothing but himself.

"Well," I said. "Then we'd better find out who's been reading our book."

Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like a listener.
