## 13. What Sable Burned

I woke to the smell of tallow and dragon-fire and the distinct sensation of having been used as kindling.

That's not a figure of speech. I mean it the way you'd mean *I woke with a headache* or *I woke with sand in my boots* — a plain statement of fact about the state of my body. There were gaps in me. Not wounds; I've had wounds, and wounds announce themselves with blood and screaming and a certain honest drama. These were quieter. Patches of skin along my forearms, across my collarbone, down the left side of my ribs where the ink had been: gone. Smooth as river stone. And where the skin was gone, so was whatever I'd written there.

I lay on a pallet in the back room of the Drowned Gull — our tavern, our headquarters, our increasingly besieged home in a city that refused to forget itself — and I took inventory the way I always did, top to bottom, memory by memory, because that is what a woman does when her body is her archive.

The night of the Reclamationist assault on the drowned city was there. The fog singing through the streets like a blade drawn slow. Corvane shouting. Wendell running for the archive wing with the ledger clutched to his chest like a child. Me at the gate, deciding — no, not deciding, *knowing*, the way you know to breathe — that the gate would not hold unless it was fed.

So I fed it.

I burned my own written memories. Struck flint to my forearm and let the fire read what I'd spent thirty years tattooing into my skin, every name and date and route and face the fog had ever tried to take from Vell, and I gave them to the gate, and the gate held, and we won the night, and I woke up missing pieces of myself the way a book wakes up missing pages.

Here's what was still there:

My mother's face. My father's hands on a tiller. The first ship I ever stole, the *Kestrel's Regret*, and the harbor master whose ledger I forged to do it. The taste of stolen plums in Highsummer. The way Wendell Amberdale says *Sable* like he's testing whether the word will hold his weight. The whole shape of my life, more or less, from the outside.

Here's what wasn't:

Anything between my twelfth year and my nineteenth. Seven years, gone, a hole in the middle of me shaped exactly like a girl I could no longer remember being. I knew she'd existed. I could see the edges of her, the way you see the edges of a door that's been bricked over. But the girl herself — her name for things, her fears, whoever taught her to sail, whoever broke her heart first, whether anyone had ever been kind to her — smoke. All smoke.

And here's the thing that made me laugh, alone, in the dark, in a voice that came out wrong: I'd done this to other people. Not the burning. But I'd watched the fog take memories from villages, from sailors, from whole towns softening into nothing, and I'd always thought — smugly, safely, with ink under my fingernails — *not me. I write it down. I keep it. The fog can't have what I've already claimed.*

Turns out the fog doesn't care who claimed it first. Turns out fire doesn't either, once you invite it in.

* * *

Wendell found me an hour past dawn, sitting on the tavern's flooded steps with my feet in the water and my sleeves pushed up, staring at the blank stretches of my own arms like they might confess something if I glared hard enough.

"You're awake," he said, which is the sort of thing bone-carvers say instead of *I've been sitting outside your door all night listening for you to stop breathing.*

"Barely." I didn't look at him. "How bad?"

He sat down beside me. He'd brought the ledger. Of course he'd brought the ledger. That book went everywhere with him now, slept closer than a lover, and I suppose I couldn't complain, since half the entries in it were mine.

"Your burns are clean," he said. "Corvane salved them. The child asked after you."

"The child asks after everyone. It's her whole personality." I flexed my fingers. "Wendell. How much did I lose?"

He was quiet long enough that the water lapping the steps got loud. Then he opened the ledger to a ribbon-marked page and said, "Do you want the answer, or do you want me to read it to you?"

"What's the difference?"

"The answer is a number. Reading it to you is a person." He turned the book toward me, careful, the way he handled everything — tokens, names, other people's griefs. "You told me once that you started writing on your skin because paper burns and fog eats ink off anything else. You said your body was the only shelf nobody could raid." He tapped the page. "So I wrote you down too. Everything you ever told me. Every entry you inspired. I didn't know why, at the time. I just — it seemed like the kind of thing a keeper should do."

I looked at him then. He had the expression he got when he was about to do penance for a crime he hadn't committed yet: jaw set, eyes apologetic, hands steady.

"Read it," I said.

* * *

He read to me while the tide came in around our ankles, and I'm going to tell you what he read, because I need it to exist somewhere outside my head, and right now the only safe place in Vell is a dead man's ledger.

*"Entry four hundred eleven,"* he began. *"Told to me by Sable Quill, third night out from the forgotten forest. At twelve years old she was sold to a salvage crew working the shallows off Cael Harrow. Not sold by strangers — sold by her mother's brother, who owed a debt to the guild and paid it in flesh, which is how the guild liked its debts paid before it learned to prefer names. She dove wrecks for six years. She learned to hold her breath longer than any diver on the coast, and she learned that holding your breath is just another way of refusing to speak."*

"That one I remember," I said. My voice came out flat. "Diving. I remember the diving."

"Keep listening."

*"Entry four hundred nineteen. In her fifteenth year she found a wreck the guild had marked sealed — a guild ship, sunk deliberately, carrying name-tokens bound for the border wards. She brought up a chest of them. She read the names. She was the first living soul in two hundred years to read those names aloud without permission, and the choir heard her."*

I went cold. Colder than the water. "I never told you that."

"No," Wendell agreed. "But you told Ivetta, and Ivetta told me, and I cross-reference. Keeper's habit." He turned the page. "There's more."

*"Entry four hundred twenty-three. The guild caught her. They didn't kill her — killing a girl who reads names aloud makes martyrs, and martyrs sing. Instead they took the seven years. All of them. They sent a Reclamationist to peel the memory of the chest, the wreck, the reading, everything from her mind, and they returned her to the salvage crew as a warning. She remembered none of it. She only knew, afterward, that she couldn't stand the sound of her own voice underwater anymore, and she never knew why."*

The water lapped. Somewhere above us, in the drowned city, the choir was singing its low endless chord, and for once I wished it would shut up, because it sounded like pity.

"So that's the hole," I said. "That's what's been sitting in the middle of me my whole life. A girl who found the truth and got emptied for it."

"And then—" Wendell's voice did something complicated— "then this. Entry four hundred forty. Written the night before the stars went out, from your own telling. She escaped the salvage crew at nineteen. She stole a boat, a chart, and a set of needles, and she started writing everything down. Not because she remembered what was taken. Because she decided that whatever the guild erased next, it would have to erase twice. She made herself into a record the fog couldn't close." He looked up. "She named herself Quill that day. Her birth name is in the earlier entries, if you want it."

Did I want it?

I sat with that question while the tide climbed another step. Somewhere in me, a girl of twelve was being handed to her uncle. Somewhere in me, a girl of fifteen was opening a chest of stolen names with salt-stung fingers. Somewhere in me, a girl of nineteen was standing on a stolen deck, needle in hand, inking her first word into her own arm — and I could feel the shape of that moment even though I couldn't see it, the way you feel a scar's outline through a sleeve.

"No," I said finally. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. If I take the name back, I take the whole package — the uncle, the crew, the emptying. I'd be inheriting her." I pulled my knees up. "I didn't choose any of that. But I chose Quill. I remember choosing Quill. That part's mine."

Wendell nodded slowly, and I could see him turning it over against everything he believed — because his whole creed, his whole beautiful doomed project, was that names must go home, that every taken thing must be returned to its owner. And here I was, offered my own name back, refusing delivery.

"It's different," I said, before he could argue. "Don't give me the look. The drowned *want* their names. They were robbed. I was robbed too, but the robbery isn't the same, because—" I struggled for it, hands moving, the way they do when the words are deep. "Because their names were taken *from* them. Mine was taken *into* me. It's still in there, under the burn scars. It's not lost, Wendell. It's just not load-bearing anymore. I built a person on top of the gap. A good one. A smuggler with a map-skin and a foul mouth and a crew she'd die for. Why would I tear that down to recover the foundation?"

"Because foundations matter," he said quietly. "I spent twenty years not asking where the tokens came from. I told myself the ledgers balanced, and balance was enough. It wasn't. The gap underneath grew until it swallowed a kingdom."

"That's not the same thing."

"Isn't it? You're proposing to leave a debt unpaid because paying it would be uncomfortable."

I laughed — a real laugh, sharp and surprised, the first one since I'd woken. "Gods below, bone-carver. Did you practice that line?"

"I've been arguing with you in my head for three days. You're a formidable opponent." He closed the ledger on his finger, keeping the place. "But I'm not trying to win. I'm trying to ask the question honestly, because you deserve an honest question, not comfort. What do you do with a self that has a hole in it?"

* * *

The child found us at midday. She came down the steps barefoot, water streaming off her, hair full of weed, with the absolute confidence of someone who has never once considered that a flooded stairwell might be dangerous. Born after the stars went out. Nine years old. Named herself Wren, because she said the old names all felt like borrowed coats.

"Sable." She planted herself in front of me and stared at my arms with the frank horror only children and surgeons can manage. "Your pictures are gone."

"Some of them."

"All the boats." She counted on her fingers. "You had the *Kestrel's Regret* on your left arm, and the *Marrow Thief*, and the little one you wouldn't tell me the name of, and the map of the Sunken Roads from your elbow to your wrist, and the—" she faltered, and her face did something young and stricken— "and my birthday. You had my birthday written on your shoulder. You said the fog would never get it because you kept it where you could see it."

Oh.

Oh, hells.

I had forgotten that. Not forgotten — lost. Burned at the gate with everything else, given to the stones to keep the Reclamationists out, and I hadn't even known it was gone until a nine-year-old stood in front of me grieving it harder than I could.

"Wren." I reached out and she came to me, fierce, gripping my shoulders, and I held her and looked at Wendell over her head. He had the ledger open again. He was already writing.

"Tell me her birthday," he said. "Tell me everything you remember about giving it to her. I'll enter it. It'll live in the book now, and the book doesn't burn."

And that — that was the moment the whole argument resolved itself, or rather dissolved, the way fog dissolves when the sun finally gets serious. Because I looked at this man, this gentle, guilt-ridden, impossible man, recording a child's birthday in a ledger of stolen souls because a smuggler's skin had failed her, and I understood what I'd been circling all morning.

The record isn't the person. I'd thought it was, for thirty years. I'd thought if I just wrote enough, inked enough, archived enough, I'd be safe, permanent, un-losable. But the record is not the person. The record is what the person *chose*. Every entry in my skin was a decision: *this matters, I claim this, the fog can't have it.* And now the skin was blank in places, but the decisions remained. I had chosen Wren's birthday. I had chosen the roads, the boats, the names of dead ships. I had chosen, at nineteen, to become a thing the guild couldn't fully erase. Burning the entries didn't unmake the choices. It just meant I had to make them again.

Which is, I suppose, what being alive is. Making your choices again, every morning, on purpose.

"I remember," I told Wendell. "It was the fourth day of the Deepmonth, two years after the stars went out. She was born in the flooded quarter, in a boat, during a storm, and her mother sang to her the whole time because the choir was singing too and she wanted her daughter's first sounds to match." I swallowed. "She died eight months later. Fever. Wren doesn't remember her voice. I wrote it down for her — the song, all four verses — and I sang it to her every year on the day, and I had it on my shoulder, and now it's ash on a gate in a drowned city."

"Not anymore," Wendell said, and showed me the page. His handwriting, neat as prayer: the date, the storm, the boat, the mother, the song, all four verses, transcribed from my recitation while I spoke. "Now it lives in the ledger. And when this is over — when the debt is paid and the book closes — I'll copy it out fair for you. On paper. On stone. Wherever you want it. It shouldn't have to live on a person. People burn."

"People burn," I agreed. "But people also choose what rises after." I lifted Wren's chin. "Fourth day of Deepmonth, sprout. Say it back."

"Fourth day of Deepmonth," she said, solemn as a coronation. "Are you going to sing it tonight? The song?"

"If you want."

"I want." Then, with the merciless pivot of childhood: "Does it hurt? Your arms."

"Only when I think about it."

"Then don't think about it." She patted my shoulder — the blank one, kindly, as if comforting the absence directly — and ran back up the stairs, trailing water and weed, and I heard her bellowing for Corvane, and life in the Drowned Gull resumed its usual volume.

* * *

Evening found me back on the steps with Wendell, both of us watching the choir-light move through the upper windows of the drowned city like weather. My arms ached. My head ached worse. But something had settled in me, some internal argument concluded.

"I've decided," I said.

"Mm?"

"I'm not taking the name back. The one from the entries. The girl in the hole stays a girl in the hole." I turned my forearm over, studying the smooth blank stretch where the Sunken Roads used to run. "But I'm not leaving it blank either. Blank is what the guild wants. Blank is their whole business model." I held out my hand. "Give me a needle and ink. Tonight I start writing again — new entries, my entries, chosen ones. First line goes right here." I tapped the bare skin. "*Sable Quill, keeper by choice, burned at the gate and came back anyway.*"

Wendell passed me the needle without a word, but he was smiling, that small crooked smile he thinks he hides. Then he didn't hide it.

"You know," he said, "when this is finished — when the last name is read and the ledger closes — there may not be a Vell to keep records in. There may not be anything."

"Then I'll keep records in whatever comes next." I dipped the needle. "Somebody has to remember what we chose. Might as well be the woman who writes on herself."

I pressed the point to my skin and drew the first stroke, and it hurt, and I let it hurt, because pain is just the body's way of countersigning.

We were halfway through the second line when Corvane came down the stairs three at a time, warden-fast, face gray as drowned slate.

"You need to hear this," he said. "A bird just came in from the coast. The guild master isn't waiting for parley anymore. He's coming to the city himself — and he's bringing the refinance writs. Signed ones. With living names already entered." He paused, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "The first name on the list is Wren's."

The needle stopped in my hand.

Well, then. I looked down at my half-finished inscription — *burned at the gate and came back anyway* — and thought: apparently the sentence isn't done yet.

Neither am I.
