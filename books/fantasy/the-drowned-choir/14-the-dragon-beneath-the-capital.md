## 14. The Dragon Beneath the Capital

There is a particular kind of silence that lives at the bottom of a drowned city, and I want to be clear that it is not the silence of absence. It is the silence of a room full of people who have all agreed, without speaking, not to mention something.

We had been descending for two days.

The choir led us down through streets that had given up being streets — corridors of flooded masonry where eels moved like ink spilled in slow motion, past doorways whose lintels were carved with names of families who had probably once complained about the price of bread. The water grew warmer as we went deeper, which was wrong, and everyone knew it was wrong, and nobody said so, because the alternative explanations were worse.

Corvane walked at the rear with his hand never far from his warden's blade, though what he expected to fight down here I couldn't guess. The child — Pip, because she refused to answer to anything with more syllables — rode on Sable's shoulders and kept asking questions in the relentless way children do when they are actually asking *are we safe* over and over in different costumes.

"Why's it warm?"

"Dragon," Sable said.

"Why's there a dragon?"

"Because somebody had to keep the lights on."

"Is it a nice dragon?"

Sable considered this with the seriousness she gave to everything, including tide tables and the question of whether my hair was an insult or merely a tragedy. "It's an old one," she said finally. "Old things aren't nice. They're just *true*. It's different."

Ivetta drifted ahead of us all, her drowned light guttering green through the black water, and every so often she would stop, tilt her head as if listening to something just below hearing, and point. And we would go where she pointed, because when a dead girl who has been dead for three hundred years tells you she knows the way, you go.

I carried the ledger against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped under my coat. I had taken to sleeping with it. Not out of devotion — out of the specific paranoia of a man who has learned that institutions will burn books before they'll admit the books are right.

"You're doing the thing again," Sable said quietly, falling into step beside me.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you hold the book like it's going to be asked to dance."

"I'm holding it like it's worth more than my life, which is a fact I resent."

"Resentment keeps the arms strong." She glanced back at Pip, then forward into the dark. "Wendell. What happens if the fire's already out?"

I hadn't let myself think it. That's the honest accounting. A ledger-keeper learns early that you don't write down the number you're afraid of until you absolutely must, because ink makes things real and real things demand responses.

"Then Vell finishes dying," I said. "And everything we've done has been a very long walk to watch it happen."

"Good. As long as we're agreed." She said it dryly, but her hand found my shoulder for half a second — Sable Quill's entire vocabulary of comfort compressed into one brief pressure — and then she moved ahead to argue with Corvane about whether the tunnel branching left was a sewer or a temple, a debate that could only be settled by someone going first and finding out.

The choir sang us down.

I should describe what that was like, because I have tried since to describe it and failed, and failure deserves documentation too. It wasn't sound, exactly. It was more like the memory of sound, the way a stone remembers the shape of the river that wore it. The deeper we went, the more distinct the voices became, until I could pick out individuals: a fisherman humming a work-song about nets; an old woman singing a lullaby in a dialect dead before the guild was founded; a boy, no older than Pip, who only ever sang one rising phrase, over and over, like a question he'd been asking for centuries.

*What did you bring me?* the boy sang. *What did you bring me?*

"Patience," I whispered, and felt absurd, and did it anyway.

* * *

The last stair was carved from a single piece of black stone, and it went down further than stairs have any business going. At the bottom, the water simply stopped — not drained, not parted, just *ended*, a wall of dark sea standing vertical beside the air like a curtain someone forgot to take down. Beyond it lay a cavern the size of a cathedral's ambition, its ceiling lost in shadow, its floor ribbed with cooled lava that had flowed and hardened in patterns like frozen argument.

And in the center, on a plinth of fused glass-sand, burned the ember.

I say *burned*, but that word belongs to fires that consume. This was the opposite of consumption. It was a coal the size of a fist, glowing a deep patient red-gold, and around it the air bent and shimmered, and I could feel it in my teeth, in the fillings of my teeth, in the marrow behind them. Warmth rolled off it in slow tides. Every hearth in Vell, every forge, every candle — I understood suddenly, the way you understand a debt when the collector arrives — every flame in the kingdom was this one, lent out.

"It's so small," Pip said, disappointed, and then immediately contradicted herself by refusing to come within ten feet of it.

"That's not small," Corvane said. His voice had changed. I turned and saw the warden stripped away from him; he looked like a man standing in front of the altar of a god he'd stopped believing in years ago and discovering the god had noticed. "That's the First Ember. Wardens are taught it's a story. A founding myth. *The dragon that slept beneath the capital and warmed the stones.* We were taught it the way you teach children about grandmothers who died before they were born."

"And now?" Sable asked.

"Now I'm looking at it."

The dragon was harder to see, which sounds impossible, but true things often are. At first I took the vast shape coiled around the plinth for geology — ridges of black rock, folds of obsidian, a landscape that happened to rise and fall with a rhythm too slow to be breath. Then the rhythm hitched, and the whole cavern breathed with it, and I understood that I had been standing inside a lung.

One eye opened.

It opened the way a harbor opens — gradually, enormously, with the sense of something deciding to permit your presence rather than merely noticing it. The eye was the size of a cart wheel and the color of the ember, and it regarded us with an expression I can only call *bookkeeping*. Not anger. Not welcome. Assessment.

"Oh," said the dragon.

Its voice didn't come through the air so much as through the floor, the walls, the water-wall, my own skeleton. It was a voice that had been talking in its sleep for a thousand years and had gotten very good at not waking itself up.

"Oh," it said again, with something almost like amusement. "A ledger."

I looked down at my chest, where the oilcloth bundle sat. The warmth of the ember had soaked through my coat, and beneath my hands I could feel — I swear this — the ledger warming too, the vellum softening, the ink stirring faintly like silt in current.

"You can—" I began.

"I wrote the first page." The eye blinked, a landslide in miniature. "Or rather, I watched it be written, and fire remembers what it sees. Bring it here, bone-carver. You've made me listen to your footsteps for two days. Let me see what they were carrying."

I crossed the glassy floor. My legs performed their duties adequately, which I noted with distant gratitude. Up close, the heat was not painful but *insistent*, like a conversation you couldn't leave. I unwrapped the ledger and held it up, and the dragon drew one long breath through nostrils the size of cellar doors, and the pages lifted and fluttered though there was no wind.

"Yes," the dragon said softly. "Yes. There it is. The Guild of Rare Exchanges. I remember when they were six clerks in a rented room above a chandlery, arguing about whether memory could be collateral." The great head shifted, scales grinding like millstones. "They decided it could. I told them it couldn't. Nobody asks the furnace for accounting advice."

"You know what's in it," I said. Not a question. I was learning, slowly, how to speak to old things: state facts, offer nothing, wait.

"I know what's owed through it. Three hundred years of refinancing. Names borrowed against names borrowed against names, interest compounding in the dark while the wards stood and the stars shone and everyone agreed not to look at the arithmetic." The eye narrowed — a geological event. "And now the wards have fallen, and the stars are gone, and the bill has come due, and a bone-carver with ink-stained fingers has walked into my bedroom holding the account book." A pause, vast and dry. "You'll forgive me if I don't leap for joy. Leaping is beyond me, and joy is beyond the situation."

Behind me, the choir had gone quiet. All of them. Even the boy with his endless question. The silence they left was enormous, expectant, the silence of creditors assembling.

"The ghosts," I said. "The Drowned Choir. Their names were sold—"

"Their names were *taken* and *sold*, which is worse, and you know it's worse, which is why you said sold." The dragon's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "Don't soften your own confession to me, keeper. I'm older than flattery."

Heat crawled up my neck. Sable, at the edge of my vision, crossed her arms and watched me with the expression of a woman keeping score.

"They want their names back," I said. "I mean to give them. All of them. I've started reading the ledger aloud — every entry, every name, returned to its owner. But the fire is guttering. I can see it. And I don't understand enough to fix any of this. So I'm asking. What are the rules? What can actually be done?"

The dragon was silent for a long time. Long enough that Pip sat down on a ridge of cooled lava and started braiding a fringe of Sable's coat out of boredom, and long enough that I counted my own heartbeats to ninety-one before the answer came.

"One truth," the dragon said. "You asked for one truth, and truth is the only currency I deal in anymore, so attend carefully, because I will not repeat myself and I will not elaborate.

"A debt can be paid. Full value, honestly rendered, freely given. This closes the account and both parties walk away lighter. It is rare. It is beautiful. It usually costs the payer more than they expected, because honest accounting reveals honest totals.

"A debt can be transferred. One debtor takes up another's obligation — willingly or by trickery, and the trickery is always legal, which is why I despise law. Your guild has been transferring for three hundred years. Refinancing. Rolling the drowned into new instruments, new wards, new collateral. Each transfer made the debt bigger and the debtors farther from it. That is how your kingdom stayed warm: by ensuring no one ever stood close enough to feel the heat of what they owed.

"And a debt can be refused." The eye fixed on me, and the weight of it was physical, a hand pressing down on the crown of my skull. "Refusal is not forgiveness. Understand this. Forgiveness does not exist. It is a story debtors tell themselves so they can sleep. The ledger does not forgive. The fire does not forgive. When a debt is refused, it does not vanish — it *returns to its source*, with all accumulated interest, and the source must bear it. The guild refused the drowned for three hundred years. Every refusal went home to them, and they stacked it, and stacked it, and called the stack *stability*."

The ember flickered. Just once. Just slightly. But every person in that cavern saw it, and every ghost in the choir felt it, and somewhere far above us, in the fog-eaten country that had been Vell, I would have bet anything that another village softened another degree toward forgetting.

"So those are the options," the dragon finished. "Paid. Transferred. Refused. Choose among them, little keeper, and choose soon, because I have been burning myself to warm people who sold my warmth's price to strangers, and even my patience is a finite resource."

My mouth was dry. "And if none of the options are survivable?"

The dragon made a sound I initially took for shifting stone and then recognized, with a chill, as laughter.

"Then you have discovered the secret all honest accountants eventually learn," it said, settling its chin back onto its coils, the eye half-closing. "Survival was never one of the columns."

* * *

We made camp in the cavern's mouth, in the band of warmth the ember pushed out against the cold sea. Sable rigged a line for drying our soaked clothes. Corvane took first watch out of pure habit, sitting with his back to a pillar and his eyes on the stair. Pip fell asleep mid-sentence, which was the most merciful thing that had happened to anyone in weeks.

I sat apart with the ledger open on my knees, reading entries by ember-light, whispering names into the dark. A cooper from Harrow's Bend. Twin sisters who worked the eel-boats. A ferryman who had charged fair prices, according to the marginal note in some long-dead clerk's hand, *which is presumably why he was chosen.* With each name, a voice in the choir would lift, test itself, grow brighter — and then settle, waiting, because a name returned is not a debt paid. The name was theirs again, but the *taking* still stood on the books.

"Wendell." Sable lowered herself beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. She nodded at the ledger. "How many left?"

"Four hundred and sixty-two that I've found. Probably more hidden in the appendixes. The guild loved appendixes."

"And after?"

"After, there's one more entry. I told you."

"You told me there was one more entry. You didn't tell me whose." She said it lightly, the way she said dangerous things, watching my face instead of the page. "I can read, Wendell. I read everything. It's my whole personality. The last unpaid name in that book is written in the same hand as the first. A founder's hand. And the name attached to it is *Amberdale*."

The ember-light moved between us. Somewhere in the choir, the boy with the question began singing again, softly, patiently. *What did you bring me?*

"There's a version of tonight," I said, "where I lie to you, and it goes badly for both of us."

"I've met that version. I don't like him."

"Then: yes. My name is in the ledger. Has been since before I could walk. The guild didn't raise me out of charity, Sable. They raised their *collateral*. I'm not the keeper of the debt." I closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "I'm part of what's owed."

Sable was quiet for a moment. Then she reached out and, with two fingers, tilted my chin up so I had to look at her — Sable Quill, who had written her own history on her own skin so that no institution could ever take it from her.

"Then we pay it," she said. "Whatever it costs. That's the whole plan. It was always the whole plan. You just hadn't read far enough ahead to know you were in the book."

Before I could answer — before I could decide whether to laugh or weep, which with Sable were always adjacent — the temperature in the cavern dropped.

Not gradually. All at once, like a lid closing. The ember's warmth pulled back from the walls, and the water-wall at the cavern's mouth shivered, and Corvane was on his feet with his blade drawn before the echo of the cold had finished arriving.

The dragon's eye opened fully. Its nostrils flared, drawing in a long sample of the descending air, and the expression that crossed its ancient face was one I had seen before — on clerks, on collectors, on men reading the final line of a contract they thought they'd escaped.

"Visitors," the dragon rumbled. "Many. Armed. And carrying—" another breath, and its voice flattened into contempt so old it had fossilized, "—*paperwork*."

From the stair above, torchlight bloomed, dozens of flames burning the wrong color, and a voice called down into the dark, courteous and terrible, in the accent they teach you in the Guild of Rare Exchanges before they teach you anything else.

"Keeper Amberdale. We know you're down there. Come up, and bring the ledger, and perhaps we can discuss terms."

Beside me, Sable cracked her knuckles, one hand at a time, like punctuation.

"I hate terms," she said.
