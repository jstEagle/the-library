## 15. The Guild Master's Offer

The parley flag arrived at dawn, which was a lie of a word now, since there had been no proper dawn in weeks — only the fog thinning from charcoal to ash-grey while the drowned city's dragon-fire lit the water the color of old bruises. The flag itself was white silk embroidered with the Guild of Rare Exchanges' sigil: two hands exchanging a coin that had no face on either side. I'd stitched one of those banners myself, once, as an apprentice. I remembered pricking my thumb and being told that blood on guild silk was good luck.

I had believed so many things like that.

"They're asking for terms," Corvane said. He stood at the edge of the flooded plaza where our little company had made its camp, squinting at the messenger boat through eyes that had spent thirty years doing exactly this kind of work for the other side. "Safe conduct for one day. One negotiator each. No Reclamationists within a hundred paces of the fire-chamber."

"And you believe them?" Sable asked. Her arms were crossed over the faded script still healing across her forearms — the memories she'd burned to hold the gate, re-inked by Wendell-from-the-ledger in a hand that wasn't quite hers. She hadn't decided yet who she was when the record was all that remained. But her suspicion was fully intact, which was reassuring.

"No," Corvane said. "But I believe he'll come himself. That's what matters."

"Why?" the child, Pip, asked. She was sitting on a half-submerged balustrade with her feet in the water, which everyone had stopped telling her not to do, because the water here remembered being streets and mostly behaved about it.

"Because," I said, before Corvane could answer, "Master Halloway has never once in his life sent anyone to do something he could do more cruelly himself."

The choir stirred in the stones around us. They'd been quieter since the battle — grieving Ivetta, though none of us had said that aloud either, because saying it would make it true in a way none of us were ready to carry. But they murmured now, a low tide-sound under the plaza, and the nearest ghost — a fisherman whose name I'd read back to him three nights ago, Aldous, who had chosen to stay anyway — drifted up through the cobbles like smoke deciding to be a man.

*He brings a gift,* Aldous said. *The living always bring gifts when they come to apologize.*

"He isn't coming to apologize," I said.

*No,* Aldous agreed, and faded back down, satisfied.

* * *

Master Halloway came at noon, alone, in a skiff that cut the flooded avenue without a ripple, as if the water had been paid to look elsewhere. He was smaller than I remembered. That was the first betrayal of the day, and it wouldn't be the last. In my years at the guildhouse, Master Halloway had been a fixed point of the universe — the man who'd found me half-starved at nine years old carving animals out of chicken bones behind a butcher's shop, who'd said *you have clever hands, boy, we can use clever hands*, and meant, though I didn't know it then, *we can use clever hands to carve names off the dead.*

He stepped onto the plaza and looked at our camp — Sable sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen, Corvane standing very straight in his warden's coat with the stolen names unstitched from his seams, Pip watching from her balustrade with the unblinking attention of a child born after the stars went out, who had never learned that adults were supposed to be impressive.

"Amberdale," he said. "You've let yourself go."

"You've let everything go," I said. "The stars, the borders, most of the eastern provinces. We've all got our projects."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. He'd always liked that I talked back. It was, I understood now, part of why he'd kept me: a keeper who argued was a keeper who thought, and a keeper who thought was a keeper who could eventually be trusted with the whole terrible ledger. He'd been grooming me for this conversation for twenty years. Possibly longer. Possibly since the chicken bones.

"I'm here under parley," he said. "You have my word no hand will be laid on you or yours today."

"Your word," Sable said flatly.

"My word," he said, and to his credit — or whatever the guild equivalent of credit was; collateral, probably — he didn't flinch under her stare. Sable Quill had skin covered in the handwriting of everything the world forgot, and people tended to flinch. "May I sit? Standing negotiations go badly. Everyone gets rhetorical."

There was a drowned tavern nearby whose roof poked above the waterline like a whale's back, and someone — Corvane, I suspected, who was quietly domestic in ways that embarrassed him — had rigged a table on it from salvage. We sat there. Halloway produced a flask of plum brandy, the expensive kind, the kind the guild reserved for closing deals, and poured four cups, and looked pointedly at Pip until Corvane moved her out of earshot, which took some argument, and even then she sat close enough to hear everything, which I suspect Halloway counted on.

"To honest dealing," he said, and drank.

"Let's skip to the dishonest part," I said. "It saves time and I know it's coming."

"Wendell." He set down his cup. And here is the thing I have never managed to explain properly to Sable, who asks sometimes, in the dark, why I ever hesitated: when Master Halloway said my name, twenty years of belonging came with it. Warm bread in the guild kitchen. The smell of bone dust and lamp oil. Being told, every night of my apprenticeship, *good work, Amberdale*, by the only person who had ever said anything of the kind. He had built me the way I built tokens — carefully, from material that didn't know what it was for.

"The wards are failing," he said. "You've seen it. Vell is being unmade from the edges inward, and in perhaps a season there will be nothing left to save. What I offer is simple. Give me the ledger. Let the guild refinance the debt — we have done it before, quietly, competently, for four hundred years—"

"By selling the dead their own names back and pocketing the difference," Sable said.

"—and in exchange," he went on, without so much as a blink, "Vell stands. The wards anchor. The stars relight. Every village that dissolved last month comes back. Every person the fog ate remembers their mother's face again. You have seen what forgetting does, Amberdale. You walked through Marrowfield."

I had. I still dreamed about it. A woman fading mid-sentence, reaching for a kettle that was becoming an idea of a kettle. A boy calling a name after a dog that was no longer anywhere, including in the language.

"It works," Halloway said softly. "That's the thing no one wants to say out loud. It *works*. Four hundred years of peace, bought and paid for. The drowned don't suffer, Wendell. They're past suffering. Names are only worth something to the living, and the living are worth everything. That's not cruelty. That's arithmetic."

Under the table, in the stone, the choir was silent. Not absent — I could feel them, hundreds deep, listening — but silent, the way a courtroom is silent. They wanted to hear what I'd say. Or maybe they wanted to hear what I'd almost say.

"And the refinancing," I said. "Corvane tells me your new scheme uses the living. Starting with a child."

Halloway's face did something very small and very sad, an expression I recognized from a hundred ledger audits: the look of a man regretting a necessary expense. "Collateral has to be real," he said. "A debt of names can't be secured against abstractions anymore. The old stock is spent." He glanced toward Pip, and away, quickly, as if the glance itself cost him. "One child, Amberdale. Anchored against one child's name, held in trust, released when the country stabilizes. She'd live a whole life. She'd never know."

"She'd never know," I repeated.

"That's what trust means."

"That's what *theft* means," said Sable, "with better stationery."

* * *

He must have known words weren't going to be enough. I think he'd known it before he ever launched the skiff. So after the brandy, when the light through the fog had gone the color of pewter, he reached into his coat and brought out a token.

Bone-carved. My work. I knew it the way you know your own signature — the grain of the femur I'd split, the fine chisel-work along the rim, the name scored inside in the cipher only guild keepers could read. Except this token was blank. Unscored. Polished smooth as an egg.

"Do you remember," Halloway said, "your room at the guildhouse?"

And he turned the token over in his fingers, and the world changed.

* * *

Here is what I saw:

My room. Third floor, east window, the one that caught morning light because the guildhouse faced the harbor and the harbor faced east and the stars — the *stars*, all of them, the whole spilled salt-cellar of them — were wheeling overhead in their ancient, lawful patterns. My cot with the blanket worn soft. My tools racked in order of size. On the desk, the ledger I'd kept for eleven years, closed, square, finished. Downstairs, the smell of bread. Someone laughing in the yard — Joss, it was Joss, big stupid loyal Joss who'd taught me dice and never once beaten me at them honestly. And beyond the window, Vell entire: the capital dry and golden on its hill, the river running silver, the border beacons burning, the kingdom whole from edge to edge, humming like a struck bell, *safe*.

And I was in it. That was the unbearable part. I was in the room, and I had a name, and the name fit me like my own hands, and somewhere below a voice called *Amberdale! Bread's up!* and I felt myself turn toward the door—

"Halloway." My voice came out wrong. Thin. "Stop it."

"Why?" he said, from very far away, or very close. "Look at it. Really look. This is what you're trading away, Wendell. This exact morning. It exists. It's *owed* to you. All I ask is that the dead stay dead and quiet, the way the dead have always been, everywhere, since the beginning of—"

"*Stop it.*"

The choir tore the vision open. I felt them do it — a chord struck through the stone and the water and my own ribs, dissonant and furious and grieving, and the golden morning ripped like wet paper, and I was back on the tavern roof with brandy in my cup and tears on my face and Master Halloway looking at me with something that was almost tenderness.

"There it is," he said gently. "That's the look. Every keeper wears it once. Grief for a thing that never stopped existing — that's the cruelest trade there is, Wendell. I'm offering to give it back to you. All of it. Your name, your room, your friend Joss, your stars. And all it costs—" he spread his hands, reasonable, ruined, magnificent in his reasonableness— "is that a few thousand people who are already dead remain unthanked. Unnamed. Unpaid. They won't feel it. I promise you that. The dead feel nothing. That's the entire point of being dead."

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the fog to move twice.

Then, from beneath the table, from the stone, from the drowned street below and the drowned streets below that, the choir began — not loudly. Just one voice at first. A girl's voice, thin as thread.

Ivetta's voice. Or the shape her voice left behind, the way a bell leaves its ringing in the air. She had unraveled herself shielding Pip, and what remained of her was less than a ghost and more than a memory, and she sang the way rain sings on a roof: without asking permission, without needing an audience, just because falling is what rain does.

She was singing a name. Her own. The one I'd read back to her in a lantern-lit cellar a lifetime ago, the first act of justice anyone had offered her, the one word in the world that had belonged to her and been sold.

Other voices joined. Aldous. The fisher-wives. The ferryman. The child-drowned and the old-drowned and the just-plain-drowned, thousands of them, each singing their own name, quietly, in rounds, a fugue of ownership — *mine, mine, mine, mine* — not angry. Worse than angry. *Certain.*

Halloway heard it too. I watched him hear it. And I saw — this is the thing I want written down somewhere, in some honest ledger — I saw that it frightened him. Not the ghosts. The *certainty*. For four hundred years the guild had told itself the dead feel nothing, and here were the dead, feeling, in harmony, and the arithmetic cracked right down the middle.

"You'd throw away a kingdom," he said, "for a song."

"I'd throw away a *lie*," I said, "for the truth. They're not the same kingdom. Only one of them deserves stars."

He stood. He gathered his flask, his cup, his blank token — and paused with the token in his palm, and for one naked moment Master Halloway looked tired, tired the way only men who have carried a secret for four centuries get to be tired, and he said, quietly, not as a guild master but as the man who had once fed a bone-carving orphan his first hot meal:

"I raised you, Wendell."

"I know," I said. "That's what makes it a debt too. And I intend to pay it honestly. Just not the way you'd collect it."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he dropped the blank token into my cup of brandy, where it sank and lay gleaming at the bottom like an egg in a nest.

"Parley ends at sundown," he said. "After that, I take the child, the ledger, and the fire-chamber, in whatever order costs least. You have my word on that, too."

The skiff took him away without a ripple. The water had been paid to look elsewhere.

* * *

We sat on the tavern roof a long time after. Sable refilled the cups. Corvane came back with Pip asleep against his shoulder, which he permitted, stiffly, the way a wall permits ivy. Below us the choir's round faded to a single voice, then to the sound the sea makes when it's thinking.

"He showed you the room," Sable said. Not a question. She'd seen my face during it; her whole trade was reading faces and writing down what they hid.

"He showed me the room."

"And?"

I turned the blank token over in my fingers. Smooth. Unsung. Waiting for a name — any name. His gift, or his trap, or both; with the guild those had always been the same object.

"When I was nine," I said, "he gave me supper and a bed and told me my hands were clever. Everything I am was built in that house. If he'd asked me this a year ago, I'd have handed him the ledger and thanked him for the chance."

Sable waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the ten thousand things written on her.

"But he didn't ask a year ago," I said. "He asked *now*. After Marrowfield. After the choir sang me their names. After Ivetta—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "He asked me to choose between a perfect yesterday and a few thousand truths. And the horrible thing, Sable, the thing I keep turning over like a stone, is that I *want* the yesterday. Some cowardly little piece of me wants it so badly I can taste the bread."

"Of course you do," she said. "That's what makes it a temptation and not a menu."

I laughed, which hurt, which was how I knew it was real laughter.

"So we refuse him," Corvane said. "Obviously. We refuse, we fortify, we—"

"No," I said.

They looked at me.

"We don't refuse him. And we don't accept him. Because I've spent my whole life letting one man decide things in a room, and look what it bought." I stood up, and the choir rose with me, faintly, like dust in light. "This choice unmakes a country. Either way. So it doesn't belong to me, and it doesn't belong to him. It belongs to everyone it touches — the living *and* the dead. So tomorrow morning, we put it to a vote. Warden. Smuggler. Child. Choir. Everyone speaks. Everyone counts."

Sable smiled slowly, and began rolling up her sleeves, where there was, I noticed, fresh blank skin waiting to be written on.

"Well," she said. "Somebody find me ink. History's about to happen, and I intend to keep the record."

Below us, in the tide, the choir began to harmonize.
