## 16. The Vote of the Living

The guild master's offer sat in the room like a corpse at a wedding.

He had left it there himself, or rather he had left the *shape* of it — a vision still hanging in the air above the drowned tavern's long table, shimmering faintly where his magic had been. I could see it if I squinted: Vell as it was. The guildhouse with its brass fittings and its smell of ink and tallow. My workshop, my bench, my bone-knives in their oiled roll. A version of me behind the counter, content, unremarkable, *unnamed by no one*, because in that vision I had never learned anything worth losing sleep over.

The stars were relit in it. All of them. It was very beautiful and it was a lie stitched over a mass grave, and the man who'd shown it to us had bowed and said, *Take until the tide turns,* which is the sort of thing powerful men say when they believe the answer is already bought.

The tide was turning now. I could hear it against the drowned windows, patient as everything else in this city.

I looked around the table at the people who had, through no fault of their own and considerable fault of mine, become the only family I had. Corvane, the defected warden, sitting like a man who expected the chair to arrest him. Pip, the child born under no stars, swinging legs that didn't reach the floor. Sable Quill, arms crossed over the faded scars where her written memories used to live. And everywhere and nowhere, the choir — the Drowned Choir, filling the tavern like water fills a hull, waiting to see what the living would do.

I had spent my whole life believing that ledgers were balanced by the person who kept them. That was the first lie the guild ever taught me. So before I answered a man who thought my soul came with a price column, I did something no guild master would ever authorize.

I put it to a vote.

* * *

**Corvane**

Corvane spoke first, because Corvane always spoke first when speaking was dangerous. It was, I think, a discipline. A way of paying for his years in the Reclamationists one confession at a time.

"I wore stolen names for eleven years," he said. His hands lay flat on the table, palms down, as though holding something in place. "You know what they feel like? Like wearing a coat made of other people's skin. Warm. Fitted. You stop noticing after a while." He looked up. "That's what the guild master is offering all of you. A coat. And he's asking you not to notice whose it is."

"The wards held for four hundred years," I said, because someone had to say the devil's line so it could be properly killed.

"They held," Corvane agreed, "the way a dam holds. And every year the guild went down to the reservoir of the dead and quietly took a little more water. Refinanced. Rolled the debt forward. Do you know how many names are in that ledger, Amberdale?"

"Nine thousand, two hundred and six."

"And every one of them a person who drowned, whose name was pulled off their ghost like a boot off a foot, and sold to hold up the sky." He shook his head slowly. "I helped stitch those names into wardens. I felt them scream. We called it *attunement*. There's a word for what we actually did, but the guild retired it along with everyone who remembered it."

He turned to face the shimmering vision of old Vell hanging in the air — the perfect streets, the perfect stars, the perfect me behind my perfect counter.

"He showed you a country," Corvane said. "I'll tell you what I see. I see a coat. And I have spent two years learning to be cold instead."

* * *

**Pip**

Pip was nine years old and had been born three months after the stars went out, which meant she had never seen one, which meant — as she informed us regularly — that everyone else was being sentimental about a bunch of dots.

But she climbed onto her chair anyway, because Pip never missed an opportunity to stand on furniture, and looked at the vision of restored Vell with the flat, unimpressed stare of a child evaluating a vegetable.

"There's stars in it," she said.

"Yes," I said.

"But they're *his* stars. They're the ones from before." She frowned at it. "They're secondhand."

Sable made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"My mum used to talk about them," Pip went on, quieter now, her legs going still. "She said when the stars come back, everything goes back too. The fog goes away. The forgetting stops." She looked at me. "Is that true? If we take his offer?"

I thought about lying to her. I want that recorded somewhere: I thought about it, briefly, the way you think about anything easy.

"No," I said. "The fog is the debt coming due. Taking his offer means the debt stays unpaid. It just gets hidden again. Your mother would still forget things. People would still fade. It would just happen politely, indoors, where nobody has to watch."

Pip nodded slowly, the way children nod when adults finally tell them the truth and it's worse than the lie but at least it has edges.

"So the stars wouldn't really come back," she said. "Just pictures of stars."

"Just pictures of stars."

"Then no," Pip said, and hopped down off the chair, and added, with the terrible economy of nine-year-olds: "Also he smells like the guildhouse and the guildhouse smelled like lying."

* * *

**Sable**

Sable took longest. I think part of her wanted to refuse instantly, loudly, and with profanity — that was the Sable of the maps and the smuggler's run and the gate she'd burned herself to hold. But the Sable sitting here had gaps in her. Whole years of her own handwriting gone from her skin, read back to her from my ledger entries like a stranger's biography she happened to be starring in. She knew better than anyone at this table what it cost to lose your record of yourself.

"You know what he's actually offering me?" she said at last. She wasn't looking at the vision. She was looking at her own forearm, at the smooth blank stretches where the memories used to be inked. "A world where nothing changes. Where I'm exactly who I was before I met any of you. Before the choir. Before—" she gestured vaguely at me, "—before *this whole disaster of a man*."

"That does sound restful," I offered.

"Shut up, Wendell." But her mouth twitched. "Here's the thing about the person I was before. She was a smuggler because she couldn't be anything else. Everything legal belonged to people who could pay for it. Every road, every name, every star. She ran contraband through the cracks because the cracks were the only unowned ground left in Vell." She finally looked up at the shimmering image of the old kingdom. "And he wants to seal the cracks. Restore the walls. Put everything back where it belongs, in its ledger, under its owner."

She stood. She was not tall, but she had a way of standing that made rooms rearrange themselves around her.

"I lived in the cracks," she said. "The cracks are where I found the only people who ever wrote me into their story without charging admission." Her voice roughened. "So no. No parley, no restoration, no secondhand stars. If Vell comes back, let it come back different. Let it come back owing nothing. Because I have been erased once already this month, and I will burn whatever's left of me before I help do it to nine thousand ghosts."

* * *

**The Choir**

And then it was the dead's turn, which is not a sentence I ever expected to write, and less expected to mean.

They didn't speak the way the living speak. The choir's answer came as the room filled — truly filled — with them, faces surfacing in the lamplight like reflections rising through dark water. Ivetta came last, as she always did, a girl of perhaps fourteen with riverweed in her hair and four hundred years of patience in her eyes.

"Wendell keeper," she said. Her voice was the sound a bell makes underwater. "Read us the offer. In the ledger's words. Not his."

I understood. The guild master had dressed his proposal in silk — *restoration, continuity, mercy*. But I had the oldest volume open on the table between us, and the ledger had its own grammar, its own merciless bookkeeping. I turned to the page the vision corresponded to and read it as it was actually written:

*"Proposal of refinance, final instrument: the debt of 9,206 names shall be renewed in perpetuity, secured against the living population of Vell, interest payable in memory, principal payable never. Signed under parley."*

The tavern got very quiet. Even the sea against the windows seemed to lean in.

"You see," Ivetta said gently, "he offers to keep us. Not to free us. To keep us, the way a miser keeps coins — for their weight, not their face." She drifted closer, and where she passed, the lamplight bent toward her, hungry for her. "We were singers. Did the guild ever know that? Before we drowned, we had voices. Names. Songs nobody wrote down because songs belong to everybody." She looked around the table, at each living face in turn. "He asks you to choose the cage because the cage is gilded and familiar. But we have been in the cage four hundred years, and we are telling you from inside it: *no.*"

Her voice rose then, and the choir rose with her, dozens of drowned throats finding one chord, and the chord was not anger. It was something worse and better than anger. It was certainty.

"No country built on unpaid names deserves its stars," Ivetta said. "Let it end honestly. Let us go home singing. And let whatever grows after grow in soil that owes nothing to anyone."

* * *

The vote, when I finally called it, took less time than reading a single ledger entry.

"All in favor of refusing the guild master's offer — of letting Vell end, and the debt be paid in full, and the door be opened, whatever's on the other side of it?"

Corvane raised his hand like a man signing a confession. Pip raised both of hers, because Pip did nothing by halves. Sable raised hers and then, deliberately, reached over and raised mine with it, which I suppose made it unanimous and also constituted the most intimate thing she'd done since burning her maps.

And the choir — the choir simply sang *yes*, in a chord that rattled the drowned windows and sent the lamplight dancing and made the vision of old Vell flicker like a candle in wind.

"Then it's decided," I said. My voice cracked on *decided*, and I let it. "Outside any law. Outside any guild. By vote of the living and the dead."

The vision collapsed. All that remained of the guild master's beautiful lie was a smell of brass polish and a faint chill, like a door closing somewhere far away.

Pip looked at me. "What happens now?"

"Now," I said, "he stops asking."

Because that was the thing about men like him, and I had worked for one long enough to know. A parley is only ever a stall. Somewhere above us, in the fog-choked ruins of the capital, the guild master was already counting soldiers, and stitching names, and deciding that if the living would not sell the dead, he would simply take the ledger by force.

The tide finished turning. And far overhead, through fathoms of grey water, the place where the stars should have been stayed black — but tonight, for the first time, it felt less like a sky missing its lights and more like a stage waiting for new ones.

We had voted. Now we would have to hold the theater.

* * *

It began, as these things do, with a sound none of us wanted to hear: boots. Many boots, on stone, descending through the flooded upper city — and beneath them, faint and wrong, the sound of names being sung by mouths that had no right to know them.

Reclamationists. A full company of them.

Sable was on her feet before the echo died. "How long do we have?"

Corvane's face had gone the grey of old paper. "If they're already in the archive wing — minutes."

I closed the ledger and held it against my chest, nine thousand two hundred and six names and one of mine, and felt the choir gather close around us like a tide deciding which shore it loved.

"Then we'd better get to the ember," I said, "before he does."
