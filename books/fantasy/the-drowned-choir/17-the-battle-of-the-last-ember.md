## 17. The Battle of the Last Ember

I have been a Reclamationist for eleven years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things about myself: that I am good at violence, that I hate the men who taught it to me, and that my name — the real one, the one stitched into my collar by the Guild's own needle — is not mine to say aloud.

Tonight, all three of those facts came due at once.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes do, with someone being polite.

The guild master came down into the drowned city under a parley banner — white silk, embroidered with the scales of the Guild of Rare Exchanges, spotless despite the fog that had eaten everything else in Vell down to its bones. He walked through streets where the water stood knee-deep and did not splash. That was the first wrong thing. The second was that the fog parted for him. It didn't flee; it *deferred*, the way a dog defers to the man who holds its leash.

Wendell met him on the steps of the flooded basilica, and I stood behind them both with Sable and the child and about four hundred ghosts pretending to be architecture, and I listened to the guild master offer Wendell Amberdale his whole life back like a man offering a beggar a coin he'd stolen from him in the first place.

I won't repeat the offer. You've heard versions of it your entire life. *Come back. Nothing has to change. We'll fix the stars, we'll fix the wards, we'll pretend the drowned were never people at all.* The only novel part was the vision he conjured above the water: Wendell's workshop rendered in perfect miniature, the bone-dust in the lamplight, the kettle just off the boil, a younger Wendell carving tokens with clean hands and an untroubled heart.

It was beautiful. That's what made it obscene.

And then Wendell said no — said it quietly, the way he does everything, as though refusing to damn a kingdom were the same as declining a second cup of tea — and the guild master smiled, and I felt the parley die between them like a snuffed candle, and I knew what came next because I had been the thing that came next for eleven years.

"Corvane," the guild master said, without looking at me. "You're out of position."

He wasn't wrong. I was standing on the wrong side of history, which from his perspective is the same thing.

* * *

The fire-chamber lies beneath the capital, at the end of a stair that the dragon-fire keeps warm the way a body keeps a bed warm after the sleeper has gone. The last ember sits in a bowl of black stone the size of a cathedral font, and around it, carved into the floor, is the ledger-altar — a ring of names older than the kingdom, older than the debt, maybe older than language. When the choir sings down there, the flame leans toward them like a listener.

The guild master brought sixty Reclamationists down that stair.

I know because I counted them, and because I had spent the previous day teaching Sable Quill every weakness stitched into their design. Wardens are made, you understand. Not born. A Reclamationist carries stolen names sewn into his skin — thirty, forty, sometimes fifty — and each name is a hook, and each hook can be pulled. The Guild calls this discipline. I call it what it is: we are puppets who volunteered, because the alternative was being a puppet who didn't.

"Sixty," Sable said, when I told her. She was rolling up her sleeves, which meant she was preparing to burn her own memories again, and my chest did something complicated and unwelcome. "You're sure?"

"I trained most of them."

"That's either very good or very bad."

"It's both," I said. "That's the job."

Wendell was at the altar already, the ledger open on the black stone, his bone-carver's hands flat against the page like a man warming himself at a fire. He looked up at us — at all of us, the smuggler with ink scabbing her forearms, the traitor warden, the child named Pip who had never seen a star and refused to be impressed by anything else either, and the dead, hundreds of them, standing in the water with their lantern-light faces turned toward the ember.

"I'll need time," he said. "There are nine thousand names. Reading them properly — giving each one back the way it deserves — it takes what it takes."

"How long?" Sable asked.

"All night," Wendell said. "Maybe longer. The fire doesn't have longer." He said it the way he says everything, gently, as if apologizing to arithmetic itself.

"So we hold the stair," I said.

"So we hold the stair," Sable agreed, and grinned at me with all the warmth of a drawn knife, and I understood in that moment why the fog had never managed to make her forget anything: some people are simply too stubborn to be erased.

* * *

They came at moonrise, except there was no moonrise, because there had been no moon since the stars went out, so they came at the hour when moonrise used to be, which is somehow worse.

The Reclamationists descended the great stair in file, torches burning a green I recognized — ward-fire, fed on names, the same fuel that had held the borders of Vell for six hundred years. Their leader was Warden Hesk, who had been my partner once, back when I still believed the Guild reclaimed things instead of people. His face hadn't changed. That was the horror of it. Eleven years, and the Guild had kept him exactly as it wanted him, the way you keep a tool oiled.

"Corvane." His voice carried down the stair, flat as slate. "Return to the fold. Your names miss you."

"My names," I said, "can rot."

I felt them try, anyway. Every Reclamationist carries a pull-cord of sorts — a word of command keyed to the names in another warden's skin — and when Hesk spoke mine, thirty-two stitches in my left arm lit up like a net of white-hot wire, and every stolen soul in me turned toward the stair and *wanted* to go home.

Here is what the Guild never understood about the things it makes: it stitches names into us to control us, but names are not leashes. Names are people. And people can be reasoned with.

*I know,* I told them, inward, the way I'd learned to speak to the chorus in my own flesh. *I know you want to rest. But home isn't up those stairs. Home is at that altar, and the man reading the book is going to give you back. Hold on. Hold the line with me. One more hour of being owned, and then you're free.*

The wire in my arm dimmed. Somewhere inside my skin, thirty-two dead people chose me over the Guild, and I have never in my life been given a gift half so large.

"Hold the stair!" Sable shouted, and the choir answered.

* * *

I will not pretend I can describe what happened next in any order a sensible person would recognize. War in a drowned cathedral, fought between fog and fire and song, does not arrange itself into tidy passages.

The choir sang, and the fog — the great forgetting fog that had been eating Vell from its edges — came down the stair like a tide answering a bell. Where it touched a Reclamationist, the stolen names in his skin began to *leave*, unraveling stitch by stitch, and a warden who loses his names loses everything the Guild built him out of. Some of them collapsed into confused, ordinary men. Some of them simply stopped, mid-stride, forgotten even by themselves.

The guild master countered with fire. Green ward-fire, hurled in sheets, and where it struck the ghost-choir it burned — not flesh, they had none, but *presence*, burning holes in the dead the way acid burns cloth. I watched a woman who had been singing for two hours straight take a lash of it across the shoulders and come apart into embers and silence, and I heard Sable scream something profane and magnificent and charge the source.

Sable fights the way she remembers: completely, and at terrible cost. She had written her battle-plans on her arms that morning — actual plans, routes and timings inked between old scars — and when the ink ran out of room she started writing new ones on the backs of her hands, and when the fire took her maps she burned older entries instead, feeding her own past into the gate-spell that kept the guild's reinforcements from flanking us through the water-gates. I saw her hesitate over one line of script — a name, a date, something soft — and then press her palm to the stone and let it go, and whatever memory it was, it went out of her face as it went out of the world, and she kept fighting anyway.

That is love, if you want my definition. Love is burning something you cannot replace so that someone else can keep something they cannot survive losing.

Pip was supposed to be behind us, in the alcove, being small and safe. Pip had other ideas, because Pip was eight years old and had been born into an apocalypse and considered danger a form of weather. The child darted along the wall toward the altar — toward Wendell — carrying the water-flask of dragon-ember oil that kept the last flame fed, and a Reclamationist broke from the line to intercept, and I moved to stop him, and I was too far, too slow, eleven years of training failing me at the exact moment it mattered —

— and Ivetta got there first.

She had been at the front of the choir all night, the dead girl with the river-stone eyes, the one whose name Wendell had read aloud first and whose verdict had rebuilt our whole ragged purpose out of shame. She crossed the chamber the way grief crosses a room — instantly, and through everything — and she put herself between the warden's blade and the child, and she spread her arms, and she sang.

Not a battle-song. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger survives us. In her last moment, facing a blade forged to erase the dead, Ivetta of the Drowned Choir sang a *lullaby*. I knew it. Every child in Vell knew it. It's about a boat coming home.

The blade went through her and unraveled her, and she did not scream, because the dead are done with screaming; she came apart the way a chord comes apart when the singer runs out of breath — note by note, reluctantly, each one holding on as long as it could — and the last thing she did with what remained of herself was push Pip clear with hands made mostly of light, and look across the chamber at Wendell, and mouth two words.

*Finish it.*

Then the girl who had demanded justice for nine thousand strangers spent the last coin of herself buying ten seconds for one living child, and the fog took her shape, and the choir's song broke into a thousand voices crying out at once — and I learned that a choir in mourning sounds exactly like a choir in fury, and that the difference does not matter to the people standing in front of it.

What the choir did to the guild's line after that, I will not describe in detail. The fog has ways of making men forget themselves, and the dead had ways of letting it.

* * *

Hesk found me at the foot of the stair, near the end, when the battle had degraded into single combats and the green fires were guttering. He had lost his helmet. Underneath, his face was finally changing — aging, sagging, becoming a man's face instead of a tool's — because the fog had stripped half his names away and what was left underneath was just Hesk, tired and fifty and afraid.

"Corvane," he said. "Tell me it's true. Tell me the book really gives them back."

"It's true," I said. "I've seen one returned. It's the only beautiful thing the Guild ever built, and they built it by accident."

He lowered his sword. Then he turned around, drew himself up, and put himself between the choir and the last of the loyalist wardens, fighting — badly, bravely, humanly — for the other side, and I did not have time to feel anything about it because the guild master was moving toward the altar.

* * *

I reached the fire-chamber's heart in time to see the end of the fight and the beginning of something worse.

Wendell stood at the ledger-altar with his voice worn down to a rasp, nine hours of names behind him, thousands of ghosts already departed singing through walls that no longer existed. The ember in its bowl had burned low — a red coin, a dying coal, Vell's whole heart down to its final flicker. The guild master came through the smoke with ward-fire coiling off his hands like snakes, and Sable hit him from the flank and bought three seconds, and Wendell bought three more by doing the bravest cowardly thing I have ever witnessed: he simply did not stop reading. He read through the fire. He read through the guild master's grip closing on his shoulder. He read the next name, and the next, because the names were the weapon and the shield and the country all at once.

And the guild master — the man who had refinanced death for six hundred years, who had offered my friend a counterfeit life with one hand while holding the bill in the other — reached for the ledger to tear it shut.

The debt reached back.

I saw it happen. I will spend the rest of my life seeing it. The unpaid names rose off the page like steam, like a congregation, like a tide, and they closed over the guild master the way water closes over a dropped stone, and the last sound he made was not a scream but an entry — a voice reciting figures, balances, arrears — as the ledger he had served collected the account he had spent his whole life pretending didn't exist.

Silence, then. Real silence, the kind Vell hadn't known in months.

Wendell swayed at the altar, gray-faced, hands shaking, and turned the page.

And I saw his face change. I saw him read the last entry — the final name in nine thousand, the one debt still open — and I saw him understand it, and accept it, and begin, terribly, to smile.

"What?" Sable demanded, limping to his side, ink-black and bloodied and fierce. "Wendell. What does it say?"

He didn't answer her. He looked at the ember, guttering, minutes from dark. He looked at the choir, gathered close, waiting. He looked at the last name in the ledger the way a man looks at a door he has always known was there.

"The fire's almost out," he said softly. "We should hurry."

And he picked up the bone-stylus, and began to carve.
