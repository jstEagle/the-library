## 22. The Keeper of No Name

The trouble with having no name is that people keep trying to give you one.

I have been called Old Bones by the fisher-children, the Ledger-Man by the harbor-folk, That Quiet Fellow Who Fixed the Oarlock by a woman whose gratitude outlasted her memory of the favor. The town council — three chairs, one table, a great deal of arguing about drainage — once formally proposed "Amberdale" as a courtesy, on the theory that every man ought to be called something. I thanked them, declined politely, and carved them a new gavel. The old one had split from enthusiasm.

It has been eleven years since Vell ended with a chord instead of a scream. Eleven years since I held a door open in drowned stone while a country's dead walked through it singing, and the living followed, blinking, onto a shore under stars that had never heard of us.

Eleven years, and I am still choosing.

* * *

Mornings here begin with salt. The settlement sits where the shore flattens into meadow — we call it Shorefall, though the name was voted on for so long that half the town still refuses to use it on principle — and the sea keeps its own hours, indifferent to ours. I wake before dawn because old habits are the last things to drown, put the kettle on, and take down the ledger.

Not *the* ledger. That one is gone, balanced and closed, its final entry paid in full by a man who no longer exists under that name. This one is smaller. Alder boards, bone clasps — I carve my own clasps now, always have, always will; a man should know his own hands even when he doesn't know his own name — and pages of good rag paper that Sable trades for out of a port two days' sail south.

It is an honest ledger. That is the whole of its virtue and it is enough.

In it I record births. Not the guild way — not names taken, weighed, collateralized, entered as debt against some cosmic account nobody agreed to open. Here, a birth goes in like this:

*Fourth day of the Herring Moon. Born to Maren Ostler and her wife Tilde: a daughter, seven pounds, loud as a gull in a chimney. Name given freely: Sennet. Witnessed: myself, the midwife Brava, and one extremely unimproved cat.*

Freely given. Freely witnessed. Freely kept. Three freedoms, and the whole difference between what the Guild of Rare Exchanges was and what this town decided to be.

Some mornings, when the light comes up gold over the water and the ink dries slow, I think about old Master Corvane — warden, defector, the only Reclamationist who ever chose a side rather than a wage — and how he'd have laughed at these entries. He spent his life among wardens stitched with stolen names, men who wore other people's identities like coats made of skin. He died two winters back, in his bed, an extravagance none of us expected him to afford. His last words were a request that I enter his death in the ledger *honestly*, which I did, including the part where he tried to bribe the fever and lost.

*Corvane, called Warden, called Friend. Owed nothing. Left everything. Paid in full.*

That's the epitaph I'd want, if I wanted one. Which I don't, yet. I'm busy.

* * *

The workshop smells of tallow and brine and, faintly, of dragon-fire — though that last may be memory. Memory has a smell here. The whole shore does. When the wind comes off the water at dusk you can catch something under the salt, something warm and enormous and patient, like the inside of a banked hearth the size of a mountain. The children say it's the sea. The children are wrong, but I let them have it. A town needs its mysteries, and they've already used up mine.

I carve. That is the work, when the ledger is tended. Buttons and toggles, mostly, in the honest economy of a new place. Toys: little ships with sails that actually catch a breath of wind, which took me four years to engineer and made me briefly famous among the under-tens. And tokens.

Tokens are delicate work. In the guild, a name-token was a theft — a person's name cut into bone and held, so the person could be held too. I cut thousands of them across twenty years and never asked where the names came from, because asking questions in the Guild of Rare Exchanges was a career-shortening activity. Now I cut them the other direction. A mother brings me a lock of hair or a first tooth, and I carve her child's name into bone, and she carries it home, and the name belongs to the child — not to me, not to any book, not to any ward or wall or ancient bargain. It's a gift with a hole in the middle, a reminder that names can be carried without being owned.

Ivetta would have understood. Ivetta understood everything, in the end, except why anyone would need telling.

* * *

Sable Quill still writes on her skin.

Less than she used to. After the burning — after she fed her own written memories to a gate of fog to hold it shut against the guild's last assault — there were gaps in her, weeks and years gone like torn-out pages. For a long time she treated her arms and chest as territory to be re-conquered, inscribing everything twice, sleeping with ink by the bed in case she woke forgetting why she'd want it.

But somewhere in the last few years the war-footing eased. These days the writing on her is less archive and more diary. She keeps the important things — the names of everyone at the table, the route home from anywhere, the fact that she loves us, which she wrote on her left forearm in letters an inch high so she'd never have to squint at it — and lets the rest stay loose. She says forgetting a little is a kind of tide. You don't fight the tide. You just make sure the important cargo is lashed down.

She runs the trade now, out of Shorefall's single proper ship, the *Second Chorus*. (Naming things after choirs is apparently contagious. I started it. I accept responsibility.) Twice a season she sails south to ports where nobody has ever heard of Vell, buys paper and dye and the particular bitter tea Corvane loved, and comes home with stories about places where the stars have always been these stars, and nobody finds that strange, and nobody has ever sold a name.

On those nights she sits at my workbench and tells me everything, fast, the way she does, and I write it all down in a second ledger — the story-book, the children call it, though it is emphatically not for children — because a town that remembers itself by choice is a town the fog can't touch. We learned that in the drowned capital, watching stones soaked in dragon-fire refuse to forget. Memory held on purpose is armor. Memory held by force is a prison. The whole history of Vell fits inside that sentence, if you write it small.

"How's the choosing going?" she asked me last night, feet on my table, tea in my cup, which is how she indicates affection.

"Still deliberating," I said.

"It's been eleven years."

"I'm thorough."

She grinned at me — Sable Quill, smuggler, keeper, the woman who refused to forget when forgetting was the law of the land — and said, "You know what I think? I think you're not choosing a name. I think you're choosing whether you need one."

I didn't answer, because she was right, and because she'd have written it on her arm otherwise.

* * *

The child — I still call her the child, though she is fourteen now and mortally offended by it — is the town's historian. Of course she is. She was born after the stars went out, the first of the new sky, and she carries more of Sable's burned memories than anyone guessed: fragments came to her in dreams for years, images of a drowned city and a burning gate and a woman with ink to her elbows holding a doorway against the dark. She wrote them all down. She writes everything down. She has filled forty-one volumes with the history of Shorefall, and she interviews me monthly, formally, with a list of questions, and takes no nonsense whatsoever.

Today's question, delivered with the gravity of a magistrate: "Do you miss being someone?"

I set down my carving knife and considered it properly, because she deserved a proper answer and because, honestly, so did I.

"When I was Wendell Amberdale," I said, "I knew exactly who I was. Bone-carver, third rank, keeper of ledgers, resident of a room above the guildhouse with a view of a courtyard I never questioned. I knew my name, my rank, my duties, my debts." I turned the little wooden ship in my hands. "And every single one of those certainties was either a lie or a cage. Most were both. So no. I don't miss being someone. I miss being certain, the way you might miss a fever — it felt vivid at the time."

She wrote that down. Then she looked up with her mother's stubbornness and her borrowed ghosts in her eyes and said, "But you're someone now. You just won't say who."

"That," I told her, "is the best question anyone's asked me all year, and I'm not answering it on the record."

She huffed, snapped her book shut, and stalked out — pausing at the door to inform me that dinner was at Sable's and I was expected, which is the historian's way of saying *you are loved, be punctual.*

* * *

Here is what I have learned about identity, in eleven years of not having one, and I offer it free of charge, which is more than the guild ever did:

A name is not a self. A self is a pattern of choices, repeated until they become character, witnessed until they become real. The guild thought a name was a handle you could grab a soul by, and they were wrong, and their wrongness drowned a kingdom. What I am is not missing. What I am is *unrecorded* — and there is a freedom in that which I would not trade for every certainty in the old world.

When I die — later, I hope; the ledger has blank pages yet — whoever finds my body will find a man with no name and a great many friends. They'll bury me facing the water. Someone will carve the stone, and I have already told them what it should say, in the event I haven't chosen by then:

*He was still deciding. He was happy about it.*

* * *

This evening I walked down to the tide-line alone, as I do most evenings, and sat on my rock — the flat one, the thinking one — and let the day drain out of me the way the water drains off the flats.

The choir came, as it always comes.

Faintly. Always faintly now, and always content. On the far side of the door, in whatever comes next, the Drowned Choir sings on — Ivetta's voice at its center, clear as a struck bell, the girl who demanded justice and got it, name by name, read aloud by a penitent's shaking voice over the guttering of the last fire in Vell. They went through singing. The country ended with a chord. And sometimes, on quiet evenings, when the tide is right and my heart is quiet too, the sound crosses over — thin as light through fog, but unmistakable.

They weren't singing anything sad. That's the thing people get wrong about elegies. Tonight it was a working song, something rhythmic and rolling, the kind of song you sing hauling nets or hauling hope, and underneath it, woven through it, harmonies I didn't recognize — new voices, joining. Voices that had never drowned. Voices that had walked through the door after us and found, on the other side, that singing was the local language.

I listened for a long time.

Then, because no one was watching, I hummed along. Badly. I have never once been in tune, a fact the choir finds endlessly amusing — I can feel the amusement in the harmonies, a bright ripple like sun on water. Eleven years of humming off-key at the dead, and they still let me sit in. That, I think, is what family is: the ones who harmonize around your wrong notes until the whole sounds true.

The tide came in. The stars came out — these stars, the strange generous scatter of them, constellations nobody in Vell ever named, waiting patiently for us to give them names freely given. Somewhere behind me the settlement lit its lamps one by one: Sable's window, the historian's desk-lamp, the tavern where the found family gathers without needing to call itself that.

I opened the ledger to tomorrow's page. Blank. Honest. Waiting.

"Ink's dry," I told the tide. "No rush."

And in the surf, faintly, sweetly, entirely without judgment, the choir sang me home.
