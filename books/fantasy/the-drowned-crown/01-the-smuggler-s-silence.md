## 1. The Smuggler's Silence

The marmot was lying to me.

I want to be clear about that up front, because everything else in this story—the parts where I'm clever, the parts where I'm lucky, the parts where I'm neither—depends on you understanding that I knew it from the start. The marmot sat on its haunches at the mouth of the pass, whiskers frosted white, and told me with great confidence that the trail ahead was clear of soldiers, and I heard the lie the way you hear a wrong note in a song your mother used to sing: instantly, and with a small ache behind the ribs.

*Clear,* it said, in the way marmots say things, which is less words than weather. *Clear clear clear, warm burrow, no boots, no boots, no—*

There it was. No boots. You don't think about boots unless there are boots to think about.

"Try again," I told it, crouching in the snow so we were eye to eye. It was a fat one, well-fed for midwinter, which meant someone's grain stores had been leaking. Probably mine. Probably the stores I was currently smuggling past three checkpoints to a widow in Hollowmere who paid me in smoked fish and didn't ask questions, which made her my favorite client in the whole frozen spine of Thornfell.

The marmot blinked. Marmots are not subtle liars. Their thoughts sit right at the surface of them like stones in a shallow stream, and most people walk past those stones their whole lives without ever looking down. I look down. It's the family curse, or the family gift, depending on how much you enjoy knowing what the world thinks of you. Spoiler: the world mostly thinks about worms, and warmth, and fear. It is very rarely thinking about you. I had built an entire life on that fact.

*Boots,* the marmot said again, sheepish now, the way animals get when caught out. *Many boots. Black coats. Smell of iron and ink. Coming slow.*

Iron and ink. That was the patrol, then. Not the local garrison, who smelled of woodsmoke and bad ale and could be bought with either. Iron and ink meant Crown men. Inquisitors' clerks rode with them sometimes, and clerks wrote things down, and things written down have a way of becoming permanent, and permanence was the one thing I had spent twenty-six years avoiding like a debt collector.

"Which way?" I asked.

The marmot pointed its nose east, toward the scree field above the frozen river. *Old way. Cold way. Thin ice over deep water. But no boots.*

"No boots," I agreed, and gave it the last of the dried apple from my pocket, because even liars deserve dinner. "And if anyone asks, you never saw me."

*Who would ask?* said the marmot, genuinely puzzled, and trotted off to its warm burrow, already forgetting me. That's the trick of beasts. They can't hold a human face in their heads for more than a few minutes. I envy them for it. Sometimes, on long nights in drafty rooms, I practice forgetting my own.

* * *

Let me tell you the rules, since rules are the closest thing I have to a biography.

One: never give your true name. Casper Sterling is real enough—it's on exactly two documents in the world, both forged, both mediocre—but I answer to Cass, to Sterling, to "oi, you," to whatever a client or a customs man needs me to be. A name is a hook. People hang memories on names, and memories are how they find you again.

Two: never sleep twice in the same bed, work the same route twice in a season, or drink in the same tavern twice in a month. Variation is fog. Fog is life.

Three—and this is the important one, the one I carved into myself young enough that I don't remember learning it—never let anyone see the gift. Because the gift is the loudest kind of strange there is. A woman who talks to wolves gets burned in some towns and worshipped in others, and both ends of that spectrum end with crowds, and crowds end with someone writing your name down.

I know what you're thinking. *A smuggler with a conscience chapter coming, surely. A found family. A redemption arc.* I could hear you thinking it, if you were a marmot. But no. This is the story of how well my rules worked, right up until the night they didn't, and I'd appreciate it if you let me have a few more pages of peace before the blood starts.

So: the scree field. East, above the river, under a sky the color of old pewter. I had forty pounds of salt pork, a bolt of southern wool, and a letter sewn into my coat lining that I hadn't read and didn't want to, because letters are evidence and evidence has weight. The wind came down off the high peaks with that particular Thornfell bite, the kind that finds the gap between glove and sleeve and files away at you all day. My breath froze in the wool of my scarf. Somewhere below, under six feet of blue ice, the river moved in the dark like something dreaming.

I like the cold, if you want the truth. Cold keeps people indoors. Cold means empty roads, and empty roads mean I am alone with the only company that never lies to me by accident: the beasts, who lie constantly but always on purpose, which I respect.

The ravens found me at the top of the scree. Ravens always find me; I've stopped flinching at the shadow of wings.

*Dead thing,* said the nearest one, hopping along a stone. *Dead thing, dead thing, by the ford. Man-shaped. Still warm-ish. Crows arguing over the eyes.*

My stomach did something complicated. "How long dead?"

*Not long. Walking-dead. Falling-down-dead.* Ravens have no grammar for dying versus dead; everything past a certain point is just meat with opinions fading out of it. But *warm-ish* snagged in me like a hook in wool.

"How far?"

*Ford. Down. Follow the smell of blood.*

I should have gone east. I want that on the record too. Every instinct I owned, every rule, every hard-won inch of my careful forgettable life said: a dying man by a ford is a door, and doors open onto other doors, and behind one of them is always a crowd with torches. I went west instead. I've had years to wonder why, and the honest answer is that I heard him before I saw him—not his voice, he didn't have one left, but the animals around him. The ford's fox had been circling him for hours, making a sound I'd never heard a fox make. Grief, maybe. Or curiosity. With foxes the two are often the same animal.

But that's the next part of the story, and I'm getting ahead of myself, and a woman whose whole survival depends on sequence should know better.

* * *

The patrol caught my scent—or rather, their dogs did—at the far edge of the scree field, an hour after I'd left the marmot and its convenient lies behind. I heard the dogs before I saw the men: six hounds, chained and frustrated, their minds full of rabbit-ghosts and the red shout of my trail.

*Girl-smell,* the lead hound reported to its handler, in the simple, honest way dogs report everything. *Wool and tallow and smoke. Girl-smell going east.*

East. Good dog. There was a hare trail crossing mine not fifty yards back, and I'd stepped in it deliberately, because hares go east through the scree and vanish into a warren maze that no hound alive untangles. I'd been laying false trails since I was nine years old, when a customs officer in Greyharbor grabbed my wrist and asked my name and I looked at the mule beside him and borrowed its boredom, its flat gray nothing-thoughts, until the officer's eyes slid off me like rain off oilcloth. He'd looked straight at me and seen nothing worth remembering. That was the first time I understood what the gift could do when I turned it outward—not just listening, but blurring. Not invisibility. Forgettability. The difference matters. Invisible things are interesting. Forgettable things are furniture.

I lay flat behind a shelf of granite and watched the patrol come through the pass below me. Six soldiers in black coats, iron at their hips, and riding at their center a thin man on a gray horse with ink-stained fingers and a ledger strapped to his saddle. A clerk-inquisitor. Even at four hundred yards I could feel the dogs' confusion radiating off him like heat off a forge—he kept yanking their attention back to the trail with a whistle, and the whistles hurt them, and they didn't understand why the girl-smell kept unraveling into hare.

*Wrong,* the lead hound kept saying, miserable. *Wrong wrong wrong. Was girl. Now hare. Was girl.*

"I know, lad," I murmured into the granite. "Hold the line."

The clerk-inquisitor raised a gloved hand and the column stopped. He turned his face slowly across the landscape the way a man turns a page, and I felt the gift strain against what I was asking of it. This was the part that cost. Blurring yourself from a dog is easy; dogs want to be distracted. Blurring yourself from a man who is trained to notice is like holding a door shut against a tide. Sweat froze at my hairline. I borrowed stillness from the stones themselves—that's the deepest well I have, the mineral patience of rock, thought moving slower than glaciers—and I became, as far as any mind on that slope could tell, a lichen-covered boulder with an opinion about nothing.

The clerk looked directly at me. I am nearly sure of it. His eyes crossed the exact place I lay, paused—paused, a half-second too long, the way a man pauses when a word is on the tip of his tongue and won't come—and then moved on.

*Nothing here,* I pushed at him, gentle as snowfall. *Nothing here. Nothing here.*

He clicked his tongue and the column moved on, east, following my hare.

I lay there another quarter hour anyway. Rule four: never trust a silence you didn't verify. Then I rose, brushed the frost from my coat, and stood in the gray light shaking—not from cold, though the cold helped disguise it—from the specific exhaustion of having held a mountain's patience in a human skull for ten minutes. My hands wouldn't close properly. I flexed them and breathed and watched the patrol shrink to dots on the eastern trail, and I felt the thing I always felt after: the wary contentment. The small, banked fire of it. They were gone. Nobody had written my name down. The world had looked at me and filed me under *nothing*, and *nothing* was the safest country in the world, and I was its sole citizen and queen.

I allowed myself thirty seconds of being pleased with myself. I recommend this practice. Triumph hoarded in small portions keeps longer than triumph gorged.

Then I turned west, toward the ford, toward the warm-ish dead thing and the fox making its grief-sound, and I remember thinking—I swear on every rule I owned—I remember thinking: *just a look. Just to see. Dead men tell no tales, and neither do I.*

That's the joke, isn't it. That's the cosmic punchline I've been chewing on ever since. Dead men tell no tales.

This one told mine.

* * *

The ford was a quarter mile of frozen shallows where the river spread itself thin over gravel bars, ice laced across it like lacework gone wrong. And there, half in the water, half out, was the man.

He'd crawled, that much was obvious. There was a furrow in the snow behind him, a dragged line like something written by a giant's finger, and it ended where he lay: an old man, older than old, in a courier's leathers gone gray with road-dust and darker with blood. His side was soaked through. The blood had frozen in a fan around him, black-red on white, and steam still lifted faintly off the wet at his middle, which meant the wound was fresh and the cold was losing the race against it.

The fox was there. That fox. The one that had been shadowing me for weeks—I'd assumed it was working the same routes I was, living off the same leaks in the same granaries, because that's what foxes do. It sat three feet from the dying man, perfectly still, tail wrapped over its feet, watching him with an attention no fox gives a corpse. Foxes look at dying things with appetite. This one looked at him the way you look at a locked door you've walked past your whole life and just noticed the keyhole.

And the man—here is the part I will be retelling, or being retold, for the rest of whatever I have left—wasn't looking at the fox. He wasn't looking at anything. Except that when my boot crunched the ice at the ford's edge, his head turned, smooth as a compass needle finding north, and his eyes found me across forty feet of frozen shallows, and he smiled.

Not the smile of a man seeing help. The smile of a man seeing the last piece of a puzzle click home.

"Sterling," he said. My name. My true name, the one on two forged documents, the one I'd given to no living soul in eleven years. It came out cracked and steaming, half-frozen, and it went through me colder than the wind off the peaks. "Knew... you'd come. Knew it. The birds said. The birds always said."

Every rule I had screamed at once, a chorus, a landslide. *Run. Run run run.* My body actually turned. My body got three steps east before the fox spoke.

Not barked. Spoke. One word, in a voice like dry leaves and banked embers, in the speech-under-speech that beasts use, except no beast had ever shaped a human word in it before, and no beast had ever spoken it in a voice that landed in my chest like a hand:

"Wait."

I waited. God help me, I waited. I stood on the ice of a frozen ford in the teeth of Thornfell winter, with a Crown patrol somewhere behind me and a dying stranger bleeding his name into my ears, and I waited, because the fox had spoken, and because the dying man had begun, with fingers blackened by cold, to drag something from inside his coat.

It was a map. Folded small, stained dark, tied with a cord. He held it out to me the way you hand a live coal—carefully, reverently, with both hands, as if it weighed more than he did. Which, I suspect, it did.

"Return," he whispered. Blood at his lips now, bright against the frost-gray of his beard. "What was sold. Return what was—" A cough took the rest. He pressed the map into my hands and folded my fingers over it with his own, and his grip was stronger than it had any right to be, the grip of a man spending his last coin all at once. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, and looking into them was like looking into water over something deep.

"You have her chin," he said. Wonderingly. "You have her—"

The cold took the rest of that sentence too, and then it took him. Not gently. The shiver ran through him once, hard, and then he settled, the way snow settles, and the steam stopped rising, and the fox bowed its head.

I stood there holding a bloodstained map I had never wanted, hearing the words *you have her chin* echo in the hollow place where my name used to feel safe, and downstream, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind off the pass—

Dogs. Baying. Coming west.

They'd found the hare. Hares run out of tricks eventually. So, it turns out, do I.

The fox looked up at me, and its eyes were the color of the deep water under the ice, and it said, in that leaf-and-ember voice, the second word it would ever say to me:

"Run."
