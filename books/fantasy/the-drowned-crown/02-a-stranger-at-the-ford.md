## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The ford at Hollowmere was the kind of place that made a body believe in bad luck. The river came down off the Thornfell slopes in three gray braids, met in the middle of a shallow bowl of stones, and then couldn't decide which way to go, so it went all of them at once. Ice laced the edges in February. The crossing was knee-deep if you picked your line right and hip-deep and drowning if you didn't.

I'd crossed it forty times in six years. I knew it the way you know a tooth that aches — intimately, resentfully, with a running list of everything that could go wrong.

That morning the list got shorter and worse.

I'd come down from the high pass with a sledge of contraband tea, lamp oil, and one crate of something I'd agreed not to ask about, bound for the widow Brannock's cellar in Millbrook. Ordinary run. Ordinary weather. The marmots had been chatty on the way down — they always are in late winter, hungry and gossipy as washerwomen — and their chatter said: *cold coming, wolf-smell on the north wind, two-legs with iron on the south road.* Two-legs with iron meant a patrol. Patrols were fine. Patrols were predictable. You stepped off the trail, stood still among the pines like a stump, and let them ride past believing the forest was empty, because to a soldier a forest is always empty unless it's shooting at him.

My rules were simple, and I'd kept them so long they'd worn grooves into me like water wears stone:

One. Never be remembered.

Two. Never be owed anything by anyone who can find you.

Three. Never take what a dying man offers, because dying men offer nothing for free.

I want it noted, for the record, that I broke rule three through no fault of my own. He was already dead when he broke it for me.

* * *

I heard him before I saw him, which was strange, because the beasts hadn't said a word about him.

That's the thing nobody believes about my gift until they see it: it isn't that animals talk. They don't talk. They *know*, the way you know fire is hot without needing a lecture on it, and what I do is stand close enough to that knowing to feel its warmth or its teeth. Birds know where grain is hidden. Deer know where men have passed. Rats know which cellar walls are thin. And every creature within half a mile of a dying man knows death the way a house knows a storm — a pressure change, a hush, a turning-toward.

The ford knew. That was the wrongness. The river itself seemed to lean toward the eastern bank, the way a dog leans toward a door before the knock comes. The fish under the ice had gone quiet. Even the crows in the alders had stopped their arguing and were looking downstream, all of them, forty black heads turned the same direction like weathervanes in a wind that wasn't blowing.

I set down the sledge. This was, in hindsight, the moment I should have picked it back up.

He came around the bend of the river with the staggering, deliberate walk of a man who has decided that falling over would be a waste of the steps he has left, and he was wrong about that, but you have to admire the arithmetic. Gray-bearded, big through the shoulders the way old soldiers and old oxen are big — not fat, just built once, long ago, for a purpose. His left side was soaked dark from ribs to hip and it wasn't river water. It steamed faintly in the cold, which told me the wound was fresh and hot, and the fact that he was upright at all told me he was stubborn past the point of sense.

His eyes found me across the water and did not do what eyes usually do when they find me.

This requires explanation. When people look at me — smugglers, guards, toll-keepers, priests — their gaze slides. Not magic, exactly; more like a habit I've cultivated down to the marrow. I dress gray. I stand in the gray parts of rooms. I answer questions in a voice pitched to be forgotten before the sentence ends. Six years of never being remembered, and I can tell you the exact texture of being looked *through*: it feels like cold water finding a crack in a boot. Unpleasant, familiar, survivable.

This man looked at me the way a man looks at a lit window in a country where all the windows should be dark.

"No," I said out loud, to him, to the crows, to whatever was arranging this morning behind the sky. "No, I'm not— go somewhere else."

"Help," he said, reasonably enough, and fell face-first into the shallows.

* * *

Here is what I want understood about what happened next: I am not kind. Kindness gets you remembered. Kindness gets people asking questions in taverns about the gray woman who pulled a stranger from the ford. I have walked away from worse than a drowning man. I once watched a toll-collector beat a boy over two coppers and I bought my passage with the exact correct coins and left, because intervening costs more than the coin, always, forever, and the free cities are full of ghosts who learned that lesson one act of generosity too late.

But he fell in the shallows on his side, and the current took his collar, and his boots caught on the stones so he didn't wash loose, he just lay there half-drowned in eight inches of water, breathing in that wet tearing way, and the crows started up all together, and the river said — I swear this, though I've never told anyone — the river said *him, him, him,* like a bell struck once.

So I broke rule three. I waded in and dragged him onto the shingle, and I knelt there in my good gray coat getting soaked to the elbow in a stranger's blood, telling myself the whole time: *you'll drag him above the waterline, that's mercy enough, then you take the sledge and you're gone and this never happened.*

Above the waterline. That was the plan. A man could drown in eight inches; a man could also freeze; moving him above the waterline was practically bookkeeping.

His hand shot out and closed on my wrist with the grip of a man hauling himself up a cliff, and I learned that dying men are stronger than living ones, because living men hold on with their arms and dying men hold on with everything they have left, which turns out to be more.

"Listen." His breath smelled of copper and pine resin, someone's field-medicine. "There's no time. They're behind me. Grey cloaks, grey horses, a woman with—" he coughed, wetly, terribly, "—a woman with white gloves. Don't let her touch you. Whatever she asks. Whatever she offers. Her hands don't forget what they hold."

"Wonderful," I said, because terror makes me flippant, always has. "Sir, I'm a tea merchant. You've got the wrong—"

"You're a Sterling."

Everything in me went still. The river kept talking — *him, him, him* — but I stopped hearing it.

Nobody knows that name. My mother buried it so deep even I only dig it up in winter, alone, saying it once to make sure I still own it. I'd never said it aloud to another living soul. There were maybe four people alive who knew it, and I'd spent money I couldn't spare confirming that two of them were dead.

"How," I said.

"Bought back what I could." He fumbled at his chest, at the lacings of a coat stiff with old wear, and brought out an oilcloth packet the size of a prayer book, wrapped tight, tied with waxed cord, stained along one edge in a way I already understood I'd spend years wishing I hadn't seen. He pressed it into my hands and folded my fingers over it with both of his, and his hands were freezing, and they shook, and they did not let go. "Return what was sold. Do you hear me? It isn't theirs. It was never theirs. Return what was sold."

"I don't know what you sold. I don't know who you are."

"You will." Something moved through his face then — relief, or grief, or the particular peace of a man setting down a load he's carried so long his spine grew around it. "You have her eyes. Nobody ever told you? You have her eyes exactly."

Whose eyes. I asked whose eyes. He smiled — actually smiled, blood on his teeth, gray lips — and said, "Ask the fox," which was such a mad thing to say that I almost laughed, except that a fox had been following me for three weeks, keeping just past arrow-range, watching me with amber eyes like it was waiting for me to catch up to something, and I had told myself it was after my trail rations.

"Go east," he said. "Not the road. Under. The old ways. And girl — Casper—" my name in his mouth sounded ancient, worn smooth like a river stone, "—don't be sorry. Being found isn't the same as being caught."

Then the light went out of him between one breath and the next, the way a lamp goes out when you carry it into wind, no drama, just gone, and his hands slid off mine into the snow, and the ford went silent all at once, every crow, every fish, every whispering current, like the whole valley had leaned in to listen and now leaned back.

I sat in the slush holding a dead man's parcel and discovered I was crying, which was infuriating, because I didn't cry, I *especially* didn't cry over strangers, and rule one, rule one, rule one rang in my skull like a cracked bell.

I wiped my face with a sleeve that was already ruined. I told myself: leave him. Take the sledge. The tea run matters. The tea run is real life. This is an interruption.

Instead I dragged him up among the alder roots, out of sight of the far bank, and folded his hands over his chest, and stood there like an idiot for the length of ten heartbeats because some dead grandfather-shaped stranger had known my name and I needed those ten heartbeats to hate him properly for it.

Then I took the map, left the sledge — the tea run, the oil, the unasked-about crate, six years of careful ordinary commerce, abandoned in the snow like a shed skin — and I ran.

* * *

They came twenty minutes later. I know because I watched from the rocks above the eastern bank, flattened behind a granite shoulder with the parcel jammed inside my coat against my sternum, feeling its bloodstain soak through my shirt like a brand.

Five riders. Grey cloaks, grey horses, and the horses were wrong — too quiet, too obedient, ears flat in a way that said these animals had been taught to stop having opinions. Royal colors stitched at the shoulder: a mountain split by a sword. Vaelric's mark. Inquisition outriders, the kind that don't collect tolls or chase smugglers, the kind that exist for one errand, and I could guess whose errand lay folded under the alder roots below.

And at their head, riding easy, a woman. Neat as a pin. Middle years, sensible wool traveling clothes, a face like somebody's favorite aunt — the kind who remembers birthdays, asks after your mother, slips you sweets. She reined in at the ford and sat looking at the water for a long moment while her riders spread out along the banks with the unhurried competence of people doing a job they're very good at.

Then she dismounted, crouched, and touched the surface of the river with two fingers.

White gloves. Kid leather, immaculate, buttoned at the wrist.

The river flinched. I felt it from fifty yards and thirty feet up — felt the whole braid of it recoil from her fingers like a hand from a stove, felt the fish go flat and blind in the shallows, felt the crows on the far bank go silent one by one, west to east, like snuffed candles. Whatever Wren Halloway — though I didn't know her name yet, wouldn't learn it until later, would come to wish I never had — whatever she was touching that water for, the water objected at a level deeper than instinct.

She straightened. Looked east. Directly at my rock.

I have been hunted by dogs, by hawks, by one memorable falcon in the Duke's employ that could pick a rabbit's heartbeat out of a hillside. I know what being *sensed* feels like from the animal side, and standing behind that granite I felt it from the middle: her attention moved over the slope like a slow hand over a bedsheet, checking for the lump underneath.

"Don't be sorry," I whispered, furious, to a dead man. "Being found isn't the same as being caught. Easy for you. You're done."

Her head turned a degree. Listening. Of course she was listening; the aunt-face sharpened into something patient and bottomless, and she raised one white-gloved finger and beckoned, gently, the way you beckon a child out of hiding when it's time to come indoors.

Every hair on my body stood up and voted to run.

I ran. Not along the ridge — she'd expect the ridge — but down and east through the alder scrub, low, fast, letting the fox-trail I'd scouted weeks ago take me into the ravine where the ground fell away in broken shelves no horse would follow. Behind me I heard a whistle, short and clear, and hooves, and once, distantly, her voice calling something reasonable and terrible across the water: *"We only want what he carried! Whoever holds it — you're not in trouble! We only want to help!"*

Rule one: never be remembered.

Somewhere behind me a horn sounded, twice, and was answered from the south, from the direction of Millbrook, from the direction of the life I'd spent six years building out of gray cloth and small favors and forgetting.

I ran east with a dead man's blood drying against my ribs and a parcel I couldn't open, couldn't return, couldn't put down, and with every stride the truth of it settled into me colder than the ford: the invisibility I'd woven around myself, thread by thread, year by year, was torn. Somewhere in a ledger in a grey tower there was now a line with my family name in it, inked in a dying man's last breath, and the woman with the white gloves had looked at my rock and *beckoned.*

In the ravine, where the shadows pooled blue and the ice hung in teeth from the ledges, something fell into step beside me on the rim above — light-footed, red-furred, keeping pace without effort.

The fox looked down at me with its amber eyes, and it did not ask for rations.

It looked, I thought wildly, like it was about to say something.
