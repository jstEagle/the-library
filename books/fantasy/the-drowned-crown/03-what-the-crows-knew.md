## 3. What the Crows Knew

The trouble with crows is that they're gossips. The trouble with goats is that they're mad. The trouble with me, that morning, was that I needed both.

I'd made it nine miles from the ford before I let myself stop — a hollow under a granite shelf above the frozen river, small enough to heat with a handful of coals from my pack-stove, invisible enough that I'd slept in it twice before without ever telling myself where it was. My rules are simple. Never sleep in the same place twice in a row. Never tell anyone your name twice. Never be remembered. Nine years of smuggling through Thornfell and those three rules had kept my neck attached to the rest of me better than any blade or charm.

And then a dying man had gone and ruined all of it by pressing a bloodstained map into my hands in front of four royal inquisitors with very good memories.

I unrolled the map on my bedroll by coal-light and looked at it again, the way you look at a debt collector's letter, hoping it'll have changed. It hadn't. It was drawn on vellum gone soft as cloth at the folds, inked in something darker than any ink I knew, and it showed the Thornfell interior — but wrong. The mountains were there, the river was there, but threaded through them were roads no living map carried: causeways running straight as spears through country that had never known a straight road, converging somewhere under the massif the old-timers called the Hollow Crown. At the center of the convergence, the cartographer had drawn a small circle and written one word inside it in a hand so fine I had to hold it an inch from my eyes:

*Pledge.*

Not *treasury*. Not *vault*. Not any of the dozen words a smuggler learns to read at a glance because they mean money. *Pledge* — which means pawned, which means held against payment, which means somebody gave something up and meant to take it back and hadn't yet.

I sat with that for a while, listening to the river grind its ice, and then I went out into the grey morning to find someone who'd talk to me about it. Which meant crows. And, if my luck ran true, a goat.

* * *

Crows are the best informants in the mountains because they're paid in things nobody else values: shiny rubbish, dead mice, and flattery. I keep a pouch of buttons and broken mirror-glass for exactly this purpose. The trick is to never ask directly. Ask a crow "who drew this map" and it will lie to you, because crows lie the way rivers run downhill — naturally, constantly, and toward their own advantage. But ask a crow what it saw, and pay it well, and it will tell you true, because crows can't resist being the ones who know.

I found them in a stand of black pines on the south slope, forty-odd of them holding what I can only describe as a committee meeting over a frozen elk carcass. I tossed the first button high so they'd see it flash.

"Good morning," I said. "Lovely feathers. Especially yours."

The nearest crow — big male, missing a toe, clearly the chairman — cocked his head at me. *"Buttons,"* he said, in the flat clattering way of his kind. *"More buttons."*

"Buttons for seeing. What did you see by the river ford, two days past? Men in grey and red. A man dying on the ice."

*"Saw,"* said the crow, catching the button out of the air with the smugness of a merchant closing a sale. *"Grey men. Red men. Dogs that smell like smoke. They dug in the snow where the man died. They found nothing. They were angry."*

"They found nothing?" My stomach did a slow, unpleasant turn. "They didn't find the blood?"

*"Blood froze black. Black snow. Grey men licked it."* The crow shuddered, feathers rippling. Inquisitors tasting blood to read it — I'd heard rumors of such tricks, and had filed them under stories told to frighten smugglers. Apparently they were stories told to describe smugglers' Tuesdays. *"One man tasted and shouted. He said a name. He said—"*

The crow stopped. Crows always stop when the price runs out. I fed him two more buttons and half my mirror-glass, and he preened and delivered.

*"He said: Sterling. He said: find the Sterling line. He said: it ends with her, or it begins with her."*

I stood very still among the pines while forty crows watched me with the patient interest of creditors. *Sterling.* My name, spoken by an inquisitor's mouth, fifty miles from anywhere I'd ever used it. My rules said burn the map, cross the pass, become a new woman with a new name by nightfall. My rules had kept me alive for nine years.

But my rules had never accounted for the word *pledge*, written in blood-dark ink, sitting under a mountain like a debt waiting in a strongbox.

"Anything else?" I asked the chairman, mostly to delay deciding.

*"The goat knows,"* said the crow, and every crow on that carcass laughed — that dry rattling chorus they make, like sticks breaking. *"Ask the goat. The goat remembers the roads. The goat is mad, but madness keeps what sense forgets."*

* * *

The goat lived, if you could call it that, in the ruins of a shepherd's hut halfway up the valley — a white brute with one clouded eye and horns grown in a spiral like a question mark, which struck me as fitting. His name had been Barnabus once, according to a rotting leather collar, but the shepherds who'd owned him were twenty years dead and Barnabus had been alone with his thoughts ever since, which explains everything.

Goats don't talk the way birds do. Birds trade; you pay and they perform. Goats just say whatever is in them whenever it happens to fall out, like a sack splitting. You have to listen to all of it and sift for gold.

"Barnabus," I said, settling onto a fallen roof-beam at a respectful distance from the horns. "Old fellow. How's the winter finding you?"

*"Winter is a mouth,"* said Barnabus, gazing at me with his good eye. *"The whole world is a mouth and we are all going down it slowly. Some faster than others. The fast ones scream. Have you screamed lately?"*

"Not this week."

"That's the spirit. Or the lack of it." He chewed at a fence post with tremendous philosophical commitment. "You smell of crows and buttons and fear. The fear is new. Fear smells like copper pennies left in rain."

"I need to ask you about the drowned roads."

Barnabus stopped chewing. For a long moment the only sound was wind worrying the loose boards of the hut, and then the old goat turned his whole head — both eyes, cloudy and clear — toward the high peaks above us, toward the massif the maps called the Hollow Crown.

"You shouldn't say that word," he said quietly, which from a goat who thought the world was a mouth counted as gravitas. "*Drowned*. The roads aren't drowned. Drowning ends. The roads are *kept*. Kept under the water the way a lender keeps a ledger. Everything under the mountain is kept. The court below keeps. The king above takes. And the queen—"

He stopped again. Goats, unlike crows, cannot be bought. They can only be waited out.

"The queen what?" I prompted.

"The queen is *held*," Barnabus said, and his voice dropped into something that wasn't madness at all, that was older and sadder than anything I'd heard from a human mouth in years. "Held in pledge. That's what the seal on your paper says, girl. You've been carrying it around asking crows, and the answer was chewing your fence post the whole time. Held in pledge against a debt unpaid. The crown of water and light. The heart of the binding. Every beacon in the free cities burns off it, every ward on every road, every blessing the old kingdom laid down like foundations — all of it anchored to her, and the anchor-hold belongs now to the usurper, and the usurper means to *pull*."

My mouth had gone dry. "How do you know what's on my map?"

"I remember the roads," Barnabus said simply. "I walked them before the water came up. All of us did, in the before-times. The beasts remember what men bury. That's your gift, isn't it? That's why you can hear us. The old blood hears the old memory. Why do you think the inquisitors tasted your blood and spoke your name?"

"I don't have any old blood. I'm a smuggler. I steal brandy for people with more money than sense."

"You're a Sterling," said the goat, "and Sterlings sold something they had no right to sell, and one of them has spent his whole life crawling back up the mountain to unsell it." He nodded his spiral horns at my pack, where the map lay rolled against my spine. "And now he's dead on the ice, and the paper's come to you, and you're standing here asking a mad goat whether the world is still a mouth."

The wind came down the valley and set the loose boards clattering, and I sat on my roof-beam and felt the ground tilt under my certainties. Nine years of being nobody. Nine years of rules. And underneath it all, apparently, a name that inquisitors tasted in blood, a family that trafficked with kings, a debt that had crawled uphill on dying legs to reach me specifically — because it would reach me specifically, wouldn't it? Because the courier hadn't stumbled on me at the ford by chance. Because nothing about me had ever been chance, except my own careful construction of it.

"I didn't ask for this," I said, and heard how young and petulant it sounded.

"Nobody asks for inheritance," said Barnabus, resuming his chewing. "That's what makes it inheritance."

* * *

I was halfway back down to my hollow, moving fast, doing arithmetic — three days to the pass, four days behind me Halloway's dogs, five days of food — when I realized I was no longer alone.

He sat on a boulder beside the trail like he'd been carved there: the fox. Rust-red, black-stockinged, with that pale ash-grey wash across his muzzle that had made me think *old* the first time I saw him, weeks ago, except that he moved like water and his eyes were the wrong color entirely — a pale silver-grey, like frost on slate, like nothing a fox should have. He'd been following me since the first hard freeze. I'd named him Trouble in my head, fed him twice against my better judgment, and caught him watching me more times than I could count with an attention no animal spends on a woman eating jerky.

Animals watch predators. Animals watch prey. This fox watched me the way a lawyer reads a contract.

"Shoo," I said, without conviction.

The fox tilted his head. And then, in a voice like dry leaves and distant bells, in perfect intelligible speech that no beast had ever used with me in nine years of listening, he said:

"*Inheritance.*"

I stopped walking. The whole forest seemed to stop with me — the wind, the crows overhead, the grinding of the river ice, everything hanging on that single word.

"No," I said. "No, absolutely not. Beasts don't talk. I listen to them, that's the arrangement, that's how it's always been, they don't talk *back*."

"This one does," said the fox, rising to his feet with unhurried grace. Up close, the ash on his muzzle wasn't age. It was color — the grey of wood after fire, of stone after flood. "You've been asking crows and madmen about a thing you should have asked me about weeks ago. I've been waiting for you to be ready. I'm given to understand you may never be ready, so I've decided to stop waiting."

"What are you?"

"Ashen," he said, and the name landed on the snow between us like a coin on a counter. "Bound servant of the drowned queen, keeper of her road, witness of her pledge. I knew your grandfather, Casper Sterling. I liked him better than I like you. He asked fewer questions and walked faster."

Every instinct I owned — nine years of them, all pointing the same direction — screamed at me to run. Burn the map, change my name, put three ranges between me and this talking impossibility and never once look back. That was the rule. That had always been the rule.

But I stood there instead, boots in the snow, looking into frost-slate eyes, and I heard myself ask the question I'd been circling since the ford since the blood since the word *pledge*, the question curiosity had finally dragged out ahead of fear:

"What was sold?"

Ashen's tail curled. If a fox can smile, he smiled.

"Now we're getting somewhere," he said. "Walk with me. There's a knight hunting you who wants the same answers, and a cartographer who'll want cutting loose, and an inquisitor who wants your throat — and I promise you, little heir, every one of them knows more about your family than you do. We have perhaps two days before the fastest of them finds you. I suggest we spend them making you dangerous instead of merely haunted."

Behind us, far down the valley, a crow laughed — that dry rattling sound, like sticks breaking, like a debt being counted.

I picked up my pack. Against every rule I had, I followed the fox.
