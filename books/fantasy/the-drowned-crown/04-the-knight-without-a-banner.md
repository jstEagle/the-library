## 4. The Knight Without a Banner

I had been following the girl for six days before I finally understood that she was following me back.

That should have been my first warning. In thirty years of soldiering—first for a queen, then for no one at all—I have learned that there are two kinds of people in the world: those who are watched, and those who watch. The watched ones die surprised. The watchers die tired but rich. And here was a smuggler barely old enough to be called *woman* who moved through Thornfell like smoke through a keyhole, and every time I thought I had her trail, I found her boot prints *beside* mine, matching my pace, as if we were two halves of the same hunt and neither of us had been told.

Her name, I learned from a trapper who charged me three coppers and a lie about his mother's health, was Casper Sterling. Smuggler. Beast-speaker—or so the trapper whispered, crossing himself with the particular fervor of a man who has seen something he cannot explain and would very much like to never see again. The gift is forbidden, of course. Everything worth having in Vaelric's Thornfell is forbidden. But I did not care about her gift. I cared about the map.

The map that a dying courier had pressed into her hands at the river ford, according to the same trapper, who charged me another copper for that too. I was being bled dry by informants, which is what happens when you have no banner, no order, no seal, and nothing to offer a man but coin and the faintly embarrassing sincerity of your eyes.

I had a sword, though. That still counted for something in the mountains. Not much. But something.

* * *

The waystation at Hollowmere had been a shrine once, before the Drowning. You can still see it in the bones of the place—the arched windows meant for stained glass, now boarded against the wind; the alcove where a saint's statue stood, now home to a stove that smoked like a dying man's last words. The free cities call these places waystations. The mountain folk call them what they are: churches nobody could afford to keep holy.

I reached it at dusk on the sixth day, half-frozen, my left boot leaking snowmelt into my soul, and found the common room empty except for a one-eyed innkeep, a fire that had given up on ambition, and a marmot sitting on the table nearest the hearth, watching the door with an expression I can only describe as *expectant*.

Animals had been strange around me all week. Crows following at a distance. A fox—red as a brand, wrong-eyed in a way I couldn't name—sitting on ridgelines watching me pass. I'd told myself it was the weather. I am very good at telling myself things.

"Stew," I told the innkeep, laying down coin. "Bread. Whatever passes for wine."

"Wine's vinegar," he said. "Bread's stale. Stew's a mystery."

"Honesty. Rare in these parts." I took the corner table with its back to the wall and its view of both doors, because some habits outlive the men who need them, and waited.

She came in twenty minutes later, shaking snow from a hood the color of wet slate, and I will grant her this: she almost fooled me. She played the tired traveler well—shoulders slumped, hands chapped-red, moving toward the fire with the boneless gratitude of someone who has walked too far. She ordered food in a voice pitched low and flat. She did not look at me.

That was the mistake. Everyone looks at me. It is not vanity; it is arithmetic. A big man, scarred, armed, alone, in an empty room—eyes flick to him whether they mean to or not. Her eyes went to the innkeep, the stove, the window boards, the exits. Never to me. Never once. And a person who does not look at you has already looked at everything about you, which means she knew exactly what I was before she walked through that door, which means she came anyway.

Which meant she wanted something. Or she was testing whether I'd do it in public.

I finished my mystery stew—it was goat, and not recently—and followed her when she rose to take a room.

* * *

Her door was the third along the corridor. Mine was the fifth. I knocked on hers at midnight, when the innkeep's snores had settled into a rhythm you could set a watch by, and I heard her move inside—not to the door, but away from it, to the wall beside the window, where the floorboard wouldn't creak.

"Casper Sterling," I said through the wood. "I know about the ford. I know about the courier. I know what he gave you."

Silence. Long enough that I began composing my next approach. Then, through the door, dry as winter grass:

"You know a lot for a man with no coat of arms. Your sword hilt's wrapped to hide the maker's mark, your boots are knight's issue resoled twice, and you've got the posture of someone who spent twenty years getting yelled at by people fancier than him." A pause. "So either you're a very well-dressed bandit or a very badly dressed knight. Bandits don't knock."

"I knocked," I admitted, "because killing you over a piece of parchment seemed excessive, and because I've been trying to kill you in my head for six days and you kept winning."

The latch didn't move. "Flattering. Go away."

"The man at the ford," I said. "Before he died. What did he say to you?"

More silence. Then, quieter: "Why does a disgraced knight want to know?"

And there it was. The word *disgraced*, thrown back at me like a stone I'd left lying in the road myself. I leaned my forehead against the cold door and considered, briefly, the wisdom of walking away. I had gotten good at walking away. It was practically my coat of arms: a man departing, proper heraldic style, looking neither left nor right.

But I had also sworn an oath once, to a woman with water in her crown and stars in her voice, and oaths don't die just because the person they were sworn to does. They just go quiet. They wait under the floorboards of you, like coins in an old house, until some night exactly like this one, when a stranger behind a door asks the right question in the right voice.

"He said *return what was sold*," I said. "Didn't he."

The silence changed quality. It stopped being a wall and became a held breath.

"Who sold what?" she asked, and her voice had lost its armor. Underneath it was something young and frightened and furious about being frightened.

"That," I said, "is a long story, and this door is very cold, and I would rather tell it sitting down. I'm going to step back from the door now. You can put a knife in my hand when I open it, if it makes you feel better. Most people do."

* * *

She let me in, and she did put a knife in reach—a short, wicked thing she kept loose under her palm on the table between us, the way other people keep a cup of tea. We sat across from each other in a room with one candle, one bed she'd clearly decided I wasn't touching, and a window she'd unshuttered just enough to permit a fast exit and a slow fall onto the stable roof. Thorough, our smuggler. I respected it.

"You first," she said. "No banner. Why?"

Fair. I gave her the short version, which is the only version I own.

"My name is Aldric Vane. Ser Aldric Vane, if you're feeling generous, which most people stopped being eleven years ago. I was sworn to Queen Isolde Maren—the queen they call the Drowned One now, though she didn't drown, whatever the songs say. She was murdered, and her court was scattered, and her soul—" I stopped. Started again. "There are things I saw the night the palace fell that I have spent eleven years pretending I didn't see. One of them was men carrying a small chest down into the deep vaults while the queen lay dying above, and the chest *screaming*. Not the men. The chest. And I stood aside, because there were eight of them and one of me, and I told myself that standing aside was strategy. Survival. Sense." I turned my cup in my hands. "It was cowardice wearing sense's clothes. I've worn the disgrace ever since. Stripped of rank, struck from the rolls, name scraped off the gate of the order I served. Vaelric's clerks were very thorough. They enjoy thoroughness."

Casper said nothing for a while. The candle guttered and steadied.

"And the map?" she said finally.

"Isolde's cartographer made three copies of a chart to the court beneath the mountain. The true court—the old one, the one Vaelric built his palace on top of like a man building a house over a grave. The chart shows the vaults. All of them. Including the one where a certain chest went screaming." I met her eyes. "Eleven years I've hunted those copies. Two were destroyed—I burned one myself, in a fit of conscience I regret daily. The third vanished with the courier who carried it. A man named Emrys Sterling."

I watched her face when I said the name. I'm not proud of it. But I needed to see, and what I saw was nothing—perfect, practiced nothing, a shutters-closed expression that would have fooled anyone who hadn't spent eleven years reading faces for a living.

"Never heard of him," she said.

"No," I agreed. "I imagine you haven't."

She stood up so fast the knife skittered. "Get out."

"Sterling is a common name in the—"

"*Get out.*" Her voice cracked down the middle, and underneath the crack was the sound I'd been listening for since the ford: grief. Fresh grief, eleven days old, still bleeding. "You come in here with your sad story and your dead queen and you say that name like you have any idea—" She stopped herself. Breathed. When she spoke again it was quiet and terrible. "You don't know anything about him."

"I know he died at the ford with the map sewn into his coat lining," I said gently, "and I know that whoever he was to you, he ran for years to get that chart somewhere safe, and I know that the people hunting it are called inquisitors and that their leader is a woman named Wren Halloway, who was kind once, and is not anymore. And I know that in roughly ten weeks, when the thaw comes, King Vaelric intends to march on the free cities using power drawn from the thing in that vault—which means that whatever your grandfather was running from, it has caught up with you, and it will not stop at a locked door in a waystation."

The word landed. *Grandfather.* I hadn't planned to say it. It simply fell out, the truth doing what truth does, and I watched her armor fail all at once—not dramatically, not with tears, but the way ice fails on a pond: silently, structurally, everywhere at once.

She sat back down. Picked up the knife. Put it down again, deliberately, which from her I understood was a surrender more complete than any tears.

"How," she said, "do you know Halloway's name."

"Because she was the one who read out my sentence," I said. "Eleven years ago. She was younger then. She apologized while she did it, which was somehow worse." I rubbed my jaw. "She believes in Vaelric the way the devout believe in gods—totally, and without the benefit of evidence. She'll have your scent by now. She'll have the ford, the courier's body, the direction of travel. She is patient and she is brilliant and she does not sleep enough, which makes her dangerous in the specific way that tired believers are dangerous: they stop seeing anything that isn't the shape of their faith."

Casper stared at the candle flame. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone flat and practical, which I would learn was how she handled everything that hurt.

"So. You want the map. I have the map. You've been chasing me for six days instead of just taking it, which means either you're stupid or you need me for something the map doesn't show." Her eyes came up, sharp as her knife. "Which is it?"

"Both, probably. I'm told stupidity is a chronic condition." I leaned forward. "The chart shows roads and doors, girl, but the vault opens to blood and to words, and I have neither. The queen's line opened it. Her blood, her names, her rights. I'm a sword with a grudge—you're the key, whether you like it or not, and I suspect you don't, and I respect that, and I'm going to say the rest anyway."

"Say it fast."

"Come with me. Not because I'll protect you—I'm one man, and Halloway travels with twelve, and arithmetic is arithmetic. Come with me because you cannot carry this alone. Whatever Emrys Sterling was to you, he chose you for this, and he must have known what choosing you cost. He was buying something back. I intend to help him finish the purchase, even posthumously. Especially posthumously." I spread my empty hands. "I have no banner. No order. No king. What I have is thirty years of knowing how soldiers think, how inquisitors hunt, and how to stand in a doorway when it matters. It isn't much. But it's honest, and honest is rarer than gold in Thornfell this season."

She was quiet a long time. Outside, the wind worried the shutters, and somewhere below us the innkeep rolled over in his sleep, and the whole world seemed to hold its breath around the two of us and one candle.

"Why?" she asked at last, and it wasn't suspicion anymore. It was the real question, the naked one. "Eleven years. Why does it matter to *you*? She's dead. The kingdom's dead. Everyone's made their peace. Why spend your life on a corpse's errand?"

And because she had earned it with that question—because it was the question I asked myself at three in the morning in a hundred cheap rooms exactly like this one—I answered honestly.

"Because I stood aside once," I said. "Once. And it has been eleven years, and I have discovered that a man can survive disgrace the way a tree survives lightning—scarred, upright, technically alive—but he cannot survive doing it twice. If I stand aside again, there won't be enough of me left to bury." I shrugged, feeling the absurd lightness that honesty sometimes brings, like setting down a pack you'd forgotten you were carrying. "You have your rules, I expect. Ways you keep yourself safe. Never be remembered, never be owed, never be known. I had rules too. Stand aside. Keep your head. Let the great wheels grind whoever they grind. And they worked, our rules—they kept us breathing. But they didn't keep us *alive*, Casper Sterling, and I think you know the difference, because I watched you count the exits in a common room full of nobody, and nobody counts exits unless they're very tired of needing to."

Something moved behind her eyes—recognition, unwelcome and undeniable, the look of a person meeting their own reflection in a stranger's face and finding it older and uglier than hoped.

"You talk too much," she said.

"It's the disgrace. It made me chatty."

A long exhale. Then she pulled the map from inside her coat—wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, handled like something both precious and cursed—and laid it on the table between us without opening it, as if opening it might constitute agreement.

"We're not partners," she said. "We're not friends. You don't know my business, I don't know yours beyond the sad parts, and the moment this ends, we part ways and forget each other's faces."

"Naturally," I said.

"If you betray me, I will disappear so thoroughly that even the crows will forget you existed."

"A reasonable and proportionate threat. Accepted."

"And Ser Aldric?" She looked at me across the candle, and for one moment the smuggler's mask slipped entirely, and what looked out at me was a girl holding a dead man's last request in her lap and no idea what to do with it. "If you're lying about any of this—if this is a trap—I want you to know that I'll understand. People always want something. It's fine. Just tell me what it is first, before I start believing you."

I thought about the chest that screamed. I thought about a queen's voice saying *stand fast, Ser Aldric* in a corridor full of smoke. I thought about eleven years of doors I'd let close.

"What I want," I said, "is to be the man in the doorway this time. That's all. That's the whole of it."

She studied me for the space of three heartbeats. Then she untied the cord.

We bent over the map together, and I was explaining the western approaches, the drowned causeways, the gates marked in a cipher I'd spent a decade failing to crack, when we both heard it at the same instant: hooves. Many. Coming up the frozen road at a pace no honest traveler uses at the second hour past midnight.

Casper's head came up. Across the table, her eyes met mine, and I saw the arithmetic finish behind them—the same sum I'd done, the same answer.

"Inquisitors ride in threes," I said quietly, reaching for my sword. "That sounds like twelve."

Below us, in the yard, a horse screamed, and a voice I remembered from eleven years ago and had prayed never to hear again called out, clear and cold and terribly polite:

"Ser Aldric Vane. We know you're in there. And we've come for the map—and for the granddaughter of Emrys Sterling."
