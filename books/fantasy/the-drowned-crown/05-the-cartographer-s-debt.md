## 5. The Cartographer's Debt

I had been following Casper Sterling for nine days before I ever spoke to her, which is a personal record for restraint, and I want it noted that restraint does not come naturally to me.

My name is Odile Marchand, and I am very good at knowing where things are. It is the only thing I have ever been good at, and I did not choose it so much as it chose me the way a debt chooses you—suddenly, at an inconvenient hour, with men standing behind the man who delivers the news.

For six years I have drawn maps for the Guild of the Sealed Compass, which sounds like a romantic institution and is in fact the cartographic arm of King Vaelric's bureaucracy. We map roads, borders, aquifers, mine shafts, and—this is the part they don't put on the guild seal—the routes by which people disappear. Prisoner transports. Inquisitor circuits. The quiet roads to the quiet places. My master, Corvain Lisse, draws the beautiful ones himself, the presentation copies with gilded coastlines, and then apprentices like me draw the working copies, the ones that get folded into saddlebags and carried to places where the ink dries brown instead of black.

I tell myself I didn't know what the working copies were for. This is a lie I maintain the way other people maintain gardens: daily, diligently, and with increasing awareness that everything underneath is dead.

Nine days ago, a courier came through the guildhouse in Greywater with his left arm bound and his right hand shaking, and he asked Master Lisse for a copy of the Thornfell deep-passes. I was the one who inked it. And because I am the sort of person who reads documents upside down out of professional compulsion, I noticed that the courier's request slip bore a seal I had seen exactly once before—on the file marked *Sterling, E.* A file so old its string had rotted. A file my master kept locked in the bottom drawer of his desk, the drawer he opened only at night, by candlelight, when he thought the guildhouse slept.

Emrys Sterling. Dead thirty years, if the ledger said true. So why was someone asking after his granddaughter?

Then the courier died at a river ford, and a smuggler with mud on her boots walked out of the Thornfell passes carrying something wrapped in oilcloth, and every instinct I possess—which are excellent instincts, it's the character that's questionable—stood up and pointed.

So: nine days of following Casper Sterling through country that hated travelers on principle. Nine days of sleeping in ditches with my drafting tools wrapped against my chest like a second heart. Nine days of watching her talk to animals—which I assumed at first was loneliness, having practiced a fair amount of it myself—and then realizing, with a cold slide of the stomach, that the animals were talking back.

And now here is the part where I explain how I came to be sitting in the loft of the Drowned Lantern waystation while twelve of Vaelric's inquisitors dismounted in the yard below, listening to Ser Aldric Vane reach for his sword, and thinking, with the peculiar clarity that arrives only when everything has gone wrong:

*Good. Now I can finally introduce myself.*

* * *

Let me be honest about the arithmetic, since everyone else at that table was doing sums.

Twelve inquisitors. One disgraced knight whose sword arm had gone soft in eleven years of exile. One smuggler whose primary tactical doctrine was *run*. One fox of indeterminate allegiance. And me—a woman armed with a compass, three pens, and a mouth that had gotten me fined twice and flogged once.

The odds were not good. But I had spent six years watching men like the one shouting politely in the yard, and I had learned the single most important lesson of my indenture: inquisitors do not actually want violence. Violence creates paperwork, and paperwork creates witnesses, and witnesses create questions. What inquisitors want is compliance. They want you to open the door yourself, hands visible, expression contrite, and hand over whatever they've named before they have to name it twice.

Which meant the waystation had perhaps four minutes before someone got tired of politeness.

Four minutes to decide whether I was going to spend the rest of my life as the woman who drew the maps, or become—for however long I lasted—the woman who burned them.

I went down the ladder without bothering to be quiet about it. There was no longer any percentage in quiet.

"Vane," I said, low and fast, crossing the common room. "Ser Aldric. Eleven years ago you were attainted at Redford Bridge. Do you know why the charge stuck? Not the treason—nobody believed the treason. It stuck because the tribunal had a map. Drawn by the Guild of the Sealed Compass. Showing your supply route, your fallback positions, your crossing points." His face had gone the color of old tallow, and I kept going, because the clock in my head does not stop for anyone's feelings, including mine. "I know because I found the original last winter in the archive. It bears my master's watermark. Corvain Lisse drew the map that hanged your honor, Ser Knight, and I have spent six years inking his working copies, and I will be damned if I die in this room still holding his pens."

Casper Sterling stared at me across the table. She had a talent for staring—I'd watched her do it to marmots—but on her it looked like assessment, whereas on me it presumably looked like what it was, which was terror wearing ambition's coat.

"You're the one who's been following us," she said. "Since Greywater."

"I'm the one who's been following *you*," I corrected. "He was incidental. You were carrying something wrapped in oilcloth, and I would stake my guild certification—it's worth slightly less than nothing, but I'm sentimental—that it was a map. And not just any map." I held out my hand. "Show me the corner. Just the corner."

She didn't move. Below us, boots were crossing the yard, unhurried, disciplined, the sound of people who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered.

"Sterling," I said, and here my voice did something I hadn't authorized—cracked, just slightly, like bad plaster. "In about ninety seconds those men come through that door, and if they find that map on you, you'll wish they'd simply killed you, because what they do instead is longer. I know what their longer looks like. I've mapped the buildings where they do it." I swallowed. "I recognize the watermark on your map. Lisse's late work—the grey-gold fleck in the sizing, visible only at an angle, in good light. He made the paper himself for commissions he never entered in the guild ledger. Black commissions. And if that map is what I think it is, then it's the only document in existence that proves what my master sold, and to whom, and—" I stopped. Made myself finish. "And I need it. Not to sell. To burn. Or to use. I haven't decided. But I need it more than you need to keep it hidden, because unlike you, I can read it, and unlike him—" I jerked my chin at Vane "—I know exactly what we're up against, because I've spent six years drawing its floor plans."

The fox, which had been draped along the windowsill like a piece of discarded finery, opened one amber eye and looked at me with an expression I can only describe as *amused audit*.

"She lies well," the fox said.

Everyone in the room froze except me, because I had already used up my capacity for surprise around day four of following a talking fox, and all that remained was indignation.

"I don't lie," I said. "I *draft*. There's a difference. A lie changes the territory. A draft just shows you the version of it that suits the moment."

"That's the same thing," said Casper Sterling, "with better penmanship."

Below us, the polite voice called again: "Ser Vane. The door. Please. I dislike repeating myself, and I dislike doors even more."

Aldric Vane had not spoken during any of this. He stood by the shuttered window with his hand on his sword and his face turned toward some middle distance where eleven years ago was probably happening again. When he finally looked at me, it was the look of a man appraising a horse with a limp that might be curable.

"If you're lying," he said, "I'll kill you myself, and I won't enjoy it, which will make it worse for both of us."

"Noted," I said. "If I'm lying, you can bill my estate. It consists of one compass and a warrant for my arrest."

"The door," said the voice below, and this time it was not polite at all.

* * *

Here is what happened next, and I relate it with the professional precision of someone trained to record events exactly, because afterward nobody believed me either.

They did not break down the door. That was the frightening part. The lock simply clicked, all by itself, the way a lock does when someone outside has the key—and the Guild of the Sealed Compass issues keys, of course, master keys, iron ones stamped with the sealed compass, and I heard that particular click in my sleep for weeks afterward.

The woman who came in first wore grey, unrelieved, cut close, the uniform of someone who has renounced color as a form of vanity. She was perhaps forty, with a long patient face and eyes that had the terrible quality of genuine kindness—not performed kindness, not weaponized warmth, but the real thing, aimed at you like a lamp. I knew her name before she gave it. Everyone in the guildhouse knew Wren Halloway. She was the inquisitor they sent when they wanted a town to surrender *gratefully*.

"Inquisitor Halloway," Aldric Vane said, and his voice had gone flat and formal, the voice of a man reading his own indictment.

"Ser Vane." She inclined her head with what looked, horribly, like respect. "You've kept yourself hidden. That was wise of you." Her gaze moved past him—to Casper, to the fox, and lastly to me. It stopped on me the way a finger stops on a line of text.

"And you," she said, "are not a knight or a smuggler. Ink under the fingernails. Calluses in the wrong places for a blade. A guild satchel, guild issue, third pattern." She smiled faintly. "You're a cartographer."

"Odile Marchand," I said, because there was no point denying it and every point in controlling it. "Journeyman, Second Class. Indentured to the Guild of the Sealed Compass, Greywater house."

Something moved behind her kind eyes—recognition, and beneath the recognition, appetite, carefully folded flat.

"Marchand," she repeated. "Apprenticed under Corvain Lisse."

"Yes."

"Then you'll know the commission we're seeking." She took a slow step into the room. Her riders filled the doorway behind her, and the lamplight caught the compass-seal pins at their collars, each one a small silver circle with a needle that pointed, always, toward whoever currently owned you. "A document bearing Lisse's private watermark. Stolen property of the Crown, taken from a courier who died of natural causes"—her gaze flicked briefly, meaningfully, toward the direction of the river ford—"at the ford below Thornfell. Hand it over, Marchand, and your indenture ends tonight. Full manumission. The guild seals your papers with honor. You could walk out of here a free woman and draw maps of anywhere you liked."

And there it was. Six years of ink-stained nights, of drawing prison-route after prison-route and telling myself I was only making lines on paper, of watching Master Lisse lock that bottom drawer—all of it offered back to me in one sentence, by a woman with kind eyes, in exchange for an oilcloth bundle I hadn't even seen yet.

Manumission. Freedom, certified and sealed.

I want to report that I hesitated nobly. What I actually felt was a flash of pure, animal temptation, followed immediately by something colder and better: the memory of the file marked *Sterling, E.*, and the rotted string, and the question I'd carried for nine days—*what does a man's granddaughter have that's worth killing a courier over?*—and the answer arriving all at once, whole, the way a coastline resolves when you finally get high enough to see it.

The map wasn't stolen property of the Crown. The map was evidence *against* the Crown. That's why the courier ran. That's why they wanted it quietly. And that meant—

That meant my freedom was being priced exactly correctly, which is to say cheaply, because freedom bought with someone else's chains isn't manumission. It's just a transfer of ownership with nicer paperwork.

"No," I said.

Halloway tilted her head, genuinely curious, the way a scholar is curious about a text that contradicts the canon. "No?"

"No. And moreover, Inquisitor—" and here I did the bravest and stupidest thing of my life, which is saying a great deal, because my life has been a sustained competition between the two "—you should know that I've memorized the guild's commission ledgers. All of them. Including the black ones. Including the one that records a payment, nineteen years ago, from a source I can trace, for a document concerning a soul held in pledge beneath Thornfell. Burn this map, and the payment remains. Kill me, and my memory remains—in whatever head you leave attached. The map is safer in our hands than in yours, because *we* have no reason to make it vanish. You do."

The silence that followed lasted perhaps three seconds and cost me approximately ten years.

Wren Halloway looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her recalculate—not whether to kill me, I think she was always going to consider that, but what killing me would cost. A journeyman who dies quietly is nothing. A journeyman who has announced, in front of witnesses, that she holds ledger-memories of the Crown's black commissions—that's a thread, and pulling threads is how tapestries unravel, and Halloway struck me as a woman who understood tapestries.

"You're bluffing," she said softly.

"I'm a cartographer," I replied. "We don't bluff. We estimate, and we include our margin of error. My error margin, in this case, is your career."

Behind her, one of her riders shifted, hand drifting toward a blade, and Aldric Vane's sword cleared its scabbard with a sound like a sentence being finished, and Casper Sterling—who had been silent through all of this, watchful, her eyes moving between Halloway and me like someone reading two texts against each other—did something I did not understand until much later.

She whistled. Two notes, low, nothing dramatic.

Outside, in the dark of the yard, something answered. Several somethings. The horses began to scream—not in pain, in *opinion*—and I heard the distinctive wet thunder of a dozen hooves deciding simultaneously that they were elsewhere. Riders shouted. A lantern went over. And through the shutter slats I saw the stable doors standing open and the inquisitors' remounts streaming out into the night like water finding its level, and among them, moving low and fast, shapes that were not horses and were not afraid.

Wren Halloway did not turn to look. That frightened me more than anything else that night. She kept her kind eyes on Casper Sterling and said, almost gently:

"The granddaughter of Emrys Sterling. He tried for thirty years to buy back what he sold. I wonder what you'll try." Then, to her riders, without raising her voice: "Mounts first. We ride at dawn. There is more than one road under that mountain."

They withdrew. It was not a retreat; I want to be precise about that. It was a rescheduling.

* * *

Afterward, in the wrecked common room, with the door barred and the horses gone and the fox eating something unspeakable off the hearth, Casper Sterling finally unwrapped the oilcloth and laid the map on the table between us, and I saw it entire for the first time.

Bloodstained—yes. Old blood and new, soaked into the fibers, and I knelt over it with my loupe and my lamp and forgot, for a full minute, that I was a woman who had just gambled her life on a bluff, because the draftsmanship was *magnificent*. Lisse at the height of his powers, or near it—but not Lisse alone. The base survey was older, different hand, heavier line, a style I knew only from the archive fragments of the drowned kingdom's own charters. Someone had built Lisse's black commission on top of a map of the old kingdom itself. Of the roads under the water.

And there, in the lower margin, half obscured by a brown bloom of dried blood: the crest.

I knew crests the way priests know psalms. I leaned closer, and closer, and the room got very quiet around me, and then I said, in the flat voice I use for measurements I don't like:

"This isn't a smuggler's mark. This is a house crest. An old one. Sterling." I looked up. "This map belongs to your family. It may have been *made* by your family. And the blood on it—" I paused, hating the next words, saying them anyway because accuracy is the only integrity I've ever reliably possessed "—is recent enough to belong to the man who carried it. Which means someone with your name and your blood spent his last strength getting this to Thornfell. To you."

Casper Sterling looked at the crest for a long time. The fox stopped eating. Even Aldric Vane leaned in from the wall.

"Well," Casper said at last, and her voice was rough and dry as a winter reed. "Someone had better teach me to read this thing. Marchand. You said you can."

"I said I could read it," I corrected, unpacking my pens with hands that were not entirely steady, and discovering—to my considerable annoyance—that they had stopped shaking the moment I touched the instruments. "Reading it is free. Understanding it costs extra."

"What's the price?"

I thought of Halloway's kind eyes. Of the bottom drawer. Of six years of lines on paper leading to quiet rooms.

"The price," I said, "is that you don't let me go back. Whatever happens—if I weaken, if I bargain, if I get clever and try to trade you all for my papers—you hold me to tonight. Agreed?"

Casper studied me for a moment with those assessing eyes, and then, unexpectedly, she almost smiled.

"Agreed," she said. "Welcome to the party. We're doomed, the company's questionable, and the fox bites."

"I heard that," said the fox, with dignity.

* * *

We left the Drowned Lantern an hour before dawn, taking the back track north toward the sunken causeways, because Halloway had said *there is more than one road under that mountain*, and I had understood her perfectly: she didn't need to catch us today. She needed only to herd us onto ground of her choosing.

I walked at the rear with the map in its oilcloth against my chest, and behind us the waystation shrank into the grey, and somewhere on the wind, faint as a watermark held to the light, I thought I could still smell ink drying brown.

Six years I had drawn the roads other people disappeared on. From tonight, I intended to find out where they went—and, if the old kingdom was generous and my luck held, burn every map that led there.

Starting, I suspected, with the one I was carrying.

It was, I reflected, adjusting my satchel strap and keeping my eyes on Casper Sterling's broad back ahead of me, a truly terrible plan.

I had drafted worse.
