## 6. Roads That Drown

The drowned roads began where the map said they would, which was the first disappointment of the day. I'd been hoping the bloodstained parchment would turn out to be wrong about something—anything—but no. There at the edge of the pines, exactly where Odile's finger had stabbed twice with the confidence of a woman who'd rather be anywhere else, the old causeway slid down into black water like a stairway into a throat.

"Well," I said. "It's very wet."

"It's a causeway," Odile said. "They're built over water. That's the entire concept."

"I'm familiar with the concept. I'm objecting to the execution."

Aldric said nothing, because Aldric had spent three days saying nothing in that particular way he had, the silence of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. He stood at the head of the sunken road with his hand resting on his sword hilt—not gripping it, just resting, the way you'd touch a friend's shoulder before delivering bad news—and looked out across the flooded plain.

The old kingdom hadn't drowned all at once. That was the thing nobody told you. The songs made it sound like a single terrible night, a wave, a curse, done. But what lay before us was slower than that: a whole country gone under by inches, church spires standing in six feet of water, orchards turned to forests of dead gray fingers, and running through all of it, the causeways—the raised stone roads the old kings had built so their processions would never have to touch mud. The water had taken everything except the roads. It had kept them like a promise.

"The beasts are going to hate this," I said.

"You can hear them?" Odile asked. She always asked it the same way, half suspicion and half hunger, like I was a locked drawer she'd found in a house she was robbing.

"Everyone can hear them. They're being extremely loud about it."

And they were. I don't know how to explain my gift to people who think hearing animals means hearing barking. What I heard came up through the soles of my boots first, then through my teeth, then behind my eyes: a chorus without words, or with words underneath the words, hundreds of voices braided together—fish and frogs and things older than either, things that lived in the dark under the ice of the deep pools. And every one of them was singing the same note.

*Go back.*

*Go back go back go back.*

"They say the roads remember," I translated, because apparently I'd become a translator now. "They say we shouldn't walk them."

"Roads don't remember," Odile said.

"Then you explain why the fish are chanting."

She opened her mouth, closed it, and did the thing she did when her professional pride collided with her survival instinct: she pulled out her notebook and started sketching the causeway's stonework, as if documentation were a form of winning the argument.

Aldric finally spoke. "How far to the first waypoint?"

"Half a day," Odile said, not looking up. "There's a tollhouse on the causeway. Dry, if the roof held."

"And behind us?"

I didn't need the gift for this one. For two days there had been a sound following us through Thornfell—not hoofbeats, nothing so honest. A *clean* sound. Boots in rhythm. Inquisitors marched in rhythm; smugglers never did. Rhythm was for people who believed the road belonged to them.

"Half a day," I said. "Maybe less."

"Then we walk the wet road," Aldric said, "and we don't stop walking."

The fox trotted past his boot and onto the causeway first, tail high, as if it had been waiting for someone to make the decision so it wouldn't have to pretend to care about the debate. I'd stopped pretending the fox wasn't coming with us around day two. It had opinions about our campsites. It stole from Odile's rations exclusively, which I chose to interpret as taste.

We went down into the water-country.

* * *

Walking a drowned causeway is like walking the spine of something enormous and patient. On either side the water stretched flat and pewter-colored, broken by rooftops and bell towers and once—an entire windmill, sails still attached, frozen mid-turn like an insect in amber. The air smelled of cold stone and old rot and, strangely, lilacs. Lilacs shouldn't have survived. Nothing should have survived. But the water here wasn't dead water. Things moved beneath it. Long things.

"Don't trail your fingers," I told Odile, who was trailing her fingers.

"I'm measuring the waterline."

"You're baiting something with fingers."

She pulled her hand back with the exaggerated dignity of a scholar who has been persecuted by science. The fox gave me a look that I swear contained the word *finally*.

By midday the chorus changed.

I felt it before I heard it—a tightening behind my sternum, the way a room changes when someone stops talking. The endless *go back* fractured into pieces, and the pieces rearranged themselves, and what came up through the stones was no longer one warning but many voices, urgent, overlapping, tumbling over each other like people shouting through a closed door.

I stopped. Aldric walked two more steps before he noticed, which told me how tired he was; a week ago he'd have noticed before I did.

"Casper?"

"They're not just warning us anymore." I crouched, put my palm flat against the wet stone. The chorus poured up my arm. "They're—naming something. Naming someone." I listened harder, jaw clenched, sweat cold on my neck despite the chill. Animal speech isn't words, mostly. It's weight and shape and intent. But this had been shaped by something human-shaped, something that had passed this way and left an impression the water-creatures could still read. "A woman. Walking behind us. Fast. The beasts are afraid of her."

"Afraid of a woman on foot?" Odile scoffed. Then she caught my expression and stopped scoffing. "What kind of woman do the fish find frightening?"

"One who smells like iron and hymns." I stood, wiping my palm on my coat. "One the eels call *the quiet net*. She doesn't chase. She closes."

"Inquisitor," Aldric said quietly. Not a question. He'd served the crown once; he knew the breed.

"Halloway," I said, and the name tasted borrowed, because I'd only heard it whispered in a waystation by a carter who crossed himself after saying it. Wren Halloway. The Queen's Mercy, some called her, back when mercy was still the court's business. Now they called her Vaelric's hound, and even the hounds of Thornfell, actual four-legged ones, went silent when her name moved through the valleys.

"How far?" Aldric asked.

I reached out again, reluctantly, the way you reach into a hole you suspect contains a snake. The chorus gave me distance the way animals give everything: in fear-units, not miles. Close enough that the perch had stopped spawning in the western pools. Close enough that a heron had abandoned a nest it had tended for three years.

"Close enough that we should stop talking about her," I said.

We stopped talking about her. We walked faster instead.

* * *

The tollhouse appeared out of the mist in the late afternoon, squatting on a widening of the causeway like a toad on a log. Its roof had mostly held. Its door had not held at all—it hung off one hinge, and beyond it the single room showed a hearth, a collapsed cot, and forty years of bird droppings arranged with an almost architectural ambition.

"We're not stopping," I said.

"We're stopping," Aldric said. "You haven't slept properly in two days. Neither have I. Odile is drawing maps in her sleep, which I'm told is a bad sign."

"It's dedication," Odile said.

"It's a bad sign," Aldric and I said together, and then glared at each other for having agreed.

Here is what nobody tells you about traveling with people: it ruins you for solitude. I had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being alone—alone in crowds, alone in partnerships, alone in bed with my boots on in case I needed to leave through a window. Alone was safe. Alone was *mine*. And yet here I was, arguing about rest schedules with a disgraced knight and an indentured cartographer, and worse, feeling something adjacent to relief that they were there. It was like discovering a splinter you'd been carrying so long you'd mistaken it for bone.

We made camp in the tollhouse. Aldric insisted on watch rotation, I insisted on sleeping near the door because I always slept near the door, and Odile insisted on a fire until Aldric pointed out that smoke over open water is visible for a mile in every direction, at which point she insisted on a fire *anyway*, and lost, and sulked magnificently over cold rations.

The fox sat on the windowsill facing west, toward the way we'd come, and did not move for hours.

That should have been my second warning. The chorus was the first. The fox's stillness was the second. I ignored both, because I was tired, and tired people believe tomorrow will behave like yesterday.

I woke to the chorus screaming.

Not singing. Screaming—a wall of sound pouring up through the causeway stones, every creature in the water-country at once, and beneath it, cutting through, a sound I knew from nightmares I hadn't had yet: the particular wet crunch of boots leaving a boat.

I was on my feet with a knife in my hand before my eyes finished opening. "Aldric. *Aldric.*"

He was already moving—I'll give him that, the man slept like a cat that had been knighted. No lantern. Good instincts. We met in the middle of the dark room, and outside, torchlight bloomed on the water, five points of flame where there should have been none, reflected double on the flat flood so it looked like ten men arriving, then twenty.

"Back door?" he murmured.

"There's no back door. It's a tollhouse."

"Then we improvise."

Odile was awake, clutching her satchel of maps to her chest like a child. "How many?"

"At least five," I said. The chorus fed me the rest: five men in iron and wool, moving with that hated rhythm, and one figure who made no sound at all, whose footsteps the water refused to report, whom the beasts could only describe as a *shape like a closed door*. Halloway. She'd sent her riders wide through the shallows and come at the tollhouse from three sides, patient as arithmetic.

"She knew we'd stop here," Odile whispered. "It's the only dry ground for eight miles. It's in *my own notes*—" Her face went white in the dark. "I mapped these routes for the guild. I handed them the tollhouses on a plate."

Later, I'd learn to tell her that her guilt had bought us thirty seconds, because she knew exactly which wall of the tollhouse was thinnest. But later hadn't arrived yet. Later was still a luxury.

The door exploded inward.

* * *

Fighting in a room the size of a pantry is not fighting; it's violence with furniture. The first rider came through the doorway torch-first, which was generous of him, because it meant Aldric could see him well enough to break his wrist with the flat of a sword. The man screamed and dropped the torch, and the torch landed in forty years of bird droppings, which, it turns out, burn beautifully. The room filled with light and a smell I will be describing to healers for years.

Two more came through the door behind the first. I got my knife into the thigh of one—he folded sideways into the hearth, which was not lit, thank every small god—and then a gauntleted fist found the side of my head and the world went white and sideways and full of bells.

Through the ringing, the chorus poured into me, unbidden, furious. The water-things were screaming directions. *Left. Behind you. The tall one, the tall one, the TALL ONE—*

I ducked. A blade meant for my throat took a splinter out of the doorframe above me instead. The tall rider—Halloway's lieutenant, some carved-from-frozen-beef specimen—recovered fast, too fast, and came again, and I did the math every smuggler does in that moment, the quick sad arithmetic of reach and steel, and the answer was: I lose.

I didn't lose.

Aldric happened to him.

I don't mean fought him. I mean *happened*. The disgraced knight came out of the smoke like a debt collector, and for about four seconds I understood why the old kingdom had trusted men like him with its borders. He didn't duel the lieutenant; he removed him from the conversation—one parry that turned into a shoulder-check that turned into the lieutenant meeting the doorframe face-first with a sound like a dropped melon. The man slid down the wall and stayed down, breathing but philosophically absent.

Which left Aldric open to the fourth rider, the one neither I nor the chorus had flagged, because the fourth rider hadn't come through the door at all. He'd come through the collapsed section of the east wall, quiet as a confession.

"Aldric!" My voice cracked on it.

He turned—too slow, burdened by honor and fifty pounds of mail—and the rider's blade took him across the ribs. Not deep. Not shallow. The ugly middle distance that bleeds a lot and kills slowly, which is the worst place a wound can live.

Aldric grunted the way other men scream, pivoted inside the blade's retreat, and put his sword through the rider's knee. Then they were both on the ground, and I was on the fourth rider with my knife and my fear, and Odile—Odile Marchand, cartographer, coward by her own repeated public testimony—swung the tollhouse's iron scale-weight into the back of the man's helmet with both hands and a sound like a cathedral bell.

He stopped participating in events.

Silence. Smoke. The torch guttering in its nest of ancient filth. Outside on the water, the fifth torch was retreating—fast strokes, no rhythm now, panic breaking formation—and beside it, at the edge of the torchlight, a shape on the surface of the flood that might have been a woman standing on the water, watching, or might have been shadow and wishful thinking. The chorus hissed around her absence like wind around a closed door.

Then that was gone too, and it was just us: three people and a fox, in a burning bird-latrine, breathing hard.

* * *

Aldric sat propped against the hearth while I peeled his mail back from the wound, and he made the specific mistake of looking down at it.

"That's—" he began.

"Don't narrate it," I said. "Narrating makes it real. We're pretending it's a scratch until I finish stitching."

"It's a scratch," he said loyally, and then went gray as the water outside when I poured the spirits in.

Odile held the needle-thread steady for me because my hands weren't as steady as I wanted them to be, and neither of us mentioned that. The fox sat on Aldric's other side, unnervingly close, and watched the stitching with the focus of a surgeon auditing a colleague.

"You stepped in front of it," I said, somewhere around the fourth stitch. "The blade was for me. You know it was for me. You had the door covered; you could've let it come and taken him clean."

"Yes," Aldric said.

"That's not strategy. That's not even good tactics. You're the one who knows how to fight; I'm the one who's supposed to be expendable. That's the whole arrangement."

He was quiet long enough that I thought he'd pass out, which would have been convenient for both of us. When he spoke, his voice was low and rough, the voice of a man reading from a document he'd signed long ago.

"When I broke my oath, everyone I was sworn to protect died anyway. Every one. Because I stood aside. Because I calculated." His eyes found mine, and they were clear, damn him, clear and tired and certain. "I have done the mathematics of standing aside, Casper Sterling. I will spend the rest of my life not doing it. You may as well build your arrangements around that."

I looked back down at the wound and pulled the next stitch tighter than strictly necessary, because the alternative was letting him see my face, and my face was doing something I hadn't authorized.

"Fine," I said. "But the next time you take a blade for me, I'm billing your estate."

"Noted," he said, and almost smiled, and then hissed as I tied off.

Odile sat back on her heels, ink-stained fingers shaking, and looked at the unconscious rider, the broken door, the blood on the floor that was mostly not ours. "They'll regroup," she said. "Halloway doesn't lose people like this and write it off. She'll close the causeway ahead of us. Every crossing, every weir, every tollhouse between here and the mountain—she'll salt them all."

"Then we don't use her crossings," I said.

"The causeways are the crossings."

"No." I looked out the shattered doorway at the drowned kingdom, at the black water lying flat and listening, at the bell tower of some drowned church standing sentinel a mile out, its bronze bell green with forty years of patience. The chorus had gone quiet again, but not empty. It was waiting. Everything out there was waiting, and I suddenly understood that it had been waiting for a long time—for someone who could hear it, maybe, though I flattered myself, or maybe just for anyone foolish enough to answer.

"The beasts said the roads remember," I said. "So we ask the roads for another way. Under the water, if we have to."

Odile stared at me. "You want to walk into the flood."

"I want to not die on a road designed for tax collectors. Yes. Into the flood."

The fox rose from Aldric's side, stretched, and jumped down onto the causeway stones outside. It looked back at us once—me specifically, me specifically and pointedly—and then trotted a few paces along the causeway and stopped at a spot where the water lapped over the stones, and sat, and waited.

Like it knew the way.

Like it had always known the way.

"Oh, I hate that," Odile said.

"Yeah," I said, and picked up my pack, and followed the fox down to the water's edge, where the drowned road went on into the dark, and something far below us, in the deep places of the old kingdom, turned over in its sleep and began, very slowly, to sing my name.
