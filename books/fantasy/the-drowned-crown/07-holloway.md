## 7. Holloway

I have been called many things in my service to the Crown. Zealot. Hound. That charming little epithet the smugglers of Thornfell use — *the Winter Widow*, because wherever I ride, someone's husband stops coming home. I confess I've never minded that one. It has a certain music to it.

But the word that stings, the one that makes me pause over my morning tea like a woman finding a hair in it, is *kind*.

They say it in the villages we pass through. Not to my face — no one is fool enough for that — but behind shutters, in the low voices of women drawing water. *She used to be kind, they say. Before. She delivered babies once, they say. She sat with the dying.* All true. All useless now, the way a child's shoes are useless after the child grows. Kindness was a garment I wore in a colder country, and I have not gone back for it.

Here is what those village gossips do not understand, and what I will set down plainly, because this account may be all that survives me: kindness is not a virtue. It is an economy. You spend warmth where warmth buys safety, and you hoard it where it buys nothing. In the plague camps of my youth, I learned that a gentle hand and a firm hand cost the same to extend, but only one of them keeps the fever from spreading. The Crown taught me arithmetic. Vaelric taught me what the sum was *for*.

And the sum, dear reader of whatever future finds this page, is spring.

* * *

We made camp that night in the ruins of a tollhouse on the old causeway road, three days behind the fugitives and losing ground. My riders grumbled about it — Sergeant Brakke loudest, as ever, a man whose complaints were so reliable you could navigate by them. I let them grumble. A soldier who complains aloud is a soldier still attached to the world, and attachment is useful. It gives you something to promise them.

I sat apart with the dispatch case and read the King's letter again by lamplight, though I had long since memorized it.

*The binding holds while the soul endures,* he had written, in that beautiful, patient hand of his, a hand that had signed pardons and death warrants in equal measure and never once trembled. *While it endures, the floods obey the old covenant: they rise for no army, part for no invader, drown only the faithless. But the soul fades, Wren. It has faded for twenty years, kept as it is kept, and when it gutters out the covenant dies with it. Then the thaw-waters will rise as waters do — without loyalty, without mercy, without respect for borders drawn by men who flattered themselves.*

*The free cities believe their beacons protect them. Beacons are prayers, Wren. Prayers require someone listening.*

*I am going to give them a listening god. And you are going to bring me the map before anyone else teaches them to pray louder.*

There it was, laid out as plainly as a recipe. The queen-soul — stolen, purchased, *sold*, the word the dying courier had gasped at the ford before my quarry took him away into the dark — was not a trophy. It was a keystone. While it endured in the vault beneath the mountain, the old covenant held: the drowned roads stayed drowned, the flood-plains stayed passable, and the free cities slept behind a wall of water that remembered whose side it was on.

Vaelric meant to move the armies across at spring thaw. Everyone assumed he would wait for the floods to recede. Everyone assumed the floods were weather.

They were not weather. They were a treaty. And treaties, like everything else in this world, can be renegotiated by whoever holds the collateral.

I understood why he needed the map found quickly. What I understood less well — what kept me awake past my watch, staring at the tollhouse rafters — was why the map existed at all. Someone had drawn the way to the vault. Someone had carried it out of the court, bleeding, and died pressing it into the hands of a smuggler girl who wanted nothing to do with any of it. Treason has a shape, and this shape was too desperate to be ambition. This was a man paying back a debt.

I did not yet know whose debt. I would learn. The mountain tells you everything eventually, if you interrogate it long enough. That is the one thing it has in common with people.

* * *

Brakke found me at dawn, or what passed for dawn under the cloud deck — a gray brightening, the sky deciding against us again. He had a prisoner. Of course he had a prisoner. Brakke collected prisoners the way other men collected debts, indiscriminately and with relish.

"Goat herder," he said. "Caught him driving his herd off our line of march. Says he didn't know."

"Did he know?"

"He knew something." Brakke grinned. He had a gap in his teeth that made the grin worse than a scowl. "His goats knew something too. Kept looking up the slope toward the high passes. Goats don't look uphill unless something's up there."

I went to see the man myself. This was policy, not mercy — I'll be clear about that, because I know how these accounts get twisted. You cannot delegate interrogation entirely, because delegated cruelty drifts. It becomes sport. Sport becomes sloppiness. I have watched inquisitors in the southern provinces slide down that slope until they were torturing farmers for the pleasure of it and calling it loyalty, and their intelligence was garbage, because terrified men tell you anything and anything is worthless. Fear is a solvent. It dissolves the truth along with everything else.

So I sat across from the goat herder on a fallen lintel, poured him tea from my own kettle, and asked him his name.

"Tomas," he said. His hands shook around the cup. Old hands, cracked with cold, honest hands. I had known ten thousand of them.

"Tomas. I'm going to tell you exactly how this goes, because I respect you enough not to waste your time." I always said some version of this. It worked better than threats, and it had the additional advantage of being true. "You drove your goats across our line either by accident or by design. If by accident, you'll tell me what your goats saw, and you'll go home, and I'll pay you for the disruption. If by design — if someone paid you to watch the passes, count riders, carry word — then you'll tell me who, and you'll go home richer, and I'll consider the matter closed. What I will not abide is being lied to slowly. Do you understand the difference between these outcomes?"

He understood. They always understood. He looked at his tea for a long moment, and then he said, in the voice of a man setting down a weight he'd carried too far: "The girl. The beast-speaker. People say she's got the map. The one the old man bled out carrying."

"And who says this?"

"The fox." He laughed, a short unhappy sound. "That's the joke of it, Inquisitor. Nobody says it. The animals say it. Whole valley heard it from the crows by now. There's a fox walks with her, and the crows say the fox talks, and the fox says she carries the blood-map to the court, and—" He stopped himself, which was interesting. Men who stop themselves mid-sentence are men with loyalties.

"And?"

"And nothing. That's all I know. Except—" He hesitated again, and I waited, because waiting is half the trade. "Except the beasts are saying something else too. Something that doesn't make sense. They're saying the mountain's waking up. That the water under it is turning over. My goats wouldn't graze the lower meadows yesterday. Wouldn't go near the streams." He looked up at me, and there was fear in his face, but not the fear of me. That was rarer, and more informative. "You're Crown soldiers. You should know the land itself is counting days."

I thanked him, paid him, and sent him home. Brakke objected — *she knows things, let me work her* — and I told him what I always told him: that a goat herder who cooperates freely today will cooperate freely tomorrow, and one we break today will lie to us forever after. He sulked. He was good at sulking. It was nearly his only skill besides riding.

But I will admit here, in the privacy of ink, that the man's words followed me up the trail that day. *The water under the mountain is turning over.* I had grown up in the free cities. I had been a midwife's daughter in Corvess before the war, before the plague, before the Crown found me sorting the living from the dead with steady hands and promoted me into a life where the sorting never stopped. I knew the old stories the way everyone did — the drowned queen, the covenant of waters, the beacons lit every generation to keep the memory alive. Children's stories. We told them the way you tell children not to swallow stones: not because the danger was real, but because obedience was.

Except the beasts were not telling children's stories. Beasts do not allegorize. When the crows said the mountain was waking, the crows meant it the way they meant *hawk* or *winter*: as a fact with claws in it.

* * *

We found the ambush site on the fourth day. Or rather, the crows found it for us — a wheeling black argument above the treeline, and Brakke, to his credit, read it before I did.

Holloway's riders had caught the fugitives at a bend in the sunken causeway two nights prior. I could reconstruct most of it from the ground: crossbow bolts spent in a fan against the stone, blood frozen in a drag-line, and then — and this was the part I made my sergeant describe twice — the wolves.

"Wolves came off the ridge," Brakke said. He was not a fanciful man, which made his report worse. "Dozens. Came right through our line like we weren't there. Took Halloway's horse's throat out and went for the girl's attackers instead. Not us. Them. Like they knew the difference." He spat. "Animals don't know the difference."

"No," I said quietly. "Animals don't. Speakers do."

Because that was the thing the Crown's files had on Casper Sterling, smuggler, thief, occasional courier of goods the tariff schedules preferred not to name: *suspected gift, class uncertain, magnitude unknown.* The files were thin, because forgettable people leave thin files, and she had built a life on being forgettable. But I had read the older files, the sealed ones, the ones from the purges. Beast-speakers ran in certain bloodlines of Thornfell, and the Crown had burned most of those bloodlines a generation ago for precisely the crime I was now looking at written in wolf-tracks and frozen blood: the crime of making the wild country take sides.

She had turned the wolves. She had stood in front of soldiers with crossbows and reached into the oldest thing in the mountains and asked it for help, and it had come.

I should have felt triumph. The target was confirmed; the map was real; the trail was hot. Instead I felt something colder settle in my chest, and it took me a mile of riding to name it.

It was recognition.

I knew what it cost to reach past yourself for power you'd sworn never to use. I had done it once, in the plague year, when the magistrates ordered the sick quarter sealed with the healthy inside it, and I had stood in the gate with a torch in my hand and understood that the man giving the order was wrong, catastrophically, unforgivably wrong, and that I would light the fire anyway, because the alternative was becoming the kind of person who decided which orders deserved obedience — and everyone who thought that way, I had been taught, ended up a tyrant or a corpse. So I lit the fire. Two hundred people. I counted them afterward. I have always counted.

Kindness, I told myself then, is what gets you killed. Conviction is what lets you live with the arithmetic.

Casper Sterling had just done the opposite of what I had done. She had refused the arithmetic. She had spent herself recklessly, exposed her gift to every crow and wolf and inquisitor within fifty miles, for the sake of three strangers and a dead man's errand. It was strategically insane. It was the act of someone who had not yet learned what things cost.

And some traitorous chamber of my heart, the one with the midwife's daughter still living in it, had watched those wolf-tracks and thought: *good.*

I put that chamber back in its box. I am practiced at it. But I record the moment here, because an account that hides its cracks is a forgery, and I promised myself this document would be honest even where I could not be.

* * *

That evening, a hawk came down to my gauntlet out of a sky the color of dirty pewter, and brought with it the King's seal and a single sheet.

*Wren,* it began — first names now, always first names, the intimacy of a man who owned you and wished you to feel it as affection. *The beacon at Corvess went dark last night. Marrowdale follows within the week. Despair spreads faster than plague, and despair serves us: a city that believes its god is dead will not resist the living king. But despair cuts both ways. If the girl reaches the vault, if the soul is restored, the covenant renews and everything I have built drowns with the thaw. You understand what I ask of you. You have always understood. Bring me the map. Bring me the girl. The mountain must not remember her name.*

I read it twice. I fed the hawk from my fist and felt its small, terrible heart beating against my glove, quick as a clock counting down.

Corvess. My city. Where I had learned to sort the living from the dead, and where the beacons had burned every spring of my childhood, orange against the harbor fog, and my mother had lifted me up to see them and said, *as long as those burn, the water remembers us, little wren, and the water has a longer memory than any king.*

She had died believing it. Most of Corvess had died believing it, in the end. And now the last beacons were going out one by one like eyes closing, and the King proposed to fill that darkness with himself.

Here is the confession this chapter exists to make, and I make it without flinching: I believed him. I believed him the way the drowning believe in rope. Because I had seen what the world was without a strong hand on it — the plague camps, the burning quarter, the free cities fat and complacent behind their wall of borrowed miracles, praying to a drowned woman who had not answered in twenty years. Vaelric offered order. Real order, enforced order, order that did not depend on the goodwill of ghosts. Every horror I had committed, I had committed in service of that offer, and a person cannot repudiate the offer without repudiating every step that brought her to hear it. I was too far down the road to ask whether the road was built on anything.

Wasn't I?

The hawk shifted on my glove. Below me, the causeway stretched south toward the drowned kingdom, and somewhere ahead of it, a smuggler girl walked with a knight and a cartographer and a talking fox, carrying a dead man's blood toward a vault full of somebody's stolen soul. Carrying, the beasts insisted, an inheritance.

Inheritance. The word snagged in me like a fishhook. Files said orphan. Files said no kin. Files, I was beginning to suspect, said a great many convenient things.

I turned south. Behind me, faint under the wind, I could almost swear the rivers sounded different than they had a week ago — deeper, busier, like water turning over in a sleep it was about to wake from.

Three days' hard ride to the Gate of Teeth. I meant to arrive first.

I always arrive first. It is the one kindness I have never managed to give up: I get there before the killing starts, so that perhaps, if the arithmetic allows, I can find a reason not to start it at all.
