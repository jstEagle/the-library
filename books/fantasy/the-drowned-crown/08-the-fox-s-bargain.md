## 8. The Fox's Bargain

The mountain fell on us at the worst possible moment, which is the only moment mountains ever choose.

One heartbeat I was hauling Odile by the collar out of the drip-line of the causeway arch, and the next the world went from grey to black with a sound like a god clearing his throat. Dust filled my mouth, my eyes, my lungs. Somewhere behind me Aldric was shouting my name — or Odile's name, or possibly a prayer; with Aldric it was hard to tell the difference — and then the shouting stopped, muffled, distant, as if someone had closed a door between us.

Which, in fairness, someone had. Several tons of someone.

I sat very still in the dark and took inventory, because that is rule one of being buried alive: panic spends air faster than fear does, and I have never been able to afford either.

Ribs: bruised, not broken. Left arm: scraped but obedient. Map: against my breastbone, wrapped in oilcloth, exactly where it had been since a dying man made it my problem. Air: enough for an hour if I didn't talk, which was going to be difficult, because talking to things that shouldn't be able to answer me is the one habit I've never managed to break.

"Hello?" I said to the dark. So much for an hour.

My gift has rules. I've spent thirty years learning them the hard way, the way you learn anything when nobody will teach you: beasts speak in wants, mostly. Hunger, fear, territory, the dull liturgy of survival. A mule thinks about its feet. A crow thinks about your eyes. Nothing thinks about tomorrow, and nothing — this is the important part — nothing has ever spoken *to* me. They speak around me, past me, through me. I am a window they don't know is glass.

So when a voice came out of the darkness of my collapsed tunnel and said, "You're bleeding on the queen's road," I did what any sensible smuggler would do.

I screamed, drew my knife, and cut myself on my own sleeve in the process.

"There's no need for that," said the voice. It was dry and unhurried, the voice of something that had watched a great many people die and found most of them repetitive. "The blade won't reach me, and even if it did, I've been dead longer than your family has been disappointing."

I knew that voice. Of course I did. It had been following me for weeks — a rust-red fox that appeared at the edge of every campfire, every ford, every bad decision, sitting at a polite distance with its tail curled around its feet like a gentleman waiting to be introduced. I'd told myself it was after my rations. I'd told myself a lot of things.

"You," I said.

"Me," it agreed. Somewhere to my left, two points of pale light opened in the blackness. Eyes. Of course. Because the dark wasn't frightening enough on its own terms. "Though you've been calling me 'that damned fox' for six weeks, so perhaps we should establish better terms."

"How are you talking? Beasts don't—" I stopped. My heart was doing something embarrassing against my ribs. "Beasts don't talk."

"No," said the fox. "They don't. Which should tell you what I am, if you're half as clever as you pretend to be when you're haggling over brandy."

I pressed my back against the rubble wall. The stone was cold and wept meltwater down my spine, which was almost comforting, because at least water behaves according to rules I understand.

"A spirit," I said. "The old kind. The kind the free cities burned their stories about."

"The kind the free cities *forgot* about," the fox corrected, and there was something under the dryness there, something old and bruised. "Burning came later. Forgetting was easier. Nobody guards what they've decided never existed." The eyes blinked, slow as a cat's. "You remember, though. You hear them. Every beast between here and the sea has been gossiping about the woman who listens. Do you know how tedious it is, hearing your own name whispered by magpies across three valleys?"

"I never told them my name."

"You never had to. Names travel underground, little smuggler. Down here especially." A pause, and when the voice came again it had lost some of its amusement, like a coat taken off indoors. "Down here, names are currency. And yours has just come into circulation."

I thought about that. I thought about the crows in chapter three of my misfortunes — the ones who'd told me about a court beneath the mountain, a soul held in pledge, a usurper's seal pressed into wax older than any kingdom I'd ever robbed. I thought about the goat, poor mad thing, screaming *the crown remembers* until its owner led it away. And I thought about the map against my chest, warm now, though oilcloth holds no heat.

"What do you want?" I asked. Because everything wants something. That was the first law of my gift, and I'd built my whole life on it.

"Ah," said the fox. "There she is. The negotiator. Good. This will go faster if you're insufferable rather than terrified."

"I can manage both simultaneously. It's a professional skill."

"I know. I've been watching you haggle with marmots." The eyes drifted closer, or seemed to; in the dark, distance is a rumor. "Here is your situation. Your knight and your cartographer are digging. They'll dig for hours before they reach you, and when they do, they'll find either a living smuggler or a very tidy corpse, depending on whether the second collapse comes before or after. There *will* be a second collapse. The mountain is settling along the old fault, and the old fault runs beneath the queen's road, and the queen's road does not forgive interruptions. Also — and I mention this only because you seem fond of knowing the full price of things — Wren Halloway's riders are four hours behind you and moving faster than a man with a hole in his shoulder can dig."

My stomach dropped through the floor of the mountain and kept going. "Aldric's hurt?"

"He'll live. He's too stubborn to do otherwise, and your cartographer has discovered she's braver than her debts. They make an odd pair. She's currently lecturing him about load-bearing rubble while he ignores her, which I believe is how all healthy partnerships begin." The fox sat, and I heard it — actually heard it, the soft double-click of haunches meeting stone. "But they cannot dig fast enough. Not alone."

"And you can get me out."

"I can take you out. Through ways that aren't for human feet. Ways that haven't opened since the queen wore her crown." The pale eyes held steady on mine, and I felt the strange vertigo of being *seen* by something that wasn't a beast and wasn't a person and had never once, in all my life of listening, asked me for anything — until now. "But the ways open for bargains, not favors. That is their law, and I am bound by their law more tightly than you could imagine."

"Then name your price."

"That," said Ashen — though it hadn't given me the name yet, and wouldn't for another hour, and I would spend that hour wishing I'd never learned it — "is the difficulty."

* * *

We negotiated in the dark for what felt like a season and was probably forty minutes.

It went badly, in the way that negotiations go badly when one party knows everything and the other party knows the map is warm against her chest and the mountain is groaning overhead like an old man getting out of a chair.

"Gold," I offered. "I know where Vaelric's tax convoys cross the Thornfell shallows. Unmarked. I could make you rich."

"I was rich once," said the fox. "Rich in a way you couldn't count. It bought nothing that mattered."

"My silence, then. Whatever you are, whatever you're doing, I'll never speak of it."

"You already speak of it constantly, to crows and goats and marmots. Besides—" and here the voice did something terrible and gentle, "—you'll forget me soon enough anyway. Everyone does. It's practically your motto. *Never be remembered.* I've watched you enforce it on yourself for years. You're very good at it. It's the cruelest thing you own."

I didn't answer that. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have cracked my voice, and a smuggler whose voice cracks might as well hang up her boots.

The mountain groaned again. Closer, this time. A trickle of pebbles ran down the rubble pile beside me like sand through an hourglass, and I understood suddenly, viscerally, that the hourglass was running for me specifically.

"Then tell me what you want," I said, "and stop making me guess. I'm good at guessing prices. It's my trade. But you keep the ledger shut, and shut ledgers frighten me more than inquisitors do."

For a long moment the fox said nothing, and the silence had weight, the way a held breath has weight.

"A promise," it said finally. "Not yet named."

"That's not a price. That's a blank check."

"Yes," said Ashen, agreeably. "Exactly that."

"No. Absolutely not. I've seen what blank checks cost. Half the tragedies in the old songs start with someone saying *a promise not yet named* and the other half start with someone saying *what's the worst that could happen.*"

"Most of those songs get the details wrong," said the fox. "I was there for several of them." The eyes moved closer, close enough now that I could see the shape around them — the sharp face, the ears laid back, the whole small body of it sitting composed in the ruin of a fallen kingdom's road as though the rubble were a drawing room. "Listen to me, Casper Sterling, because I will say this plainly and only once. I have served a dead queen for twenty years. I have watched her name rot, her roads drown, her loyalists die one by one with her debt unpaid in their mouths. The last of them died eight days ago at a river ford, and put his blood and his hope into the hands of a stranger because he had no one left to give it to."

The cold went through me then, all the way through, colder than the meltwater.

"You knew him."

"I carried messages for him for longer than you've been alive. He was a fool and a saint and he smelled of pipe smoke and regret, and he loved a granddaughter he was never allowed to meet." The fox's voice didn't waver, but something underneath it did, the way ice does before it goes. "He asked me, at the end, to watch over the map. I have done so. Now the map asks something of me, and I ask something of you, and the asking requires trust, and trust—" the pale eyes blinked, "—trust is the one coin you have never once spent. I've watched you hoard it your whole life. Solitude as savings. Isolation as interest. A fortune of loneliness, Casper, and not one copper of it ever spent on another living soul."

"That's not fair."

"No," it agreed. "It isn't. Fairness stopped being available to any of us some time ago. What remains is necessity." It rose, and in the dark its shadow seemed larger than a fox's shadow had any right to be, larger than the tunnel, larger perhaps than the mountain, though I was frightened and frightened eyes exaggerate. "Give me your promise. Unnamed. Unpriced. To be called in when I name it — and I swear to you by the drowned crown and the queen it belonged to that I will not call it lightly, nor cruelly, nor for anything that would make your grandfather's ghost ashamed of both of us."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I dig you out the slow way, alongside your friends, and we take our chances with the collapse and the inquisitors, and I grieve another twenty years." A pause. "I would prefer not to grieve another twenty years."

Somewhere above me, the mountain exhaled. Pebbles sang down the rubble. The air had gone thin and mineral-tasting, and I thought — absurdly, in the last quiet moment I would have for a long time — of the marmot from the winter run, the chatty one, telling me about its cache of seeds as if seeds were the whole of what mattered. Maybe they were. Maybe wanting simple things was the only honest life available, and I had traded mine away at a river ford for a bloodstained map and a dead man's last words.

*Return what was sold.*

I hadn't known then what was sold. I was beginning to. And beginning-to-know felt like standing at the edge of deep water, which is fitting, because everything since the ford has felt like that.

"If you betray me," I said, "I will spend eternity haunting you. I'm very annoying when I want to be. Ask anyone who's tried to remember me."

"Oh, I intend to remember you," said Ashen. "That's rather the point."

I put out my hand into the dark, palm up, the way you offer a hand to a dog or a stranger or a god, and felt the impossible warmth of a small rough tongue touch my knuckles, once, formally, like a seal pressed into wax.

"It's done," said the fox. "Now hold still, and don't scream — you'll want to, everyone does — and whatever you see, remember that the ways beneath the mountain belong to the queen, and while I walk them, so do you."

The darkness split open.

Not light — something older than light, a seam in the stone unfolding like a paper flower, and beyond it a corridor that breathed. Walls veined with water that flowed *upward*. Roots hanging like the rigging of sunken ships. And everywhere, everywhere, the murmur of beasts — thousands of them, centuries of them, their voices braided together into a low tide-sound that I realized with a lurch was not murmur at all but *singing*, a song without words that nonetheless contained a name, repeated, mournful, patient:

*a queen, a queen, a queen beneath the water.*

"Walk," said Ashen, and stepped through ahead of me, and its coat in that light was not red at all but ash-grey, ash-white, the color of something burned down to what it truly was.

I walked. Rule two of being buried alive, after all: when a door opens, go through it, and ask questions on the other side.

Behind me, with a sound like a held breath released, the tunnel I'd been trapped in sealed itself forever — and somewhere far above, faint as memory, I heard Odile shout my name, and Aldric answer, and I pressed my hand to the warm map against my chest and followed a dead queen's fox down into the dark, carrying a promise I had already given and would not understand until the day I paid it.
