## 9. Salt and Snowmelt

I have always believed that the fastest way to ruin a good lie is to tell it in a beautiful place. Beauty makes people honest. It's a design flaw in the world.

The chapel proved me right.

We found it at dusk on the ninth day of the drowned roads — a chapel half-swallowed by the old flood, its bell tower leaning like a drunk against a friend, its nave filled to the second step with water so clear it looked like glass laid over stone. Salt crusts bloomed along the arches white as candlewax. Someone had loved this place once; someone had built it with care, carved saints into the lintels whose faces the water had worn smooth and gentle, so that every saint looked like they were about to forgive you something.

Which was unfortunate, because I had come in carrying enough guilt to fill the font twice over.

"We'll shelter here," Aldric said, in the tone of a man who had decided and would brook no cartographical objection. He was favoring his left side where Halloway's riders had opened him up at the causeway ambush — the wound bound now, but bound badly, by my hands, because Casper's were busy holding a fox and Aldric refused to sit still long enough for anyone to do it properly. "High ground above the waterline. One entrance. Defensible."

"One entrance also means one exit," I said, because I am contractually incapable of not pointing that out.

"Then we'd best hope nothing comes in."

"Hope," I said, "is what people use instead of maps."

Casper looked at me across the flooded nave, firelight from the little driftwood blaze catching the edge of her face, and said nothing at all. She had gotten very good at saying nothing at all. It was, I suspected, her native language. The fox sat in the crook of her arm like a stole made of judgment, and its amber eyes found mine and stayed there a beat too long, the way they always did, as if Ashen could smell whatever I wasn't saying.

Which, as it turned out that night, was quite a lot.

* * *

We made camp on the raised chancel, which felt faintly blasphemous and entirely practical, which is my favorite combination. Aldric cleaned his sword. Casper went out into the shallows and stood there for a long while with her boots off and her skirts kilted up, head tilted, listening to whatever the water had to say. She came back once, dripping to the knee, and reported: "The fish are gossiping. Nothing follows us. Yet." Then she lay down with her back to us both, which was her way of participating in the group.

That left me and Aldric and the fire and the silence, and I have never in my life met a silence I couldn't talk my way out of, except that night, when it took me three hours even to try.

Here is what you should know about me, since apparently I'm telling everything now: I did not stumble into Vaelric's guild. I climbed into it. There was an examination. I placed third of four hundred. There was a ceremony. There was a pin. I wore the pin.

Odile Marchand, indentured cartographer, debt of two hundred crowns and eleven years' service, maker of fine maps for discerning clients. That's what the guild seal said under my name. What it didn't say was who the discerning clients were, or what the maps were *for*.

"Aldric," I said finally, to the fire, because looking at him directly seemed unwise.

"Hm." He didn't stop cleaning the sword. He never stopped cleaning the sword. I think if you cut him open you'd find whetstone where the heart should be, and I mean that as a compliment.

"The map. The courier's map. The watermark — the crowned fish over crossed keys — I told you I recognized it."

"You did."

"I recognized it," I said, "because I drew it."

The sword stopped moving. That was the only sign he gave me. In the shallows outside, water lapped the drowned pews with a sound like slow applause, or slow condemnation; with chapels it's hard to tell the difference.

"Not that map," I said quickly. "Not the blood-map. But the base survey. The routes. The — " My voice did something humiliating in the middle, a small crack, and I hated it, and I kept going anyway, because if I stopped I would never start again. "Vaelric keeps prisoners, Aldric. Not just the queen's soul in the vault. People. Loyalists. Anyone who remembered her reign too fondly, anyone who asked questions, anyone whose land he wanted. And someone had to chart the routes between the prisons. The transfer roads. Which cells flood in spring thaw and which don't. Which tunnels the guards can abandon and let the water do the work."

I pulled my knees up to my chest like a child, which is exactly what I felt like.

"There's a prison under Lake Verreille," I said. "It doesn't have bars. It has a sluice gate. When Vaelric is done with someone, the gate opens, and the lake closes over them, and the guild marks the site on the master charts as *depth sounding, unrevised*. That's what we called them. Unrevised soundings." I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "I mapped forty-one unrevised soundings before I ran. Forty-one. I used to tell myself I didn't know what they were. For the first year I genuinely didn't. And then I knew, and I kept drawing, because the alternative was the sluice gate, and I — "

I ran out of words. Me. Odile Marchand, who once talked a border guard out of a full search of her satchel by delivering a fifteen-minute lecture on the migratory patterns of eels. Out of words, sitting in a drowned chapel, watching a disgraced knight decide whether I was worth forgiving or whether he should simply open the door and let the cold finish me.

"Casper knows," I said. "She must know. She heard the fox say the word *inheritance* and she still let me walk beside her, and I keep waiting for her to leave me at some crossroads with my share of nothing, and she hasn't, and I don't understand why, and it's making me insane."

Aldric set down the sword.

He was quiet a moment longer. Outside, snowmelt ticked off the ruined roof in a rhythm like a patient clock. Then he said, "How old were you?"

"What?"

"When you took the indenture. How old?"

"Sixteen," I said. "My father owed the guild. The debt transferred. It's — that's how it works, in the free cities. Debts are inheritable. Didn't you know that?" And then I heard myself, and closed my eyes. "Of course you knew that. Honor works the same way. You've been paying your grandfather's debts since the day they stripped your banner."

"Yes," Aldric said simply.

And that was the thing about Aldric Vane that I had been slowly learning on the road and could not unlearn now: he did not perform forgiveness. He didn't make a speech about mercy or second chances or the goodness buried in every heart. He just looked at the facts, weighed them like a man weighing coin, and rendered judgment.

"Sixteen-year-olds don't open sluice gates," he said. "Men do. You were a girl with a pen in a machine, and the machine was Vaelric's, and the day you understood the machine, you ran. Running cost you everything. Your name, your trade, your city. You've been hunted by guild wardens for three years."

"Two and a half."

"Two and a half," he conceded, with the ghost of something that on another man might have been a smile. "You could have sold us to Halloway at the weir. One word shouted across the water and you'd have had your debt struck clean. Vaelric pays informants well; I've seen his ledgers."

"How have you seen his ledgers?"

"That's a story for a warmer night." He picked the sword back up, considered it, and set it down again unfinished, which for Aldric was practically a gesture of wild abandon. "You're here. You're drawing us new routes every morning while we eat, and I've checked them against what I remember of the old kingdom, and they're good. They're better than good. So here is my judgment, cartographer, and you may record it however you like: the woman who drew those forty-one soundings owes forty-one debts. The woman who's getting us to the court alive is paying them. I forgive the girl. The woman I'm proud to march with."

I want to tell you I said something witty. I want to tell you I had a quip ready, some barbed little jewel about knights and ledgers. What I actually did was put my face in my hands and cry like a sixteen-year-old with an inherited debt, silently, in front of a man I'd known eleven days, in a chapel full of forgiving saints, while the snowmelt ticked off the roof like the world's most patient absolution.

Aldric pretended not to notice. He picked the sword back up and cleaned it. It was the kindest thing anyone had done for me in three years.

* * *

Casper, of course, had heard everything.

I realized it when I went to refill the waterskin at the edge of the flooded nave and found her sitting on the third pew, knees drawn up, watching the moonlight come through the broken clerestory windows and shatter on the water. She didn't look at me. She was very good at not looking at people; it was practically her profession.

"The fish are still gossiping," she said.

"Are they."

"Mm. They say the chapel priest drowned at his post. Stayed in the flood year, ringing the bell, guiding boats through the dark. The water took him and he never left. They say the bell still rings sometimes, underwater, when it thinks someone's lost." She turned her head then, and her eyes caught the moonlight, flat and silver and unreadable. "I think he'd approve of the company."

"Casper — "

"You mapped prisons." Her voice wasn't cruel. It was worse than cruel; it was level, the voice of someone stating weather. "Forty-one of them. And then you spent three years running, and eleven days walking toward the worst one, on purpose, unpaid, with two strangers and a fox." She shrugged, a small motion. "I did the arithmetic somewhere around the weir. It comes out in your favor."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"Because you needed to say it first." She looked back at the water. "Things you confess get smaller. Things other people catch you hiding get bigger. Everyone knows that. Even the fish."

I sat down on the pew beside her. The wood was cold and salt-crusted and it creaked like an old man settling, and I thought: this is the strangest pew I have ever occupied, and I have napped in a cathedral in Ceresse during a tax riot.

"I had a plan," I admitted. "When I blackmailed my way into this company. Get close, learn what the map really was, copy it, sell it back to the guild, buy out my indenture, retire to somewhere flat and dry and boring."

"And now?"

"Now I keep forgetting to check whether today's the day I do it." I laughed, and this time it came out almost right. "Do you understand how annoying that is? I'm a professional. I have contingencies. I have *escape routes*, Casper, I have them memorized, I draw them in my sleep — and every morning I wake up and Aldric is rebandaging his wound wrong and you're having a conversation with a marmot about tolls, and I just... stay. I stay and I draw the next day's route. I've stopped planning to leave you and I never decided to stop. It happened to me like weather."

Casper was quiet for a long moment. Then, so quietly I nearly missed it under the lap of the water:

"I know. I stopped planning to leave you too. Somewhere around the drowned mill." A pause. "It's very inconvenient."

"It's the worst," I agreed.

And there it was — the first true warmth of it, small and ridiculous, two liars and a knight and a fox in a flooded church, none of us meaning to belong to anything, all of us belonging anyway. I won't pretend it fixed me. Guilt isn't a coat you take off; it's a stain that fades. But sitting there in the salt-and-moonlight hush, shoulder to shoulder with a woman who could hear the dead priest's bell ring underwater, I felt the stain shift a shade lighter, and I thought: maybe forty-one soundings can be revised after all.

Behind us, Ashen flowed down from the chancel and settled between our feet like a shared possession neither of us would admit to wanting, and Casper said, "He does that when he thinks someone's told the truth. He's very smug about it."

"He's a fox."

"He's a *smug* fox. There's a difference."

I was still smiling at that — actually smiling, the muscles confused from disuse — when the bell rang.

* * *

Not the memory of a bell. Not a fish-story bell. The real one: a single deep stroke that rolled up out of the flooded nave beneath our feet, through the stone, through the water, through my ribs, and died away into a silence that was somehow louder than the sound.

Casper went rigid. Her head snapped toward the water, and I watched her listen — truly listen, whole body bent toward the dark like a compass needle finding north.

"What is it?" Aldric was already up, sword out, moving to the doorway with the economy of a man who had done this a hundred times and expected to do it a hundred more. "Riders?"

"No." Casper's voice had gone strange. Flat and far away, the voice she used when the beasts spoke through her. "No riders. The bell — the priest — he's ringing it *for* someone. He only rings it when someone's lost." Her breath fogged in the cold air. "Someone's coming up the drowned road behind us. Alone. On foot. Wounded, I think — the water tastes like blood."

"Another courier?" I said. "Another dying stranger with a map?"

"No." Casper rose slowly, staring at the black water where the moonlight broke and knitted itself back together, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing, and I saw that her hands were shaking.

"It's a child," she said. "There's a child walking the drowned road. And she's calling for help in a language that died with the queen."

Outside, far off down the sunken causeway, something answered her — a light, small and blue and wrong, bobbing nearer through the dark.

And the bell rang a second time.
