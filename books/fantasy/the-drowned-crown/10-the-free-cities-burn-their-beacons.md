## 10. The Free Cities Burn Their Beacons

I have carried a great many things in my life. A sword, for most of it. A grudge, for the rest of it. Shame fits neatly in a saddlebag if you fold it right.

But I had never before carried news that could end the world, and I confess it sat poorly among my other effects, taking up more room than it should have.

The pigeon found us at dawn on the fourth day out of the drowned chapel—a ragged gray thing that dropped out of a sky the color of old pewter and landed on Odile's pack as though it had been paid to do so. Which, in a manner of speaking, it had. Odile kept a correspondence with three guild cartographers and one disgraced harbormaster, all of whom owed her favors she collected like a miser collects coins: rarely, and with visible delight at their suffering.

She read the message twice. Then she went very still in the way of people who have just learned something they will spend the rest of their lives wishing they'd learned slower.

"Odile," I said. "Out with it."

"The beacons." Her voice came out flat as a pressed flower. "They're dousing them."

Casper looked up from the fire she was coaxing—she never let me tend fires; said I built them like a man burying evidence—and Ashen lifted his head from where he'd been draped across her boots, doing his impression of an ordinary fox, which he had never once managed convincingly. Ordinary foxes don't sigh.

"Which cities?" Casper asked.

"All of them." Odile's hands were shaking. I noticed because Odile's hands never shake; they calculate, they draft, they steal small objects off tables when she thinks no one is looking, but they do not shake. "Corvane went dark three days ago. Sable Harbor yesterday. The message says Meridian lit its beacon at dusk and put it out by midnight." She swallowed. "They're calling it the Mourning Watch. They believe the queen is dead past all saving, and they think—" her voice cracked and reassembled itself, "—they think if the binding dies clean, the flood-magic dies with it, and Vaelric's armies won't be able to cross the thaw."

"And they're wrong," Casper said quietly.

"They're wrong," Odile agreed. "The binding doesn't die clean. It *breaks*. And when it breaks—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. We had all seen what the drowned roads did when the magic frayed at the edges: water that remembered being a wall, bridges that forgot how to hold, beasts gone strange and grieving. If the whole binding snapped at once, every river in the old kingdom would remember its oldest shape. The free cities were not putting out their beacons to save themselves. They were putting them out to drown themselves faster, so the ending would be quick instead of long.

Despair, I have learned, is rarely cowardice. More often it is arithmetic performed by someone who has lost count of their reasons to hope.

* * *

We stood around that dead little fire for a long moment, four of us—or three of us and one ancient spirit wearing a fox like a borrowed coat—and I watched the news settle into each of them differently.

Odile began, immediately and furiously, to calculate. Distances, thaw dates, the melt-line on the high passes. Her fingers moved in the air as though sketching a map only she could see, which, knowing Odile, she probably could.

Casper went quiet. Not her usual quiet—the practiced, forgettable quiet of a woman who has spent her whole life making herself hard to look at—but a different kind. A listening quiet. I had learned by then that when Casper went still like that, she was hearing something the rest of us couldn't. The marmots, probably. Or the crows. Or whatever passed for gossip among the stones.

And Ashen—Ashen looked at the eastern horizon, where the mountains of Thornfell cut the sky into jagged teeth, and his eyes were not a fox's eyes at all. They were old, and they were wet, and for once he made no effort to hide it.

"How many beacons are left?" I asked.

"One," Odile said. "Meridian's is relit each night by volunteers. Old men, mostly. Children carrying oil up two hundred steps because their grandparents told them the queen might come back." She laughed, and there was nothing funny anywhere near it. "They'll run out of believers soon. Believers burn faster than oil."

"Then we go faster than both," I said.

It was, I will admit, a stupid thing to say. It was the sort of thing I'd said my whole life, back when I wore a white cloak and believed that saying brave things loudly was the same as being brave. But nobody laughed at me. Casper nodded slowly, and Odile started packing, and Ashen rose and shook himself and trotted east without waiting, which was answer enough from him.

That is the thing about found families that no one warns you about: they make your stupid brave things sound reasonable. Alone, I would have said *we should ride harder* and then ridden alone and died alone in some gorge with my honor still unpaid. With them, the same sentence became a plan. That is either love or conspiracy, and I have stopped trying to tell the difference.

* * *

We rode hard for two days through country that grew stranger the farther east we pushed. The drowned roads here ran above ground again, half-sunk causeways of pale stone marching across marshland that had once, Ashen told us, been palace gardens. He told us in his own fashion—which meant he spoke perhaps six words a day and made each one feel like a coin pried from a dead man's fist.

On the second evening, we made camp in the lee of a fallen colossus, some old king or god whose marble face had been underwater for a century and now wore a beard of moss. Casper fed the horses. Odile sharpened her pencils, which is what she does instead of praying. And I sat watch, as I always took first watch, because sleep and I had been estranged for eleven years and neither of us missed the other much.

That was when Casper came and sat beside me.

She didn't say anything at first. Neither did I. There is a particular comfort in silence shared with someone who doesn't need it filled, and I have come to treasure it the way misers treasure gold—irrationally, and with a constant low hum of anxiety about losing it.

"You served her," Casper said at last. Not a question. "The queen. You've never said what she was like."

I turned my whetstone over in my hand. "You want the truth or the version that makes a good story?"

"I'm a smuggler, Aldric. I want the truth. Stories are what people hide inside when the truth gets heavy."

Fair hit. I gave her the truth.

"She was tired," I said. "Everyone remembers her as radiant, and she was, but what I remember is tired. She was young—younger than I am now, gods help me—and she'd inherited a kingdom held together by water and promises, and she worked like a woman bailing a boat with a cup. I was her shield-sworn for three years. Do you know how many times I saw her rest?" I shook my head. "Once. One afternoon, on the lake at Corvane, she fell asleep in a rowboat and let it drift, and her handmaiden and I stood on the shore pretending not to guard her while she slept. That's the memory I keep. Not the coronation. Not the battles. A woman asleep in a boat, trusting the water to hold her up because everyone she trusted had already been spent keeping everything else afloat."

Casper was quiet a moment. "And Vaelric?"

"Vaelric was charming," I said. "That's the part history will get wrong, if history survives to get anything wrong. He wasn't a monster skulking in shadows. He was generous, and funny, and he remembered everyone's name. He sat at her council table for six years and agreed with her in public while buying her loyalists in private. When the soul-brokers came to him—when he bought her soul out from under her like a merchant acquiring a debt—he didn't even need soldiers. He needed patience and a ledger." My jaw ached; I realized I'd been clenching it. "I found out too late. I raised the alarm, and by then it was done, and when I called him traitor in front of the court, do you know what happened? Nothing. Everyone looked at me the way you look at a madman. Because he was still charming, you see. Still generous. And I was just a knight shouting, and shouting knights get stripped of their cloaks."

"That's why the gate took your battle," Casper said softly. "At the Gate of Teeth. Your worst memory."

"It took my disgrace," I corrected. "The battle itself I'd have given freely. Losing it—that was the worst of it. Though I'll tell you a secret." I looked at her. "I don't miss it. The shame. There's a hole where it used to sit, and the hole aches, but it aches cleaner than the shame did."

Casper studied me with those flat gray eyes that saw too much. "You're telling me this because of the beacons."

"I'm telling you this because tomorrow we ride into the mountain's shadow, and one of us may not ride out, and I wanted someone besides me to know what we're riding toward. Not a symbol. Not a crown. A tired woman in a boat who deserves to wake up." I shrugged, awkward. "Also because you asked."

She almost smiled. On Casper, an almost-smile is a state occasion.

"The crows say the passes are clear," she offered. "Snowmelt's early this year. Halloway's riders are two days behind us and falling further—they lost horses at the weir and their captain is arguing with her own scouts about whether we exist."

"Do we exist?" I asked.

"Not according to the official reports," she said. "I bribed a crow to eat one."

* * *

It was near midnight when Ashen spoke.

He did it the way he always did—appearing between us without sound, sitting down as though he'd been part of the conversation for hours, and then ruining the effect by talking.

"The last beacon," he said, "is dying faster than the girl's map shows."

Odile woke instantly, which is a cartographer's training; we once tested her by dropping a book near her head and she caught it midair and apologized to it. "Faster how?"

"Faith is fuel," Ashen said. "The beacons burn on belief as much as oil. Every city that goes dark makes the others darker. Grief is contagious. So is surrender." His tail curled around his feet, precise as punctuation. "Three nights. Perhaps four. After that, the binding thins past mending, and no soul returned can anchor it. The queen would wake into a drowning world."

"Three nights," I repeated.

"The mountain gate is two days' ride," Odile said, already scratching figures in the frost with a stick. "If we push—if we kill the horses' pace and our own—we can make it in one and a half. Barely. Assuming no ambushes, no weather, no acts of any god currently employed by Vaelric."

"Assume all three," I said. "Then plan anyway."

"We'll need to travel light," Casper said. She was already unbuckling half her pack, sorting with a smuggler's economy—keep, burn, abandon. Watching her decide what to leave behind, item by item, without hesitation, I felt something twist in my chest. Here was a woman who had practiced loss until it was a skill. I thought of my own pack, stuffed with a dead order's relics I couldn't name the reason for keeping, and resolved—again—to burn them, and knew—again—that I wouldn't.

"Aldric." Ashen's voice pulled me back. The fox was looking at me with those old eyes. "When we reach the court, Vaelric will offer you something. He offers everyone something. For you it will be the cloak. The honor restored, the name cleared, the disgrace unmade. He keeps such things in his vaults like a fisherman keeps hooks."

"And if he does?"

"Remember the woman in the boat," Ashen said simply. "Not the cloak. Cloaks are how he dresses his traps."

I looked at the fox a long moment. "How do you know what he'd offer me?"

Ashen's ears flicked—the closest he ever came to embarrassment. "Because I have watched men bargain with him for three hundred years," he said, "and it is always the same three bargains: the past, the future, or the self. He will offer you your past. He offered the girl her future. And he has already taken—" the fox paused, choosing, "—he is patient with the self."

None of us asked what he meant. Some doors you leave shut until you're strong enough to open them.

* * *

We broke camp an hour before dawn, in the dark, in the cold, and rode east into the mountain's shadow at a pace that was going to cost us, and we all knew it, and none of us said stop.

Somewhere behind us, two days back, Wren Halloway was arguing with her scouts about whether we existed. Somewhere ahead, beneath a mile of stone, a stolen soul hung in a vault like a lamp in a locked room. And somewhere far to the west, on a tower in Meridian, children were hauling oil up two hundred steps to keep one small flame alive against the dark—not because they had proof, not because they had hope they could defend in argument, but because their grandparents told them the queen might come back.

I thought about that flame the whole ride. I thought about faith as fuel, and grief as contagion, and how despair spreads like water finding cracks, and how the only thing I have ever known that pushes back against it is a handful of stubborn people refusing, together, to put out their light.

Eleven years ago, I lost my cloak because I shouted a true thing alone, and no one stood with me.

Riding east through the freezing dark with a smuggler, a liar, and a fox, I understood at last that the shout had never been the failure. Standing alone had been. Whatever waited for us under the mountain—bargains, vaults, a king dressed in charm—I would not be standing alone this time.

Behind us, the last beacon burned. Ahead, the mountain waited with its teeth.

We rode toward the teeth. It was, I reflected grimly, the most honorable thing I had done in eleven years, and I intended to survive it purely out of spite.
