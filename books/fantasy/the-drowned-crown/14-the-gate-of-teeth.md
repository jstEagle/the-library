## 14. The Gate of Teeth

I have been called many things in my life—knight, traitor, coward, fool—and I will confess that most of them were earned honestly. But standing before the mountain's gate that morning, watching the fog peel back from a doorway carved with more teeth than any mouth in nature, I learned there was one name I had never yet been given: *last through the door*.

The gate had no doors. That was the first thing Odile noticed, because Odile notices everything except when to stop talking about it. The archway stood forty feet tall and open as a beggar's palm, black stone veined with something that glimmered like fish-scale, and the passage beyond simply went dark—not shadow-dark, but *absence*-dark, the kind of dark that made my eyes insist they were closed when I knew better.

"No portcullis," she said, sketching it in three strokes. "No bars, no chains, no lock. Which means the lock is us."

"Cheerful," said Casper.

The fox sat at her heel with its tail curled around its feet like punctuation. Ashen, it had named itself, in the cave-in, in a voice like dry leaves deciding to be a voice. It looked up at the archway now and made a sound I can only describe as a laugh, if a laugh could be filed down to a point.

"It tests," Ashen said. Its speech was still new, still strange—a fox learning to spend words like coins it wasn't sure were legal tender. "Each traveler. Each debt."

"Tests how?" I asked.

Ashen turned its amber eyes on me, and for a moment I felt flayed, which was answer enough.

"The worst thing you remember," Casper said quietly. She'd gone pale under her windburn. "That's what it takes out of you. Isn't it? That's what the crows said about the deep courts. The gates don't keep people out. They keep the *weight* out."

"And if we refuse?" Odile asked.

"Then we camp outside Vaelric's mountain until spring thaw," Casper said, "and Halloway catches us, and we die in the snow with our memories intact." She shrugged. "Tempting."

I should say something here about fear. People imagine knights are brave, which is a slander invented by people who have never been knights. We are not brave; we are *practiced*. There is a difference, and the difference is that a practiced man knows exactly what shape his fear is and has already decided, in some calmer hour, what he will do when it arrives. My fear had arrived at Redford Bridge eleven years ago and never entirely left. It wore my old commander's face. It sounded like screaming horses and the wet crunch of a charge going wrong because I gave an order a heartbeat too late—or a heartbeat too early; the tribunal never could decide, and neither, in the small hours, could I.

Forty-one men. That was the number. Forty-one men who followed Ser Aldric Vane into a ford that broke them, because their knight misread the water. Eleven died. The rest carried the story home, and the story grew teeth of its own, and by the time the tribunal finished with me there was nothing left to strip but the banner, so they stripped that instead.

I had carried that day for eleven years the way a man carries a stone in his boot: not always feeling it, but never once forgetting it is there.

Now a door wanted it.

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the archway itself, which felt like sleeping in the jaws of something patient. Odile built a fire small enough to deny. Casper spoke to the mountain goats on the scree above us—they told her, in her flat reporting way, that the stone "hums when it's hungry," which none of us chose to examine closely.

It was Odile who worked out the mechanics, because Odile cannot leave a mechanism alone any more than a dog can leave a bone.

"It's a toll, not a trial," she said, jabbing her charcoal at her sketch. "Look—the arch isn't judging us. It's *weighing* us. Worst memory goes in, gate stays open long enough for the rest of you to pass. One payment per opening. That's why there's no lock. The lock is that nobody sane pays."

"How do you know it opens at all?" Casper asked.

"I don't. But every gate in the deep courts runs on the same principle—Vaelric's whole architecture is built on the idea that everyone will choose to keep their wounds." She said it lightly, but her jaw was tight, and I knew then that she'd already done her own arithmetic. Odile had spent two years indentured to the Guild, mapping prison routes for a king she despised. Her worst memory had a face and a ledger number.

Casper's, I suspected, was older and quieter. A woman who builds her whole life around being forgettable does not arrive at that construction site by accident.

And mine—

Mine was a bridge.

"We draw lots," I said.

Everyone looked at me.

"We draw lots," I repeated, "and whoever draws the short straw pays, and the other two go through, and we don't discuss it further, because discussing it is how the discussion wins."

"That's the stupidest plan I've ever heard from a man wearing armor," Odile said.

"You're not wearing armor," Casper pointed out.

"He's wearing armor *on the inside*, is my point. It makes him stupid in a very specific way." Odile folded her arms. "Nobody draws lots. Nobody volunteers. We sit here until someone admits what they're actually afraid of, and then we decide like adults."

There was a silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above, a goat said something rude.

"I'll pay," Casper said finally, and her voice was steady in the way that cost her something. "Whatever it takes out of me, I've survived worse. I built myself to survive worse. That's rather the whole—"

"No," I said.

She blinked at me. In all our months on the drowned roads, I don't think I'd ever interrupted her. She was the one who heard everything; interrupting her felt like shouting in a library.

"No," I said again, and found the rest of it rising in me like water finding its level. "You need your memories. All of them. You especially—you're walking into a vault that wants your soul, and you propose to arrive *pre-shorn*? Whatever the queen's return requires of you, you'll need every scrap of yourself to weigh against it. And Odile—" Odile opened her mouth. "Odile needs hers too, because the moment we're inside, we'll need her to read that map, and the map is written in blood she helped spill whether she likes it or not. She carries it. That's the price she's already paying."

"Generous of you to volunteer my suffering," Odile muttered, but her eyes were wet, and she didn't argue.

"And you?" Casper said. "What noble burden do you propose to carry, Sir Aldric?"

And there it was. The question I'd been walking toward since Redford Bridge, though I hadn't known the road had a destination.

I took off my gauntlets. It seemed like the sort of thing that ought to be done bare-handed.

"Eleven years ago I killed eleven of my own men with bad judgment," I said. "I've rehearsed that sentence ten thousand times. I've said it to tribunals and taverns and the ceiling of every room I've slept in since. And I have learned something about it, which is that carrying it has made me careful, and careful has made me slow, and slow is just another word for dead when Halloway's riders come over the ridge." I looked at the dark mouth of the gate. "This door doesn't want my pain. It wants my *worst*. And my worst is a bridge in the rain. So I'm going to give it the bridge, and I'm going to walk through lighter than I've walked in eleven years, and I am going to hate myself for the relief, and then I'm going to get over that too, because that's what honor actually is. Not the banner. Not the title. It's paying what's owed and walking forward anyway."

Nobody spoke. The fox's tail moved once, like a period set down gently.

"Aldric," Casper said softly, "if it takes that day, it takes *them*. The eleven. You won't remember their names."

"I know."

"You won't remember why you stopped calling yourself a knight."

"I know that too." I smiled, and it hurt, which seemed appropriate. "But I'll remember the last year. I'll remember a smuggler who talks to goats and a cartographer who argues with doors and a fox with delusions of grandeur. If the ledger has to balance, let it balance toward what's coming instead of what's gone."

Odile wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, as though annoyed at the weather. "For the record," she said, "this is the stupidest plan I've ever heard from a man wearing armor."

"Noted," I said.

"For the other record—I'm glad I got blackmailed onto this road." She said it fast, like pulling a tooth. "In case the door takes anything else. In case it's greedy."

"It's not greedy," Ashen said. "It's honest. There is a difference." The fox rose, stretched, and padded to the archway, where it sat looking back at us with those banked-fire eyes. "Come. The gate wakes at moonrise. It is nearly hungry."

* * *

Moonrise came the way it does in the high passes—all at once, like a struck match. Light poured down the scree and touched the archway, and the fish-scale veins in the black stone lit from within, and the darkness inside the gate *breathed*.

I felt it before I saw anything: a pressure behind my eyes, gentle as a thumb finding a bruise. It knew where the bruise was. Of course it did. Doors like this are not clever; they are simply *true*, and truth requires no searching.

"Order matters?" Casper asked Ashen.

"The payer goes last," the fox said. "The gate holds while the wound is in its teeth. When it swallows, the way closes. Walk quickly."

Odile went first, sketchbook clutched to her chest like a shield she'd forgotten how to use. She stepped over the threshold and the dark accepted her without ceremony—one moment her boots, then her knees, then all of her, gone into the absence.

Casper hesitated. She turned back and looked at me, and I want to record, for whatever record survives, exactly what she looked like: windburned, exhausted, fox-shadowed, absolutely unwilling to cry and furious about almost doing it anyway.

"When we get out of this," she said, "you're telling me about Redford Bridge. The real version. Not the tribunal version."

"There isn't another version."

"Liar. Everyone has another version." She almost smiled. "Tell it to me after. Promise."

"I promise," I said, and understood, even as I said it, that I was promising something the mountain would not let me keep. Promises are debts too. Perhaps the gate would accept one.

She went through. Gone.

The pressure behind my eyes bloomed. Rain, suddenly, everywhere—not real rain, remembered rain, the cold needle-fall of that morning at the ford, the gray light, the sound of the water running higher than my maps had promised. I saw the far bank. I saw my men waiting on my word. I heard my own voice give the order, and I heard, as I had heard ten thousand times, the exact half-second where a wiser man would have waited.

*I could take it back,* I thought at the gate. *Even now. Let me carry it. Let me be slow and careful and alive with my stone in my boot.*

The gate did not answer. Honest doors don't haggle.

I thought of the eleven names. Brannoc, who sang off-key. Tam, who owed me two coppers and a joke. Little Wren—no, that was the inquisitor's name, funny, the mind tangles at the end—Corwin, then. Corwin who was nineteen. I said all eleven names to the dark, deliberately, like a man setting a table for guests who are about to leave forever.

Then I stepped forward, and the gate took the bridge out of me.

I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy is the last service I can render those men. It did not hurt. That was the terrible part. It was like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was wearing—eleven years of weight leaving my shoulders in a single breath, and beneath it, the shocking lightness of a body that had been braced so long it had forgotten straightness. The rain vanished. The screaming vanished. The half-second vanished.

What remained was a shape where the memory had been. An outline. I knew *that* something had been taken, the way an amputee knows weather coming in a limb that isn't there. I knew I had paid. I knew I had chosen it. But the choosing had no object anymore. I had given away the coin and kept only the receipt.

Honor reclaimed, the poets would say. Honor *relocated*, I'd have said, if I'd still known what for.

I walked through the dark. It was warm, oddly, like the inside of a cupped hand. On the far side, torchlight—real torchlight, or something pretending—fell across a descending stair cut into living rock, and Odile caught my arm, and Casper was there, alive and whole and looking at me with an expression I couldn't read.

"Well?" she said. "How does it feel?"

I searched myself honestly, the way I'd once searched a battlefield for survivors.

"Lighter," I said. "And I can't tell you why, and that's the worst of it. I know I lost something. I know it mattered. I know—" I frowned, chasing a shape already dissolving. "I know I promised you something. Didn't I?"

Her face did something complicated. "After," she said. "You'll tell me after."

"After," I agreed, and meant it with everything that remained.

Ashen trotted past us onto the stair, tail high, and paused at the edge of the torchlight. "The gate is fed," the fox said. "But hear me, knight-who-was. What you gave is not destroyed. Nothing below the mountain is destroyed. It is *kept*." Amber eyes flicked back toward each of us, and its voice dropped to that leaf-dry near-whisper. "Including the souls. Including hers. Come. The court sings vespers soon, and you do not want to hear what they sing."

We descended. Behind us, faint as a held breath, I thought I heard rain on a bridge—but that was impossible, because I no longer knew what a bridge had to do with me.

Above, far above, through forty fathoms of stone, something began to close.
