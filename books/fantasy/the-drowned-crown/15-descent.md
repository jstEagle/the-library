## 15. Descent

The stair went down for a very long time.

I want to be precise about that, because I have been down a great many stairs in my life—cellar stairs, smuggler's stairs, the kind of stairs you descend at night with stolen goods in your coat and a prayer on your lips—and none of them prepared me for this. This was not a staircase so much as a throat. The mountain had swallowed something once, and we were traveling down its gullet toward whatever it could not digest.

"Anyone else's knees objecting?" Odile asked behind me. Her voice echoed wrong. In Thornfell, echoes came back to you honest and ragged. Here they came back polished, like someone had taken the trouble to smooth them before returning them.

"My knees," Aldric said, "have been objecting since the Gate of Teeth."

His hand was still pressed flat against his chest, the way it had been since the constructs let us pass. He'd given them his worst memory—the battle where he'd lost his banner, his honor, his name—and I kept waiting for him to mourn it. He didn't. He walked like a man who had set down a heavy pack and was surprised by how light his shoulders felt, and how empty.

Ashen trotted ahead of us, fox-red against the pale stone, and did not need light. That should have told me something. It did tell me something. I chose, for perhaps the hundredth time on this journey, not to listen.

*You always do that,* said the marmot part of my brain, which never shuts up. *You hear exactly what an animal is telling you and then decide it means nothing because the alternative is terrifying.*

The marmot had been right about the patrol in chapter one of my misfortunes, too. Marmots are insufferable that way.

The stair ended without warning. One moment stone under my boots; the next, a floor so smooth it might have been water frozen mid-ripple, and beyond it—

Light.

Not torchlight. Not the blue corpse-glow of deep places. This was warm, golden, generous light, the kind that falls through high windows in late afternoon, and it came from everywhere and nowhere, and beneath the mountain there are no windows.

"Oh," Odile said softly. "Oh, that's not right."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

We stood at the edge of a court.

I have seen courts from the outside—smuggled past their gates, sold goods to their kitchens, listened through stable walls while lords and ladies said things to each other that would curdle milk. Courts are loud places. They run on noise: flattery, gossip, the clink of cups, the rustle of silk pretending it isn't afraid.

This court was silent except for singing.

They were arranged across a vast hall like figures in a music box—pale men and paler women in clothes of beautiful cut and no color at all, standing in ranks upon tiers of white stone, and they were singing Vaelric's praises. I recognized the melody because I'd heard it in villages all the way up the drowned roads, worn smooth as river stones by generations of mouths. A harvest song. An old song, older than Vaelric, older probably than the mountain. They had taken the oldest song anyone knew and filled it with his name.

And their voices—

Their voices were perfect. Every note true, every harmony exact, every entrance precisely together, and it was the most horrifying thing I had ever heard, because there was nothing in it. No joy, no grief, no weariness, no longing. Music is what feeling does when it can't stay quiet anymore, and this was feeling's corpse, embalmed and set to humming.

"They traded it away," Ashen said. The fox sat at my feet, tail curled over its paws, amber eyes reflecting light that had no source. "Piece by piece. Fear first—it's the heaviest piece, most of them gave that early. Then grief. Then memory of grief, which is nearly the same thing. Then wanting. Then remembering what wanting felt like." The fox's ears flattened. "Vaelric's courtiers are not prisoners, Casper Sterling. They are customers."

"How long has he been collecting?" Aldric asked quietly.

"Since the queen drowned. He learned her secret—that a soul can be held apart from a body, kept in pledge, spent like coin—and he learned it works. And he offered the frightened people of his new court a bargain no frightened person can refuse." Ashen looked up at me. "Safety. In installments."

Odile had gone the color of the stone. She was a cartographer; she thought in maps and margins, and I watched her arrive at the thought she arrived at: *every one of these people is a place on a map where something was taken.*

"We should go around," she whispered. "There has to be a service corridor. There's always a service corridor. Palaces are ninety percent corridors nobody important uses."

"There is," Ashen said. "But you cannot reach the vault without passing the singers. The court is the lock, in its way. Vaelric built it that way. What better guard than people who will raise no alarm, offer no resistance, and simply—" the fox's muzzle wrinkled, "—*watch* you?"

* * *

We went in along the wall, which was a plan, in the sense that walking into a fire along the wall is a plan.

Up close, the courtiers were worse.

Their skin was pale the way paper is pale, the way things are pale when the color has been drawn out of them rather than never put in. Their eyes were lovely and vacant as winter ponds. One woman turned her head as we passed—not quickly, not with suspicion, just with the slow gravity of a weathervane finding wind—and smiled at us.

It was a kind smile. That was the worst part. It was the smile you give a stranger to make them feel welcome, and it cost her nothing, because everything it should have cost had already been paid out to a king in a dark vault, and what remained was only the shape of kindness, hollowed and hung up like a bell with no clapper.

"You've come a long way," she said. Her voice was soft and pleasant and perfectly pitched. "The road above is dangerous in thaw season. You must be tired. His Majesty provides rest for all travelers who come to him honestly."

"We're just passing through," I said.

"Everyone says that." She nodded serenely. "Everyone arrives saying they're passing through, and then they stay, and then they understand. It's so much easier here. You'll see. All the sharp edges are gone."

Behind her, the choir swelled into another verse, and I heard the words properly for the first time:

*Blessed is the crown that keeps us,*
*blessed the hand that holds,*
*we gave our nights unto the king*
*and he shall keep our souls—*

They sang about it. They sang about what he'd taken from them, gratefully, in four-part harmony, and some animal part of my brain that had been quiet since the stair finally spoke up, and it wasn't the marmot this time. It was every creature I had ever listened to, all at once, saying the same thing: *run.*

I am good at running. Running is my oldest skill. Before the gift, before the map, before any of it, I survived Thornfell by being forgettable and fast, and every instinct I owned was screaming at me to take Odile's wrist, take Aldric's sleeve, take Ashen by the scruff if the fox would permit such insolence, and go back up that throat of a stair and out into the cold honest world where echoes come back ragged and fear comes back whole.

Instead I looked at the woman's hands.

They were folded neatly at her waist, and on the third finger of her left hand was a ring—a plain band, worn thin, the kind of ring a working woman wears, a fisherwoman or a farmer's wife or a smuggler's daughter. And engraved inside it, invisible to anyone but the wearer, would be a name. Someone had given her that ring. Someone had loved her enough to mark her finger, and she had sold the memory of it for safety, and now she wore the evidence of love she couldn't feel and smiled at strangers and sang praises to the man who'd made the trade possible.

"Casper." Aldric's hand on my shoulder. Low voice. "Keep walking."

"She had a family," I said. My own voice sounded strange to me, scraped raw. "She has a ring, Aldric. She doesn't know why she wears it."

"I know."

"He didn't chain them. He didn't even threaten them. He just—" I gestured at the hall, the tiers, the hundreds of pale faces turned toward their king's absent throne with expressions of mild, contented nothing. "He made being empty comfortable. He made surrender feel like coming home."

"Yes," Aldric said. "That's what conquest looks like when it's done well. I served a queen who understood that. It's why he feared her." Something moved behind his eyes—grief reaching for a memory that wasn't there anymore, finding the hole where it used to live. "I know he feared her. I can't remember why anymore. But I know."

* * *

We found the singers' gallery by accident, which is how I find everything, and it was there that I saw my future.

It was a small antechamber off the main hall, and in it sat an old man, mending a tapestry. He had a needle in his hand and thread the color of dusk, and he worked with great care and great calm, and when he looked up at us his face was smooth and untroubled as a lake at dawn.

"Travelers," he said pleasantly. "How nice. It's been ever so long since anyone new came down."

"What happened to your soul?" I asked. I hadn't meant to ask it. It came out of me the way blood comes out of you when you've been cut and haven't noticed yet.

He considered the question with genuine, gentle puzzlement. "My soul? Why, it's kept safe, of course. His Majesty keeps it for me. In the vault, with everyone else's. Nothing to worry about—nothing can hurt it there. Nothing can touch it. Nothing can *change* it." He smiled and returned to his stitching. "That's the whole point, you understand. When you keep everything that matters locked away somewhere perfect, nothing that happens to you can matter. It's very restful."

I looked at his hands. Old hands, clever hands, hands that knew their work. And I thought about my gift—the beast-speech, the listening, the thing that had made me a freak and a fugitive and, though I'd have bitten my tongue off before admitting it, useful. The thing that had brought a dying man to my ford. The thing that had led me here.

If I gave it away—if I gave *myself* away, piece by piece, the way he had—I would still have my hands. I would still be able to work, to walk, to smile at strangers. I would sit in a warm hall under a light with no source, and I would sing songs about how safe I was, and I would mean them, because meaning requires a self and mine would be in a vault where nothing could hurt it.

Nothing could hurt it. Nothing could reach it. Nothing could *love* it.

"That's what he'll offer me," I said. I don't know if I was talking to Aldric or Odile or Ashen or myself. "Down there. In the vault. He won't threaten me. He'll offer to make it stop hurting. Being hunted, being alone, hearing things no one wants spoken—he'll offer to take the weight off. And the terrible thing is that it would work. It would work beautifully."

"Yes," said the old man, looking up again with his lake-calm face. "It does. Would you like to hear about it? I have time. We all have so much time here."

"*Casper.*" Odile this time, gripping my arm, her cartographer's fingers strong as iron bands. "Look at me. You are the least comfortable person I have ever met. You sleep sitting up. You apologize to goats. If you ever became *restful*, I would assume you were dead and start planning the funeral."

Despite everything—despite the choir, despite the ring, despite the old man's patient needle—I laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out.

"She's right," Aldric said. "You're a menace to peace and quiet. Don't change."

Ashen said nothing. The fox only looked at me, and in those amber eyes was the vision-memory of a drowned queen, a woman who had owned a soul worth stealing, and I understood that the fox was showing me the other future. Not the vault. The crown. The weight of a whole self, kept whole, at any cost.

Two futures, then. Comfortable emptiness or costly wholeness.

The old man returned to his tapestry, and I saw what he was mending: a scene of the court itself, woven years ago, when the colors were still bright. In the old weaving, the courtiers' robes were red and gold and green. He was unpicking the colors, thread by careful thread, and replacing them with white.

He was happy doing it. That was the horror. He was *happy*.

* * *

We left the gallery and crossed the last stretch of hall toward the far doors—black wood banded in silver, taller than three men, and cold enough that frost feathered the stone within arm's reach of them. The vault lay beyond. I could feel it the way I felt weather coming, a pressure behind my teeth, a hum in the bones of my ear. Somewhere past those doors was a soul in pledge, a crown of water and light, and a price I was beginning, with the stubborn clarity of a beast hearing a trap spring, to suspect I already knew.

The choir reached the final verse of their harvest hymn, and this time the words changed. Not the old words. New ones, sung with the same perfect hollowness, and every pale face in the hall turned—not toward us, not toward the doors, but toward the darkness of the stair we had descended—and sang:

*A stranger comes, a stranger goes,*
*the king knows all that passes—*

They had known we were here the whole time. Of course they had. They weren't guards; they were bells, and we had been ringing them since we set foot on the stair.

And beneath the hymn, from the black mouth of the descent, came footsteps. Measured. Unhurried. The footsteps of someone who has never once needed to run.

Aldric's sword cleared its sheath with a sound like a struck bell. Odile pulled the map from her coat. Ashen's fur lifted along its spine, and the fox spoke one word, quiet as ash falling:

"Halloway."

The inquisitor stepped into the golden light with snowmelt still on her boots, and she was smiling, and her smile had nothing hollow in it at all.
