## 17. Halloway's Doubt

I have always been good at believing things.

It is not a skill people respect. They admire swordsmanship, they admire piety, they admire the particular talent for flattery that gets a person promoted in Vaelric's court. But belief—sustained, disciplined, load-bearing belief—is the rarest gift I know of, and I have cultivated mine like an orchard. Every doubt that sprouted, I pruned. Every question that grew crooked against the doctrine, I staked and tied until it grew straight again or died.

For eleven years I have served the Inquisition of King Vaelric the Uniter, and in eleven years I have never once let a belief fall.

Which is why it was so inconvenient when Ser Aldric Vane threw a dead child's boot at my feet on the ice road below the Gate of Teeth.

* * *

You should understand something about how I found them. It was not luck, though I told my riders it was. Luck is what you say when the truth would make you sound like a woman who has been chasing three fugitives across half a kingdom because she cannot stop thinking about one of them.

Casper Sterling. The smuggler. The beast-speaker.

I had read her file the way you read scripture: thoroughly, repeatedly, looking for the parts that confirmed what you already believed. Beast-speakers were abominations under the Concordat. Beast-speakers were how the old kingdoms had fallen—the animals whispering treason into soft ears, borders dissolving because no border can hold against a wolf who doesn't recognize it. That was the doctrine. That was what I taught the novices, standing very straight in my grey cloak with my grey eyes and my voice like a door closing.

But her file had gaps. And I have spent enough years reading confessions to know that gaps are where the truth goes to hide.

She'd been flagged first at age nine, after the flood at Millbrook, when a child was pulled from the weir by dogs acting in concert too clever for dogs. The inquisitor who interviewed her wrote: *Subject displays unusual calm. Recommend observation.* Then nothing. Nine years of nothing. Someone had buried the file, and someone with authority had kept it buried, and in all my years of service I had learned exactly one iron law:

In Vaelric's kingdom, mercy leaves fingerprints.

So when the order came down to run down the smuggler and her companions before they reached the court beneath the mountain, I obeyed—I always obey—but I also rode ahead of my own riders, alone, on the pretext of scouting the pass. And when Aldric stepped out of the snow-mist onto the causeway with his sword drawn and his face wearing the expression of a man about to do something unforgivable, I did not immediately call for him to be taken.

"Ser Halloway." He said my name the way you'd spit out something bitter. "You've lost weight."

"You've gained a conscience," I said. "Where did you find it? I've been looking everywhere."

He didn't smile. Aldric Vane had never once smiled at me, not even back when we'd both been young and unbent and serving the same queen whose name we weren't allowed to speak anymore. That was the thing about Aldric. Even disgraced—even stripped of banner and title and sent into exile with his honor hanging off him like a torn sleeve—he had never learned to lie well, and he had never stopped trying anyway.

"The children," he said.

The word went through me like a blade going through wet canvas. Quiet entry. All the damage after.

"I don't know what you mean."

"The queen's heirs, Wren. The two little ones who 'died of fever' the winter after the Drowning. You swore an oath over their cradles. I stood beside you. You cried, if you'll recall, though you'd deny it now."

"I recall that you were cashiered for cowardice at Redford Bridge," I said, "so perhaps we shouldn't discuss oaths."

"Cowardice." Something moved behind his face—not anger, worse than anger, which is pity. "Yes. That's the story. Here's another story."

He reached into his coat, slow, deliberate, the way you move around a spooked animal—and gods forgive me, but he was right to. My hand was already on my hilt. Eleven years of belief, and it turns out belief weighs nothing at all when your body remembers being twenty-three and afraid.

He drew out not a weapon but a packet of oilskin, tied with cord, and dropped it in the snow between us. Then he took one item from it—a small boot, leather, child-sized, its sole stamped with the mark of the royal nursery—and tossed it so it landed at my feet, spinning once, coming to rest toe-up like a small drowned thing.

"From the cairn at Hollowmere," Aldric said. "Under the stones. Not burned, not buried in consecrated ground. Stacked under rocks like a poacher hides a carcass. There are two skeletons up there, Wren. Small ones. And their skulls—" His voice caught, and he mastered it, because Aldric could master anything except happiness. "Their skulls were broken. Fever doesn't break skulls."

The wind came down the pass and moved the mist around us, and the boot lay there, and I looked at it, and I made the mistake I had never once made in eleven years of service.

*I believed it.*

Not gradually. Not with the careful scaffolding of evidence and inquiry that I would have demanded from any novice presenting the same claim. I saw that small boot with its nursery stamp and I believed, instantly and totally, that King Vaelric the Uniter had murdered two children, and that I had knelt at their christening, and that the god I served had let me spend a decade guarding the man who did it.

Belief is a strange country. You can live there your whole life and never see the borders until you cross one.

"That's not possible," I said, and my voice sounded far away to me. "The physician certified—"

"The physician was Odile Marchand's guildmaster's brother," said Aldric, "and he retired to a coastal estate the following spring. Check it. You're good at checking. It's the only part of the job you ever loved."

That was true, and it cut, because he knew it was true. I had joined the Inquisition out of grief—everyone does, though none of us admit it—and I had stayed because the work suited the shape of my mind. Questions, answers, the clean click of a case closing. A world where wrongness could be found and named and burned out. I had told myself for eleven years that this was justice.

Standing on the ice road with a dead child's boot melting a ring into the snow at my feet, I understood for the first time that I had built my whole life inside a house whose foundations I had been forbidden to inspect.

"Why show me this?" I asked. "Why not just run? You have hours on us. By dawn you could be at the gate."

"Because you'll catch us anyway," Aldric said simply. "You're better than your riders, and you don't tire, and you don't stop. So I'm asking you instead. One knight to another." He hesitated, and then, quietly, with the care of a man laying down a card he can't take back: "One friend to another."

"We were never friends."

"No," he agreed. "We were something more honest than friends. We were the last two people alive who remember her court. Everyone else who danced in those halls is dead or bought." He looked past me, up the pass, toward where the mountain's shoulder hid the gate. "She's up there, Wren. Or the thing that was stolen from her is. The smuggler carries the key in her blood, and the fox—"

"The fox," I repeated flatly.

"There's a fox. It's complicated."

Of course it was complicated. Everything true was complicated; only doctrine came in clean lines. That should have warned me. Instead I heard myself ask, in the voice of a novice rather than an inquisitor:

"If the heirs were murdered—if the king—" I couldn't finish it. Eleven years of vocabulary, and I couldn't assemble the sentence.

"Then everything you burned was kindling for a liar's fire," Aldric said. "And every confession you closed was a grave you dug." He wasn't cruel about it. That was the worst part. He said it the way you'd tell a woman the road ahead is flooded—gently, factually, giving her time to turn around.

I thought about the file with its nine-year gap. *Someone had buried it.* I had always assumed it was some minor functionary's laziness. But mercy leaves fingerprints, and so do cover-ups, and I suddenly remembered that the file bore a seal of second review—the Inquisitor-Prime's own office—and that the Inquisitor-Prime answered to no one but the crown, and that I had kissed that signet ring every morning for six years.

I thought about Casper Sterling at nine years old, displaying *unusual calm*, recommended for observation, and then vanished from the record as neatly as two small skeletons vanished under a cairn. Buried by kindness. Hidden from the machine. And here she was, eleven years later, walking straight into the heart of that machine carrying whatever the dying courier had given her, and my orders were to stop her, and my orders came from—

"Your riders," Aldric said. "How close?"

"Two miles. Maybe less. They'll reach this stretch within the hour." I heard myself say it and marveled, distantly, at how easily treason left the mouth once the first word was out. Like blood. Like anything else under pressure.

"And behind them?"

"Halloway's second company holds the weir. The gate garrison is forty men." I paused. "Was forty men. I reduced the watch rotation last week, citing supply concerns. There are nineteen at the gate tonight, and the eastern postern is logged as sealed but the bar's rope is rotted through; I inspected it myself and filed no repair order."

Aldric stared at me. For a long moment neither of us spoke, and the wind filled the silence with the sound of the mountain breathing.

"You've known," he said finally. "Some part of you has known for years."

"I've suspected," I corrected. "Suspecting is not knowing. Knowing requires proof, and proof requires—" I stopped. Laughed, briefly, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Proof requires trusting yourself to look. And I haven't trusted myself since Redford Bridge either, Aldric. Did you know that? They gave you cowardice and gave me promotion, and we both got the same wound, just dressed differently."

He came toward me then, slowly, and did the most dangerous thing anyone had done to me in a decade: he put his hand on my shoulder, the way you greet a brother-at-arms, the way we used to greet each other in another life, in a courtyard full of banners that no longer existed.

"Come with us," he said.

And I want to tell you—since no one else will ever hear this account, and the mountain keeps no records—that for one whole breath, I wanted to. I wanted it the way the drowning want air. To set down the grey cloak and the grey eyes and the orchard of pruned beliefs, and walk up the pass beside the last honest man I knew, and knock on the doors of that pale underground court with my sword drawn and my conscience finally, finally aligned with my hand.

Then I heard the bells.

Small, high, silver-toned—the signal bells of my own outriders, two miles back, meaning they'd found sign. Meaning the hour I'd promised Aldric was already spent.

"No," I said, stepping back. His hand fell away. "If I vanish, they'll send for the Prime's own guard, and those aren't soldiers, they're instruments. You'd be hunted by something that doesn't sleep or doubt." I managed something that was almost my old dry smile. "Besides. Someone has to be inside the machine when it breaks."

"Wren—"

"Nineteen men at the gate. Eastern postern, rotted rope, unlogged. The garrison changes watch at moonset; there's a quarter-hour gap on the eastern wall." I was speaking fast now, pulling eleven years of knowledge out of my ribs like arrows. "Halloway's company will be ordered to hold the weir until relieved—they won't advance without me, I've trained them to wait for my word, it's the one flaw I built into them on purpose, gods help me, I built it years ago without knowing why."

"You planned your own rebellion and called it discipline," Aldric said.

"I called it thoroughness. Rebellion is just thoroughness with a conscience attached." The bells rang again, closer in feel if not in fact, and I straightened, and I watched the old Wren settle back over my face like a visor coming down. But underneath the visor, everything was different. Everything had a crack in it now, and light comes through cracks or it doesn't come at all. "Go. Take the smuggler and go. When you reach the gate, the watch will be looking west, because I will report movement on the western approach, and I will report it convincingly, because lying to the Inquisition is the one skill I have never practiced and apparently it comes naturally."

"And if they ask you to lead them after us? Into the deep places?"

"Then I'll lead them," I said. "Slowly. Wrongly. Through every flooded corridor and dead-end passage Marchand's guild ever mapped." I held his eyes. "And at some point, Aldric—at the moment it matters most—I will choose. I don't yet know what choosing looks like. But I know it's coming, and I'd rather meet it armed."

He gripped my forearm, soldier-fashion, hard enough to hurt, which was its own kind of blessing. "The queen remembers her own," he said. "Even now. Especially now."

"The queen is dead," I said automatically—doctrine, reflex, the orchard's last pruning—and then I heard my own words and hated them. "The queen is *stolen*," I corrected. "There's a difference. Go make it matter."

He went. Up the pass, into the mist, gone. I stood alone on the ice road with the wind and the bells and a dead child's boot, and after a moment I picked the boot up, wrapped it back in the oilskin with the rest of the proof, and tucked it inside my coat, against my heart, where I keep the things I am not yet strong enough to carry in my hands.

* * *

My riders found me an hour later, kneeling at the cairn site, taking notes. I showed them nothing. I told them the trail bent west, and they believed me, because I have spent eleven years becoming the kind of person who is believed.

Do you understand the cost of that? It takes a decade to build a reputation for incorruptibility, and you spend it in a single hour, and the coin never comes back. From tonight forward, every report I file will be a small murder of the truth, and each one will feel like cutting a branch from my own tree. I have been the most reliable instrument in the Prime's cabinet. Tonight I begin my long, patient work of becoming unreliable—and I must do it so gradually, so artfully, that no one notices the rot until the whole structure falls exactly where I want it to fall.

Tonight I reported movement on the western approach. Tomorrow I will misfile the gate garrison's strength. The day after, I will lose a dispatch. And somewhere in the deep roads beneath the mountain, a smuggler who talks to beasts and a fox who is more than a fox and a cartographer with guilty hands and the last knight of a drowned court will be moving toward the vault where a stolen soul waits in pledge—and when they get there, when the moment comes, the King's own Inquisitor will be standing between them and the King's justice.

Everyone believes I will draw my blade and end it.

They are almost right.

I will draw my blade.

Where it lands—where it *lands*—is the only prayer I have left. I turned it over all night in the dark of my tent, while the mountain hummed above me like a struck bell: not a prayer to the god of the Concordat, whose scriptures I had recited over burning confesses for eleven years. An older prayer. The kind you make to a drowned queen whose cradle-oaths you broke without knowing it, whose children's bones you slept ten feet above and never thought to dig.

*Let me be quick,* I prayed. *Let me be brave. And if I cannot be either—let me at least be late.*

At moonset the garrison changed watch. In the east, unseen, a rotted rope waited.

And far above, on the mountain's white shoulder, something small and red ran along the ridgeline against the stars—running downhill, running toward the gate, running like a message with legs—and I watched it go, and I felt the last branch fall from the tree.

The fox had come to open the way.

And I had come, at last, to decide what I was for.
