## 20. Aftermath of Water

I have drawn maps for tyrants. I have drawn maps for merchants who wanted to move contraband past customs men with soft eyes and softer hands. I have drawn a map of a prison I helped design, though I did not know it was a prison at the time — that is the sentence I have been rehearsing for three days now, and it still tastes like copper.

But until this morning, I had never drawn a map while weeping.

It is not dignified. Let the record show that Odile Marchand, journeyman cartographer of the Guild of Vaelric's Court, sat on a wet step in a drowned throne room with her ink freezing in its pot, and cried like a girl whose mother had just told her the dog had gone to live on a farm.

The water was going down. That was the miracle nobody had time to appreciate. When the queen rose — when Casper poured herself into that crown of light and the drowned woman stood up out of her own name — every binding Vaelric had woven into the mountain came apart at once, and the flood-waters that had drowned these roads for twenty years remembered they were supposed to be rivers. They left the way guests leave a party when the host has died: politely, all at once, taking things with them that no one agreed to lend.

Which meant the passages were opening. And which meant someone had to map them.

Someone always has to map them. It is the curse of my profession. Other people get to stand in awe; I stand in awe *and* take notes.

* * *

Here is what the court beneath the mountain looked like as it emptied:

Imagine a cathedral full of candles, and then imagine that each candle was a person who had sold pieces of themselves to stay safe — a memory here, a joy there, a name, a face, a grief they decided was too expensive to keep. The pale courtiers. They had sung Vaelric's praises in voices like wind through reeds, because praise was cheap and everything else cost.

Now the vault was open, and the queen's restoration had cracked something fundamental in the architecture of the bargain, and all those sold pieces were coming home.

They went up. That is the only word for it. They did not walk to the exits; they *ascended*, drifting through stone as if stone had only ever been a courtesy, rising through the mountain's throat toward the grey daylight none of them had seen in years. Some of them laughed. Some of them made small sounds of recognition, like people finding their own houses after long wars. One old courtier paused beside me on his way to nothingness, looked at my map, and said, "You've got the third stair wrong," and then he dissolved into light, and I corrected the third stair, because even the dead deserve accurate cartography.

Aldric stood in the middle of it all with his sword point-down on the flagstones like a man at a funeral, which, I suppose, he was attending — his own, in a way. He had fed his worst memory to the Gate of Teeth two days ago. The battle that broke him. The order he obeyed, or didn't, depending on which version of the story you believed before the gate ate the evidence. He would never know which man he'd been. He carried that absence the way he carried everything: straight-backed, jaw set, as if absence were a weight you could train with.

"You're staring," he said, without looking at me.

"I'm observing. There's a difference. Staring is free."

His mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. It had been doing it more often since the weir, since Halloway turned her blade aside and bought us the corridor, since the world stopped trying to kill us for approximately one afternoon. "How long until your map is done?"

"Maps are never done. That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing anyone believes." I dipped my pen. "Give me until the water finishes leaving. Then I'll give you every passage from here to the surface, marked, measured, and annotated so thoroughly that no one will ever be lost down here again. No one will ever be *kept* down here again. That's the part that matters."

He nodded slowly. "Your master drew this place once."

"My master drew this place once," I agreed. "And I inked the borders. And I told myself it was just a commission." I smoothed the vellum. "So this one's mine. Not the guild's. Mine. Every line paid for."

He didn't say anything kind, which I appreciated. Aldric's kindnesses tend to arrive as silence, positioned strategically.

* * *

Casper had not moved in six hours.

She lay where she had fallen — where we had caught her, Aldric and I, mid-embrace, mid-burning — on the steps below the empty dais, wrapped in every cloak the three of us owned between us. Her breathing was slow and even and terribly ordinary. Her face was smooth in a way that frightened me more than blood would have. Blood tells you a story. Smoothness tells you nothing.

Ashen sat beside her. The fox had not been a fox-shaped thing in some hours; it had let itself become large, or perhaps it had simply stopped pretending, and now it was a creature the size of a wolfhound with fur like woodsmoke and eyes that held weather. It had not spoken since the crown finished drinking. I think speaking cost it something. I think most things cost everyone something, down here, up there, everywhere; I think that is the whole of economics and I should have studied theology instead.

"Will she wake?" I asked it. I had learned, over the weeks, that Ashen answered questions you actually meant.

"Her body woke hours ago. She is dreaming without furniture." The fox's voice was still that strange layered thing, an old woman and a young one and something neither. "We took the rooms out of her. We can build new ones."

"With what materials?"

"With us." Ashen looked at me then, and I felt audited. "With stories. Memory is not only what happened to you. It is also what other people refuse to let go of."

I wrote that down. Old habits. Then I underlined it, which is not an old habit, but seemed warranted.

* * *

By late afternoon the last of the water had gone, and the throne room stood revealed in a state I can only describe as *embarrassed*. Twenty years underwater had softened everything — the pillars wore shawls of silt, the banners had rotted into abstract art, the great seal of the drowned kingdom on the floor was cracked through the middle like a dropped plate. Vaelric's additions had fared worse. His black iron bindings had shattered into fragments that lay scattered across the flags, and every fragment was rusting *fast*, visibly, hungrily, as if the metal were eager to be done with having existed.

The queen walked among it all barefoot.

I want to record accurately what she looked like, because I am a professional and professionals record accurately, and also because I do not trust my own memory anymore — I have seen too much of what happens when memory becomes currency. She was tall. She was dark-haired, and the hair moved as if it were still partly water. She wore a gown the color of deep river glass, and where the drowned crown had been — the crown of water and light that Casper had filled with herself — there was now nothing, and the nothing fit her better than any iron ever could have.

She stopped in front of our little camp. Our little camp being: one disgraced knight, one fox-spirit, one cartographer with ink on her chin, and one smuggler dreaming without furniture.

"You kept a stranger's promise," the queen said.

Her voice did a strange thing in that hall. It did not echo. It *arrived* — in the chest first, then the ears, like hearing your own name called across water.

"Technically," I said, because someone had to, and apparently it was always going to be me, "we kept several promises, most of them made by other people, under duress, in the dark. The stranger in question was her grandfather. He spent forty years trying to buy back what was taken from you. He died at a river ford before he could finish the job." I gestured at Casper, at the cloaks, at the whole ridiculous arrangement. "She finished it. What it cost her—" My voice did something unscheduled. I cleared my throat and consulted my map as if it contained guidance. "What it cost her is itemized elsewhere."

The queen knelt. A queen. Knelt. On wet flagstones, in front of the three conscious members of our party, and bowed her head.

"I knew a man of the Sterling line once," she said. "He mended nets in my harbor town and argued with me about tariffs. He had his daughter's laugh and his granddaughter's stubbornness, though of course I couldn't have known that last part yet." She looked up, and her eyes were the exact color of the moment before rain. "Tell me what he asked for. At the ford. His last words."

"'Return what was sold,'" Aldric said quietly.

"And she did." The queen rose. She touched Casper's forehead with two fingers, and the touch left a faint shimmer, like breath on cold glass. "That debt is paid beyond payment. Whatever she lost, she lost as a gift freely given, and gifts freely given leave a shape behind. You'll see. Shapes remember."

Then she turned to me, and I discovered that being perceived by a restored queen is very much like being perceived by Ashen, except with better posture.

"You mapped the ways out."

"Someone had to. Your Majesty, if I may — the guild's charts of this place are in circulation. Vaelric's surveyors, my hand among them. As long as those exist, this court can be found again, entered again, *used* again. So I'm drawing a new one. Complete. Public. Every passage named and numbered, copies to every free city, so that this place belongs to everyone's knowledge and therefore no one's ambition." I swallowed. "It's also an apology. In cartographic form. It's the only form I've got."

The queen smiled, and the temperature of the entire cavern improved by several degrees, which I am fairly sure was not intentional on her part and is going to make her reign either wonderful or exhausting.

"Cartographer," she said, "when your guild comes asking why their indentured daughter's work bears no guild mark, tell them the truth: that the mark was washed off in the flood, and the flood does not negotiate."

* * *

We carried Casper up ourselves. All four of us — yes, including the fox, who took turns, and whose turn involved carrying Casper in a manner I have elected not to describe because I have a reputation and also because I don't fully understand how it worked and I refuse to put speculation in a field document.

The new passages were everything I'd charted and half again more, because the mountain, unbound, had begun rearranging itself like a sleeper stretching after a long illness. Stairs where walls had been. Air moving through corridors that had been drowned airless for decades. Twice we passed ascending lights — souls still making their way home, unhurried now, nodding to us as they passed like neighbors out for evening walks.

At the second such encounter, Aldric stopped and watched one drift by — a young man, or the light that had been one — and said, "I keep waiting to feel something about the gate. About what I gave it. And there's just... a clean space. Like a tooth gone from the row."

"Does it hurt?"

"No. That's the strange part. It just whistles a bit, in the wind."

I drew the passage we were standing in. I labeled it, after some deliberation, *Vane's Corridor*, and did not explain the label, and he saw it, and said nothing, and stood a little straighter for the rest of the day. That is how honor works, I have decided. Not in grand gestures. In labels on maps, in silences positioned strategically, in a friend deciding your worst day gets a good name.

* * *

We reached the surface at dusk on the third day, at a mouth of stone high above the treeline of Thornfell, and the evening air hit us like forgiveness.

Below us, far off across the valleys, the free cities were dark. The beacons still cold. But there were lights moving in the lowlands — riders, watchfires, the first confused stirrings of a world that had felt the mountain break and did not yet know what it meant. Somewhere out there, Wren Halloway was alive, holding a door she had chosen to hold, carrying proof that would crack her king's story open like ice at thaw. Somewhere out there, spring was coming whether anyone was ready or not.

We made camp in the lee of the stone mouth. Aldric built the fire with the competence of a man who has done it ten thousand times and remembers none of the specific occasions. I spread my master map — the true one, the public one, the apology — and began fair-copying the final sheets by firelight.

And Casper opened her eyes.

Not all the way. Not for long. But she turned her head toward the firelight, toward the three of us — the knight, the cartographer, the smoke-colored fox curled against her side like a living scarf — and she frowned, the small frown of a person trying to place a word on the tip of the tongue.

"Do I..." she said. Her voice was rusty. "Do I know you?"

The silence lasted exactly as long as it takes a heart to decide something.

"Yes," said Aldric.

"Yes," I said.

"*Yes,*" said Ashen, in the voice of an old woman, a young one, and something that was neither, and something that was, unmistakably, family.

Casper looked at us for a long moment. Then, bewildered, exhausted, and — I would swear this on every map I ever falsified — a little comforted, she said:

"Well. Someone had better start at the beginning, because I don't seem to have one."

I uncapped my ink.

As it happens, I had brought paper.
