## 21. The Name I Gave Away

The first thing I knew was the smell of boiled linen and tallow, which is the smell of every infirmary in every free city, and the second thing I knew was that I did not know my own name.

I want to be clear about how strange that is. Not frightening, at first—strange, like waking in a room where all the furniture has been moved two inches to the left. You know what everything *is*. You just don't know why it's there, or whose it was before it was yours.

There was a woman asleep in a chair beside my bed with ink on her fingers and a map folded under her cheek like a pillow she didn't trust. There was a man standing at the window with his back to me, broad as a door, wearing no colors at all—which, for a man built like that, is its own kind of uniform. And on the sill, washing one paw with the concentration of a scholar correcting a manuscript, sat a fox.

The fox looked at me first.

"You're awake," it said.

I did not scream, which I count as evidence of good character, because a talking fox will do that to you. The woman woke instead, dropped her map, said something that was half my name and half a prayer—"Casper, oh gods, Casper"—and then the man turned around and his face did something complicated, relief and grief holding hands, and I lay there under three pairs of eyes that all seemed to be waiting for me to say something I had no idea how to say.

"I'm sorry," I managed. "Who's Casper?"

* * *

They told me the shape of it over the next days, in pieces, taking turns like people passing a heavy load.

The woman was Odile Marchand, cartographer, formerly indentured to Vaelric's guild, currently free and still slightly astonished by it. She talked fast when she was nervous, which was always, and she drew while she talked, so that by the third day my bedside looked like a mapmaker's fever dream: causeways, weirs, a mountain cut open like a fruit to show the court inside.

The man was Ser Aldric Vane, though he flinched when you used the *Ser*, so mostly I didn't. He'd been a knight of the drowned queen's guard. He spoke slowly, like each word cost him something, and he kept looking at me as if checking whether I was still there.

And the fox was Ashen, who declined to explain itself beyond "I am bound, and now less bound than I was," which is the sort of answer that sounds wise until you realize it's a fox dodging a question.

Here is what they told me had happened, or the parts of it they could bear to say out loud:

A dying man gave me a map. I crossed the drowned roads of the old kingdom with these three. We reached the court beneath Thornfell. There was a usurper king named Vaelric, and a stolen soul—the soul of the drowned queen herself—and the only way to give it back was to pay for it with memories of equal weight.

Mine.

"Whose memories?" I asked, the first night, when I still thought this was a story about someone else.

Odile and Aldric looked at each other. It was Aldric who answered, in that slow way of his, like a man walking on ice he'd already fallen through once.

"Yours," he said. "All of them. Everything from your birth to the moment you poured yourself into that crown."

"And what came back?"

He was quiet a long time. "You did," he said finally. "Breathing. Warm. Alive." A pause. "That was the part we cared about."

* * *

You would think losing everything would feel like losing everything. It doesn't. It feels like being handed someone else's life and being asked to sign for it.

I knew things without knowing them. My hands knew knots—I found myself tying off a loose bandage with a smuggler's hitch before I caught myself doing it. My ears knew things too. When a sparrow landed on the windowsill and chirped something dismissive about the quality of the bread crumbs below, I understood it, perfectly, casually, the way you understand your own language, and I sat very still afterward with my heart going like a drum, because whatever else I had given away, the gift had stayed.

"That doesn't surprise her," Odile said softly, when I told them. She wasn't looking at me; she was looking at her hands. "She said once—it was in the mountains, after the weir—she said the gift was the only thing anyone had ever given her that couldn't be taken back. Not by her mother, not by the guilds, not by kings." Her mouth twisted. "I think she'd have been furious if it went with the rest."

*I*. She said *she*, and meant *me*, and I felt like a man reading his own obituary written by strangers who had loved him better than he'd loved himself.

"What was I like?" I asked. It became my question, over those weeks. I asked it the way other patients ask about the weather. "What was I like? Was I kind? Was I awful? Be honest, I can take it."

"You were awful," Odile said promptly, and then ruined it by grinning. "You were suspicious of everyone. You had rules. Never be remembered, never owe anyone, never sleep in the same place twice. You tried to abandon us in chapter six."

"There are no chapters," Aldric said wearily.

"It's how I keep it straight," Odile said. "It's a long story. It deserves structure."

But here is the thing I noticed, the thing I kept noticing until it wore a groove in me: whenever they described me, they described someone worth missing. They didn't soften her. They told me about the woman who turned wolves against soldiers and then couldn't meet their eyes for a day. They told me about the woman who lied to them, ran from them, planned to slip away in the night at least four times that Odile knew of. And they told me about the woman who came back anyway. Who stood between Aldric and an inquisitor's blade. Who walked into a vault full of her own worst price and paid it without flinching, because somewhere along the drowned roads of a kingdom that everyone said was dead, she had stopped being alone, and she had decided that was worth more than remembering why she'd wanted to be.

"She chose it," Aldric said, the night I finally asked the question underneath all my questions: *did she know what it would cost?*

"She knew," he said. "Ashen told her. In the cave-in, weeks before. The promise was never named, but she made Ashen tell her the whole weight of it before she agreed to anything." His jaw worked. "I argued with her. In the vault. I told her we'd find another way. There wasn't another way, but I told her anyway, because I could not stand—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Because I have lost enough people who died for causes. I could not stand to lose one who lived for hers. But she looked at me and she said, *Aldric, I spent my whole life making sure no one would grieve me. Let me spend it once on something worth grieving.*"

The room was quiet. The fox, curled at the foot of my bed, opened one eye.

"She said that?" I asked.

"She said that," Ashen confirmed. Its voice was dry as autumn leaves. "I remember everything. Someone in this company has to."

* * *

The grafting, Odile called it. That was her word, borrowed from orchard-keepers: you take a cutting from a tree that bears well and bind it to rootstock that survives, and if you're patient, the two become one tree. Memory, she insisted, worked the same way, if the soil was willing.

So they farmed me.

Every evening, after the healers made their rounds and the lamps were trimmed, they told me the story again—not the summary, the *story*, with all its bad weather and worse jokes. Odile had started writing it down, actually, in a notebook she guarded like contraband: *The Drowned Crown, being a true account of the road beneath the mountain.* She read aloud from it some nights, and corrected herself other nights, and once threw the whole notebook across the room because she'd gotten the order of the chapel confession wrong and Aldric wouldn't let her revise history in her own favor.

"You confessed to mapping prison routes," Aldric reminded her. "In a flooded chapel. While crying."

"I was *not* crying, the water was dripping—"

"It was a roof, Odile. The roof was intact."

"The roof was *compromised*—"

I laughed. It startled all three of us, the laugh, coming out of me unbidden, and Odile went pink and Aldric smiled like a man watching spring come early, and I realized that this was the mechanism, this was the whole trick of it: they weren't telling me facts. Facts slide off a person with no past to stick them to. They were telling me a story, and stories don't need your permission to take root. Every night the tale went in a little deeper. The marmot who talked too much. The crows and the half-mad goat. The gate of teeth, and what Aldric had paid to hold it open—he doesn't remember the battle that disgraced him anymore, and when I asked if he regretted it, he said, "I remember what I learned from it. The rest was just scenery." Halloway, the inquisitor who turned her blade aside at the last. The vault. The crown of water and light.

And the last thing, which they only told me once, and only because I demanded it, and only because Odile's rule was *we don't lie to her, lying is how Vaelric got a whole kingdom*:

At the end, mid-embrace, my past burned away. And the last expression on my face, according to both witnesses independently, was joy.

* * *

Some pieces rooted faster than others.

My hands remembered knots and locks and the particular way you move through a crowd so that no eye catches on you. Those came back within days, muscle-deep, older than memory. My gift came back whole, and with it a small crisis, because the infirmary cat had opinions about the healers and shared them freely, and I spent one entire afternoon learning that Mistress Ellery watered down the tonic and blamed the cats for the missing spoons.

My name came back last, and it didn't come back so much as get handed to me, over and over, until I accepted it.

"Casper," Odile would say, tossing me an apple across the ward. "Catch, Casper."

"Casper," Aldric would say at the door each morning, "the healer says you're to walk today. Don't argue with her, Casper. You'll lose."

"Casper," Ashen said once, from the windowsill, in that dry leaf-voice, "was the only human I ever met who bargained fairly with something older than kingdoms. Do not make me revise my opinion."

And one gray morning, waking before any of them, I lay in the infirmary quiet and said it to myself—*my name is Casper Sterling*—and it fit. Not like a key finding its lock. Like a graft taking. Like something cut from one tree, bound to another, growing anyway, because someone had been patient with the soil.

I cried a little. Then the sparrow on the sill informed me that I was leaking, and I laughed instead, and that was that.

* * *

On the day they let me walk to the end of the street and back, spring was working at the edges of the city—snowmelt loud in the gutters, the harbor smelling of salt and thaw. Aldric walked on my left, Odile on my right, Ashen trotting ahead like a scout who owned the road, and people looked at us, and for the first time in either of my lives, I found I didn't mind being seen.

"We got a letter," Odile said, casual, too casual. "Yesterday. From Thornfell."

"From Thornfell," I repeated.

"From the queen's stewardship. Well—from the queen. She's taken mortal charge of the mountain, did we tell you that part? Yes, we told you that part." Odile fidgeted with her notebook. "Anyway. The beacons are being relit at spring equinox. All the free cities, together, the first time in a generation. And she asks—" Odile glanced at Aldric, who nodded once, slow, giving her the go-ahead like a man handing over something fragile. "She asks whether the woman who returned her name would care to attend. As an honored guest. Or, her words—" Odile cleared her throat, consulted the letter, and read: "*'As family, if she'll have such a thing.'*"

The street noise filled up the silence. Snowmelt, cart wheels, a fishmonger arguing with a gull that was arguing back in fluent Gull.

"I don't remember her," I said quietly. "I don't remember any of it. Is that—is that fair to her? To walk into her coronation or whatever it is, and not know why she's looking at me like that?"

"She won't mind," Aldric said. "Believe me. She spent a hundred years as a crown of water in a dark room. She knows exactly what it is to be handed back to people who remember you better than you remember yourself." He looked down at me, and there was the grief and the relief again, holding hands, but easier now, like old friends who'd stopped fighting over the bill. "Besides. You won't be walking in knowing nothing. You'll be walking in knowing us."

Ashen stopped at the corner and looked back, tail high, amber eyes catching the new sun.

"Well, Casper?" the fox said. "Shall we go and be remembered?"

I took Odile's arm, because my legs were still traitors, and Aldric's shoulder, because he offered, and I grinned at a fox, because apparently that was my life now.

"Lead on," I said. "I hear the road's beautiful this time of year."
