## 22. Beacons Rekindled

The first thing I learned about having a family was that they wake you up at dawn for no good reason.

"Get up," Odile said, shaking my shoulder with the enthusiasm of a woman who had never once in her life been on time for anything and had decided to compensate by making everyone else early. "You'll miss it."

"Miss what?"

"The beacons."

I lay there a moment in the narrow bed of the Wayfarer's Rest, staring at the water-stained ceiling, doing the arithmetic of who I was supposed to be. My name was Casper Sterling. I knew that because Aldric had told me eleven times, patiently, the way you'd teach a child her letters. I was a smuggler—or I had been; apparently I'd retired from that, which struck me as the sort of decision a sensible person would make and therefore one I couldn't remember making. I could speak to beasts. That I remembered. That I felt in my bones like the ache of an old scar, familiar and mine.

Everything else was a country I'd heard described secondhand.

"Casper." Odile again, insistent. "Up. The whole city's going to the walls."

* * *

Briarhaven smelled of woodsmoke and thaw, of mud and new green things pushing up through old ruin. The free cities had spent a hard winter learning to hope again, and hope, it turned out, looked like everyone in town climbing onto the ramparts before sunrise carrying torches that hadn't been lit in years.

They stood along the old beacon line—the chain of fire-towers the drowned queen had built when the world was younger, the ones that spoke city to city across the dark: *we are here, we hold, we are not alone.* Then Vaelric came, and the beacons went cold one by one, and the silence between the cities grew so long that people forgot the towers were ever meant to speak at all. They'd started dousing them entirely last autumn, out of despair. I'm told I learned this from Odile over dinner, twice, and cried both times. I don't remember. But I believed it, because I'd seen the way she told it—hands moving, drawing the coastline in the air like she was correcting an error in one of her maps.

Now the towers were manned again. Now the whole wall of Briarhaven was packed shoulder to shoulder, breath steaming in the cold spring air, waiting.

"They say the queen herself lights the first one," said a boy beside me, up on his father's shoulders. "The drowned queen. She's real. She came down off the mountain."

"She's real," Aldric said quietly, beside me. He was in no livery at all—Ser Aldric Vane, knight without a banner, wearing a plain wool coat with a mended elbow, and he seemed lighter for it than I'd ever seen him in armor. "I can vouch for that personally."

"You've met her?" the boy demanded.

"I've carried her crown," Aldric said, "a very long way."

And then the first tower caught.

It went up like a held breath finally released—a column of gold against the grey dawn, and the crowd made a sound I'd only ever heard beasts make, that low collective note of a herd safe on open ground. And then the next tower answered, far off to the south, a spark on a distant ridge. And then the next. And the next. City to city, ridge to ridge, the old sentence being spoken again after all these silent years: *we are here, we hold, we are not alone.*

I found I was crying. I didn't know why, exactly. I knew the shape of why—I knew that somewhere under the missing months there was a version of me who had walked into a mountain and given away everything she was to light these fires—but the feeling itself was unaccompanied by any picture, any face, any name. Just grief and joy holding hands with nothing to point at.

A weight settled on my shoulder. Ashen.

The fox sat on the parapet beside me as if parapets were where foxes belonged, fur the color of banked embers, eyes reflecting the beacon-light like two small captive flames. He had not left my side since I woke in the infirmary. When I asked him why, he said, "Because someone has to remember," which was either very sweet or very ominous, and with Ashen it was usually both.

"Do you want to know?" he asked now, softly, under the cheering. "What happened. What you did. I could show you. It would cost you nothing now—the price is paid."

I watched the beacons burn their way down the spine of the world.

"No," I said.

Odile turned sharply. "Casper—"

"It's not that I don't want to know." I scratched Ashen behind the ears, and he permitted it with the weary dignity of a monarch accepting tribute. "It's that whoever I was, she did it *so that* I wouldn't have to carry it. She bought me clean. The least I can do is not go rummaging through the till."

Ashen made a sound that might have been a laugh. "She said something similar. Right before she—" He stopped. "Right at the end."

"What did she say?"

"That she hoped you'd be happier than she was." His tail curled around his feet. "She wasn't unhappy, mind. She just didn't trust happiness. Thought it was a door left unlocked."

"And now?"

"Now I think she'd like to see how you handle it."

* * *

The queen came to Briarhaven three days later, and she came on foot.

No procession worth the name—just a tall woman in a travel-stained cloak walking up the causeway road with six pale courtiers trailing her like ghosts learning to walk again, and half the city running alongside trying to get a look at her. She was not what I expected. I'm not sure what I expected. Someone more golden, maybe. Someone who glowed.

Instead she looked tired in the specific way of someone who has slept a hundred years and is still catching up, and she stopped dead in the market square when she saw me, and the whole crowd went quiet around us like a held note.

"Casper Sterling," she said.

"That's me." I glanced at Aldric. "Allegedly."

Something moved across her face—grief and gratitude tangled past separating. "You don't remember me."

"No, ma'am. Sorry. I'm told we met."

"We did." She reached out and took both my hands in hers, and hers were cold as river-water and steady as stone. "Then let me tell you what I remember, since one of us should. I remember waking in a vault of names, and finding a girl standing over me pouring her whole life into my crown like it was nothing. Like it was a cup of tea she was offering. I remember her laughing while she did it, because she was terrified, and that is the bravest thing I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a knight hold a gate of teeth open with his bare hands." A glance at Aldric, who went red as a brick. "I remember everything you gave me, child. Every smuggled cargo, every winter alone, every beast you ever spoke with. You gave me your grandfather back too—he was woven all through you, that stubborn, faithful man. I will keep him well."

I didn't know the man she meant. But my chest hurt anyway, in the place where knowing lives, and I nodded.

"Why?" I asked. It had been bothering me since the infirmary. "Why me? Why my blood? Why did the soul answer to my family at all?"

"Because your grandfather sold me," the queen said simply. "He and others, desperate men in a desperate year. They sold my soul to save their families from Vaelric's purge, and every one of them regretted it within the hour, and only your grandfather lived long enough to do anything about it. He spent forty years buying his way back toward me. And then, at the ford—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—he spent the last thing he had. Not coin. Blood. A map drawn in it, keyed to his own line, so that it could only ever lead home to you."

"He wanted me to finish it."

"He wanted you to *choose* it. There's a difference, and he knew it. He wrote in his journal—I have it now—that he refused to beg his granddaughter's ghost for anything. So he begged you instead. Living. With a choice." She squeezed my hands. "You chose better than he dared hope."

Behind her, one of the pale courtiers stepped forward—young, hollow-cheeked, but with color coming back into him week by week, like a painting restored. He bowed to me, deep and formal.

"My lady," he said, "on behalf of the court beneath the mountain—we who traded ourselves away piece by piece—we owe you our names. All of them. If ever you want yours back, even a piece of it, the vault stands open."

I thought about it honestly, right there in front of everyone. A piece of myself, returned. A memory or two, like coins found in an old coat.

"No," I said again, and found I meant it more each time. "Keep them. Put them somewhere useful."

The courtier smiled—first smile I'd seen from any of them—and the queen laughed, a sound like ice going out of a river.

"Then I have something else for you," she said. "Thornfell needs a steward. The mountain has been lawless so long its very stones have forgotten courtesy. The beasts respect few people. They respect you."

"I'm a smuggler," I said automatically.

"You were," said Odile.

"You're a *retired* smuggler," Aldric corrected, "which is a completely different profession with much better hours."

"The mountains need someone who listens," the queen said. "Not rules them. Listens. The wolves have been asking after you all winter, did you know? They call you the One Who Named Them. Names travel underground, Casper. Yours traveled farther than most."

I looked at Ashen. He looked back with an expression of profound innocence that fooled absolutely no one, least of all me.

"You knew about this," I said.

"I may have mentioned to certain wolves," Ashen said, "that a certain person might be persuaded to take an interest in mountain affairs. In exchange for certain considerations regarding rabbit populations."

"You negotiated with the wolves. On my behalf. Without asking me."

"I negotiated on behalf of *everyone*. It's called diplomacy. You should try it sometime instead of threatening marmots."

"I never threatened a marmot."

"There are witnesses."

* * *

We left Briarhaven a week later, on a bright morning with the mud still singing with meltwater, and I use "singing" technically rather than poetically—the worms really do hum in spring, low and contented, and if you put your ear to the ground you can hear the whole earth gossiping about the weather.

Our company was four. Five, if you counted the cart, which Odile insisted we count, because she had filled it with surveying instruments and paper and ink enough to redraw half the known world, starting with the drowned roads, "so that no one is ever lost down there again." She'd been released from her guild debt by royal decree—Vaelric's contracts voided root and branch—and her response to freedom had been to immediately indenture herself to a project of her own choosing, which she insisted was completely different and nobody was allowed to comment on.

Aldric rode at the front. No banner still, but the queen had given him a badge of office—steward-protector of the mountain roads—and he wore it pinned inside his coat, where only he knew it was, which told me more about him than any heraldry could have. Some honors you display. Some you just carry.

And Ashen trotted at my heel, as he had trotted at my heel through every version of my life I could remember and several I couldn't.

The road north climbed toward Thornfell, and Thornfell, for the first time in living memory, was not trying to kill anyone. The passes stood open. The patrol roads were empty of inquisitors—Wren Halloway had disbanded what remained of them herself, and gone east, they said, to the orphan houses of the riverlands, to keep whatever vows she still kept. I'd never met her. I knew her only as a name that made Aldric's jaw tighten and Odile go quiet, and as the woman who had held a door against Vaelric's own guard so that we—I—could live. I raised an imaginary glass to her anyway. Some debts you pay to strangers.

At the first waypoint above the valley, we stopped to eat, and a marmot emerged from the rocks to conduct negotiations regarding our cheese.

"Half the wheel," it said, "and I'll tell you where the best grazing is between here and the high pass, plus which bridges the flood took out."

"One slice," I said, "and I'll not mention to the eagles that you're fat enough to be worth diving for."

"Two slices and you drive a hard bargain for someone who supposedly retired from crime."

"Retirement is a state of mind." I cut the slices. "Also I'm keeping the third slice. Consider it tax."

The marmot took its winnings with dignity and told us, unprompted, that the wolves had left a mark on the boundary stone for me—a paw print pressed into fresh clay, which among Thornfell's packs meant *the road is ours to watch and you may sleep safely on it.*

I touched the print. It was still soft.

"Well?" Odile asked, watching my face. "What did it say?"

"That I'm expected."

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of the first true crags, under a sky rinsed clean by wind, and the beacons burned behind us to the south—one, two, five, nine, a constellation pinned to the dark earth—and ahead of us the mountain rose black and enormous and, for the first time, hospitable.

Aldric took first watch. Odile fell asleep mid-sentence over her maps, as usual, one hand still curled around her pen. Ashen curled in the crook of my knee, warm as a coal.

And I sat up a while longer, listening.

The mountain talked all night, the way it always had—it was only that now I understood it was talking *to* me. The goats grumbled about the grazing. An owl complained about the owls. Somewhere below, the flooded roads murmured their long slow song, the one about the queen who drowned and the queen who rose and the girl in between who gave everything and asked for nothing, not even her memory back.

That girl. I wondered about her constantly, the way you wonder about a sister you never met. Was she happy, before? Was the loneliness a wound or a wall? Did she know, walking into the mountain, that she was walking out of it into this—into a firelit camp with a snoring cartographer and an honorable fool and a fox who loved her in the prickly, sideways way foxes love?

I liked to think she'd guessed. She'd spoken to beasts her whole life; she must have known that beasts understand the one thing people won't say aloud, which is that no one makes it through alone, and the ones who try are the ones the mountain keeps.

"Go to sleep," Ashen said, without opening his eyes.

"In a minute."

"You're brooding. It's undignified."

"I'm *appreciating*."

"Brooding with extra steps." He opened one eye, ember-bright. "For what it's worth—she'd have liked this. The fire. The idiots. The road."

"You can't know that."

"I knew her for a very long time, and I know you, and I am telling you as a spirit of considerable age and no patience whatsoever: she would have liked this enormously, and then she would have pretended not to, and then she would have fallen asleep smiling, exactly like you're about to."

I lay back. The stars were thick overhead, and the beacons burned to the south, and the wolves' mark dried slowly on the boundary stone, keeping its promise.

Tomorrow we'd climb higher. Tomorrow Odile would map the passes and Aldric would argue with a bridge troll and I'd spend an afternoon mediating a territorial dispute between a lynx and a badger, because apparently that was the job now, and apparently I'd accepted it, and apparently—for the first time in either of my lives—I wasn't looking over my shoulder while I did it.

No one was hunting me. Nothing was sold. The road was open in every direction, and I knew exactly who I was:

Casper Sterling. Beast-speaker. Steward of Thornfell. Sister to a knight, a mapmaker, and a fox.

The mountain breathed around us, ancient and patient and, at last, kind.

And I fell asleep smiling, exactly as promised.
