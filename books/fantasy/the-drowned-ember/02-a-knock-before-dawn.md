## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The knock came three hours before the guild's bells were due to ring, and Paloma Halloran was awake to hear it because she had fallen asleep over Ledger Nine and dreamed of drowning in ink.

She woke with a gasp, cheek stuck to the page, and peeled herself free with the careful shame of someone who knew exactly how much trouble a drooled-upon ledger could cause. The candle had drowned in its own wax. Beyond the scriptorium's tall windows, Vaelmoor Harbor lay black and still beneath a sky the color of wet slate. The lamps of the lower city floated on the dark water like a spilled constellation.

Then: the knock again. Not at the scriptorium door — at the *guild* door. The great iron-bound one that faced the harbor steps and had not been answered after dark in forty years, because nothing came up those steps after dark except drunk sailors and the tide.

Paloma sat very still. The knock came a third time, smaller somehow, or perhaps she was only hearing it more clearly now that her heart had stopped hammering. Three knocks, evenly spaced. Patient. The knock of someone who expected the door to open because doors always opened for them.

*Someone should answer it.*

She thought of Aldous Vane's rooms on the third floor, of Senior Keeper Marris two doors down, of the six other keepers whose snores and turning-over she could practically hear through the stone. She thought, too, of the way they would look at her if she woke them for a knocking at an hour when knocking meant nothing good — the fond, dismissive tilt of the head, the *child*, the *back to bed*. She was nineteen years old and had been auditing the guild's ledgers alone for eleven months without a single error, and still they handed her quills the way one hands quills to a clever parrot.

So she went down herself, taking a lamp from its bracket and telling herself it was because she was the nearest, and not because some small stubborn coal in her chest wanted, just once, to be the one who answered.

The entry hall swallowed lamplight the way the harbor swallowed rain. Its vaulted ceiling vanished into shadow; the guild crest — an ember cupped in two stone hands — loomed above the inner door, soot-darkened by four centuries of candle smoke no one had ever been permitted to clean, because even the soot was catalogued. Paloma crossed the cold flagstones, set her hand on the bar, and hesitated.

Through the speaking-grille, at the height of a person much shorter than any sailor, something breathed.

"Who's there?" she called, and heard her own voice come out thinner than she'd have liked.

The answer was a word. One word, in a language made of soft consonants and long vowels that seemed to keep going after they ended, like ripples outlasting the dropped stone. It slid under the door and through the grille and into Paloma's ear, and every hair on her arms stood up — not from fear exactly, though fear was in there somewhere, mixed in like salt in seawater. It was the sound of a word she did not know and yet recognized, the way you recognize a face in a crowd of strangers because you've seen it painted on a wall your whole life.

She lifted the bar before she decided to. The door swung inward on hinges that groaned like something waking.

On the top step stood a child.

Not a beggar-child, though mud covered everything — thick grey-brown harbor mud, the kind that stank of rot and old fish and, faintly, underneath it all, of salt and something sweeter, like flowers grown underwater. A girl, maybe seven years old, maybe eight. She was soaked to the skin despite the fact that it hadn't rained in nine days. Her hair hung in ropes. Her feet were bare, blue-white with cold, and the mud on her legs rose past her knees as if she had waded through something deep — but the harbor steps below her were dry to within arm's reach of the waterline.

Her eyes were the color of lantern light seen through fog.

"Hello," Paloma said uselessly.

The child said the same word again. Then another sentence, quick and urgent, her small hands rising to gesture — *come, come, hurry* — and Paloma understood the urgency perfectly while understanding none of the words, which frightened her more than anything else, because language wasn't supposed to work that way. Language was inventory. Language was what she *did*. Every tongue in Vaelmoor had a grammar, and grammars could be learned, and learned things could be entered into ledgers, and entered things could be managed. This was a language that refused to be foreign. It felt less like hearing words and more like remembering them.

"You'd better come inside," Paloma heard herself say, which was against about nine separate rules of the Ember Guild, starting with Rule One and getting worse from there.

* *

The child sat where Paloma put her — on the bench by the great hearth, wrapped in a novice's blanket that had been sitting in a cedar chest since before Paloma's time — and looked around the entry hall with an expression that made the back of Paloma's neck prickle.

It wasn't wonder. Children looked at the guildhall with wonder; Paloma remembered looking at it that way herself, the day the guild took her in at age eight, orphaned and nameless, dazzled by the height of the ceilings. This child looked around the hall the way Paloma looked around a warehouse she'd been sent to appraise. Measuring. Checking things off.

And everywhere the child's gaze rested, the air smelled faintly of the sea.

That was the detail Paloma couldn't stop circling. The blanket smelled of cedar and then, increasingly, of brine. The hearth fire burned low and greenish for a quarter hour. The flagstones beneath the bench darkened slowly with damp, spreading outward from the child's bare feet in a rough circle, and Paloma watched the damp reach the edge of a floor tile and *stop*, precisely, as if it respected the tilework.

"Can you tell me your name?" Paloma asked.

The child looked at her. Said a string of syllables that were almost certainly her name — they had the shape of a name, the cadence — and then, seeing Paloma's blankness, pressed one muddy finger to her own chest and repeated it.

"Nen," Paloma tried. It was the closest her mouth could get. "Is that right? Nen?"

Something flickered across the child's face — not quite pleasure, not quite grief. She nodded once, gravely, and touched her chest again: *Nen.* Then she pointed at Paloma, head tilting, waiting.

"Paloma." She tapped her own sternum. "Paloma Halloran. Keeper — " and here the old reflex nearly finished the formula, *Keeper of the Third Rank, Guild of the Ember, in service to the city of Vaelmoor,* but something stopped her, some instinct she didn't have a word for yet, and instead she just said, "Paloma."

Nen smiled. It transformed her face entirely, and for a moment she was just a cold, wet, exhausted child sitting by a fire, and Paloma's chest ached with a tenderness that alarmed her in a completely different way.

Then Nen pointed at the ceiling, and said a word.

Paloma's blood went cold, though she couldn't have said why. The word was short, liquid, unmistakably a question. And the child was pointing not at the ceiling generally but at a specific place — the spot directly above the entry hall, where, according to every floor plan in the archive, there was nothing but the scriptorium's storage closet and, beneath its floorboards, sealed and catalogued and never opened in living memory, the Old Vault.

"No," Paloma said. "There's nothing there."

Nen looked at her with terrible, patient pity, the way one looks at someone who has miscounted their own fingers, and pointed again.

* *

Paloma told herself she went to the archive for blankets. That was the official story she composed on the stairs, quill-sharp and ready for any senior keeper who materialized: *the child needed dry clothes, I was being resourceful.* But the archive was on the way to nowhere useful, and her feet knew it, and by the time she admitted to herself what she was doing she was already standing in the restricted stacks with her lamp raised, facing the shelf where the founding ledgers lived.

Rule Twelve forbade unaccompanied access to the founding collection. Rule Thirteen forbade it altogether after dark. Paloma had spent her entire guild life treating the rules as scripture, and she stood there in the dust-smelling dark and realized, with a strange floating sensation, that she was going to break both of them, and that she could not fully explain why, and that this did not slow her down at all.

*Because the child pointed,* she told herself. *Because a mud-soaked girl who appeared out of a dry night pointed at our oldest secrets like a woman pointing at her own house.*

The founding ledgers occupied a case of blackened oak, each volume chained at the spine — chains of honor, really; no lock had ever held against a determined keeper, and the guild preferred its restrictions symbolic. Ledger One was the oldest book in Vaelmoor. Every keeper knew that. Every keeper also knew that nobody read Ledger One anymore, because it was written in pre-kingdom script, half of it ciphered, and the last scholar who claimed fluency in it had died eighty years ago taking his notes with him.

Paloma drew it out. It was heavier than she remembered, or she was lighter than she'd been. She carried it to the reading table, laid it open under her lamp, and turned pages until her hands trembled.

She wasn't sure what she was listening for until she heard it.

Nen's voice drifted faintly down through the floors — the child had begun, softly, to sing. A lullaby, or something shaped like one, rising and falling in that language of long vowels that outlasted themselves. Paloma stood frozen over Ledger One, and the hair rose on her arms a second time that night, because the marginalia on the page before her — cramped annotations in a later hand, squeezed between columns of pre-kingdom script — matched the melody note for note. Not the tune. The *words*. Someone, generations ago, had written down this exact song in the margins of the guild's first ledger, and had known it well enough to transcribe it, and had then — Paloma flipped forward, backward, forward — had then written beside it, in trade-glyph, a single notation she knew intimately because she used it daily:

*Received in full.*

Received. As if the song were a payment. As if someone had sung it once, across some boundary Paloma couldn't name, and the guild had recorded it the way they recorded barrels of pitch and bolts of dyed wool.

Her mouth had gone dry. She turned the pages faster. The marginalia thickened toward the middle of the ledger — words, phrases, whole lines of the drowned-sounding language, annotated in trade-glyph with values. *Received in full. Received, partial, balance owed. Received in full.* Page after page. A language treated as currency. Voices treated as goods.

And then, near the end of the volume, the entries changed character entirely. The receiving-hand grew hurried, ugly. One line was scored through so violently it had torn the vellum. Beside the tear, in letters dug deep enough to scar three pages beneath:

*NO MORE PAYMENT. LET THEM COME IF THEY DARE.*

Below that, nothing. The rest of the ledger was blank. Two hundred blank pages, as if the book itself had stopped breathing.

Downstairs, the singing stopped mid-phrase.

Paloma's lamp guttered. In the sudden deeper dark she heard her own heartbeat, and beneath it — or thought she did, or *felt* she did, rising up through the stone foundations of the guildhall, through four hundred years of accumulated ledgers and rules and soot-darkened crests — a slow, rhythmic pressure, like breath, like tide, like something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the harbor and beginning, at last, to wake.

She ran back down the stairs with Ledger One clutched to her chest like a shield, breaking Rule Fourteen (no removal of founding documents) without a second thought, and burst into the entry hall already talking:

"Nen, I need you to listen — I found — "

She stopped.

The hearth had gone out. Not died down: *out*, the coals black and frosted with a rime of salt crystals, glittering in the lamplight. The blanket lay folded on the bench. Neatly. Deliberately. And Nen stood at the far end of the hall, beneath the soot-darkened crest of the ember in its stone hands, looking up at it.

She turned. Her lantern-fog eyes found Paloma, and found the ledger in Paloma's arms, and something in her small face shifted — recognition, relief, and beneath it, so faint Paloma might have imagined it, fear.

Nen spoke one more word. Just one. And this time, though Paloma still could not translate it, she understood it completely, the way you understand a blow.

It meant: *finally.*

Above them, in the scriptorium's storage closet, in the sealed dark of the Old Vault that had not been opened in living memory, something knocked.

Three times. Evenly spaced. Patient.

The knock of someone who expected the door to open, because the debt was theirs, and they had come to collect.
