## 3. What the Ledgers Omit

The Founding Vault opened only twice a year, and Paloma had never once been permitted inside. She had polished its brass door for three years, though — knew the way the keyhole's teeth were worn to a soft shine, knew the faint smell of cold iron and old tallow that seeped through the seam like a held breath. So when Senior Keeper Vane himself summoned her at the ninth bell with the words *"bring your ledger-hand, girl, we're short-staffed,"* she felt something dangerous bloom in her chest before she could smother it.

Trust, she thought. Or at least its cheap cousin, usefulness.

The vault sat beneath the guildhall's oldest wing, down a stair that predated the guild itself — the masons' marks on the walls were in no style any living architect taught. Torches guttered as they descended, and Vane carried the great ring of keys against his hip where they clinked like distant bells underwater.

"Rules," he said, without turning. "You touch nothing I don't hand you. You write nothing I don't dictate. You speak of nothing you see." He paused on the last step. "Especially that last one."

"Yes, Senior Keeper."

He looked back at her then, and his face was unreadable in the torchlight — a man made of angles and gray wool, sixty years of ledgers folded into the lines around his mouth. "You've been asking questions, Halloran. In the reading room. About the founding covenants."

Paloma's stomach dropped. "Only cataloguing inconsistencies—"

"I know what you call it." He held her gaze a moment longer than was comfortable, then pushed open the vault door. "Inconsistencies are how keepers go mad. Remember that."

* * *

Inside, the Founding Vault was smaller than she'd imagined and colder than it had any right to be. Shelves of black oak rose floor to ceiling, and every document on them was bound in salt-white leather that seemed to drink the light. At the center stood a single lectern, and upon it, under glass, lay the founding covenant of the Ember Guild.

She had seen copies. Every keeper had seen copies — the fair transcription displayed in the Great Reading Room behind velvet rope, all flourished script and wax seals, the proud record of Vaelmoor's oldest institution. The original was supposed to be identical. That was the whole point of an original.

Vane lifted the glass with both hands, reverent as a priest. "You will transcribe page nine through fourteen. The transcription in the reading room has... drifted. We restore fidelity every generation or so." He set a fresh quill and an inkpot before her. "Begin."

Paloma bent over the covenant, and within four lines her blood went strange.

The handwriting was not the same.

Oh, it was old — older than anything she'd ever handled, the letters tall and narrow, pressed hard enough into the vellum to leave grooves she could feel with her fingertip. But it wasn't the hand of First Keeper Edmure Voss that appeared on the reading-room copy, the hand she'd memorized from a hundred admiring studies. This was a different scribe entirely. Looser. Faster. A hand that wrote the way people spoke when they were afraid the door might close.

"Senior Keeper," she said carefully, "the reading room copy is attributed to First Keeper Voss."

"It is."

"This isn't his hand."

Vane did not look up from the inventory he was pretending to check. "Hands change over years, Halloran."

"Not like this." She turned the page. "This writer forms their *th* differently. Their ink is different — see the brown cast, iron-gall cut with seawater, which no guild scribe would—" She stopped.

Because the page ended.

Not at a sentence. Not at a paragraph. The vellum simply stopped, mid-word, the final letters sheared away in a clean vertical line that no moth, no rot, no accident of centuries could produce. Someone had taken a blade to the founding covenant of the Ember Guild, and they had done it neatly.

She looked up. Vane was watching her now, and there was something in his face she had never seen there before — not anger. Something wearier and far more frightening.

"How many pages?" she asked. Her voice came out very small in the stone room.

"That is not your concern."

"How many *pages*, Senior Keeper?" And then, because three years of being dismissed had finally curdled into something with edges: "I count the stubs. Eleven razored leaves between folio eight and folio fifteen. Whatever was written here, someone decided the guild's own founders couldn't be trusted to remember it honestly."

For a long moment the only sound was the torches breathing.

Then Vane crossed the room, took the covenant gently from beneath her hands, and closed it. When he spoke, his voice was quiet, which was worse than shouting.

"You are nineteen years old. You have neat handwriting and a good memory, and in twenty years, if you learn patience, you will make a fine senior keeper yourself. But you are not entitled to what is in this vault. No one is. That is rather the point of it." He replaced the glass cover. "The pages you're missing were removed by unanimous vote of the full conclave, one hundred and forty years ago, for reasons recorded nowhere because the vote itself was sealed. Do you understand what that means? It means eleven keepers wiser than either of us looked at those pages and agreed that Vaelmoor would be safer without them."

"Safer," Paloma repeated. "Or more comfortable?"

Something flickered across his face — there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "Mind your quill, Keeper Halloran. Transcribe what remains. That is the work."

But her hands were shaking too badly to hold one, and he saw it, and after a moment he sighed and said the session was concluded.

* * *

She did not go back to her dormer. She went to the infirmary ward instead, where the child had been kept since the night of the knocking.

They'd named him provisionally — the intake ledger read *Unknown male, approx. six years, found at guild door, mud, hypothermia, nonverbal* — but the healers had started calling him Nen, because it was the only sound he made that resembled a name, pointing at his own chest and saying it like a question he was asking the world. He had been in the guildhall five days. The physicians pronounced him healthy. The linguists pronounced him impossible.

Paloma found him sitting cross-legged on the infirmary floor, drawing.

He drew with charcoal stolen from the hearth, on the backs of discarded inventory sheets, and he drew the same thing over and over: streets. Streets at angles that hurt to look at, running up walls and along ceilings; towers that widened toward their tops like inverted spires; windows everywhere, glowing, hundreds of them, each one holding a small fierce point of light. Lanterns. The city was made of lanterns. He drew doors standing open on water, and figures in the doorways, and always, always, at the heart of it, a great hall whose roof was the underside of a harbor — ships' keels passing overhead like slow clouds.

"Nen," she said softly.

He looked up. His eyes were dark and unblinking and much too calm for a child pulled half-drowned from a harbor threshold, and when he saw her ledger-satchel he pointed at it, urgent, and made a sound — a rising, pleading note.

"You want to see?"

She knelt and opened the satchel, and he took from it — gently, with both hands, the way one handles a sleeping animal — her personal practice book. Then he flipped past her exercises to a blank page and began to draw again, fast, charcoal whispering.

Paloma watched, and the cold from the Founding Vault climbed back into her bones.

Because she recognized the marginalia.

The oldest ledger in the guild's collection — Ledger Zero, they called it, locked in a case in the reading room, its margins crawling with annotations in a script no scholar had ever matched to any living language — that script was pouring out of this child's charcoal like it was his mother tongue. Which, she realized with a lurch, it probably was. He wrote little notes beside his drawings. Labels. *This is the lantern-hall. This is where the tide comes in wrong. This is my grandmother's door.*

She couldn't read them. But she could compare. That evening, after lights-out, she would take her practice book to the reading room case and hold the two scripts side by side, and she already knew what she would find: identity. Perfect, impossible identity, across however many centuries separated Ledger Zero's anonymous annotator from this mud-soaked six-year-old who drew drowned cities from memory.

The child finished, studied his work, and then did something new. He tore the page free, folded it with careful small fingers, and pressed it into her palm. Then he pointed at her chest, the way he'd pointed at his own, and said a word.

It was not her name. She was almost sure it was not her name. It sounded like *kehn-ah-VAH-lin*, and he said it the way the old priests said *vessel* — with weight, with warning, like a title.

"What does that mean?" she whispered.

Nen smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone watching a slow tide come in, patient, inevitable, fond of the shore even as it covers it.

* * *

She lasted until the second bell past midnight before she gave in and went to the reading room, taking the folded drawing with her, telling herself it was cataloguing, it was diligence, it was exactly the work a keeper did.

Ledger Zero lay in its case under lamplight. She unlocked it with the junior key she technically wasn't permitted to use alone, and laid Nen's drawing beside the open margin, and the two scripts stared at each other across the glass like twins separated at birth.

Identical. Letter for letter, ligature for ligature. The child hadn't learned an archaic dialect. He *was* the archaic dialect, walking around in bare feet, eating the guild's porridge, drawing cities that existed only in documents everyone insisted were decorative.

Her eyes moved from the drawing to the ledger proper, following the old entries, and stopped at the entry dated to the year of the guild's founding. The fair copy in the display frame summarized it: *Covenant ratified between the Ember Guild of Vaelmoor and the undersigned parties, terms annexed.*

Annexed. The word sat there, innocent, and Paloma felt her heartbeat slow and deepen, the way it did when she stood at the top of a high stair in the dark.

Terms annexed. An annex was a separate document. A companion text. Bound or filed alongside the principal instrument.

Eleven razored pages. Folios eight through fifteen. Right where a covenant's terms would live.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty reading room. "No, no, no."

She was still standing there, Ledger Zero open, the child's drawing trembling in her hand, when the lamps behind her dimmed.

Aldous Vane stood in the doorway in his nightclothes and coat, gray-faced, holding a candle that shook almost imperceptibly. He looked at the open case. At the drawing. At her.

"I warned you," he said quietly, "about inconsistencies."

"Senior Keeper, the child writes Ledger Zero's script. The annexed terms are the missing pages. The founding covenant isn't incomplete by accident — it was *gutted*, and whatever was in those pages, the guild promised it to someone, and I think that someone just sent us a six-year-old boy to collect—"

"*Enough.*" The word cracked like ice giving way. Vane crossed the room in four strides and closed Ledger Zero with a boom that echoed off the vaulted ceiling. For a moment his hand stayed flat on the cover, and Paloma saw — clearly, unmistakably — that it was trembling.

When he looked at her, all the gray authority had drained out of his face, and what remained underneath frightened her far more: an old man, very tired, keeping watch over something terrible.

"Halloran," he said, "there are things the guild did before it was the guild. Before there were rules, or oaths, or reading rooms with velvet ropes. The men and women who founded this place did what was necessary so that everything after them could be comfortable enough to forget." He straightened, and the mask came back down, but slowly, like a visor. "Forgetfulness is not theft. Forgetfulness is mercy — the kind you extend to a city of two hundred thousand souls who sleep better not knowing what their grandmothers traded to keep the harbor calm."

"The child knows."

"The child," Vane said, "is not your concern. Return to your dormer. Speak of this to no one. And tomorrow morning, request reassignment to the tax rolls." His voice gentled into something almost kind, which was the cruelest thing yet. "The tax rolls are honest work, Halloran. Nothing there will ever look back at you."

He gathered the drawing from her unresisting fingers, folded it once, and tucked it into his coat. At the door he paused, his back to her.

"For what it is worth," he said, "I am sorry you found the stubs. Most keepers live their whole lives and never do."

Then he was gone, and Paloma stood alone among the sleeping ledgers, and understood, with the first full taste of it bitter on her tongue, that the betrayal was not some stranger's crime in some distant century.

It was the house she loved. It had always been the house she loved. And somewhere beneath the harbor, in a city of lanterns, something patient had begun to turn the pages back.
