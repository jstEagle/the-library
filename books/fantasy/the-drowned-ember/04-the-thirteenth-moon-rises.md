## 4. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The tide came in at midnight, and it came in wrong.

Paloma knew it before she knew why — the way you know a room has been rearranged in the dark. She was awake in her narrow cell of a room above the guildhall's counting floor, listening to the harbor breathe through the open window, and the breathing had changed. The water should have been pulling back from the pilings by now, hissing over mud flats studded with rope-ends and broken crockery. Instead it was climbing. She could hear it climbing, a slow wet whisper against stone that had no business being wet.

She dressed without lighting a lamp and went down through the sleeping guildhall, past the racks of ledgers standing like headstones, out into the cold salt air of the Ember Quay.

The moon was gone. Not merely hidden — gone, three nights into the dark of its cycle, and the thirteenth moon of the year had always been the unlucky one, the one the old dockhands spat over their shoulders at. Under that blind sky the harbor lay black as poured ink, and it was rising up the quay steps. Rising. Paloma counted the steps as she descended toward it, because counting was what she did, because numbers were the only prayer she trusted. Eleven steps dry. Nine. Seven. The water climbed while she watched, unhurried, deliberate, like something taking possession of property it had long been denied.

"Off the steps, girl." A night-watchman behind her, lantern raised. "You'll be drowned standing there gawping."

"It's going backward," she said. "The tide. It's coming in when it should be going out."

The watchman squinted at the black water, then at her, and she saw him decide not to believe her, because believing would cost more than he could pay. "Moonless nights make fools of everyone," he said, and walked on.

But Paloma stayed until her feet were soaked, watching the harbor swallow the quay step by step, and she thought of Nen asleep upstairs in the blanket nest the keepers had grudgingly allowed, and of the marginalia in the founding ledger, and of the pages razored out, and she felt the first cold thread of understanding work its way under her skin: *the impossible things were never nonsense. They were invoices.*

* * *

Senior Keeper Aldous Vane did not sleep badly; he slept precisely, four hours, always, and woke before dawn to review the day's correspondence. On the morning after the backward tide, the correspondence included a report from the harbormaster's office, written in the cramped hand of a junior tide-clerk who had clearly been told to put it down and clearly wished he hadn't.

*Tide gauge at Stone Pilings recorded ingress during ebb window, duration 3 hours 40 minutes. Gauge at Saltmarket recorded same. Instrument error suspected. Instruments replaced. Error recurred.*

Vane read it twice, then fed it to the candle flame and watched it curl into ash.

There had been tides like this before. He knew because he had made it his business, thirty years ago, to know — a young keeper hungry for the guild's secrets, digging where the senior men kept their boots planted firmly on the lids of things. He had found the payment schedules. Small sums, entered in a cipher only three living people could read: two barrels of remembered storms, one name freely surrendered, six years off the life of a condemned man whose years would otherwise hang idle. Petty tribute, paid quarterly to an account that had no bank, no holder, no location except *down*. Paid for generations. Paid so quietly that most keepers went their whole careers believing the impossible-things vaults were mere eccentricity, the guild's quaint habit of cataloguing what could not exist.

Vane had believed it too, once. Then he had seen the schedule, and understood that the guild was not collecting curiosities. It was paying rent.

And rent, unlike tribute, implied a landlord who could evict.

He dressed, bound his gray hair back, and went down to the counting floor, where the morning shift was assembling and Paloma Halloran was already at her desk, which did not surprise him. The girl worked like someone trying to earn a place the guild had never offered. He had watched her for months now with a feeling he refused to name — something between pity and premonition. She had found the razored covenant. She had asked questions in exactly the wrong tone: not the frightened tone, not the respectful tone, but the tone of an auditor who has found a discrepancy and intends to reconcile it.

"Keeper Halloran," he said. "A word."

She followed him to the window alcove. Below, the harbor lay flat and innocent in the gray light, as if last night had never happened.

"You heard about the tide," Vane said.

"I stood in it."

"Then you'll understand why I'm telling you this once, plainly, keeper to keeper." He let the word *keeper* do its work; he saw it land, saw her straighten slightly, hungry for the inclusion. "Whatever you think you found in the founding ledger, whatever the child's babbling suggests to you — drop it. There are currents in this guild older than any of us, and they run deeper than the harbor. A clever swimmer drowns in them."

"With respect, Senior Keeper," Paloma said, and her voice was steady, which annoyed him more than tears would have, "the harbor is running backward. I don't think we get to choose anymore whether we're swimming."

Vane looked at her for a long moment. He wanted, absurdly, to tell her everything — the schedules, the cipher, the quarterly payments, the way the senior circle had voted, year after year, decade after decade, to keep paying rather than face the account. But confession was a debt too, and debts were the one currency he had spent his life hoarding.

"The tide will correct itself," he said. "It always has. Go back to your columns."

He turned away before she could see that his hands were not entirely steady.

* * *

Three nights later, the moon still blind, the courier died.

His name was Tam Wicker, nineteen years old, fastest legs in the guild's service, and he was carrying a sealed dispatch from the guildhall to the customs house on the far side of the quay — a distance of four hundred paces along dry, cobbled, lamplit street. Paloma knew all of this because she was the one who had prepared the dispatch, and because she arrived at the scene while Tam was still lying where he fell, surrounded by a ring of strangers who did not know what they were looking at.

They thought at first he'd been struck. There was no wound. They thought at first he'd fainted. His lips were blue. And when Paloma pushed through the crowd and knelt beside him and took his hand, water ran out of his sleeve cuff and pooled on the cobblestones.

Seawater. Cold, clean, smelling of deep harbor and something older beneath it.

"He just stopped," a fishwife was saying, wringing her hands. "Walking along, whistling, and then he went down like — like something pulled him—"

Drowned. Paloma knelt on dry stone under a dry sky, holding the hand of a boy who had drowned in four hundred paces of open air, and around her the crowd murmured its comfortable explanations — bad heart, bad luck, bad oysters — because the alternative was to look at the black water at the end of the quay and wonder what it wanted.

She looked. The harbor lay still as glass. Far out, past the breakwater, where no boat was moored and no lamp hung, a light moved. Greenish. Patient. It bobbed twice, the way a lantern bobs when carried by someone walking, and then it sank, deliberately, like a courtesy bow.

Paloma closed Tam's eyes and sat back on her heels, and the denial she had been carrying — the reasonable, keeper-trained conviction that there must be a clerical explanation, a cipher misread, a coincidence of weather — broke apart in her chest like ice on a spring river. The debt was real. It had never been metaphor. Every impossible thing in every vault was a line item in an account the guild had stopped honoring, and the account holder had begun making collections directly.

Nen was waiting for her when she got back, sitting cross-legged on the counting-floor flags in the dark, having escaped whatever corner the night porter had assigned. The child looked up at her with those eyes like deep water and said, in the pre-kingdom tongue that Paloma was beginning, shamefully, to understand:

*"You counted the steps."*

"Yes," Paloma said. "Eleven down to seven."

*"It is faster each time."* Nen drew a breath, and the child's small hands curled into fists. *"My people are patient. But patience is not the same as permission. The moon turns soon. When the thirteenth moon fills, if no signatory stands—"*

"If no signatory stands, what?"

Nen didn't answer. The child simply looked toward the window, toward the invisible harbor, and Paloma followed the gaze, and in the black glass of the night she saw reflected — faintly, impossibly — the green lantern-light, rising.

* * *

Vane convened the senior circle at dawn, and for the first time in his tenure, the circle convened afraid.

He stood at the head of the long table in the chartroom, where the great harbor map was inked across the tabletop, and he laid out the facts the way he laid out everything: flatly, in order, without adjectives. The backward tides, three consecutive moonless nights, duration increasing. The gauge readings. Tam Wicker, dead of drowning on dry land, seawater in his lungs confirmed by the physician, who had signed the certificate with a shaking hand and asked to be paid in advance.

"And the light?" asked Keeper Ondrey, oldest of them, her voice papery. "The green light the girl Halloran reports."

"Halloran reports many things," said Keeper Brase, and there was an edge in it. "The child. The razored covenant. Now lanterns on the water. I say again what I said before: the girl is ambitious and unstable, and she is leading us by the nose into superstition."

"She is leading us nowhere," Vane said. "I am showing you the gauges. Are the gauges superstitious? Is the physician's certificate?" He pressed both palms flat on the map, on the inked harbor, and felt the wood cold as drowned stone beneath them. "Gentlemen. Madam. We can continue to call this weather, or instrument error, or the fancies of a junior clerk. Or we can say the word we have all been avoiding since the first backward tide."

Silence. Outside the chartroom windows, the harbor glittered, harmless-looking, beautiful, full of its secret.

"The arrears," Ondrey said at last, very quietly.

"The arrears," Vane agreed. "We stopped paying in full eighty years ago. We have been sending token sums ever since — enough, we calculated, to defer. To manage. To buy time." He heard how it sounded, said aloud, in daylight, and despised how it sounded, and despised more the young man he had been who had helped draft the deferral schedule, arithmetic neat as prayer. "Our calculation assumed the creditor would remain patient indefinitely. I believe we may now consider that assumption falsified."

Brase slammed his palm on the table. "Then we pay! We open the vaults, we send down whatever the old schedule demands—"

"The old schedule demands what we no longer have," Ondrey said. "Names freely given. Years unspent. Memories whole. You cannot counterfeit those, Brase. That was always the covenant's cruelty and its elegance. The coin is the self."

"Then we find another way. We always have."

"Have we?" Vane asked, and let the question sit. "Or have we simply been borrowing?"

No one answered. Through the windows the sun climbed, gilding the water, and Vane thought of Tam Wicker whistling his way along the quay, and of the green light bowing beneath the surface like a creditor tipping his hat, and he understood — with the clarity that comes only when denial finally drowns — that the time for small payments was over. The account had been called in. All of it. Principal, interest, and penalty.

And somewhere below the harbor, something patient was checking its calendar, and the thirteenth moon was turning toward its fill.

"We have until the moon turns," Vane said. "Perhaps less. I want the founding covenant brought to me — all of it, including whatever pages this guild once thought to cut away. And I want Keeper Halloran in my office within the hour."

Brase frowned. "Halloran? Why her?"

"Because she has already read what we forbade," Vane said, gathering the ash-gray reports into his case, "and because she stood in the tide and counted the steps, and because — gods forgive us all — she is the only person in this building who has behaved as though the debt were real." He paused at the door, looking back at the sunlit map, at the little inked city huddled beside its little inked sea. "The rest of us have been pretending for eighty years. Pretending, it turns out, accrues interest too."

Behind him, unnoticed by anyone, the tide gauge in its glass case on the chartroom wall ticked softly upward — recording, even here, even now, water that was not there, rising.
