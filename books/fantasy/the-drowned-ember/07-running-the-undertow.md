## 7. Running the Undertow

The envoy's lantern-light was still burning on the water behind them when Paloma took Nen's hand and ran.

She had no plan. That was the first honest thought she'd had all night, and she clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood — not because it would save her, but because it was hers. Behind her, the guildhall doors had boomed shut. Ahead of her, the lower quarters of Vaelmoor lay drowned to the second story, streets turned to canals, lamplight shivering on black water that should not have been there. The tide had come in at dusk and simply refused to leave.

"Keep hold," she said. "Don't let go, whatever you hear."

Nen's fingers were cold as coins pulled from a fountain, but the grip was strong — stronger than a child's grip had any right to be. The child said nothing. Nen hadn't spoken since the guildhall, since the elders had pointed at that small mud-streaked figure and used words like *offering* and *terms* and *acceptable loss*, and Paloma had understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade sliding between her ribs, that she was looking at a ledger entry. A line item. *One child, against one city.*

She had been raised by ledgers. She knew what happened to entries once they were written.

* * *

The lower quarters had always been Vaelmoor's underside — fishwives and rope-makers, smugglers' stairs and shrines to gods whose names the Ember Guild had struck from its records two centuries ago. Now the water had made the place into something else entirely: a mirror-city lying just beneath the real one, and every window they passed held a doubled flame, every doorway a doubled dark.

They waded down Copperlane with the water at their thighs. It was warm. That was the thing Paloma kept returning to, the detail her mind snagged on like a nail — the water was warm, harbor-warm, blood-warm, in the middle of a cold autumn night, as if the sea itself had a fever. As if something below were *running hot*.

Nen stopped so suddenly that Paloma's arm jerked back.

"What is it?"

The child tilted their head, listening. And then Paloma heard it too — or didn't hear it, which was worse. The city behind them had gone quiet. Not muffled, not distant. *Stopped.* No dogs, no watch-bells, no drunk singing in the taverns on higher ground. The whole of Vaelmoor holding its breath.

Then, under the water, a sound like a great door being drawn open very slowly.

"We need to get off the main channels." Paloma scanned the rooftops jutting from the flood. "There. The rope-walk. If we can get up—"

She hauled Nen onto a drainage arch, then onto the low roof of a chandler's shop, tiles slick and treacherous underfoot. From up here the drowned quarter spread out below them like a map of a country that had never existed — and in the middle of it, in the wide basin where Fishmarket Square had been, something was glowing. Not lantern-light this time. A column of pale gold rising through the water itself, steady as a candle in a windless room, and around it the flood moved in slow circles, turning, turning, like water finding a drain.

Like water being *pulled* somewhere.

"That's where they'll expect you to go," Paloma whispered. She didn't know how she knew it; she only knew it the way she knew arithmetic. "That's the front door. So we don't use the front door."

Nen looked up at her. In the dark, the child's eyes caught the gold light and held it — and for a moment Paloma saw the harbor in them, the whole drowned depth of it, and her breath went thin.

*What are you?*

But asking questions was a luxury for people who weren't running. She found a handhold in the chimney brick and started along the rooftop spine of Copperlane, Nen following with an ease that unsettled her more than any speech could have. The child moved across wet tile like the tile had asked permission.

* * *

They rested in the belfry of a shrine whose god's name Paloma had never learned, sitting among pigeons and old bell-ropes, sharing a heel of bread she'd stuffed in her coat pocket that morning without knowing why. Guild training, maybe. Keepers were taught to carry small things against large needs.

"Nen." She said the name carefully, tasting it. "When the envoy came — when it spoke — did you understand it?"

A long silence. Then, slowly, the child nodded.

"And the guild elders. When they talked about sending you back. Did you understand *that*?"

Another nod. Smaller.

Paloma looked at her hands. They were shaking, and she made them stop, because keepers' hands did not shake, and if she was no longer anything else tonight she was still that. "I'm going to tell you what I think, and you can nod or not. I think you weren't lost. I think you were *sent*. I think you're not a message, exactly. You're—" She searched the ledgers in her head for the right term and hated herself a little when she found it. "You're collateral. Pledge-goods. Something given over to prove the giver means to pay."

Nen's face did something complicated and old. Children's faces shouldn't do that.

"The covenant," Paloma went on, quieter. "The one my guild broke. They gave you to us as proof they meant to deal honestly. And now the debt's called due, and the guild wants to give you *back* — not home, not honored, just handed over like a coin passed across a table to make the shouting stop."

Nen reached out and touched her wrist. Cold fingers, warm pressure. And into the space between them, in a voice like water over stones — the first sound the child had made since the guildhall — Nen hummed three notes. Low, then high, then low again. A phrase. An argument, almost.

"I don't understand," Paloma whispered. "I'm sorry. I wish I did."

But she understood enough. She understood that the drowned realm had sent its last-born heir into a city of thieves in good faith, and the good faith had rotted, and now the heir was a bargaining chip with a price on their head from both directions — worth everything to the realm below, worth nothing except as payment to the guild above. Everyone wanted custody of Nen. No one wanted custody *of* Nen.

Well. Paloma Halloran had spent six years being useful to people who didn't see her. She knew exactly how that felt.

"Then it's us," she said. Her voice cracked and she let it. "For now. Until we find someone who'll actually help. Us two. All right?"

Nen leaned against her shoulder, and the warmth of the child seeped through her soaked coat, and Paloma sat in a dead god's belfry and felt something kindle in her chest that she had no ledger-category for. Not duty. Not guilt, though there was plenty of that. This was fiercer and simpler and it scared her more than the flood did.

*I will burn this whole city down before I let them hand you over,* she thought, and knew it was true, and knew the old Paloma — the neat-handwriting girl who loved the guild that barely saw her — had just gone under the water for good.

* * *

They moved again before dawn, along the rooftop spine toward the upper town, where the flood hadn't reached and a person might find a horse, a gate, a road out. But Vaelmoor at night had other plans.

The hunters found them at the boundary of Saltrow, where the roofs stepped down and the streets ran dry. Three figures in oilskin cloaks, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who are paid whether they succeed or fail. Guild colors at their throats, dimmed to grey.

"Keeper Halloran." The leader's voice was mild. That was somehow the worst part. "Senior Keeper Vane sends his regards. He says there's been a misunderstanding, and if you bring the child to the Saltgate station, all of this will be sorted out by morning."

"Sorted out," Paloma repeated. "You mean entered. You mean weighed and recorded and paid out."

The man shrugged, and the gesture said everything: *I move goods. What the goods are isn't my department.*

"You're guild couriers," she said. "Sworn carriers. Your oath says nothing travels sealed unless it travels safe. Well, look at me. Look at this child. We are *sealed goods*, courier, and we are not traveling safe. So either your oath is broken or your masters are liars, and either way I'm not coming."

Something flickered across the man's face — respect, maybe, or regret — and then he whistled, and his companions fanned out along the street, cutting off the corners, and Paloma grabbed Nen and ran.

They went down instead of up, because down was flooded and the hunters were dry-landers. Paloma plunged them both off the Saltrow steps into chest-deep water, gasping at the warmth of it, dragging Nen into the canal that had been Tanner's Way. Behind her, boots splashed and swore. Above her, the moonless sky pressed down like a lid.

The water helped them. She couldn't have said how — currents shifted to carry them past obstacles, debris slid aside before it could strike, and once a submerged cart seemed to lean deliberately out of their path. The drowned quarter wasn't fighting them. The drowned quarter, she realized with a chill that had nothing to do with cold, was *escorting* them.

It knows its own, she thought. It knows what's in my arms.

They came up through a coal cellar into a ropewalk alley above the waterline, both of them streaming and sobbing for breath, and Paloma pressed Nen against the wall and put her body between the child and the alley mouth and waited for the boots to catch up.

They didn't. The alley stayed empty. Somewhere behind them, faintly, came the sound of the hunters retreating — not giving up, she knew. Regrouping. Reporting. Vane would know within the hour exactly where they'd surfaced, and next time there would be more than three, and next time they wouldn't bother with offers.

Nen tugged her sleeve. Pointed down the alley, to where a faded sign creaked on its bracket: a lantern crossed with a knife, painted in colors the rain had half-eaten.

Paloma stared at it. She knew that mark. Every keeper knew that mark — it appeared in the oldest ledgers as a marginal doodle, a joke passed down through generations of bored scribes: *the Lantern and Knife, where exiles drink.* A smugglers' den. A legend. And beneath the legend, in one of the founding-era folios she'd memorized in her restless nights, a single name attached to it like a tag to a seized lot:

*Maren, I. — expelled, all rights revoked, whereabouts unknown.*

"Well," Paloma said aloud, to Nen, to the night, to whatever was listening under the water. "Looks like we know a person after all."

She took the child's cold hand in hers. Behind them the flood glowed faint gold and waited. Ahead, the sign creaked.

Three knocks, she remembered. On the third knock, say the word the ledgers redacted.

She hoped she'd read carefully enough.
