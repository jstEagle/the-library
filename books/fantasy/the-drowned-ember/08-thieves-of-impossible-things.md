## 8. Thieves of Impossible Things

The Saltglass Row began where Vaelmoor's maps gave up.

Paloma had learned that from a courier's gossip years ago, back when she still believed gossip was a currency she couldn't afford. Now, with Nen's small hand locked in hers and the harbor breathing wrong behind them, she walked past the last honest streetlamp and into the district the city pretended not to have — a crooked spine of warehouses leaning against each other like drunkards keeping secrets, their windows patched with sailcloth and stained glass stolen from three different centuries.

Nen had not spoken since the lower quarters. The child walked with the particular silence of someone who had been taught, recently and painfully, that being heard was dangerous. Paloma kept hold of that small hand anyway. It was cold as a coin left in a fountain, but it squeezed back every time the tide-sound swelled behind them, and that squeeze was the only map Paloma trusted now.

"You're sure this is the place?" she asked, for the fourth time. The question was really for herself. She wasn't sure of anything anymore except the shape of razored pages and the weight of a ledger that lied.

The answer came from above them.

"Nobody's *sure* of anything on Saltglass Row, little clerk. That's what makes it valuable."

A woman crouched on the warehouse roofline like a gargoyle who had decided respectability was beneath her. Even in the dark, even at forty feet, Paloma knew her — not from memory, because Ilsa Maren had been exiled before Paloma could walk, but from the guildhall itself: the founder's portrait in the reading room, the one the senior keepers always hung slightly crooked, as if straightening it would be an admission. Sharp jaw. Hair the color of unpolished bronze, cut short and practical. Eyes that caught light the way water catches a dropped lantern.

Ilsa Maren descended the warehouse wall the way other people descended stairs.

"I know your face," she said, landing without sound. She wore a smuggler's coat with too many pockets and a knife at each hip and, absurdly, a guild signet ring on a cord around her neck — the founder's mark, worn like a trophy or a wound. "Halloran's girl. The one with the neat handwriting."

"They talk about me?"

"They talk about everyone. It's how institutions stay alive when they've stopped doing anything worth living for." Ilsa's gaze slid to Nen, and something moved behind her eyes — recognition, quick and unwelcome, like a fish surfacing in a pond she'd thought frozen. She crouched to the child's level. "And you. You smell like the deep places."

Nen stared at her. Then, slowly, raised one hand and drew a shape in the air: a spiral, tightening inward, ending in a point.

Ilsa went very still.

"Inside," she said, all the play gone out of her voice. "Both of you. Quickly."

* * *

Her warehouse was less a home than an argument with the concept of one. Crates stacked to the rafters, each stenciled with symbols Paloma half-recognized from the oldest ledgers — the pre-kingdom marks, the ones that matched the marginalia, the ones that matched the sounds Nen made in sleep. Shelves of jars held things that should not fit in jars: a wind that rattled against its glass. A shadow coiled like a sleeping eel. A jar labeled, in a careful hand, *ONE (1) TUESDAY, UNUSED.*

"You trade in covenant-goods," Paloma said. Not a question. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears — harder than it had been a week ago.

"I trade in *inventory*." Ilsa lit a lantern whose flame burned blue-green, the color of deep water. "What things are called depends on who's asking and what they're willing to pay. Sit. Both of you. There's tea, if you don't ask what's in it."

They sat. Nen curled into the corner of a bench like a cat claiming territory, watching everything. Paloma set her satchel on the table between them — the satchel with the founding ledger inside, the one she had stolen from her own guild four nights ago, the theft that had turned her from keeper into fugitive.

"I need your help," she said. "The drowned realm is rising. The covenant was broken — by the guild, generations ago. They took the payments and never delivered them. Memories. Names. Years. Maybe more. And now—"

"And now the thirteenth moon is turning, and the harbor has started running backward, and a courier drowned in dry air on Wexler Street, and the senior keepers are holding meetings behind triple-locked doors." Ilsa poured tea into three cups without looking at any of them. "Yes. I read the tides too. Go on."

"The ledgers have pages missing. Razored out. The founding covenant — the original terms — they're gone. I thought maybe you'd have copies. Or knowledge. Or—" Paloma stopped, hearing herself, hearing the desperate arithmetic of a clerk who believed every problem had a filing solution. "You were there. You founded the guild. You must know what was taken."

The silence stretched long enough for the jarred wind to rattle twice.

Then Isla laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of someone cutting a rope they've been clinging to.

"*I* founded the guild," she said. "That's what the portrait says. That's what the histories say. Do you want to know what actually happened, little clerk? Because it isn't flattering, and it isn't simple, and once you hear it you can't put it back in the jar."

"I've spent my whole life putting things in jars," Paloma said. "Tell me."

Ilsa drank. Set down the cup. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — flattened, stripped, the voice of testimony rather than conversation.

"I didn't found the Ember Guild. I *betrayed* it into existence. There was a circle of us, before the guild, before the charter — scholars, mostly, and two priests who'd lost their faith and replaced it with curiosity. We found the drowned realm the way you find anything worth stealing: by accident, and then on purpose. They weren't monsters, whatever the stories say now. They were *people*, just people who lived where we'd drown. And they had things we wanted. Things that couldn't be bought or built. Years of life, decanted. Memories, bottled. Names that could be worn like coats. And they wanted things from us in return — small things, at first. Songs. Stories. Our dead, buried in their water instead of our dirt, so they'd have company."

She turned her cup slowly on the table.

"We wrote the covenant together. A real one, fair terms, both sides signing. I was the human signatory. Me — Ilsa Maren, twenty-six years old, so proud of my beautiful handwriting." Her mouth twisted. "And then the others got greedy. Quietly, at first. A payment received and not delivered — one memory, just to see if the drowned realm would notice. They noticed. But the breach was small, and we apologized, and we paid it back, and we told ourselves the ledger was balanced. That's the trick of it, girl. That's the trick every institution learns. You can steal anything if you only steal a little at a time and call each theft a rounding error."

"And you let it happen," Paloma said quietly.

"I *was* it happening." For a moment, the smuggler's armor cracked clean through, and underneath was just an old woman who had outlived her own best self. "I signed the severance. When the circle voted to cut the covenant entirely — keep the goods, end the obligations — I was the one who held the pen, because my signature carried the binding weight. They needed the founder's mark. I gave it to them. I told myself the drowned realm was fading anyway, that the payments were going to waste in a dying place, that it was mercy to redirect them to living humans." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible. "It was theft. Clean, documented, beautifully recorded theft. I built the guild's first archive out of the proceeds. Then I exiled myself, because staying would have required me to keep lying, and I discovered I was only good at so many sins before they started tasting wrong."

Nen had uncurled during the telling. The child watched Ilsa with an expression Paloma couldn't read — not hatred, not forgiveness. Something older than either. When Ilsa finished, Nen reached across the table and touched the guild signet hanging at her throat, very lightly, the way you touch a scar.

Ilsa covered the child's hand with her own.

"Yes," she said softly. "I know what you are. Last-born. Living collateral. They sent you up as a pledge, and the guild took you in and filed you under *anomaly* instead of returning you, because returning you would mean admitting the debt existed." She withdrew her hand and stood, and the armor came back up, plate by plate. "So. You want my help. Here's what my help looks like: I know where the original covenant sleeps. The true text, unrazored, in the sunken quarter below the old tide-gates. I can get you to it. I can get you through the vault-seals, which were my work and answer to my blood. And I can probably keep Vane's hunters off you long enough to matter."

Hope rose in Paloma's chest like a tide.

"What do you need from me?"

"There it is." Ilsa smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of a woman who had learned exactly what questions cost. "Payment. In advance. Non-negotiable."

"I don't have money."

"Money's for people trading in things that exist." Ilsa sat back down, leaned forward, and laced her fingers. "Let me tell you how commerce works outside the law, little clerk, since your guild never taught you. Inside the law, you pay with coin, and coin is a promise that other people agree to honor. Outside the law, there's no one to agree. So you pay with pieces of yourself. Reputation. Secrets. Time. Name." She tapped the table. "I've been listening to you since you walked in. You speak like a ledger. Every sentence balances. You think justice is a column that adds up if you're careful enough. So here's your lesson, free of charge: *justice has no column.* Out here, nobody owes you anything. Not me, not the city, not the drowned. If you want something done, you buy it. And the only currency you're carrying that I want—"

"My name," Paloma said.

The word came out of her flat and small, and she hated how small it sounded.

Ilsa tilted her head. "Fast learner."

"No." Paloma stood, and her chair scraped, and Nen flinched, and she made herself slow down. "No. I won't give you my name. My name is the last thing the guild hasn't taken. They took the truth from their own records. They took the covenant. They tried to take *me* — offered me to the envoy like a receipt. Everything I am is in that name. Paloma Halloran, third shelf, junior bench, keeper of the western ledgers. If I give that up, what's left?"

"Someone new," Ilsa said simply. "Which is what you'll have to become anyway, if any of this works. Look at me, girl. I gave up my name sixty years ago. 'Ilsa Maren' died the day I signed the severance — the guild struck it from every roll, and the woman walking around in her body became nobody, legally speaking. Nobody can't own property. Nobody can't testify. Nobody can't be arrested, because there's no one to arrest." She spread her hands. "It's the freest and loneliest thing that ever happened to me. And I'm telling you, as the person who invented the price you're haggling over: it's survivable. People survive becoming new. What they don't survive—" her eyes flicked to Nen, to the window, to the backward-running dark beyond it, "—is the thirteenth moon."

Paloma looked at Nen.

The child was watching her with those deep-water eyes, and there was no plea in them, and that was somehow worse. Nen had already been given away once — handed across realms as a pledge, a living promissory note. The child knew better than anyone what it was to be payment. And still those eyes said, calmly, *if it comes to that, I understand,* and Paloma felt something in her chest go hard and bright as forged iron.

"No," she said again, but differently this time. "Not like that. I'm not trading Nen's freedom for mine, and I'm not giving up my name because you've decided it's the going rate. Tell me what the name buys. Exactly. Itemized."

For the first time, Ilsa Maren looked genuinely surprised — and then, faintly, pleased, the way a teacher is pleased when the student finally argues back.

"Itemized," she repeated. "Fine. Your name, freely surrendered, erased from every record in Vaelmoor — I have the means, don't ask — becomes a *weight*. Covenant-magic runs on names, girl. It always did. That's why the guild traded in them. A name freely given has power that a name merely used doesn't. Give me yours, and I can open the sunken vault without breaking it. I can bind Vane's hunters out of the drowned quarter for one night. And I can forge you a passage-signature — a legal nonexistence that lets you stand before the drowned court as a party in your own right rather than the guild's property." She paused. "In exchange, you lose your history. Your bench. Your shelf. Every record that says you belonged anywhere. You will be, on paper and in magic both, no one. Can you live with that arithmetic?"

Paloma sat back down. Her hands were shaking. She put them flat on the table to stop them, and felt the grain of wood that some other exile had once planed smooth.

She thought of the guildhall. Of the warm reading room and the smell of ink and the way Senior Keeper Aldous Vane had said *some debts are above your station, child* — and the way he'd looked at her afterward, not with contempt but with fear, as if she were a page he couldn't quite razored out. She thought of how much she had loved that building, and how the love hadn't stopped even after she learned what it was built on, and how that was perhaps the most shameful thing of all.

"I want it in writing," she said. "Your terms. All of them. Before I decide."

Ilsa barked a laugh — a real one this time, startled out of her. "Still thinking like a clerk."

"Always," Paloma said. "But I'm learning to write in the margins."

* * *

They negotiated until the blue-green lantern burned low.

Ilsa drew up the terms on a scrap of sailcloth, in a script so old Paloma recognized it from the deepest strata of the archives — the pre-kingdom hand, the covenant-tongue, mixed with common characters like two rivers braiding. Nen read it aloud in a whisper, translating as they went, and each translated clause landed in Paloma's stomach like a stone dropped down a well:

*The name PALOMA HALLORAN, freely yielded, shall be struck from all rolls, registers, and remembrances within the walls of Vaelmoor...*

*...and the yielding party shall henceforth hold no rank, no bench, no shelf, no claim upon the Ember Guild, nor it upon her...*

*...in consideration whereof, the trader MAREN shall grant passage to the Sunken Archive, warding against pursuit for one turning of the moon...*

One line, near the bottom, Nen hesitated over. Read it twice. Then looked up at Paloma with something that might have been awe.

"This one says," the child said carefully, "that what is given cannot be taken back. Even by the giver. Even by the drowned. Even by the moon." A pause. "It says you will be *unwritten*, and unwritten things can sign where written things cannot."

Paloma went cold. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Ilsa said quietly, "that I may be selling you a key and calling it a price. Or a price and calling it a key. I honestly don't know which. The old magic rarely explains itself. That's the other lesson of the lawless places, girl: sometimes you pay first and learn what you bought later." She held out the charcoal stub. "Sign, or don't. Dawn's coming, and Vane's hunters sweep the Row at first light. I'd rather not explain to them why I'm harboring guild property."

Guild property. The words should have stung. Instead they settled into place like the last piece of a lock mechanism, and Paloma understood, finally and completely, that there was no version of the coming days in which she remained what she had been. The guild had already decided she was expendable. The drowned realm had already marked her city for collection. The only choice left — the only real choice, the first real choice of her entire careful, balanced, well-filed life — was who she would be on the far side of it.

She thought of her mother's voice saying *you have such neat handwriting, Paloma, you'll go far.* She thought of the empty space where the razored pages had been. She thought of Nen's cold hand squeezing hers in the flooded dark, trusting her without any contract at all.

Paloma took the charcoal.

Her hand did not shake. That surprised her most of all — that at the edge of erasure, her body was calmer than it had ever been filling out shipping manifests.

"If I do this," she said, "you tell me everything else. No more lessons priced separately. Where the vault is. What's inside. What happened to the children the guild paid away — all of them, not just Nen. Everything you know about what the drowned realm actually wants." She met Ilsa's eyes. "Full disclosure. That's my counter-term. Justice has a price — fine. But I'm not paying it blind."

Ilsa studied her for a long moment. Somewhere outside, the harbor sighed against the seawall — and sighed again, a heartbeat later, in the wrong direction.

"You drive a hard bargain for someone with no assets," the smuggler said. "Done. Full disclosure. Now sign your life away, little clerk. And when you do—" she nodded at the blank space beside the terms, "—don't write your name."

Paloma frowned. "Then what do I—"

"Write what you intend to become. The cloth takes intent. It always has." Ilsa's voice dropped, almost gentle. "Names are for things that stay the same."

The charcoal hovered over the sailcloth. Below them, through the floorboards, through the pilings, through forty fathoms of black and rising water, Paloma felt — or imagined she felt — something vast turn over in its sleep and begin, slowly, to listen.

She wrote four words. She did not let herself read them back.

The sailcloth drank them in like salt water into sand, and somewhere in the walls of Vaelmoor, in a hundred dusty registers, in the great roll of the Ember Guild itself, a line of ink faded to nothing — and a woman who had been Paloma Halloran stood up from the table lighter and emptier than she had ever been, holding nothing but a debt, a child, and a direction: *down.*

"Now," said the woman who had once been Ilsa Maren, snuffing the lantern, "let's go rob a vault."
