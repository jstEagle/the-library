## 9. Payment in Names

Ilsa Maren's counting room smelled of tar and old paper, and it was there, over a table scarred by a hundred smuggled bargains, that Paloma learned what her name was worth.

"Sit," Ilsa said, and did not look up from the ledger she was balancing. She balanced ledgers the way other people sharpened knives — with an economy of motion that suggested she had done it ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. "You want my ship, my crew, and my knowledge of the drowned quarter. That's three things. Three things cost."

"I have coin," Paloma said. "Not much. But some."

Ilsa's laugh was short and dry as a struck flint. "Coin." She set down her pen at last and looked up, and her eyes were the gray of harbor water in winter — the same eyes Paloma had seen staring out of a portrait in the guildhall's founding gallery, forty years younger and painted in oils instead of salt-scars. The founder of the Ember Guild, exiled before Paloma was born, sitting in a lamp-lit loft above a smuggler's den with ink on her fingers to the second knuckle. "Child, when the thirteenth moon finishes its turn, coin will be what the drowned use to weigh down their dead. I deal in things that keep their value."

Nen sat beside Paloma on the bench, very still, wrapped in one of Ilsa's spare coats that swallowed the child like a tide swallows a gull. The child's eyes moved between the two women, tracking words in both tongues now — Nen had begun to catch fragments of common speech, though had not yet spoken it.

"What things?" Paloma asked.

Ilsa turned her ledger around so Paloma could read it. The columns were not coin. They were not goods. The entries read: *one summer, aged; one face, remembered by its mother; the sound of a sister's voice; a name, guild-sworn.*

Paloma's stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept dropping, all the way down to the harbor mud.

"You know what these are," Ilsa said quietly. It was not a question.

"The covenant-goods." Paloma's voice came out flat. "What the guild traded across the water. Before they stopped paying."

"Before they started *taking*," Ilsa corrected. She closed the ledger with a soft, terrible finality. "I was there for the last honest entry. I wrote half of them myself, in another life, under another master. And when the guild decided the payments were too dear, I said: this debt does not die because we stop acknowledging it. They exiled me for saying it. So now I sit above the waterline and trade in the only currency both realms still honor." She spread her hands. "Memories. Years. Names. Things that are *yours* — truly yours, bone-deep yours — which is why they're worth something to someone else."

Paloma looked at Nen. The child looked back, and in that look was everything Paloma had carried since the night of the knock before dawn: the mud, the strange syllables, the marginalia in the oldest ledger matching word for word. *The drowned realm's last-born heir, sent as living collateral.* If the guild had its way — if Vane's rivals had their way — Nen would be given back to the water on the thirteenth moon, and the covenant would be honored in the cruelest possible reading, and Vaelmoor would go on pretending nothing had ever been owed.

Three days. The moon would turn in three days.

"Name your price," Paloma said.

* * *

The price was a name.

"Not any name," Ilsa said, pacing the length of the counting room while rain needled the windows. "It has to be a *guild* name. A sworn name, entered in the Ember rolls, witnessed and sealed. Those carry weight below — the drowned remember every name the guild ever bound itself with, because those were the names the covenant was written against. A guild-name is a promise made flesh. Yours has value." She stopped pacing. "But understand what you'd be selling. Not the word. The *membership*. The record of you. Every ledger line that says *Paloma Halloran, keeper of the Ember Guild* — gone. Struck. Not crossed out; erased, as if it was never written. You'd walk out of here unable to prove you were ever one of them. No guild door would open to you. No guild archive would answer your hand. The wards on the hall wouldn't know your touch."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, I take you into the drowned quarter, past the hunters Vane has surely set on you by now, to the sunken archive where the original covenant sleeps. And I get you out again, if getting out is possible." Ilsa's mouth twisted. "I won't lie about the odds. The lower city is already drinking the harbor. The old magic down there is awake and hungry and it doesn't distinguish between debtors and witnesses."

Paloma sat with it. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, she could feel the tide running backward. It ran backward every moonless night now. Tonight the sky was blind, and the whole harbor was exhaling when it should have inhaled.

She thought about the guildhall. She thought about the smell of the ledger-room in the early morning, lamp-oil and vellum and the particular dust that lived in the spines of books no one opened. She thought about Senior Keeper Aldous Vane teaching her, once, years ago, how to hold a quill so the ink wouldn't blot — his big hand curled over hers, patient, before he became the man who warned her to stop asking questions. She thought about the bench where she'd eaten lunch every day for six years, and the window seat in the scriptorium where she'd hidden as a girl to read the romances the guild confiscated, and the way the doors had always known her, swinging wide at her step without being pushed.

That was what a name was. Not the sound of it. The *knowing*. Sixty years of records that said she belonged, and every stone in that building agreeing.

And they had offered her up. Put her name forward to the envoy of brine and lantern-light like a coin tossed to a beggar — *take the clerk, take the smallest one, take her and leave us be.* She could still hear the Guildmaster's voice, smooth with practiced regret. Abandonment by the only home she had. She had run rather than be spent.

So why did the thought of selling the name herself hurt worse than being sold?

Because this way it was *hers*. This way she chose it.

"I'll pay," Paloma said.

Ilsa studied her for a long moment. Whatever she saw in Paloma's face seemed to satisfy something, or perhaps to sadden something; her expression held both. "You're certain."

"No," Paloma said honestly. "But certainty is a luxury for people with time. Do it."

* * *

They did it at Ilsa's own table, with instruments Paloma had never seen in the guildhall despite six years among its archives — because the guild did not keep instruments for *unwriting*, only for writing. A shallow dish of black salt. A candle of grey tallow that burned without flickering. A strip of parchment that was not parchment at all but something thin and pearly, like the inside of a shell, harvested — Ilsa did not say from what — beneath the harbor.

"Give me your hand," Ilsa said. "Writing hand."

Paloma extended her right hand. Her fingers were stained permanent brown at the middle joint, the mark of every keeper, ink worked so deep into the skin it might have been born there. Ilsa took the hand and turned it palm-up, and her grip was surprisingly gentle for a woman who looked like she could break a mast over her knee.

"This will feel like a tooth coming out," Ilsa said. "I won't pretend otherwise. I've sold two names myself. Both times I screamed. You're allowed to scream."

"What did you sell them *for*?" Paloma asked, because if she was going to do this she wanted to know what the market looked like on the far side.

"The first bought my exile — ironically. Payment to the guild to let me leave alive after I spoke against them. The second bought passage for a family the guild was going to feed to the tide, back when the small payments started. A mother, a father, two children." Ilsa's jaw tightened. "The guild called it *stabilizing the ledger*. I called it murder with bookkeeping. We disagreed professionally." She picked up a stylus of dark glass. "Last chance to keep your name and find another way."

"There is no other way. There's only ways that cost less of me and more of everyone else." Paloma steadied her breath. "Write."

Ilsa began to write on the shell-parchment, and Paloma felt it immediately — a pulling sensation starting in her fingertips, as if the ink were being drawn not onto the page but *out of her*, up through her arm. The stylus traced letters Paloma couldn't read, older than the common alphabet, older maybe than the kingdom, and with each stroke something loosened.

It was exactly like a tooth. Worse, actually, because a tooth is one root in one socket, and this was roots everywhere — in the guildhall doors that knew her step, in the archive shelves that leaned toward her hand, in the sixty years of entries bearing her name, in the memory of Vane's hand over hers teaching her the quill. All of it connected, all of it anchored in four syllables, and Ilsa was drawing those roots out one line at a time.

Paloma did not scream. But she made a sound low in her throat, involuntary, animal, and her free hand found the edge of the table and gripped until the wood creaked. Beside her, Nen's small hand covered hers — the child's palm cool and slightly briny, always slightly briny — and held on.

The last stroke fell. Ilsa lifted the stylus.

The name left.

There was no better word for it. It didn't fade; it *departed*, the way a guest departs a house, taking its coat and its warmth with it. Paloma sat in the ringing silence afterward and reached inward for *Paloma Halloran, keeper of the Ember Guild* and found — a socket. A clean, empty socket, shaped exactly like the thing that had been there. Around it, everything else remained: her memories, her mother's face, her love of rain, her fury at Vane, her terror for Nen. Everything except the belonging. She could remember belonging the way an amputee remembers the limb — vividly, precisely, uselessly.

"Oh," she said. It was a very small word for a very large absence.

Tears came without permission. She let them. Grief for identity freely surrendered was still grief, and it deserved its hour, even if the hour had to be short.

Ilsa blew out the grey candle and folded the shell-parchment into her ledger, into a column that now read, in the old tongue, *one name, guild-sworn, freely given.* "It's done," she said, and her voice was gentler than Paloma had yet heard it. "Every roll, every seal, every ward that knew you — the knowledge is gone from them. You could walk into the guildhall tonight and the doors would treat you as a stranger. Which, incidentally, may save your life, since Vane's hunters will be looking for a keeper, not a stranger." She paused. "What will you be called? People need something to shout when the flood comes."

Paloma wiped her face. The tears had washed tracks through the grime of three days' flight, and she found she didn't care.

"Nothing," she said. Then, testing the shape of it: "For now — nothing. I'll sign the next thing with whatever I've become by then."

Ilsa snorted. "Poetic. Poets drown same as anyone." But there was no contempt in it. There was something almost like respect, and something under that, almost like recognition — one nameless woman looking at the making of another.

Nen tugged Paloma's sleeve. When she looked down, the child was holding out the stub of charcoal Ilsa had provided earlier, and a scrap of wrapping-paper, and on it Nen had drawn — carefully, in the manner of a child who understood that pictures were the only safe language — two figures standing on a line between water and air. Above one figure, Nen had drawn a small blank space where a name-tag should be, and then, deliberately, a lantern.

Paloma looked at it for a long time.

"Yes," she said finally, her voice rough. "That's about the size of it."

* * *

They left within the hour, because the moon would not wait and neither would the water.

Ilsa led them down through the smuggler's warren — stairs behind false walls, passages that smelled of the sea long before the sea appeared — and at the last landing, where a rusted door stood open onto the drowned edge of the lower city, she stopped and turned back to look at Paloma.

"Once we're under, there's no renegotiating," Ilsa said. Rain streamed off the eaves beyond her; beyond the rain, streets lay black and mirror-still, ankle-deep in water that flowed the wrong direction, seaward, patient, obedient to a moon that hadn't risen yet. Lanterns floated unattended in the flooded intersections, burning green. "The archive vault sits at the heart of the old quarter, thirty feet down and sealed with founding-magic. Getting in is the easy part. What's inside—" She hesitated, and for the first time since Paloma had met her, Ilsa Maren looked uncertain. "What's inside decides whether we're reforging a covenant or opening a grave. Are you ready to find out which?"

Paloma touched the empty place inside herself where her name had been. It ached like weather. It ached like a promise.

Somewhere across the drowned city, a bell that no one had rung began to toll — slow, deep, counting down toward the thirteenth turn.

"No," Paloma said. "But I'm going anyway."

She stepped through the door, and the water rose to meet her like an old creditor, and together — the nameless keeper, the smuggler-founder, and the drowned heir — they went down into the breathing dark.
