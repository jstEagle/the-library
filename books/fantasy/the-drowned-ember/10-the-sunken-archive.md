## 10. The Sunken Archive

The drowned quarter did not look drowned at first.

That was the cruelest part of it, Paloma thought as Ilsa Maren's skiff slid between the rooftops of Old Vaelmoor — that the city below the waterline still looked like a city. Chimneys rose from green murk like fingers reaching for a sky they'd never touch again. A weathervane turned slowly in a current Paloma couldn't feel, pointing stubbornly toward a north that no longer existed. Window shutters hung open, as if the families inside had only stepped out for an hour and meant to come back.

"Forty years since the flood took it," Ilsa said, poling them forward with a long oar tipped in black glass. "They called it the Sinking. Officially, a storm surge. Unofficially—" she jerked her chin at the submerged streets sliding beneath them, "—the harbor swallowed its own throat. The guild wrote it up as an act of the gods. Gods had nothing to do with it."

Below the keel, shapes moved in the deep. Not fish. Fish didn't travel in lines.

Paloma kept Nen close, the child wedged between her knees in the skiff's belly, wrapped in an oilskin two sizes too large. Nen hadn't spoken since the warehouse — hadn't needed to. The child's eyes tracked everything below with the calm of someone looking at a familiar room.

"You've been down there," Paloma said quietly. "Before."

Ilsa's laugh was a dry rasp. "Once. To fetch something worth more than my life, and I got out owing more than I brought." She looked back over her shoulder, and in the grey morning light Paloma saw how old the smuggler actually was — older than her swagger, older than her scarred hands. "You don't go into the drowned quarter to take, girl. You go to beg."

* * *

The entrance was a church.

Or what remained of one — the Chapel of the Twin Tides, its bell tower leaning at an angle that defied both architecture and decency, its upper windows just breaking the surface like eyes barely open. Ilsa moored the skiff to a rotted spire and began pulling gear from a locked chest: breathing hoods of grey leather, weighted belts, lamps that burned without flame, giving off a cold blue light that made everything look like it was already underwater even before they went under.

"Hoods last four hours," Ilsa said. "The vault is twenty minutes' walk if the currents are polite. They won't be. Stay behind me, breathe shallow, and whatever you see looking at you through the windows — do not knock."

"Knock," Paloma repeated.

"People live down here who aren't people anymore. Some of them remember being neighbors. Some of them remember being owed." Ilsa strapped a knife to each forearm. "Politeness costs nothing. Curiosity costs everything."

Nen tugged Paloma's sleeve and pointed at the chapel door, half-submerged, its carved lintel worn smooth by decades of tide. Two figures were carved there — one standing on the waves, one rising from beneath them, their hands meeting over a lantern. The same image, Paloma realized with a lurch, that was stamped in gold on the cover of the guild's oldest ledger. The founding seal. She'd inked copies of it a hundred times without ever once asking what the two figures were doing.

*Marriage,* Nen had said, in the warehouse, in the child's broken common tongue. *Not contract.*

She touched the carving once, quickly, like touching a sleeping animal, and followed Ilsa down.

* * *

The water took her the way sleep takes the unwilling — all at once, and full of pressure.

Inside the hood, the world compressed to lamplight and the sound of her own breath, ragged against leather. Below the surface, Old Vaelmoor became something else entirely. The murk thinned as they descended along a sunken boulevard, and the blue lamp-glow caught details the surface had hidden: mosaic floors intact beneath silt, a fountain still trickling with some impossible spring-fed current, doors with names painted on them in a hand so careful it could only have been a ledger-keeper's.

*Halloran,* said one door.

Paloma stopped dead. Ilsa's hand closed on her shoulder and squeezed — *keep moving* — but for three heartbeats Paloma floated in front of that door, staring at her own family name flaking off sunken wood. Her grandmother's street. Her grandmother's house. The guild had records going back six generations; Paloma had assumed the Hallorans had always lived in the high quarter, near the guildhall, keepers to the bone.

But no. Someone had come up from down here. Or someone had been left behind when the water came.

*Ilsa.* She shaped the name against the hood, and the old woman turned, read her face, and shook her head once. *Later.* Everything was later with Ilsa Maren. Later, after the vault. Later, if they lived.

They walked the length of the boulevard while the drowned city watched. Faces appeared in windows — not bodies, not quite, but impressions, like faces pressed into wet clay and withdrawn. Some watched with curiosity. One, an old man shape in a doorway, raised a hand in something that might have been greeting or might have been warning, and Ilsa bowed her head slightly as she passed, the way you bow to a magistrate.

At the boulevard's end stood a building that should not have existed.

It was round. Every other structure in the drowned quarter was square, practical, Vaelmoor-made. This thing was seamless, curved like the inside of a shell, its walls a pale nacre that gave back the lamplight in slow ripples. No door. No windows. Just a single seam running from roof to ground, thin as a hair, glowing faintly where the blue light touched it.

"The archive," Ilsa said. Her voice came strangely through the water, muffled and close at once. "Built before the Sinking. Built, if the stories are true, before Vaelmoor itself. The drowned realm raised it for us — a gift, a gesture of good faith. Somewhere to keep the covenant safe, on the understanding that neither side would ever need to break it open."

"And the air?"

"Old magic doesn't drown, girl. The vault keeps its own weather." Ilsa ran her palm along the seam. Where she touched it, the nacre brightened, and Paloma felt the pressure around them shift — a low hum she sensed in her teeth rather than her ears. "It knows guild-hands. It's been waiting a long time to be angry about it."

The seam split.

Air rushed out in a column of bubbles, and then the wall simply opened, folding back like a flower that had been holding its petals shut for forty years, and warm dry air breathed out into the sea. Paloma stepped through with Nen's hand locked in hers, and the water fell away from them at the threshold like a curtain drawn aside, and they stood in a room full of light with the ocean pressing against every wall.

* * *

The vault was a sphere perhaps thirty paces across, and every inch of its inner surface was shelving.

Shelves curved up and over their heads, following the shell of the building, and on those shelves — stacked, sorted, labeled in two scripts, one human and one that moved when she wasn't looking directly at it — sat the contents of a civilization's honesty. Jars of preserved memory, swirling faintly. Bundles of years, tied with cord, ticking softly like sleeping insects. Names, written on slips of vellum that whispered to each other in the dark. And books. Thousands of books, their spines uncracked, their pages smelling of salt and cedar.

Paloma had spent her whole life in the Ember Guild's archive. She had believed, until this moment, that she knew what an archive was: order imposed on knowledge, knowledge hoarded as power. This was not that. This was a treasury. Every item here was a payment received or a promise made, catalogued with a reverence the guild had never once shown its own ledgers.

"It's beautiful," Nen said.

Paloma looked down. The child had pulled back her hood, and the air inside the vault accepted her as if she belonged — no dripping, no shivering, the brine drying from her skin in soft curls of steam. Nen was looking up at the shelves with an expression Paloma had never seen on her before: recognition. Homecoming. A child walking into a room that remembered her.

"This is where they kept our side of it," Nen said. "What your people gave us. What we gave them. It was supposed to be equal. It was supposed to be *even*." The child's small hand closed into a fist. "Look at the top shelves."

Paloma looked. The upper reaches of the vault, nearest the crown of the sphere, were nearly empty. Dust lay thick where goods should have been. Only a few jars remained, and their contents had gone grey and inert, dead memories rotting in glass.

"And the bottom?" Ilsa asked, though her voice said she already knew.

"The bottom is full." Nen's voice cracked. "Your guild kept taking. We kept giving. And then one day the taking stopped being honest, and your keepers came with razors instead of pens, and cut the truth out of the book, and sealed this door, and told the city the drowned realm was a story for frightening children." The child turned, and in the vault's pearl-light Paloma saw that Nen's eyes had gone fully dark, pupil and iris alike, twin drops of the harbor. "We waited forty years. Then we sent me."

The hum of the walls deepened. Paloma felt it in her sternum now, a vibration like a held note approaching its breaking point.

"Ilsa," she said carefully, "you said the vault would show us the original covenant. Where is it?"

Ilsa had gone very still near the sphere's center, where a single pedestal rose from the floor — the only furniture in the whole shining space. On it lay a slim volume bound in something that was not leather and not scale, its covers fused shut with a seam of gold. The twin-tides seal, life-sized, the two figures' hands meeting over the lantern.

"There," Ilsa said. "And there's our problem, girl. That binding isn't wax. It's consent. The covenant sealed itself when the guild broke faith — it can't be opened by force, by theft, by cleverness. I've had forty years and every tool a smuggler can buy, and I never got past that seam." She looked at Paloma, and for the first time since the warehouse, the old woman's confidence was gone, replaced by something rawer. "I brought you because you're a keeper. Because maybe guild-hands, honest ones, mean something to it. But if it opens for anyone, it'll be for someone willing to sign. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Whatever's in that book, opening it is the first signature. There's no reading it and walking away."

Paloma approached the pedestal. Up close, the gold seam pulsed faintly, in rhythm — she realized with a chill — with her own heartbeat. She laid her fingers against it.

The vault screamed.

Not the walls — the water. Through the nacre shell, from every direction at once, came a sound like a thousand voices shouting through fathoms of sea, and the pearl-light stuttered, and the hum climbed to a pitch that set her teeth aching. Ilsa spun toward the entrance, knives out, and Paloma understood one heartbeat too late.

The vault had known guild-hands. It had also, apparently, announced them.

"They're here," Ilsa snarled. "Vane's hunters. Three boats at least — I marked them at the spire line an hour ago and hoped I was wrong." She shoved Nen behind her. "Sealed door or not, they'll cut their way in. They've got guild-iron, and guild-iron bites through anything the drowned realm built, because it was built to bite *us* first."

Through the curving wall, distorted by water and nacre, Paloma saw them: lantern-light moving along the sunken boulevard, six points of flame descending in formation, unhurried, certain. Men in grey oilskins with the Ember Guild's sigil burned into their shoulders. Ledgers of their own, she'd have bet her life — hunters who wrote down what they caught.

"How long?" she asked.

"Minutes. Less." Ilsa was already dragging crates of the dead memories across the floor, stacking them against the entrance seam. "Girl, listen to me. Whatever you're going to do with that book, do it fast, because we are leaving through the back of this vault or not at all, and the back is a prayer and a current and nothing else."

Paloma pressed both palms flat against the covenant's cover and closed her eyes.

*I'm not them,* she thought, pouring everything into the contact — the shame of the razored pages, the door with her name on it, Nen's fist, the empty top shelves. *I'm not Aldous Vane. I'm not the guild that fed children to the tide. I'm the one who counted what was taken. Let me read the debt.*

The gold seam warmed under her hands.

Then it cooled — deliberately, unmistakably cool, like a hand withdrawing — and a voice that was not a voice spoke through the shell of the vault, through the water, through forty years of silence, in the pre-kingdom tongue that Paloma somehow understood anyway:

***Not yet. Not alone. When the moon turns, bring one of ours and one of yours, and let each give what cannot be taken back. Until then, keeper, run.***

The seam of the covenant went dark. And outside, the first blow of guild-iron rang against the vault's entrance like a bell tolling for the drowned.
