## 11. Vane's Confession

The iron ram struck the vault door a ninth time, and Aldous Vane felt each blow land somewhere behind his ribs.

He stood at the mouth of the sunken street with the floodwater at his shins and forty years of guild discipline wrapped around him like a coat that no longer fit. Behind him, six keepers held the ram's ropes, their lanterns guttering in the salt wind that came off the harbor without any wind above to drive it. Before him, through the warped glass of the vault's outer seal, he could see them: the girl Halloran — no, not Halloran anymore; she had sold the name, which was either madness or the most disciplined act he had witnessed in decades — and the smuggler Ilsa Maren, bleeding somewhere out of sight, and the child.

The child.

Vane closed his eyes and let the water lap against his knees, and for one unguarded moment he was not Senior Keeper of the Ember Guild but a young clerk again, twenty-three years old, standing in this same flooded quarter on the night of his first payment.

*You will carry it down yourself,* old Keeper Sorrel had told him. *That is the whole of the office, boy. The ledgers are theater. The covenant is theater. What keeps the city dry is that once a season, someone from the guild walks into the dark and gives the deep what it asks for, and comes back up having seen nothing.*

*Gives it what?* he had asked.

Sorrel had looked at him with something that was almost pity, or perhaps envy, worn so long it had fossilized into kindness. *Whatever fits in the box.*

* * *

The tenth blow rang out. The vault did not answer.

"It's not working," said Keeper Dren, hauling back on the rope, his voice pitched high with fear he thought he was hiding. "Senior Keeper, the seal won't take the ram. It's like hitting the harbor itself."

"Then we stop hitting it," Vane said, "before you bring the whole drowned quarter down on our heads."

They looked at him — six faces, lantern-lit, waiting for instruction the way they had waited all their careers, because that was what the guild made of people: hands that did not think. He had been those hands once. He had spent thirty years becoming the mind that directed them, and every hour of those thirty years he had told himself the same sentence, the way a man tells himself a prayer whose god he stopped believing in before the first amen:

*It is necessary. It is necessary. It is necessary.*

"Fall back to the stair," he ordered. "Set the watch at the floodline. No one enters the water."

"And the girl?" Dren asked. "And the child? The vote was—"

"I know what the vote was." The vote had been eleven hands raised, his own among them, solemn as a funeral: *the thirteenth moon shall receive the collateral.* Collateral. That was the word they had chosen, the word he himself had drafted into the motion, because Vane was good with words, had always been good with words, and words were how the guild buried things alive while calling it bookkeeping.

"The girl is a signatory now," he heard himself say. "Or nearly one. You do not ram a door when the person behind it holds the key."

Dren frowned. "Key?"

"Go," Vane said, and the keepers went, boots splashing up the drowned stair, until he stood alone in the flooded street with the vault humming its low, patient note and the water rising by inches around him, though the tide tables said the harbor was falling.

He should have gone with them. Every rule he had ever written said so. Instead he waded forward, set his palm flat against the vault's outer seal — cold glass, cold as the bottom of the sea — and spoke to the dark inside.

"Halloran." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Paloma. I know you can hear me. The vault carries sound the way the ledger carries ink. Everything written here is written forever. So hear this, and write it down, since writing is the only sacrament either of us trusts."

The water stilled. Even the wind off the harbor seemed to hold its breath.

"You want to know why the pages were razored from the founding covenant. You want to know what your neat little ledgers have been balancing all these years, why the impossible things came in and never went out, why the guild grew rich on debts it never collected." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I'll tell you. Not because you've earned it. Because someone has to say it aloud before the moon turns, and it seems I am the only coward left with the stomach for confession."

* * *

The first payment Vane ever carried down had been a memory.

He had not known that then. The box was lead-lined and sealed with guild-wax, and Sorrel had told him only: *carry it to the third arch below the tide-gate, set it on the stone, knock twice, walk away, and do not look at the water.* He had done exactly that, because obedience was the first virtue the guild taught and the last one it forgave you for losing.

But on the walk back up, he had found himself unable to remember his mother's face.

Not faded. Not dimmed. *Absent* — a hole in his memory shaped like a woman, ringed by everything around it: the kitchen, the smell of bread, the chipped blue cup she drank from. He could describe the cup perfectly. The hand that held it was gone, scooped out like a price paid.

He had gone to Sorrel, shaking, and Sorrel had poured him brandy and said, "Now you understand the office. The deep doesn't take coin, boy. Coin is a human fiction. It takes what's real. Memories. Years. Names. And once, long ago, when the guild was younger and greedier, it took other things — and kept them. That's the theft your razored pages used to record. We were meant to be brokers. Passing vessels. The goods flow through us, both directions, and the covenant holds. But the founding keepers learned they could take delivery and never pay out. They learned the deep forgets, between payments, what it was owed. So they stopped paying. And when the deep began to remember—" Sorrel had turned his cup slowly on the table. "We started paying again. Small things. A memory here. A year there. Enough to keep it sleeping. Enough to keep the delay going one more generation, and then one more, always promising ourselves we'd find a way to end the debt honestly someday."

"And if we don't?" Vane had asked, twenty-three and still capable of a straight spine.

Sorrel had smiled the smile Vane would spend thirty years learning to wear. "Then we keep paying. That's what duty is, boy. Doing the necessary thing past the point where it can be forgiven."

* * *

For fifteen years, Vane carried the boxes down. Memories mostly — condemned criminals who sold their worst hours, the dying who traded their last year for their family's comfort, volunteers from the Memory Houses whose names the guild recorded and then, in a stroke of institutional mercy he still could not decide about, erased. The deep slept. The tides behaved. The city of Vaelmoor went on believing its harbor was only water.

Then came the famine winter, when the guild's stores ran thin and the volunteers dried up, and the deep stirred harder than it had in a century — tides running backward, fish rising dead with lantern-light in their eyes, the thirteenth moon showing itself a full night early. And Sorrel, old and frightened and holding the office he had passed like a disease, had come to Vane with a list.

Not a list of memories.

A list of children.

"Foundlings," Sorrel said. "No kin. No records outside our own. The orphanages owe the guild more than they can ever repay — we'll call it debt settlement. The deep takes a child the way it took the founder's daughter, back at the breaking, and sleeps another decade, and we buy the city ten more years to find an honest answer."

"There is no honest answer," Vane had said. "You told me so yourself."

"Yes," Sorrel agreed. "So the question is only whether we are men who flinch."

Vane had not flinched. That was the thing he needed Paloma Halloran to know, needed the vault itself to know, needed carved into whatever eternal record the drowned realm kept in place of a soul: *he had not flinched.* Seven children in nineteen years. He had carried four of the boxes down himself. He had knocked twice on the stone at the third arch below the tide-gate, and walked away, and not looked at the water, and each time he had come back up the stair a little lighter, as though the deep took a weight from the carrier too — some portion of the guilt, weighed and deducted, entered neatly in a column he could not see.

That was the horror of it. Not the deed. The *bookkeeping* of the deed. That atrocity could be amortized. That seven small coffins could be spread across nineteen years of ledgers until each one cost less than a bad harvest, less than a courier's lost shipment, less than the ink used to record it. The guild had taught him that anything could be made bearable if you divided it into small enough payments.

And now the balance had come due all at once, with interest, and the interest was a flood walking up the streets of Vaelmoor on legs of brine and lantern-light.

* * *

"Halloran." His knuckles rapped the seal, twice, an old reflex that made his stomach turn. "The senior keepers have been feeding the drowned realm for sixty years. Memories. Years. Names. Children. Seven children. I carried four of them myself, and I signed the receipts, and I slept afterward, and that is the part I cannot make you understand — that I *slept*. Duty is a mask that fits so well you forget your face is under it. I have worn mine so long I genuinely do not know anymore whether I am a monster who believed he was a servant, or a servant who became a monster so gradually the change never showed in any ledger."

His voice broke on the last word. He let it. There was no one left to see the Senior Keeper of the Ember Guild weep into a flooded street except a girl who had every reason to hate him, a smuggler who had every reason to despise him, and a child of the deep who had every reason to collect.

"They voted tonight," he went on, quieter. "Eleven hands. Yours truly among them. They mean to give the deep the child on the thirteenth moon — Nen, the heir, the last-born — and call it settling the account. One payment to end all payments. It won't work. You know it won't work. The vault told you as much: *one of ours and one of yours, each giving what cannot be taken back.* A stolen child given unwillingly is just another theft. Another entry in the column of what we owe. They would be paying the debt with more debt, and the deep knows it, and I know it, and I raised my hand anyway, because that is what I do. I raise my hand. I sign the receipt. I sleep afterward."

He pressed his forehead against the cold seal.

"But I am done sleeping," he said. "Here is what I can give you, keeper. Here is my payment, freely given, what cannot be taken back: the truth, spoken aloud, on the record, with my name attached — the name I have left, such as it is. The guild will call it treason. Let them. Treason against a body that feeds children to the deep is the only honor left in this city."

From inside the vault, faintly, came a sound. Not the voice-that-was-not-a-voice. Something smaller. Footsteps, perhaps. Or the soft scrape of a chair drawn toward a door.

Vane straightened, wiped his face with a wet sleeve, and became, one final time, the Senior Keeper: spine straight, voice level, the mask settled into place — though now, at last, he wore it knowing it was a mask, and that knowledge changed nothing and everything.

"Listen carefully, because I will only say this once, and then I go back up the stair and play my part to the end, because a man who has been the mechanism for thirty years cannot stop being the mechanism in a single night — but a mechanism can jam. When the vote comes to the floor, I will move to delay. I will lose. But losing takes time, and time is the only currency I have ever been able to earn honestly. Three days until the moon turns. I can buy you two of them, perhaps. Use them."

The seal warmed under his palm — deliberately, unmistakably warm, like a hand pressing back.

And then, through the glass, through the dark, through forty years of silence finally broken, a voice answered him. High. Young. Speaking the common tongue of Vaelmoor, haltingly, like someone remembering words learned long ago and laid aside.

"*We did not want your children, keeper.*"

Vane went very still.

"*We wanted you to remember us. Your founders promised we would be remembered — that was the marriage-vow, the whole of it, the only term. And instead you wrote us down in books and locked the books away, and fed us your forgetting, year after year, so you would not have to feel the loss of us. My people are not rising to drown you.*"

A pause. The water in the street trembled with something that was not a wave.

"*We are rising because we are lonely.*"

The warmth beneath Vane's palm faded. Somewhere beyond the vault, deeper than the vault, in the green-black throat of the harbor, a thousand drowned lanterns flickered alight at once — and Aldous Vane, keeper of forty years, destroyer of seven small lives, stood alone in the rising water and understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning, that everything he had ever paid had been payment for the wrong crime entirely.

Above him, muffled by stone and flood, the guild bell began to toll.

The vote had moved. The moon was turning. And he had two days — perhaps — to teach a city of forgetting how to remember.
