## 12. Nen Speaks

The night before the moon turned for the last time, Nen dreamed in salt.

Not the waking kind of dream — not the visions the elders had pressed into her small skull like flowers between pages, all duty and drowning and the long gray procession of her ancestors walking down into the dark. This dream was warm. In it she sat at a table with a lamp burning yellow, and a woman with ink-stained fingers was teaching her letters, and no one was asking her to be the last of anything.

She woke in the smuggler's loft above the flooded chandlery, and the dream was still on her tongue like honey that hadn't finished melting.

Paloma was asleep sitting up, which Nen had learned she did when exhaustion finally caught her — back against the wall, ledger open on her knees, pen still hooked between two fingers as if even unconsciousness might be interrupted by an entry worth making. The single candle had burned down to a thumb's width. Rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere below, Ilsa Maren's crew moved cargo through water thigh-deep, and their voices came up through the floorboards in low, wet murmurs, half words, half tide.

Nen watched Paloma sleep and felt the thing in her chest that she had been carrying since the harbor — since the envoy of brine and lantern-light had walked out of the black water and the guild had offered up a child as payment and another child had run instead. The thing had a shape now. She could almost read it.

It looked like the marginalia in the oldest ledger. It looked like her mother's hands. It looked like a door left open in a house where everyone had died.

*Say it,* the thing said, in the pre-kingdom tongue, in the voice of every drowned elder who had ever sung her to sleep. *You were sent to be spent, not heard. Say nothing. Be collateral. Be quiet.*

But the dream was still sweet on her tongue. And Paloma, asleep, had one hand curled loosely near Nen's shoulder — not touching, just near, the way you leave a door open.

Nen opened her mouth, and for the first time since she'd walked out of the sea, she spoke the common tongue.

"You're holding the book wrong."

---

Paloma came awake the way a cat comes awake — all at once, upright, pen already moving. Then her eyes found Nen in the candlelight and went wide, and wide again, wider than Nen had ever seen them go, and Nen realized with a small, strange satisfaction that she had just done something no one in this whole dry city had believed possible.

"Nen." Paloma's voice cracked on the second syllable. "Nen, you— say it again. Please. Say anything."

"You're holding the book wrong," Nen repeated. Her common tongue was clumsy, each word arriving slightly damp, slightly bent, like shells carried up from deep water. "Spine down. You crack it that way. Grandmother would beat your fingers."

Paloma looked down at the ledger in her lap. She turned it over, adjusted her grip, and then — absurdly, wonderfully — laughed. It was a broken sound, half sob, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth to keep the rest of it in, but her eyes were shining and her shoulders shook.

"Three weeks," she whispered. "Three weeks I've been talking *at* you. Drawing pictures. Acting out whole pantomimes in front of Vane's hunters like a madwoman. And you could speak the whole time."

"Not the whole time." Nen pulled her knees up under the wool blanket Ilsa had given her, the one that smelled of tar and cloves. "I learned it from you. From the ledgers. From listening." She touched her own throat, surprised by the ache there. "It hurts. Your language. It has no water in it. Every word is a stone."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry." Nen frowned, searching for the right stones. "Be *listening*. There isn't much time, and I have been so quiet, and everyone keeps deciding things about me while I'm quiet." She thought of the ritual chamber they'd described in whispers above her head. She thought of the thirteenth moon. "I'm done being quiet."

Paloma set the ledger aside entirely — Nen noted this, marked it, treasured it: the keeper of books, setting aside a book — and turned to face her fully, cross-legged, close enough that the candlelight touched them both.

"Then tell me," Paloma said. "Tell me everything. Start wherever you need to start."

Nen took a breath, and it tasted of rain and lamp oil and the far-off salt of the harbor, and she began.

---

"In the drowned city — you call it nothing, because you razored its name out of your records, but its name is *Vaelmoor-beneath*, the elder sister, the first Vaelmoor — we do not write things down." She held up her own small hands, turning them in the light. "We keep memory in bodies. My grandmother held four hundred years in hers. When she spoke a memory aloud, whoever listened *carried* it after, and it did not grow smaller in the carrying. That is what your guild took. Not trinkets. Not treasure. *Us.*"

Paloma had gone very still. Nen watched her understand, word by word, the way ice understands spring.

"The covenant," Nen went on, "was made before your kings, before your guild had a name. The two cities were one city once — one harbor, one people, some living above the waterline and some below, and the water rose slowly enough, in those years, that going under was a choice made gently, family by family, generation by generation. We did not drown. We *moved*. And the ones who stayed above promised the ones who went below: *you will not be forgotten. Every year we will trade. You give us what the deep grows — light, salvage, calm waters, the impossible things. We give you our memories, our names, our years, so that none of us is ever lost.*"

Her voice caught. She pushed through it.

"It wasn't a contract. Contracts are for strangers. It was—" She searched, and found the word in Paloma's own tongue, the one she'd read a hundred times in the razored margins' surviving fragments. "*A marriage.* Two houses becoming one house. That is why the seal was written in blood from both sides. That is why it cannot be enforced, only *kept*. You do not enforce a marriage. You wake up every day and choose it."

"And they stopped choosing it," Paloma said quietly.

"They stopped choosing it." Nen nodded. "Generations ago. But here is what your senior keepers do not understand, what your Vane does not understand, what even Ilsa Maren maybe does not understand." She leaned forward. "We are not angry that you kept our gifts. Gifts are given; keeping them is no crime. We are angry that you stopped giving *back*, and worse — that you pretended the marriage had never happened. You cut our names out of your founding covenant. You razored us out of your own history. Do you know what it is to be forgotten by your own spouse?"

Paloma's face did something complicated and terrible. Nen watched grief move across it like weather.

"The payments," Paloma said. "The small ones. The children Vane's faction has been sending down, year after year, to delay—"

"Were never demanded." Nen's voice went flat and cold, colder than any depth. "The elders *refused* them, at first. Sending a child to pay a debt of memory is like paying a wedding vow with a corpse. But the guild kept sending, and each refusal weakened us further, because every refused payment is a wound in the covenant itself, and the covenant is the only thing holding both cities above and below in balance. So the elders began to accept them. To keep the seal from tearing entirely. Do you understand? Your guild's mercy is killing my world faster than its cruelty would have."

The rain filled the silence. Below them, the smugglers had gone quiet, listening, though perhaps they didn't know they were listening.

"So what does the drowned realm want?" Paloma asked. Her voice was careful, the voice of someone walking a floor she suspected was rotten. "When the envoy came. When the tides ran backward. What is it your elders actually demand?"

And here Nen hesitated, because the true answer was so small, so human, so unbearably sad, that she had carried it all this way without ever saying it aloud, and saying it felt like opening her ribs.

"To be remembered," she said. "That's all. That is the entire debt. Not vengeance. Not drowning. The flood is not a weapon, Paloma — it's a *symptom*. A world that is being forgotten begins to forget how to hold its own shape. Our streets soften. Our dead unname themselves. Our lanterns gutter because no one above remembers lighting them. The water rises because we are losing the argument with oblivion, and the water is what forgetting looks like when it wins." She swallowed. "The elders sent me — the last-born, the heir, the one whose body holds the most memory — not as collateral. As a *reminder*. They sent me hoping someone above would look at my face and see their own history looking back. And instead your guild voted to sacrifice me on the thirteenth moon."

Paloma made a sound like something breaking underwater.

"I know," Nen said, gentler now. "I know you didn't vote. I know you stole me back. I know Ilsa bled for it." She reached out and put her small hand over Paloma's ink-stained one. "I'm not telling you this to hurt you. I'm telling you because you asked me to stop being quiet, and because you are the only person above the waterline who has ever treated me as a *signatory* instead of a sum."

"A signatory," Paloma repeated. Her eyes had gone distant, calculating, the way they did when a ledger balanced in her head. "Nen. If the covenant was a marriage — if it needs willing parties, chosen daily, kept rather than enforced — then the old document doesn't matter. It crumbled to salt the moment a human hand touched it because it was never meant to be *handled*. It was meant to be *lived*."

"Yes."

"Which means it can't be restored. Only reforged. By people who choose it. One from each realm." Paloma was almost talking to herself now, words tumbling, the pen-hand twitching toward a ledger that wasn't there. "One from each realm, willing, named or unnamed, signing in their own—"

"*Stop.*" Nen's grip tightened. "Before you finish that sentence, hear the cost. Whoever signs becomes the covenant. Not its keeper. Its *body*. The balance of two worlds, held in one life. Every tide, every debt, every memory traded across the waterline, passing through them forever. The elders showed me what the first signatories became. They didn't die, Paloma. They *widened*. Until they were more harbor than person." She held Paloma's gaze. "I will not let you volunteer for that in a burst of guilt. Guilt is just another currency, and I am so tired of being paid for with other people's feelings."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The candle guttered. Somewhere out over the harbor, faint as a heartbeat, the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its turn.

"Then don't let me," Paloma said at last. "Come with me. Watch me. If I reach for it out of guilt, pull me back. But Nen — if I reach for it out of *choice*—" She smiled, and it was the smile from Nen's dream, the lamp-yellow one, the one where nobody was the last of anything. "Someone has to stop choosing the guild's way and start choosing yours. Someone has to be the door left open."

Nen looked at her for a long time. Then she did something she had not done since leaving the water: she sang, very softly, three notes of the elder-melody, the one that meant *witnessed, witnessed, witnessed*, and she saw Paloma feel it in her sternum like a struck bell.

"That's yes," Nen explained. "In case your language doesn't have a word for it. Most of your words are for owning things. Mine are for keeping them."

Paloma laughed again, wet-eyed, and reached for the ledger after all — spine held carefully downward, the correct way — and began to write, fast, in both tongues, while Nen dictated the terms of a marriage three thousand years widowed.

They worked until the candle died.

And it was in the dark afterward, in the salt-smelling dark where the rain had stopped and the harbor had gone strangely, terribly silent, that Nen heard it: footsteps on the stairs below. Many of them. Moving in the guild's cadence, the cadence she'd memorized in the ritual chamber — and a voice she knew, thin and strained and unmistakable, saying:

"—found them. Both of them. Vane sends his regards."

* * *

Nen was on her feet before the echo settled, pulling Paloma up by the wrist. Through the shutter slats she saw them crossing the flooded street below — six figures with lanterns hooded in oiled cloth, wading hip-deep through water that should not have been there, water that was rising even as she watched, rising in perfect stillness, like a held breath.

And at the rear of them, half-submerged, moving against the current of his own hunters, came a seventh figure — tall, gray-robed, lantern-light flickering inside him like a flame behind glass.

Senior Keeper Aldous Vane had come himself.

And he was not looking at the smugglers' loft.

He was looking, with an expression Nen recognized from her grandmother's oldest memories — the look of a man reading his own death warrant and finding, horribly, that the handwriting was his own — directly at the harbor.

Where the water had begun, very softly, to glow.
