## 13. The Broken Seal

The water was glowing because the vault was waking.

Paloma understood this the way she understood arithmetic — not all at once, but in a cascade of small certainties that assembled themselves into a single terrible sum. The drowned quarter below them was not merely flooded ruin. It was a lockbox, and something inside it had felt the turning of the moon like a hand on a doorknob.

"Down," Ilsa Maren said. "All of you. Now."

The smuggler moved through her own loft with terrifying economy, kicking aside a hatch Paloma had taken for part of the floor. Beneath it: stone steps slick with weeping condensation, descending into green-black dark. The air that rose from it smelled of old iron and older salt.

"You've had this under your bedroom the whole time," Paloma said.

"I've had everything under my bedroom the whole time." Ilsa lit a lantern from a shelf — an actual lantern, wick and oil, absurdly ordinary — and thrust it at Nen. "Child holds the light. Light doesn't argue, light doesn't ask questions. You two, behind me. If I say run, you run toward me, not away from me. People always run the wrong way. It's how they die."

Above them, through the loft's salt-crusted windows, the gray-robed figure of Aldous Vane had stopped at the waterline. He stood there in the rising tide like a man waiting for a ferry, hands folded, patient as a ledger line. He did not call out. He did not need to. His stillness said everything: *I know where you're going. I will be there when you arrive.*

"He'll take the long way," Ilsa muttered, watching him. "Around by the bell tower stairs. That buys us perhaps twenty minutes. Perhaps fifteen, if his knees are better than they look."

"They aren't," Paloma said. "He complains about them every guild meeting. Every single one."

Something almost like a smile crossed Ilsa's face and died there. "Then we have twenty minutes to break into a vault that has killed better thieves than me, read a document older than the kingdom, and find out how to stop the sea from eating our city." She swung down through the hatch. "Move."

* * *

The stair went down farther than the building above could possibly have contained. Paloma counted steps out of habit — forty, sixty, ninety — until counting became its own kind of prayer. The walls changed around them as they descended: brick to granite, granite to something darker, veined with pale mineral lines that caught the lantern-light and seemed, when Paloma wasn't looking directly at them, to shift. Water wept through everywhere but never gathered. It ran upward along the veins, back toward the harbor, against every law she'd ever transcribed.

Nen held the lantern high. In its glow the child's face was calm in a way that frightened Paloma more than any panic would have. This was the drowned realm's last-born heir, walking down into the deep places of the world like a girl going home.

"Do you feel it?" Nen asked softly. Not the pre-kingdom tongue — common speech, halting but hers now, learned these past weeks in stolen hours. "The humming. Like a struck bell that won't finish ringing."

Paloma listened. And beneath the drip of water and Ilsa's measured footfalls, she heard it: a note too low for ears, felt instead in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the ink-stained pads of her fingers. A sound like a covenant holding its breath.

"The vault knows you're coming," Ilsa said over her shoulder. "It's known since the moon turned. Old magic isn't asleep, girl. It's just slow. Slow as tide. But it notices everything, and it forgets nothing, which is more than I can say for the guild that built it."

At the bottom of the stair, the passage opened.

The vault was not a room so much as a wound in the world — a spherical hollow perhaps thirty feet across, its walls fused glass-smooth, ribbed with arches of black metal that met at a keystone overhead. The air inside was dry. Perfectly, impossibly dry, though the harbor pressed in on every side beyond the walls, held back by nothing visible, by nothing except the same low note that hummed in Paloma's bones. At the chamber's center stood a pedestal of the same black metal, and on it, sealed beneath a dome of what looked like solidified moonlight, lay a book.

Not a large book. That was the first shock. Paloma had spent her life among ledgers the size of tombstones; she had imagined the founding covenant as a cathedral of vellum. This was scarcely bigger than a hymnal, bound in gray leather gone soft with age, its cover stamped with two interlocking circles — one open, one closed.

"That's it?" she whispered. "That's everything? The whole covenant?"

"Small things hold big debts," Ilsa said. "You'd know that if you'd ever actually read your own guild's ledgers instead of just keeping them." She circled the pedestal without touching it, eyes narrowed. "Now. The dome. In my day there were three keys. Two were lost when the guild purged us. The third—" she tapped her own breastbone "—was supposed to be me. Founder's blood. They razored my name out of the rolls, but they couldn't razor it out of the magic. Sloppy work. The guild has always been sloppy about what it steals."

She pressed her palm flat against the dome.

The moonlight-solidity rippled like the surface of the harbor. For a heartbeat it resisted — and then, with a sound like a sigh heard underwater, it opened. Not shattered. Opened, folding back on itself in petals, courteous as a door held for a guest.

Ilsa snatched her hand back as if burned. "Well," she said quietly. "That's either very good or very bad. It shouldn't have been *easy*."

Paloma stepped forward before fear could finish forming. Her hands — keeper's hands, ink-stained, precise, dismissed — reached for the book.

The moment her skin touched the cover, the covenant crumbled.

There was no violence in it. That was the horror. The gray leather sloughed away like ash, like frost breathed upon, and the pages beneath — pages dense with script in two interleaved tongues, human and pre-kingdom, columns married line to line like hands clasped across a table — dissolved even as she lifted them. Letters lifted off the vellum and hung in the air a moment, glittering, legible, *true*, and then collapsed into white grains that poured through her fingers and scattered across the floor.

Salt. The whole book, in the space of four heartbeats, became salt.

"No." Paloma dropped to her knees among the drifts of it, scrabbling, trying to gather the grains, knowing even as she did it that it was madness — you cannot reassemble a sentence from sand, cannot restore a word once the meaning has left it. "No, no — I barely touched it, I only—"

"It's not your fault." Ilsa's voice came from very far away. "Look at it, girl. Look properly."

Paloma looked. Through the falling haze of salt, she saw what she should have seen at once. The crumbling hadn't started where she touched. It had started everywhere at once — the whole book failing together, uniformly, like a thing that had been dead for years and had simply been waiting for someone to notice.

"It was already broken," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, flattened, as if the vault were pressing on it. "The seal didn't break when I touched it. It broke when—" When the guild severed the bargain. When they kept the memories and the names and the years and walked away. When they fed it children in secret to keep the corpse quiet. "It broke generations ago. We've been guarding a dead letter. All of it — the ledgers, the impossible things, the payments — we've been making offerings to a contract that no longer exists."

"Not quite." Nen crouched beside her in the salt. The child took a pinch of it, let it run through her fingers, and watched it fall with an expression of terrible tenderness. "Dead things don't hum. Listen."

Paloma listened. The note was still there. Fainter now, fraying at the edges like a rope strand by strand — but there. Holding back the harbor. Holding back the thirteenth moon.

"The words are gone," Nen said. "But the promise isn't. A promise doesn't live in paper. Paper is just where people write down that they were brave enough to make one." She looked up at Paloma, lantern-light swimming in her dark eyes. "My grandmother told me. Before the end. She said: *when the letter fails, look for the signatories.*"

* * *

They found the signatories' clause because Paloma made herself read the salt.

That was the trick of it — the one thing her years of training had actually given her, the thing no senior keeper had ever thought worth teaching a child with neat handwriting: how to read a damaged record. You worked backward from structure. You counted the lacunae. You asked what shape of argument would fit the holes. Kneeling in the wreck of the covenant, Paloma closed her eyes and rebuilt it in her head the way she rebuilt every corrupted ledger page — from margins inward, from logic outward.

Two interlocking circles on the cover. One open. One closed.

"The seal is two signatures," she said slowly, aloud, because saying things aloud was how she made them real. "Every covenant needs a voice on each side. The founders wrote their names here — the guild's first keeper and whoever spoke for the drowned realm. And when the guild broke faith, the signatures didn't just sit there. They *were* the binding. Kill the honesty between them and the whole instrument starts to rot. The words went first. Then the paper. The signature is the last thing to go." She opened her eyes. "How long does the last thing take to go?"

Ilsa had gone very still by the pedestal. "Moon-tides," she said. "The vault runs on them. Always has. Thirteen turns to a full cycle of the deep moon, and the deep moon is turning now — you've seen the harbor running backward, haven't you? That's the mechanism winding down. When the thirteenth turn completes..." She drew a line across her throat with one thumb, unhurried, matter-of-fact. "The note stops. The water comes in. Not just the lower quarters. Everything."

"How long?"

"Three days."

Three days. Paloma sat back on her heels in the salt of the oldest promise in the world and felt despair come for her the way she'd always feared drowning would feel — not as a wave but as a slow, reasonable, inevitable filling. Three days to reforge a covenant whose text no longer existed, in a language half of which she'd learned from marginalia, requiring signatories from two realms, while the guild that had caused all of it hunted them through the flooded streets and its senior keeper stood patiently at the waterline reading his own death warrant.

And then, underneath the despair, something else stirred. Something small and stubborn and familiar. It took her a moment to recognize it, because it wore no guild livery and carried no senior keeper's approval.

It was the feeling she got when a ledger refused to balance — and she refused to stop.

"Then we don't need the old covenant," she said.

Isla raised an eyebrow. "Oh? And what do we need, clerk?"

"A new one." Paloma stood. Salt fell from her skirts. Her hands were shaking, and she looked at them — her keeper's hands, the hands everyone in the guildhall had patted and overlooked for eleven years — and made herself say the rest. "The old one is dead. Fine. Dead instruments get superseded. That's not scandal, that's *procedure*. Any scribe knows it. What we need is a new instrument, freshly executed, with valid signatories on both sides. The text can be rewritten — I can rewrite it, I've been reconstructing it in fragments for weeks, I know its architecture better than anyone alive. The drowned realm's signatory is standing right here." She looked at Nen, and the child lifted her chin. "Which leaves the other side."

Silence. Even the vault's hum seemed to lean in.

"The guild will never agree," Ilsa said flatly. "Vane would sooner drown than admit fault, and the rest of them would sooner drown *you*. They already tried handing you to the envoy like a receipt."

"I know." Paloma's voice did not shake. She was surprised by that. "I'm not asking them."

"Then who signs for the humans? You?" Ilsa laughed, short and hard. "Girl, you're nameless in the rolls the moment anyone checks. No guild standing behind you, no office, no seal. You can't bind a city. You can barely bind a parcel."

"I bound myself to this," Paloma said. "When I traded my name to you. That's what a name *is*, Ilsa — it's what stands surety for your word. Mine's already spent. Which means everything I am now is available to sign with." She knelt again, deliberately, and pressed her palm into the salt, into the grave of the first covenant, and felt the hum answer faintly through her skin like a pulse finding a new wrist. "The old signatories signed as keeper and as speaker. Offices. Institutions. Things that could betray each other and survive. Maybe that's why it rotted — too much institution, not enough person." She looked up at Ilsa, and then at Nen, and the despair in her chest did not vanish; it compacted, condensed, hardened into something with edges. "Three days. I need ink, I need the fragment translations from my rooms, and I need to stand in front of the guild council and give them one last chance to do the right thing on the record. After that, I sign without them."

"And if the drowned realm rejects a signature with no weight behind it?"

"Then I'll have three days to become weight."

For a long moment Ilsa Maren studied her — the exiled founder, the smuggler of impossible things, the woman who had watched the guild steal a world and had never once, in forty years, seen anyone try to give anything back. Whatever she was looking for, she appeared, reluctantly, to find it.

"Well," she said at last, hauling herself upright with a grunt. "You'll want the good ink, then. I have some. Stolen, naturally."

Nen slipped her small hand into Paloma's salt-white one. Above them, muffled through fathoms of held-back harbor, the deep moon continued its turn.

And somewhere far overhead, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the vault's outer door grinding shut — and the measured, patient footsteps of men who had found the stair.
