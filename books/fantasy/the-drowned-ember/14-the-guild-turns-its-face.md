## 14. The Guild Turns Its Face

The guildhall had not changed, and that was the cruelest thing about it.

Paloma stood in the entry rotunda where she had spent eleven years of her life, and the marble under her boots was the same cool gray it had always been, worn into a shallow valley by generations of keepers' feet. The same oil lamps burned in their iron brackets. The same smell of tallow, old paper, and beeswax polish hung in the air, so familiar it ached. Somewhere above, a clerk was sharpening quills; she could hear the rhythmic whisper of the blade, a sound that had once been as soothing to her as rain.

She was soaked to the waist, salt-crusted, with Nen's small hand gripped in hers and Ilsa's blood — dried now, brown, unremarkable-looking — still under her fingernails from where she had pressed the wound closed on the ferry across the harbor. She looked like what she was: a thief who had stolen a child out of her own guild's ritual chamber.

Two doorwardens flanked the inner arch, and both of them stared at her as if she were a ghost that had come back wrong.

"I need to speak to the Conclave," Paloma said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "All of them. Now."

"The Conclave is in session," said the older warden, a woman named Breck whom Paloma had once helped copy tide-tables for. Breck's eyes went to Nen, then away again, quickly, the way people look at something they are ashamed of. "They know you're here. They've known since your boat touched the quay."

Of course they did. Vane's hunters had failed, but the harbor had eyes, and the guild had always been better at watching than at listening.

"Then they can save us all some time," Paloma said. "Take me up."

* * *

The Conclave chamber sat at the top of the guildhall, beneath the great skylight of green glass through which the thirteenth moon's borrowed light filtered down like something drowned. Nine seats ringed the raised dais. Eight were filled. Vane's seat — Senior Keeper Aldous Vane, disgraced, confined to his quarters since the raid — stood empty, and Paloma felt its absence like a missing tooth. Whatever else Vane was, whatever confessions he had made in the dark of the sunken archive, he had at least told her the truth when no one else would.

At the center seat sat First Keeper Ondrell Marsh, a broad, silvering man with the kind of face built for portraits: strong jaw, patient eyes, the whole architecture of a man who wanted to be remembered well. Paloma had admired him since she was nine years old. He had once knelt down in the ledger hall to show her how to true a warped page, his big hands gentle with the paper, and had said, *A keeper's first duty is to what the record owes the truth.*

She thought about that sentence now, standing on the cold floor below the dais, and understood for the first time that it had always been a lie keepers told themselves.

Nen stood beside her, small and silent, wearing clothes Ilsa had stolen for them both — a smuggler's coat cut down, sleeves rolled four times. The child's eyes moved over the chamber with an expression Paloma had learned to read in the flooded quarter: the flat, watchful calm of someone cataloguing exits.

"Paloma Halloran." Marsh's voice filled the room without effort. "You stand accused of theft, assault upon guild wardens, conspiracy with an exile under sentence of banishment, and the desecration of the ritual chamber. Do you deny it?"

"No," Paloma said. "I did all of it. And I'd do every piece of it again."

A murmur ran the ring of seats. Marsh lifted one hand and it died.

"Then you understand there are consequences—"

"No." She heard her own voice crack against the word, and made herself slow down. This was the part that mattered. Not her defense. Not her name — she didn't have a name anymore; she had traded it to Ilsa Maren in a smoky room above a smuggler's den, and felt it leave her like a tooth pulled, and had never once regretted the price. What mattered was the three days. What mattered was the moon. "First Keeper, I'm not here to answer charges. I'm here because the thirteenth moon completes its turn in three nights, and when it does, the covenant comes due in full. The drowned realm rises. All of it. And the only thing that stops it is a reforged covenant signed by a willing signatory from each realm."

"We have read your report," said Second Keeper Ysolde Crane, dry as dust, from Marsh's left. "Such as it was. A dead exile's scrawl and a child's drawings."

"It's not a report. It's the founding covenant's own terms. I've read them in the original hand. The vault is real. I've stood inside it. The covenant crumbled because this guild let it — because this guild *chose*—" She stopped herself. Chose her words. Cold clarity, she told herself. Not rage. Rage would be dismissed as a child's tantrum, and she had spent eleven years being dismissed as a child. "Because this guild chose, generations ago, to take the goods of the bargain and stop paying for them. Memories. Names. Years. Everything the ledgers record as 'impossible things' — those weren't curiosities. They were payments. We are thieves with very neat handwriting."

Silence. The green light fell across the dais and turned all their faces faintly underwater-colored.

"And your remedy," Marsh said at last, carefully, "is that the guild sign this new covenant. Bind itself — bind *Vaelmoor* — to the drowned realm. As what? Debtors? In perpetuity?"

"As partners," Paloma said. "As signatories. It was never a contract between merchant and customer, First Keeper. It was a marriage. That's what the old tongue says, in the margins, in the words nobody bothered to learn. The drowned realm doesn't want vengeance. It wants to be *remembered*. It wants to be counted. That's all any of this has ever been about. Someone to keep their books."

Marsh exchanged a glance with Crane. It lasted less than a heartbeat, but Paloma had spent her life reading marginalia, reading the things written between the lines, and she saw it pass between them like a coin passed under a table.

"You've given this considerable thought," Marsh said. "More than most clerks twice your age. And I want you to know that the Conclave takes it seriously. But you must see the position we're in. You bring us a covenant whose terms we cannot verify, brokered by a banned smuggler now deceased, attested by a child of the drowned realm who—" he paused, and his voice gentled into something worse than harshness, something kind, "—who is, by your own account, the realm's last-born heir. Its living collateral."

Paloma's stomach went cold. "Don't."

"The guild has obligations," Marsh continued, as if she hadn't spoken. "To Vaelmoor. To forty thousand souls above the waterline. To the continuity of the record itself, which has survived every flood, every war, every dynasty, because we do not act rashly, and we do not act on the word of a nameless girl and a drowned child."

"There is another way," said Third Keeper Hask, from the far side of the ring, and Paloma turned to look at him — a narrow, precise man she had barely ever noticed, which she realized now had been the point of him. "There is a way written into the original terms themselves. If the debt cannot be paid in full, it may be paid in kind. Collateral for collateral." He folded his hands. "The realm sent us an heir. The covenant's own logic accepts an heir returned."

The whisper of the quill-sharpener upstairs seemed suddenly very loud in Paloma's memory. She understood, with a clarity so cold it burned, that this had been decided before she walked in. That the session she had interrupted had not been debating her proposal. It had been waiting for her to deliver the child to them.

"You knew," she said. "You've known for three days what the moon requires, and your plan is to give them Nen."

"We give them *justice*," Hask said. "Proportionate, lawful, sanctioned by the covenant's own terms—"

"The covenant is *salt*!" The words tore out of her before she could hold them. "I watched it crumble! There are no terms anymore — there's nothing lawful left, there's only what you build next, and you're choosing to build it out of a *child*—"

"Order," Marsh said, but softly, and that softness told her everything.

* * *

They debated for two hours.

That was the obscene part, Paloma thought later. How reasonable it all sounded. Keeper after keeper rose to weigh the question with genuine gravity — she could see that it was genuine, that these were not monsters but careful, tired people doing arithmetic with lives — and the arithmetic always came out the same way. One child against a city. Collateral against catastrophe. Precedent, even: had not the senior keepers paid small installments for years, quietly, dutifully, to hold off this very day? Vane had confessed it in the dark with tears in his eyes, horror wearing the mask of duty, and here was the mask's twin sitting on eight faces in green light.

They voted by raising ledgers — the old way, each keeper lifting their bound volume shoulder-high, a gesture meant to say *the record bears witness*. Paloma stood at the floor's center and watched seven ledgers rise.

Crane did not raise hers. Neither did old Master Fenwick, whose hands shook too badly to lift anything, and who wept openly and was gently ignored. Seven of nine. More than enough.

"The Conclave rules," Marsh said, and his portrait-perfect face did not falter, which was somehow the worst betrayal of all, because she could see it cost him. "On the night of the thirteenth turn, the heir of the drowned realm shall be presented at the harbor's throat in accordance with the old forms. The guild shall bear witness. The record shall show that justice was done."

"The record will show that you murdered a child and called it bookkeeping," Paloma said.

Marsh looked at her for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was quiet enough that only she could hear it.

"Girl, I read your audit reports. Two years running. I know exactly how good you are. Which is why I am telling you this as a kindness: go home. Whatever home you have left. Because in three days, when the water rises or it doesn't, this city will need someone to blame, and you have made yourself very easy to find."

* * *

They put her out through the delivery court, past the loading docks where she had once spent summers inventorying crates of impossible things — bottled voices, jars of borrowed summers, a king's shadow folded in oilcloth — and none of the porters would meet her eye. One of them, a boy younger than she was, slipped a wrapped bundle into her arms without a word: bread, dried fish, a flask of clean water. Then he walked away fast, ears red.

Small mercies. Small debts. She was learning how much of the world ran on them.

Nen waited by the gate, and Paloma crouched to the child's level, the bundle against her chest, and found she had no idea what to say. *They voted.* How did you tell a child that a room full of the most careful people in the city had weighed their life against a number and chosen the number?

But Nen was looking up at her with those dark, lantern-lit eyes, and it was Nen who spoke first, in the careful common tongue the child was still learning, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

"They were afraid."

"Yes."

"Afraid people make the ledger balance wrong." A pause. "Below, we have a word for it. When the elders count fear instead of truth." Nen searched for it, frowning. "We call it *drowning on dry land*."

Paloma laughed — a short, broken sound with nothing in it of humor — and pulled the child close, forehead to forehead, salt hair against salt hair. Found family of two, and the whole city's law arrayed against them.

Three days. Seven raised ledgers. A ritual chamber already prepared, she'd bet her life on it — probably had been prepared since the courier drowned in dry air, since the tides first ran backward, maybe since long before.

Which meant there was no persuading them. No appeal, no audit, no honest argument that could reach men who had already balanced their books. The law would not deliver justice. The law had just voted, in green light, with a show of hands, for drowning.

So it would have to be delivered some other way. By thieves. By smugglers' heirs. By a nameless girl with a pen and three days and nothing left to lose but a child she had already decided, somewhere in the flooded dark, that she would drown the whole harbor before surrendering.

"Nen," she said, standing, shouldering the bundle, looking out past the guild walls toward the harbor, where the water lay flat and gleaming and patient as an open page. "How would you feel about breaking into my guildhall one more time?"

Something moved behind the child's eyes — not fear. Recognition. The last-born heir of the drowned realm almost smiled.

"They'll have changed the locks," Nen said.

"They always do," Paloma said. "It's the first thing cowards do. Luckily for us—" she tapped the satchel at her hip, where Ilsa Maren's bloodstained ledger rode against her ribs like a second heart, "—I kept the old keys. Every one the guild ever recorded. Neat handwriting counts for something after all."

Far out in the harbor, deep beneath the flat grey water, something answered: a single point of lantern-light, rising slowly toward the surface, like a held breath finally beginning to rise.
