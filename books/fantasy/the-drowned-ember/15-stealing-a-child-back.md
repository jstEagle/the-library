## 15. Stealing a Child Back

The guildhall had never looked like a fortress to Paloma. For six years it had looked like home: the crooked weathervane shaped like an open ledger, the brass door-handles worn to shine by generations of keepers' palms, the smell of tallow and old vellum that seeped into her clothes so deeply she'd stopped noticing it. Now, crouched in the shadow of the chandlery across Ember Square, she saw it as a besieged thing — lanterns burning in every window, keepers pacing the parapets with harpoons meant for fish and repurposed for people, and above it all the thirteenth moon climbing like a slow white wound through the clouds.

Two days. The moon would complete its turn in two days, and they meant to put Nen on the altar stone before it did.

"They've doubled the watch," Ilsa murmured beside her. The smuggler's face was half-hidden under a hood the color of wet slate, but her eyes caught the lantern-light and threw it back, sharp as coins. "Four at the door, two on the roof, and Vane's got his best people inside. He knows you'll come."

"Then he's wasted good people on a guess."

"He didn't guess." Ilsa's voice was flat. "He counted. You're a keeper, girl. Keepers always come back for their ledgers." A pause. "Or their children."

Paloma's jaw tightened. *Nen is not mine.* But the thought rang false even in the privacy of her own skull. Somewhere between the flooded lower quarters and the sunken archive, between trading away her name and watching Ilsa bleed, the child had become hers in every way that mattered — the way a debt becomes yours when you sign it.

"I can't do this quietly," she said. "I know the guildhall's bones. Every creak of them. But there are thirty keepers in there, and some of them trained me."

Ilsa smiled, and it was not a kind smile. It was the smile she'd worn in the smuggler's den, over cards, over knives, over the price of impossible things. "Then don't be quiet. Be honest. It frightens them more."

* * *

They went in through the coal chute — the one indignity Paloma had complained about every delivery day of her apprenticeship, and which now delivered her into the guild's cellar like a rebuke. She dropped first, boots hitting ash-soft floor, Ilsa landing behind her with a grunt the older woman tried and failed to smother. In the lantern-light of the coal store, Paloma saw the stain blooming through Ilsa's side where the hunters' blade had found her in the archive tunnels, hastily bound with salt-soaked linen that was doing less good than Ilsa pretended.

"You should be lying down," Paloma whispered.

"I've been lying down my whole life waiting for something worth getting up for." Ilsa rolled her shoulder and grimaced. "The ritual chamber. Which way?"

"Up. Grand stair, then the east gallery, then the Vault Room." Paloma closed her eyes and let the guildhall assemble itself in her memory, floor by floor, keeper by keeper. "There'll be a ward-sign at the gallery door. Keeper's knock, three-and-two. They'll have changed it."

"They'll have changed it to what?"

"To whatever Vane thinks I'd never guess." She almost laughed. "Which means he'll use the old one. He's sentimental about protocol. He made me memorize the founding knocks my first week and told me tradition was the only armor a ledger has."

Ilsa looked at her for a long moment. "You're going to make a terrible criminal and a magnificent legend. Come on."

The cellar stairs were guarded by exactly one person: Brannic Odo, third-rank keeper, a broad soft man who had once taught Paloma to bind folios without cracking the spine. He sat on an upturned crate eating bread and cheese with the resigned air of someone assigned the post where nothing ever happened.

Nothing happened until Paloma stepped out of the dark and said, "You're supposed to chew with your mouth closed, Brannic. Rule eleven of the Codex of Conduct."

He froze mid-bite. "Paloma?" His eyes went to the hooded figure behind her, and understanding arrived like cold water. "Oh. Oh no. They said you might— Paloma, you can't. There's a vote posted. Your name's struck. You're not—" He fumbled for the words, and settled on the cruelest true one. "You're not guild anymore."

"I know," she said. "I sold it. And I'd sell it again tomorrow, because they've got a nine-year-old chained to an altar upstairs and they're calling it law." She kept her voice low and steady, though her heart was hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out. "Move, Brannic. Or don't move, and I go through you. Either way I'm going up."

Brannic looked at her — at the stranger wearing the face of the girl who'd inked his quarterly reports — and did the arithmetic keepers are trained to do: assets, liabilities, exposure. Then he set down his bread, stood aside, and said, "Third landing's got a tripwire. Left side. Vane had the artificers rig it yesterday." His voice cracked. "Tell the child the guild's sorry. Someone should say it."

Paloma squeezed his arm as she passed. It was the last kind thing either of them would manage for hours.

* * *

The grand stair rose through the heart of the building, and halfway up, the sounds reached them: chanting. Low, rhythmic, many voices braided into one, the cadence of the Litany of Accounts — the guild's oldest prayer, the one recited at initiations and funerals. Hearing it now, aimed upward toward the ritual chamber like a drawn bow, turned Paloma's stomach. They weren't praying. They were *balancing*. Preparing the books for an entry written in a child.

Ilsa heard it too, and her hand found Paloma's wrist. "That changes things," she breathed. "A full litany means every senior keeper's in that room. Means the halls between here and there are thin."

"Thin is fine. Empty would be better."

"The world doesn't give empty, girl. It gives thin, and you run through it fast."

They ran. Through the west corridor where portraits of dead keepers watched with oil-painted disapproval; past the scriptorium where Paloma had spent ten thousand hours bent over columns of figures; along the gallery with its high windows full of thirteenth-moon light, silver and wrong, falling across the floorboards in bars like the ribs of some enormous drowned thing. At the gallery door Paloma gave the three-and-two knock — the founding knock, the sentimental one — and the ward-sign flared green instead of red, and she felt a wild, bitter triumph that lasted exactly until the door opened.

Keeper Marenne Sol stood on the other side with a loaded crossbow.

Of everyone. Of course it was Marenne — the woman who had supervised Paloma's first audit, who kept candied ginger in her desk drawer for nervous apprentices, who had once defended Paloma to Vane himself with the words *"she counts truer than any of us."* The crossbow didn't waver. Marenne's hands had always been steady. That was the whole problem with steady hands: they held anything you put in them.

"Don't," Marenne said. Her voice was careful, the way voices get when they're carrying something heavy across ice. "Don't make me report you fighting. Walk out. Whatever you think is happening in that room—"

"A child is going to die in there, Marenne."

"A *debt* is going to be paid." But her eyes flickered, just once, toward the sound of the litany, and Paloma saw the doubt there, buried alive but breathing.

"Whose debt?" Paloma took one step closer, close enough that the bolt's point nearly touched her sternum. She thought of chapter and verse, of Aldous Vane's confession in the archive tunnels — *we have been feeding it small payments for years, including children* — and she spent the truth like a coin she could never get back. "Vane's been paying it for twenty years. Quietly. Off the books. Children, Marenne — sent down the harbor throat one at a time while the rest of us balanced columns and called ourselves honest. This isn't settling the covenant. This is feeding the thing we stole from so it stays quiet a little longer. You're not a keeper tonight. You're a cook."

The crossbow dipped. One inch. Two.

Ilsa moved like smoke — a hook of the ankle, a palm strike to the wrist — and the crossbow clattered to the boards, and Marenne cried out more in shock than pain, and then Ilsa's knife was at her collar and Paloma was shouting "*No!*" and catching Ilsa's arm.

"She hesitated," Ilsa snarled. "Hesitation gets children killed."

"Not tonight it doesn't." Paloma knelt, wrenched the crossbow away, and snapped the string over her knee. Then she looked up at Marenne, kneeling, wide-eyed, unharmed. "Gallery door. Lock it behind us from the outside if you can bear it. If you can't — sit in the scriptorium and don't look at anything until morning. That's the smallest honest thing left to you."

She didn't wait to see which Marenne chose. Behind her, she heard the woman's breath hitch once, and then the gallery door boomed shut, and the lock turned, and Paloma allowed herself one heartbeat of hope before the alarms began.

* * *

Vane had rigged the hall, not the doors. Bells — small, silver, hung in the corridors like the guild's own version of the drowned realm's lanterns — began ringing everywhere at once, a shrill metallic rain. And the litany below broke off mid-verse.

"They know," Ilsa gasped. They were running again, up the east service stair, Ilsa's breath coming shorter and shorter, her hand pressed hard against the soaked binding at her side. "How long—"

"Thirty seconds to the chamber. We hold the door until we're through, and then we bar it and take the roof stairs down to the harbor wall. Can you climb?"

"I can climb," Ilsa said, in the voice of a woman deciding it was true.

The ritual chamber had been, in Paloma's apprentice years, the Receiving Room — a place of tall windows and long tables where foreign envoys were offered wine and flattery. The guild had remade it in three days. The tables were gone. In their place stood the old altar stone hauled up from the crypt, black and salt-scarred, ringed with chalk lines and guttering candles. Chains — *chains*, forged iron, the guild's treasury melted down for them — ran from Nen's wrists and ankles to iron rings set deep in the stone.

Nen was standing. That was the first thing Paloma saw, and it undid her worse than tears or terror would have: the child stood straight, chin up, mud-brown hair loose, watching the assembled keepers with the ancient, unblinking patience of the tide itself. Nine years old and bearing it like an heir, because that was what the child was. Sent as collateral. Raised for this the way Paloma had been raised for ledgers.

Around the altar stood the senior circle, twelve robed figures, Vane at their head, the Litany of Accounts still hanging unfinished in the air. Every face turned toward the door as Paloma and Ilsa burst through it.

For one suspended moment, nobody moved. Paloma knew half these people. Had drunk tea with them. Had corrected their transcriptions, gently, in margins, because she couldn't help herself.

"Paloma." Vane's voice carried the exhausted calm of a man who had expected this since the moon first turned backward. "You're nameless. You have no standing here. What happens in this room is guild business."

"No." She walked forward, and to her own surprise, the circle parted — not from fear, but from the strange paralysis of people confronting a rule that didn't exist yet. "It's *ledger* business. And I'm the only living ledger left in this building." She pointed at the altar. "Cut the child down."

"Or what?" Keeper Drosselmeier, old, dry, his voice like paper tearing. "You'll write us a strongly worded entry?"

"Or I tell the truth about what's under this floor." She let it out, all of it, loud enough for the youngest keepers pressed against the far wall to hear. "The covenant wasn't a contract, it was a marriage. Our founders didn't pay for miracles — they traded vows with the realm beneath the harbor, and then they razored those vows out of the book and kept everything. The memories. The names. The years. Everything in our vaults is stolen goods. And for twenty years this council has paid the interest in *children*, off the books, in secret, rather than admit the theft. Ask him." She turned to Vane. "Ask him what happened to the ferryman's daughter in the year of the backward tide. Ask him what happened to the lamplighter's twins."

Silence. The candle flames leaned sideways, all together, as if the room had inhaled.

Vane's face didn't change. But his hand moved — a small gesture, two fingers flicking downward — and four keepers near the windows drew steel.

"*Now,*" Ilsa barked, and threw herself forward.

What followed, Paloma would remember forever in fragments, the way disasters file themselves: the crash of the candlestand going over; Ilsa's knife flashing, not to kill but to maim, opening Keeper Hask's sword-arm from elbow to wrist with a stroke so precise it was almost calligraphy; Vane shouting "Take them *alive*, she's worth more than the cargo"; Paloma reaching the altar and hauling at the chains, her fingers slick, finding the padlocks and smashing them against the stone's edge until the iron gave; Nen's arms around her neck, light as a bird, hot as a coal; the door bursting inward with reinforcements from below; and Ilsa turning to face all of it alone, planted between the keepers and the two of them like a breakwater, bleeding freely now, laughing —

"Go, girl! Roof stairs, *go!*"

"We go together!"

"Together means *after!* Move!"

And Paloma — who had spent her whole life obeying the guild, then defying the guild, and had promised herself she'd never again obey anyone — obeyed. Because Ilsa's word had the weight of a signed entry, final and binding, and because there was no time to argue. She ran for the roof stairs with Nen clinging to her back, and the last thing she saw over her shoulder was Ilsa Maren, exile and thief and founder, holding the doorway with a knife in each hand and blood sheeting down her coat, keeping the line the way keepers keep a ledger: absolutely, and at any cost.

* * *

The roof was slick with sea-fog. Below, Ember Square seethed with lanterns and shouted orders, and beyond it the harbor lay black and waiting, its surface trembling with the thirteenth moon's reflection — except the reflection was wrong, Paloma realized, the moon in the water sitting a finger's width higher than the moon in the sky, as if the drowned realm were already rising to meet it.

"There." She pointed to the drainage spout that ran down the seaward wall to the old customs quay — a route she'd climbed on dares as an apprentice, when she'd still believed the guild was the most dangerous thing in Vaelmoor. "Can you hold on to me?"

Nen answered by wrapping arms and legs around her like a burr. The child hadn't spoken since the chamber. Didn't need to.

Paloma went over the edge.

The descent was forty feet of wet stone and old terror. Halfway down, a keeper's face appeared above them — Hask, cradling his ruined arm, staring down — and then a crossbow bolt sparked off the wall a hand's width from Paloma's ear, and then another, and then a shape swung out over the parapet after them, and Paloma's arms burned and her grip sang with pain and she thought, absurdly, *rule eleven, chew with your mouth closed,* because her mind fled to nonsense when death leaned close—

The shape following them stopped. Jerked. Looked down at the knife buried in its shoulder, and lost its hold, and crashed onto the customs quay below with a sound Paloma would hear in her sleep for years.

Ilsa stood on the parapet above, swaying, one knife gone, using the other to point down at them.

"*Quay arches,*" she rasped, her voice shredded. "Smugglers' door. Tide gate code's your mother's birthday, girl — yes, I read your file, sue me. Get the child to Ilsa's boat. Go *now.*"

"You're coming too." Paloma's voice cracked on it. "Both of us or neither. That's the deal. That was always the deal."

Something moved across Ilsa's face — surprise, then tenderness, then a decision arriving like a ship making landfall. She swung her leg over the parapet to follow.

And the keepers came over the roof behind her.

Later, Paloma could never say how many. Three, perhaps four, pouring over the parapet in a wave of steel and lantern-light, and Ilsa turned to meet them, and the fight that followed was short and terrible and mostly hidden from view — Paloma clinging to the wall with a child on her back, hearing it in fragments: the ring of blades, a shout, a body striking stone somewhere above and to the left, Ilsa's laugh, ragged and triumphant, and then — cutting through everything, quiet as a closing book — Ilsa's voice, pitched to carry down:

"Girl. Look at me."

Paloma looked up.

Ilsa stood at the parapet's edge, alone among fallen shapes, one hand pressed to her side where the linen had come away entirely and the wound beneath was black and vast. She was smiling. It was the card-table smile, the price-of-impossible-things smile, and it did not waver even as her knees did.

"Debts need a debtor," she called down, and her voice was already thinning, fraying at the edges like rope over a pulley. "Make yourself worth owing. Boat. *Go.* Finish the entry."

"Ilsa—"

"*GO.*"

Paloma went. There was no other obedience left in the world that mattered. She dropped the last ten feet, rolled, gathered Nen up, and ran for the smugglers' door beneath the quay arches, giving the tide-gate code with shaking hands — her mother's birthday, of all the cursed ironies — and the old door groaned open onto black water and the low silhouette of Ilsa's boat riding at its hidden mooring.

Behind her, above, the sounds from the parapet had stopped.

She stood in the doorway with Nen's hand locked in hers, and the harbor wind came up cold off the water, carrying the smell of brine and lantern-oil, and somewhere beneath the black surface of the harbor, lights were beginning to rise — dozens of them, then hundreds, the drowned realm's lanterns ascending through the deep like a city deciding to answer a summons.

The thirteenth moon cleared the clouds at last, whole and white and final.

"One turn left," Nen whispered, in common tongue, in Paloma's own language, the first words the child had spoken since the chains. Small fingers tightened around hers. "We have to carry her with us. Down. They'll let her in, if we ask. They let everyone in, in the end."

Paloma looked back once at the guildhall — at the parapet she could no longer see, at the woman she could not leave and could not reach — and stepped into the boat.

"Hold on to me," she said, and pushed off from the shore of everything she'd known, into the rising dark.
