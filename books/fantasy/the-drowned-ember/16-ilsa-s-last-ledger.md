## 16. Ilsa's Last Ledger

The wound was a hot coal laid low across her belly, and Ilsa Maren had been smuggling long enough to know what that meant. She had watched men die of gut wounds in the drowned quarter's alleys — the way they went quiet first, saving their breath for the pain, the way their eyes went from door to door as if measuring which one death would come through.

She was doing it herself now, she realized. Measuring doors.

There were no doors here. Only the boathouse at the edge of Sallow Reach, its roof holed by three decades of storms, moonlight and rain coming through together in silver threads. Nen had dragged a tarp over the gap where water could pool; the child worked with the grim competence of someone who had watched drowning from the other side. Paloma knelt beside Ilsa with her hands pressed against the wound, and her hands were shaking, and Ilsa thought: *she presses like a keeper. Like someone trying to hold a page flat while the ink dries.*

"Stop that," Ilsa said.

Paloma didn't stop. "We can bind it. There's a tincture — smugglers use it, you told me once, for—"

"For cuts. This isn't a cut." Ilsa reached up and caught the girl's wrist. Her own hand surprised her — how light it had gotten already, how much of her seemed to have leaked out through the hole the guildhunter's blade had left. "You're wasting pressure on a dead ledger, girl."

"Don't say that."

"I've said worse to better people." But Paloma's face — gods, that face. Stubborn as a barnacle. Ilsa had spent forty years making sure no one looked at her like that, and now she was going to die being looked at like that anyway. The world had a sense of humor. It just kept it very well hidden.

Nen came and sat on her other side, cross-legged, small hands folded. The child's skin still held a faint shimmer of harbor-light, though it dimmed a little more each hour they stayed above the waterline. Homesickness, or dying by inches. Ilsa couldn't tell, and hadn't asked. There was only so much guilt a woman could carry in one night, and hers was already stacked to the gunwales.

*You're bleeding out in a leaking boathouse because you went back for them,* she told herself. *Forty years clean of the guild, forty years of saying Maren owes no one nothing, and one girl with neat handwriting walks into your shop and inside three days you're taking a blade meant for her.*

It wasn't even a hard blade to take. That was the humiliating part. When the hunter came around the corner of the ritual chamber stair — Vane's man, quick and professional, knife already committed toward Nen — Ilsa hadn't thought at all. Her body had moved the way it moved when cargo shifted on a deck: without consultation. And then the knife was in her instead of the child, and the hunter was dead under Paloma's ledger-weight (the girl had hit him with an actual ledger, Ilsa would laugh about that if laughing didn't hurt so much), and here they all were.

"You knew this would happen," Paloma said quietly. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse. It was recognition.

"I knew it might." Ilsa shifted against the hull plank and paid for it with a white bloom behind her eyes. "Everything might happen. That's what makes it trading and not theft. You price the risk."

"What did it cost? What did you trade to come back to the guildhall tonight?"

"Nothing you need to enter anywhere."

"Ilsa."

"*Keeper.*" The old name came out rougher than she intended. Paloma flinched, and Ilsa almost apologized, and didn't. "No. You gave that up fair and square. Best bargain anyone ever made me. Don't go wanting it back now that it's inconvenient."

Paloma's jaw set. Rain ticked through the holes in the roof. Somewhere out on the black water, a bell buoy clanged twice and stopped — the wrong rhythm, the backward rhythm, the tide pulling the sound the wrong way. Three days until the thirteenth moon finished turning. Two now, maybe, if dawn counted for anything, and dawn never counted for anything anymore.

"Nen," Ilsa said.

The child looked up. Those eyes — lantern-colored, deep-water patient.

"You told the girl downstairs you'd found something in your grandfather's things. Before you were sent up. Tell it again. All of it. I want to hear it whole."

Nen glanced at Paloma, who nodded. So the child spoke — that soft, strange accent, vowels shaped by pressure and current — and told it again: how the heir-children of the drowned court were raised on two stories, one of the marriage between the realms, one of the betrayal; how the eldest story-keeper below had gone silent a generation ago, and with her silence the old terms had begun to fray; how Nen had been sent up not as vengeance but as *collateral*, living proof that the account was still open, still waiting, still owed. How Nen's grandfather had kept a fragment of the original covenant — a strip of vellum no bigger than a hand — hidden in a hollow oar, and passed it to the child with instructions written in the pre-kingdom tongue:

*When the moon turns the thirteenth time, this must be signed by willing hands on both sides. Not the guild's hands. They are bought. Willing hands. Any willing hands.*

Isla listened with her eyes closed, cataloguing. That was the whole of smuggling, really — listening, and knowing what a thing was worth before anyone else did.

"And you brought it," she said.

Nen drew the strip of vellum from inside the oilskin wrap at their throat, where it had lived since the flooded quarters, and laid it on Ilsa's blanket without letting go of one corner, the way children hold things they have been told matter more than their lives.

The vellum was salt-stiff and gray as fish skin. But the writing on it was fresh — or rather, it renewed itself, ink moving faintly beneath the surface like eels under ice. Half the words were the pre-kingdom tongue. The other half were older human script, the founding hand, the same hand Ilsa had seen once upon a time in a vault she should never have been shown.

She had been young then. Nineteen, newly sworn, clever enough to be dangerous. Aldous Vane — not senior then, just another ambitious clerk — had taken her down to see the covenant on a pretext, and she had read the terms through the vault glass, and she had understood in about four minutes what the guild had actually done. Not lost the bargain. *Robbed* it. Taken the payments — memories, names, years, the impossible things the ledgers recorded so neatly — and then sealed the vault and called the debt settled because no one below could walk up and demand receipt.

She had gone to the founder's council with it. She had been nineteen, and certain, and she had stood in front of nine keepers with her evidence in order and her heart hammering like a courier's fist on a door.

They had thanked her. Promoted her. Given her a wing of the archive to manage, and quietly reassigned everyone who might have corroborated her, and waited for her ambition to grow comfortable. It took eleven years. Then they razored the pages, and when she objected again — older, louder, less forgivable — they exiled her so smoothly she nearly believed she'd chosen it herself.

Forty years she'd carried that. Forty years telling herself the ledger was balanced: the guild stole, she stole back, everyone was crooked, nobody owed. A comfortable arithmetic. It had taken a mud-soaked child and a nameless clerk to unbalance it again.

And now she was lying in a boat-house with her life running out of her like bilge water, and the strip of vellum was on her chest, and there was exactly one thing left worth buying.

"Paloma," she said. "Come here. Closer. I'm not going to shout it."

Paloma leaned in. Up close, Ilsa could see the exhaustion ground into her — the salt-stiff hair, the split lip from the raid, the bruise blooming along her jaw where a guildhunter's baton had clipped her. Seventeen hours since they'd pulled Nen from the ritual chamber. Seventeen hours since Ilsa had stopped the knife. She looked like a woman who had run past the end of her strength and kept going on pure grievance.

Good. Grievance was fuel. Ilsa had run on it for forty years.

"The vault under the harbor is cracked," Ilsa said. "Has been for months — that's why the tides run backward, why the courier drowned in dry air. The covenant's dying, and a dying covenant doesn't just expire. It *inverts*. Every unpaid debt comes due at once, with interest compounded over three hundred years. That's the flood you saw rising. That's what the thirteenth moon is for. The realm below isn't attacking Vaelmoor, girl. It's foreclosing."

"I know. I read the marginalia. The interest clauses—"

"No, you don't know, because you haven't done the last calculation." Ilsa gripped her arm, harder than she meant to. "A broken covenant needs reforging. A reforged covenant needs signatures. One from each side. The drowned will sign — they've been begging to sign for three centuries. But the human half—" She coughed, and tasted copper, and swallowed it like a professional. "The human half has to be signed by someone with standing. Someone the old magic will recognize as having authority to bind. The guild could do it. The guild *was* built to do it. And the guild voted tonight to drown a child instead."

"They voted to sacrifice Nen. On the moon-turn."

"Aye. Blood payment. The coward's signature — write it in someone else's blood and call it settlement." Ilsa's mouth twisted. "It won't work, mind. The old terms won't take stolen blood. It'll just buy them a few years of quiet while the rot goes deeper. But they'll do it anyway, because cowards always pay the installment plan."

"So we stop them."

"We already stopped them. Tonight. For about a day." Ilsa laughed, which cost her, and paid anyway. "Vane will rally them. He's got the votes, the seals, the self-righteousness. You can't fight the guild with a smuggler and a child, girl. Believe me. I've tried it with considerably more than that."

"Then what?"

Ilsa looked at the vellum. The ink moved beneath its surface, patient as tide.

Then she looked at her own hands. Smuggler's hands. Knife-scarred, rope-burned, honest in the way that thieves are honest — they took only what was agreed. Forty years of keeping her word precisely because she'd watched her guild break theirs.

There was a version of the smart move here. The smart move was to tell the girl to run for the borderlands, take the child somewhere the flood wouldn't reach, let Vaelmoor learn what its grandfathers had purchased. The smart move was to die warm and dry-ish with her accounts closed and her name unattached to any final disaster.

Ilsa Maren had never once made the smart move when there was a true one available.

"Give me your knife," she said.

Paloma froze. "Ilsa—"

"Not for anything you're thinking. Give it here."

Paloma hesitated — one heartbeat, two — then drew the short blade from her belt and placed it in Ilsa's palm. The handle was slick with rain. Ilsa turned it, found the spot on her forearm above the wound where the bleeding was slowest, and set the edge against her own skin.

"Watch," she said. "Both of you. Watch like it's the last page of a book, because it is."

She cut. A line across the forearm, shallow but wide, and her blood came welling up dark in the moonlight — more of it than there should have been, as if the wound in her belly had opened some deeper vein of her. She didn't flinch. She'd been cut worse by cheaper people.

Then she pressed her palm flat against the strip of vellum, over the blank space where the human signature belonged, and spoke the old words.

She hadn't spoken them in forty years. She'd sworn she never would. But the founding script was carved into her memory deeper than any oath of exile, because she'd read it through the vault glass at nineteen and understood it was the most beautiful fraud ever inked — and a fraud, once you understand it, can be rewritten honestly.

*"By blood freely given,"* she said, in the founding tongue, *"and debts freely owned: I, who was keeper and am cast out, who was named and am nameless, sign for the human side — not for the guild, which broke faith, but for the debt itself, which remains. Let this stand until a worthier hand takes up the pen. Let this be the bridge until the bridge is built. I sign as payment. I sign as pledge. I sign."*

The blood sank into the vellum. The ink beneath the surface stirred, tested the new signature like a tongue testing a wound — and accepted it. The strip glowed faintly, lantern-colored, and Nen made a small sound, and the bell buoy out on the water rang *forward* for the first time in weeks.

Then the glow steadied, and dimmed, and Ilsa felt it — distinctly, undeniably — the way you feel a hook set in your flesh. Something on the other end of that signature had taken hold. Something below had felt her blood land on the page and said, in the wordless way of ancient bargains: *received.*

"There," she breathed. "That's the human half. Provisional. Mine." She let the vellum rest on her chest, glowing softly over her heart like a lamp in a window. "It buys the realms a little time. Days, not years. A provisional signature holds the account open — it doesn't settle it. For that..."

She stopped. Looked at Paloma.

Paloma had gone very still. The kind of still that comes right before understanding arrives and rearranges everything in the room.

"Ilsa. What does a provisional signature require to become permanent?"

"It requires a full signatory. Someone with power of their own — not borrowed authority, not guild office, not stolen names. The old magic wants a person who can *bind themselves*, willingly, body and ledger both." Ilsa smiled, and it hurt her face, and she did it anyway. "Which is the problem, isn't it. Everyone in Vaelmoor with standing is either guild — poisoned, compromised, cowardly — or bound to some other law. Except."

"Don't," Paloma whispered.

"Except a woman who sold her guild-name to me. Fair trade, witnessed, entered in my books." Ilsa lifted her head off the plank, an inch, two, her whole remaining strength poured into it. "You think I took your name for passage, girl? I've ferried orphans across the Sallow for bread crusts. I took your name because the moment I saw you argue terms with a dying smuggler over a bowl of stew, I knew what you were. You're not a keeper. Keepers *record* debts. You—" her breath caught, held, released— "you *become* them."

"Ilsa, you're dying, you're not thinking—"

"I have never thought more clearly in my life. Dying concentrates the ledger wonderfully." She caught Paloma's wrist again. This time the girl didn't pull away. "Listen to me. Listen, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to be extremely annoying about it forever, wherever dead smugglers go."

The rain thickened. Through the holes in the roof it came down harder, drumming on the tarp Nen had rigged, and the child moved closer, kneeling on Ilsa's other side, small hands hovering over the glowing vellum as if warming them at a fire.

"Debts need a debtor," Ilsa said. "That's the whole secret of the covenant, the thing the guild never understood in three hundred years of thieving. They thought the impossible things were *goods*. Memories, names, years — inventory to be warehoused and skimmed. Fools. They were *payments*. Proof that the two realms touched. Proof that what one side gives, the other side owes, and what is owed makes kin. The guild broke the marriage because they couldn't stand owing anything. And a world that refuses to owe—" her voice frayed, mended— "a world that refuses to owe becomes a world that only takes. Look at Vaelmoor. Look at it. Three hundred years of taking."

Paloma's eyes were wet. She didn't wipe them. Keeper discipline, even now.

"You can't fix that with a scribe's hand," Ilsa went on. "You can't fix it with Vane's seals or the council's votes. It fixes one way only. Somebody stands between the realms and says: *I will carry the account. Put it all in my name. I will remember what you forget, and speak for what cannot speak, and hold the balance with my own weight.* That's what a living ledger is. Not a book. A person. The covenant doesn't need a new contract, Paloma. It needs a new heart."

"And you want it to be mine."

"I want it to be *offered*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." Ilsa's grip tightened. "I'd never order a girl into the harbor's throat. But I'll tell you the truth, which is rarer and worth more: there's no one else. The guild won't. Vane can't. I'm done in about an hour. And Nen—" she looked down at the child, whose lantern eyes were fixed on her with terrible patience— "Nen is collateral, not signatory. The realm below sent their last-born to be *held*, not to bind themselves. If it were allowed, believe me, they'd have signed with the child's blood already and spared us all this weather."

Nen said something soft in the pre-kingdom tongue. Then, carefully, in common: "*She is right. My grandmother tried. The terms refuse a hostage. They wait for a volunteer.*"

"They wait for a volunteer," Ilsa repeated. "Three hundred years, they've waited. And tonight, in a leaking boathouse, there's finally a volunteer-shaped hole in the world and exactly one person who fits it."

Silence, except the rain and the distant wrong-then-right rhythm of the bells.

Paloma looked down at her own hands. Ilsa watched her do it — watched her turn them over, palms up, the way a person checks whether they're holding anything. As if she might find her old life still tucked in them somewhere: the guildhall, the archive smells, the senior keepers calling her *child* in that fond-dismissive tone, the small warm cage she'd loved and chafed in all at once. She had traded all of it away for a boat ride and a bitter lesson about the price of justice. And now the woman who'd taught her the lesson was asking her to pay the second installment.

"You feel it leaving," Ilsa said gently. "The guild-name. I told you it would. Like a tooth pulled."

"It's worse than that." Paloma's voice was steady. Barely. "It's like — the shelf where the tooth was. The empty place. I keep putting my tongue against it."

"Aye. That's the shape of it." Ilsa smiled. "Now listen close, because here is the part nobody tells you. That empty place? It's not a loss. It's *room*. A name is a wall, girl. It tells you where you end. Yours is gone — which means there is no place you end. No guild boundary, no ledger margin, no line where the human world stops and the drowned one starts. You're the only person in either realm with no walls left." Her breath whistled. "That's not damage. That's a doorway."

Paloma bowed her head. For a long moment she said nothing, and Ilsa let her have the silence, because some decisions must be made in silence or not at all.

When Paloma looked up, her eyes were dry.

"If I do this," she said. "If I sign. What happens to me? Truly. Not the poetry. The terms."

"Truly?" Ilsa considered lying, decided the girl would catch it, and honored her with truth. "You become the account itself. Both tongues live in you. Every debt between the realms passes through your keeping — every memory traded, every year lent, every impossible thing given or returned. You'll feel the balance like a second heartbeat. You'll know when either side cheats, because you'll ache where they cheat. And you can't put it down. Not ever. Not in death, maybe. A living ledger is living until the realms are one again, and the realms have never been one, and may never be." She paused. "It's the loneliest post ever created. And the most necessary. And there's no pension."

Despite everything, Paloma laughed — a wet, cracked sound, but real.

"And Nen?" Paloma asked.

"That's yours to negotiate. Not mine, not the elders'. Yours. That's the whole point of signing free — you make your own terms." Ilsa's voice was going quieter now. She could feel the edges of things going soft. Not yet. *Not yet.* There was one entry left. "But I'll tell you what I see, since I'm the only merchant here with clear books. I see a child who was sent up as a hostage and chose, somewhere in the flooded quarters, to be your family instead. Realms don't bind that. Ledgers don't record it. It's the one entry no covenant has ever managed, and you'll be carrying the pen."

Paloma's hand covered Ilsa's on the vellum.

"Then show me how to sign," she said.

Ilsa exhaled — a long, careful breath, the kind you take before lifting something too heavy. She guided the girl's hand to the strip of vellum, to the space beside her own bloody mark. But she didn't let her press yet.

"Not here. Not now." She tapped the parchment once, weakly. "This holds the account open. The real signing happens below — at the covenant's root, in the harbor's throat, under the thirteenth moon. The old magic won't take a signature made in fear or hurry. It wants ceremony. It wants witnesses. It wants—" a ghost of her old smuggler's grin— "it wants a proper closing, with all parties present and the goods on the table. We robbed these people of a wedding three hundred years ago. The least we can do is give them a real one."

"Below." Paloma's throat moved. "Through the drowning streets. With Vane hunting us and the flood itself after us."

"Now you're pricing the risk properly." Ilsa's vision was graying at the edges. She fought it down. There was time. There was *always* time, if you were stubborn enough to spend it well. "One more thing. My kit. Green oilcloth, bottom of the boat. Open it."

Nen fetched it. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen: a pen. Old, dark, iron-nibbed, its barrel carved with the founding script — the same hand as the vellum. Ilsa had bought it thirty years ago from a wreck-diver who didn't know what he'd pulled from the drowned quarter, and had never once dared use it, because a pen like that only writes true for a signatory, and she had never been one.

Until tonight.

"It's yours," she said. "It's been waiting for you longer than you've been alive. Everything waits for you, girl. That's what I finally understand. The guild waited. The realm below waited. Even I waited — forty years pretending I was done with them, and the moment you walked in my door I started packing for a war." Her breath hitched. "Take the pen. Take the vellum. Take the child. Go down when the moon calls, and sign like the whole world is watching, because it is — both of it."

"Ilsa—"

"Say it back to me. What I told you. Say it back so I know it took root."

Paloma's jaw trembled once and locked. She leaned close, so the words landed on Ilsa's skin like warmth.

"Debts need a debtor," she said. "Make yourself worth owing."

"Aye." Ilsa's eyes were burning now, and she let them. "And you will be. I've read a thousand faces across a thousand bargains, and I'm telling you the truth as my last entry: you're worth owing already. You were worth owing the day you audited those crumbling ledgers and *cared* about the debts in them. All that's left is for the worlds to catch up to what I know."

Her strength went out of her all at once then, like a tide withdrawing. She sagged back against the plank. The vellum rose and fell on her chest with each shallowing breath, glowing faintly, a lamp in a window over dark water.

Nen curled against her side, small and warm. Paloma held her hand.

Outside, far off across the black harbor, the water began to glow — thousands of points of light rising through the depths, lantern after lantern after lantern, ascending from the drowned realm toward the surface like a city deciding, after three hundred years, to come up for air.

Ilsa watched them through the holes in the roof, and smiled.

"They're coming to witness," she murmured. "Get ready, keeper. The closing's begun."

* * *

She lasted until the first lanterns broke the surface.

They rose just beyond the boathouse — spheres of soft gold drifting up out of the harbor like seeds returning to the sky, dozens of them, then hundreds, and where they gathered the water went calm and glassy, reflecting a sky that had no right to be that clear. In the light of them, Ilsa could suddenly see the whole harbor plain: the drowned quarter's rooftops just beneath the surface, streets laid out in silver, windows glowing from below. The old city. The married city. Still there. Always there.

"They're beautiful," Nen whispered, in common, watching the lanterns with tears cutting clean lines through the salt on their cheeks.

"They're *invoices*," Ilsa said, and laughed until it hurt, which was fine, because everything hurt now and pain had become simply the weather of her. "Every one of those lights is a payment somebody below kept safe for three hundred years. Waiting for us to honor our side." Her hand found Paloma's and squeezed, once, with the last strength she had. "Don't waste it. Whatever it costs. Don't waste—"

The sentence didn't end. It just ran out of her, the way the tide runs out, unhurried, certain of its return elsewhere.

Paloma held her through it. Held her hand the way you hold a ledger open — firmly, respectfully, unwilling to let the page turn until every word was read. Nen sang something low in the pre-kingdom tongue, a few notes, the kind of song sung for lanterns going out and lanterns being lit.

Afterward, Paloma closed Ilsa's eyes with two fingers, keeper-fashion, the gesture for a finished entry. Then she sat very still for a moment, the iron-nibbed pen in one hand and the glowing vellum in the other, a dead smuggler's whole estate in her lap.

Then she stood, and looked out at the lantern-lit harbor, and at the distant spire of the guildhall where men were preparing to murder a child on the turn of a moon, and her face did something Ilsa would have paid good money to see.

It became the face of a signatory.

"We need a boat," Paloma said to Nen. "And we need it before the moon finishes turning."

Out on the water, as if in answer, every lantern in the harbor began to drift — slowly, deliberately, in one great converging current — toward the boathouse door.

They were coming to collect her. All of them. The debt was calling in its debtor.

And Paloma Halloran — nameless, unwalled, holding a dead woman's pen — walked out through the doorway of the boathouse to meet the tide.
