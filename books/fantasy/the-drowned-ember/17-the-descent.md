## 17. The Descent

The moon began to rise before they reached the waterline.

Paloma felt it first in her teeth — a pressure, a hum, like biting down on a struck bell. Then the light came, wrong-colored, a bruised silver that did not belong to any sky she had ever trusted. It climbed over Vaelmoor's eastern towers and laid itself across the flooded streets below like a hand smoothing a bedsheet, and everywhere it touched, the water rose to meet it.

"Paloma." Nen's voice was small and very steady. The child had stopped crying somewhere on the stairs out of the guildhall, after Ilsa fell, and had not started again. That frightened Paloma more than the flood did. "The moon is looking for us."

"I know."

"It will find us faster if we stand still."

"I know that too." She shifted Nen higher on her hip — the child was seven years old at most, all elbows and salt-damp hair, far too heavy to carry and far too precious to put down — and looked out across the lower city from the mouth of Tanner's Stair.

Vaelmoor's lowest quarter had always been a place of ramps and switchbacks, streets built like the insides of a shell, spiraling down toward the harbor. Now it was a place of rivers. Water sheeted across the cobbles ankle-deep, then knee-deep, moving with a purpose no tide had ever shown. It did not lap. It *climbed*. And it was not empty: pale shapes moved beneath its surface, lantern-glow drifting like fish, and every so often one of them paused — she could feel it pause — as if listening through the stone for footsteps.

Behind her, up the stair, the guildhall bells were still ringing the alarm Vane's rivals had ordered rung when they discovered the ritual chamber empty. Let them ring. Let the whole rotten tower hear it. She had stolen their sacrifice out from under their incense and their cold arithmetic, and there was no going back up that hill, not ever.

There was only down.

"The harbor's throat," she said aloud, mostly to make herself believe it. "Ilsa said the old floodgate channel. Under the customs house. That's where the deep road starts."

Nen nodded against her shoulder. "That is where my mother went."

Paloma's throat tightened. She did not ask. There would be time for griefs later, if there was a later, and right now every second the moon climbed was a second the water gained.

She took the stairs two at a time.

* * *

The lower city fought her the whole way down.

Not the people — the people were gone, evacuated in the panicked weeks since the tides had turned, or huddled behind sandbagged doors on the high terraces, pretending the sound of rising water was only weather. What fought her was the city itself: doorways swollen shut, cellar grates torn open and vomiting brown water across the lanes, a wine cart abandoned mid-street with its horse long fled and its barrels bobbing like bloated corpses. At the corner of Salter's Row the water was waist-high and running hard toward the harbor, and Paloma had to turn sideways, one arm locked around Nen, and let the current shove her along the wall until her fingers found an iron drainpipe and held.

A shape passed beneath her, close enough to touch.

It was a man. Or it had been. He drifted face-up through the floodlit water with his eyes open and full of soft green light, and around his neck, glinting, hung the courier's badge of the Ember Guild. Paloma knew him. Everyone in the ledgers' office had known Tam Wicker, who whistled while he copied and owed her three games of tiles.

He had drowned in dry air, weeks ago, on a moonless night. Now the water had come to collect the rest of him.

"Don't look," she whispered to Nen, and turned the child's face into her collar, and hated herself for the lie, because Nen had already seen, and Nen's mother was down there too, somewhere, in the country of lanterns, and the child knew better than anyone in Vaelmoor what the drowned looked like when they waited.

"He isn't suffering," Nen said into her neck, muffled. "They don't suffer. They just wait to be remembered. That's the worst part. That's why the covenant mattered."

Paloma held her tighter and kept moving.

The customs house squatted at the bottom of Salter's Row like a fat black beetle, its ground floor already swallowed, its brass fittings green with new salt. Ilsa Maren — bleeding, laughing, furious Ilsa, who had signed a covenant in her own blood and pressed the pen into Paloma's hand with the last strength she had — Ilsa had told her the way in. *Every smuggler in Vaelmoor knows the throat, girl. The guild sealed the floodgate two hundred years ago and called it an improvement. We called it a door and kept a key.*

The key was under the third bollard on the loading quay, wrapped in oilskin, and the loading quay was already underwater.

Paloma stood at the top of the quay steps with the water at mid-thigh and the moon a hand's width above the rooftops, and made herself breathe. Terror sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She let it sit. She had learned that much from Ilsa: terror was not the enemy. Terror was fuel, if you were brave enough to burn it instead of letting it burn you.

*Debts need a debtor,* Ilsa had said. *Make yourself worth owing.*

"All right," Paloma said, to the child, to the dead, to herself. "We go under."

She filled her lungs, clamped Nen against her, and dove.

* * *

The water was warm. That was the horror of it — warm as bathwater, warm as something living, and thick with light. The drowned lanterns hung in the flooded street like jellyfish, and their glow followed her as she swam, blind and straining, along the submerged colonnade of the customs quay, counting bollards by touch. One. Two. Three.

Her fingers found the oilskin packet lashed beneath the iron ring. She tore it free, kicked for the surface, and came up gasping inside the customs house's flooded doorway, where an air pocket sagged under the ceiling like a trapped breath.

Inside the packet: a key of black coral, and a scrap of paper in Ilsa's slashing hand.

*Throat gate sticks. Hit it twice, curse it once, hit it again. She likes manners. — I.M.*

Despite everything — despite the blood still crusted under Paloma's fingernails, despite the covenant-half folded against her heart in its waxed leather sleeve, despite the moon climbing outside — she laughed. A short, cracked, ugly laugh, but a laugh.

"What is it?" Nen asked.

"Nothing. Someone being themselves." She waded deeper into the building, following the slope of the floor, feeling for the great square shadow of the old floodgate that the maps in the guild archive swore had been bricked over in the reign of King Alderic the Fat. The water rose past her waist, her ribs, her shoulders. Nen rode on her back now, arms locked around her throat, cheek pressed to hers, and the child's breathing stayed slow and even in a way no human child's should have, and Paloma remembered — not for the first time — that Nen was collateral, heir, last-born of the drowned realm, and that the sea did not choke its own.

Her foot found a step. Then another. Stairs, descending into the dark, and at the bottom of them, when she ducked beneath the surface and blinked salt from her eyes, a wall of fitted granite blocks — and set into it, half-hidden by two centuries of silt and barnacle, a door-shaped seam with a lock the size of a dinner plate.

She surfaced into the last of the air pocket, which was shrinking, the ceiling pressing down, the whole drowned building groaning around them like a ship in weather.

"Two hits, a curse, two more," she recited. "Manners."

She drove the coral key into the lock and struck it with the heel of her palm. Once. Twice. The mechanism ground, choked, refused.

"Come on." Her voice cracked. Outside, through the high shattered windows, the bruised silver light was strengthening, and she could feel the moon turning overhead like a vast slow wheel, and somewhere above the guildhall bells had stopped ringing, which meant the keepers had stopped searching, which meant they believed the thirteenth moon would do their work for them. "You miserable, stubborn, beautiful old door, open. Open for her. Open for the last of them. Open because two hundred years of stolen names are in this ledger and someone has finally come to pay."

She hit it twice more.

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking in the earth's own hand.

The gate swung inward six inches and the air pocket collapsed; the sea poured through the gap ahead of them, eager, almost courteous, and Paloma kicked off the bottom and let it take them — down a throat of cut granite older than the kingdom, older than the guild, a tunnel bored when Vaelmoor's founders and the realm below still spoke to each other in the same tongue. The lantern-light lined the walls here, embedded in the stone itself, guiding without words. The water carried them fast, too fast, and Nen's arms tightened around her neck, and Paloma folded the child's hands together over her heart and thought: *whatever comes, we arrive together.*

Then the tunnel spat them out into open black water under an impossible sky, and Paloma understood, with a lurch that had nothing to do with swimming, that they had left Vaelmoor behind entirely.

* * *

The harbor's throat opened onto the drowned realm.

She broke the surface in a vast cavernous dark where the water lay flat as hammered tin, and above her — impossibly, terribly — hung the underside of the harbor itself, the keels and pilings and sunken spires of old Vaelmoor, the drowned quarter, upside-down like a reflection that had decided to stay. And beyond it, rising through the gloom, the true city of the deep: towers of coral and glass, streets lit by ten thousand drifting lanterns, terraces where pale figures stood motionless, all of them facing outward, all of them watching the single small breach where a clerk and a child had come tumbling through a door two hundred years shut.

And above it all, visible through a wound in the cavern roof, the moon.

The thirteenth moon. It hung directly over the water, swollen and silver-bruised, and even as Paloma watched, it seemed to lean closer, and the flat black surface of the drowned sea began to tremble.

"Paloma." Nen slid off her back and stood upright on the water — stood, weightless, unafraid, and for the first time since the guildhall the child looked like what she was: royalty in rags, an heir come home. "They see us. My mother's people see us."

"Will they let us speak?"

"They have to." Nen turned, and her eyes held the lantern-light, and her voice was steady as a keel. "But the moon turns fully in less than an hour. And the elders are bound by the old terms, and the old terms say that if no signatory stands before the turn completes—" The child's composure flickered, just once, showing the seven-year-old underneath. "—then the debt collects itself. All of it. Both realms."

Paloma looked down at the waterlogged leather sleeve pressed against her chest, where Ilsa Maren's blood had dried to rust between the pages of the covenant's human half. She looked up at the terraces, at the thousands of waiting faces, at the moon leaning down like a creditor at a debtor's door.

Then she lifted her chin, and trod water, and shouted in a voice raw from smoke and salt — shouting in common tongue and, stumbling, syllable by remembered syllable, in the pre-kingdom speech she had spent three months learning from marginalia:

"I am Paloma Halloran! Keeper of the Ember Guild's ledgers — and keeper of nothing else, because I gave the rest away! I bring the stolen covenant! I bring a signature paid in blood! And I am coming to your court to speak the truth the guild never spoke — so either open your roads to me, or watch me drown trying!"

For a long moment, silence. The lanterns drifted. The moon leaned.

Then, all across the drowned city, ten thousand lights went out at once — and relit, one by one, in a line, forming a road of fire across the black water, leading down into the dark, toward the Court of Lanterns.

Nen took her hand.

"Walk," the child said. "They're listening."

Paloma stepped off the surface of the sea — and the sea, for the first time in two hundred years, held her up.
