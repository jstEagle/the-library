## 18. Court of Lanterns

The harbor's throat did not end so much as open.

One moment Paloma was falling through water that held her like a hand, Nen's small fingers laced through hers, the flood roaring past them like a river deciding to be an ocean — and the next, the water simply stepped aside, parting around a stair of black coral that spiraled down into light.

Not sunlight. Nothing so kind. The light came from lanterns — thousands of them, tens of thousands — drifting in the deep like a sky turned upside down and set to burning underwater. They hung at every height, some no larger than a fist, some vast as cathedral bells, and each one burned with a slow green-gold flame that did not flicker, because there was no wind here, only current, and the current had opinions.

Nen's grip tightened. *We are expected,* the child's eyes said, though the mouth said nothing yet. Since the descent had begun, Nen had spoken less and less of the common tongue, as if each fathom downward stripped another layer of it away, revealing the older language beneath like sediment washed from stone.

The stair delivered them onto a floor of nacre that gave beneath Paloma's boots like packed sand, yielding and remembering. She looked up and understood, with a lurch, that they stood in a court — not a hall built by hands, but a hollow in the drowned city where the architecture of Vaelmoor's sunken quarter had been colonized, over centuries, by the realm below. Bell towers leaned together to form arches. A customs house had become a colonnade. And everywhere the lanterns, rising in slow constellations toward a surface that glimmered far above like the memory of a sky.

They were not alone.

The elders sat in a crescent of thrones grown from wreck-wood and pearl — nine of them, or perhaps eleven; their outlines shifted when she wasn't looking directly, the way fish do. They were tall in the way that deep things are tall, robed in kelp and sailcloth and one in what Paloma recognized, with a sick twist in her stomach, as guild livery gone green with age. Their faces were lantern-lit and half-dissolved, features suggesting rather than declaring: here an eye like a coin at the bottom of a well, there a mouth made of ripple. Between them and the two small figures on the nacre floor stretched a hundred feet of open court, empty except for the light.

And the light was watching her.

Paloma had imagined this moment a hundred times on the way down, and in every imagining she had been eloquent. She had rehearsed arguments the way she'd once rehearsed ledger entries — precise, balanced, every figure in its column. Now, standing in the actual weight of all that attention, every rehearsal evaporated. What remained was a mud-stained clerk with salt-cracked lips and a child's hand in hers, and the truth, which was the only thing she had carried down intact.

So she gave them the truth.

She knelt — not because anyone commanded it, but because her legs decided for her — and drew Nen down beside her, and she spoke to the crescent of lantern-faces in a voice that cracked on the first word and steadied after.

"My name is—" She stopped. The name caught in her throat like a fishbone. *Paloma Halloran, keeper of the Ember Guild* — but that was a lie now, wasn't it? She had sold the name. It lived in Ilsa Maren's ledger, purchased and paid, and whatever answered to *Halloran* these days was a ghost walking around in borrowed membership. "My name is nothing you can hold me to," she amended, and saw several of the elders lean forward, ripple-mouths curving in something that might have been approval. "That is the first honest thing I can tell you. The guild erased me before I came. I am not here as its representative. I am here because someone has to be."

The eldest elder — the one in the ruined livery, seated at the crescent's center — raised a hand like driftwood. The gesture was small. The whole court responded to it: every lantern dimmed a shade, and the silence deepened until Paloma could hear her own heart working against the pressure of the deep.

"Speak," the elder said, and the voice came from everywhere, from the water itself, from the nacre under her knees. "You have three turnings of the moon. We will listen to none of them wasted."

* * *

She told them everything.

She began where the guilt began — not with the razored pages, not with Aldous Vane's careful evasions, but with the founding covenant itself, which she had read in fragments in the sunken archive while the vault groaned around them and Vane's hunters hammered at the door. She recited what she could remember verbatim, because recitation was the one craft she had mastered: the terms of exchange, the goods traded across the boundary between air and water. Memories. Names. Years. The impossible things the Ember Guild had built its fortune cataloguing — the bottled recollections, the deeds to houses that had never stood on any street, the vials of other people's childhoods — all of it payment, all of it tribute flowing down into the harbor's throat in the city's first century, when Vaelmoor and the drowned realm had been, if not friends, then at least bound.

"And then we stopped paying," Paloma said. "That's the plain shape of it. Somewhere around the third generation, the keepers looked at the ledgers and saw wealth instead of debt. Every memory we were supposed to send down became a memory we could sell. Every year owed became a year kept. My guild didn't lose your covenant. We robbed it. We robbed it slowly, politely, in beautiful handwriting, and then we razored out the pages that proved what we'd done, and we called ourselves keepers ever since." Her voice did not shake now. That surprised her. "I have audited those ledgers line by line. I know the exact date the payments stopped. The fourth of Thawmonth, in the year the harbor master's daughter drowned. I used to think that entry was a tragedy recorded. Now I understand it was a theft committed. We took a child's death and used it as our excuse to close the books."

The lanterns burned on, unmoved, or moving in ways too deep for her to read. But the water had gone very still around her, stiller than water should be, the way a room goes still when a speaker has said the thing everyone knew and no one dared pronounce.

"I won't tell you the ones who did it are dead and beyond justice, because that would be an excuse, and I promised myself I wouldn't bring you excuses. Some of their names are still written in the guild rolls. Their successors sit in the guildhall right now, warm and dry, voting on how best to dispose of this child." She felt Nen flinch beside her and squeezed the small hand. "Yes. You should hear it plainly. Your last-born heir came up to us carrying your claim, and the Ember Guild voted — yesterday, by lamplight, with quills — to give Nen back to you dead. A sacrifice on the thirteenth moon. They think a death balances a theft. They think grief is a currency." She lifted her chin and met the eldest elder's coin-dark eye. "I stole the child back. Ilsa Maren died doing it. So no, I don't stand before you innocent. I stand before you as the only human in Vaelmoor willing to say aloud what my guild owes you, without subtracting anything first."

Silence. The long, pressurized, listening silence of the deep.

Then one of the outer elders spoke — a thin voice like water finding a crack in stone. "The guild sent us payments. Small ones. For years." The ripple-mouth shaped the words with something worse than anger: weariness. "A memory here. A name there. Enough to remind us the debt lived. Enough to keep the tide patient. Did you know that, little clerk? Your senior keepers have been making interest payments on a stolen fortune, decade upon decade, buying themselves time with scraps."

"I found the entries," Paloma said quietly. "Disguised as maintenance costs. Harbor dredging. Candle wax." She swallowed. "I also found what some of those payments were. I know children went down. I'm not going to pretend I didn't read those lines, and I'm not going to soften them. Your patience was bought with the worst currency we had. That's not a defense of the guild. It's the fullest accounting I can give."

Something moved through the court then — not a sound, but a pressure, a shifting of all those lanterns at once, like a great beast turning over in sleep. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. Paloma couldn't tell, and she had learned, in the flooded streets and the sunken archive, that the drowned realm did not perform its feelings for human benefit. What it felt, it simply was, the way the tide simply was.

The eldest elder rose. Up close, the ruined livery resolved into detail that made Paloma's chest ache: embroidered along the hem, still legible beneath the rot, ran the old guild motto. *All Things Accounted.* This elder had been human once. Or had worn humanity once, or been paid in it, or married into it — the covenant had been a marriage, Nen had said, not a contract, and Paloma was only beginning to understand how literally the child had meant it.

"You speak truth," the elder said. "We have waited a long time to hear a human speak truth in this court. Do you know what we heard instead, century upon century? Excuses dressed as law. Apologies drafted by committees. Once, a keeper came down carrying a beautifully illuminated scroll of contrition, sealed in wax, worth more than everything it apologized for." A sound moved through the crescent — laughter, or the memory of laughter, cold and green as deep current. "Truth is rarer than any good in your guild's inventory. You have brought it freely. Know that it moves us."

Paloma's heart lifted — one wild, foolish inch — before the elder's next words pressed it back down.

"But it does not unbind us."

* * *

"The covenant was not a punishment," the eldest said, and the whole court seemed to settle in to hear words that had clearly been spoken many times before, to many desperate visitors, across many generations. "Understand what we were to each other. When Vaelmoor was young, its founders did not conquer the harbor. They courted it. Two realms, one water between them, and a bargain struck as bride-price and dowry both: the dry realm would send down its memories, its names, its years — the things that make a life heavy — and the wet realm would hold them, keep them safe from fire and forgetting, and in return the sea would stay gentle at the city's throat. Not servants. Spouses. The exchange was not tribute. It was intimacy. It was how two peoples remembered each other."

Nen stirred against Paloma's side. The child's voice, when it came, was small and clear and carried impossibly through the waterless water. "When the payments stopped, it was not a debt left unpaid. It was a door closed in the middle of a conversation." Nen looked up at the crescent of elders. "For four hundred years, we have been talking to a shut door."

"And a marriage starved of speech becomes either a grave or a war," the eldest said. "We chose war slowly. The tides turning backward. The courier drowning in dry air. These were not attacks, little clerk. They were letters. Knocking. The way one knocks on a door that will not open — harder, and harder, until the door breaks." The coin-dark eye fixed on Paloma. "But the old terms bind us still, as they bind you. The covenant was signed by signatories, and it can only be remade by signatories. We cannot forgive what we are empowered to collect. The sea cannot choose gentleness; it can only keep its vows or break them. If the vow is broken, the flood comes. That is not cruelty. That is grammar."

"Then change the grammar," Paloma said. "Reforge it. The archive told us — a willing signatory from each realm. You have yours." She touched Nen's shoulder, gently. "The child came up as collateral. Let the child come down as heir. Let Nen sign for the drowned realm, willingly, in front of this court. That half is done, if you allow it."

Murmurs moved through the crescent like fish through reeds. Several elders turned to Nen, and the lantern-light bent toward the child, and for a moment Paloma saw the court's whole attention gather on that small mud-stained figure with something that was unmistakably hope — old, battered, embarrassed hope, the kind that has been disappointed so long it has learned to hide.

"It may be so," the eldest said slowly. "The heir's consent would carry. But the other half—" The driftwood hand turned palm-up, an ancient gesture of impasse. "The human half requires a human signatory with standing. Not a thief of children. Not an exile. Not a nameless clerk who sold her membership to a smuggler for boat passage and a dead woman's loyalty." The eye did not accuse; that was the terrible thing. It merely stated, the way the tide states. "The guild holds the standing. The guild must sign. The guild that razored the pages, that sold your heir back to us dead, that has fed us scraps and lies for four hundred years — the law of the covenant gives the standing to them, and to no one else."

"Then the law is wrong," Paloma said.

"The law is the marriage," the elder replied. "It is wrong the way a bone that healed crooked is wrong. It still holds you up."

Paloma got to her feet. Her knees ached from the nacre; her lungs ached from speaking truth in a place that drank truth like brine. Above them, far above, through leagues of darkening water, she could feel the moon beginning its turn — the thirteenth moon, the last one, dragging the whole ocean behind it like a sled. Time was not running out. Time was running *down*, the way water runs down, finding every low place, filling every crack.

"Then hear my last accounting," she said, "and judge whether I have standing enough for even one thing."

The court stilled.

"You say the guild must sign, and the guild will not — I watched them vote. You say the law binds you to collect, and collection means drowning every soul in Vaelmoor, the bakers and the dockhands and the children who never wrote a single ledger line. You say the covenant is a marriage. Then let me tell you what I know about marriages, as a person with nothing left but what I know." Her voice rang off the bell towers, off the customs-house columns, off ten thousand patient lanterns. "A marriage where one party keeps perfect books and the other drowns the household is not a marriage. It is a grudge with ceremony. Your ancestors deserved better. *Nen* deserves better. And so, gods help me, does my city — guilty, thieving, beautiful, mine. I did not come down here to beg you to waive the debt. I came to pay it. All of it. In the only coin I have left, which is myself. So ask me your terms. Ask me anything. I will sign anything true. I will owe anything just. Only tell me there is *some* grammar in which this ends with two realms alive."

The eldest elder regarded her for a long time. Around the crescent, the other elders had gone motionless in a new way — not the stillness of judgment, Paloma thought, but the stillness of people watching a scale begin, at long last, to tip.

"There is one grammar," the eldest said at last, and the lanterns all through the drowned court dimmed, as if the words required all the light in the realm to be spoken. "Older than the covenant. Older than the marriage. The founders wrote it into the first page and then feared it and razored even the memory of it out. A signatory need not be chosen by standing." The coin-dark eye held hers. "A signatory may be *made*. By consent, by cost, and by witness of both realms. It has never been done. It was never meant to be done. The price is not written down anywhere, because no one has ever been foolish enough to ask."

Above them, through the whole weight of the sea, the moon completed the first third of its final turn — and every lantern in the court guttered once, all together, like a held breath.

"Ask anyway," Nen whispered, in the old tongue and the common one at once, and the two languages sounded, for the first time, like the same language.
