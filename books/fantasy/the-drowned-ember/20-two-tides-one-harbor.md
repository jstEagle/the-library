## 20. Two Tides, One Harbor

Nen woke to the sound of the sea letting go.

It was not a gentle sound. The whole harbor groaned like an old man rising from his knees — timber and stone and ten thousand tons of water all shifting at once, settling into channels that had been denied them for three days. Nen lay in the narrow bed in Ilsa Maren's smuggler's loft above the Saltrow stairs and listened to Vaelmoor exhale.

Below the window, the flood was retreating down the streets it had swallowed. Water slid off rooftops in silver sheets. The lower quarters emerged one story at a time, dripping, ruined, alive. Somewhere a bell that had been underwater since the second night rang once, muffled and astonished, and did not ring again.

Nen pressed both palms flat against the cold floorboards and felt for the pull.

For as long as Nen could remember there had been a pull — a thread of salt running from the center of the chest down through the soles of the feet, through stone, through silt, through the long dark throat of the harbor, all the way home to the court of lanterns. It was the thread every child of the drowned realm was born with, the one that tightened when the elders called and slackened when they slept. It had pulled Nen up out of the deep and across the borderlands and into the mud of Vaelmoor's harbor road, three days' walk from nowhere, carrying nothing but a message no one could read.

The thread was still there.

But it was different now. Thinner. Not cut — Nen would have known if it were cut; the absence would have been a scream — but stretched, the way a rope stretches when two ships that used to sail together are finally allowed to drift apart on purpose instead of tearing each other's hulls open.

*Two tides, one harbor.* That was what Paloma had written in the covenant's first new entry, her hand steady even though everything else about her was shaking. *Each realm keeps its own water. Neither keeps the other's debts.*

Nen got up and went to the window.

* * *

Paloma was already awake. Of course she was. She sat at the little table by the loft's single lamp with the fresh ledger open before her, writing, and Nen watched her for a moment before speaking because watching Paloma Halloran work was its own kind of lesson.

She wrote the way she had done everything these past weeks: completely. No flourish, no hesitation, no looking up to see who approved. Her pen moved and the world arranged itself around the words. When she finished a line she blotted it carefully, turned the page, and only then noticed Nen standing barefoot in the gray light.

"You should sleep," Paloma said. "You signed a covenant twelve hours ago."

"You signed two."

"One of them wasn't mine." But her mouth did the thing it did when she was pleased and trying not to be — a small crooked movement, quickly folded away. "How do you feel?"

Nen considered the question honestly, because lying to Paloma had never once worked and trying had become embarrassing for them both. "Lighter. And heavier. Both at once." Nen held up a hand and turned it in the lamplight. The skin was still faintly luminous at the edges, the drowned-realm glow dimming now that the thirteenth moon had completed its turn and the old terms were ash. By midwinter, Nen suspected, even that would be gone. "I don't shine anymore."

"Does that frighten you?"

"It should be frightening. It isn't." Nen came and sat across the table, and the ledger lay between them like a third person at the meal. "Down below, everyone glows. Everyone is lit from inside. You can't hide anything — your fear shows, your anger shows, your love shows. I used to think that was honesty. Now I think it was just... being seen whether you wanted it or not." Nen touched the page where Paloma had written the opening line in two tongues, the pre-kingdom script flowing beneath the common letters like current under ice. "Up here you have to choose to be seen. That's harder. But it's yours."

Paloma looked at Nen for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, "They'll come for you today. The lantern-court. They'll want their heir back."

"I know."

"They sent word at dawn. Through the harbor door of the guildhall, which is apparently a thing that exists now." Paloma's jaw tightened. "Vane stood in front of it for an hour before he could make himself knock. He wanted me to translate the summons. Me. After everything."

"What did it say?"

"That the court convenes at slack tide tonight. That the heir of the drowned realm is expected." She hesitated, and Nen saw her choose her next words the way she chose ink — deliberately, knowing they would stain. "That if the heir does not return, the covenant holds anyway. The realms stay bound. But the court will consider the debt of the child unpaid, and they will grieve it, and grief has a way of becoming tide."

* * *

The city spent the day wringing itself out.

Nen walked through Vaelmoor with Paloma and watched the human realm do something the drowned realm had never learned: recover messily. There was no ceremony to it. Fishermen hauled their boats off the tops of houses with ropes and blasphemy. A woman whose bakery had stood in four feet of harbor water for three days opened her doors anyway and sold soaked loaves at half price to anyone who could pay and some who couldn't. Children — human children, pink-lunged and loud — raced paper boats down the flooded stair-streets while their mothers shouted from doorways, and the shouting was mostly laughter wearing a disguise.

Nobody glowed. Everybody was visible anyway, if you knew how to look.

At the guildhall steps, keepers were carrying ledgers out into the sun to dry — hundreds of them, laid open like sleeping birds along the balustrade. Nen stopped and stared. Some of those books were older than kingdoms. Some of them held entries in the pre-kingdom tongue, copied faithfully by hands that had never understood a word of what they recorded.

"They kept us," Nen said slowly. "All this time. In the margins. In the impossible-things column. Every memory they bought, every name, every year — they wrote it down and locked it away and told themselves it was inventory."

"Yes," Paloma said.

"And now the books are drying in the sun."

"Yes." Paloma's voice had an edge Nen had learned to read: not anger at Nen, never that. Anger at the shape of things. "Aldous Vane is inside right now, resigning in front of the full conclave, because the law requires a signatory to witness the dissolution of a keeper's oath and I refused to do it for him. I want him to say the words himself. I want them all to hear what they did, said plainly, by the man who did most of it."

"Will he?"

"He'll say the words. Whether he hears them is between him and whatever's left of his conscience." She glanced sideways. "You don't have to go in. Nobody in there is going to be kind to you."

"I know." Nen looked up at the guildhall — the black basalt face, the harbor door at its base that now stood open for the first time in centuries, seawater lapping gently at its threshold like a dog waiting to be acknowledged. "But I'm the one who has to decide by tonight. I should decide standing in the place where the decision lives."

* * *

Inside, the great ledger hall smelled of salt and candle smoke and old paper. The drying racks filled the air with a soft rustle, hundreds of pages turning in a wind that came from nowhere and everywhere — or perhaps from the harbor door, breathing.

The conclave was still assembled from the morning's session, senior keepers in their gray robes, and when Nen walked in beside Paloma the whispering started like rain on a roof.

Nen had been in this hall once before, as cargo. Dragged here in chains of woven kelp to be given to the thirteenth moon, while men Nen had never harmed voted with raised hands to drown a child in exchange for their own comfort. Nen remembered the faces. Most of them were the same faces now, and none of them would meet Nen's eyes.

Good, Nen thought. Let them look at the floor. The floor remembers too.

Keeper Brannock — the one whose vote had come loudest, whose voice had said *the arithmetic is simple* — actually flinched when Nen passed close. Nen stopped beside him. Looked up. Waited.

"The covenant stands," Nen said, in careful common tongue, loud enough for the whole hall. "Your city will not drown. Your children will not be taken. The tides belong to themselves again. This is what mercy looks like when it comes from my grandmother's court. I would remember it, if I were you. Mercy is rarer than vengeance, and costs more to give."

Brannock's mouth opened. Closed. He bowed his head — not deeply, but truly — and Nen walked on, and something in Nen's chest settled into place like a bone going back into joint.

Not forgiveness. Not yet, maybe not ever. But the weight of being owed something had lifted, and Nen found the lightness almost dizzying.

* * *

Slack tide came at dusk, and with it the summons.

Nen stood at the top of the Saltrow stairs in the last violet light, and the harbor lay flat and glassy as poured lead, holding the first stars. At the base of the stairs, where the water met the stones, the surface began to glow.

Lanterns. Dozens of them, rising from the deep — not fire but the true lantern-light of the court, cold gold shot through with green, carried by hands that were more suggestion than flesh. The elders came partway. They always would, now; the covenant bound both directions. Three figures of brine and shimmer stood waist-deep in the shallows, and the eldest raised a voice like a wave breaking far offshore.

*Nen. Last-born. The water asks for its heir.*

The thread in Nen's chest pulled — gently now, respectfully, the way one tugs a sleeve rather than a chain. Home. The warm dark. The singing dark. The court where every face glowed and every heart was legible and no one had ever, ever had to choose to be seen because seeing was simply what the water did.

Paloma stood a few steps back, hands clasped behind her, giving Nen the space to do this alone. That was its own kind of love, Nen was learning. The human kind. The kind that hurt the giver.

Nen walked down the stairs. Stood at the waterline. The eldest elder extended a hand of drifting light.

And Nen said, in the pre-kingdom tongue, the language of the deep:

*I am not coming back.*

The harbor went very still.

*The covenant is reforged,* Nen continued, and the words came easier than any Nen had ever spoken in either tongue, because they had been growing in Nen all day like a pearl around a grain of truth. *But a covenant needs a living ledger — someone who belongs to neither shore and both. Paloma Halloran gave up her name to buy our freedom. Ilsa Maren gave her blood to seal it. I am the last-born of the drowned realm, and I choose to stand on the dry side of the water, beside the ledger, so that when your people look up and wonder if the surface-world remembers them, they will see one of their own walking in the sun, and they will know.*

*The heir does not abandon the realm,* the eldest said, and there was sorrow in it, vast and tidal. *The heir IS the realm.*

*Then the realm walks with me,* Nen said. *Every tide that touches this harbor carries you to me. Every entry Paloma writes in your tongue carries me to you. Grandmother — I spent my whole life being sent places. As message. As collateral. As payment. For once, let me arrive somewhere because I chose it.*

The lantern-light flickered. The three elders looked at one another, and Nen saw — really saw, for the first time without the glow making everything legible — that they were afraid. Afraid of the empty court. Afraid of the silence after the last-born's voice went quiet forever. Afraid, perhaps, of a world where children could refuse.

Then the eldest laughed. It sounded like surf finding a beach it had missed.

*You argue like a Halloran already.*

*Someone has to teach me properly,* Nen said. *She's very strict about margins.*

The eldest lowered the lantern-hand, and from the deep rose a single object, turning slowly upward through the green-gold light: a small bell of black coral, unbroken, dry despite the sea streaming off it. The heir's bell. Given to the last-born at birth, sunk with the rest of the nursery treasures when the realm decided its heir would be safer as a hostage than a child.

*Then carry this,* the eldest said. *Ring it when the water forgets your name. Ring it when the land forgets ours. And when you have a child of your own someday — however that comes to you — bring them to this stair at slack tide, and let them choose too. That is the new covenant, little one. Not the one written in the book. The one written in you.*

The bell settled into Nen's hands, light as a promise.

The lanterns sank. The harbor darkened to ordinary midnight blue, and the first real tide of the new age came in — clean, unhurried, keeping its own time — and washed over Nen's bare feet with a temperature that was neither warm nor cold but simply *present*, like a handshake.

Nen stood there until the glow faded from the water entirely. Then climbed the stairs, bell in hand, back toward the lamp in the window and the woman bent over the ledger who had not moved from her seat the entire time, because she had promised not to watch unless Nen wanted watching, and she kept her promises the way she kept her books: exactly, and forever.

"Well?" Paloma said, not looking up.

"I'm staying."

"I know." Pen scratching. "There's a spare cot. And tomorrow we start teaching you bookkeeping, because if you're going to live above water you're going to earn your keep like everyone else, and no, glowing is not a marketable skill anymore—"

"Paloma."

She looked up.

"Thank you for not deciding for me."

Her pen stilled. Something moved across her face — surprise, then a fierce quick tenderness she tried to file away and failed. "That's the whole point of you," she said. "Of us. Of—" she gestured vaguely at the ledger, the harbor, the ruined and recovering city, the whole improbable arrangement. "Everything, now. Nobody decides for anybody. We just... write it down honestly afterward."

Nen set the black coral bell on the table beside the lamp, where it caught the flame and threw a small green-gold circle on the wall — the first lantern-light ever to burn on the dry side of the harbor by invitation rather than invasion.

Outside, far below, the tide turned. And in the deep water past the harbor mouth, faint but unmistakable, something began to sing — and Nen, grinning, sang back.
