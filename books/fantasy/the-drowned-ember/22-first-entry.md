## 22. First Entry

The guildhall smelled different now.

Paloma noticed it the morning she came back — three days after the flood receded, two after Vane's resignation letter was read aloud in a chamber so silent you could hear the harbor breathing through the windows. The old smell of tallow and dust and the particular sourness of ink left too long in its pots had been joined by something else. Salt, yes. But under the salt, something green. Kelp and rain and the cold sweetness of deep water where light still reaches.

The drowned realm's breath, seeping up through the stones. Through her.

She stood in the doorway of the Scriptorium with her hands full — a fresh ledger bound in gray calfskin, unruled, unbegun — and let herself feel the strangeness of walking into this room as neither clerk nor stranger. The keepers who passed her in the corridor did not quite bow. They did not quite meet her eyes either. They performed a new maneuver she had begun to think of as the Vaelmoor Shuffle: a half-step aside, a murmur that might have been *Keeper* or might have been nothing at all, and then a swift retreat into whatever doorway was nearest.

She had been registered, per the Guildmaster's grudging decree, in a category that did not exist until she forced it to. The roll book now contained a line written in four different hands because no single scribe would claim it:

*Halloran, Paloma — neither member nor stranger. Standing: signatory. Precedent: none.*

Precedent: none. She had laughed when she first read it, alone in the archive at midnight, the laugh coming out cracked and startled like a bird flushed from cover. Four hundred years of law, and she was the exception the law had to grow around. A stone in the riverbed that the water now bent to pass.

*You are the ledger,* Nen had told her, the night the covenant was signed, their small hand pressed flat against hers over the salt-white page. *Ledgers don't belong on shelves.*

They were wrong about one thing, she thought now, setting the gray ledger down on her old desk — the desk by the north window, third from the fire, the one she'd fought for at fourteen and been granted at sixteen because nobody senior wanted a post so close to the drafts. Ledgers belonged exactly where someone would open them.

She opened this one.

The first page was blank in the way that only truly new things are blank — not empty but waiting, the paper holding its breath. Outside the window, Vaelmoor was drying itself out in stages. Scaffolding climbed the lower quarters like ivy. Carters shouted about mud. Somewhere a bell that had been underwater for a month rang badly, its voice waterlogged, and nobody minded, because a bad bell meant the city was still there to ring it.

Nen arrived the way Nen always arrived: without footsteps. One moment Paloma was alone; the next there was a child sitting cross-legged on the window seat, still wearing the strange layered clothes of the drowned court, though they had taken to adding human touches — a wool scarf Ilsa's smuggler crew had given them, boots two sizes too big that clacked when they walked. Their skin held the faint lantern-glow it always would now, the mark of the realm that had made them heir, but their eyes were dry and bright and entirely awake.

"You started without me," Nen said.

"I sharpened pens. That's not starting."

"It's the beginning of starting." They slid off the seat and climbed onto the chair beside hers, chin propped on folded arms, watching the blank page with the gravity of a magistrate. "What will you write?"

"The truth." Paloma tested the pen's nib against her thumb. "All of it. Starting with what we took."

* * *

It took her most of the day to write the first entry, and she wrote it twice.

The first version failed because she wrote it the way the guild had taught her — in the clean passive voice of institutional record. *Goods were received. Terms were altered. Payment was withheld.* She got four lines in before the words curdled in her mouth. That voice had razored pages out of covenants. That voice had voted, in a warm room above dry water, to put a child on an altar because the arithmetic was tidier that way. She scraped the ink back off the page with a knife, leaving the ghost of the words like a scar, and decided to leave the scar. Let the record show what language the guild had spoken first, and what it cost to speak plainly over it.

The second version she began with the date in both tongues. The common script came easily. The pre-kingdom tongue she wrote slowly, matching character for character the marginalia of the oldest ledger, the strokes Nen had guided her hand through a dozen times in the weeks since the signing — down-strokes like oar-blades, the looping sign for *water-that-remembers*, the small hooked glyph that meant *we*. Not *I*. The language apparently had no word for a person alone; even its pronouns assumed company.

That felt right. That felt like the whole truth of the last season in a single grammatical fact.

Then she wrote:

*Entry the First, of the Covenant Reforged.*

*Let it be recorded honestly, since honesty is the only payment this page will accept.*

*Nine generations ago, the Ember Guild entered covenant with the realm beneath Vaelmoor harbor. The terms were these: memory for memory, name for name, year for year — a marriage of two peoples, each owing the other the keeping of what the other could not hold alone. The drowned cannot keep dry memories; paper rots below. The living cannot keep the deep names; our mouths forget them by morning. So we traded. What they gave us, we wrote down. What we gave them, they sang.*

*And then the guild broke it.*

*Not all at once. We kept a memory here, a name there, telling ourselves we would return them. We taught ourselves that receipt was ownership. By the fourth generation we had stopped singing back entirely. By the seventh we had razored the terms from our own founding document and called the impossible things in our vaults "acquisitions." We built a city on goods we had stolen from a partner we pretended was a myth.*

*The debt came due on the thirteenth moon. It should have drowned us all.*

*It did not, because a child was sent instead of a wave, and a nameless clerk chose truth over excuse, and a dying thief paid a debt that was never hers with blood that was. Ilsa Maren, founder and exile: the covenant bears your signature in the place of honor. I have written it in gold. You would have hated that. Good.*

*The terms now stand as follows, sworn beneath the last turn of the thirteenth moon, by signatory of the drowned realm and signatory of the surface, witnessed by both courts:*

*First. All goods unjustly held are returned or acknowledged, item by item, year by year, until the account reads true. This work has begun. It will outlive everyone now living. That is what a covenant is.*

*Second. Neither realm shall take from the other what the other has not freely given. The word "freely" is doing necessary work in that sentence, and every future keeper will be examined on it.*

*Third. There shall be exchange again — memory for memory, song for song, child visiting child in safety, lanterns answering lanterns across the harbor line. The marriage is not restored. Marriages do not restore; they are remade daily or not at all. We remake it daily.*

*Fourth. The undersigned binds herself as the living ledger between the realms: keeper of what the drowned cannot write, singer of what the living cannot hold. This office has no predecessor and no term. It ends when the sea forgets the shore, which the drowned assure me is never.*

*Signed in the sight of both waters,*

*Paloma.*

*No house. No guild-name. No title but this one.*

She sat back. Her hand ached. The candle had burned down to a knuckle of wax, and through the window the sky had gone the deep bruised blue that came just before the harbor lamps were lit.

Nen had read every word over her shoulder — she'd felt their breath on her ear, patient as tide. Now they reached out and touched the signature, very lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal.

"It's missing something," they said.

Paloma's stomach dropped. "What?"

"Me." Nen took the spare pen, dipped it with exaggerated care, and beneath her name drew a single glyph in the pre-kingdom script — the small hooked one. *We.* Then, underneath, in careful common letters they had learned only that winter: *Nen, last-born of the drowned court, who stayed.*

"There," they said, satisfied. "Now it's true."

* * *

The ceremony, if you could call it that, happened at dusk.

Once a month now — on the new moon, when the veil between tides wore thin — the covenant demanded acknowledgment. Not sacrifice, not tribute. Acknowledgment. Paloma carried the gray ledger down through the guildhall, past the Scriptorium where junior keepers were already being taught the pre-kingdom alphabet (badly, loudly, with much complaint, which she counted as the healthiest sound the guild had made in a century), past the vault where the returned goods lay sorted into cedar trays, each tray ticketed in two languages, down the great stair and out onto the harbor steps.

The water stood calm and black under the starless sky. At the third step from the bottom, where the old covenant-stone had been re-set into the masonry, she stopped and opened the ledger across her knees.

This part had taken practice. The first month, she had simply read the entry aloud in common and felt the silence below like a held breath. The second month, Nen had coached her through the response-lines in the old tongue, and halfway through, the harbor had lit.

Lanterns. Hundreds of them, rising from the deep in slow spirals — not fire but something older, the cold gold glow of the drowned court, drifting up through fathoms of dark water like seeds climbing toward a sun that wasn't there. The whole harbor had glowed that night. Fishermen had fallen to their knees on the quays. Children had run along the seawall shrieking with joy rather than fear, which Paloma privately considered the truest measure of how much Vaelmoor had changed.

Tonight she read the First Entry entire — the honest parts, the shameful parts, the terms — her voice carrying flat and clear across the black water. When she reached the fourth clause, the binding of herself, her voice caught, the way it still did, the way she suspected it always would. Giving yourself away sounded simple until you heard yourself say it.

Then Nen stepped forward to the water's edge, small and glowing, and sang the answer-line in the pre-kingdom tongue — the phrase that meant, as near as translation allowed, *heard, remembered, answered; we are two and we are one.*

The harbor waited.

One heartbeat. Two. Three — long enough for doubt, which was also part of the ritual now; the drowned insisted on it, said faith untested was just habit.

Then the light came.

It rose the way it always did, but fuller tonight, denser, spirals within spirals, lanterns answering lanterns all the way down until the harbor floor itself seemed kindled and the black water burned gold from beneath like a lampshade of sea. The reflection reached the guildhall windows. It reached the scaffolding on the lower quarters, where workmen stopped and leaned on their rails to watch. It reached, Paloma knew, the little shrine the fisherfolk had already built on the north quay — a shelf with a candle and a bowl of harbor water, tended by an old woman who claimed, implausibly, to remember her grandmother singing to the deep, and who had turned out to be the guild's most valuable source of recovered covenant-songs.

Somewhere in that glow, she knew, the elders of the drowned court were reading the entry as it rose — the covenant carried meaning upward on the light itself, word by word, and they would weigh it tonight in their court of lanterns, and tomorrow or the day after, a small envoy of brine and glass would walk up the harbor steps with the drowned reply, and the work would continue. Item by item. Year by year. Daily, or not at all.

Nen's hand found hers. Small, cool, faintly luminous, gripping hard.

"They liked it," the child said. "The long part about Ilsa. It made the water go warm."

Paloma laughed — really laughed, the sound surprising her, loose and unguarded in a way she was still learning to permit herself. Above them the stars were coming out over a drying city. Behind them the guildhall blazed with ordinary lamplight, full of arguing apprentices. Below them a civilization turned its face upward, patient and vast and, against every odd her training had ever taught her to calculate, forgiving.

She closed the ledger gently, as you close a door on a sleeping room.

"Come on," she said. "Tomorrow we start Entry Two."

"What's Entry Two?" Nen asked, scrambling up the steps beside her, boots clacking.

Paloma paused at the top of the harbor stair and looked back once at the fading gold.

"The list," she said, "of everything we're giving back — and everything we're giving forward. It's going to be the longest book the guild has ever kept."

Nen grinned, lantern-bright. "Good thing," they said, "that we've got forever."

And far below, one last lantern rose alone through the dark water — not a message, not a summons, just a light set burning at the harbor's throat like a lamp left in a window — and Paloma Halloran, neither keeper nor stranger, signatory and scribe and home, lifted her hand in answer before turning toward the hall where supper waited, and the fresh pages, and the morning's work.
