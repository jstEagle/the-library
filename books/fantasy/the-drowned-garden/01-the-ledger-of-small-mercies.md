## 1. The Ledger of Small Mercies

The rain came down like a verdict.

Tamsin Thornbury stood in the doorway of the Ferryman's Rest with her hood up and her heart going like a trapped bird, watching the marsh road dissolve into grey water. Behind her, up the hill, Odile's cottage sat dark against the reeds, its chimney cold for the first time in eleven years. She had put the fire out herself. That was the thing that kept snagging in her chest — not the leaving, but the small domestic violence of drowning someone's hearth. You could steal from a witch. You could curse her under your breath for a decade. But you did not put out her fire unless you meant never to come back.

She meant it.

*Never be anyone's apprentice again.* She said it to herself the way other people said prayers, except she didn't believe in gods, only in names, and hers felt thin as a reed tonight.

The ferryman was drinking at the corner table, a barrel of a man called Doss with hands like knotted rope and eyes that had seen too many people leave this town at bad hours. He looked up when she came in, and his gaze went straight past her face to the satchel at her hip, because everyone in Mirefen knew what a hedge-witch's apprentice carried, and everyone knew what it meant when she carried it out into the rain alone.

"Late crossing," he said. "Water's high."

"Then I'll pay for the risk." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"With what?" He turned his cup on the table. "Coin's no good to me if the Guild finds out I ferried a runaway. And there's nothing else in Mirefen worth anything, except—" His eyes flicked to her hands. "Except what you've got in your fingers."

Her fingers. Of course. Word traveled even through standing water: the old woman on the hill had an apprentice who could *read*. Not letters — anyone could puzzle out letters, given years. Tamsin read the true names of things. Put a charm in her palm and she could tell you what it actually was, underneath whatever its maker wanted it to be. A luck-knot woven from fear. A healing salve cut with pond scum. A love-token stitched by someone who hated the person they'd stitched it for.

Odile had called it a gift. Then, in the last year, she had started calling it something else. *A lantern,* she'd said once, in that soft voice she used when she was being most frightening. *And every lantern draws moths, girl. Some of them wear crowns.*

That was three days before the criers came through Mirefen with the royal decree, before the word *hunt* entered the town's mouth and stayed there like a fishbone.

"I don't sell charms," Tamsin said now. "I make them."

"Same thing, from where I'm sitting."

"It isn't." But it was, tonight. Everything was the same thing tonight: survival wearing whatever coat it could find.

Doss shrugged, vast shoulders rolling like the marsh itself settling. "Then keep your principles and your pretty fingers dry. There's a back room here. Sleep till morning, walk back up the hill, apologize however witches like to be apologized to. Old Odile's fierce, but she raised you from what, eight? Nine? She'll take you back."

The tavern's warmth pressed against her like a hand trying to turn her around. And for one long moment — one shameful, human moment — she let it. She thought about the cottage smelling of mugwort and tallow, about the ledger Odile kept in her own cramped hand, every charm dispensed, every debt owed, every secret told to the marsh grass by a desperate client faithfully recorded. She thought about how Odile had taught her letters on that ledger's margins, tracing the shapes with a finger still wet from the rain.

Then she remembered the look on Odile's face yesterday, when the decree was read aloud in the square. Not fear. Not anger. *Calculation.* The quick, sideways arithmetic of a woman measuring her apprentice against her own neck, and finding which one weighed less.

Maybe she'd imagined it. Maybe she hadn't. But Tamsin had learned the first law of reading true things: *what a thing is matters more than what it says it is.* And last night, Odile had locked the strongbox that held Tamsin's mother's ring — the one thing of hers Tamsin had left — and called it *for safekeeping*, in a voice that made safekeeping sound like a cell door.

"No," Tamsin said. "I need to cross tonight."

"Then I need paying." Doss leaned forward. "One charm. Something real. None of your weather-bones or fever-knots — something that'll matter on open water."

Her stomach dropped. This was the part she'd been dreading, the part she'd rehearsed in the cottage while pretending to sleep: the arithmetic of flight, where everything you owned got spent faster than you could count it. She opened the satchel with hands that wanted to shake and made them not.

Inside: three fever-knots, two weather-bones, a salve, a stub of chalk, her mother's ring — no. The ring was gone, locked away, and thinking about it felt like pressing on a bruise, so she didn't. What remained was the thing she'd made last winter, back when making things still felt like joy. A small charm of braided rushes and river-clay, sealed with wax, meant to keep a boat steady in crosscurrents. She'd made it for Odile's fishing skiff. Odile had never used it. Hadn't even thanked her, just set it on the shelf with the others, another finished lesson among unfinished ones.

She held it out. "Steady-water charm. It works. I've tested it."

Doss took it and turned it over in his rope-ridge hands. "Tested how?"

"On me. In the mill race, in flood season."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way men look at something they're deciding whether to respect or use. "You're the one who reads them, though. That's the story. You can tell what a thing truly is."

"Yes."

"So tell me what this is."

She took it back for a moment, held it in her palm, and let her gift open like an eye. The charm resolved beneath her attention: braided rush, river clay, beeswax, and beneath all of it, wound through the material like a thread through cloth, the name she'd spoken over it — *hold, hold, hold fast against the turning* — still bright, still honest, because she'd made it wanting nothing but a boat that stayed upright.

"It's true," she said quietly. "It does exactly what it says. That's rarer than you'd think."

Something moved behind Doss's face — not warmth, exactly, but the nearest thing to it that Mirefen produced. He pocketed the charm. "Back door. Ten minutes. Don't be seen carrying light."

---

She almost didn't take the ledger.

It happened in the eleven minutes between the tavern and the skiff, in the window of time when a fleeing girl becomes a thief without quite noticing the border crossing. She had gone back up the hill — stupid, reckless, necessary — because she'd forgotten the chalk, and chalk was the one tool her gift couldn't do without, and a gift without tools was just a voice shouting into wind.

The cottage door wasn't locked. That should have frightened her more than it did. Inside, the dark smelled of mugwort and old smoke, and Odile's great chair stood empty by the dead hearth, and on the worktable, in plain sight, lay the ledger.

Not Odile's ledger. A different one — thick, swollen with damp, bound in cracked green leather gone black at the corners. Tamsin knew every book in that cottage. She had dusted this one for years and never once been allowed to open it. *Trade papers,* Odile said. *Boring as ditchwater. Worthless since the man who wrote it drowned.*

Worthless. Except Odile was a woman who boiled bones for their marrow and rendered tallow down to the last drop, who kept a jar of buttons sorted by the griefs of the people who'd brought them. Odile did not keep worthless things. Odile kept things whose worth hadn't come due yet.

Tamsin stood in the dark with the rain hammering the shutters and heard her own heartbeat say: *take it.*

Not for the contents. For the principle. If she was going to spend the rest of her life being no one's apprentice, then let the first thing she owned outright be a book her mistress had lied to her about. Let it sit in her satchel like a small stone of proof: *you kept secrets; now I keep one back.*

She took it. It was heavier than it looked, the way guilty things always are. She also took the chalk, and — after one breath of hesitation at the hearth — a twist of dried herbs from the rafter-hook, because spite was a fuel too and she was running low on everything else.

Then she was out the door, down the slick path, through the reed-beds where the water grabbed at her boots, to the skiff where Doss waited under a hood of oilcloth with the current pulling impatiently at the mooring line.

"You went back," he said. Not a question. An accusation shaped like one.

"For chalk."

"For chalk." He snorted and shoved off from the bank. "Sit low. Keep the satchel between your knees. And pray your old mistress sleeps deep, because if she wakes and finds her house robbed, there isn't a charm on this water that'll save you from what she sends after."

"She won't send anything," Tamsin said, and found, to her surprise, that she believed it — and that believing it hurt worse than fearing it would have. Whatever Odile did next, it wouldn't be rage. It would be something patient. Something with a longer reach than any curse.

The marsh opened around them, black water stitched with rain, the far shore a rumor of darker dark. Halfway across, the current turned treacherous — a crosscurrent where the channel bent, the kind that took skiffs sideways into the reeds and held them until morning, until searchers, until worse. Tamsin felt the boat begin to swing and put her hand flat on the hull without thinking.

"Hold," she whispered. Not a spell. Just the word, and under it the name of the wood, the name of the water's push, read quick and rough through her palms. The skiff steadied. Doss looked at her hands, then at the water, then said nothing at all, which from him was a eulogy of gratitude.

They grounded on the far shore below a broken watchtower, where the trade-road began its long crawl toward Saltmere and the sea. Doss hauled the skiff up, pocketed his charm, and jerked his chin eastward.

"Road's that way. Three days' walk if the weather holds, five if it doesn't. Saltmere's big enough to lose yourself in, if losing yourself is the trade you're after." He paused, and for a moment the ferryman's flat pragmatism cracked enough to show the man beneath. "Girl. Whatever you're running from — run *through* it, not just away. Things that get run from catch up. Things you walk through stay walked."

"That's very wise for a man who charges children charms for river crossings."

"That's why I charge children charms." He turned back to his boat, then stopped. "There's criers on this road already. Royal men, asking after gifted folk by name. They had a list. Your name wasn't on it yet." He didn't look at her. "Yet."

Then he pushed off into the rain, and Tamsin Thornbury stood alone on the far shore with wet boots, stolen chalk, a twist of spiteful herbs, and a book she'd been told was worthless.

* * *

She walked for two days before she dared open it.

Nights she spent in drainage culverts and hay barns, sleeping in fits, waking at every owl-call convinced it was Odile's voice. Days she walked with her hood down like any mud-lark pilgrim, because a girl with her hood up on an empty road was either rich or marked, and she was neither, or would be neither if the world had any mercy in its ledgers.

On the second evening, holed up in the roof-loft of a tithe barn with rain coming through the thatch in slow silver threads, she finally unbound the green leather and turned to the first page.

The handwriting hit her like a slap. Not Odile's cramped, secretive script — this was a merchant's hand, confident and looping, each entry dated in a system she half recognized: the old Guild notation, the kind salt-factors used, with columns for goods received and goods delivered. Except the goods were wrong. No grain, no salt, no cloth. The entries read:

*Bottled storm, minor, temper of a jilted bride — 1 — delivered.*
*Borrowed year, twenty months remaining, taken from a consumptive weaver (willing) — 1 — delivered.*
*Memory, first love, intact, seller remorseful — 1 — held.*
*Dragon-fire, vial, drawn living — 1 — delivered. Buyer: [name burned out].*

Tamsin sat very still in the hay-dust and the silver rain-light, and the barn seemed to lean in around her.

She knew what bottled storms were the way marsh children know them: stories, warnings, things hedge-witches invoked to frighten clients out of foolish bargains. *You want your rival's luck? Fine. But somewhere there's a jar with a storm in it, girl, and storms don't like jars.* She had thought they were stories. She had thought borrowed years were stories. She had thought dragon-fire was the kind of story you told children so they'd stop playing near the old kilns.

But here it was, itemized. Priced. *Delivered.* Decades of deliveries, page after page, in a hand that grew shakier toward the end, the dates marching on past wars and coronations and the great fever-year that even Mirefen remembered, when the old king sickened and the bells rang ready for mourning and then, inexplicably, rang again for recovery.

There — she found it without looking for it, the way you find a name in a crowd. The entries changed that month. The handwriting steadied. The prices doubled. And in the margin beside the first new entry, in different ink, someone had written a single word in a language Tamsin didn't know but her gift, traitorously, half-heard: a word that tasted of iron and honey and something vast turning over in its sleep.

She closed the book.

Her heart was doing something complicated — fear and greed and awe fighting for the same narrow ground. Because this was what she'd stolen, then. Not ditchwater. Not trade papers. A ledger of impossible things, kept by some dead Guild-factor with a shaky hand and a buyer whose name had been burned out of history.

And burned out deliberately. She ran her thumb over the scorch mark and felt, faintly, under the char, the ghost-shape of letters. Her gift stirred toward them like a dog toward a closed door, and she pulled it back hard. Some doors you didn't open on a night you couldn't lock again.

Below her, in the barnyard, a dog barked twice and stopped. Hoofbeats, maybe. Wind in the poplars, more likely. She lay back in the hay with the ledger under her jacket, over her heart, where stolen things belong, and stared at the roof beams while the rain counted the hours.

Three days to Saltmere. A city big enough to lose yourself in — if losing yourself was the trade you were after. She had a knack nobody could be allowed to see, a name that might be on a list by now, a dead factor's book full of miracles priced by the unit, and eleven years of Odile's voice in her head saying *lanterns draw moths.*

Let the moths come. She was done being the flame someone else kept in a jar.

*Never be anyone's apprentice again.* She said it once more, out loud, to the rain and the rats and the dark, and this time it didn't feel like a prayer. It felt like a signature — the first entry in a ledger of her own.

Tomorrow she would walk east. Tomorrow she would find out what a girl with dangerous fingers and a book of impossible things was worth in a port city built on buying and selling everything the crown pretended didn't exist.

Tonight, she slept — badly, with one hand on the green leather, dreaming of a word that tasted of iron and honey, and of a buyer whose name had been burned out, and of the sound bells make when they ring for a king who should have died.
