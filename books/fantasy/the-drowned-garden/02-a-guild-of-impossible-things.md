## 2. A Guild of Impossible Things

Saltmere smelled of salt, tar, and other people's money.

Tamsin had never seen a city before. The marsh towns she'd grown up in had a hundred souls at best and one road apiece, and Odile's cottage at the edge of the fen had been the last lit window for a day's walk in any direction. Saltmere was ten thousand lit windows, stacked and jumbled up a hillside like a drunkard's idea of the stars. Gulls screamed over the harbor in voices that sounded personally offended. Ships crowded the quays with their masts crossed against the grey sky, and everywhere — on crates, on banners, on the brass plaques nailed to doors — the same mark: a root worked in gold leaf, branching downward instead of up.

*Roots grow down,* her mistress had told her once, in one of the rare moods when she taught instead of snapped. *Anything worth keeping keeps its strength where no one thinks to look.*

She hadn't thought about Odile in four hours, which was a record, and she hated herself for the lapse. Fear made a fine whetstone but a poor companion. She pulled the stolen ledger out of her satchel for the tenth time since dawn and checked it was still there, which it was, because books did not evaporate, and then put it away again without reading it, which took discipline.

The trouble was that she could not stop reading it.

Not the ink — the ink was dull enough. Columns of figures, entries of goods bought and sold, initials of clerks long dead or promoted. She'd read a thousand ledgers like it doing Odile's accounts: tallow, eel-fat, dried nettle, charms sold by the pennyweight. This ledger's goods were stranger — *one storm, bottled, medium violence; two years, borrowed, from a farmer near Harrow's Bend, repayment deferred; one memory, secondhand, slightly wept-on* — but strangeness alone wouldn't have stopped her heart in the market square that morning.

What stopped her heart was that the ledger had a true name, and she could hear it.

Everything did, if you had the knack. Most things kept their names close, the way shy people keep their hands in their pockets. A charm had one. A curse had one. A well had one, and a bridge, and the old king's face stamped on the copper coins — though the coin's name came out wrong somehow, blurred, like a word said through water, and Tamsin had decided years ago not to think too hard about that. But a book? Books were just paper and patience. Books were safe. Books couldn't have names.

Except this one did, low and patient under its columns of figures, a name like a held breath, like something waiting to be counted. And when she let herself listen — really listen, letting the gift rise in her the way Odile had spent six years teaching her to suppress, to cage, to *never use where anyone might see* — the ledger answered. The figures rearranged themselves behind her eyes into something truer than arithmetic. Bottled storms became weather with somewhere to be. Borrowed years became people who would age twice as fast until the debt cleared. And one entry, recurring monthly for eleven years, in a hand different from all the others:

*One measure dragon-fire, drawn living. Buyer: withheld. Payment: rendered.*

Dragon-fire. Drawn living. From what? Dragons were gone. Everyone knew dragons were gone, the way everyone knew the king was hale and the harvests were fair and the drownings were necessary. Eleven years of monthly entries, and every entry paid in full, and nowhere in three hundred pages a single line saying *what for*.

She'd been standing in the fish market reading the air around a ledger like a madwoman when the harbor bell began to ring, and she'd run, because running was becoming a habit, and now here she was, climbing a hill through streets that steamed after the rain, following a gold-leafed root to the door at the top of Copperlane where a woman in a grey coat stood waiting as though she'd been expecting Tamsin since Tuesday.

"You've been circling my building for an hour," the woman said.

"I've been admiring the architecture."

"It's ugly. I built half of it and I regret the rest." The woman had iron-grey hair pinned back with what looked like a fountain pen and eyes the color of a ledger line: ruled, straight, and very hard to cross. "You're the girl from the marshes. Bought passage off Captain Ilse with a luck-charm this morning. Paid double what the charm was worth, which tells me you didn't make it yourself."

Tamsin's stomach performed a slow, cold roll. "You've been watching me."

"Captain Ilse drinks at my table. You read her ledger while she counted your coin, and your lips moved, and then you went white and gave her the charm for free." The woman stepped aside from the door. "Nobody goes white over figures, girl. They go white over names. Come in before someone else notices what I noticed. There are people in this city paid by the crown to notice exactly that."

Every sensible thing in Tamsin's body said *run*. She had gotten good at running. But she was tired, and wet, and the alternative to this door was the harbor, where the bell had rung twice more since noon, and bells meant decrees being read, and decrees lately meant drownings.

She went in.

* * *

The Guild of the Gilded Root looked, from inside, like what it pretended to be: a counting-house. Clerks scratched at desks in a hall that smelled of lamp oil and beeswax. Ledgers lined the walls in locked cases, hundreds of them, spines dated back further than Tamsin could guess. It should have been comforting. Instead the whole room hummed at the edge of her gift like a hive she'd thrust her hand into — every book murmuring its name at once, some bored, some bitter, one or two, near the back of the oldest case, sounding almost like screaming.

"The Guild deals in impossible things," the woman said, walking fast. "Storms. Years. Sleep. Luck, measured by the dram. We buy them, bottle them, sell them on, and above all—" she tapped a clerk's desk as she passed, "—we count them. Everything that comes through this house gets entered, priced, and balanced. A thing without a price is a thing without a leash." She stopped at a door marked with a brass plaque worn nearly blank. "My name is Verity Crane. I'm the mistress-of-coin. Which means everything in this building is mine to account for. Including, as of this morning, you."

"I'm not for sale."

"Everyone is. The only question is the currency." Verity opened the door onto a small office where a fire burned too cheerfully for the room's austerity. "Sit. Answer honestly. If you lie to me I'll know, because I've been lied to by professionals for thirty years and amateurs bore me. What did you hear when you read Ilse's manifest?"

"That her ship is named *Patient Mercy*, that she's superstitious about the number nine, and that she's carrying contraband sleep in the false bottom of her third crate."

Verity sat down across from her, unhurried. "And the ledger you stole."

Here it was, then. Tamsin set her satchel in her lap like a shield. "I didn't steal it. I *took* it. From Odile Vane. My mistress. Who was going to—" She stopped. Rerouted. "It's hers. I took it. That's theft, whatever you call it."

"Noted. What did you hear in it?"

Tamsin weighed lying and found, to her surprise, that she didn't want to. Not because Verity Crane frightened her, though she did, but because in six years no one had ever asked Tamsin Thornbury a question expecting a real answer. Odile asked questions the way traps ask questions.

"There's an entry," Tamsin said slowly. "Recurring. Monthly. Dragon-fire, drawn living. The buyer's name is withheld, but the payment column says *rendered*, and rendered means the buyer isn't paying coin. Coin has its own name and this one's been paid in something else. Something the ledger doesn't have a column for." She looked up. "Eleven years. Who draws fire from a living dragon every month, and what are they buying with it?"

For the first time, Verity Crane's ledger-line expression flickered. It was small — a tightening around the eyes, a fractional stillness — but Tamsin had spent six years learning to read people the way she read things, and she caught it.

"That," Verity said, "is above your station."

"Then why ask?"

"Because I needed to know whether you actually heard it or merely guessed. Guessers are cheap. Hearers are—" she paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—dangerous. And occasionally indispensable." She laced her fingers on the desk. "Here is what will happen otherwise, girl. Within a week, someone reports a marsh-witch's runaway apprentice who reads true names in a public market. The crown pays for such reports. You'll be taken, declared gifted, and drowned in the harbor, and the crowd will cheer, because crowds always do. That is the alternative to this conversation."

"And this conversation is better how?"

"Because I'm offering you employment. Clerk, third class. Room in the attic, meals, wage of eight silvermarks the month. In exchange, you keep accounts, you keep your gift behind your teeth, and you keep your existence quiet." Verity slid a sheet of paper across the desk: a contract, dense and clean. "The Guild protects what it employs. That is not kindness; it's policy. Damaged assets don't count."

Tamsin looked at the contract. Eight silvermarks. An attic. Walls full of books whose names whispered all night. A roof between her and the decree.

It also looked exactly like a cage with better upholstery than Odile's.

"No apprenticeship," she said.

Verity raised an eyebrow.

"I signed nothing for Odile Vane either, not properly, and look how that ended. No indenture, no term of years, no clause where the Guild owns my work or my name. I'll clerk for wages. I can leave when I want. Write it that way or find another hearer."

Something that might, in poor light, have been approval moved across Verity's face. She drew the contract back, dipped a pen, and struck out three clauses with quick, surgical strokes. "Wages only. Notice given, departure permitted. Satisfied?"

"Suspicious," Tamsin said. "But yes."

"Wise girls survive longer than trusting ones." Verity turned the paper around and offered the pen. "Sign."

Tamsin signed. Her own name looked strange on Guild paper — *Tamsin Thornbury*, plain as porridge, no true name required, no binding in the margins. She checked anyway. She checked three times. The paper stayed honest.

Which was when the office door opened without a knock and a large man leaned in, rain still dripping off him, and said, "Verity. Criers on the quay. New decree."

"We've had new decrees all month."

"Not like this one." He came fully into the light, and Tamsin's whole body went rigid before her mind caught up: the build of him, the way he stood with his weight balanced and his hands loose — she'd seen men stand like that in the fen villages when the king's riders came through. Huntsman. Or huntsman-shaped.

"This is Bram Hale," Verity said, without introduction-flourish, as if naming a piece of furniture. "Formerly of His Majesty's Royal Hunt. Currently of my payroll, guarding shipments. Bram, this is Tamsin Thornbury, our new clerk, third class. Try not to frighten her."

"Too late," Tamsin said.

Hale looked at her properly then, and she braced for the flat appraisal she'd seen from crown-men assessing value, but what she saw instead was something wearier. He looked at her like a man reading a list of things he'd failed.

"New decree," he repeated, to Verity, ignoring Tamsin entirely. "It's not the usual edict. It's a proclamation of hunt. Open season. Every gifted soul in Saltmere, listed by district, with rewards." He set a folded broadsheet on Verity's desk. "And there's a seal on it that shouldn't be there."

"A seal?"

"The royal signet." Hale's jaw worked. "I carried that signet for nine years. I know its weight in wax better than my own mother's face. This impression is wrong. Too shallow. The lion's mane faces left instead of right." He looked up, and his voice dropped to something rawer. "The king's signature has changed, Verity. Same name. Different hand."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the great hall, a clerk laughed at something, ordinary and human, and the sound seemed to come from another country.

Verity unfolded the broadsheet and read it with the same expression she'd give an invoice found to be short. When she finished, she folded it again, precisely along its original creases, and said only: "That is a matter for the crown, not the Guild. Our position is neutrality. It has kept us alive through three reigns."

"Your neutrality kept Wren Amsel alive too, did it?" Hale said. "Courier boy. Runs your eastern route. He's got the gift, Verity, everyone knows it, he lights his pipe with a snap of his fingers when he thinks no one's looking. He's on that list. District four."

"He's on *every* list. He's been on lists since he was twelve. Lists are not knives."

"This one comes with knives attached."

"Then the Guild will consider how to respond," Verity said, in a voice that closed like a drawer, "after due deliberation. Which is how institutions stay standing, Bram, while individuals get drowned. You may go."

Hale went, but at the door he paused and looked back at Tamsin — one long, level look, the look of a man checking a name against a list of his own — and then he was gone, boots loud down the corridor.

Verity exhaled through her nose. "Forget him. He's useful and haunted, in roughly equal measure." She stood, straightened a coat that didn't need straightening. "Attic room's up the back stair. Supper's at seven. Your first task is tomorrow: reorganize the receiving manifests, third case, east wall." At the doorway she stopped, and did not turn around. "The entry you heard. The dragon-fire. You'll not speak of it again. Not to Hale, not to anyone. Some balances exist so the world stays upright, girl. Ours is one of them. Good night."

Then Tamsin was alone in a warm office with a contract in her hand and a hundred humming books beyond the wall, and she ought to have felt safe, and part of her did, a shameful, exhausted part that wanted eight silvermarks and an attic and to stop running.

But she kept hearing Hale say it. *Same name. Different hand.* And beneath the hall's hive-hum, beneath the ledgers and their patient names, she could almost hear that eleventh-year entry whispering its withheld buyer, its rendered payment — as if the whole Guild, this warm, impossible, counting house of wonders, were itself a ledger entry in some vaster book.

*One shelter, gilded. Price: withheld. Payment: rendered.*

She picked up her satchel and went to find the attic, telling herself the shiver down her spine was only the draft.
