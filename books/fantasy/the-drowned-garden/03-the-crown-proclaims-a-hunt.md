## 3. The Crown Proclaims a Hunt

Bram Hale heard the decree before he saw the crier, and his body knew it before his mind did — that particular cadence of official proclamation, the drum-and-trumpet rhythm designed to make obedience feel like celebration. He was mending a net on the Saltmere quay, a thing he did badly and often, because idle hands had once been hands that held warrants, and he'd learned to keep them busy.

The crier mounted the customs-house steps in royal livery gone salt-faded at the seams. Behind him, two soldiers in the king's black and gold. The crowd gathered the way crowds do — slowly, then all at once, fishermen setting down their lines, fishwives abandoning their arguments, children climbing onto crates for a better view.

"By order of His Majesty, the King, long may he reign—"

Bram's hands stopped moving.

"—let it be known throughout the realm: the gift is an abomination before crown and gods alike. Those who traffic in true names, who read what was not written, who work wonders outside the law of church and crown, are hereby declared enemies of the realm. Let them be sought. Let them be found. And let them be given to the water."

*Given to the water.* Not drowned. Drowning was a mob's word, ugly and honest. *Given to the water* dressed it in ceremony, made it sound like a tithe, like something owed.

He'd written proclamations like this once. Not the words themselves — clerks did words — but he'd carried them. He'd nailed them to doors in villages where he knew every name, and stood watch while neighbors read aloud the legal murder of people they'd shared bread with. He'd told himself it was law. Law was a fine word. It held up under almost anything.

"Any soul who shelters the gifted," the crier continued, "any soul who conceals them, feeds them, or fails to report them, shares their crime and shall share their fate."

A murmur ran through the crowd — fear, mostly, but Bram caught other notes underneath. A woman near him crossed herself twice with her left hand, the old way, the way you did when you wanted protection from something specific. A boy of maybe ten looked at his own fingers with sudden suspicion, as if the gift might be visible there, a stain or a mark.

And one man, a rope-seller Bram half-knew, said quietly to his neighbor, "About time. My cousin lost her harvest to some hedge-witch's weather-bargain." His neighbor nodded. There were always those who cheered. That was the part Bram had never gotten used to — not the cruelty of the crown, which was at least predictable, but the relief on ordinary faces. The hunt gave people permission to stop pretending they loved their neighbors.

The crier unrolled a second parchment, longer, sealed with wax the size of a plum. "Let all hear the King's own hand and seal upon this charge—"

That was when Bram stood up.

Not deliberately. His legs simply decided, hauling him through the crowd toward the steps, close enough to see. The soldiers tensed; the crier faltered mid-word. "Something amiss, friend?"

"I want to see the seal," Bram said. "I've a right. It concerns my trade." He let a little of the old authority into his voice, the flat court accent he usually buried under harbor drawl. It still worked. Ten years out of service, and it still worked.

The soldier glanced at the crier, who shrugged — what harm in letting a rough-looking man squint at wax? — and tilted the parchment.

Bram looked.

The great seal was right. Of course it was right; seals were stamped from matrices kept under guard, and no spymaster alive would risk a forged matrix for a public proclamation. The crowned stag rampant, the nine stars of the old alliance pressed clean and deep in green wax. Any clerk, any magistrate, any huntsman would pass it without a second glance.

But Bram had spent eleven years reading the king's signature. He'd carried documents bearing it across three kingdoms' worth of roads. He'd watched the king sign — actually watched, from his post at the council chamber door, a dozen times, before he'd been dismissed, before the disgrace, before all of it. King Aldric wrote his name like a man signing away something he loved: the long descending stroke of the *A*, the strange doubled loop in the *d* that clerks called the king's knot, the final flourish that trailed off like a sigh, ink thinning as though the hand grew tired of its own authority halfway through.

The signature on this parchment had the king's knot. Had the descending stroke. But the flourish ended in a small, precise hook — a clerk's terminal, tidy as a ledger line. No tiredness in it at all. And the ink pressure never wavered. Every letter bore down with exactly the same weight, first stroke to last, like something that had learned the shape of the name but not the feeling of writing it.

Signatures were like walks, like laughs, like the way a man held his cup. You could copy a walk well enough to fool a stranger. You could not fool the man who'd walked beside him for eleven years.

Bram stepped back. Made himself nod, grunt something about good handwriting, turn away. His heart was going hard against his ribs, and he kept his face fixed in the bored expression of a man who'd seen a hundred decrees and cared about none of them.

*The king's hand has changed.*

There were innocent explanations. Illness changed hands. Age changed hands. A stroke, a palsy, a new scribe trusted with the royal sign-manual — kings signed thousands of things; most by proxy. Except this wasn't a proxy mark. This was meant to be the king's own hand, and it was perfect in the way only a forgery could be perfect, flawless in the way only something nonhuman could be flawless.

Or — and here was the thought he'd been circling for ten years, the heresy he'd never dared finish — the explanation wasn't innocent at all, and it wasn't illness either.

He walked back to his net. Sat down. Picked up the shuttle. His hands were steady, which surprised him.

* * *

They gave the first ones to the water that same evening, because the decree had come with names attached.

Bram learned later that the lists had been compiled in advance — that somewhere in the capital, clerks had been writing down names for months, waiting for the seal to be affixed. The first taken in Saltmere was a charm-mender off Coppersmith Row, a gentle old man who charged a copper to un-kink cursed hinges and had once fixed Bram's stove for free. The second was a girl of sixteen who could make milk keep three days past spoiling. Small gifts. Harmless gifts. The kind the old laws had ignored for generations, the kind the crown had always pretended not to see, because a kingdom runs on small mercies and everyone knows it.

The soldiers took them at dusk and marched them to the end of the long pier, where a platform had been built — built already, Bram noted, its timber still pale, sawdust not yet washed from the planks. Built in advance. The whole thing had been arranged before the decree was ever read, which meant the decree wasn't a response to anything. It was theater. The play was written; they were just waiting for the curtain.

The crowd came anyway. That was the worst part. They came in hundreds, filling the quay and the seawall and the rigging of ships, because grief and horror are no match for spectacle, and the crown knows it. Torches hissed in the sea wind. Somewhere a fife played — someone had decided drownings needed music.

Bram stood at the back, hood up, and hated himself with a thoroughness that felt almost professional.

He watched the old charm-mender go first. The man didn't struggle. He looked out at the water the way a farmer looks at weather coming — resigned, assessing, already elsewhere. When they tipped the bench he went under with barely a splash, and the crowd made a sound Bram had heard before, at executions, at battles: that soft collective exhale, half horror, half satisfaction, the sound of people confirming to each other that the world still worked, that the rules still applied, that they were safe so long as they were not the ones on the platform.

The girl fought. Of course she did. She was sixteen and her gift was keeping milk sweet, and she fought like sixteen, screaming her innocence in words that broke apart in the wind. Her mother was in the crowd — Bram saw her, saw soldiers holding her arms, saw the exact moment the woman stopped struggling and started praying, lips moving fast, eyes fixed on the water where her daughter would be.

Bram had put people on platforms like that. Not this exact platform. Others. In other towns. He'd been good at it — that was the poison of it, he'd been *good* — patient, thorough, fair-minded even, giving the gifted every chance to recant, to bind their gift away, to swear oaths. Some took the chances. The ones who didn't, he'd delivered. And he'd told himself, all the way down the years, that he'd been kinder than the alternative, that a quick drowning beat a mob with torches, that he'd followed the law because the law was all that stood between order and the fire.

Then he'd found out what the law served. Then he'd read the private directives, the ones that never reached the provincial courts, the ones that explained why certain gifted were taken quietly and shipped east instead of drowned, why the quotas existed, why the hunt never truly ended no matter how few were found. He'd understood that he hadn't been keeping order. He'd been harvesting. And he'd said so — loudly, in the wrong room, to the wrong man — and been stripped of rank and sent into the world with nothing but his disgrace.

Ten years, and he'd told himself the story was finished. Tonight the story turned a page.

The girl went under. The fife played something bright. The crowd dispersed in twos and threes, talking in low voices, some weeping, some silent, a few — Bram saw them, always a few — smiling the careful smiles of people who'd reported a neighbor and were pleased with the outcome.

He stayed until the torches burned down. Then he walked the length of the pier, past the platform, now dark and dripping, and stood at the very end where the wood groaned with the swell, and looked at the black water.

"You're getting slow, Hale," he said to it. To himself. "You saw the seal and you stood there like a man at a market weighing cheese."

Because that was the thing he couldn't shake, the thing crawling up his spine worse than the drownings, worse than the music: the wrongness of the signature meant the decree didn't come from the king. Which meant the hunt didn't come from the king. Which meant everything he'd done — the arrests, the deliveries, the eleven years of lawful horror — had been done in the name of a dead man or a hollow one, and the thing that wore the crown had simply picked up the harvest where he'd left it, with better organization and no pretense of mercy.

He'd suspected. For ten years he'd suspected, in the way you suspect a house is rotten — you see the crack, you put furniture against it, you live around it. Tonight the floor gave way.

* * *

He found the Guild's countinghouse by midnight, because if anyone in Saltmere knew what moved behind the crown's face, it was the Guild of the Gilded Root — traffickers in bottled storms and borrowed years, dealers in impossible things, the one institution in the city that kept books on power the way power kept books on people.

The door wardens knew him. Guild men always knew Bram Hale; his face had been famous once, in the bad way. They let him in anyway, which told him the Guild was nervous, and nervous institutions made mistakes, and mistakes made openings.

Verity Crane received him in a counting room lit by a single storm-lamp — a bottled tempest doing duty as a light source, its lightning coiling slow behind glass, throwing shadows that moved wrong. She sat behind a desk of black oak, small and neat and utterly still, hands folded, watching him with the patience of a woman who calculated interest by the hour.

"Huntsman," she said. No title. She never gave him the title.

"Ex-huntsman."

"Ex-huntsman. You've come at midnight smelling of torch-smoke and self-loathing. I can guess why. What do you want?"

"The decree today. The seal was true. The signature wasn't."

Something flickered behind Verity Crane's eyes — not surprise, he thought. Confirmation. As if she'd been waiting for someone else to say it aloud.

"Go on," she said.

"The king's hand has changed. Not aged — *changed*. Same pressure on every stroke, start to finish. No living man signs like that. I carried the king's documents for eleven years. I know his hand the way I know my own gait." He leaned forward. "Whatever signed that decree, Mistress Crane, it isn't Aldric Thorne. And your Guild sells to the palace monthly, and you know it, and you've known it longer than I have."

The storm-lamp flickered. Lightning coiled. Verity Crane did not move for a long moment, and Bram understood he had just done something irreversible — spoken the heresy aloud, in the heart of the one institution that profited from keeping it quiet.

"You're asking me to confirm treason," she said at last, mildly, "on the strength of handwriting."

"I'm asking you to tell me what's in the ledgers."

"The ledgers," said Verity Crane, "are confidential."

"Then tell me this instead." He stood. "There's a girl in this city — new, hedge-trained, sharp-eyed — and your wardens pulled her off the street two days ago instead of handing her to the crown. Why does the Guild of the Gilded Root shelter a runaway apprentice it doesn't know, unless it's because she reads things the rest of us can't? And if she can read what I just saw in that seal—" he tapped the desk, once, "—then she's worth more than every vial in your vaults. And whatever's wearing the king's face will want her drowned before solstice."

For the first time, Verity Crane's folded hands came apart.

"Get out," she said quietly.

Bram smiled without warmth. "Gladly. But you'll send for me within the week. Institutions like yours always do."

He let himself out into the rain, into streets where the fife music had stopped and the harbor lay black and flat as poured lead, and behind him, in the countinghouse window, the bottled storm threw its crooked shadows against the glass — and he thought of the pale timber of the platform, built days before the decree, and the signature that never tired, and the word rising in him like water:

*Sooner.*
