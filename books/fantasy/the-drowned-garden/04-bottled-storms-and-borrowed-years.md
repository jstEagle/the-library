## 4. Bottled Storms and Borrowed Years

The Guild of the Gilded Root kept its inventory in a cellar beneath the counting-house, and the cellar was colder than any honest place should be.

Tamsin learned this on her ninth morning in Saltmere, when Verity Crane handed her a ring of black iron keys and said, "You'll take the deep ledgers now. Mind the third shelf — it bites."

"The shelf bites."

"The ledger does. It was bound by a man who hated auditors." Verity's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile but never quite arrived. "You'll get along."

Tamsin had spent eight days learning the surface of the Guild: the front room where merchants traded in charms and counter-charms, the ink-stained clerks, the smell of tallow and salt that lived in everything. She had copied bills of lading for bottled fog and reconciled accounts for luck, sold by the pinch. She had told herself this was ordinary work, that impossible things were just inventory with better margins.

Then she went down into the cold, and the ordinary ended.

* * *

The deep ledgers were shelved like relics, each one chained at the spine with wire thin as hair. Tamsin unhooked the first and carried it to the reading table, where a single lamp burned behind glass that had gone amber with age.

The cover read, in faded gilt: **STORMS, ACQUISITIONS, YEARS 1–40**.

She opened it.

The handwriting changed hands every few pages — decades of clerks, generations of them — but the entries themselves were uniform in their strangeness:

*Bought: one squall, North Reach, unspent. Seller: a fisherman's widow who wished never to see open water again. Price: passage inland and a new name.*

*Sold: three days of clear weather, wedding of Aldous Vane. Buyer's note: "worth every coin; it rained sideways at our own."*

*Taken in trade: seventeen years from a dying man who no longer wanted them. Condition: years must be spent before Midwinter or they sour. Disposition: sold to a merchant of forty who wished to be twenty-three again. Returned unsold: four years, soured. See disposal log.*

Tamsin turned pages until her fingers ached. There were storms here that had been bought whole off the sea — hurricanes folded down to the size of a fist, rattling faintly in their glass like trapped birds. There were winters purchased by orchard-keepers and summers by funeral directors. There was an entry for *one hour, borrowed from a hanging*, which she read three times and then made herself stop reading.

And threaded through all of it, in every hand, in every decade, ran the same refrain. Small sales. Quiet sales. A memory of a mother's face, sold by a man who could not bear to keep it. A first love, extracted clean as a tooth. Grief, by the ounce.

The Guild did not deal in wonders, Tamsin realized. The Guild dealt in *relief*. People came to its door carrying things too heavy to hold, and the Guild weighed those things, priced them, and poured them into bottles.

It was monstrous. It was also, she understood with a lurch, the kindest trade she had ever seen.

She hated how long she sat with that.

* * *

On the eleventh day, she found the dragon-fire.

It wasn't hidden. That was the first strange thing. The second strange thing was that there was only ever one entry for it, renewed — never repeated, never closed — at the top of a ledger page otherwise devoted to lamp oil and candle wax.

*Dragon-fire: one vial. Renewed monthly. Buyer: standing order. Payment: rendered in gold, collected by courier, no name given.*

No name. In thirty years of meticulous bookkeeping, across dozens of clerks who recorded the shoe size of a storm seller's dead husband, *no name*.

Tamsin sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above her, the counting-house floorboards creaked under Verity's measured steps.

Her gift woke the way it always did — not summoned, but surfacing, like something rising through dark water. When she looked at a written thing and let her eyes go soft, the true names underneath began to show through, the way a watermark shows when you hold paper to light. Odile had called it *reading the bone of a word*. The crown called it drowning-magic. Tamsin called it the only thing she'd ever been good at.

She let her eyes soften over the entry.

The words *dragon-fire* sat on the page like any other words. But beneath them, faint as breath on glass, the true names stirred — and what she saw made her stomach drop.

Fire has a true name, and it is old, and it is proud, and it does not like being bottled. This fire's name was worn smooth, the way a stone wears in a riverbed — handled, decanted, renewed, month after month after month, for thirty years. And wound through the fire's name like a thread through cloth was a second name, a buyer's mark, a claim of ownership so old and so deep that the fire had stopped struggling against it.

The fire belonged to someone. Not the Guild. The Guild was only the tap.

Tamsin closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping thing.

* * *

She meant to leave it alone. She wants that noted, wherever such things are noted — she meant to.

But the Guild paid her in coin and shelter and the first safety she had known since the marsh town, and safety makes a person curious about the walls around it. So on the twelfth day she pulled the acquisition logs, and on the thirteenth she cross-referenced the courier schedules, and on the fourteenth she found Bram Hale in the yard behind the counting-house, mending a saddle he had no horse for, and asked him the question she'd been carrying for two days.

"Who buys the fire?"

Bram's hands stopped moving. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a huntsman's stillness still living somewhere in his shoulders, and when he looked up at her his face did something complicated. "You've been in the deep ledgers."

"Verity gave me the keys."

"Verity gives everyone keys eventually. It's how she finds out who uses them." He set down the saddle. "Why do you want to know?"

"Because there's no name. Thirty years of entries and no name. Everything else in those books has a name, a price, a condition, a disposition. This one thing is a hole shaped like a buyer."

Bram was quiet for a while. The yard smelled of tar and gull-droppings and the sea doing its endless arithmetic against the harbor wall.

"I was crown once," he said finally. "Royal hunt. You know that much."

"You caught the gifted." She kept her voice flat. It cost something.

"I did." He didn't flinch from it, and she resented him a little for not flinching. "And I'll tell you what I know, because you'll find it anyway — you've got the look of someone who finds things. The fire goes up to the palace. Once a month, same courier route, same sealed wagon. I drove that wagon for six years before they cast me out, and in six years I never once saw the buyer. But I saw the palace gates open for that vial the way they open for nothing else. Not for ambassadors. Not for the king's own kin." He picked the saddle needle back up, worked it once through the leather. "Whatever sits on that throne drinks deeper than kings drink."

"And the Guild sells to it."

"The Guild survives because the Guild sells to it. That's not the same as knowing what *it* is." He glanced at her then, quick and sharp. "You should stop asking, girl. Questions like yours have a way of getting answered by people you don't want answering them."

Tamsin thought of the fire's name, worn smooth as river stone. Thought of thirty years of monthly renewals, patient as tide.

"I don't think it's a question," she said. "I think it's an account. And accounts balance."

* * *

That night she dreamed of the fire.

Not the vial — the fire itself, or whatever fragment of it her gift had touched. In the dream it was vast and guttering both at once, a bonfire seen from very far away, and it was tired in a way that made her chest hurt. Fires burn. This one *endured*, which is a different and worse discipline, and it had been enduring so long that endurance had eaten most of what it was.

In the dream it looked at her. Fire shouldn't be able to look at anyone. It looked at her anyway, and though it said nothing — though it would be many chapters of her life before it said anything — she woke with the absolute certainty that it knew she had read its name, and that it was glad, and that gladness frightened her more than anger would have.

She lay in her narrow bed above the counting-house and listened to Saltmere breathe: the harbor bells, the drunk singing two streets over, the rain starting up against the shutters. Safe. Warm. Paid.

Complicit.

The word arrived without ceremony, the way true names do. She turned it over and examined it honestly, because Odile had taught her that much at least — that a gift unused in self-examination curdles into a curse aimed inward. Every month, a vial went up to the palace. Every month, the Guild was paid in gold that kept its doors open, its couriers riding, its clerks fed, its cellar cold. Her wages came out of that gold. Her bed. The lamp behind amber glass. The safety she had sworn, in a marsh town in the rain, never to need from anyone again.

She had needed it. She had taken it. And the price of it climbed a hill to a palace once a month in a sealed wagon, and no one wrote down the buyer's name.

Tamsin got up, lit the stub of candle she wasn't supposed to have, and pulled the current ledger toward her across the blanket. She would do her work. She would keep the books clean and the columns straight, because she was good at it and because the work was warm and because the alternative was the marsh and the rain and Odile's voice saying *you are not ready to be alone*.

But she opened to the fire's page, and she dipped her pen, and in the margin — small, neat, deniable — she wrote the only honest entry the ledger had seen in thirty years:

*Buyer unknown. Buyer named in fire. Ask the fire.*

Then she blew out the candle and lay back down, and did not sleep, and did not regret it, and told herself the margin note was for no one.

She was wrong about that. Margins in the Guild's deep ledgers were read by more eyes than hers.

Three days later, a courier arrived from the capital with the monthly requisition — and for the first time in thirty years, the standing order for dragon-fire carried a second line, added in a hand Tamsin didn't know, in ink that shimmered faintly wrong when her gift brushed it:

*And ask the clerk who keeps your books whether she has questions.*
