## 5. What Odile Left in Her

The storm came up the harbor road on a Tuesday, and Odile came with it.

Tamsin knew before anyone told her. She was at her desk in the Guild counting-house, third floor, a room with one salt-fogged window and a view of the roof of the tannery next door, when the ink in her well shivered. Not from her hand — she'd learned to tell that tremor apart by now, three weeks into honest wages and dishonest sleep. This was the other kind. The kind that meant a name was moving toward her through the rain.

She set down her pen. The ledger lay open beneath it, columns of bottled storms and borrowed years in her own neat hand, and for a moment she simply looked at it, the way you look at a door you already know is about to open.

"Thornbury." Verity Crane's voice came down the stairwell before the knock did. "Visitor."

"I know," Tamsin said, and heard how flat her voice went, and hated it.

She met Odile in the Guild's front hall, because there was nowhere else to meet her. The hall was all dark wood and older money, hung with the Guild's odd trophies: a ship's bell that rang backward, a taxidermied gull with two shadows, a framed first-edition contract signed in blood that had faded to brown. Rain ran off Odile's cloak onto the imported tile and pooled around her boots like something spilled.

Her mistress — former mistress, Tamsin corrected herself, and the correction hurt worse than the word — stood in the middle of the hall as if she owned it, which, knowing Odile, she had already priced.

"You look fed," Odile said.

It was not an apology. It was not anything. It was just Odile, gray-streaked hair pinned back with what Tamsin recognized, with a lurch, as one of her own hairpins — left behind in the flight, stolen along with everything else she'd taken. The ledger. The charm. The pin.

"You look wet," Tamsin said.

Something moved behind Odile's eyes that might have been approval. Then she turned to the hall at large — to Verity Crane descending the stairs with a ledger-clerk's measured tread, to Bram Hale lurking by the trophy wall pretending he wasn't listening, to the junior clerks frozen over their desks — and raised her voice into the register she'd used at market days and funerals, the one that carried without effort because it refused to be ignored.

"I've come to disown my apprentice."

* * *

Later, Tamsin would understand it was a performance, and a good one, and that its whole purpose was protection. But standing in the hall with the rain still dripping off Odile's hem, all Tamsin felt was the floor going out from under her.

"A thief," Odile announced, to the hall, to Saltmere, to whatever ears the crown kept in every port town's counting-houses. "She stole from me. A ledger, a luck-charm I paid good silver for, and thirteen years of my teaching, which is the only thing of mine she couldn't carry off but took anyway." She said it the way you read a bill of sale: itemized, final, no room for appeal. "I wash my hands of her. Whatever she does, whatever she is, it's hers alone now. I'll not be held answerable for it."

The word *is* landed like a hook. Whatever she **is**. Tamsin watched the junior clerks exchange glances. Watched Bram Hale go very still by the trophy wall. Watched Verity Crane's face do nothing at all, which was its own kind of message.

"You can't—" Tamsin started.

"I can." Odile turned to her then, and her voice dropped, and her eyes were the coldest thing in the room. "You're no daughter of mine. You're no apprentice of mine. You're nothing I made. Go on being nothing somewhere else."

Tamsin had fled this woman across half a marsh country. She had traded a charm for passage, slept in ditches, lied to gate-wardens, and never once cried, because crying was a debt and she didn't owe anybody. She wasn't going to cry now, in front of the Guild, in front of the woman who had taught her that.

So she did the only thing left. She straightened her spine, lifted her chin, and said, loud enough for the hall:

"Good. Then I'm free to say it: you were a bad teacher and a worse mother, and everything worth having in me, I taught myself."

The silence afterward had weight. Somewhere upstairs a clerk's chair creaked. Odile looked at her for a long moment — and there, just for a heartbeat, under the ice, Tamsin saw something flicker that might have been pride, or grief, or both wearing each other's coats.

Then Odile spat on the tile — deliberately, theatrically, between them — turned, and walked out into the rain.

* * *

Verity found her twenty minutes later in the counting-room, staring at the same column of numbers she'd been staring at since the hall. The ink had dried crooked where her hand had shaken.

"That," Verity said, "was either the worst thing I've seen done to an employee or the best thing I've seen done for one, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Neither can I," Tamsin said.

"Mm." Verity sat on the edge of the desk, uninvited, which was how she always sat. "Here's what I can tell you. By tomorrow noon, every crown informant in Saltmere will have heard that Odile Grieve publicly cast off her runaway apprentice. Which means that when the hunt's lists reach this city — and they will reach this city — your name arrives pre-disowned. A hedge-witch's castoff, no craft worth seizing, no master to interrogate. You become, officially, nothing."

Tamsin stared at her. "She did it on purpose."

"She did it loudly, in a room full of witnesses, at some cost to her own reputation among people who matter to her." Verity picked a speck of lint off her sleeve. "Odile Grieve has been playing the long game since before you were born. I'd take the protection and stop asking what it cost her, if I were you. Sentiment is expensive and she strikes me as a woman who balances her books."

But Tamsin wasn't listening anymore, because the door had opened again, and Odile had come back in — quietly this time, hood back, rain in her hair, looking suddenly every year of her age and several more.

"Not here," Odile said. "Somewhere with thin walls and thick doors."

* * *

They walked. Odile wouldn't take a carriage, wouldn't take the main streets, led them instead through the fish-market's back lanes where the awnings dripped and nobody important ever looked. She didn't speak until they reached a chapel of the Tide-Mother, abandoned, its tide-pool font gone green, its doors long since swollen shut — except Odile put her palm against the wood and murmured something too low to catch, and the doors remembered they were doors.

Inside smelled of salt and old prayers. Rain drummed the roof. Odile lit no candle; she seemed to prefer the gray.

"The hunt," she said, without preamble, "isn't royal work."

Tamsin's stomach dropped. "What?"

"The decree. The drownings. Everyone assumes it's crown policy — the king protecting his realm from hedge-craft, standard fear, standard fire." Odile turned a broken pew-stub with her boot. "I've read the decree, child. Actually read it, not listened to a crier mangle it. And the binding on it is hedge-magic. Marsh-work. My work. Someone wrote that decree the way I'd write a charm against blight — knotted intent, buried triggers, a name-thread running through the whole text. That's not court sorcery. Court sorcery is showy and symmetrical. This is ugly and personal."

Tamsin felt cold that had nothing to do with the chapel. "Someone at court knows hedge-craft."

"Someone at court knows *my* hedge-craft." Odile's voice went quiet, which was worse than loud. "Or close enough to fool me for a full page. And here's the part you're not going to want to hear, so I'll say it fast, the way you pull a splinter."

"Don't—"

"Your name is in it, Tamsin. Not on the lists yet. In the weave. There's a thread in that decree with your shape on it. Whoever wrote the hunt built it to find you specifically — eventually. Everything else is noise. The drownings, the criers, the terror in the harbor districts — that's the decoy-flush, driving the gifted into the open so the real quarry walks into the net on her own feet." Odile finally looked at her, and her eyes were tired and fierce and unbearably fond, and Tamsin wanted to hit her and hold onto her in equal measure. "You weren't fleeing when you ran from me, girl. You were being driven. And someone with a court seal and a marsh-tongue wants you delivered alive."

"Why?" The word came out small. "Why me? I'm nobody. I read names, that's all, I—"

"You read true names," Odile said. "Of impossible things. Do you understand how rare that is? Do you understand what a person who could read the true name of, say, a king could do?" She let that sit in the green-smelling silence. "Neither do I. Neither does whoever wrote that decree. But they think you can, and they want you found before you figure out what to point yourself at."

The rain filled the pause. Tamsin thought of the ledger in her satchel, the one she'd stolen believing it worthless, the one whose true names had gotten her hired by the Guild of Impossible Things. She thought of the vial of dragon-fire renewed monthly to a buyer she wasn't cleared to know. She thought of a signature that had changed, though she didn't know yet that she knew it.

"Come back with me," she said finally. "To the Guild. Verity will—"

"No." Odile said it gently, which was crueler than shouting. "If we're seen together after today, the disowning means nothing. I stay away from you, publicly, permanently. Privately—" she reached out and, for the first time in thirteen years, touched Tamsin's face, thumb brushing the cheekbone, brief as a struck match, "—privately, you remember that everything I did in that hall was a lie except the last part. You were never nothing. You were always the best thing I made, and I made you to leave me."

Tamsin's throat closed. Thirteen years of discipline, and it nearly broke on a wet chapel floor.

"Is there anything else?" she managed. "Anything I should—"

"One thing." Odile stepped back, and the fondness banked itself, and the hedge-witch came forward, practical and grim. "The decree's name-thread — the one shaped like you. It doesn't lead to the crown. It leads *through* the crown to somewhere else. I couldn't follow it far without it feeling me following. But whoever holds the other end knows your gift, knows your face, and knows you're in Saltmere." She pulled her hood up. "Trust the Guild as far as it's useful. Trust the huntsman less than that. Trust no one who's kind to you without a reason."

"And trust you?"

Odile smiled, and it was the old market-day smile, sharp as a gutting knife.

"Never," she said. "That's why it's safe."

Then she was gone, out the remembered doors, into the rain, leaving Tamsin alone in a dead chapel with a name-shaped thread pulling tight around her ribs and the sudden, terrible understanding that the hunt had never been hunting the gifted at all.

It had been hunting her. And somewhere above the drowned harbor, behind a borrowed face, something already knew she'd figured it out — because the rain on the chapel roof, when she listened properly, wasn't rain at all.

It was spelling her name.
