## 6. The Drowning of Wren Amsel

The criers came at dawn, and by noon the whole of Saltmere had turned out for a drowning.

Tamsin heard it first as noise — the particular roar a harbor town makes when something terrible is about to happen and everyone has decided to enjoy it. She was in the counting room with the winter ledgers spread around her like fallen leaves, and the sound came through the shutters and made the ink in her well tremble.

"Market's closed," Verity said from the doorway. She said it the way she said everything, flat and final, but Tamsin had learned to read Verity Crane the way she read everything else, in the true names under the surface words. *Market's closed* meant *stay inside*. It also meant *I am going down there*, because Verity was already pulling on her coat, and her face had the color of paper before it burns.

"What's happened?"

Verity paused with her hand on the latch. For a moment Tamsin thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "They took Wren Amsel."

The name went through Tamsin like a fishhook. Wren. The courier who had taught her which staircases in the Guildhall creaked and which didn't, who could run the seawall at low tide with a satchel of bottled storms on her back and laugh about the wind. Wren, who was nineteen, who chipped a tooth falling off the customs roof last spring and had refused to have it mended because the gap made her whistle through it when she laughed.

"Wren isn't—" Tamsin stopped. Started again. "Wren keeps her head down. She's careful."

"She's gifted." Verity's voice did not change at all, and that was how Tamsin knew how bad it was. "She lit a lamp for an old woman on Candle Lane without striking flint. In front of four witnesses. One of them sold her for two crowns and a pardon."

The roar outside swelled, and beneath it Tamsin could hear the bells now, all of them, every church in Saltmere ringing the pattern that meant *the crown's justice is done here today*.

"The decree says drowning," Verity said. "For the gift. By water, publicly, so the sea itself can witness the cleansing." She opened the door. "Stay in the counting room. That's not a kindness, Tamsin. It's arithmetic. You're new. No one knows your face yet, and I intend to keep it that way."

Then she was gone, and Tamsin stood alone among the ledgers with her heart going like a trapped bird.

She lasted four minutes.

* * *

Bram found the crowd already three deep along the harbor rail when he arrived, and he hated every single one of them, and he hated most that he understood them. He had stood in crowds like this once. He had been the reason for crowds like this once, standing on scaffolds in six different towns with a warrant in his hand and his face arranged into something solemn, while people cheered, because cheering was easier than thinking, and he had thought so too, right up until the day he stopped.

He pushed through to the rail. The tide was half out and the drowning-stool stood on the sand flats below the harbor steps, a chair bolted to a timber frame with iron rings, positioned where the returning tide would do the work the executioner couldn't be bothered with. Cheap. Efficient. The crown had refined the practice over the years until it required almost no one to be cruel on purpose. That was the trick of it, Bram thought. You built a machine where the cruelty was distributed so thin that no one had to carry more than a thimbleful, and then everyone could sleep.

Two huntsmen in royal blue flanked the stool. Between them, wrists bound, stood a slight figure in a courier's oilskin coat, and even from here Bram could see she wasn't crying. Her chin was up. Her hair had come loose and the harbor wind was throwing it around, and she was looking out at the water with an expression Bram recognized from the inside of a mirror, years ago, on the one occasion he'd been close enough to see a condemned person's face properly: she was memorizing it. Filling herself up with the sea so that whatever came after, some part of her would still be looking at something beautiful.

A priest of the Gilded Tide was reading the decree. His voice carried badly over the wind, but the crowd supplied the parts everyone knew. *Anathema. Unclean. Cast out from among the honest.* And then the part that had changed since Bram's day, the part that made his stomach drop through the boards of the harbor:

"In the name of Aldric, King of the Nine Shores, Keeper of the Flame, Beloved of the Deep—"

Beloved of the Deep. That was new. That title hadn't existed when Bram wore the blue coat. Someone at court had added it, the way you'd add a line to a ledger, and the crowd had swallowed it whole because crowds swallow anything with enough gold thread on it.

He looked up at the palace on its hill above the town, white walls catching the noon light, and felt the old heresy stir in him, the one that had gotten him cast out: *the seal is wrong*. He'd known it the moment the criers unrolled the decree. The king's signature had changed — not aged, not slanted with illness, but *changed*, the way a forged hand changes, careful where the real hand was careless. He had spent ten years reading warrants. He knew what a king's tired hand looked like. This was not it.

On the sand flats, the huntsmen were binding Wren Amsel to the stool.

The crowd began to sing.

It was a hymn, an old one, about the sea taking back what was impure, and it was sung in every harbor town on the Nine Shores, and Bram had sung it himself, once, with a warrant in his pocket and a family's door under his fist. The sound of it rose off the water and came back doubled, and Wren Amsel, nineteen years old, whistled-toothed, fastest runner in the Guild of the Gilded Root, lifted her head and sang the next verse louder than the crowd.

Bram's hands closed on the rail hard enough to hurt.

There was nothing to do. That was the truth of it, the arithmetic Verity would have appreciated. Two huntsmen, forty yards of open sand, a crowd of thousands, and the tide coming in whether anyone sang or not. Any man who moved would only add a second body to the count, and the Guild had rules, and the rules said the Guild survived, and survival meant standing at rails like this one with your hands full of nothing.

He stayed. He watched. He counted the minutes until the water reached her waist, because someone should count, because no one else in this entire singing crowd was going to.

* * *

Tamsin didn't remember deciding to go down. She remembered the counting room, and then she remembered the smell of the crowd — tar and wet wool and fried onions, someone selling roasted chestnuts three feet from the rail as if this were a festival — and then she was pressed against the harbor steps with Verity's grip biting into her arm, and Verity's voice in her ear, low and furious: "I told you to stay."

"You can't let them—"

"I can't stop them." Verity's face was gray. "Look at me. There are two hundred Guild members in this crowd right now, and every one of them is going to stand here and watch, because that is what the Guild *is*. We move goods. We don't move crowns. If we drew steel on the harbor today, tomorrow there'd be soldiers in the Guildhall and every name in my ledgers would be worth a pardon to somebody." Her grip tightened. "So we watch. And we remember. And we pay our debts. Do you understand me? We *remember*."

But Tamsin wasn't listening anymore, because they were leading Wren to the stool, and Wren had seen her.

Across forty yards of wet sand, across a wall of singing bodies, Wren Amsel's eyes found Tamsin's and held them, and Tamsin saw her smile. With the chipped tooth. The gap-whistle smile, the one that meant *race you to the seawall*, the one that meant *the ledgers are wrong, come look*, the one that meant *you're one of us now, book-girl*.

Wren mouthed something. Tamsin read her lips through the hymn, through the wind, through the roaring in her own skull.

*Don't look away.*

And Tamsin, who had spent her whole life being told to keep her gift hidden, keep her head down, keep her name small — Tamsin did not look away. She stared back with everything she had, pouring it across the sand like light, and Wren nodded once, satisfied, and turned to face the sea.

The tide came in.

It came in the way tides do, indifferent and enormous, filling the channels first, then the flats, climbing the timber frame inch by patient inch. The huntsmen withdrew to the steps. The priest finished his hymn and folded his hands. The crowd sang the closing verses, the ones about the sea keeping what it loves, and somewhere behind Tamsin a woman said to her companion, "Well, it's cleaner this way, isn't it, no blood on the sand," in the tone of someone discussing laundry.

The water reached Wren's shoulders. Then her chin. She never stopped looking at the horizon, never once turned toward the shore, toward the people, toward any of it. She faced the open water like a courier facing a road.

And Tamsin, watching, felt her gift move without her permission — the way it always did, the way it had with the ledger, the way it did with anything written or named or bound. Because the sea had a name. Everything had a name, if you could read it, and the sea's true name was vast and cold and older than kings, and for one terrible moment Tamsin felt it turn its attention toward her, curious, the way a mountain might notice an ant that spoke.

*Stop*, she told herself. *You can't. You can't command the tide. You'd drown the whole harbor.*

She could feel it anyway. The shape of the words. The way the sea's name would sit in her mouth like a stone, and the way the water would listen, and the way it would not stop with Wren — it would take the fishing fleet, the steps, the crowd, the town. A gift was not a wish. Odile had told her that a hundred times, usually while hitting her knuckles with a switch: *the power doesn't care what you meant, girl, only what you said*.

So Tamsin stood on the steps and held the sea's name behind her teeth like broken glass, and the water climbed, and climbed, and covered Wren Amsel's face, and the crowd sang the last verse, and the bells rang, and it was done in less time than it takes to boil a kettle.

Afterward the crowd dispersed quickly, the way crowds do, already talking about supper. The chestnut seller packed up his brazier. Two men argued cheerfully about a football match. The stool stood empty on the flats, waiting for the next time, and gulls landed on its frame as if it were any other piece of harbor furniture.

Verity's hand was still on Tamsin's arm. "Come away," Verity said quietly. "There's work."

"No." Tamsin heard her own voice and barely recognized it. It was very steady. Something had happened inside her, some structural event, a keel laid down in deep water. "No, I don't think there is. Not the kind you mean."

"Tamsin—"

"They cheered." She turned and looked at Verity, and Verity, mistress-of-coin, reader of men, took half a step back. "They brought their children. They'll tell the story tonight at supper and make it funny. And the Guild will write it in a ledger — I know you will, I've seen the entries — *one courier lost, operations unaffected* — and that will be the whole of it. Operations unaffected." Her hands were shaking now, finally, but her voice stayed level, and it frightened her a little, how level. "My father drowned for the same reason. Did you know that? In a river, in a village nobody remembers. And everyone who watched went home to supper too."

Verity said nothing. Around them the harbor emptied, and the light was going long and gold, and the empty stool sat on the flats with the gulls on it.

"I used to think the law was the thing that kept people safe," Tamsin said. "That if you just stayed small enough, quiet enough, the law would pass over you like weather. But the law wrote this. The law built the stool. So fine." She looked out at the water, at the place where the frame stood, and the sea's name was still in her, vast and cold, and she held it now not like glass but like a tool. "Then justice has to be built by hand. Outside the law. By people willing to get their hands wet. And I think—" she stopped, steadied, started again, "I think the Guild has to decide which side of the rail it stands on. Before someone decides for it."

Verity studied her for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she filed it; Tamsin could practically hear the ledger entry. "You sound like someone I used to know," Verity said at last. "Before the Guild made her sensible." She turned toward the steps. "Come. There's a requisition came in this morning I want you to look at. Palace seal. Dragon-fire, monthly, as usual — but the amount's changed. Doubled."

Tamsin followed her up from the water's edge, and behind them the tide finished its work alone, and the gulls screamed, and the bells fell silent one by one across the city, and in the counting room that night Tamsin opened the palace requisition with steady hands and read, in the true names beneath the ink, the first word of something that had been hiding in the Guild's books for years:

*Hunger.*
