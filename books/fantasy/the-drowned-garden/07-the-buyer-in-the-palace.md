## 7. The Buyer in the Palace

Bram Hale had been inside the palace twice before in his life. The first time he was nineteen and being decorated for a hanging well done. The second time he was twenty-six and being stripped of his badge for refusing another. Both times, he remembered thinking the place smelled like lilies over rot.

It still did.

He came in through the servants' gate at the river side, carrying a crate of Saltmere oysters on one shoulder and a forged chit in his breast pocket. The chit was Verity's work — she'd charged him for it, of course, forty crowns and a look that said she expected him to die and wanted her investment back either way. The Guild needed to know who was buying dragon-fire by the vial, month after month, year after year. And the only place that question could be answered was behind these walls, tonight, at the Feast of the Turning Tide, where every noble house in the capital would be drunk and masked and careless.

Bram had spent eleven years learning how men moved when they were hunting. He'd spent three more learning how they moved when they were afraid. Tonight he intended to use both lessons to walk into the brightest room in the kingdom and not be seen at all.

The kitchen swallowed him without ceremony. Steam, shouting, a scullery boy who took the oysters and never looked up. Bram set down the empty crate, pulled a serving coat off a hook, and became furniture. It was a skill no one taught you in the huntsman corps — or rather, they taught it backwards. They taught you to see the man pretending to be furniture. Unlearning it had taken years, and even now it cost him something, walking slow when every nerve screamed *move*, keeping his eyes down when they wanted to sweep the room.

*You are nobody,* he told himself, joining the line of servers carrying trays toward the great hall. *You are a coat with legs.*

The doors opened, and the light hit him like a wave.

* * *

The great hall of the Winter Palace had been built by a king who wanted his guests to feel small. Bram knew its history the way he knew most ugly things — from the inside. Vaulted ceiling painted with constellations that didn't match any real sky. Columns of white marble veined with gold, so that the whole room seemed lit from within by veins of fire. A thousand candles. A thousand masks.

The masque was in full flood. Lords and ladies in gilded shapes — stags, swans, suns, ships — turned through figures on the dance floor while musicians played something old and minor from a gallery above. Servants wove between them with wine. At the far end, raised on a dais beneath a canopy of cloth-of-gold, sat the royal box.

Bram had seen the king once before, from a distance, at a review of the hunt. That had been nine years ago, early in the new reign's vigor, and the man on the reviewing stand had been broad-shouldered, loud-voiced, famous for riding down the coast road in a day. King Aldous the Restored, they called him. The king who came back from the fever. The king who lived.

Bram worked his way along the wall with a tray of spiced wine he never intended to deliver, and looked at the king.

Something in his chest went cold and quiet, the way it used to go before a kill.

Aldous sat enthroned beneath the canopy, crowned, robed in ermine, his scepter upright in his fist. From thirty feet away, in candlelight, through the blur of a servant's lowered eyes, he was perfect. Too perfect. That was the first wrongness, and it was small: kings age badly. They bloat, they stoop, they grow jowls and rheumy eyes. This Aldous was fifty-eight by Bram's count and had the face of a coin — smooth, struck, unchanging. The same face, Bram realized with a slow crawl of ice up his spine, as the face on the coins. Not similar. Identical. As if the mint had copied the man yesterday and the man had not dared to change since.

*Coin-face.* He filed the thought away and kept moving.

The second wrongness came when the king stood.

The court was toasting something — a betrothal, a victory, some fiction — and Aldous rose to acknowledge it, arms spreading in benediction, and for half a heartbeat his shadow stayed seated.

Bram stopped walking. A lady in a moth-mask nearly clipped the tray; he murmured an apology and drifted on, but his heart had begun to hammer in a way he hadn't felt since the drownings started, since the decree, since the night he'd read the hunt's writ and known the seal was wrong.

Lamplight lies. He knew that. Shadows stretch and stutter; a hundred candles make a hundred shadows and none of them honest. He made himself find the king's true shadow — the one cast by the great candelabrum directly above the dais, the cleanest light in the hall — and watch it.

The king raised his goblet. His shadow raised its goblet a half-second later.

Not stretched. Not refracted. *Late.*

Bram had hunted a skin-walker in the fen country once, early days, back when he still believed the crown's monsters were all on the other side of the law. He remembered how it moved — a hair behind its own body, like a puppet whose strings had too much slack. He'd put a bolt through it and told himself the crown paid him to do exactly that, and slept fine for a week, and then hadn't slept properly for a year.

The thing on the dais wore a king the way that thing had worn a ferryman. Badly. Beautifully. With slack in the strings.

* * *

He needed closer. He hated himself for needing closer, because every instinct he owned was screaming the opposite direction, but Verity hadn't sent him to come back with a feeling. She'd sent him for names. And names, Tamsin Thornbury had told him just last week, tapping the ledger with one blunt finger, are written in the details.

"The buyer signs," she'd said. "Everything signs. Even fear signs. Find me a signature."

So Bram found a reason to cross the floor. A dropped fan near the dais steps — he retrieved it, bowed, delivered it to a duchess whose perfume could have curdled milk. Then a spilled cup. Then a tray that needed carrying to the royal box itself, because the proper server had been waylaid, and Bram simply appeared at the right moment wearing the right coat, and no one questions a man doing work.

Up close, the king was worse.

The candlelight that flattered everyone else found nothing to flatter here. There was no pore, no scar, no stubble, no line of sweat on a man robed in fur under a thousand candles in a crowded hall. The skin had the finish of glazed porcelain. And the eyes —

The eyes were the worst part, because they were *kind*. Warm brown, crinkled at the corners, the eyes of a grandfather in a storybook. They swept the court with love. They rested on dancing children with delight. And they did not blink often enough. Bram counted. Once in four minutes. A man that dry-eyed would be weeping with pain.

"Your Majesty honors us," simpered the lord beside the throne, and the king smiled, and the smile arrived like weather — correct, warm, and utterly without anyone home behind it.

Then the king spoke, blessing the feast, and Bram heard the voice and felt the floor tilt.

Because he *knew* that voice. Not the king's voice — he'd heard Aldous speak at the review, a big coastal barrow of a voice, salt and gravel. This voice was lighter, sweeter, pitched to carry like a bell. But under the sweetness there was a rhythm he recognized, a cadence, a way of ending sentences on a rising breath —

It was the cadence of the criers. Of the hunt's proclamation. Of the decree that had put Wren Amsel in the harbor water. The voice of the hunt wasn't an imitation of the king's voice. The hunt's voice was this voice. The king was the copy.

His hands were steady. He noticed that distantly, the way you notice rain out a window. Eleven years of hunting had built a man whose hands didn't shake, and he supposed he should be grateful, because shaking hands would get him killed in the next ten minutes.

Because the king turned and looked at him.

* * *

There is a moment in every hunt — Bram had explained it to green hunters a dozen times, back when he was the one doing the explaining — when the quarry knows. Not hears you, not smells you. *Knows.* The woods go still in a particular way, and the animal looks directly at the place where you are, and you understand that everything you believed about your own invisibility was a story you told yourself.

The king's kind brown eyes crossed the hall, passed over a hundred masked faces, and settled on a servant holding a tray of wine.

And stayed.

One second. Two. Around them the court danced and laughed and poured champagne down its gilded throat, and the King of the Realm looked at Bram Hale with an expression of gentle, bottomless attention, like a fisherman studying a fly on the water.

Bram bowed. Deeply. Servant-deep, eyes down, the tray held out slightly as offering. His heart was a war drum. He kept his breathing in the long, slow rhythm they'd drilled into him for tracking — four counts in, six counts out — because panic has a smell, and whatever this thing was, he no longer trusted it to be limited to the five senses.

"You may rise," the king said, pleasantly, to someone. Perhaps to him. Perhaps to no one.

Bram rose partway and did not meet those eyes again. He retreated the way water retreats — unhurried, inevitable, finding the path of least notice. Back along the dais. Back past the columns. Back into the crowd, which closed around him like a wound closing, and only then did he let himself breathe.

But he'd gotten what he came for. Better than a signature.

While bowing, eyes down, he'd had the dais before him — and the king's left hand resting on the armrest, and on the fourth finger of that hand, half-hidden by the scepter's grip, a ring. Not a signet. Not a wedding band. A band of black metal, dull as cooled iron, and set in it a sigil Bram had seen exactly once before and hoped never to see again: a circle broken by a vertical stroke, like an eye shut by a blade.

Hedge-mark. Old hedge-mark. The kind Odile's people scratched on doorposts to mean *a name is kept here.*

The crown was wearing a king whose name was being *kept* somewhere. Bound, stored, signed for — like inventory. Like a ledger entry.

*Tamsin,* he thought. *God help us both, you were right.*

* * *

He should have left then. Any sensible man would have left then. But the ring had a maker's mark on its inner face — he'd caught the glint of it, tiny, engraved — and marks meant makers, and makers meant names, and he was already damned, so he drifted toward the antechamber behind the dais where the king's household gathered between appearances, and he listened.

Servants' gossip is the true chronicle of any palace. In ten minutes, leaning against a wall with an empty tray, Bram learned that the king had not eaten in public in three years; that his physicians visited nightly and left puzzled; that the Queen had "gone to the seaside" two winters ago and no one could say which seaside; that the king walked the same corridor at the same hour every night, alone, though the guards were forbidden to follow; and that once, a chambermaid who saw him without his crown had been dismissed so suddenly her family couldn't find her.

"He talks to himself," whispered a laundress, gleeful with fright. "My cousin heard him. Through the door. Only it weren't talking like a man talks. It was like — reciting. Over and over. Same words."

"What words?" someone asked.

She glanced around, lowered her voice to almost nothing. "*Half* words. Like he couldn't finish them. Like something was missing."

Bram's blood went thin and fast.

Half a name. The thing wearing the king didn't have all of it. Whatever bound Aldous — whatever contract, whatever fire-fed binding kept a dead man walking in borrowed shape — was incomplete. The entity recited night after night, trying to close a circle it couldn't close, missing a piece.

And a binding with a missing piece is a lock with a broken tooth.

He was still turning that over when the doors to the antechamber opened and the king came through, unmasked now, trailing courtiers, and the whole room bent like wheat. Bram bent with them, forehead nearly to the floor, heart slamming, and through the curtain of his own hair he watched the royal shoes cross the marble toward him.

They stopped.

Silk hem. Gold thread. The scent of lilies over rot, thick enough to taste.

"Your tray is empty," the king observed, in that sweet bell of a voice, to the top of Bram's head.

"Yes, Majesty." His own voice came out flat and servile and perfect. Years of practice. Thank the dark for years of practice.

"A servant should always be carrying something," the king said musingly. "An empty-handed man has nothing to offer. And yet—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table, "—everyone offers me something, don't they? Every soul in this room. Whether they know it or not."

"Yes, Majesty."

"Look at me, oyster-man."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere a musician had stopped playing. Bram lifted his head and met the kind brown eyes, and let his face do what a servant's face does — blank, respectful, faintly stupid — while behind it every survival instinct he possessed screamed like a gull.

The king studied him for a long moment. Then, softly, almost tenderly, almost *sadly*, he said:

"I know your face."

"No, Majesty. Common face. Oyster-man's face."

"I knew your father's face too," the king said, and smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near the eyes, which had gone still as pond water. "Huntsmen. You all have the same face. The loyal ones and the traitors. It's the same face, in the end."

The words dropped through Bram like stones through deep water. *He knows me. He remembers the corps. He remembers—*

And then the king's gaze flicked — just flicked, a bird's movement — to the space beside Bram, to the empty air at his shoulder, and the pond-water eyes narrowed a fraction, and the king murmured, so quietly that only Bram and perhaps God could hear it:

"There's a name following you, oyster-man. Whose is it?"

Bram's mind went white. Because he knew — in the marrow way, the hunter's way, the way that needs no evidence — that the thing was not asking about a person. It was asking about the ledger. About Tamsin's gift, the reading-of-names, the true-name sense the crown drowned people for. Something of hers clung to him — a scrap of paper, a charm, a word she'd written in the margin of the map she'd drawn him of the palace — and the thing on the throne could *smell* it.

"Mine, Majesty," he said. "Just mine."

"Mm," said the king. "Keep it close, then. Names are heavy. They drag a man down." The kind eyes warmed again, the mask sliding smoothly back into place, the grandfather returning. "Like stones. Like drowning." He raised his voice to its bell-bright public register. "Wine for this good fellow! An empty tray is a sad sight at my feast!"

Laughter rippled. Courtiers echoed it. A page came running with a decanter, and the moment broke like surface tension, and Bram was nobody again, a coat with legs, a common face, bowing and backing away while the King of the Realm turned to bless a blushing debutante as if he had never once looked into a servant's soul and weighed it.

* * *

He didn't stop moving until he was three streets from the palace, hood up, in the rain, in the dark, with the harbor lights smearing gold across black water. Then he stopped in the lee of a shuttered chandlery and put his hand against the wet brick and breathed, four counts in, six counts out, until his stomach agreed to stay where it was.

Half a name. The entity didn't have the king's whole name. It recited fragments nightly, hungry, unfinished. It had smelled Tamsin's gift on him like smoke on a coat. And it wore a hedge-binding ring on its finger, iron-black, marked *a name is kept here* — which meant the true name existed, written or spoken or stored, somewhere, in some vault or ledger or grave.

The Guild sold the fire that fed it. Month after month. Vial after vial. Verity's books would show the payments; Tamsin's gift might read what the ledgers hid. If anyone could find where a dead king's name was kept, it was the runaway apprentice with the knack for impossible things.

Bram pushed off the wall and walked toward Saltmere, toward the Guildhall, toward the lamp he knew burned late in the counting room, and the rain came down harder, and he found he was almost running.

Because he'd heard the thing recite too — not through a door, but standing in front of it, close enough to touch. One fragment, murmured like a prayer, like a hunger, like a key tried in a lock over and over for years:

*"Al... Al-d..."*

Half a name. And Bram Hale, heretic, traitor, huntsman, carried it through the rain like a coal he was afraid to drop — knowing that somewhere in the Guild's ledgers, in a dead man's entries, the other half was waiting to be read.
