## 8. Entries in a Dead Man's Hand

The Guild's archive lived in the cellar beneath the counting house, and it smelled the way old money smells — tallow, dust, and something faintly sweet, like fruit going over. Tamsin had been given a lamp, a stool, and a stack of ledgers that predated her grandmother, and told by Verity Crane to "reconcile the historical accounts before the solstice audit."

Reconcile. As if numbers were sheep that had wandered.

She started at the beginning because she didn't know any other way to work. Odile had taught her bookkeeping the way other mistresses taught prayers: *the ledger never lies, but the hand that writes it always does.* Read past the ink, girl. Read what the pen was afraid to say.

So she read.

The earliest volumes were beautiful in the way old things are beautiful — leather gone soft as earlobes, margins crowded with a clerk's tidy annotations, entries in a copperplate so even it looked printed. Bottled storm, one gale, sold to the Harbormaster of Saltmere, year of the Long Frost. Borrowed years, three, sold privately, buyer unnamed. A memory, forty years of marriage, purchased by a widow who could not bear to keep it and could not bear to lose it. Tamsin read that entry twice and had to put the ledger down for a moment, her throat tight, because she understood the widow completely.

*Focus,* she told herself. *You are a bookkeeper now. Bookkeepers do not weep over inventory.*

But her gift kept pulling at her like a dog on a lead. Every entry had a true name folded inside it, the way a letter has a name folded inside an envelope, and when she let her attention rest on the words they opened for her like hands. The storms were named. The years were named. Even the memories had names, small and private and unbearably sad.

And every seventh entry or so, in ledgers spanning four decades, there was a payment that had no name at all.

Not redacted. Not smudged. Simply blank — a line item reading *monthly retainer, special inventory, per standing contract*, with a sum attached and nothing else. No buyer. No description. No true name she could read, because there was nothing written there to read. It was a hole shaped like a transaction, repeated faithfully on the first of every month for thirty-one years.

Tamsin set down her lamp and looked at the hole.

Thirty-one years. She did some arithmetic on the back of a scrap of wrapping paper. Thirty-one years was longer than she had been alive. It was longer than most apprenticeships, most marriages, most grudges. Someone had been paying the Guild of the Gilded Root every single month since before Tamsin could walk, for an item so secret it couldn't be named even in a ledger that named everything else.

"Special inventory," she said aloud, just to hear how foolish it sounded. The cellar didn't laugh.

* * *

It took her two more days to find the seam, because Verity kept sending her actual work — invoices to copy, disputes to arbitrate between a storm-broker and a year-lender who both claimed the same thunderstorm — and because the answer, once she found it, was hiding in the most boring volume in the room.

The Guild's own minutes. Not sales. Meetings.

She hadn't meant to read them. They were bound in plain gray boards and shelved with the tax records, and tax records are where curiosity goes to die. But she was cross-checking the blank payments against Guild expenses — trying to find what the money bought, if not from the buyer's side then from the seller's — and there it was, buried in the minutes of a meeting held in the autumn of a year whose number made her sit very still.

*Item the third:* the minutes said, in that same flawless copperplate. *The Master reports the special inventory requires renewal. Discussion of sourcing. Motion carried.*

Sourcing. Renewal. As though they were discussing candles.

Tamsin turned pages with fingers that had begun to shake. The minutes ran year after year, and the special inventory appeared in them again and again, always obliquely, always in that bloodless committee language. *Renewal secured. Cost increased; motion carried. The Master cautions discretion regarding the special inventory. Motion carried.* And then, in a meeting held in midwinter of the year everything changed:

*Item the first: His Majesty has recovered from his fever, praise be recorded. The city bells rang for three days. Item the second: the special inventory is henceforth to be renewed monthly without further discussion. Motion carried.*

Tamsin read it four times.

His Majesty has recovered from his fever.

She knew that fever. Everyone knew that fever, the way everyone knows a famous murder. The old king — Bram's king, the young queen's father, the man on the coins — had sickened in midwinter thirty-one years ago with a sweating sickness that killed half his household, and the kingdom had mourned him for nine days, and then he had risen from his bed on the tenth day and walked out onto the balcony, thin as a winter branch but alive, alive, and the country had celebrated itself hoarse. Miraculous recovery. God's mercy. The priests still preached about it.

Tamsin pulled the sales ledgers toward her and laid them open beside the minutes, decade by decade, and made a timeline on the wrapping paper in her neat apprentice hand.

Fever. Nine days of mourning. Bells.

First monthly payment: dated the first day of the month following the recovery.

Second payment: one month later. Third: the month after that. On and on, unbroken, through plague years and war years and the year the harbor froze, through the deaths of two Guild Masters and the appointment of a third, through Tamsin's own birth, which happened somewhere in the middle of those faithful little entries without ever knowing they were being written.

The payments began the month the king came back from the dead.

Her lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick with hands that would not quite obey her, and the cellar seemed suddenly colder, and very far underground, and full of paper.

Because here was the thing her gift showed her, the thing she couldn't unknow once she'd seen it: the blank entries weren't just unnamed. They were *unnamable*. When she looked at them the way Odile had taught her to look — past the ink, into the thing itself — the blankness looked back. It was not an absence of information. It was information wearing the shape of an absence. Something in those transactions had been deliberately, powerfully unwritten, and only one kind of magic she knew of could unwrite a thing in a ledger and make the ledger agree to forget.

Hedge-magic. Binding-work. The kind Odile did. The kind the crown had declared punishable by drowning.

Someone had used the forbidden craft to hide these payments. Which meant someone with the gift had worked for the crown — or been taken by it — and their work sat here in the dark, month after month, buying something the Guild called *special inventory*, for a buyer who could not be named, starting the very month a dead man got up and walked.

Tamsin thought about the vial. She'd seen it only once, during her second week, when Verity had shown her the crown jewels of the inventory: bottled storms ranked like soldiers, decades of borrowed years sleeping in their cradles of glass, and at the end, alone in a case lined with black velvet, a vial no bigger than a thumb, holding a fire that burned without heat and gave off light the color of the last coals of a dying world.

Dragon-fire. The last of it. Renewed monthly, Verity had said, by a mysterious buyer.

Renewed. Not replenished — *renewed*. The word had slid past her then. Now it caught in her chest like a fishhook. You renew a subscription. You renew a lease. You renew a life.

You do not renew fire unless the fire is being spent.

She sat in the cold cellar with her timeline spread out before her, and the pattern sang its terrible song, and she felt the thrill of it move through her — the pure, clean joy of the puzzle closing, of chaos becoming order, of the hidden thing stepping out of the shadows and showing its face. This was what she'd been made for. This was the gift working as it was meant to work, better than it had ever worked, reading truth out of a dead man's careful hand.

And underneath the thrill, cold as the cellar floor, the implication unfolded itself one wing at a time.

If the payments began when the king recovered, then either the recovering king had started buying something from the Guild — or something else had started buying it *for* him. Something that needed dragon-fire every month, forever, the way a lamp needs oil. Something that had been alive — sustained, fed, *kept* — for thirty-one years on the last fire in the world.

Something that signed the king's name.

Bram had said the seal was wrong. He'd said it quietly, guiltily, the night the criers came, the way a man admits to a heresy he's been carrying alone: *I served under that signature for eleven years. That isn't it. The letters are right but the hand is wrong.* She'd thought it was grief talking, or old bitterness. Huntsmen were sentimental about their kings.

But Bram had been right. Of course he'd been right. The king had died in his bed thirty-one years ago, and the kingdom had gotten a miracle instead of a funeral, and every month since, on the first of the month, a payment had crossed this desk in the dark to keep the miracle walking.

Tamsin pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars. She wanted very badly to be wrong. She ran the check again anyway, because Odile had taught her that too: *hope is not a method, girl. Verify.* The dates held. They held perfectly. Thirty-one years of perfect, damning, monthly fidelity.

The worst part — the part that made her stomach turn over slowly, like a boat taking water — was realizing who else must know. The Guild Masters across four decades had minuted it, obliquely, in committee-speak, but they had known. *Discretion regarding the special inventory. Motion carried.* Verity knew; Verity knew everything that touched the coin. The Guild hadn't been selling to the crown.

The Guild had been keeping the crown alive. Or keeping the thing in the crown alive. There was a difference, and Tamsin suspected the difference was the whole point, and she suspected also that the difference was exactly what she was not supposed to notice.

She thought of Wren Amsel, drowned in the harbor while the crowd cheered, for the crime of having a gift like hers. She thought of the hunt proclamations nailed to every door, written — Odile had said — in hedge-magic, binding-work, the forbidden craft wielded by the very office that forbade it. She thought of the drownings, the lists, the criers, the fear rolling through Saltmere like fog.

All of it built on top of this. All of it, every gallows and every decree, serving a dead man's chair.

And the Guild — her Guild, the shelter that might have been a trap, the first place anyone had ever hired her instead of hanging her — had been the quiet partner in it for thirty-one years, taking its coin on the first of every month and writing *special inventory* in a dead man's hand.

Tamsin blew out her lamp and sat in the dark for a long time, because the dark felt honest.

When she finally relit it, she did three things, quickly, the way you pull a splinter. She copied the timeline onto a fresh sheet in cipher — Odile's cipher, the one the old woman had drilled into her with raps across the knuckles, certain it would never matter. She hid the original minutes back among the tax records, exactly one finger-width out of alignment, so she would know if anyone else found them. And she wrote, at the bottom of her ciphered page, a question she could not yet answer:

*Who renews the fire — and what happens the month they stop?*

Then she heard footsteps on the cellar stairs. Slow ones. Deliberate ones. The kind Verity Crane made when she wanted you to hear her coming.

Tamsin closed her ciphered page inside her sleeve and turned, arranging her face into the blank competence of a good bookkeeper, as the door at the top of the stairs opened and Verity stood silhouetted against the lamplight above, looking down into the dark at her.

"The solstice audit," Verity said, "has moved up. His Majesty's buyer will collect early this year."

She smiled, and it did not reach her eyes.

"And Tamsin — I'll need you to witness the renewal."
