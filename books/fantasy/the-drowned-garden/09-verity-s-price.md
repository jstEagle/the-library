## 9. Verity's Price

Verity Crane had three rules for running the Guild of the Gilded Root, and she had never broken any of them.

One: never let a client see you sweat. Two: never lend what you cannot afford to lose. Three: never, under any circumstances, tell the whole truth to anyone — not your couriers, not your partners, not the mirror.

She was about to break rule three in her own counting room, at midnight, with rain hammering the shutters like a creditor who wouldn't take no for an answer. She was about to do it because of a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice with ink-stained fingers and eyes that saw too much, and because Bram Hale stood by the door looking like a man who had already decided how he'd die and found the arrangement acceptable.

"Lock it," Verity said.

Bram shot the bolt. The sound was small and final, like a coin dropping into an empty purse.

Tamsin Thornbury sat across the counting table from her with the Guild's oldest ledgers stacked between them, and she did not look afraid. That was the problem. Everyone looked afraid in this room eventually — clients, couriers, once even a Guildmaster who'd thought himself untouchable. Fear was the tax everyone paid on knowing too much, and Verity collected it the way she collected everything else.

The girl just waited. Patient as interest accruing.

"You've been in my archives," Verity said. "Every vault. Every sealed shelf."

"I keep the books now." Tamsin's voice was level. "You gave me the keys."

"I gave you the *low* shelves."

"The low shelves connect to the high ones. That's rather the point of books." Tamsin laid one hand flat on the oldest ledger — forty years old, its spine cracked, its cover stained with something Verity preferred to think of as tea. "Vial of dragon-fire, one measure, delivered monthly, buyer anonymous, payment drawn on an account that doesn't exist in any bank I can find. It started forty years ago. It hasn't missed a month. Not through floods, not through wars, not through the plague year when half your couriers were dying in the quarantines. Do you know what kind of buyer pays without fail through a plague?"

Verity said nothing. Rule one.

"A buyer who dies if you stop," Tamsin finished softly. "A buyer who *cannot afford* to die."

Outside, the rain redoubled. Somewhere in the harbor, the tide was coming in over the drowning-stones, where they'd put Wren Amsel last week, and Verity made herself not think about that, the way she'd made herself not think about a great many things for forty years.

"Bram," she said, without turning her head, "how much did you tell her?"

"Nothing." He leaned against the door, arms crossed, scarred hands quiet. "She didn't need telling. She reads things, Verity. You knew that when you hired her instead of hanging her. You hired a knife and now you're surprised it has an edge."

"I hired a bookkeeper."

"You hired a girl who can read the true names of impossible things, in a city where that gets you drowned, because you needed someone who could read the true names of impossible things." His voice was flat, but there was something underneath it — old guilt wearing new clothes. "Don't insult both of us."

Verity looked at Tamsin for a long moment. The lamp guttered, steadied. Forty years of careful arithmetic sat between them, and the girl had done what no auditor, no rival guild, no crown inspector had ever managed: she'd found the pattern.

Well. If the truth was going to come out anyway, Verity had always believed it should come out on her terms, at her price, with her hand on the scale.

Rule three, then. Just this once.

"Sit properly," she said. "This will take a while, and if you interrupt me, I'll start again from the beginning, and I promise you'll enjoy it less each time."

* * *

"Forty-one years ago," Verity began, "there was a different king on the throne. Aldric the Fourth, they called him the Winter King, though not fondly. He'd ruled nineteen years and the kingdom was — I want to be precise here, because precision matters — the kingdom was dying. Not metaphorically. The harvests had failed six years running. The rivers ran thin. The old magic that kept the marshes drained and the orchards fruitful and the storms off the coast — it was failing everywhere, all at once, like blood leaving a body. The learned men called it the Unraveling. Farmers called it God turning his back. The crown called it taxes, because the crown calls everything taxes."

Tamsin's mouth twitched. Good. A girl who could still smile during the ugly parts might survive them.

"The king took a fever in his thirty-ninth year. The physicians gave him a month. The priests gave him less. And then—" She turned the oldest ledger around and tapped the entry. October, forty-one years past. One vial. First delivery. "—he recovered. Miraculously, the chronicles say. Overnight, the chronicles say. He rose from his sickbed pale but hale, and within a year the harvests came back. Within five, the rivers ran full. The Unraveling stopped. People wept in the streets. They called it the Restoration."

"And it wasn't," Tamsin said quietly.

"It was bought." Verity met her eyes and did not flinch, because flinching was for people who hadn't paid for the right to look away and chosen not to. "Something answered the fever. Something came into the king — or perhaps the king went somewhere else and something came back wearing him; the accounts differ, and the only witness who might have known the difference died inconveniently soon after. What is certain is this: whatever sits on the throne now is sustained. Maintained. Fed. Once a month, every month, for forty-one years, the Guild of the Gilded Root has delivered one measure of dragon-fire to a palace servant who does not speak, who pays from an account that draws on nothing, and who never once has asked for credit."

The rain filled the silence.

"Why?" Tamsin asked. "Why would the Guild — why would anyone agree to that? Keeping a thing like that alive. Feeding it."

"Because of what happened before we agreed." Verity rose and walked to the window, mostly so she wouldn't have to watch their faces. It was easier to say with her back turned. Easier to say anything, with your back turned; that was why executioners worked from behind. "In the first year, the Guild refused. My predecessor, Master Corvane — brilliant man, greedy in the ordinary ways, which is to say safely — he got the offer and he laughed at it. Anonymous buyer, absurd price, a substance we barely held in stock. He declined politely and thought no more of it.

"Three weeks later, the crown issued the first gift-decree. Every person born with the talent — hedge-witches, name-readers, storm-callers, the lot — declared enemies of the realm. Subject to trial. Subject to drowning." She heard Tamsin's breath catch and kept her eyes on the glass, on her own reflection ghosted against the black harbor. "And the decree had teeth in a way no decree ever had. Huntsmen who could find the gifted anywhere. Trials that convicted everyone. Sentences carried out within days. In two years, the drownings broke the gifted communities of three provinces. My predecessor understood, eventually, what he was looking at. It wasn't persecution. It was a demonstration. The crown was showing us what refusal cost, and it was billing the demonstration to the very people the Guild existed to serve."

"That's monstrous," Bram said from the door. His voice had gone rough. "That's — you're saying the hunt exists *because* the Guild said no."

"I'm saying the hunt exists because the thing on the throne wanted the fire, and it has never once cared which argument persuaded us. Yes was the cheaper word." Verity turned back. Her reflection stayed at the window behind her, and she found she didn't mind leaving it there. "Corvane negotiated. It took him four years and most of his hair. The final terms are these: the Guild delivers the fire, monthly, forever. In exchange, the Guild is tolerated. Protected. Our charters honored, our caravans unmolested, our existence written out of every decree. The gifted are still hunted, still drowned — that clause Corvane could not move, and I have spent thirty years trying and moved it not one inch — but the Guild itself is spared. We are the only institution in this kingdom permitted to traffic in the impossible, because we pay tribute to the impossible."

"To keep it alive," Tamsin said.

"To keep *ourselves* alive. Let us be precise." Verity came back to the table and sat, and put both hands flat on the wood the way she did when she meant business. "I know how it sounds. I've heard how it sounds in my own head at night, and I'll tell you what I tell myself, since you're apparently owed my whole soul tonight. The fire costs the Guild little — the dragon produces more than we need simply to keep it alive, and the surplus funds every charity, every sanctuary passage, every forged passport that has moved four hundred gifted children out of this kingdom over twenty years. Did you know that? No. Of course not. Because I don't publish my charities, I just run them. The fire keeps the throne stable, and a stable throne means no war, and no war means the ports stay open and the granaries fill and the drownings — the drownings happen at a rate we can bear, a few dozen a year, terrible, unforgivable, *bearable*, whereas refusal means the decree doubles and trebles and the gifted of this kingdom go into the water by the hundred until there is no one left to save. I have done that arithmetic every single day for thirty years, girl, and every single day the answer comes out the same: the ledger balances. Barely. Ugly. But it balances."

"Do you believe that?" Tamsin asked.

It was a simple question, and simple questions are the sharpest ones. Verity looked at her — this runaway with her stolen ledger and her drowned-king arithmetic of her own — and realized the trap she'd built herself into. Rule one said don't let them see you sweat. Rule three said never tell the whole truth. She'd broken three, and one was already bleeding.

"No," Verity said. "Some nights I believe it. Most nights I check the arithmetic and find I've been rounding down."

* * *

Bram pushed off the door. "Then we end it."

"Sit *down*." Verity didn't raise her voice; she never had to. "You've been waiting all evening to say that, huntsman, and I'm going to give you the courtesy of explaining precisely why you won't."

She pulled the middle ledger toward her — fifteen years old, her own first year as mistress-of-coin — and opened it to a page marked with a plain brass clip. Nothing dramatic. Verity didn't believe in drama; drama was what people used when their numbers were bad.

"The terms of our charter aren't written in any treaty. There is no treaty. There's an account, a courier, and mutual understanding, and mutual understanding is enforced exactly one way." She turned the page around. "Every five years, the crown sends an auditor. Not a tax auditor — a special one. He reviews our deliveries, confirms the schedule, checks our stores. And every five years, he also takes a copy of our complete client registry. Who buys our storms. Who sells us their years. Which villages hedge-witches serve. Where the gifted children have been hidden."

Tamsin went very still. "They have our lists."

"They've *always* had our lists. That's the other half of the bargain, and it's the half nobody outside this room knows, and it's the half I would kill to protect — have killed, if we're doing confessions tonight, though mine can wait for another evening." Verity kept her voice level, clinical, the voice of the counting room, because if she let it shake even slightly she was lost. "Understand the architecture of it. The Guild holds the fire that feeds the crown. The crown holds the lists that feed the hunt. Neither side can strike without destroying itself. If we stop the deliveries, the thing on the throne begins to fail — and before it fails, it burns us, and it burns the gifted whose names sit in those registries, and it does it out of spite if nothing else, because dying things are spiteful. If the truth comes out — if anyone publishes what you've found, girl — the crown falls, yes. And the Guild is charged as its accomplice. Forty years of feeding a monster makes you the monster's butcher, in the eyes of every mob in the kingdom. Our charters burn. Our vaults are seized. Our people — every courier, every clerk, every child in our keeping — they hang us from the same gallows they build for the crown, and they sleep well afterward, believing justice was done."

"So you hide it," Bram said. "Forever. You balance the bodies against the bread and call it stewardship."

"I *survive* it. Survival is the first duty of any institution worth the name. An institution that dies for its principles leaves its people to die without them." She snapped the ledger shut. "That is my price, Thornbury. That is what I am paid, and what I pay. Now you know everything. The fire feeds the king-that-isn't. The lists feed the hunt. The Guild stands between them and calls the arrangement peace. Expose it, and we all burn together. Hide it, and we burn slowly, a few names a month, forever. Those are the only two entries available in this particular book. Choose."

Tamsin didn't answer at once. She looked down at her hands — ink-stained, chapped at the knuckles, a working girl's hands — and Verity watched her think, really think, the way she'd watched few people think in this room. Not calculating. Something older than calculating.

"When you say the arithmetic doesn't balance," Tamsin said finally, "what do you mean? Exactly."

Verity laughed — short, surprised out of her, unwelcome. "Of course that's what you ask. Not *is it true*. Not *who else knows*. You ask me to show my work." She leaned back. "I mean Wren Amsel. I mean that I signed the requisition that sent her to the harbor district the week she drowned, because the route was efficient, and efficient routes are how I've always thought, and I rounded her down. I mean that every month I approve the fire delivery and tell myself the alternative is worse, and every month the alternative gets a little worse because the thing on the throne grows hungrier and bolder and the drownings creep upward, one or two a year, so gradual that no single ledger line ever shows it — but I've read forty years of lines, girl. I know what the curve looks like. I mean that I have been rounding down for so long that I no longer trust my own sums, and a mistress-of-coin who cannot trust her own sums is already bankrupt, whatever the books say."

The lamp flickered. Rain. Tide. Somewhere below, the vaults held their impossible inventory in the dark, and the dragon — though Tamsin didn't yet know about the dragon, not truly, not what it *was* — the dragon burned on, guttering, patient.

"So what do we do?" Bram asked. Quietly now. "If you won't expose it and you won't hide it—"

"I didn't say either of those things. I said those were the only two entries *available*." Verity straightened, and felt something shift in her chest that she hadn't felt in thirty years — dangerous, expensive, unfamiliar. Hope, probably. Or its accounting equivalent. "But ledgers get amended. Terms get renegotiated. Corvane negotiated with a monster and settled for survival because survival was all he could imagine. I've had thirty years to imagine more, and lately I've had—" she nodded at Tamsin "—a bookkeeper who reads true names, and a huntsman who's seen the thing's face and lived. Which raises a question neither Corvane nor I could ever ask before."

"Which is?" Tamsin said.

"What does the fire actually *do*?" Verity folded her hands. "We deliver it. We've never once asked what it feeds, or how, or whether a monthly measure is medicine or merely maintenance — whether the thing on the throne needs the fire itself, or needs something the fire carries. You read true names, Thornbury. So here is your next assignment, and understand that it's also my confession: find out what we've actually been selling for forty years. Read the fire. Read the account that draws on nothing. Read the courier who doesn't speak. If there's a third entry in this book — a way to unmake the bargain without unmaking everyone bound inside it — I need to know before the next delivery."

"Which is when?" Bram asked.

Verity glanced at the calendar on the wall, at the small mark she made each month, and felt the old familiar cold settle into her stomach — the cold of a due date approaching.

"Nineteen days," she said. "On the solstice, as it happens. The crown always takes its fire on the longest night. It says the dark makes the transfer cleaner."

Nineteen days. Outside, the rain eased at last, and beneath the shutter Verity heard the harbor bell toll the hour — slow, patient, indifferent as arithmetic — counting down toward the longest night of the year.
