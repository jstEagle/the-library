## 10. A Huntsman's Confession

Bram had rehearsed the confession forty times in his head and every version ended with Tamsin walking out of the room.

He told himself that was why he kept waiting. That it was strategy. That a huntsman knew when to move and when to hold still, and this — her bent over the solstice ledger in the Guild's back office, lamplight catching the ink stains on her fingers, Odile's marginalia finally giving up its secrets — was not yet the moment.

It was cowardice dressed as patience. He'd worn the uniform long enough to know the difference.

The office smelled of tallow and old paper and, beneath it, the faint mineral tang that clung to everything on this floor, the ghost of the vault below where something vast and dying breathed slow as a tide. Verity had gone to draft letters she would probably burn. Odile had gone wherever hedge-witches went when they wanted to be alone with their grudges. And Bram had been standing in the doorway for the better part of an hour like a man loitering outside a confessor's booth, except he was the one who needed absolution and there was no priest alive qualified to give it.

"Bram." Tamsin didn't look up. "Either come in or stop breathing at me from the doorway."

He came in. Sat across from her. The chair creaked under him the way it always did, and he thought, absurdly, *if I die tonight let it be from something more dignified than a Guild office chair.*

She looked up then. Her eyes were the same grey as the marsh water he'd once dragged a screaming girl out of, years ago, and he had to look away first.

"You've been circling me for three days," she said. "Since Wren's drowning. Since you started standing watch outside my door without being asked. You sleep with your boots on and your hand near that knife, and you flinch every time I say my own name out loud. So either you're planning to kill me, which would be stupid, or there's something you need to say and you're hoping I'll die before you have to say it."

"The second one," Bram said.

"Good. Honesty. Start there."

* * *

He started, as all his stories started, with the king. The real one. Aldric the Fourth, who had hunted beside Bram's father, who had laughed too loud and drunk too much and loved his kingdom in the clumsy, genuine way of a man who never had to count the cost of anything. Bram had been nineteen when he took the crown's warrant, twenty when they gave him the black-and-silver of a royal huntsman, and twenty-one the first time he put irons on a gifted child.

"They weren't drownings, then," he said. "That came later, after the fever, after the decree. In the early years it was quieter. A knock at the door. A wagon. The families were told their people were being taken somewhere safe, somewhere the crown could *study* them. Some of them believed it. Most of them just didn't have a choice."

Tamsin had gone very still. She did that when she was reading something — he'd watched her do it over ledgers, over seals, over the true names hidden in ink. She was reading him now, and he could feel it, feel her gift brushing against the shape of what he was saying the way she brushed against the names of impossible things.

"And you?" she said quietly. "Did you believe it?"

"I believed whatever made it possible to keep doing it. That's what belief is for, most days. A tool. You hold it up between yourself and the thing you're doing and it casts a shadow big enough to work in." His hands were flat on the table. They had not shaken during the confession yet, and he willed them not to shake now. "I was good at it. That's the part I need you to understand. Not good despite myself, not reluctant, not haunted while I worked. I was *good*. I could find a gifted child through three locked doors and a lie. I could smell the gift on someone before they knew they had it. There were men in the hunt who needed the writs and the informers and the paid neighbors. I just needed to stand in a market square and listen for the wrongness."

"The wrongness," Tamsin repeated.

"The sound gifted things make under the skin. Like a bell rung underwater. I've had it since I was a boy — no gift of my own, just the ear for other people's. The crown called it a talent." He almost smiled. "They were right. It was. I wasted it on them for eleven years."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"There's a name I want to say," Bram said, "and I need you to let me get all the way through it before you decide what happens next. Can you do that?"

Something moved behind her eyes — the quick calculation of a woman who had survived Odile Grieve's apprenticeship, who had traded a charm for a boat and stolen a worthless ledger and talked her way past a Guild that hanged people for less. She was deciding whether to grant him the mercy of the whole sentence.

"Say it," she said.

"Thornbury." Her face didn't change. "Corwin Thornbury. Marsh-town man. Ferryman, part-time, and a mender of small charms — nothing grand, nothing the crown would even call a gift, just enough to sweeten a sick cow or find a lost ring. Enough to matter to the people who loved him." Bram's voice held level by main force. "There was a bounty notice. Anonymous informant. Standard procedure. I went down with two others on a wet morning in autumn, and he met us at the door of his house with a boat-hook because he knew exactly what we were, and he fought, and I—"

He stopped. Not because the words failed. Because Tamsin had risen from her chair, slow, both palms flat on the table, and the lamp flame had gone tall and thin and blue, and every ledger on the shelves around them had begun, very softly, to hum.

"Tamsin."

"Don't." Her voice was quiet and terrible. "Don't you dare soften it. Don't you dare put *and I* at the end of that sentence like the rest of it is too holy to speak. Say what you did. Say it in the plain words you used for it in your reports. I know you filed reports. Men like you always file reports."

"*Apprehended subject following resistance,*" Bram said. "*Subject deceased.*" The old formula rose up out of him like bile, eleven years of it, the language of the thing itself. "That's what I wrote. That's what I wrote about your father, and I wrote it in a steady hand, and I ate supper that night, and I slept."

The humming ledgers fell silent all at once, which was somehow worse.

"My mother died the following spring," Tamsin said. "Of grief, the village said. Of a wasting sickness, the physician said. Of nothing anyone could name, and I was nine years old, and Odile came for me a year later because a gifted orphan is a loose thread and loose threads get found." She hadn't sat back down. "You stood in my father's doorway. You stood in it, and you wrote *subject deceased*, and then three weeks ago you knocked on another door and asked me to trust you with my life."

"Yes."

"And you waited until now to tell me because?"

"Because if I'd told you on the first day you'd have run, and you'd have run straight into the hunt, and I couldn't protect you. Because Verity needs you and I need Verity to need you and none of that is a reason, it's just arithmetic, and arithmetic is how I justified everything for eleven years." He stood too, because sitting felt like a lie of comfort. "And because some part of me hoped Corvel's clerks would catch me before I had to say it out loud. There. That's the truest entry in the whole ledger. I hoped to die in custody rather than confess to you."

The blue flame steadied. Faded to gold. Tamsin lowered herself back into her chair, and when she spoke again her voice was scraped raw and very controlled.

"Why tell me at all? Why now?"

"Because of the solstice." He turned his hand palm-up on the table, the way a man shows he's unarmed, though they both knew his hands had never needed a weapon to be dangerous. "Because in nine days we walk into the drowned garden together and unbind a dead king's name, and if it goes wrong we die, and if it goes right we die anyway but slower, and I will not go into that water carrying this. Not against you. You deserve better than a knife at your back that has a name on it."

"A name," Tamsin said. "My father's name."

"Your father's name."

She was silent for a long time. Outside, Saltmere harbor muttered against the seawall; somewhere above them the Guild's night-clerks scratched their pens, keeping the books of impossible things, storms and years and memories and fire. The ordinary sounds of the world going on, indifferent to whether Bram Hale left this room alive.

"I should kill you," Tamsin said at last, conversationally, and the horror of it was that she sounded like she was doing sums. "Or turn you out, and let the hunt do it. That would be justice. That would be the clean entry in the book." She looked at him, and her eyes were dry, and that was worse than tears. "But here's what I keep coming back to, Bram. My father fought you with a boat-hook. He wasn't stupid. If he'd had any chance at all, he'd have taken it. Which means he knew, standing in that doorway, that there was no winning — and he fought anyway, because that's what you do when the thing at your door is wrong. And now there's a different wrongness wearing the crown, and the only people willing to stand in its doorway are a disgraced huntsman, a vengeful witch, a mistress-of-coin with burned fingers, and me." She exhaled slowly. "So I don't get to spend my rage on the instrument when the hand that held it is still wearing the crown. I need every weapon I can carry. Even the ones I hate."

"That's not forgiveness," Bram said.

"No. It isn't." She pulled the solstice ledger toward her and opened it, and her voice dropped into the clipped cadence of a woman getting back to work, which was its own kind of wound. "It's a conditional entry. *Subject retained pending outcome.* You fight beside us at the solstice, you hold the door the way you say you can, you put that talent of yours — that beautiful, rotten talent — to work finding the gifted instead of hunting them, for the rest of your life, starting now. You do that, and maybe someday I write a different word in this book. Maybe I don't. Forgiveness isn't a debt you can pay off, Bram. It's not in your ledger to settle. It's in mine, and I may simply choose never to balance it."

Bram nodded. His throat wouldn't manage words.

"But hear me," she said, and now the lamp flared again, gently, gold and warm, and he understood that this was her gift speaking, the thing the crown would drown her for, turned toward him like a lantern. "If you ever lie to me again — if there is one more name, one more report, one more *subject deceased* you've been sitting on — I won't rage and I won't grieve. I'll just stop trusting you, instantly and completely, the way a lock stops trusting a key that's been cut wrong. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"Then say the rest of it. All the names. Tonight, start to finish, and I'll listen, because someone owes them that much — someone who was there." She pushed the lamp toward the center of the table, between them, and opened a fresh page in the ledger, and dipped her pen. "Start with my father. Full name, date, what he said, if he said anything. We're going to write them down. All of them. Every person you apprehended, every family you broke, in your hand and mine, and when this is over — if there's an *over* — those pages go to the survivors, or to the ground, whichever they'd have wanted. That's your penance. Not my forgiveness. The record."

Bram looked at the blank page for a long moment. Eleven years of reports lived in his head, indexed and cross-referenced, the crown's own filing system turned against itself at last.

"He said something," Bram managed. "Your father. Before. I never wrote it down because it didn't fit the form."

Tamsin's pen stopped.

"What did he say?"

"He looked at me — not at the others, at me, he could tell I was the one who'd found him — and he said, *"Whoever taught you to listen taught you wrong. Someone should teach you right."*" Bram's voice cracked on the last word, eleven years late. "And then he swung the boat-hook."

Tamsin closed her eyes. When she opened them, she slid the pen across the table to him.

"Then start there," she said. "Write that down first."

* * *

They worked until the third hour past midnight. Name after name, date after date, Bram speaking in the flat voice of a man reading his own indictment and Tamsin writing in her neat clerk's hand, asking the questions a record requires — age, gift, kin, what became of the family — questions that were knives each time, and he answered every one.

Near the end, as the lamp burned low, Tamsin paused over the growing list and said, without looking up:

"Odile warned me someone at court knows my name. Knows it *truly*, she meant — knows enough to bind me, not just find me." She tapped the fresh ink of the last entry. "And tonight I'm wondering how the hunt found my father. An anonymous informant, you said. In a marsh town of forty people, where everyone knew everyone and half of them owed him charms."

Bram went cold. "You think the informant—"

"I think anonymous informants get paid," Tamsin said quietly. "And I think whoever informed on my father knew exactly what the crown does with gifted men — and did it anyway. Which means either they feared him, or they were promised something." She looked up, and in the guttering light her face was pale and set. "Bram. Pull the old warrant files. Find me the informant's mark on my father's bounty notice. Because if the same hand informed on half these names — if someone was feeding the hunt from inside, years before Corvel ever learned to read my ledgers — then this hunt didn't start with the thing wearing the crown."

She turned the ledger toward him. At the bottom of the page, beneath the last name, she had written a single line in her precise clerk's script:

*Find the hand that fed the hunt.*

Bram picked up the pen.
