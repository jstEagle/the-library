## 11. The Solstice Ledger

The marginalia appeared on the third night, when the tallow burned low and Tamsin's eyes had gone grainy from forty years of ledgers compressed into a fortnight of reading.

She had been cross-checking the fire entries again — the monthly vial, the palace buyer, the neat sums in a dead man's hand — when she noticed that the candlelight caught differently on page 214 of the oldest ledger. Not ink. Something beneath the ink. She tilted the page toward the flame and held her breath, because breath fogging old vellum was a hedge-trick she'd learned at nine years old and never quite unlearned.

*Show what's hidden,* she breathed across the paper.

The words rose like drowned things surfacing.

Not Odile's handwriting — older, cramped, slanting hard to the right as if the writer had been in a hurry or afraid of being read over the shoulder. But threaded through it, in the margins, in the spaces between columns of storm-vintages and borrowed years, were annotations in a script Tamsin knew better than her own heartbeat: her mistress's hand, fine and vicious, correcting the older notes the way Odile corrected everything — with contempt and precision in equal measure.

Tamsin sat back. Her chair creaked. Somewhere below, the Guild's counting floor murmured through the boards, clerks calling figures into the dark hours, the building breathing its ledger-breath around her.

Odile had written this. Or finished it. Or *translated* it, which was worse, because translation meant someone else had known first.

She turned back to the beginning of the ledger and breathed on every page, one by one, until dawn came gray through the shutters and her lungs ached with hedge-words. By the time Verity Crane's knock came at the door, Tamsin had filled eleven sheets of her own paper with the recovered text, and her hands would not stop shaking, and she could not have said whether it was fear or hope doing the shaking.

"You look like you've seen a ghost," Verity said, setting down tea Tamsin didn't remember asking for.

"Worse." Tamsin slid the sheets across the desk. "I've seen an instruction manual."

* * *

Verity read the way she did everything: fast, twice, lips moving slightly on the second pass as though tasting each clause for poison. When she reached the fourth sheet she stopped moving her lips. When she reached the seventh she set the pages down flat, aligned their edges with one another, and looked at Tamsin for a long moment over the rim of her spectacles.

"Say it plainly," Verity said. "I deal in plain things."

"The king is bound." Tamsin heard her own voice go flat and clerkish, the voice she used for numbers too large to feel. "Bound by name-magic, decades ago, by whoever wears him now. The entity isn't possessing him the way a spirit possesses — it's *wearing his name*. The face, the signature, the seal, all of it hangs off a borrowed word. And a borrowed word can be returned."

"Returned how?"

"There's a rite. Unbinding. It needs three things." She counted them off against the desk edge, one finger at a time. "The king's true name, spoken whole. The winter solstice, when the year's ledger closes and every debt comes due. And ground where the binding was made — which wasn't the throne room, wasn't the crypt. It was underneath. There's a garden under the palace, Verity. An orchard. Planted by the old king himself, before he ever wore the crown, for—" she checked her sheet, though she didn't need to; the words had already burned themselves into her, "—for his daughter, who had the gift. He planted it so she'd have somewhere the crown couldn't follow. And then the crown followed anyway."

Verity took off her spectacles. It was, Tamsin had learned, the closest thing the mistress-of-coin had to weeping.

"And the cost," Verity said. "There's always a cost. I've never yet met a rite that came free, and I've audited rites."

Tamsin looked down at the seventh sheet. She had read it perhaps thirty times. The words hadn't changed. She'd hoped, in some childish corner of herself, that reading them again might change them.

"The unbinding needs fuel," she said. "Name-work does. You're unwriting something the world has agreed to believe, and the world doesn't give up its agreements cheaply. The rite has to be fed with fire that isn't ordinary fire. Fire that remembers being alive." She swallowed. "Dragon-fire."

"No," Verity said.

"All of it."

"*No.*"

"It's not a request I'm making, it's arithmetic. The rite consumes the last dragon-fire in the world. Every drop in the vault. The thing sustaining the king dies, the binding unravels, and the fire goes with it. That's the price on the page. Odile wrote it out like a bill of sale, because that's what Odile does. She wanted no one able to claim they weren't warned."

Verity stood. She walked to the window, opened the shutter, and stood looking out at Saltmere harbor, where the morning fog was lifting off water that two weeks ago had taken Wren Amsel and given back nothing. When she spoke again her voice had gone quiet and terrible, the voice of a woman reading a column of losses.

"Do you understand what you're describing? The Guild runs on that fire. Not metaphorically. The vault-furnace heats our bond-houses. Our bottled storms are sealed with it. Half our inventory is inert without it. The dragon-fire *is* the Guild, girl. You're proposing we burn down the only home you have."

"I'm proposing we burn down the thing keeping a corpse walking," Tamsin said. "Yes. I've done the sums."

"Then let me do them too." Verity turned from the window, and her eyes were dry and bright and merciless. "The crown falls. Fine. Good, even. And then what? Every client who bought a borrowed year learns their purchase is worthless. Every noble house holding a bottled storm watches it gutter. The Guild's debts come due all at once, and unlike the year, we do not close our books at solstice. We drown in them. You'd be trading one drowning for ten thousand."

"I'd be trading a lie for the truth."

"The truth has killed better people than either of us." Verity picked up the sheets again, squared them, and — to Tamsin's astonishment — did not burn them. "I'm not saying no. I want that understood. I am saying that if we do this, we do it knowing exactly what we are spending. Bring me the vault numbers. All of them. If the Guild is going to die, it will die with accurate books."

She left. Tamsin sat alone with the tea going cold and the seven sheets spread out before her like a map of a country she'd been conscripted into invading.

Hope, she thought, felt exactly like this. Like a number you couldn't take back once you'd entered it.

* * *

Bram Hale read the sheets standing up, the way he read everything, as though sitting might mean staying and staying might mean being caught. His jaw worked through the first three pages. At the fifth he said, "The garden. Under the palace." At the sixth: "I carried prisoners past a door in the cistern level once. Sealed. Huntsmen weren't cleared for it. I always thought it was a wine cellar."

"A king's orchard," Tamsin said.

"For a gifted daughter." Bram's voice did something complicated on the word *gifted*, and Tamsin remembered — not for the first time, but with new weight — that this man had hunted people like her father. That his remorse was real and his hands were still his hands. "And the name. This says you need the king's true name. Spoken whole. Do you have it?"

"No."

"Does anyone?"

Tamsin thought of the ledgers. Of the way names moved through them — bought, sold, redacted, restored. Of the single entry she had never been able to account for: a payment, decades old, made in a currency the Guild's own conversion tables couldn't parse. A payment marked only with a symbol she'd assumed was a clerk's error.

A small coiled shape. A wing folded closed.

"I think," she said slowly, "that someone in this city has been keeping it. And I think they've been keeping it the only place a true name can't be stolen or forged."

"Which is?"

"In something that never writes it down. In something that just *is* it." She met Bram's eyes. "How much do you know about dragons, huntsman?"

He laughed, short and disbelieving. "They're stories. They died before my grandfather's grandfather."

"Everything in the Guild's vaults was a story once," Tamsin said. "Storms in bottles. Years in jars. Come with me. I want to show you an entry that isn't an error after all."

* * *

They went down at dusk, when the counting floor emptied and the vault stairs belonged to the lamplighter and the rats. Tamsin carried the old ledger and a lantern. Bram carried the sword he'd sworn he'd retired, which told her something about how much he believed in retired things.

The deep vault was behind three doors, and the third door was not locked but *warded*, and the ward was old enough that it predated the Guild's current cipher system entirely. Tamsin pressed her palm to it and read it the way she read everything — quickly, hungrily, the gift rising in her like water finding its level.

*Keep what sleeps,* the ward said. *Feed what keeps.*

Her stomach dropped. "Bram. What feeds the vault?"

"Ferren handles it. Monthly requisition. Lamp oil, charcoal, and—" He stopped. "And one vial drawn off the top, delivered to a palace buyer. I signed the escort chit once, years ago. I never asked what was in the vial."

"We both know what's in the vial now."

"Yes," Bram said quietly. "We do."

The inner chamber was warm. That was the first wrongness — deep vaults should be cold, and this one breathed heat like a sleeping animal, which, Tamsin realized as her eyes adjusted, was precisely what it was.

The dragon filled the room the way grief fills a house. Not vast — that was the horror of it, it had been made *small*, folded down and down by whatever craft had built this cage until it fit a space meant for wine casks, scales dulled to the color of old brass, wings pinned in folds of flesh and scar. Chains ran from its limbs into the walls, and the chains were not iron. She read them without wanting to. Ledger-chains. Contract-metal. Every link inscribed with clauses.

It was dying. It had been dying for a very long time, patiently, the way only something immortal can die.

And it was awake.

The eye that found her was the size of a dinner plate and the color of a banked coal, and it did not move, and it did not need to. Tamsin felt it read *her* — felt her name turn over in that gaze like a coin tested between teeth.

"Tamsin Thornbury," she said, because it seemed suddenly important to offer what she was, freely, in a room full of things kept against their will. "I keep your ledgers. I didn't know. I swear I didn't know you were here."

The dragon's nostril flared. A sound came out of it, low, less a voice than a landslide deciding to be polite.

*The little reader,* it said. Its voice arrived inside her skull rather than her ears, and it tasted of smoke and old gold. *The one who reads true. They told me you'd come eventually. Everything in this building is a promise waiting to be called due.*

"Who told you?" Tamsin whispered.

*A woman with cold hands and colder handwriting. She left instructions. She said: when the girl who reads true finds the solstice pages, tell her the rest.* The great eye closed, then opened, and something almost like humor moved in it, bitter as ash. *Tell her the name is here. Tell her it has always been here. Tell her I have been carrying it for sixty years like a stone in my chest, and I would very much like to put it down.*

Tamsin's knees nearly went. Bram caught her elbow, steady as a doorframe.

"Your name," she managed. "You'll give it to me? The king's true name — you're its keeper?"

*I am more than its keeper, child. Ask your mistress why a dragon's word was ever collateral for a king's life. Ask her who brokered that bargain, and what she was paid, and whether she has ever once slept a whole night since.* The eye dimmed, guttering, and the heat in the room faltered like a candle in wind. *But not tonight. Tonight I am too poor to speak. Come back fed and ready, or don't come back at all — a name spent halfway is worse than a name never spent.*

"What do you need?"

*What I have always needed.* The chains clinked as the dragon settled, folding its ruined wings tighter. *To be paid what I am owed. To burn once, freely, at the end — not drip forever into other people's lamps. Bring me to the garden at solstice. Let the last of me go where it chooses instead of where the contracts say. Do that, and the word is yours.*

Tamsin stood in the warm dark with the ledger heavy under her arm and the weight of it all settling onto her shoulders: the rite, the solstice, the garden, the fire — every drop, the whole Guild's furnace, Verity's life's work, spent in one burning act of freedom for a creature everyone alive believed was a story.

Above them, muffled through stone, the city bells rang the hour. Twenty-two days to solstice.

"Well," Bram said into the silence. "Now we know what we're spending. Question is whether Crane lets us."

"She will," Tamsin said, and was surprised to find she believed it. "Because Verity Crane has never once in her life allowed an inaccurate book. And ours—" she touched the ledger, and beneath its leather she could feel the marginalia waiting, patient as buried seed, "—ours has been lying for sixty years."

Behind them, as they climbed toward the lamplit world, the dragon began — very softly, like a furnace remembering music — to hum.
