## 12. The Last Dragon

**TAMSIN**

Verity Crane did not knock on doors; she made them feel as though they had always been open for her.

"Bring your coat," she said from Tamsin's doorway at an hour when even the harbor rats were asleep. "And nothing you value. The vault takes a toll on sentiment."

Tamsin had learned to read Verity's silences over months of shared ledgers, and this silence was wrong — too long, too careful, like a sum that wouldn't balance no matter how many times you ran it.

"Which vault?" Tamsin asked. "The storm cellar? The memory—"

"The deepest one." Verity's voice was level, but her hands were not. She wore gloves indoors, which she never did. "The one that isn't on any ledger I've ever let you touch."

That should have been the end of it. A mistress-of-coin does not explain herself, and an accountant does not ask twice. But Tamsin had spent the last week with Odile's marginalia swimming behind her eyes — *the unbinding requires a name, and names are given only by their owners* — and she was tired of being managed by people who thought they were protecting her.

"You're going to show me," Tamsin said.

"I am going to *show* you," Verity agreed, in the tone of a woman agreeing to her own execution, "because Bram refuses to lie to you anymore, and because if I don't, you'll find your own way down there and get yourself killed. Come."

* * *

They went down through the Guild in ways Tamsin hadn't known existed: past the storm cellar where bottled weather muttered in its racks like prisoners dreaming of escape; past the year-vaults, cold as a butcher's floor; down stairs that stopped pretending to be stairs and became a rope-ladder, then a ledge, then a stair again, cut into rock that sweated salt water. The air changed three times. First it smelled of coin and cedar, then of ozone and old rain, and finally of something Tamsin had no ledger-entry for — a smell like the moment before a forest fire, mixed with hot iron and, absurdly, roses.

"Bram is already below?" she asked.

"Bram has been below since midnight." Verity lifted a lantern. In its light her face looked carved and old, all the merchant's polish burned away. "He stands watch. He insisted. He said—" She stopped. Recalculated. "He said someone owed that creature a witness."

"What do you mean by that?"

"You'll understand when you see it." Verity set her hand against a door of black iron banded with silver script so dense it looked like fish scales. "And when you see it, Tamsin Thornbury, I want you to remember one thing. Whatever you feel in the next hour — pity, rage, wonder — the Guild felt it first, every day, for thirty years, and we kept the books anyway. That's what I need you to carry out of here. Not the awe. The arithmetic."

She pressed her bare palm — she had removed one glove, Tamsin noticed, only one — to the door, and spoke a word that wasn't a word, a sound like a coin dropped into deep water, and the silver script drank the light of the lanterns one lamp at a time until they stood in darkness that had texture and weight.

Then the darkness breathed.

* * *

**BRAM**

Bram Hale had stood vigil over dying things before. He'd knelt in the snow beside stags he'd run down for the crown, closed the eyes of poachers on the gallows he'd built with his own commission money. He knew what a death watched looked like: small, embarrassed, eager to be finished.

This was not that.

The vault was not a room so much as a throat — a cavern the size of Saltmere's cathedral, ribbed with stone that might once have been natural and had been persuaded otherwise. Scaffolds of black iron climbed its walls like ivy, and from every scaffold ran pipes, hundreds of them, copper and glass and something that gleamed dull gold, all converging on the center of the floor.

At the center lay the dragon.

It was smaller than Bram had expected and infinitely larger. Its body was gaunt as a famine winter, scales gone the gray-green of sea-glass, and it lay coiled around a dais of stone worn smooth by decades of its own heat. But its wings — folded tight against its flanks, webbed with veins that glowed a low, guttering orange — still spread, when it shifted, wider than the Guild's counting hall. When it exhaled, the pipes drank the breath, and somewhere above them the whole city of Saltmere was warmed by it, lit by it, its lamps fed on dragon-fire siphoned off in measured doses.

Measured doses. Bram knew the requisition schedules now. He knew the crown's share left this vault in a single vial each month, carried by courier to the palace, poured into whatever vessel sustained the thing wearing the king's face. He knew the numbers. Seeing the source of the numbers was different. It was the difference between reading about a drowning and standing in the water.

"You came," he said, as Tamsin's footsteps sounded on the last stair. He didn't look away from the dragon. You didn't look away from a fire; that was how fires caught you. "I told Verity you would."

"She told me you've been down here since midnight." Tamsin moved up beside him, and Bram heard her breath go out of her all at once.

"Yeah." He rubbed his jaw. "Couldn't sleep. Figured I'd stand watch."

"Watch against what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He nodded at the scaffolds, the pipes, the neat iron cages of glass along the eastern wall where condensed fire pooled like honey. "Against all of it. Or for it. I haven't decided." He turned to look at her then, and saw her face lit ember-orange, young and appalled and unable to look away. "You should know something before it wakes. Before you do whatever Odile's notes say needs doing."

Tamsin's chin came up. "If this is another confession, Bram Hale, I'm running low on forgiveness."

"It's not a confession. It's a warning." He took a breath. "It knows why we're here. It's known since the solstice began coming. Dragons count time differently than we do — Odile will have mentioned that, or she'll have lied about it, same thing. And it's been waiting thirty years for someone to come down those stairs who could actually hear it." He held her gaze. "Most people who come down here hear nothing. The handlers hear orders. I used to hear—" His voice caught on something old. "I used to hear my own guilt. It shows you what you bring down with it. So decide now what you're bringing."

Tamsin was quiet a long moment. Above them, faintly, the pipes ticked and sighed.

"I'm bringing a ledger," she said finally. "Blank ones. I'm hoping to fill them with the truth."

"Then it'll like you," Bram said grimly. "Truth is the one thing nobody's ever brought it."

* * *

**TAMSIN**

The dragon woke the way dawn wakes — gradually, and then all at once, and irreversibly.

One moment the great coiled shape was stone-colored and still; the next, a seam of light opened along its throat, and an eye the size of a cart wheel rolled toward them across thirty feet of dark.

Tamsin had read true names before. She'd read the name of a storm once, in a bottled lightning's flicker, and it had knocked her flat for a day. She had read the names hidden in ledgers, small sharp truths buried under columns of figures. She thought she understood what a name was.

She did not understand anything.

The dragon's eye found her and *looked*, and looking, it read her — she felt it happen, felt her own name turn over in her chest like a page, felt every entry she'd ever written laid open and weighed. She gripped the scaffold rail. Beside her, Bram went very still, and Verity made a sound she would deny to her grave.

Then, into the space behind Tamsin's eyes, the dragon spoke.

Not aloud. Not in words, exactly. It was more like being handed a book and finding it already open to the right page. What it gave her was a fragment — one word, one syllable-cluster of something vast, the way a wave gives you one cupful of the sea. It was a piece of the dragon's true name. She understood, with absolute clarity, that it was also a key. That it fit a lock she had read about in Odile's marginalia. That the lock was a king.

*Why,* she thought at it, wildly, *why give it to me?*

The answer arrived not as words but as arithmetic — Verity's language, the language of ledgers, which was apparently older than either of them:

*A debt entered thirty years ago. Payment deferred. The account comes due at solstice.*

"But I don't—" Tamsin's voice cracked in the huge dark. "I'm nobody. I'm a bookkeeper."

The eye held her. In its depths the guttering fire reflected, and within the fire, impossibly, she saw images move: a younger dragon, wings full of wind, flying over green country. This country, green. Fields where Saltmere's slums now stood. A river where the cisterns ran. And beneath the palace — she saw it plainly, saw it as though she hovered there — a garden, terraced and blooming, and a girl walking its rows with dirt on her hands, and a king laughing—

The vision cut off like a slammed door.

"What did it show you?" Bram asked quietly. He had moved closer without her noticing. "What did it say?"

"One word," Tamsin said. Her mouth tasted of smoke and roses. "One word of its name. And—" She pressed her hand over her sternum, where the word sat like a coal that refused to cool. "A debt. It says there's a debt, thirty years old, and it comes due at solstice."

Beside her, Verity Crane sat down slowly on the scaffold step, all the iron gone out of her spine.

"Thirty years," Verity repeated. "The month the old king recovered from his fever. The month the payments began." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "We thought we were buying the crown's tolerance. We weren't buying anything. We were *paying back*. Thirty years of installments on a debt we never knew we owed." Her gloved hands flexed open and shut. "Do you understand what that means? Every vial, every courier, every client we protected, every gifted child we smuggled past the hunt — none of it was commerce. It was *interest*."

"And the principal?" Bram's voice was rough.

Verity looked up at the dragon, at the wing-veins guttering low, and for the first time since Tamsin had known her, the mistress-of-coin looked afraid of a number.

"The principal," Verity said, "is everything. The fire, the Guild, the kingdom, the king. All of it, called in at once, on the longest night of the year." She rose, and her voice steadied itself the way she straightened a ledger, by force. "Which is why no one can know about this. Which is why, Tamsin, whatever you're planning with Odile's rite — the cost just changed. We assumed we'd spend the last of the fire. Now I have to price in the possibility that spending it is exactly what the debt *demands*, and that whoever set this account in motion thirty years ago wanted it spent."

The dragon's eye had drifted half-closed again, its light banking down to embers. But as they turned to climb, Tamsin felt one last touch against her mind, brief as a fingertip, and in it was neither gratitude nor plea but something worse:

Patience. The patience of a creditor. Of a creature that had waited thirty years and could wait six more days, and knew — with terrible, bookkeeping certainty — that she would be back.

* * *

**VERITY**

Verity locked the vault door with both hands and stood a moment in the dark afterward, listening to her own heart behave like an unsound investment.

Thirty years. She had inherited the arrangement from her predecessor and defended it before the Guild council as the cornerstone of their survival. She had priced human lives against vials of fire and called it prudence. And all the while she had been a servant in a debt-collection scheme older than her career, run by a dragon and a dead man between them, with the crown as courier.

She thought of Wren Amsel, the courier they had failed to save, drowned in the harbor while the Guild watched from its windows. Wren had carried the monthly vial eleven times. Wren had joked that it was warm, like carrying a bird.

"We were never the house," Verity said to the dark. "We were the teller window."

Behind the iron door, faint as a banked hearth, the dragon breathed.

She climbed. At the top of the stairs, Tamsin waited for her, wrapped in her coat, eyes still ember-bright with what she'd been given.

"Mistress Crane." The girl hesitated. "The word it gave me. If the Guild learned what I'm carrying—"

"The Guild will learn nothing from me." Verity drew her glove back on, finger by finger, and made her voice into the thing her clients feared. "But hear me, Tamsin Thornbury, because this is the last protection I can offer you. There is a bound clerk in the counting house — you've seen him, the quiet one, Pell — and everything written in our ledgers passes through his hands and beyond them, to someone at court. I've tolerated it because cutting the line would cut our throats faster than the hunt would." She met the girl's eyes. "Whatever you do with that word, do not write it down. Do not enter it in any book. Not mine, not yours, not Odile's. Some entries should exist in exactly one place." She tapped Tamsin's sternum, once, lightly. "Keep it there."

Tamsin's hand came up over the spot, guarding it, and Verity saw — with the particular dread of a woman who audits everything — that the girl had no idea what she was holding, or what it was about to cost.

Six days to solstice.

Above them, muffled by fifty feet of rock, the morning bell of the Guild rang out over Saltmere, and the counting day began.
