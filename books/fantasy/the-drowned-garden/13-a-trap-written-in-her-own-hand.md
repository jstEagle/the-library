## 13. A Trap Written in Her Own Hand

The requisition came in on a Tuesday, which was the first thing wrong with it.

Tamsin knew the Guild's rhythms the way she knew her own pulse. Tuesdays were quiet days — the harbor ledgers balanced, Verity locked in her office with the quarterly tallies, the couriers resting their legs before the midweek runs. Nothing moved on a Tuesday. Nothing official, anyway. So when a crown requisition arrived at the Gilded Root's back door, sealed with the privy seal and requesting four vials of bottled storm for "the garrison at Thornwick," delivered by courier to the old tollhouse on the Marsh Road by nightfall — Tamsin's pen stopped moving.

She sat in the counting room with the ledger open under her hands and read the requisition three times. The paper was right: heavy stock, watermarked with the crown's lily. The seal was right: pressed deep, wax the color of dried blood. The language was right: clipped, bureaucratic, the particular flavor of officialese that the palace clerks used when they wanted something without wanting to be seen asking.

But the handwriting was wrong.

Not bad. That was the trouble. It was *good* — too good, too even, every letter standing at attention like soldiers on parade. Real clerks got tired. Real clerks slurred their descenders after the third page of a long day. This hand had no fatigue in it anywhere. It was a performance of handwriting, not handwriting itself.

And there was one more thing. She held the requisition up to the window light, and there it was, faint as breath on glass: a watermark within the watermark, a second lily nested inside the first, visible only at an angle. Guild stock. Paper from the Guild's own stores, the batch she'd inventoried herself two weeks ago, entered in her own neat columns.

Someone had taken Guild paper and written a crown order on it.

"Verity," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt.

* * *

Verity Crane read the requisition twice, then set it down on her desk with the deliberate care of a woman who did not trust her own hands. Her office smelled of ink and tallow and, beneath that, the faint mineral tang of the vaults below — of storms sleeping in glass, of years corked in jars, of the single vial of dragon-fire that went out every month like a tithe.

"You're certain about the paper."

"I inventoried it. Batch forty-one, reams twelve through twenty. I counted them myself." Tamsin laid her ledger open beside the requisition, her columns marching down the page in their tidy ranks. "Twelve reams received, none issued. Except someone issued some."

"Or someone stole some." Verity's fingers drummed once against the desk. She was not a woman who fidgeted; the drumming meant the arithmetic inside her had gone ugly. "Thornwick garrison doesn't exist. There hasn't been a garrison at Thornwick since the border wars. And the crown doesn't requisition through the back door. They send a herald and an escort and they make us feel honored to be robbed."

"So we refuse it."

"We ignore it." Verity slid the requisition into a drawer and locked it. "No response. No entry in the day book. If it's a test, silence passes the test. If it's bait—" She stopped.

"If it's bait," Tamsin said, "for what?"

"For our couriers." Verity said it flatly, the way you say a thing you've already begun to believe. "A delivery to an isolated tollhouse on the Marsh Road, by nightfall, by courier. No escort requested. No receipt required. That's not how the crown collects. That's how a snare is dressed."

Tamsin looked at the drawer where the requisition lay locked away, and felt the first cold thread of something she couldn't yet name. "Then we don't send anyone."

"No one goes," Verity agreed. "I'll tell Wren's people to stand down. All runs suspended until I say otherwise."

It should have ended there. A forged order, caught, ignored, filed. Tamsin went back to the counting room and told herself the cold feeling was just the weather coming in off the harbor.

It wasn't the weather.

* * *

The couriers came back at dusk, all of them, which was the second thing wrong.

They came back fast and scattered, the way birds come home before a storm. Joss first, white-faced, his delivery satchel still strapped across his chest though he'd been nowhere near the Marsh Road. Then the twins, Pell and Sorrel, who never ran unless running was safer than walking. Last came Maren, oldest of the couriers, who had carried for the Guild thirty years and could find her way through fog by smell alone.

"They were waiting," Joss said, in the counting room, with all of them crowded around him. "At the ferry crossing. Not chasing anyone — waiting, like they knew the schedule. Like they had the schedule written down." He pulled a folded paper from his coat and dropped it on the table. "They let me go. That's the part I can't make sense of. A sergeant with the hunt's badge on his sleeve looked straight at me and waved me through. Like I was proof of something."

Tamsin picked up the paper. It was a route schedule. The Guild's route schedule — the internal one, the one that existed in exactly one place, in exactly one hand.

Hers.

Every run for the next fortnight, every courier, every waypoint, every coded rest stop. Copied out fair. Not photocopied — *transcribed*, letter by letter, in that same too-perfect clerk's hand she'd seen on the requisition. Every abbreviation rendered correctly. Every cipher expanded properly. Even her private shorthand for the safehouses along the canal — the little hooked marks she'd invented herself, meaning nothing to anyone who hadn't been taught — copied faithfully, as if the copier had simply *read* them.

Because whoever had made this copy could read them. Could read them the way she wrote them, without effort, without error.

Her eyes went to the bottom of the page, where she always initialed. There it was: *T.T.*, in her own style, her own slant — but drawn by another hand, the way a forger draws a face from a description. Close. Very close. Right up until you knew what to look for.

"Tamsin." Verity's voice, very quiet. "Where does this schedule live?"

"One copy. In my ledger room. Under lock." Tamsin heard her own voice as if from a distance. "There's one key. Mine."

"And the ledger room has—"

"One window. Barred." She was already moving, already climbing the stairs two at a time, Verity's boots behind her, the whole household of the Gilded Root rising like disturbed bees.

The ledger room door stood locked. Her key turned it. Inside, everything was exactly where she had left it — ledgers squared, pens capped, the schedule in its drawer beneath the false bottom where she kept the things that mattered most.

Everything exact. Everything untouched.

That was how she knew.

* * *

She stood in the middle of her own room, in the lamplight, and let the understanding arrive the way cold water arrives — not all at once but everywhere at once, through every seam.

Nothing had been stolen because nothing needed stealing. The room didn't need to be entered. The drawer didn't need to be opened. Because the ledgers themselves — her ledgers, her careful, honest, meticulous ledgers — had been *read*. Read remotely, read invisibly, read through something bound to them or to the room or to her.

"A bound clerk," she said aloud.

Verity, in the doorway, went very still. "Say that again."

"The Guild binds things. Storms. Years. Memories. It's what we do — it's what we *sell*." Tamsin's mouth tasted of copper. "The crown hunts the gifted, but the crown also *uses* them, quietly, the ones it can leash. Someone at court has a clerk who's bound to reading. Bound to me, or to these books, or to the ink — I don't know which. But they've been reading everything I write. Every entry. Every schedule. Every name."

She thought of all of it pouring out of her pen and into someone else's eyes. The client lists. The routes. The safehouse ciphers. The small marginal notes she made about people — *Joss's mother ill, give him the short runs; Maren distrusts the new guard at the wharf* — the intimate cartography of a community, drawn in her tidy hand and delivered daily to the hunt.

"How long?" Verity asked. Not *is it true* — Verity had already done that arithmetic and accepted the answer. How long.

Tamsin tried to remember when the handwriting had changed. When she'd started pressing harder on her nib. When she'd switched inks. "Months. Maybe since winter. Since—" Since the hunt was proclaimed. Since the drownings began. Since Wren Amsel, who had carried for the Guild for six years, walked into an ambush at the harbor that no one could explain, because no one had known her route except the schedule.

Except the schedule had been in Tamsin's hand.

The floor seemed to tilt. She put her palm flat on the desk to steady herself, and her palm landed on the ledger, open to yesterday's entries, her columns neat as fence rows, and she looked at her own writing and saw it for what it had become: a daily report to the enemy. Neat. Legible. Thorough. She had prided herself on thoroughness.

"Wren," she said. "Wren's route was in here."

"Yes," Verity said.

"And the ambush at the harbor—"

"Yes."

Tamsin had told herself, in the weeks since Wren drowned, that the Guild's failure to act had killed her — Verity's caution, the institution's cowardice, the whole rotten calculus of survival. Some of that was still true. But underneath it, colder and more personal, lay this: the ambush had been aimed. Aimed with information only one person in Saltmere possessed in full.

Her.

"It's not your hand on the warrant," Bram said from the doorway. He'd come up behind Verity without either of them hearing, which was a habit of his she'd stopped resenting and started relying on. His voice was gentle in the way of a man handling something that might go off. "You know that."

"I know the spymaster couldn't have built that trap without my handwriting." She kept her eyes on the ledger. "Corvel. It has to be Corvel — the hunt's spymaster, the one Odile warned me about, the one who knows my name. He's been sitting somewhere comfortable with a bound clerk reading my pages over his shoulder, and he's been patient, and he's been *kind* about it — waving Joss through, letting the small fish swim — because he's fishing for something bigger. The requisition today wasn't a trap for the couriers. The couriers were the demonstration. He wanted me to find the forgery. He wanted me to understand that he can read whatever he wants, whenever he wants."

"Then why show it?" Verity demanded. "Why tip his hand?"

"Because he doesn't need stealth anymore." Tamsin finally looked up. "Because he's about to make an offer, and he wants me to know the price of refusing. You don't frighten a woman into selling her friends by hiding the knife. You put it on the table between you and pour the tea."

Bram crossed the room and closed the shutters, all of them, though shutters had never stopped anything bound. "Then we assume everything you've written for months is compromised. Routes, clients, names."

"Not everything." Tamsin was thinking now, hard, the fear compressing into something sharper. "He reads what I write. So we stop writing truth. From tonight, the ledgers lie. Every route in these pages becomes a false route. Every schedule points somewhere empty. If Corvel wants to ambush my couriers, he can ambush my couriers going exactly where I say they're going — and nowhere near where they actually are."

Verity's eyes lit with the fierce joy of a merchant shown a way to turn a loss into leverage. "Feed the spy."

"Feed the spy," Tamsin said. "We write the biggest, most tempting fiction of my career. A shipment worth ambushing. Dragon-fire, even — write that, write it bold, he won't resist it. The Marsh Road tollhouse, nightfall, three days from now. Let him spend his huntsmen on an empty road."

But even as she said it, her hand had drifted again to the ledger, to the record of the last month, and the joy drained out of the plan as fast as it had come. Because the fiction would protect tomorrow. It could not protect yesterday. Yesterday was already read. Already copied. Already in Corvel's files, in that beautiful, tireless, soldier-straight hand.

Yesterday held the names of every gifted person the Guild sheltered. Yesterday held the canal safehouses. Yesterday held, in her own marginalia, in her own private shorthand, the note she'd written the week Wren died: *New girl at the rope-walk asks questions about charms. Gifted? Watch kindly.*

Watch kindly. She had written those words to remind herself to help. Somewhere in the palace, a man had read them and reached for a different kind of watch.

"Get me ink," she said, and her voice cracked on the word and she let it crack, because there was no one in this room she needed to be strong for. "Get me ink and fresh paper. And someone find out where the rope-walk girl is sleeping tonight, and move her, and don't write down where."

* * *

She wrote lies until dawn.

It was stranger work than she'd expected — harder, in its way, than honesty. Every false entry had to be perfect, because Corvel's reader would parse imperfection as signal. She invented shipments with plausible weights and plausible fees. She rerouted real couriers onto paper roads. She gave the Marsh Road tollhouse a cargo of dragon-fire so vivid she could almost see it gleaming in the dark, and she dated it three days out, and she initialed it *T.T.* in her own best forgery of herself.

Once, near the smallest hours, she paused over a blank line and realized she was hesitating because she no longer trusted the act of writing itself. Her whole life, ink had been the honest medium — Odile had taught her that much, at least, before teaching her everything else the hard way. *What's written is witnessed,* Odile used to say. What's written is witnessed. She had built her whole craft on the sanctity of the page, and now the page was a window with a man standing on the other side of it, taking notes.

She thought about Odile's warning, months ago, delivered in a hedgerow whisper: *someone at court knows your name.* She had assumed Odile meant hunted, meant feared. She understood now that Odile had meant something worse. Known. *Read.* Known the way you know a book you've been lent — patiently, thoroughly, cover to cover.

Near dawn, Bram found her with her head down on the finished pages, asleep or pretending to be. She felt his hand hover near her shoulder and not land.

"The rope-walk girl's moved," he said quietly. "Maren took her out before midnight, no paper trail. She's safe."

"For now."

"For now," he agreed. He didn't offer comfort beyond that, and she loved him a little for it. Comfort would have been a lie, and she'd had her fill of well-made lies for one lifetime.

She lifted her head and looked at the ledgers stacked around her — the great lying monument she'd built overnight, hundreds of pages of beautiful falsehood — and past them, through the barred window, to the gray light coming up over Saltmere harbor, where the water was flat and cold and held, somewhere in its history, the bodies of the drowned.

Three days, then. Three days until Corvel's huntsmen rode out to ambush a shipment of fire that existed only in her handwriting. Three days to learn how a bound clerk worked and how to break one. Three days to decide what she would do when the spymaster finally made his offer — because he would, and soon, and he would make it gently, and she would need to be ready to look a kind man in the eye and refuse him.

She dipped her pen. On a fresh sheet, in her neatest hand, she wrote a single line and left it where the reader would find it:

*To the gentleman reading over my shoulder: you are welcome to everything on this page. None of it is true. Come and take it anyway.*

She blotted it, closed the book, and went to wake Verity. They had an ambush to prepare — and this time, for once, the trap would be signed in her own hand, and it would close on the right people.
