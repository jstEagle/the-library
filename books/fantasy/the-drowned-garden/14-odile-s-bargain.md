## 14. Odile's Bargain

The spymaster's office smelled of tallow and wet stone, which told Odile everything she needed to know about the man before he opened his mouth. Corvel kept his candles short. He did not waste what burned.

"You are Odile Marsh," he said, not looking up from a paper on his desk. "Hedge-witch of Fenmoss Hollow. Unlicensed. Unregistered. The kind of woman who teaches girls to read names out of ditches."

"The kind of woman," Odile said, "who is standing in your office uninvited, past three locked doors, while your guards drink tea in the courtyard below."

That made him look up. He had a long face, patient as a heron's, and eyes that had learned to be kind because kindness got more answers than cruelty. She recognized the technique. She had invented half of it herself, back when she was young enough to think cleverness was a moral position.

"Sit down, mistress Marsh."

"I'll stand. I'm old. Sitting makes me honest."

Corvel almost smiled. Almost. "Then let us be brief. You came to me. That interests me more than anything else you might say tonight."

She had come to him. That was the wound of it, the thing she would carry home under her ribs like a swallowed nail: she had walked into the lion's office with her hat in her hand, because there was no other door left.

* * *

Three days earlier, in a rented room above a chandler's shop that stank of tallow even worse than Corvel's office, Odile had laid out every scrap she knew and found it wanting.

Tamsin was in Saltmere. Tamsin was inside the Guild of the Gilded Root, wrapped in its ledgers like a moth in a cocoon, and the crown's hunt was closing around the city like a hand around a throat. Odile knew this because she still had friends in low places — ditch-witches, bone-readers, a ferryman who took coin from anyone — and because she had spent thirty years making sure she always knew where her apprentices were, even the ones who'd stolen from her and run.

Especially those.

And she knew something else, something that had come to her on the road south, in a dream so vivid she'd woken with frost on her eyelashes in midsummer: the hunt was written in hedge-magic. Not court magic, not Guild-work. Hedge-craft. Someone had taken the oldest, meanest tools of women like her — the finding-charms, the name-knots, the drowning-rites the marsh folk used for cattle thieves — and scaled them up to catch a kingdom's worth of gifted children.

Someone at court had bought it from someone like her. Or worse: someone at court *was* someone like her, and had never once looked back.

Odile had looked back exactly once, and it had cost her Tamsin.

So she sat in the chandler's room with a stub of candle and made her list, the way she taught her girls to make lists before any working. What do I have? A name the crown wants. What do I want? Eleven people alive in Fenmoss Hollow who carry the gift and don't know yet how much trouble that is. What will I trade?

The answer sat in her chest like a stone she'd been carrying since the girl left. She picked it up anyway.

* * *

"Your apprentice reads true names," Corvel said now, folding his hands. "We know this. We have known it since Saltmere. There is a clerk in the Guild's counting-house who sees what she writes, you understand. Everything she enters in those ledgers passes through his eyes on its way to mine."

Odile kept her face still. *A bound clerk.* So that was how they'd sprung the trap on the couriers — the requisition forged in Tamsin's own neat hand. The girl would have worked that out by now too, and it would be eating her alive. Guilt was a hook; Corvel fished with it professionally.

"She reads names," Odile said. "Yes. I put that in her, or rather I failed to take it out. It comes with the blood. Her father had it."

"We know about her father." Corvel said it gently, which was worse than saying it cruelly. "We drowned him eleven years ago, mistress Marsh. In this harbor, as it happens. You may have seen the crowd."

"I heard about it." Her voice came out flat as slate. "I taught the girl afterward. Somebody had to."

"And now the crown wants her. Not drowned — not yet. Wanted. A reader of true names is worth ten regiments to whoever holds her leash. The king's—" he paused, a hair too long, a breath too careful, "—the king's advisors believe she could be turned. Bound properly, licensed, pointed at the crown's enemies. She could live fat and honored, or she could drown in the harbor with the rest. It depends entirely on where she stands at solstice."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you came to offer a trade, and I am a busy man, and I would rather hear it from you than make you perform it."

Odile almost laughed. He was good. He was very good. He'd let her think she held the opening move when he'd already read her whole hand.

"Eleven names," she said. "Fenmoss Hollow. A ferryman's daughter who can call fish out of deep water. Two brothers who can hold a roofbeam together with a word. A widow who keeps her garden green through frost. And seven more, children mostly, whose gifts haven't shown their teeth yet. Your criers have their descriptions. Your lists have their villages. Your huntsmen are two days' ride from their doors."

Corvel said nothing, which meant yes.

"Spare them. Strike them from the rolls, quietly, permanently. No drownings, no bindings, no licenses with hooks in them. They go on living stupid little lives in the mud where they belong, and no one ever looks at them again."

"And in exchange."

"Tamsin Thornbury." The name came out of her like a tooth. "Her gift, her obedience, her leash. Delivered to you whole. I'll bring her to you myself, at solstice, wherever you say. She trusts me." Bitterness rose in her throat and she swallowed it. "She still trusts me, God help her, after everything. She'll come if I send for her. And once she's yours, I'll teach you how to bind a name-reader so she can't slip the knot. No one alive knows that craft better than I do. I wrote half of it."

The candle between them guttered. Corvel watched her with those heron's eyes, and Odile understood that he was doing arithmetic, and that she was losing.

"Why?" he asked simply. "You disowned her publicly in Saltmere. Every witness says you cut her off without a backward glance. Why buy her life now, at this price?"

Because the disowning was a shield, you fool, she thought. Because I stood in a market square and called her a thief and a liar so your hunters would look at me instead of her, and it worked, and it cost me the only thing I ever made that mattered. Because I have buried one apprentice's father and I will not bury her too. Because love is a debt you pay in the coin the other person can't see.

What she said was: "I'm old, spymaster. I've outlived everyone I ever taught except her. An old woman wants one thing on her grave-marker besides the dates."

Corvel studied her a moment longer. Then he drew a paper toward himself, dipped a pen, and wrote in a small precise hand. Terms. Eleven names struck from the rolls. One name delivered. He signed it — *Corvel* — and slid it across with the pen.

"Your half," he said. "Sign, and the eleven live. Break faith, and they drown in order of age, youngest first. I find that motivates people."

Odile looked at the paper. Under the tallow light the ink shone like oil on water, and beneath the ink she could see the working — faint as breath on glass, hedge-magic woven through the contract, a finding-knot tied to each of the eleven names, and a thirteenth strand waiting, empty, shaped exactly like a girl.

He hadn't even hidden it. He didn't think she'd read it. Crown men never thought hedge-witches could read.

She picked up the pen.

* * *

She had taught Tamsin to read names the way her own mistress had taught her: start with small things. A spoon. A stone. Learn the true name of a spoon and you learn that nothing is only what it looks like. Learn the true name of a stone and you learn that everything is holding itself together by an act of will, and that will can be spoken to.

The last lesson, the one Odile had never given her, the one Odile had hoarded like a miser because giving it meant having nothing left to teach and therefore nothing left to keep the girl — the last lesson was this:

Every binding has two halves. The binder's and the bound's. Burn your half, and the knot doesn't slip — it dies. But burning your half means the bargain burns too, all of it, both directions, and whatever protection it bought you goes up with it. No hedge-witch in her right mind burns her half. That's why the craft survives, generation to generation: we keep our halves. We keep our protections, our escapes, our little treacheries tucked against our hearts like warm stones.

Odile had kept hers for sixty years.

She signed the contract. Corvel blotted it, and the thirteenth strand in the working tightened, hungry, waiting for a girl's name to fill it.

"One condition," Odile said.

Corvel raised an eyebrow.

"I keep my copy. Custom of the trade. A contract isn't sealed until both parties hold paper."

"A formality."

"All my magic is formalities, spymaster. That's what makes it work."

He considered, then nodded, and his clerk — a gray man by the door — copied the terms onto a second sheet. Odile signed that too, and took it, and felt the twin knots settle into her bones: eleven lives purchased, one life owed. It fit against her heart like the warm stone it was.

She was three steps from the door when Corvel spoke again.

"Mistress Marsh. For what it is worth — and I know what it is worth, which is nothing — I am glad it ended in a bargain. I have read your file. You were the best hedge-craft instructor the fen districts ever produced. If things had gone differently—"

"They went differently," Odile said, "because men like you kept needing them to."

And she walked out past the three locked doors, which unlocked themselves ahead of her, because she had never been stopped by them coming in either, and a spymaster who learns that a week late deserves what's coming to him.

* * *

The chandler's room. The candle stub. The paper flat on the table between her hands.

Odile sat a long time with it. Outside, Saltmere's bells rang the hour before dawn, and somewhere below a cart rattled over cobblestones carrying fish to market, ordinary as bread, and none of the people in the streets knew that eleven lives hung on what an old woman did next with a piece of paper.

If she kept it, the eleven lived. That was the shape of the trap, and it was a fine one, and she admired it even as it closed. Keep the contract, honor the bargain, deliver Tamsin to the crown bound and leashed — and the eleven lived out their stupid muddy lives, and Odile died warm and safe and paid. Burn it, and the eleven names snapped back onto the hunt's rolls like released springs, and the youngest drowned first, and Odile died alone in a city full of hunters with nothing left to trade.

There was no third option. She checked. She was very good at checking. There was no third option, unless—

Unless the knot could be killed from the other side.

Every binding has two halves. His half lived in his office, in his files, in the machinery of the hunt. Her half lived here, in her hands, warm as a stone. Burn hers and the bargain died — but a dead bargain couldn't be enforced, couldn't be *read*, couldn't tell the hunt where the eleven were. The names would snap back to the rolls, yes. But the finding-knots tied through the contract would die with it, and the hunt would be hunting blind, by description and rumor, the slow old way. And blind hunters could be outrun. Blind hunters could be *misled*. She was the best hedge-craft instructor the fen districts had ever produced, and she had one night, and she had a gift for writing things that looked like one thing and were another.

It wasn't saving them. She knew that with perfect clarity. It was buying them a chance, at the price of her own neck, and maybe at the price of Tamsin's too, because a dead bargain meant the crown had no reason to deal and every reason to take.

But Tamsin was not going to be taken. Tamsin was going to stand at solstice in a drowned garden and speak a king's true name — Odile had read the marginalia in her own old book, the rite she'd written into the margins twenty years ago for a different kingdom's crisis, never dreaming whose hands would find it — and if the girl was going to do that, then the least her mistress could do was stop being a warm stone in the machine.

Odile took out her knife. Then she put it away, because knives were for cowards' fires, quick and deniable. She took out the candle instead, and tipped it, and let the flame walk up the corner of the page the slow way, the honest way, so she could watch every word of it die.

The eleven names went first. She said each one aloud as it blackened — Brannoc, Sef, the widow Maren, little Isolde who called the fish — a roll call of the saved-and-damned, committing them to memory because memory was the only ledger that couldn't be seized. Then the terms. Then her own signature, curling and gone, sixty years of keeping her half ending in a smell like burnt hair.

The thirteenth strand — the empty girl-shaped knot — shrieked as it died. Odile felt it go through her teeth, a high thin note only she could hear, and she laughed, low and rusty, because it sounded afraid.

When the paper was ash she ground it into the table grain with her palm, every fleck, and swept the leavings into the candle flame until nothing remained but a smudge no clerk could read.

"There," she said to the empty room, to the girl who wasn't in it, to the dead man's face on every coin in the realm. "Now we're all traitors together. It's the only family I know how to make."

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Sixty years of self-preservation, and at the end of it her hands were steady as a girl's.

She had perhaps a day before Corvel discovered the bargain was dead — less, if his bound clerk felt the knot break, and he would, he'd feel it like a tooth pulled. A day to get out of Saltmere, or better, not to get out at all. A day to find her way to the Guild, to the counting-house, to the girl bent over her ledgers who believed her mistress had sold her.

Odile pulled her hood up, pinched out the candle, and started down the stairs into the dark, moving fast for an old woman, rehearsing under her breath the six words she'd need to open the Guild's door and the harder ones she'd need after that.

*Tamsin. Listen. I lied. Forgive me.*

She'd get the first four out, she judged. The rest would depend on whether the girl still remembered the lesson about spoons.
