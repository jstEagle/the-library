## 15. The Spymaster's Kindness

They took her in the fish market, of all places, between a stall selling eels and one selling nothing at all because the catch had gone to the crown that morning.

Tamsin never saw the hands. One moment she was haggling over smoked herring with a woman who had three teeth and no patience; the next there was a smell of oiled wool and iron, and a voice at her ear saying, very politely, "Don't run. It embarrasses everyone."

She ran anyway. Old habit. A hand closed on her collar — not rough, not gentle, just final — and the market went on around her as if she were being helped home drunk. That was the worst part, later: how ordinary it looked. How practiced. The crown had drowned enough gifted by now to have developed manners about it.

The carriage had no sigils. Inside it smelled of cedar and ink, which she would think about for days afterward, because ink meant ledgers, and ledgers meant someone like her.

* * *

The room they put her in was not a cell.

That was the first cruelty, though she didn't understand it yet. It was a comfortable room in what she guessed was a customs house near the harbor — good fire, a window too high to climb to but wide enough to show gulls, a table with bread and butter and a pot of honey, and a chair drawn out invitingly, as if she'd been expected for supper.

She checked the door anyway. Locked. She checked the walls for listening-marks, the old hedge-signs Odile had taught her to scratch and read, and found none — which either meant the room was clean or that whoever used it didn't need hedge-magic to listen.

She ate the bread. Refusing food out of pride was a luxury for people who weren't hungry.

When the door opened an hour later, she expected a jailer. What came in was a gray-haired man in a plain coat, carrying his own chair, which he set down across from hers before sitting in it, so that they faced each other level, knee to knee, like two people mending nets.

"Corvel," he said. "I keep the king's quiet business. You're Tamsin Thornbury, late apprentice of Odile Marsh, currently employed — I'm told — as a bookkeeper." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which unsettled her more than any snarl could have. "You've been very hard to find. I want you to know I respect that. Most people are easy to find. They want to be."

"I don't want anything from you," she said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "That's what makes this conversation worth having."

* * *

He did not threaten her.

She kept waiting for it, the way you wait for a wave you know is coming. He poured tea from a pot that had been waiting under a cozy, asked how she took it, remembered without being told that she'd said nothing and brought it plain. He talked about the weather, then about the harbor drownings — talked about them, she realized with a lurch, with genuine distaste.

"Wren Amsel," he said, and Tamsin's hands went still around her cup. "Nineteen. Courier. Could carry a message through a locked room without touching the door. Do you know what we did with that talent, before the decree? We paid for it. There was a time this kingdom understood that a gift is just a tool, and a tool is only as dangerous as the hand on it." He sighed, and the sigh was real; she could hear the seams in it. "Then the king fell ill of the mind, and everything changed. Fear makes poor law. But it does make law."

"You wrote it," she said.

"The decree? No. I administer it. There's a difference, and I cling to it the way drowning men cling to driftwood — knowing full well it isn't a boat." He set down his cup. "You're wondering when the cruelty starts. I'll spare us both. There won't be any. Torture produces lies, Miss Thornbury, and I already have all the lies I can use. What I want is the truth, and truth comes freely given or not at all."

"And if not at all?"

"Then eventually you go back to your life, poorer in secrets and none the worse in body, and I go back to mine, poorer in answers." He spread his hands. "I know how that sounds. You think I'm softening you. Perhaps I am. But consider: if I wanted you broken, you'd be broken by now, and it wouldn't have cost me a kind word."

The terrible thing — the thing she turned over that night on the comfortable bed, staring at the ceiling — was that she believed him. Every instinct Odile had ground into her said men like this wore kindness the way anglers wore bait. But Odile had also taught her to read true names, and true names didn't lie, and when she listened to Corvel the way she listened to a ledger, she heard no falseness underneath. Only weariness, and something that might have been grief, worn smooth by years of handling.

He was kind. That was the trap. Cruelty she knew how to refuse. Kindness made her want to earn it.

* * *

The second day he brought her paper.

Not for confession — for work. "You're a bookkeeper," he said, laying out a merchant's ledger with deliberate carelessness. "My clerks are competent and hate me. Amuse yourself. Find the errors. I'm told you're rather gifted at finding what pages hide."

She should have refused. Instead her fingers itched, and after an hour of pacing she sat down, telling herself it was strategy, telling herself she was studying him, and lost herself in the columns until the light changed. When she surfaced, he was watching her with an expression she refused to name.

"There," he said softly. "Do you see my problem? The kingdom has spent six years teaching people like you that their gift is a crime, and then it wonders why its brightest minds end up keeping books for smugglers instead of for the crown." He leaned forward. "I could make that stop. Not the decree — I don't write it. But its application. A legal pardon, sealed, for every name you give me. Freedom for the ones already taken who haven't drowned yet — yes, some are still alive, in the cells under the fort, waiting on the tide schedule. Wealth for you. Position. And the hunt narrows, year by year, until it's a formality nobody remembers."

"You'd pardon them," she said slowly, "in exchange for the rest."

"In exchange for the ones who matter. The ones whose gifts can unmake things. Storms. Names." His eyes held hers, mild and bottomless. "You've been reading our ledgers, Miss Thornbury. Don't look surprised — a spymaster who can't read a Guild ledger shouldn't hold the office. You know what's bought and sold in Saltmere. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. Fire that should be impossible. Somewhere in those pages you've seen names that frightened you. Give me those. Keep the hedge-witches and the charm-sellers. I'll even let you choose which names go on the list. Think of it as bookkeeping. You're good at deciding what gets written down."

And there it was — the shape of it, elegant and awful. Not a demand. An invitation to complicity dressed as mercy. He wasn't asking her to betray anyone. He was asking her to do what she did best: enter figures in a column, balance the account, decide who fit and who didn't.

"That's not pardon," she said. "That's you outsourcing the hunt to someone with better handwriting."

"It's arithmetic," Corvel said gently. "Sixty saved against however many you'd name. In every ledger I've ever kept, that's a profit. Tell me honestly, in your own columns, that it isn't."

She couldn't. That was the horror of it. She sat there doing the sum despite herself — Wren was dead, but the couriers in the cells, the hedge-wives, the boy in Gull Street who glowed faintly when he slept — sixty lives against a list, and the list would grow, and next year it would be fifty against a longer list, and the year after that forty, and somewhere down the column the numbers would quietly invert while everyone involved called it mercy.

Odile's voice, unwelcome as ever, in her memory: *A lie doesn't need to be false, girl. It needs only to be balanced wrong.*

"No," Tamsin said.

Corvel nodded, unsurprised, almost relieved. "I thought not. But I needed you to see the sum. Because here is what you don't yet understand, and what I genuinely regret having to tell you." He rose, collected his chair, and paused at the door. "You think you're choosing whether to help me. You aren't. You've already helped me — every requisition you've countersigned, every neat entry in your hand, has passed through a clerk bound to my service. I've been reading your ledgers for weeks, Miss Thornbury. I know what the Guild sells. I know who buys. And now I know what you've found in the old volumes, because you highlighter-marked it in pencil and thought pencil was invisible." He smiled, and this time it didn't reach his eyes. "The question was never whether you'd name the gifted. It's whether you'd name the buyer. And we both know you will — at solstice, in front of witnesses, unless someone stops you first."

The door closed behind him. Tamsin sat in the warm, comfortable room, in the kind man's borrowed chair, and felt the floor of the world tilt.

He hadn't come to recruit her. He'd come to tell her, courteously, that she was already caught — that her own careful hand had been the rope.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She took apart the room instead, methodically, the way Odile had taught her to take apart anything that might be lying to her.

The fire was ordinary coal. The bread was ordinary bread. The window was ordinary glass — except that glass cost money, and this much of it, in a customs house, in a room with a good lock and a view worth guarding, meant the room existed to hold someone specific. Someone the crown considered valuable. Someone the crown considered *hers*.

Which meant Corvel had told the truth about one more thing: he hadn't wanted her broken. He'd wanted her kept. Kept until solstice, kept away from Bram and Verity and Odile, kept out of the drowned garden — a bird in a gilded cage, and the cage built by a man kind enough to bring honey with it.

She pressed her palm flat against the wall and, for the first time since the marsh town, let herself read it properly — not the hedge-signs, but the deeper grammar beneath, the true names of lime and brick and salt-rot, the way she'd once read a worthless stolen ledger and heard it speak.

Every wall has a name. Every lock. Every chain of custody, every order signed and countersigned, every rotation of guards bored enough to gamble on dice.

Tamsin sat back on the bed and began, silently, to learn the room by heart — its whole small honest ledger of weaknesses — because Corvel had made one mistake, the same mistake the Guild had made, the same mistake the crown itself had made.

He had shown her exactly what he valued.

And everything with a value has a price, and everything with a price can be entered in a book — and she was very, very good with books.

Two nights before solstice, when the guard outside yawned and reached for his dice, Tamsin spoke the lock's true name under her breath, soft as a secret, and the door — kindly built, comfortably furnished, thoroughly read — opened for her like a hand letting go.
