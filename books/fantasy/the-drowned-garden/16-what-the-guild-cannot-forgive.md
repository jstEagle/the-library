## 16. What the Guild Cannot Forgive

Verity Crane had kept the Guild's books for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned one rule above all others: a ledger survives its keeper. Empires fell, kings died and were replaced by things wearing their faces, whole bloodlines drowned in harbors — but a well-kept ledger endured. It was the only immortality she had ever believed in. Numbers did not lie, did not flatter, did not betray. They simply waited, patient as stone, for someone honest enough to read them.

She was reading them now, by lamplight, in her office above the counting floor, when the first courier came pounding up the stairs.

"Mistress Crane." The boy was soaked through, though it hadn't rained since noon. His hands shook so badly he could barely hold out the paper. "The requisition. The one Tamsin Thornbury wrote. The couriers we sent—"

"How many?"

"Three taken at the Salt Gate. One dead resisting. The spymaster's men had the route memorized before our riders left the yard."

Verity set down her pen with great care, the way she did everything, because care was the only armor she'd ever been issued. "And Mistress Thornbury? Where is she now?"

"Gone, mistress. Corvel let her go. That's the worst of it — he *let* her go. She walked out of his custody this morning, and by afternoon his men knew every route in our ledgers." The boy swallowed. "They're saying she sold us. That she's been writing for the crown all along."

Of course they were saying that. People always needed a traitor more convenient than the truth.

"Thank you, Tam," she said — the boy's name was Tamwell, which had never stopped being funny — "have the night clerks assemble. Counting floor. Quietly."

He hesitated at the door. "Mistress... are we going to run?"

It was a fair question. Verity looked past him, out the narrow window, toward the harbor where three days ago a crowd had cheered while Wren Amsel drowned. Toward the palace on its hill, where something ancient and hungry wore a dead man's face and called itself mercy.

"No," she said. "We are going to count."

* * *

The counting floor of the Guild of the Gilded Root had never held so many people after dark. Forty-one clerks stood among the long desks, lamplight catching the gilt root-and-branch sigil on the wall, and every one of them watched Verity the way sailors watch a storm front.

She climbed onto her own desk rather than the platform — a small theater, but she'd learned that standing among the numbers mattered — and held up the evening's requisition ledger.

"For those who haven't heard: three of our couriers were taken tonight because the crown knew their routes. Knew them precisely. Down to the hour." Murmurs. She let them run out. "You will hear that Tamsin Thornbury betrayed us. Consider the source. Corvel released her *after* he'd already copied what he needed — through a bound clerk who has been sitting in our scriptorium for two years, reading over shoulders none of you thought to watch. He didn't need her treachery. He needed a story that would make us tear ourselves apart looking for it."

Old Marlowe, sixty years a clerk, raised a hand like a schoolboy. "Then what do we do, mistress? The hunt will come for us next. We traffic in storms. In years. Half the court owes us and half fears us, and fear is cheaper."

Verity set the ledger down.

"I have spent nineteen years protecting these books," she said. "Every name in them. Every client. Every debt. I have burned correspondence, bribed inspectors, buried two clerks' mistakes under six layers of false entries, because the ledger is the Guild, and the Guild is—" She stopped. The sentence she had recited for two decades would not finish itself tonight.

Marlowe waited. They all waited.

"The Guild is a building full of people," she said finally. "That is all it has ever been. And I find, standing here, that I have been keeping the accounts backwards. I have treated the people as entries and the entries as the thing worth saving."

She picked up the client master list — forty years of names, duchesses and dockmasters, the crown's own procurement officers, everyone who had ever bought a bottled storm or sold a borrowed year. Everything Corvel would need to dismantle the Guild person by person, and worse, everything he could use to prove the crown's own officers had trafficked in the forbidden arts. Leverage. Continuity. Survival.

She had carried it from the fireproof vault an hour ago, telling herself she was securing it.

"Bram Hale believes the king is dead," she said. "Tamsin Thornbury believes it too, and she has read more of our ledgers than any of us, and if she's right, then every month for eleven years this Guild has sold fire to a corpse's jailer. We have not been merchants. We have been the thing's apothecary." Her voice did not shake; she'd have paid good money if it had, because shaking would have meant she still had a body under the bookkeeper. "Corvel will offer us terms. He'll offer them tomorrow, or the day after. Continued tolerance, continued trade, in exchange for the lists, the ledgers, and the girl. And some of you will want to take it. That's not cowardice. That's arithmetic, and I respect arithmetic."

She struck a match.

"But I have done the sums," Verity said, "and here is what they say. A Guild that hands over its clients dies slowly and deserves to. A Guild that hands over a girl to be drowned dies instantly and doesn't deserve the dignity. There is exactly one entry left worth making, and it isn't in ink."

She touched the match to the corner of the master list. The flame took it greedily, climbing the columns of names like ivy in fast-forward, and forty-one clerks watched forty years of leverage curl into ash above the counting floor.

"Open the vaults," she said, as the last corner blackened and dropped. "Not for us. For them. Every gifted soul in this city who needs papers, passage, a hiding place, a name that reads true — the Guild's inventory is theirs now. Bottled storms for cover when the huntsmen come. Borrowed years for the old ones who won't survive a winter underground. Empty the shelves. We are no longer a merchant house."

Marlowe's voice cracked. "Then what are we?"

Verity watched the ash settle on the desks like grey snow, settling on twenty years of neat columns, and found — to her genuine surprise — that she was smiling.

"We're a refuge with excellent record-keeping," she said. "Someone fetch me a fresh ledger. If we're going to commit treason, we're going to do it accurately."

* * *

They came for her at dawn, because Corvel was nothing if not punctual.

Two royal men-at-arms on the stairs, and behind them a thin man in unremarkable grey with kind eyes and the posture of someone who had never once raised his voice. Spymaster Corvel surveyed the counting floor — the empty shelves, the scattered inventory tags, the smell of old smoke — with the expression of a man reading a very disappointing quarterly report.

"Mistress Crane," he said. "I confess I expected better bookkeeping."

"You expected obedience. Different discipline entirely."

"Where is the inventory?"

"Spent. Given away. You're welcome to audit the ashes."

"And the client lists?"

"Ash as well. Though I'm told smoke rises, so some part of them may be closer to heaven than we are."

Something flickered behind the kindness — not anger. Something more tired. "Do you understand what you've done? Eleven years, this Guild and the crown maintained an arrangement that kept the peace. Kept *him* calm. Whatever sits on that throne, it does not want much. Fire, monthly, and to be left alone. And in exchange, hundreds of gifted people in this city went about their lives unbothered. You've just traded a quiet arrangement for a war you cannot win."

"The arrangement," Verity said, "required a monthly payment of dragon-fire to sustain a dead man's stolen face, and required us to stand in the crowd and cheer when it drowned a courier named Wren Amsel, who was nineteen years old and owed the Guild four days of wages I will now never be able to pay her." She stepped closer. "You call that quiet. I call it a debt accruing interest. And today, Master Corvel, I stopped paying."

Corvel looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he inclined his head — almost a bow.

"When this ends," he said, "and it will end soon, one way or another — I hope someone writes your account of it fairly. You were the best counterparty the crown ever had."

"Write it yourself," Verity said. "You seem to enjoy forging things."

He left without threats. That was the frightening part. Men who threatened wanted something from you. Men who left quietly already had it.

* * *

By midmorning the Guild's great doors stood open for the first time in its history — not for buyers, but for the hunted. Verity stood in the entry hall directing the flood: a storm-witch and her children from the Marshside stews, an old memory-keeper wrapped in borrowed years, a boy whose gift was making locks open, which Verity noted wryly would have made him the finest thief-taker in Saltmere if the world were fair.

Bram Hale arrived near noon, mud to the knees, a huntsman's crossbow slung across his back like an apology. He scanned the crowded hall, found Verity, and crossed to her at a pace just short of running.

"Tell me she's here. Tell me Tamsin got back."

"She hasn't." Verity handed him a cup of the terrible tea the night clerks brewed. "Corvel released her yesterday morning. Either she's running, or she's working, and given what I know of that girl's handwriting, I'd bet the house on working."

"On what?"

"On the only account that matters." Verity lowered her voice. "Bram — Odile's marginalia. The solstice rite. She's going to need three things: the king's true name, the drowned garden, and enough fire to burn a hole in eleven years of lies. We can give her the third."

Bram went very still. "The dragon."

"The dragon." Verity drank her tea and grimaced at it. "The last fire in the world, and we've been selling it by the spoonful to keep a shadow fed. Well. The shadow's about to learn what happens when the apothecary closes shop." She set down the cup. "How many huntsmen between us and that vault door?"

"Too many."

"Good. I've never trusted a plan with comfortable margins." She was already moving toward the inner stair, toward the deepest levels, toward the heat that lived below the counting floors like a second heartbeat. "Come along, Huntsman. Bring the crossbow. And Bram —"

He paused on the threshold.

"— whatever you find down there, remember it was never really ours. None of it. We just kept the books."
