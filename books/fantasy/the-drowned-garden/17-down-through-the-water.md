## 17. Down Through the Water

The cistern gate was older than the palace above it.

Bram had known that before they ever reached it — he'd spent three years of his life learning every way into and out of the royal compound, back when knowing such things was his trade and not his shame. The gate sat below the waterline of the old aqueduct, behind a wall of dressed granite that everyone in Saltmere assumed was load-bearing and nothing more. Everyone except the masons who'd built it, two hundred years dead, who had left a door in it anyway because masons always did. Doors were how you got paid twice.

"Say it again," Tamsin said. Her voice echoed strangely off the wet stone, flattened by the dark, made small. "The name."

"The gate's name." He ran his thumb along the seam where the granite met, feeling for the groove. Cold water seeped through the joints and traced lines down the wall like veins. "Every stone in this city has one. Most people just never ask." He found the groove — a finger-width channel cut at hip height, worn nearly smooth by centuries of rain that no longer fell here. "The old king had all the hidden ways named. It was a kind of madness, they said. A king who trusted doors more than men."

"Was it madness?" Odile asked from the shadows behind them.

Bram didn't turn around. "Ask me again when we're through."

He spoke the name into the groove — a low word, a word shaped like water finding its level — and felt the answer come back up through his palm: a shudder, a click deep in the granite, and then the section of wall swung inward on hinges that hadn't tasted oil since before he was born, groaning like something being woken against its will.

Beyond it: darkness, and the smell of standing water, and beneath both, faint as memory, the green smell of growing things.

Verity Crane stood at the mouth of the passage with her ledger clutched to her chest like a shield she no longer believed in. She had burned the client lists four days ago. Bram had watched her do it — watched her feed forty years of the Guild's careful, complicit bookkeeping into the furnace beneath the counting house, page by page, her face perfectly composed, her hands shaking so badly that Tamsin had finally taken the tongs from her. *An institution choosing conscience over continuity.* He wondered if Verity knew yet which one she'd chosen, or if she'd find out only when there was nothing left to count.

"The vault entrance is guarded," Verity said. Not a question. She'd read the requisitions; she knew the crown's habits better than the crown did.

"It will be," Bram agreed. "Which is why I'm not going with you past the third junction."

Tamsin turned. In the grey light leaking through the half-open gate, her face was pale and set, and for a moment she looked so much like her father that Bram's chest tightened around an old, familiar stone. "No," she said.

"Tamsin—"

"No. We talked about this."

"We talked about it. I didn't agree." He kept his voice level, the way you did with spooked horses and young witches and yourself, mostly. "Someone has to hold the door. Corvel knows we're coming — Odile's bargain bought us time, not secrecy. When he figures out where we've gone, and he will, he'll send huntsmen down the aqueduct. If that gate closes behind you with no one to open it again, you're entombed. All of you. And the king stays wearing his borrowed face until the fire runs out on its own, which is years yet, and every gifted soul in this city drowns in the meantime."

"So you stay here alone, in the dark, against whatever he sends." Her voice cracked on the last word. "That's not a plan, Bram. That's a grave with a job description."

He almost smiled. She had her father's way with words too.

* * *

They went down together anyway, because arguing at gates gets you killed, and because Bram had learned long ago that the last argument you win is the one you have with someone who loves you.

The passage descended in long ramps, the walls sweating, the air thickening. Someone had built lamps into the stone at intervals — niches with iron brackets, empty now, but the brackets told him the way had once been walked often, and walked officially. Servants' knowledge. Or family knowledge.

Odile moved ahead of them all, her hedge-craft making small lights of the damp — pale moths of glow that clung to the ceiling and drifted where she gestured. She hadn't spoken much since the burning of her contract. Bram understood that kind of silence. You spend so long building a fortress out of your own worst qualities that when someone finally burns part of it down, you don't know what shape you are without it.

At the second junction the passage split, and the smell of green grew stronger, and Tamsin stopped walking.

"Do you hear that?" she whispered.

Bram listened. Water, yes — the eternal drip and hush of the cisterns. But under it, something else. A sound like wind in leaves, except there was no wind down here and no leaves, or there shouldn't have been.

"The garden," he said. "It's real. I always thought it was a story."

"What story?" Verity asked sharply. Verity hated stories; stories were unpriced assets.

"The old king had a daughter," Bram said. "Before the fever. Before any of this. She was gifted — truly gifted, the way they mean when they use the word like a sentence. And the court wanted her drowned, quietly, the way courts do things, and the king refused. So he built her a place where her gift wasn't a crime. An orchard, they said. Under the palace, fed by the springs, lit by—" He hesitated.

"By dragon-fire," Tamsin finished softly. "It was always dragon-fire, wasn't it? Even then. Even before the king died."

"I don't know," Bram said, and hated saying it. Three years a huntsman, five years a fugitive from his own guilt, and the sum total of his knowledge was still mostly *I don't know*. "Maybe the fire came later. Maybe the thing that wears him learned about the garden and used it. Maybe the garden was the first bargain." He looked down the dark ramp toward the sound of wind in leaves. "Or maybe the garden is why the kingdom survived at all, and everything since has been theft."

They went down.

* * *

The drowned garden opened out of the dark like a held breath finally released.

Bram had imagined caverns. What he found was a valley — a true valley, roofed by living rock veined with channels of faint gold light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. Water fell somewhere in threads and sheets, feeding pools that reflected the golden veins upward, so that the whole space seemed lit from below and above at once. And growing out of all of it, impossible, patient, enormous: trees.

Apple trees. Pear. Something broad-leafed and heavy-fruited that he had no name for. Their roots stood ankle-deep in clear black water, their branches hung with fruit in every stage from blossom to rot, because down here there was no season to tell them otherwise — only the slow pulse of the veins overhead, and the trees, given eternity, had decided to hold every moment of themselves at once.

"Oh," Tamsin said. Just that. Just *oh*, in a voice like prayer.

Then she began to walk among the trees, and Bram saw what she saw, and his skin prickled. Names. The trunks were carved — no, *grown* — with names. Hundreds of names, thousands, spiraling up the bark in letters that shifted like reflections. Gifted names. Every gifted soul the old king had sheltered, written into living wood, kept alive in the only record no fire could burn and no decree could outlaw.

"He planted them a census," Odile murmured. For the first time since the burning, her voice sounded less like a blade and more like a woman's. "A ledger you can stand inside of."

Verity stood very still at the edge of the water, her ledger pressed to her ribs, staring up at the golden veins. Bram watched her lips move — counting, he realized. She couldn't help it. She was pricing heaven, and for once in her life the numbers were refusing to resolve.

"The fire comes from here," Tamsin said suddenly. She had stopped beneath the largest vein, where the gold ran thickest, brightest. "This is the source. The dragon isn't just in a vault — the vault is *here*, or connected to it. The fire feeds the garden and the garden feeds—" She pressed her hand flat against the trunk of the great tree, and the letters under her palm flared softly. "And the thing upstairs drinks from the same root. That's why the rite has to be here. That's why it costs everything. We're not burning a vial. We're burning the orchard's heart."

Nobody answered. There was nothing to say that wasn't already carved into the trees.

* * *

The sound came up the ramp an hour later, while they were still finding the garden's edges: boots. Many boots. Iron-shod, in step, descending.

Bram heard them first — old habit, ears trained in the king's own hunt — and by the time the others registered the echo he was already moving back toward the junctions, checking the lamp niches, marking sightlines.

"How many?" Odile asked, coming up beside him. She didn't argue anymore either. That was the strange grace of the last few days: everyone had run out of arguments and started spending action instead.

"Twelve. Maybe fifteen. They'll have crossbows and a bound clerk to read the gate-names for them." He rolled his shoulders, working loose the ache that lived there permanently now. "Corvel won't lead them himself. He's kind, but he's not stupid."

"You could come with us," Tamsin said. She'd appeared at his elbow without him hearing her, which should have been impossible for anyone but a hedge-witch, and maybe was. "Through the vault, up into the palace proper. We hold the solstice rite and you're there when it breaks. You get to see it end."

"And leave the gate unheld." He shook his head. "You know the arithmetic."

"I hate the arithmetic."

"I know." He reached out — hesitated — and then put his hand on her shoulder, briefly, the way her father used to, back when Bram was the man hunting him and didn't know it yet. "Your father stood a rearguard once. At the ford at Marrow Bridge, when the hunt had your village surrounded. He held six huntsmen off a footbridge for the better part of an hour with a hedge-charm and a quarterstaff, so the others could get the children out." He had never told her this. He had promised himself he would tell her only if they both survived, and survival had begun to look like a currency he might not have. "I know because I was one of the six. He beat me half to death with that staff, and then he looked at me across the water and said, *You're better than this work, boy. Go be better.*"

Tamsin's eyes were wet and furious. "And did you?"

"Took me fifteen years to start." He let go of her shoulder. "Go speak the name. Let me finish paying."

She hugged him then, quick and fierce, and was gone down the ramp before he could make it awkward, which was also her father's way.

* * *

The third junction was a chamber, not a corridor — a wide vaulted room where the aqueduct's main channel crossed overhead in a stone gutter, dripping, and four passages met like the arms of a crooked star. Good ground. One approach wide enough for column formation, three narrow enough that numbers stopped mattering.

Bram took stock of what he had: a hunting knife, a quarterstaff he'd taken from the Guild's armory without asking, three vials of bottled storm that Verity had surrendered from inventory with the expression of a woman amputating her own hand, and a working knowledge of exactly how frightened trained men become underground.

He wedged the staff across the widest passage at throat height, low enough to duck, high enough to forget. He set the storm vials in cracks in the floor where a boot would find them. Then he sat down on a dry ledge, put his back to the stone, and waited, listening to the water count the seconds.

They came ninety minutes after midnight. He heard the bound clerk first — a thin, dragging voice reciting gate-names in a monotone, each name hitting the stone like a key in a lock. Then the boots, disciplined, close-ranked. Then, at the chamber's mouth, lantern-light, and the flat grey faces of crown huntsmen, and behind them a slight figure in clerk's robes whose eyes were wrong — filmed over, seeing elsewhere, reading the world through someone else's attention.

Corvel's attention. Bram was sure of it. Somewhere above them, the spymaster was watching through borrowed eyes, and Bram made sure his face showed nothing but contempt, because contempt was the one message worth sending.

"Halt," the lead huntsman called, standard procedure, offering the standard lie. "In the king's name—"

"In the *king's* name," Bram agreed, stepping out of the shadow with the staff loose in his grip. "Funny. I was just thinking about him."

The crossbow came up. Bram kicked the first storm vial.

The world went white and sideways. Thunder in a stone room is a physical thing — it picks you up and introduces you to the walls. Two huntsmen went down screaming, the rest scattered for cover, and Bram was already moving through the ringing dark, staff sweeping the legs of the man nearest, driving the second back into the third, keeping them bunched, keeping them blind, giving the thunder time to echo and multiply until fifteen men sounded like fifty.

A bolt tore through his sleeve and buried itself in the stone where his ribs had been. Another grazed his thigh, hot as a brand. He gave ground, lured them deeper into the junction, and broke the second vial at their feet, and the flash-storm filled the chamber with shrieking light and the smell of ozone, and somewhere in it he heard the bound clerk stop reciting names and start simply screaming, which meant the link had been severed or overwhelmed, which meant Corvel was blind down here, which meant minutes, maybe only minutes—

He fought the way he'd been taught and the way he'd repented of: efficiently, terribly, without hatred. By the time the storms burned out, four huntsmen were down and the rest had pulled back to the chamber mouth to regroup, and Bram stood in the middle of the junction with blood running into his boot and his breath sawing, and raised his voice so it would carry up every passage at once:

"THE GARDEN IS OPEN! THE NAMES ARE AWAKE! GO AND SEE WHAT YOUR KING PLANTED!"

Let them carry that report upward. Let Corvel chew on it.

He braced the staff across the wide passage again, lowered himself onto the ledge, and pressed his palm hard against the wound in his thigh to slow the bleeding. Above him, far above, through two hundred feet of rock and water and gold-veined stone, he could feel — or imagined he could feel — the pulse of the garden, slow and patient, waiting for a girl with a knack for names to give it back its voice.

*Hold the door,* he told himself. *Hold it until dawn. Hold it until the world changes.*

Somewhere below, the dragon's fire was guttering toward its last hour. Somewhere above, a dead man's face was smiling with someone else's mouth. And between them, in a drowned orchard full of living names, Tamsin Thornbury was walking toward the tree with her father's name in its bark.

Bram Hale spat blood, set his feet, and waited for the next wave.
