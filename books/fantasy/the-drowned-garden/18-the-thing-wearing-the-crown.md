## 18. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The water remembered being a garden.

That was the first thing Tamsin understood as she waded down through the cistern dark, Bram's lantern guttering behind her, Odile's hand iron on her wrist. The flooded orchard beneath the palace had not simply been drowned — it had been *kept*, the way the Guild kept its bottled storms. Roots still stood in rows where roots had been planted, black and patient as congregation. Fruit trees gone to bone. A wellhead at the center, dry now, its stones carved with names worn smooth by decades of water.

And above it all, on a terrace of drowned flagstone that broke the surface like an altar, stood the king.

He had come down alone. No huntsmen, no Corvel, no torches but his own — a single candle in a holder of gilded root-iron, the Guild's own mark worked into the metal, and Tamsin felt her stomach turn at the sight of it. Of course. Of course he had always had a key.

"Master Thornbury," the king said. His voice carried strangely across the water, arriving whole, without echo, as though the room swallowed every sound that wasn't his. "You've brought guests. How unlike you. Your ledgers are so very tidy. I expected you to come alone."

"You read my ledgers." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Through your bound clerk."

"I read everything in this kingdom eventually." The candlelight did something wrong to his face. It moved across his features a half-second after it should have, the way lamplight moves across a painted portrait when the painter's hand trembles. She thought of Bram's whisper in the masque gallery: *it lags, girl, the shadow lags.* She hadn't understood then. She understood now. "Come up. The water is cold, and I have waited thirty years to be understood. I would hate for you to catch your death before I've made my case."

Odile's grip tightened. "It's a trap."

"Everything is a trap," Odile's former apprentice said, and was surprised to hear how tired her own voice sounded. "But traps only work on things that run. I'm done running."

She let go of Odile's hand and waded forward.

* * *

The water was waist-deep in the channels between the tree rows, and it was warmer than it should have been — faintly, horribly warm, like bathwater someone had forgotten to drain. She tried not to think about what fed the warmth. She failed. Somewhere below them, three levels down through stone and silt, the last dragon lay guttering in its vault, its fire piped upward through copper veins in the palace walls, and some fraction of that fire bled into the water here, keeping the drowned garden from freezing, keeping the dead roots from rotting, keeping —

Keeping him alive. Or whatever this was instead of alive.

She climbed the terrace steps. Up close, the king smelled of nothing at all, which was worse than any smell. A man who had walked through water should smell of water. A man holding a candle should smell of tallow. This one smelled like the space inside a sealed ledger, like paper kept too long in a drawer.

"You're not him," she said.

"No." He said it simply, without performance, and somehow that simplicity was the most frightening thing yet. "I am not. Sit, if you like. There are stones. I have had thirty years to learn which ones don't tip."

"I'll stand."

"As you wish." He set the candle down on the flagstones between them, and the light steadied, and for a moment — just a moment — the lag stopped, and the face looking back at her was an old man's face: kind, exhausted, human. Then it slid again, half a heartbeat behind itself, and she knew she'd seen the truth of it. Both truths. The face was real. The thing behind it was not.

"Do you know what this garden was?" he asked.

"A place for the gifted. The old king planted it. For his daughter."

"For his daughter." Something moved through the borrowed face that might have been fondness, if fondness could be worn like a coat. "Yes. Princess Ilsette, who could coax wheat through frost with a song. Who could find water under stone. Whose gift was small and useful and entirely harmless, and whom the court feared anyway, because courts fear what they cannot tax." He gestured at the drowned rows. "He built this so she would have somewhere the ground didn't judge her. Somewhere the gifted could grow things together. And then the land began to fail, and the harvests came in thin, and the winters came early, and the court decided that the daughter's friends were the reason. That the gifted were drinking the rain. Hoarding the sun. You know how these arguments go. You've heard them in every market town since you were old enough to hide what you are."

Tamsin said nothing. Her hands, hidden in her wet skirts, had curled into fists.

"So the old king made a bargain," the thing went on. "Not with me — understand that. With the dragon. Its fire was already old, already guttering; the Guild had bought what remained of its hoard and its years. He asked for enough flame to keep the kingdom warm through the failing. And the price—" the candleflame bent, though there was no draft, "—the price was that someone must hold the ledger. Someone must keep the accounts balanced while the land healed. A steward. Fire is a debt, Master Thornbury. Every year bought with it must be paid for, or the debt comes due all at once, everywhere, and the kingdom burns or freezes by turns. The old king was dying. His heart was a cracked bell. He could not hold the accounts. So I held them."

"You killed him."

"He was already dead," the entity said, with the mild patience of a clerk correcting an arithmetic error. "He died of the fever in the autumn of the thin harvest. I arrived that same night, because a bargain needs a holder and no one else would take it. I wore his face because a kingdom cannot be governed by a shadow — I learned that quickly — and because his face was the only coin I had. I have ruled through it for thirty years. And in those thirty years, tell me, Master Thornbury: how many famines?"

Tamsin's mouth was dry. "I don't know."

"None." He let the word sit. "There has not been a famine in Thornevale in thirty years. Before me, there was one every generation, sometimes two. The granaries are full. The roads are safe. The drownings—" and here his voice did something terrible, something almost gentle, "—the drownings are arithmetic. Each year the fire dims further, and each year the debt grows heavier, and each year I must find weight to cut. The gifted burn brighter than ordinary souls. Their gifts draw on the same current the fire feeds. Do you understand? Every hedge-witch's charm, every courier's finding-knack, every child who can light a candle with a word — they are all drawing on an account that is nearly empty. They are spending a kingdom they do not know they carry. I do not hunt them because I hate them. I hunt them because the sums require it."

The words went into her like cold water into a boot. Because she had read the ledgers. She knew the numbers were true. Thirty years of entries in a dead man's hand, and the grain tallies matched, and the road-repair budgets matched, and nowhere in any of it was there a famine year, a bread riot, a winter the poor had frozen in the streets. Verity Crane ran a Guild of impossible things and kept immaculate books, and even Verity would have wept over margins this clean.

"That's a lie," Odile said from the water's edge. "Or near enough. Hedge-magic doesn't draw on your fire, creature. It draws on the ground, the rain, the small gods. You know it. You're lying to frighten a girl."

"Am I?" The king turned his head, and the candleflame leaned toward Odile like a listening ear. "Then why does every gift flare when the solstice nears, mistress of hedges? Why do your kind dream of fire? Ask your apprentice. She reads true names. She will have felt it — the pull, the hunger, as the year turns. Everything that works wonders in this kingdom drinks from the same well. I merely dug the well."

And Tamsin felt it even as he said it, low in her sternum, the way she'd felt it every December of her life: that ache, that itch beneath the skin, the sense of standing too close to an open oven. She had thought it was the season. She had thought it was her.

*You see,* said the thing in the king's face, without moving its lips, and she realized the voice had come inside her head, quiet as marginalia. *You can check my figures.*

*Stop that.*

*I mean you no harm. I have never meant anyone harm. Harm is simply what remains when the numbers stop balancing.* A pause, and then, almost wistful: *You would make an extraordinary steward, Master Thornbury. Better than me. I am only a holding pattern. You are a reader of true names. You could keep the accounts honestly.*

Her breath caught. Because that was the horror of it, the trap beneath the trap: she *could*. She could stand in this drowned garden with a candle and a ledger and balance a kingdom on the backs of the drowned, and she would do it neatly, and she would sleep at night, and in forty years some other runaway apprentice would come down the cistern steps with a stolen book and a knack for reading the impossible, and she would make this same speech, and it would be just as fair.

"Bram Hale," she said aloud, to break the voice's hold. "Wren Amsel. My father. Those aren't numbers."

"No," the king agreed. "They are costs. I have never pretended otherwise. But ask yourself what they purchased." He lifted the candle, and the light swept the drowned orchard, the bone-white trees, the name-carved wellhead. "Thirty years of full bellies. Ask the mother in Saltmere whose children have never once known hunger whether she would trade Wren Amsel's life for theirs. Don't answer. She already has. They cheer at the drownings, Master Thornbury. Did you watch their faces? They cheer because they are fed, and they are fed because I keep the sum, and the sum requires drowning people like you."

"And when the fire runs out?" Tamsin said. "When the dragon dies and there's nothing left to spend? What's your arithmetic then?"

For the first time, the thing wearing the king hesitated.

It was small — a flicker, a half-second longer than the usual lag — but she had spent months learning to read ledgers, and a hesitation in an account was a confession. She pressed it the way Verity had taught her to press a debtor. Quietly. Without mercy.

"There is no entry for that, is there," she said. "Thirty years of books and you never wrote down what happens at the end. Because stewards don't. Stewards assume someone else will solve it. You've been borrowing against a future you refuse to count, and the bill comes due at solstice, and you need me — or someone like me — to find more weight to cut. More gifted. More drownings. Bigger sums."

"The alternative," the king said, "is famine."

"The alternative is *truth*."

"The alternative," he repeated, and now the gentleness fell away entirely, and what looked out of the old man's face was older than the face, older than the palace, patient as sediment, "is that within three winters, half of Thornevale starves, and the survivors tear each other apart in the snow, and the drownings happen anyway — all of them, at once, without order, without mercy, without ledgers. I am not the cruelty, child. I am the *rationing* of it. Unbind me, and you do not end the suffering. You only end the accounting. Is that what your father died believing? That chaos is kinder than arithmetic?"

The word landed where he had aimed it. *Father.* She thought of Bram in the boat above, holding the way open, carrying thirty years of guilt and a huntsman's axe. She thought of Odile, hip-deep in the dark water, who had sold her name to the crown and burned the contract rather than hand it over. She thought of Verity burning the client lists, choosing people over the institution, and of Wren Amsel going under the harbor water while the crowd sang.

Everyone she loved had paid into this account. Everyone she loved was in the ledger.

And the worst of it — the thing she would carry for the rest of her life, whatever happened next — was that she could not find the flaw. Not in the numbers. The numbers were clean. The famine was real; she had seen the old harvest records, the lean years before the fire, the deaths from cold and hunger entered in careful columns. If she spoke the king's true name tonight, the entity would unravel, the debt would come due, and people would starve. Not abstractions. Mothers in Saltmere. Children who had never known hunger.

Doubt sat down inside her chest like a guest who intended to stay.

"You're waiting for me to argue," the king said softly. "I won't. I have made my case. It is a fair case. I have had thirty years to perfect it, and I confess I believe it myself, most days. That is the trouble with stewardship, Master Thornbury. Do it long enough and you forget you were ever anything but the ledger." He picked up the candle. "Solstice is tomorrow night. The fire will be spent either way — the dragon has perhaps a month left, and the debt must be serviced. So you have a day to decide what kind of keeper you are. The kind who balances. Or the kind who—"

"Who speaks," Tamsin said.

Something crossed the borrowed face. Not fear. Something before fear — recognition, maybe, the way a clerk recognizes a signature.

"Yes," it said. "That. Though I would caution you: the dragon gave you one word of its name, and you believe it gave you the king's. Names are debts too, child. Spend yours carefully."

It turned and walked to the terrace edge, and instead of climbing down it simply stepped off, into the black water, and did not splash, and the candlelight sank with it like a coin dropped into a well, down and down, until the drowned garden was dark except for the gray seep of moonlight through the cistern grates far above.

* * *

Odile reached her first, hauling her back from the terrace edge by both arms, shouting her name, and Tamsin realized she had been leaning toward the water, toward the sinking light, toward the voice that had offered her a stewardship.

"Tamsin. *Tamsin.* Look at me. What did it say to you? Inside — what did it say inside?"

"It offered me a job." Her own voice sounded far away. "It said I'd be better at this than it is. Odile — the numbers are right. The famine numbers, the harvest records, all of it. If I unbind it, people starve. Real people. I checked the ledgers myself. I *know*."

"Ledgers lie by omission," Odile said flatly. "You taught me that, or the Guild did, or grief did — someone did. What did the books *not* say?"

Tamsin opened her mouth. Closed it. Ran the columns in her head, thirty years of them, the dead king's neat hand, and found herself hunting the gaps the way she'd once hunted gaps in Odile's herb-ledgers, the entries that weren't there, the purchases that explained themselves too easily. Grain imports that never varied, not once in thirty years, not in drought, not in flood. Roads repaired on schedule through plague years. Wages that never rose and never fell.

No ledger was that stable. Stability wasn't peace. Stability was a hand holding everything down.

"It's not preventing famine," she whispered. "It's *freezing* the kingdom. Holding the whole country the way it holds the water in this garden — warm, still, dead-rooted. Nothing rots because nothing lives. The fire isn't feeding us. It's pickling us." She looked up at the grates, at the thread of moonlight, and felt the doubt shift — not vanish, never vanish, but move aside like a curtain. "And when the fire runs out, everything it's held still comes due at once. It didn't lie about the famine. It lied about who caused it."

From the channel behind them, a splash — Bram, wading fast, axe still slung, his lantern held high. "Above us," he called, low and urgent. "Huntsmen on the grates. Corvel's moved men into the palace cellars — dozens. Whatever happens at solstice tomorrow, girl, we won't be walking out the way we came in."

Tamsin looked down at her hands, wrinkled white from the water, and turned them over slowly, as if the true name might be written there.

"One word," she said. "One word from a dying dragon, and I have to bet a kingdom on it." She closed her fists. "Tomorrow night, we go down to the vault. I'm going to ask it for the rest."
