## 19. The Unbinding

The water in the drowned garden was rising, and Tamsin had called it up herself.

She stood at the center of the orchard with the vial of dragon-fire cupped in both hands, and around her the cisterns answered her will, sluicing through channels cut into the stone a hundred years ago by gardeners who had never imagined their work would be used for this. The water came up to her ankles, then her knees, black and mirror-still, reflecting the solstice stars through the grates far above. Bram had said the old king planted these trees for his daughter. Now they stood drowned to their shoulders, silver-barked and leafless, like a congregation waiting for a sermon.

*The rite needs water*, Odile's marginalia had said. *Water to hold the name, the way a bowl holds light.*

Behind her, at the mouth of the cistern tunnel, Verity Crane was burning ledgers.

Tamsin could hear it — the soft wet sound of pages going into the brazier, one after another, decades of debts and bargains and bottled years going up as steam and ash. Verity had not asked permission. She had simply taken the satchel from Bram's shoulder, knelt down, and begun, with the methodical calm of a woman balancing accounts, feeding the Guild's whole history to the fire.

"You're certain," Verity said, without looking up. It wasn't a question about the ledgers.

"No," Tamsin said. "I'm certain about nothing except the hour."

"Good." Verity fed another page to the flames. "Certainty is for people who haven't read enough books."

Odile stood on Tamsin's other side, at the edge of the flooded channel, her hands folded into her sleeves like a woman waiting out bad weather. She had not spoken since they'd come down through the water. She had given Tamsin one look at the tunnel's mouth — the same look she'd given her the day she caught her reading true names off a sick ewe, half fury and half something older and more frightened — and then she had turned to face the dark end of the garden, where the thing wearing the crown would come.

It would come. They all knew that. Corvel's kindness had bought them an hour, no more; the spymaster's men were searching the upper palace, and the entity did not need huntsmen to find what it wanted. It had worn the king's face for thirty years. It knew how to walk toward a threat.

Bram Hale stood between Odile and the dark, a drawn knife in one hand and a huntsman's hooked pole in the other, and he had not stopped shaking since they entered the water. Not from cold. Tamsin had seen his hands steady over a snare line, over a dying man's throat. This was something else.

"If it offers you anything," he said quietly, "anything at all — remember Wren Amsel."

"I remember," Tamsin said.

Wren Amsel, nineteen years old, who had carried letters for the Guild and laughed with her whole body, drowned in Saltmere harbor while the crowd sang the coronation anthem. Tamsin remembered the way the water had gone still afterward, as if even the harbor were ashamed.

She looked down at the vial in her hands.

The last dragon-fire. The Guild's furnace, the crown's lifeblood, the single flame that had been renewed monthly for thirty years by a buyer who never showed his face. When she spent it, the dragon below the vaults would gutter out — she had felt its heart slowing through the stone when she touched the vial, a vast patience dimming toward its end — and the kingdom's warmest lie would go cold with it.

And the thing wearing the king's face would unravel, because it was made of borrowed time, and borrowed time is just another debt.

*Tamsin.* The voice came from everywhere in the water at once, gentle as rain on a roof. *You've read my ledgers. You know I keep my accounts honestly.*

She didn't flinch. "You're not in the ledgers," she said. "That's the first honest thing about you I ever found."

Laughter moved across the surface of the water, small ripples spreading out from nowhere. At the dark end of the garden, the torchlight bent, and the king walked out of the shadow of the trees.

He looked tired. That was the horror of it, Tamsin thought — thirty years dead and he still looked tired, the way a good man looks at the end of a long harvest, gray at the temples, kind around the eyes. The court loved him. The city loved him. Half the kingdom would have followed him into the sea.

Only his shadow moved wrong. Half a second late, always, like a man remembering steps he'd learned from a description.

*You came,* it said, using his mouth. *I hoped you would. I confess I hoped you wouldn't, too — you're the best reader I've encountered in a century, and I do hate wasting talent.*

"You killed Wren Amsel," Tamsin said.

*I wrote a law,* it said. *Men carried it out. There is a difference, and you of all people should honor it — you, who know how much of a thing lives in its name and how little in its hands.* It took another step, and the water around its boots went glassy and dead, fish-belly white. *But let us not begin with grievances. Let me make you an offer instead, since your friend the spymaster tells me you appreciate plain dealing.*

"I don't," said Tamsin.

*Then hear it anyway.* The king's face smiled, and it was a warm smile, a father's smile, and Tamsin hated how much of her wanted to believe it. *Thirty years ago I found a kingdom dying. Not the king — the land. Blight in the wheat. Rot in the orchards. A plague coming up the river valleys that would have emptied three provinces. Your gift would have drowned you in that world, girl, along with every hedge-witch and storm-speaker and name-reader from here to the mountains. I held the rot back. I have held it back for thirty years, with a dead man's face and a dragon's last breath, and the price has been paid by exactly one person: me.* Its voice softened. *Unbind me, and the blight returns within a season. Famine within two. Is that the truth you want so badly?*

For a moment — a long moment, long enough for Verity to stop feeding the fire, long enough for Bram's grip to whiten on the pole — Tamsin wavered.

Because it was almost fair. It was fair the way a well-kept ledger is fair: every column balanced, every entry justified, the human cost filed neatly under *necessary*. She thought of the ledgers in the Guild vault, all those years she'd spent admiring their elegance before she saw what they purchased.

"That's the argument you gave yourself," she said. "In the beginning. Maybe you even believed it." She raised the vial. "But I've read thirty years of your accounts, and here's what they say. You didn't stop at holding back the blight. You started selling safety. You built a hunt. You drowned children because their gifts might someday inconvenience you. You didn't save the kingdom — you foreclosed on it." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "A ledger can be balanced and still be evil. I learned that from the best bookkeeper in Saltmere."

Verity, behind her, made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

The warmth went out of the king's face all at once, like a lamp pinched out. What remained wore his features the way a glove wears a hand.

*Then we are past negotiation,* it said, and the water began to rise.

It came up fast now, answering the entity's will instead of hers, climbing her thighs, her waist, dragging at her coat, and the drowned trees groaned as the current found them. Bram shouted and drove his pole into the flood; Odile's voice cut through the roar in words Tamsin didn't know but her blood did — hedge-magic, marsh-magic, the old craft of holding ground against a river. The water slowed. Held. Boiled in place, two wills pulling it in opposite directions.

"Now, girl!" Odie screamed. "Whatever you're going to do — do it *now!*"

Tamsin opened the vial.

The dragon-fire didn't pour. It *rose*, a thread of gold unwinding upward through the black water, and where it passed the water remembered how to be water — clean, moving, alive. She felt the rite take hold of her the moment the fire touched the flood: Odile's marginalia unfolding in her mind like a hand opening, the shape of the unbinding settling over her bones. All she had to do was speak.

All she had to do was give up the word.

The dragon had given her one word of its true name, down in the vault, one syllable of a language older than kingdoms, and she had carried it for six days like a coal in her chest. Names are not free. To spend a true name is to spend a piece of what it names — and the dragon was already dying, already guttering, and every syllable she spoke of it would burn a year of its long life into the rite. She had known that. She had promised it anyway, with her hand flat against its scaled flank, and it had closed its eye and *let* her.

*Speak,* it had said. *I have been fuel for lesser fires for thirty years. Let me be fuel for a true one.*

Tamsin opened her mouth and spoke the dragon's name into the water.

The world went white and gold.

Not loud — that was the terrible part, how quiet it was. The fire spread through the flooded garden like dawn through fog, and every drop of water became a lens, and every lens held the name, and the name was *enough*. She felt the entity's grip on the flood shatter like ice underfoot. She heard Bram shout, heard Odile chanting, heard Verity — Verity, of all people, reciting figures under her breath, the numbers of the dead, Wren Amsel and the drowned and the hunted, an accounting offered up like a prayer.

And at the center of the light, the thing wearing the crown began to come apart.

It did not scream. It *unraveled*, thread by golden thread, thirty years of borrowed time paying itself back all at once, and beneath it — revealed slowly, gently, the way a sheet is drawn from a sleeping face — was the king.

His bones. His true body, preserved these thirty years in the entity's grip, seated at the base of the oldest tree with his hands folded in his lap, wearing the crown that was not a crown at all but a collar, a chain, a leash. His face was peaceful. He had died of his fever after all, Tamsin understood, all those years ago; the entity had merely refused to let the kingdom notice.

The collar cracked. The crown fell from the bone-white skull and rolled through the shallow water and came to rest against Tamsin's boot, ordinary gold, dented, small. Smaller than she had imagined any crown could be.

The light went out.

Silence rushed in like water into a broken hull. The flood drained away through the channels, down and down, seeking the level it had been denied for thirty years, and Tamsin stood in the emptying garden with ash on the wind and the vial — empty, warm, finished — still open in her hand.

Somewhere far below, through a hundred feet of stone, she felt the dragon's heart stop.

It did not feel like a death. It felt like a debt paid in full.

"Tamsin." Odile's voice, hoarse, close. Her mistress's hand on her shoulder, gripping hard enough to bruise. "Tamsin, look at me. Are you—"

"The trees," Tamsin said.

Because the trees were moving. In the dark, in the draining water, the drowned orchard was stirring — silver bark splitting, buds swelling, and from the highest branches of the old king's tree came a scent no one in that garden had smelled in thirty years.

Blossoms. Out of season, out of darkness, out of a drowned and buried garden — blossoms, opening white as the first page of a new ledger.

Above them, muffled by stone and water, the bells of the palace began to ring — all of them at once, unscheduled, uncommanded, ringing the way bells ring when no one is holding the rope.

Verity climbed to her feet, ash-smeared, ledger-less, and looked around at the blooming dark with the expression of a woman watching everything she built burn and finding, to her astonishment, that she was glad.

"Well," she said. "Someone had better go up there and explain to the court what happened to their king."

"And someone," Odile said quietly, "had better stay down here. And write down what grew."

Tamsin looked at the crown lying in the mud at her feet. Then she stepped over it, and knelt beside the old king's bones, and closed his jaw with two fingers, gently, the way you'd close a book you'd finally finished reading.

"Rest," she told him. "Your garden kept."

Then she turned toward the sound of the bells, and the water, and whatever came next.
