## 20. Ash and Aftermath

**Bram**

The pardon came on vellum, sealed in wax that still smelled of the chancery, and it was delivered by a clerk who would not meet his eyes.

Bram Hale read it twice, standing in the doorway of the Saltmere customs house while snow came down fine as sifted flour behind him. *By order of the Regency Council, acting in the name of the Crown pending the coronation of—* The name of the heir was left blank. Of course it was. There had been no heir named in eleven years, because there had been no king to name one, only a thing wearing a dead man's face and signing a dead man's hand.

The clerk cleared his throat. "You'll want to countersign."

"No," Bram said.

The clerk blinked. "It's a full pardon. All charges. Your rank restored, your—"

"I heard him." Bram folded the vellum once, precisely, the way he'd been taught to fold dispatches in a palace that had been rotting from the inside for a decade without anyone noticing. "I'll keep it. I won't sign it."

"Master Hale, I'm instructed to say that refusal may be construed as—"

"As what? Treason?" He almost laughed. The word had lost its teeth. You could not hang a man for treason against a crown that had turned out to be a lie wearing jewelry. Half the kingdom was saying so already; the other half was too busy burning effigies to listen. "Tell them I said thank you. Tell them I said the hunt is over because its author is ash, not because anyone forgave me. And tell them—" he paused, weighing how much truth a chancery clerk could carry without spilling it, "—tell them the huntsmen who want their old commissions back know where to find me. And the ones who don't never have to see my face again."

The clerk took the unsigned pardon back like a man retrieving something hot from a fire, bowed too many times, and fled into the snow.

Bram stood a while longer in the doorway. Across the harbor, they were still pulling the drowning-cages out of the water. Three days now, since Tamsin spoke the name and the thing in the crown unraveled like smoke in wind, and the work went on — winches groaning, chains rust-bright, families waiting on the quay with dry clothes held out like offerings. Some of the cages came up empty. Some did not. Wren Amsel's cage had come up on the first day, and Bram had helped carry her, because someone should, and because he had carried others before and owed the weight.

He had hunted the gifted for six years under the old decree. He had dragged a hedge-witch named Corren Thornbury from his own garden while his daughter watched from a window, though he hadn't known whose window it was until much later, when knowing did nothing but hurt. He had told Tamsin all of it, in the cistern dark, before she let him hold the lamp. She had not forgiven him. She had done something harder: she had believed him capable of being useful anyway.

That was the only commission he wanted now.

Inside the customs house, the new council was already at work — the gifted's council, though half of them flinched at the word *gifted*, preferring *the listed*, or nothing at all. They had taken a room that used to store confiscated charms and filled it with trestle tables and people who had spent their whole lives hiding what they were. An old storm-caller argued tariffs with a woman who could sweeten well-water. A boy of perhaps fourteen, who could tell when any lock within a mile was opened, sat taking minutes in a careful schoolroom hand, and nobody had asked him to hide it.

They'd asked Bram to chair. He'd refused that too. Instead he kept the door, and the list of names, and the map of every place the hunt had reached, marked in red where the drownings had happened, so that no one on the council could ever pretend they hadn't.

Marta Quill, the storm-caller, looked up as he came in, shaking snow from his shoulders. "Well?"

"Pardon offered. Pardon declined."

A murmur went around the tables — not approval exactly. Something more cautious than that. Trust was a crop that took seasons, and everyone in this room knew better than anyone alive how quickly a season could turn.

"They'll be furious," Marta said.

"They'll be busy," Bram said. "Half the regency council is trying to prove they never liked the entity. The other half is trying to prove they never noticed it. Nobody has time to be furious at a disgraced huntsman who won't take his pardon." He hung his wet cloak on a peg that had once held a confiscated weather-charm, still faintly humming, rain-smell leaking out of it like a memory. "What do we have?"

"Petitions." Marta slid a stack across the table, thick as a brick. "Forty-one this morning. People coming out of hiding. Farmers, midwives, a ferryman who swears he can smell storms three days off. They want registration. Protection. Work."

"And?"

"And we're four people and a boy with a good memory." She rubbed her eyes. "We need records, Bram. Real ones. Names, gifts, debts owed and paid — not the crown's kind of ledger. Ours."

Bram thought of another ledger then, and another keeper of it, and a Guild house on Coinmaker's Row where the shutters had been open for three days straight, which in forty years of Guild history had never once happened.

"There's a place," he said slowly, "that might have more paper than we know what to do with."

**Verity**

Verity Crane had burned the client lists herself, and she would burn them again if anyone asked her to justify it.

She stood now in the great hall of the Guild of the Gilded Root, which no longer deserved the name and was becoming, entry by entry, something else entirely. The vault doors stood open. Not unlocked — *open*, propped wide with wedges of salt-pine, their locks hanging slack, because a locked door was a promise you made to clients and the clients were gone. The shelves that had held bottled storms stood half-empty, the bottles gone to the council's custody or smashed by crowds in the first mad week, and the space where the dragon-fire vials had lived was bare except for a single card in Tamsin's neat hand: *Spent. Gladly.*

Apprentices moved through the hall with armloads of ledgers, decades of them, and laid them open on long tables beneath the high windows. Verity had ordered every book unsealed. Every cipher broken. Every entry copied into plain script and set out where anyone could read it — the prices, the buyers, the borrowed years sold to gentry who wanted to skip past grief, the memories decanted from poor men's heads and worn like jewelry by rich ones. All of it. The whole ugly, magnificent account.

Her factors thought she had gone mad. Two had resigned rather than serve in what one of them called "a charity with inventory." Verity had signed their severance personally and wished them luck finding honest work, which was cruel, but she was tired, and cruelty was cheaper than patience some days.

"You'll ruin us," her senior factor, Aldous, had said, the night she gave the order. "An archive anyone can walk into. No secrets, no leverage, no margin. The Guild is built on knowing what others don't."

"The Guild was built on selling what others couldn't get," Verity corrected. "And the last thing we secretly knew was that our best customer wasn't alive. That secret bought us forty years of comfort and cost—" she had stopped, because the number wouldn't come out even. It never would. "Cost what it cost. We are done trading in secrets, Aldous. We will trade in truth, and see if it keeps."

Truth, it turned out, kept poorly at first and then, astonishingly, began to keep.

The petitions started small. A widow wanting proof of the year her husband had sold, so she could claim his wages back from the buyer. A man who'd had his mother's face taken from his memory and wanted, if not the memory itself, at least a written description of what he'd lost. Then the scholars came, and the lawyers, and a delegation from the new council with Marta Quill at its head, asking — politely, warily — whether the Guild might be persuaded to lend its clerks to the work of registering the gifted.

Lend. As if the Guild had anything left to lend but hands and habits.

Verity had said yes before the question finished. She had watched Marta's face carefully as she said it, and seen the flicker there — the old fear of the Guild, the institution that had priced everything and protected no one — and she had not tried to argue it away. Arguing was for people with clean books.

Now she walked the long tables, reading over apprentices' shoulders. Entry after entry in her own handwriting, from her own rise through the ranks: *One storm, summer-class, sold to Lord Perrin for the drowning of his rival's fields.* *Fourteen years, purchased from a debtor's children, ages nine through twelve.* Her hand had never shaken writing those lines. It shook a little now, copying them into plain script, and she let it. She had decided that was important — that the copies be made by the same hands that made the originals, so the correction and the crime shared an author.

At the far table, a girl apprentice looked up. "Mistress Crane? This one's marked with a black ribbon. It says... it says the contents are to be destroyed on the death of the holder."

Verity crossed the hall. The ledger was old, its spine cracked, its clasp green with age. She remembered it. She remembered ordering the ribbon put on, twenty years ago, because the entries inside concerned the palace accounts and the monthly delivery to a buyer recorded only as *H.M., by hand of the Underchamberlain.*

"Destroy it?" she said. "No. Copy every word. Set it out where the light hits it." She touched the cracked spine once, lightly, the way you'd touch a scar. "That one stays open forever."

Outside, the snow kept falling on Coinmaker's Row, and for the first time in the Guild's history, its doors stayed open through the whole of a winter day, and no one who entered was turned away, and no one who read was charged.

It was, Verity thought, watching a fisherman's son sound out the words of a storm-sale with his finger under each line, the worst business decision of her career.

She found she could live with it.

**Odile**

Odile Vane came to Saltmere without announcement, which was how she did everything, and found her former apprentice mending nets on the quay because the gifted's council paid for honest labor and Tamsin Thornbury had always been stubborn about earning her bread.

Odile stood at the top of the quay steps for a moment, watching her. The girl's hands had healed crooked — the burns from the solstice rite, the price of speaking a true name with the last dragon-fire running up her arms. She tied knots badly now and tied them anyway. That was Tamsin entire. Odile had known it the day she caught the child reading the true names of her charms aloud, delighted and unafraid, and had spent eleven years trying to teach her fear, because fear was the only armor a hedge-witch could forge.

She had failed. Thank every drowned god, she had failed.

"You look terrible," Odile said, coming down the steps.

Tamsin didn't startle — she'd felt her coming, of course, the way she felt everything now, names rising off things like heat off stone. "You look older."

"I am older. Grief is expensive." Odile sat down on the step beside her, joints complaining, and took a net out of the basket without asking. Her fingers remembered the work even if her pride objected to it. For a while they mended in silence, two women and a net, while gulls argued overhead and the harbor bells rang the hour.

"I heard about your village," Tamsin said at last. Quietly. "The contract. What you burned."

"Mm."

"Odile. You traded my name to Corvel to save them. Forty-one people. You'd have let him take me."

"Yes."

"And then you burned your half of the bargain, knowing what it would cost."

"Yes." Odile pulled a knot tight. "Your point, apprentice?"

"My point is I don't understand you."

Odile laughed — a short, real laugh, the first in weeks. "Good. Understanding me was never the lesson. Surviving me was." She set the net down and looked at the girl straight on, because this next part deserved her eyes. "I disowned you in front of half of Saltmere. I called you a thief and a liar and a disappointment, and I did it so the hunt would pass over you, and I have regretted the necessity of it every day since, and I would do it again tomorrow. Both of those are true. If you've come to some tidy resolution where one cancels the other, throw it back. The water's full enough."

Tamsin was quiet a long moment. Out past the breakwater, they were raising a marker buoy where Wren Amsel's cage had come up — white wood, no inscription yet. The council was arguing about inscriptions. Tamsin had argued for names, all of them, carved deep.

"I'm not tidy," Tamsin said finally. "I'm angry at you. I think I'll be angry at you for years. You taught me that love and treachery can wear the same coat, and now I check every coat."

"Excellent. That's the whole curriculum."

"But—" Tamsin's voice caught, and she hated it, Odile could see her hating it. "But you came. When it mattered. In the end, every time it mattered, you came."

"I did." Odile picked the net back up. Her hands were steadier doing something. "And I'll keep coming, probably, at inconvenient moments, with terrible advice. Old habits." She glanced sideways. "Unless you'd rather I didn't."

Tamsin looked out at the white buoy, and at the city above it where the palace stood empty now, its windows dark, its king finally allowed to be dead. Then she looked at the net between them, both their hands on it, mending.

"Stay for supper," she said. "I'm not pretending it's all mended either. But it holds. Like this." She tugged the net, showing the join — the old tear still visible under the new knots, if you knew where to look. "You can see the damage. It just doesn't pull apart anymore."

Odile examined the join with a craftsman's eye and found it, against all expectation, sound.

"It'll do," she said, which from her was a benediction, and they worked until the light went.

**Tamsin**

On the last evening of the year, Tamsin climbed to the customs house attic where the council kept its growing archive, and found Bram waiting with a lantern and a question.

"We've had an offer," he said. "From Crane. The Guild will register every gifted soul who wants registering — free, permanent, in their own words. Their vaults for storage. Their clerks for the writing." He hesitated. "There's a condition."

Of course there was. Everything had conditions; she'd learned that in ledgers. "Which is?"

"They want a keeper of names. Someone the council trusts and the Guild doesn't control. Someone who can read what's written and what isn't." Bram set the lantern down between them, and the light made his scarred face look almost gentle. "Quill wants you. So does Crane, God help us both. And honestly, Tam — so do I. But it's your choice. Nobody's handing you anything. Not anymore."

Tamsin looked at the shelves around her — the petitions, the red-marked maps, the lists of the living replacing the lists of the drowned — and felt the old familiar pull, the same one that had made her steal a worthless ledger in a marsh town and read it like scripture. Books of names. She had run from being bound to one woman's kitchen fire, and she had ended here, being asked to bind herself to a whole city's remembering.

But this was different, and she made herself say why, out loud, so it would stay true.

"I'll do it," she said, "if the ledger stays open. Anyone can read their own entry. Anyone can strike it. Anyone can leave." She met Bram's eyes. "I was someone's secret once. Never again — theirs or mine."

Bram smiled, slow and unpracticed, like a man remembering how. "I'll carry your terms tonight."

He gathered his cloak, and the lantern, and went down the stairs, and Tamsin stayed alone in the attic among the papers, listening to the wind come off the harbor — cleaner than it had been in years, the storms in their bottles all freed or broken, the sky keeping its own weather now.

Below her, somewhere in the dark, water moved through the old cisterns beneath the palace, through the drowned garden where green shoots had been reported, impossibly, in the dead of winter. She had promised herself she would go down and see it when the thaw came.

She opened a fresh ledger to its first page, dipped her pen, and wrote the heading she intended to keep for the rest of her life:

*A Ledger of Small Mercies — kept openly, by Tamsin Thornbury, who owes nothing and chooses this.*

Then, because the page deserved an honest beginning, she added the first entry: a ferryman who could smell storms three days off, registered in his own words, afraid and hopeful and, at last, written down.

Outside, the year turned.
