## 22. A New Entry, in Her Own Hand

The girl arrived on the first cold morning of autumn, soaked to the knees and lying about her name.

Tamsin watched her come up the garden path from the doorway of the counting house — because that was what they still called it, though no coin had passed through it in a year. The path was new, laid with stones salvaged from the palace cisterns when the water finally drained, and each one had been carried up by hand by someone who remembered what it was to be hidden. The girl walked it like she expected it to be taken away from her.

She stopped at the gate. She didn't knock. Runaways never knocked; knocking meant asking permission, and permission was the first thing you learned to stop expecting.

"You're the Thornbury woman," the girl said. Her voice had the particular flatness of someone who had rehearsed being brave.

"I'm called that."

"They say you can read names. True ones." The girl's eyes went, briefly and involuntarily, to the ledger shelf visible through the open door — the long wall of them now, the Guild's old ledgers and the new ones, bound in undyed linen instead of gilded leather. "They say you keep a book where people write themselves down."

"Who says?"

"People in the marsh towns. People who used to be afraid." The girl lifted her chin. "My name's—"

"Don't," Tamsin said. "Not yet. Come inside first. There's tea, and there's bread if Verity hasn't eaten it all, which she will have, so there's tea."

The girl hesitated at the threshold the way Tamsin herself had once hesitated at Odile's door, eleven years ago, with rain in her boots and nothing in her pockets but a stolen charm. Some thresholds you crossed carrying everything you owned. Some you crossed carrying only the certainty that going back was worse.

The girl crossed.

* * *

Her name, when she finally gave it — three days later, over the dregs of the evening tea, unprompted, the way true things always came — was Sef. Just Sef. She'd been apprenticed to a weather-worker in the eastern fens, a man who bottled fog for farmers and kept his apprentices illiterate so they couldn't read his contracts. She'd run when she found out what he'd written her into: seven years of service for room and board, renewable at his discretion, in perpetuity, if she failed to meet terms she wasn't allowed to know.

"He said the gift would rot my brain," Sef said. "He said reading names was how the drownings started, back before. He said I should be grateful he kept me ignorant."

"What did you say?"

"Nothing. I took his good knife and his second-best coat and I left in the night." A pause. "I feel bad about the knife."

"Keep feeling bad about the knife," Tamsin said. "It means you're not him. But don't give it back."

Sef laughed — a startled, rusty sound, like a hinge unused for years — and something in Tamsin's chest that had been clenched since the solstice loosened one further turn.

This was the work now. Not the great rite, not the burning of the last fire, not the entity unraveling off the dead king's face like smoke off a snuffed candle. That had taken one night and cost everything it cost. This took every day, and cost almost nothing, and mattered more.

She taught Sef letters first. Then ledgers — how to read one, how to question one, how to find the lie hiding in a tidy column of figures. *A ledger is just a story that pretends it isn't one,* she told her, echoing Verity, who echoed someone older still. *Every entry is a sentence somebody chose to write, which means every entry could have been written differently.*

"And names?" Sef asked, on the fourth week, when the frost had come and the first of the apple trees — planted where dragon-fire had fallen, in the black rich soil the draining water left behind — were dropping their leaves. "When do I learn to read those?"

"When you can tell me why you might need to refuse."

Sef frowned. "Refuse?"

"To read a name. To speak one. Someone will ask you, someday — maybe kindly, maybe with money, maybe with a knife. Reading a true name is power, and power gets borrowed, and borrowed things get spent by whoever holds them." Tamsin turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "I knew a woman who taught me everything about names and nothing about saying no. It nearly killed me, and it did kill some other people. So. When you can tell me why you might refuse, we'll begin."

Sef thought about it for a long time. Outside, Bram was splitting wood in the yard, each stroke landing with the patient rhythm of a man who had finally stopped listening for hoofbeats. Somewhere down the slope, in the archive hall, Verity was arguing with three village clerks about interest rates, loudly, joyfully, because Verity had discovered that an honest ledger could be argued over without anyone drowning, and she intended to enjoy it.

"Why I might refuse," Sef said at last. "Because if I can read a name, I can unmake someone. And if I can do that, then someone can make me do it." She looked up. "Is that right?"

"That's right."

"Then teach me."

* * *

Odile came at midwinter, as she always did now, arriving without announcement and staying exactly as long as she pleased, which was never quite long enough and always slightly too long.

They had reconciled the way scars reconcile with skin — not by forgetting the wound but by agreeing to grow around it. There had been no apology, not in words. Odile didn't apologize in words; she apologized in marginalia. The winter after the unbinding, Tamsin had found, tucked into her copy of the solstice rite, a note in her former mistress's cramped hand: *You were right about the third clause. You were also right to leave. Both things are true. I am learning to hold two truths, which is harder than hedge-magic.* It was the closest thing to *I love you* that Odile Grieve would ever commit to paper, and Tamsin had pressed it flat under the inkstone and kept it there ever since.

Now Odile sat by the fire in the counting house, drinking tea she complained about, watching Sef practice name-reading on a bowl of river stones.

"The girl has your ear," she said. "That terrible patience. You used to sit like that over my ledgers, waiting for the numbers to confess."

"They usually did."

"Mm." Odile turned a stone over in her fingers — she refused, on principle, to let a student's exercise pass unexamined. "You know what I never taught you? What I couldn't, because I didn't have it?" She set the stone down. "How to be given a name freely. Everything I learned, I learned taking. Contracts, bargains, leverage. Even love, God help me, I treated as a debt to be serviced." Her eyes flicked to Tamsin, dry and unflinching. "You built something else here. I want it noted that I noticed."

"Noted," Tamsin said. "In whose ledger?"

"Mine." And Odile smiled, thin and real, and Tamsin understood that this too was an apology, and accepted it as such.

* * *

Spring came to the drowned garden the way spring comes everywhere — messily, and all at once.

The orchard bloomed. The old king's trees, drowned for thirty years beneath the palace, had survived their flooding the way gifted children survive: stubbornly, root-deep, waiting. Where the last dragon-fire had fallen during the unbinding — where Tamsin had knelt in the black water and spoken a word older than the crown — the ground stayed warm even in frost, and things grew there out of season. Rosemary in January. A fig tree nobody had planted, fruiting twice a year.

People came to see it. At first they came quietly, hooded, the way people came to shrines in the bad years. Then less quietly. Then a family arrived from the coast with saplings and asked permission to plant, and Tamsin stood at the gate and heard herself say *yes, plant anything, plant everything,* and watched a woman cry into her own sleeves because permission was still, for some of them, a foreign language.

The gifted came out of hiding the way the garden drained — slowly, then all at once. Some took work in the archive. Some took work nowhere and simply sat in the sun, which Verity declared, after due deliberation, to be a legitimate use of a human life, though she recorded it grudgingly in the margins.

And the ledger of names filled.

That was Tamsin's book — the one Sef had asked about, the one word had traveled through the marsh towns about. Not debts. Names. Anyone could enter themselves: their true name if they wished, freely given, or a chosen name, or no name at all, just the fact of them — *I was here, I survived, I am planting beans by the south wall.* Some entries were a single line. Some ran pages. One man wrote only: *I forgive myself, effective this date.* Verity had entered the Guild itself, formally, as a member: *The Guild of the Gilded Root, re-founded, owing nothing to anyone.* Bram entered with his full name and rank both struck through, and beneath it: *Bram Hale. Woodcutter. Better at this.*

Tamsin read the entries every evening. It was, she had decided, the only accounting that mattered — not what was owed, but what was given.

* * *

On the anniversary of the solstice, she climbed down into the cisterns one last time.

The water was gone now, pumped and channeled into irrigation ditches by engineers who owed the Guild favors and were happy to pay them honestly. The drowned garden lay open to the air for the first time in thirty years, its stone arches dripping green, its paths soft with moss. In the center, where the rite had burned, the stone was fused glass-smooth, and the warmth still rose from it faintly, like a hearth banked rather than dead.

The dragon was gone. Of course it was. The last fire had gone into the unbinding, and the great body had crumbled to ash and bone-meal within days, and Verity had wept over the inventory loss with genuine grief before declaring, hoarsely, that some entries deserved to be closed. They buried the bones in the orchard above, under the fig tree. On windy nights Tamsin sometimes thought she could hear the ash shifting, but she suspected that was just the world settling into its new shape, the way any body does after it stops holding a lie.

She knelt at the glass-smooth stone. She had brought the ledger of names, and ink, and a pen.

For a year she had known what her final entry would be. She had written everyone else's possibility — the space for them, the freedom of it. But her own name, her true name, the one the gift had shown her when she was small and frightened and reading impossible things in her mistress's ledgers — she had kept it. Habit. Armor. The last locked drawer in a house otherwise opened to the light.

But armor was for the hunt, and the hunt was over. Had been over for a year. The king's bones rested; the entity was unwound; the drownings were history that children already learned about the way children learn everything terrible — quickly, and with questions adults found hard to answer.

She dipped the pen.

*Tamsin Thornbury,* she wrote, and then, beneath it, the true name, the deep one, the syllables that hummed against the teeth like the memory of fire. Writing it felt like setting down a weight she had forgotten she carried. Speaking it aloud to the empty garden felt like breathing after a long dive.

Nothing burned. Nothing unraveled. The name simply settled into the page, one entry among hundreds, freely given, asking nothing.

Above her, muffled through thirty feet of stone and earth, she heard Sef calling down the cistern stair — the girl's voice cracked halfway between child and woman now, impatient, laughing: *"Mara! Mara, the figs are ripe, and Verity says if we don't pick them today she's entering it in the ledger as negligence—"*

Tamsin closed the book. She pressed her palm once, briefly, to the warm stone — thanks, or farewell, or just touch, which was most of what either of those ever was — and stood.

"Coming," she called, and climbed toward the light, toward the orchard, toward the girl waiting at the top with juice-stained hands and a whole unwritten ledger of her own.
