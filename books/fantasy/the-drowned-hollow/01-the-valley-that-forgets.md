## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The fog came off the lake before dawn the way it always did, sliding up the reed banks and over the ferry landing like something patient that had learned to live indoors. Zephyrine Thackeray stood at the tiller of his flat-bottomed boat and let it wash around him, cold and smelling of silt and drowned leaves, and he did not think about how easy it would be to part it.

That was the discipline. Not the rowing—rowing was nothing, thirty years of it had made his shoulders into a yoke—but the *not*. The fog lay thick as wool across the water, and a single word in the old script, spoken low, would have opened a clean lane through it wide enough for two carts. He knew the word. He had known it since he was nine years old, when Aldous had caught him whispering it at the kitchen hearth and gone so pale that the boy had understood, without being told, that some words were not for speaking.

So he rowed. He put his back into the oars like any other man in the Hollow who made his living on the water, and the fog closed behind him, and by the time the first gray light found the valley he was only what he appeared to be: a ferryman, broad and quiet, taking the morning crossing.

The Drowned Hollow sat in its bowl of hills like water in a cupped hand. No road led out that a cart could manage; no river ran in. The lake filled the valley floor's lower third, black and still, and above it the village climbed the slope in crooked tiers—smokehouses, a mill, the terraced gardens where Bramble grew things that had no business growing this far north. Fog lived here the way rats lived in other towns. It never fully lifted. On clear days it thinned to a gauze overhead and you could see the ridgeline, the dark teeth of pines, and once—so the old stories said—you could see stars. Zephyrine had seen stars exactly twice in his life, both times as a child, both times with Aldous standing beside him saying nothing at all in a way that felt like a warning.

He tied up at the north landing as the light strengthened. Three passengers waited under the lean-to: the cooper's wife with a basket of eels, old Master Corvin with his ledger and his perpetual grievance about the price of salt, and one of the twins—Fen, by the mud on her boots—sitting on the edge of the dock with her legs dangling over the black water in a way that had given Zephyrine gray hairs since she was six.

"Morning, Zeph," said the cooper's wife.

"Morning." He took their coins, counted them twice because Corvin watched him do it, and pushed off.

The crossing took eleven minutes if you rowed honestly. Fen sat in the bow and trailed her fingers in the wake.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm never late."

"You were late Tuesday."

"Tuesday I stopped to pull Sorrel out of the shallows after she fell through the ice. You're welcome, by the way. She never thanked me either."

Fen grinned at him, quick and unrepentant, twelve years old and all elbows. "She says she'd have gotten out herself."

"She was blue as a corpse."

"She says that's dramatic."

Zephyrine rowed. Under his palms, where they wrapped the oar-handles, he could feel it again—that faint hum, like a struck string buried deep in his bones, the thing he thought of privately as the singing. It woke on mornings like this, when the fog lay heavy and the lake lay still, and it wanted things. It wanted him to speak. It wanted the fog parted, the water stilled or raised, the world *arranged*, and it pressed against the inside of his skin like a cat against a door.

He breathed through it. In through the nose, out slow, the way Aldous had taught him before he'd taught him anything else. *The hands are just hands, Zeph. A tool that hums is still a tool. You decide what it builds.*

Most days it worked. Most days the singing faded to nothing by midmorning and stayed nothing, and he was only a ferryman with sore shoulders and a bad knee coming on, and the Hollow went about its small business around him without ever looking twice.

* * *

The Ferry Inn stood where the landing path met the hill road, squat and timbered and older than anyone could prove, with a sign showing a boat and a lantern that hadn't been repainted in living memory. Bramble Voss had kept it for twenty years, and before her, her mother, and before that a line of innkeepers stretching back into the fog of the valley's founding like the rungs of a ladder going down into dark water.

Zephyrine came in through the kitchen door at midmorning, because that was where he always came in, and found Bramble elbow-deep in bread dough and arguing with nobody.

"—and I'm telling you, if Corvin raises the salt toll again I'll drown him in his own ledger, see if I don't—oh, good, you're back. Bread's on the board. Don't touch the loaves for the evening trade, but the end of yesterday's is yours if you want it."

He wanted it. He cut the heel off the day-old loaf, dragged it through the dripping pan, and ate standing up while Bramble punched dough with the grim rhythm of a woman settling accounts.

"How was the water?" she asked.

"Cold."

"It's always cold."

"It's always cold," he agreed.

She glanced at him then—the glance she probably thought was casual, the one she'd been giving him for years, quick as a fish rising. Looking for whatever it was she looked for. He'd never asked. Asking would mean there was something to find.

"Sorrel's been asking after you," Bramble said, back to the dough. "She wants to know if you'll take them out on the lake Saturday. Properly out, past the shallows. She's got a notion to see the drowned church spire when the fog thins."

"The fog won't thin Saturday."

"She's got a *notion*, Zeph."

He looked at the window, where the fog pressed against the glass like a face. "It's deep out there. Cold past the shallows. If anything happened—"

"Nothing will happen. You've been running that boat since before they were born." Bramble turned, wiping her hands on her apron, and gave him the look she saved for arguments she intended to win. "They're twelve. They want to see the spire. And I can't take them myself because my knees are seventy years old, and Aldous is no use on water, and you—" she paused, and something gentler moved through her voice, "—you're the closest thing to an uncle those girls have. So say yes."

He said yes. He always said yes; that was the shape his life had taken, the way water takes the shape of the basin holding it. He ate his bread, and carried a cask of ale up from the cellar for her because his shoulders were already ruined anyway, and went out the front to sit on the bench by the door where the afternoon sun—if the sun bothered to visit—came first.

The twins found him there within the hour, as they always did. They came down the hill road at a dead run, Sorrel ahead, Fen shouting something about fairness, and they collapsed onto the bench on either side of him like it was furniture built for three.

"Sorrel says you'll take us to the spire," Fen said.

"Bramble says I'll take you to the spire."

"Same thing." Sorrel leaned against his arm, damp-haired from running, and pointed down the slope toward the lake. From here, in the thinning afternoon, you could just make out the place where the water went darker—a patch of deeper black, maybe forty rods offshore. Beneath it, sixty feet down, stood the old village. The first village. The one the valley had flooded on purpose, though no one liked to say why, and whose church spire still rose near enough to the surface that on the rare clear days its tip broke the water like a drowned man's finger.

"They say people lived down there," Sorrel said. "Before."

"People lived everywhere before."

"Do you think they minded? Being flooded?"

"Fen, don't start," said Fen, in Sorrel's voice, and then, in her own: "She does this every time we go near the shore. Mam says it's because Gran used to tell her the lake dreams."

"Does it?" Sorrel asked Zephyrine, seriously, the way she asked everything.

He looked at the water. Black, patient, holding its drowned streets and its drowned church and whatever else it held beneath the fog. Something in his hands hummed, very faintly, and he pressed his palms flat against his knees until it stopped.

"No," he said. "Lakes don't dream. Lakes are just water with nowhere to go."

Sorrel considered this. "That's sad."

"That's lakes."

* * *

Aldous came at dusk, as he came every dusk, walking down from the cottage above the orchard with his stick and his stoop and his scholar's shawl against the damp. He was the nearest thing the Hollow had to a learned man—he could read, which set him apart; he kept the valley's records, such as they were, in a chest of books no one else touched—and he had been Zephyrine's guardian since before Zephyrine could remember anything else. There was no before-Aldous. There was a kitchen with a fire in it, and a stern voice teaching him letters, and a hand catching his wrist the first time the humming started, and that was the beginning of the world.

They ate together at the inn most evenings, the three of them plus whichever twins had wormed their way in, and tonight was no different. Stew, bread, Bramble's weak beer. Aldous talked about the mill accounts. Fen argued about the mill accounts. Sorrel drew a church spire in spilled ale on the table and made it talk in a tiny voice.

And underneath all of it, Aldous watched him.

Zephyrine had known that watchfulness so long he had stopped seeing it, the way you stop hearing a stream outside your window. But tonight, for no reason he could name, he saw it. Saw the gray eyes flick to his hands—resting flat on the table, deliberately flat, the way he always kept them at supper—and away again. Saw the small pause before Aldous laughed at Fen's joke, half a heartbeat too long, as if laughter were arithmetic he had to check.

"Aldous," Zephyrine said, when the girls had gone up to bed and Bramble had gone to her cellar. The fire had burned low. "Can I ask you something?"

"You may ask me anything. You always may."

"The wards." He wasn't sure why he said it. He'd never said it. In thirty years neither of them had ever said it directly, circling the word the way you circled a hole in the ice. "The ones the founders set. The ones that keep the fog in and the—" he chose the word carefully, "—the rest out. Do they ever need tending? Do they ever change?"

Aldous set down his cup. Slowly. His face did nothing at all, which was itself an answer, because Aldous's face always did something—he was a man of eyebrows and sighs and dry commentary, a face like weather.

"Why do you ask?"

"The animals were quiet this morning. All of them. The birds didn't sing at first light. Even the frogs off the reeds." He had noticed it at the landing, before dawn, and told himself it was weather. He was telling himself that now. "And the fog came up wrong. It usually comes off the water in ribbons. Today it came all at once, like a tide. Like something pushed it."

Outside, the fog pressed against the windows. Inside, the fire ticked.

"Weather," Aldous said at last, gently. "Valleys make their own weather, Zeph. You know that. The hills funnel it, the lake breathes it out. The birds were quiet because the air pressure changed—they feel it before we do. Nothing more."

"Right," Zephyrine said. "Nothing more."

But Aldous was still holding his cup, and his knuckles were white around it, and he set it down with a small careful click as though it might break, and smiled, and the smile reached everything except his eyes.

"Finish your stew," he said. "You've a morning crossing, and the fog will be thick."

* * *

He walked home along the shore path because he always walked home along the shore path, past the drying racks and the empty moorings, the lake on his left breathing its cold into the dark. The fog had come all at once today. Like a tide. Like something pushed.

At the bend where the path ran closest to the water, he stopped.

He couldn't have said why. There was nothing to see—the same black water, the same faint suggestion of the drowned spire breaking the surface far out, silvered with mist. But the humming in his hands had risen, sudden and strong, stronger than it had been in years, a chord instead of a note, and it pulled toward the lake the way a compass needle pulls, and for one unguarded moment he almost let it speak. Almost opened his mouth and gave it the word sitting ready behind his teeth, the word for *show me*.

He clenched his fists instead. Breathed. Counted, the way he'd counted since he was nine.

The humming sank. The lake lay still. Somewhere out in the dark, a bird cried once—a single raw note over the water—and then the silence came down again, total, like a lid.

Zephyrine stood a moment longer, watching the spire's faint tip in the mist, and told himself, one more time, that it was weather.

Behind him, far up the valley, a dog began to howl. Then another. Then all of them at once, every dog in the Drowned Hollow, crying at the sky.

He turned to look, and above the ridge where the stars should have been—where he remembered, from childhood, from those two impossible nights, that stars were—

There was nothing. Only fog, and the black hills, and nine points of darkness where light ought to be, arranged in a pattern he knew, that he had known since he was nine years old, drawn in the margins of a book Aldous had burned the following spring.

Zephyrine Thackeray stood alone on the shore of the lake that had drowned a village, and felt, for the first time in thirty years, afraid.
