## 2. Nine Stars Dark

The night the stars began to die, Zephyrine was mending a net.

He sat on the inn's back step with the lantern turned low, because Bramble complained that lamplight drew moths and moths drew Sorrel, and Sorrel at eleven had already eaten more moths than any child in the Hollow's history, fried or otherwise. The net spread across his knees in a gray web, and his fingers worked the knots without him—over, under, pull, cinch—the way they'd done for nine years. It was good work, mindless work, the kind of work a ferryman was supposed to do with his evenings.

The kind of work that kept his hands from remembering what else they could do.

Overhead, the sky was doing something wrong.

Zephyrine noticed it the way he noticed most things: slowly first, and then all at once, the way ice gives way beneath a boot. He had been looking up between knots—he always looked up; a man who lived beside deep water learned to check the sky the way other men checked their purses—and he had thought at first it was cloud. Thin cloud, high cloud, the kind that smears the stars like breath on glass.

But there was no wind tonight. The fog lay against the valley walls like something sleeping, and above the fog the air was clean and black and full of the ordinary constellations he had spent years learning to see as nothing but pretty lights.

One of them went out.

Not dimmed. Not veiled. *Went out*, the way a candle goes when pinched—there one heartbeat, gone the next, leaving behind not darkness but an absence so clean his eye kept sliding to where it had been and finding nothing, a hole in the fabric of the night shaped exactly like a star.

Zephyrine's hands stopped moving.

He sat very still, net across his lap, and watched, and told himself what he always told himself: you are a ferryman. Ferries do not read omens. Ferries carry cabbages and grandmothers and once, memorably, a bull calf that screamed the entire crossing. Whatever happens overhead is for priests and kings, and there are no priests or kings here, only fog and fish and Bramble's terrible stew.

A second star went out.

His heart did not speed up. That was the training in him, older than the valley, deeper than the name Zephyrine Thackeray: the stillness that came before speech, the held-breath quiet of a man listening for the word underneath the world. Nine years of practice at being ordinary, and it all fell away in two heartbeats, and he was what he had always been underneath—a swordspeaker, oath-bound, last of his order—sitting on a back step with a broken net, watching his dead teachers' warnings come true one star at a time.

Because he knew this. That was the worst of it. Somewhere in him, in the sealed room where he kept everything he had been before Aldous carried him down into the Hollow half-dead, there were lessons by candlelight, a voice like dry paper reciting the old catalogues: *the signs of the outer dark, the ninefold failing, the pattern by which the enemy tests the walls.*

Nine stars. A ninefold failing.

He counted as they died. The third and fourth went together, twin points above the western ridge, and the fifth alone, and then a long terrible pause during which a fox somewhere on the hillside began barking and stopped mid-note, cut off as if a hand had closed over its mouth, and Zephyrine understood that the animals were hearing it too—not the stars, but whatever the dying stars meant, some pressure changing in the world the way pressure changes before a storm, felt by everything wild long before anything tame.

The sixth star. The seventh.

The lake had gone absolutely flat. Not calm—flat, the way water goes when something beneath it is holding its breath. Zephyrine could see the inn's lantern reflected in it whole and undisturbed, a perfect little flame floating on black glass, and he hated it. Water should move. Water should always move. His order had taught him that stillness in living water was the oldest omen of all, older than stars, and the lesson came back to him now in a voice he could almost hear, and the voice was saying what such stillness always meant:

*Something below has noticed something above.*

The eighth star failed while he was still staring at the lake, and he caught himself leaning forward, lips parting, and realized with cold horror that his mouth had begun to shape the ninth word of the ninefold catalogue—the word that named what was happening, the word that would have answered it, the word that, spoken aloud with the gift behind it, would have rung through every ward in the valley like a bell struck inside a tomb.

He closed his mouth. Pressed his lips together until they hurt. Put his hand flat against the doorframe and let the honest, stupid, splintery wood of it ground him. *You are a ferryman. You mend nets. You are no one.*

The ninth star held.

That was wrong too. In the old teachings the ninefold failing completed itself, all nine in a single hour, a signature written across heaven. But this ninth star—the brightest of the pattern, hanging low over the southern rim where the wards ran thickest—it stayed lit, trembling slightly, like a flame deciding whether to go on burning. And Zephyrine, watching it shudder, felt the hair rise along his arms and understood what the hesitation meant, though he could not have said how he knew:

Something outside the walls was waiting. Watching to see if anyone inside would answer the first eight.

*Don't,* he told it silently, and the word wasn't a swordspeech-word, just a man's desperate thought, but he put everything into it anyway. *Don't answer. Don't look. We're nobody.*

The star steadied. Burned on. And the pressure that had been building in the night—for that was the only word he had for it, a pressure, a thumb pressed slowly against the skin of the world—eased by a degree, the way a fist loosens when it decides, for now, not to strike.

Far off, past the southern ridge, beyond the fog, beyond everything, something howled. Long and rising, many-voiced, and it was not wolves. Wolves do not grieve. Wolves do not sing in unison toward a light that has gone out. It lasted the length of three heartbeats and stopped as cleanly as it began, and after it the silence of the valley was total—not peaceful silence, but held silence, the whole hollow of the world listening.

Zephyrine sat until his legs went stiff, and the ninth star burned steady, and the lake did not move, and eventually he heard footsteps on the path behind him, unhurried, familiar, and knew them before he turned.

Aldous.

* * *

His mentor came around the corner of the inn with a shawl over his shoulders and his white hair loose, carrying two cups of Bramble's chicory brew, which tasted like punishment and warmth in equal measure. He was seventy-three and moved like a much younger man pretending to be his age, which was itself a kind of lie Zephyrine had never let himself think about directly.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Aldous said.

"The stars," Zephyrine said.

"Mm." Aldous lowered himself onto the step with a grunt, handed him a cup, and followed his gaze upward with the mild, squinting expression of a farmer assessing weather. "Cloud's thin tonight. Good night for it."

"Aldous." Zephyrine kept his voice level. "Eight stars went out."

"Did they." Not a question. Aldous sipped his brew.

"In a pattern. Over about an hour. And the animals—" He stopped. Made himself say it plainly, like a man reporting a broken fence. "Everything went silent. All at once. And there was a howling from the south that wasn't wolves."

"Migrating birds can sound strange at night."

"Birds don't stop mid-bark, Aldous."

"Foxes, then." His mentor smiled at him, and it was the same smile he'd worn for nine years, warm and creased and utterly reliable, the smile of the man who had pulled Zephyrine from a river of blood when he was seventeen and asked no questions and taught him knots and ferries and how to be small. "You've been jumpy since summer, lad. Too much time alone on the water. Your head gets full of ghosts."

It was masterful. That was the thing Zephyrine couldn't stop noticing, sitting there with the cup going cold in his hands. It wasn't a bad lie. Bad lies have seams; you can feel them if you know where to press. This one had no seams at all. *Migrating birds. Thin cloud. Jumpy lad.* Each piece of it was true-adjacent, comfortable, sized exactly right for a ferryman's understanding, delivered with the exact fond exasperation of a guardian humoring a frightened boy.

Which meant Aldous had prepared it.

Or—no. Worse. Which meant Aldous didn't need to prepare it, because lying to Zephyrine about the sky had become as natural to him over nine years as breathing, a skill worn smooth with use.

"You didn't even look up," Zephyrine said quietly.

"I'm looking now."

"You knew which patch of sky. Before I said."

Aldous drank his chicory. Above the southern ridge, the ninth star trembled on, stubborn, a lit wick in a blown-out lamp, and Zephyrine watched his mentor watch it—or rather, watched him decline to watch it, the careful way his eyes passed over that quarter of the heavens without ever quite landing, a man stepping around a grave in the dark.

"There are things in old stories about stars," Aldous said at last, conversational. "Sailors' tales. When I was young they said a dark star meant a death at sea." He shrugged inside his shawl. "I've buried enough friends, Zeph. I don't borrow trouble from the sky. The sky's farther away than our problems, and ours are real ones—the ferry rope's rotting, and Bramble's roof leaks over the twins' room, and Sorrel ate a moth again yesterday, a live one, on a dare." He patted Zephyrine's knee with a spotted hand, twice, the way you'd soothe a horse. "Whatever's burning up there or isn't, it's a long way off, and we're down here in the fog where the world forgot us. That's the bargain of this place, lad. The rest of creation gets the stars. We get each other. Fair trade, I've always thought."

And God help him, it almost worked. That was the horror of it—that some tired, starving corner of Zephyrine *wanted* it to work, wanted to set down the eight dead stars and the flat lake and the grieving howl and pick up the ferry rope and the leaking roof instead, wanted to be only a ferryman so badly that he nearly nodded and finished his cup and went to bed.

But he had been trained by the people who wrote the ninefold catalogue, and one lesson had survived every year of forgetting: *when an old man explains too smoothly, count what he doesn't say.*

What Aldous didn't say: that he hadn't come outside because of insomnia. That he'd come the moment the eighth star failed—Zephyrine had heard the inn door, had registered it without thinking, and the timing was exact. What he didn't say: that a man who truly believed in sailors' tales would have looked at the sky. What he didn't say, most of all, was anything at all about the ninth star still burning, when the whole shape of the omen hung on it, when any curious soul would ask *why eight and not nine?*

Zephyrine asked.

"Why did the last one stay lit?"

The pause was small. A lesser watcher would have missed it—one sip of chicory delayed half a heartbeat—but Zephyrine had spent nine years studying this man the way the man perhaps studied him, and he saw the pause the way you see a single false stitch in a net.

"Maybe it's shy," Aldous said, and laughed, and stood, and gathered the empty cups, and said he was for bed, and touched Zephyrine's shoulder on the way in—an old man's affectionate weight, brief and warm and, tonight, feeling exactly like a hand checking a lock.

Then the door shut, and Zephyrine was alone with the lake.

* * *

He walked down to the water because he couldn't not.

The shore path was familiar enough to walk blind—shingle, then reeds, then the flat stone where the ferry tied up—and he went down it in the dark without a lantern, because some animal part of him did not want to be seen moving toward the water tonight, even by the fog.

At the landing, the lake lay flat as poured pewter, and the ninth star floated in it, low over the southern rim, burning its uncertain burn. Zephyrine crouched at the waterline and did the one thing he had sworn, nine years ago, bleeding in Aldous's arms, never to do again.

He reached for his gift.

Just a touch. Just the way you might test a scar, pressing to feel whether it still hurts. The swordspeech lived in him like a second pulse, coiled in the wrists and throat, and he found it there, awake, attentive, straining southward like a dog at the end of a chain—and it was *straining*, that was the new thing, nine years it had slept dull and heavy as ballast and tonight it leaned toward the southern ridge with a longing that scared him worse than the dead stars, because the gift only strained toward one kind of thing.

Other speech. Other power. Something out there, past the wards, talking.

Or something down there, under the flat water, answering.

He withdrew his hand from the pulse as if from a stove and knelt there breathing, and the fog hung close around him, and the valley held its silence, and far above the ninth star went on refusing to die.

*They tested the walls,* he thought. *Eight knocks, and the ninth held, and now they're listening for who answers.*

Let it hold, he prayed, to no one, to the fog, to the dead order that had left him alive by accident. Let it burn forever. Let me mend nets until my hands forget.

Behind him, up the slope, a light came on in Aldous's window—his mentor, supposedly abed—and through the curtain Zephyrine saw the silhouette bend and reach, and saw, for just a moment before the shape moved clear, the gleam of something long and slender being lifted from where it had lain forty years under a floorboard.

Something the shape of a staff.

The silhouette straightened. Held it. Turned it slowly, considering, the way a man considers a tool he had hoped never to need.

Zephyrine rose from the waterline, wet gravel clinging to his knees, and made himself climb back toward the inn with an ordinary man's pace, an ordinary man's face, while behind him the ninth star burned and the lake did not move and somewhere beneath its flat black glass, patient as sediment, something old began—at last, after forty long years—to stir.
