## 3. The Ward-Keeper's Daughter

The wound in Isolde's side had stopped bleeding an hour ago, which was worse than the bleeding, because it meant she could no longer pretend she was going to die quickly and cleanly on the road. Now she would have to die slowly, or—far more frightening—not at all.

She walked because stopping meant thinking. The forest around her was the wrong kind of quiet. Not the hush of winter or the held breath of a hunting ground, but a silence with texture to it, like wool packed into her ears. For three days since the border, every sound had arrived slightly late and slightly flattened, as though the world were being played through a wall. She had told herself it was blood loss. She had told herself many things.

Her father's voice kept arriving too, unflattened, perfectly clear, which was its own cruelty. *You will go to the Hollow, Isolde. You will find the swordspeaker. You will tell him nothing.* And then, softer, the version of him that had pressed the ward-shard into her palm at the river crossing with hands that shook: *Tell him everything. Forgive me for not knowing which.*

She hated him with the clean, efficient hatred of daughters who love their fathers. It was a well-maintained hatred. She oiled it daily. It had gotten her over the mountains, through the pass where the ward-keepers' outriders patrolled with their gray lanterns, down into this green drowned valley that did not appear on any map she'd ever been made to memorize—and she had been made to memorize all of them.

The trees broke open without warning, the way secrets do.

Below her, cradled in fog like something set down carefully by enormous hands, lay the Drowned Hollow: a scatter of slate roofs, a ribbon of lane, lamplight already kindling gold in windows, and beyond it all the lake, flat and black and patient as a closed eye. Smoke rose from a chimney near the water's edge—a low building with a long porch, and moored beside it, a ferry barge with a lantern swinging at its prow.

Isolde stood at the tree line and felt the valley look back at her.

That was the only way to describe it. Wards had a feel; she'd grown up inside them, inside her father's workrooms where the air tasted faintly of ozone and old decisions. Dead wards felt like ash. Living wards felt like skin. But this—the whole valley hummed against her senses like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing, and underneath the hum, so deep she felt it in her teeth rather than heard it, was a second note. Older. Vaster. Patient in the way the lake was patient.

*Something is down there,* she thought. *And something is holding it down. And both of them know I've arrived.*

Her side throbbed. She looked down at herself with the detached appraisal of someone checking another person's injuries: torn riding coat, dark with dried blood; beneath it, the gash across her ribs where the ward-lattice had come apart under her father's knife and taken a piece of her with it. A wound from living magic, cut in the last hour before the wards died. Which meant—

Which meant the wound should have killed her days ago. Wounds made by active magic didn't clot. They remembered being made, and they kept remembering, faithfully, forever, unless the magic that made them was unmade first. Her father had taught her that when she was nine, using a cut apple and a candle flame, and she had thought it was a lesson about apples.

So either everything he'd taught her was wrong, or—

Or the wards hadn't fallen. They'd been *switched off*, cleanly, deliberately, the way you snuffed a candle rather than letting it burn down. And whatever had done the switching had reached all the way here, into the deepest fold of the world's pocket, and unmade the oldest working she'd ever heard of.

Isolde laughed. It came out wrong, thin and high, and she pressed her hand harder against her ribs and started down the hill toward the light, because the alternative was standing in the dark laughing until the laughing became something else.

* * *

The inn smelled of woodsmoke, tallow, and stewed eels, and it was warm in the aggressive, deliberate way of places that have decided warmth is a policy. A woman built like a doorframe presided behind the bar—gray-streaked hair pinned with what appeared to be a fishhook, forearms bare, a dishtowel over one shoulder like a general's sash. Two children, a boy and a girl perhaps ten years old, identical down to the mud on their knees, sat at the corner table pretending very badly not to stare at her.

"You're bleeding on my floor," the woman said. Not unkindly. As a statement of fact, like reporting weather.

"I'm sorry," Isolde said. "I'll bleed more quietly."

That earned her a short bark of laughter and a jerk of the chin toward a stool. "Sit before you fall down and make it everyone's problem. Fen—basin. Sorrel—don't touch the stranger's knife." The boy went; the girl stayed exactly where she was, staring with frank, unembarrassed curiosity, and Isolde found she didn't mind. Curiosity without fear was a thing she hadn't seen in months.

"You're not from the upper farms," the woman said, setting down a cup of something hot that smelled of pine and honey. "Upper farmers come down the east track. You came out of the north woods, which nobody uses, because the north woods are used by nobody." She let that sit. "Bramble. This is my house."

"Isolde." No surname. Surnames were a debt she couldn't afford just now.

"Just Isolde."

"Just Isolde."

Bramble's eyes were shrewd and brown and missed nothing, and Isolde watched them flick—to the blood, to the fine make of her boots, to the way she held her left arm clamped to her side, to the shape under her coat where the shard rode in its wrapping against her sternum. Cataloguing. Deciding. Isolde had seen that exact sequence of glances before, on the faces of gate-wardens and her father's colleagues, and she braced for the question that always followed: *who sent you, who are you running from, what do you carry.*

What Bramble actually said was: "Can you eat eel?"

Isolde blinked. "I—yes?"

"Good. Because it's eel." She turned and shouted toward the kitchen doorway, "Zeph! Another bowl!"

And that was how Isolde first saw him.

He came through the kitchen door carrying a bowl and a loaf, and the first thing she noticed was his hands. Ferryman's hands, rope-callused, scarred across the knuckles—but they moved wrong for a ferryman, too precisely, too *carefully*, the way a musician's hands move even when they're only carrying bread. The second thing she noticed was that the hum of the valley changed pitch when he entered the room. Subtly. A half-tone lower, like a string detuned by a passing body.

He looked up at her and stopped walking.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. Anyone else would have missed it entirely—the small arrest of him, the way his eyes went to her side, to the blood, and then to her face, and something moved behind them like a fish turning under ice. Then he set the bowl in front of her, nodded once, and said, "You should have that seen to. Marta's good with stitches, if you can stand her opinions."

"It's not a stitching wound," Isolde said.

"No," he agreed quietly. "I don't expect it is."

And then he simply went back through the kitchen door, and Isolde sat with her eel and her pine tea and her heart hammering like a fist on a door, because he knew. He had looked at a wound that should not exist inside dead wards and he had *known*, and he had chosen, in front of his family, to say nothing—which meant he was lying to them, or lying to himself, or lying to the world entire, and Isolde recognized the posture the way you recognize your own handwriting.

*Two liars,* she thought, and ate her eel, which was excellent, and hated herself a little for noticing that too.

* * *

They gave her a room above the boathouse, small and clean, smelling of tar and lake-water. She waited until the lamps went out one by one along the lane, until the twins' arguing faded, until the inn itself went dark except for one window where Bramble sat up late with her accounts. Then Isolde took out the shard.

It was the size of a man's thumb, a splinter of worked stone shot through with veins of silver that had gone dark as lead. Her father had carried it forty years, she now understood—or a piece of it, a piece of a piece, passed down keeper to keeper like a relic, except relics are worshipped and this had been *maintained*. Oiled. Kept sharp. It was a key, or a fuse, or a confession, depending on the day.

She was still turning it in the moonlight when the floorboard outside creaked.

"I brought clean bandages," Zephyrine's voice said through the door. "Marta's asleep. I can leave them."

A lie. Marta's lamp had gone out two hours ago; he'd been waiting. Isolde looked at the shard, at the door, at the window with its easy drop to the shingle beach below. Run, said the part of her trained by gray-robed men with gray lanterns. Always run. Trust is a resource and you are overdrawn.

"Leave them," she called back, "and I'll only wonder about you all night. Come in and I'll only wonder about you while you're here. It's more efficient."

Silence. Then the latch lifted, and he stepped in, and up close she saw what the lamplight downstairs had softened: a man maybe thirty, ordinary face, forgettable on purpose, the kind of face that had been practiced into ordinariness the way hers had been practiced into blankness. He set the bandages on the table. His eyes went to the shard in her hand and this time he didn't pretend they hadn't.

"That's ward-stone," he said.

"It's a piece of the border ward. The outermost ring. There were nine rings once." She turned it so the dead silver caught the moon. "This one was maintained by my family for six generations. We were ward-keepers. The real kind, not the storybook kind." She smiled, and knew it wasn't a kind smile. "Do you know what the storybook kind does, ferryman? They guard things. The real kind *are* guards. We don't watch the walls. We're part of the mortar."

"And now?"

"Now, four nights ago, every ring went dark at once. Not burned out. Not broken. *Switched off.* All nine, in sequence, top-down, in under a minute. Do you understand what that means?" She was leaning forward now, forgetting her ribs, forgetting caution. "Wards fail. Wards erode, they crack, they get attacked—they die messily, ring by ring, screaming, over hours or years. What happened was a hand on a lever. Someone with authority over the whole lattice chose to end it. Chose it *deliberately*. And the wound in my side—" she pulled the bandage loose, showed him the gash, clean-edged, angry-red, and utterly, impossibly closed of any fresh bleeding—"was made by living magic the same hour. It should be killing me. It isn't. Because the magic that made it is dead everywhere in the world, including here. Including wherever *you* keep yours."

The last words dropped between them like a stone into a well.

Zephyrine didn't move. But the room moved around him—she felt it, felt the valley's deep hum bend toward him like grass toward wind, and understood suddenly why the hollow had felt like a bell. It wasn't the wards humming. It was him. The wards had been built to muffle a sound, and tonight, with the muffling gone, she was hearing the sound itself.

"How long," she whispered, "have you known what you are?"

His jaw tightened. When he spoke, his voice was level, and she recognized the labor of that levelness, having performed it herself her whole life. "I know I'm a ferryman. I know Bramble took me in when I was a child and has never asked me a single question in fifteen years, which either makes her a saint or makes her afraid of the answers. I know Aldous taught me to hold my gift down the way you teach a dog to heel, and I never asked why a ferryman needs a sword-oath beaten into him before he can write his own name." He paused. "I know that tonight, for the first time, the holding-down got easier. And easier is worse. Easier means something outside me changed."

"The wards," Isolde said.

"The wards." He looked at the shard again. "My order—I was taught we *made* those wards. That they protected the valley."

"They weren't protection runes." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, six generations of keeper-knowledge spilling like blood. "I've read the founding inscriptions. My father made me copy them out as penance when I was twelve. Protection script runs outward—*keep harm from within.* What's carved on the original ward-stones of this valley runs inward. *Keep what is within from without.*" She met his eyes. "It's not a shield, Zephyrine. It's a box. And I think you've been the thing inside it."

For a long moment neither of them breathed. Outside, the lake lay black and motionless, and somewhere beneath it, Isolde felt—or imagined she felt, but did not think she imagined—the deep note of the valley shift, the way a sleeper shifts when voices in the next room speak his name.

"My mentor," Zephyrine said at last, very quietly, "told me the stars going dark was weather."

"Nine stars?" Isolde said. "In a line, then a curve, like a hook?"

He went white. "You saw it too."

"Everyone outside saw it. The ward-keepers call it the Unlatching. It's in our oldest records. It happens when the ninth ring falls." She wrapped the shard back in its cloth with hands that had finally begun to shake, now that the shaking was safe. "Your Aldous didn't mistake it for weather. Nobody mistakes that for weather. He lied to you."

"Yes," Zephyrine said. Simply. Without anger, which frightened her more than rage would have—rage would have meant the lie was new to him. This was a man confirming a suspicion he'd been carrying like a stone in his boot for years. "He lies beautifully. He's been doing it my whole life." He rose, moved to the window, stood looking out at the water. "Why did you come here? Really. Nobody crosses the north woods by accident."

Isolde weighed the truth against the trained lie—*you will tell him nothing*—and found, somewhat to her surprise, that the lie wouldn't come. Perhaps it was the eel. Perhaps it was the twins' unafraid staring. Perhaps it was simply that she was tired of being mortar.

"My father ordered me to find you and tell you nothing," she said. "Then he cut the wards himself, gave me this shard, and told me to tell you everything. In that order. Within the space of an hour." She laughed, and it came out wrong again. "I don't know which instruction was the real one. I don't think he knew either. I think—" and here her voice failed her, briefly, and she hated it, and pushed through anyway— "I think he's spent his whole life keeping a box locked, and tonight he opened it, and he couldn't live with either choice, so he made his daughter carry both."

Zephyrine turned from the window. Moonlight lay across half his face, and in the other half she saw something she recognized from her own mirror: the specific exhaustion of a person who has just learned that the prison was load-bearing.

"The lake," he said. "When the wards fell—did your father say what the wards were keeping in?"

"He said," Isolde answered, "that they were keeping it *asleep*. And that it had been asleep a very long time. And that when it woke, it would not be angry." She swallowed. "It would be hungry."

Below them, out on the black water, something moved. Not a fish-rise, not wind—a long, slow displacement, far out, as though something vast had turned over in its sleep and settled again. The ferry lantern swung on its mooring post, though there was no wind.

Neither of them mentioned it. Both of them saw it.

"We leave at first light," Zephyrine said. "There's a place under the lake I need to show you. I've dreamed about it my whole life, and until tonight I thought the dreams were mine."
