## 6. Swordspeech

Isolde did not sleep. Zephyrine knew this because he didn't either, and the inn was small enough that insomnia was a shared country with borders you could hear: the creak of her floorboards above his, the soft scrape of her chair, once—just once—the sound of something metal being drawn across a whetstone and then hastily stilled, as if she'd remembered where she was.

He found her at dawn in the boathouse, sitting on the upturned hull of the *Maren*, turning the shard of ward-stone over in her fingers like a woman counting beads on a rosary she no longer believed in. The morning fog had come all the way inside, filling the boathouse to the rafters with pale wool, and her face floated in it, bruise-shadowed under the eyes, the impossible wound on her arm bound in Bramble's good linen.

"You should be resting," he said.

"So should you." She didn't look up. "We're both very bad at being safe. I've noticed that about us."

He leaned against the doorframe. Outside, the lake lay flat as poured pewter, and somewhere on its far shore the drowned village waited under thirty feet of cold water with his name carved into the heart of it. He had not told anyone what they'd found down there. He hadn't needed to; Isolde had been there, her hand pressed to the ward-stone beside his, reading the old script aloud in a voice that shook only on the last word.

*Zephyrine.*

Not a protection. A binding. The valley built around him like a scabbard around a blade.

"Ask me," Isolde said now.

"Ask you what?"

"What you're circling. You've been circling it since we came up out of the water. You keep looking at your hands like they belong to a stranger." She finally raised her eyes to him. They were gray-green, the color of the lake before a storm, and they did not flinch. "So ask."

The fog pressed close. Somewhere out on the water, a loon called and then thought better of it.

"My order," Zephyrine said slowly. "Aldous always said they were wardens. Protectors. That they died holding the line so the Hollow could live in peace." His hands had gone cold in his pockets, curled around nothing. "You read that stone like you'd read it before. Like you were taught the letters at someone's knee. So tell me what my order really was."

Isolde set the shard down on the hull between them, carefully, the way you'd put down a knife you weren't sure wasn't poisoned.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said, "and then you're going to hate me for a while, and that's fair. But you need to hear it from someone who isn't lying to you, and I think we've established that list is short."

She drew a breath.

"The Order of the Sundered Oath—your order—was founded four hundred years ago by nine swordsmiths who killed a god."

The words dropped into the boathouse like stones into still water. Zephyrine found he couldn't move.

"Not banished. Not sealed. *Killed,*" Isolde went on, quiet and relentless. "There was a war, back then, between the god-touched kingdoms of the coast and the hill clans who refused them worship. The coast's gods walked. They took bodies. They took tribute. One of them—the one your people called the Tidefather, though that was never its name, names are leashes and it wore none—took the whole of a city as its temple and drowned the parts that displeased it. Nine smiths forged nine blades and learned to speak to them, and the speaking was the weapon. Not the steel. The *word*. A swordspeaker can command matter the way a king commands men. And matter, unlike men, does not disobey."

"And they killed it," Zephyrine said. His voice sounded far away to himself.

"They killed it. And they discovered two things in the doing. First: gods do not die cleanly. What's left of one sinks, and starves, and dreams, and dreams are dangerous. Second—" she met his eyes, and here her voice did shake, just barely— "second, the word is seductive. Every swordspeaker who ever lived ended the same way. They started as protectors. They ended as tyrants, because when the world obeys your voice, you stop asking whether it should. The order's own founders wrote that down. It's the first law of the oath: *the blade exists to kill the thing it becomes.*"

The fog breathed against the walls. Zephyrine became aware, distantly, that his hands had come out of his pockets without his deciding it, and that they were trembling—not with fear. With something worse. Something eager.

"The wards," he managed.

"The wards came later. Your order spent three generations hunting the drowned remnants of the old gods, putting them down one by one, and every generation lost more speakers to corruption than to battle. The last of them—the last nine, your nine, the ones whose names are on the stone beneath the lake—they made a final decision. There was one remnant left. The largest. Sleeping under a valley they could flood." She paused. "And there was one child born with the gift at full strength, the strongest speaker in four hundred years. So they flooded the valley. They drowned their own village to bury the god deep, and they built the border wards not to keep magic *out*—"

"—but to keep me *in*," Zephyrine finished.

"To keep the weapon leashed," Isolde said. "You're not the valley's protector, Zephyrine. You're its arsenal. The wards don't hide you from the world. They muffle you. They've been sitting on your gift since before you could walk, and everyone who raised you knew it."

He thought of Aldous. Forty years of gentle lessons, of *careful, boy, careful*, of hands over his own small hands teaching him to hold a ferry pole instead of a sword. Forty years of kindness shaped exactly like a cage bar.

He sat down on the hull beside her because his legs had stopped being reliable.

* * *

They talked until the fog burned off. Isolde told him about the ward-keepers' archives, the records her father had smuggled home in violation of his own oaths, the diagrams of the binding with a child's silhouette inked at its center. She told him how the descendants of the founders had maintained the arrangement for four centuries, breeding themselves into a priesthood of jailers, and how the wards had not fallen but been *switched off*—and that whoever had switched them off had done it from inside.

"It was Aldous," Zephyrine said. Not a question anymore. It had stopped being a question somewhere in the night.

"Aldous, or someone using Aldous's keys." Isolde's mouth went thin. "Which raises the question nobody wants to ask: why would your keeper open the cage?"

Zephyrine looked out through the boathouse doors at the lake. Flat. Patient. Thirty feet of water over a sleeping thing, and beneath the water a village, and beneath the village a stone with his name on it, and beneath the stone—

His hands were warm. That was the strange part. All this dread, and his hands were warm as banked coals, and something under his breastbone had begun, very softly, to hum.

"Show me," he said.

Isolde went still. "Show you what?"

"You know what the gift is. You've read the archives. You know how it wakes." He turned to look at her, and saw her weigh it—saw her actually stand there and measure the danger of him against the danger of leaving him ignorant, and hate both numbers. "I felt it last night. When I touched the stone. Something answered. If it's going to answer again—and Isolde, the stars went out, the wards are down, something is coming—I would rather meet it standing than asleep."

"That's exactly what a tyrant says," she said quietly.

"I know."

She held his gaze for a long moment. Then she stood, rolled up her sleeves, and said, "Not here. The inn has people in it. And not on land—land remembers commands too long. We go out on the water, where things can be taken back."

* * *

The lake received them without comment. Zephyrine poled them out past the buoy line, past the drowned village's invisible rooftops, to the deep place where the water went black-green and the echoes came back wrong. The morning was bright and cold and utterly ordinary. Gulls worked the shallows behind them. On the shore, tiny with distance, Fen and Sorrel were arguing about crab traps, their voices carrying flat across the water like everything else in the Hollow carried: domestic, safe, small.

"Feet apart," Isolde said. "Weight centered. The old texts say the word comes up through the body—if you brace wrong, it takes the body's shape instead of yours." She stood at the prow, facing him, and the wind moved her hair and she ignored it. "Now. The first thing you have to understand is that you don't *make* the word happen. You stop preventing it. The gift isn't a tool you pick up. It's a door you've been holding shut for twenty-five years, and the door is tired, and so are you."

"And if I open it and can't shut it again?"

"Then we'll know that too." Her jaw tightened. "I won't lie to you, Zephyrine. This is the moment the archive warns about. Every corrupted speaker describes it the same way. They say it feels like coming home."

"Charming."

"Do it anyway."

He closed his eyes.

At first there was nothing—just the rock of the boat, the lap of water, the gull-cries, the ordinary machinery of an ordinary morning. He almost laughed. Twenty-five years of careful nothing, of Aldous's lessons and his own discipline, of being a ferryman, only a ferryman, always a ferryman, and the great forbidden gift was a door painted on a wall—

Then he stopped holding.

It was not sound. That was the first thing he understood, and the understanding arrived whole, pre-verbal, the way you understand falling. The swordspeech was older than sound. Sound was what the world used when it had no better option. What lived in him was the language underneath—the grammar of weight and grain and consent, the tongue that stone spoke to itself in the dark of mountains, that water murmured in its sleep. It had been there all along. It had been there when he was a child crying over a broken toy and wishing it whole. It had been there every day of his life, patient as sediment, and he had been taught to call its silence peace.

*Oh,* he thought. *Oh, I have been so lonely, and I thought the loneliness was mine.*

"Zephyrine." Isolde's voice, sharp with alarm. "Your eyes—"

He opened them. The world had changed texture. He could see the lake the way he'd once seen a page of script: not water but a sentence, millions of tons of it, coherent and waiting, and beneath it the deep place where something vast turned over in its sleep and muttered in a dialect his bones recognized. He could see the boat's timbers and know each board's history, every storm it had shrugged off. He could see Isolde—her wound knitting slow and grudging, her heartbeat quick as a bird's, the iron in her blood singing its small stubborn song—and the seeing was not love or even curiosity. It was ownership. It was the way a king looks at a map.

That was the moment he should have shut the door.

Instead he heard himself speak, and the word was not in any language he knew and yet he had always known it, and it meant, as nearly as anything mortal could carry its meaning: *back.*

The lake obeyed.

There was no crash, no wave. That was the terrible elegance of it. The water simply withdrew—a perfect circle of recession spreading from the boat, smooth as a held breath, the shallows baring their pebbled teeth, the buoy line swinging drunkenly as its anchors rose into air beaded and gleaming. Three feet, maybe more. The drowned village's rooftops broke the surface all around them, black and dripping, a whole neighborhood of the dead rising into sunlight at his casual syllable, and the exposed mud breathed out a smell of centuries, and everywhere, everywhere, the water *wanted* to please him. He could feel its eagerness. He could feel how easy it would be to say *away* and watch the basin empty to the bone, to say *kneel* to the hills, to say words he didn't yet know and have the world invent meanings for them.

Three feet of receded lake. That was all. And it felt like being handed a kingdom and asked, politely, what he'd like next.

"*Stop,*" Isolde said—no, she said his name, his actual name, twice, and gripped his face in both cold hands, and her eyes were inches from his and full of a fear she wasn't bothering to hide. "Look at me. Look at *me*, not the water. Whatever it's showing you, it's showing you what you want to see. That's how it starts. That's the first lesson in every archive I ever read and the last one the corrupted speakers believed."

The circle of bared lakebed held for one long breath. Two.

Then Zephyrine unspoke the word—there was no other way to describe it, a reversal, a letting-down—and the lake sighed back into its bed with a sound like a thousand small approvals, and the rooftops sank, and the buoy line settled, and the world resumed being merely the world.

He sat down hard in the bottom of the boat. His whole body was shaking. He looked at his hands as if they belonged to a stranger, which was, he understood now, precisely backward: the stranger was what they had always been, and the ferryman was the costume.

"I enjoyed it," he said. His voice came out sanded flat. "Isolde. I enjoyed it. For about four seconds I was the best thing I've ever been, and I *enjoyed* it, and I wanted more, and the wanting didn't feel like greed. It felt like thirst. It felt reasonable."

"I know." She crouched in the rocking boat beside him, and to his surprise she didn't reach for the oars or the shard or any weapon. She reached for his wrist and pressed two fingers to his pulse, counting it, grounding him in the dumb animal rhythm of himself. "That's the seduction. It doesn't tempt you with evil. It tempts you with competence. With finally being adequate to the world." Her thumb pressed harder. "But listen to me. The lake just moved for you, and something under it noticed. Did you feel it? At the end. The deep place."

He had. In the last instant before he let the water return, something far below had gone very still—the way a household goes still when the master of the house wakes—and he had felt, distinct as a fingertip drawn along his spine, an attention older than the valley turn toward the surface and consider him.

"It knows my name," Zephyrine said.

"It's always known your name. It's on the stone." Isolde released his wrist and sat back, and her face, when he made himself read it as a face and not a map of blood and bone, was grim as winter. "And now it knows you can hear it knocking. Which means we have less time than I thought. Because a starving thing that just watched the key walk by on the surface—"

She stopped.

Out past the buoy line, in the exact center of the deep place, the flat pewter skin of the lake had begun, very gently, to swirl.
