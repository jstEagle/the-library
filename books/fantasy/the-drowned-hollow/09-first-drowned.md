## 9. First Drowned

The lake gave back its first dead on the third morning after the stars went out.

Zephyrine saw it from the ferry dock, where he had taken to standing at dawn the way other men took to prayer—hands wrapped around a mug of Bramble's bitter tea, watching the water as if vigilance were a thing that could be performed. The fog had thinned overnight. That was wrong in itself. The Hollow's fog was older than the inn, older than the ferry, older than any story Bramble told by firelight, and it did not thin. It sat on the valley like a lid on a pot.

But this morning the mist hung in rags along the far shore, and through the gaps Zephyrine watched a woman walk out of the lake.

Not swim. Not wade. *Walk*—up the shallow grade of the drowned village's sunken lane, through water that should have dragged her down and swallowed her whole, her skirts streaming, her hair heavy as kelp, until she stepped onto the shingle at the north beach and stood there, dripping, in the gray light, looking at nothing at all.

She was not breathing. He knew it the way he knew the weight of his own sword-hand: some part of him that had been trained before he could remember read the stillness of her chest and named it. Dead. Walking anyway.

"Isolde." His voice came out low, careful, the voice he used for spooked horses. She was already beside him—she slept badly now, in the loft above the ferry shed, and woke at every sound the valley made. She followed his gaze and went very still.

"That's Marren's wife," she said.

"Who?"

"The woman. From the drowned village." Isolde's knuckles whitened on the dock rail. "I've seen her face in the ward-keeper's records. The flooding of the Hollow wasn't an accident, Zeph. They flooded it on purpose—to make the lake deep enough to hold what they put under it. Whole village. Everyone who wouldn't leave." She swallowed. "That's Tamsin Hale. She's been dead two hundred years."

The woman on the shore turned her head. Slowly, like something remembering how necks worked. Her eyes found them across the water—not with hunger, not with malice, but with the mild, patient attention of someone waiting for a friend to finish a conversation with someone else.

Then she smiled, and began to walk along the shoreline toward the ferry crossing, unhurried, her bare feet leaving prints in the wet sand that filled with clear water and did not wash away.

* * *

By midmorning there were four of them.

They came up out of the lake at intervals, as if summoned by no particular bell: Tamsin Hale; a broad-shouldered man whose fishing smock had rotted to lace; a child of perhaps eight, small and solemn, wringing lake water from the hem of her dress with the endless patience of the very young; and an old man who sat down on a flat stone near the waterline and simply watched the valley, his hands folded in his lap, content as a cat in a window.

They did not speak. They did not approach the inn. They arranged themselves along the north beach like figures in a tableau and dripped, gently, onto stones that had been dry for two centuries, and the valley's animals—the ones that hadn't already gone silent—now fled the shoreline entirely. Even the crows would not cross the water.

"They're not ghosts," Isolde said. She had not slept. She stood at the inn's front window with her arms crossed tight, ward-shard hanging at her throat, and her voice had the flatness she got when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "Ghosts are echoes. Echoes repeat. These aren't repeating anything—they're *waiting*. There's a difference."

"Waiting for what?"

"I don't know. But I know what they are." She turned to look at him, and he saw she'd been building to this all morning. "Heralds. In the old records, when a god means to rise, the dead it has touched walk first. Not to harm. To *announce*. They're the knock at the door, Zeph. Something beneath your lake is about to ask to be let in, and it's sending the politest possible messengers because—" she hesitated, choosing the words with visible care, "—because it knows. It knows what you are. It knows what your order did. And it wants you to come to it *willingly*."

Zephyrine looked out at the old man on his stone. The dead man caught him looking and inclined his head—a courteous, unhurried nod, one neighbor greeting another across a fence—and Zephyrine's skin crawled from nape to heel.

"Aldous needs to see this," he said.

"Aldous," Isolde said, "already knows."

* * *

He found the mentor at the boathouse, which was itself the first wrong thing. Aldous did not go to the water. In forty years of living at the Hollow's edge, Aldous had never once been seen closer to the lake than the well path, and when Zephyrine asked why, once, years ago, the old man had said only, *I don't care for boats*, in the tone that closed doors.

Now he stood at the boathouse's open doors with his back to the room, staff in hand, watching the four drowned dead on the far shore with an expression Zephyrine had never seen on him before. It took a long moment to name it, because it fit his face so poorly.

It was guilt.

"You knew they'd come," Zephyrine said.

Aldous didn't turn. "I knew the lake might give them back. I hoped it wouldn't come to that."

"Hoped." Zephyrine came into the boathouse, boots loud on the boards. "You've been hoping a great deal lately, master. You hoped I wouldn't notice nine stars going dark. You hoped Isolde was lying about the wards. Now you're hoping the dead stay politely drowned. When does the hoping stop being a lie?"

That landed. He saw it land—in the slight drop of Aldous's shoulders, the way the staff shifted in his grip. For a moment the old man looked every year of his age, and then some, and Zephyrine felt the familiar sick twist of loving someone and being furious at them in the same breath.

"When the truth would hurt more than the lie," Aldous said quietly. "Which is most mornings, boy. Most mornings of most lives." He turned at last, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "Those people on the shore will not harm anyone. I need you to believe that, because everything else is about to get much harder, and fear makes swordspeakers do rash things."

"You said that word like it doesn't burn coming out of you."

"It always has." A ghost of the old dry humor, there and gone. "Come. If we're going to meet the dead, we'll do it properly. Bring bread."

"Bread?"

"They were people," Aldous said, moving toward the door. "Whatever they are now, they were people first. We'll greet them as such or we've no business calling ourselves the valley's own."

* * *

They went down to the north beach in the late afternoon, when the light went long and gold and made the drowned dead look almost alive—almost, if you didn't watch the water beading on their skin without ever drying, if you didn't notice that their shadows fell the wrong way, toward the lake, as if the light itself couldn't quite decide about them.

Bramble had insisted on coming, over every objection, with a basket of the dark rye she baked for funerals, because, she said, *if they've come back from drowning the least we can do is feed them*, and behind her came Fen and Sorrel, whom nobody had been able to keep home and nobody had truly tried. The twins walked on either side of Zephyrine now without discussing it, the way they'd done since the night the stars went out—an honor guard of two gangly teenagers who thought they were being subtle.

The dead waited for them at the waterline.

Up close they were worse and better than from afar. Worse, because the details accumulated: the fisherman's eyes gone pale as riverstone, the child's lips faintly blue, the fine silt caught in the creases of the old man's knuckles. Better, because there was nothing in their faces but gentleness. They looked at the living procession with the mild interest of people watching a festival arrive, and when Bramble stopped ten paces off and held up the basket, it was Tamsin Hale who stepped forward.

She moved like water finding its level. She stopped before Bramble, and bowed her head, and took a loaf from the basket with both hands, carefully, the way you take something precious from a stranger, and she held it against her chest and looked up at Bramble with an expression of such plain, human gratitude that Sorrel made a small broken sound and grabbed her brother's sleeve.

"She can't eat it," Fen whispered. "Can she?"

"No," Isolde said softly. "But she can keep it."

And that seemed to be true. Tamsin Hale pressed the loaf to her breastbone and simply held it, and something in her stillness eased, as though the gift itself were the nourishment—as though what the dead wanted was not bread but the fact of being offered it.

The fisherman came next, and took a loaf. Then the child, solemn as a little priestess, curtseying to Bramble before she took hers, and Bramble laughed—a wet, startled laugh, grief and delight tangled—and said, "Well. Manners. Your mother raised you right, whoever she was," and the child smiled with all the simple joy of a living girl, and Zephyrine had to look away.

The old man rose last from his stone. He came slowly, joints creaking with two hundred years of lake-bottom cold, and he did not take bread. He stopped in front of Zephyrine instead, and studied him for a long moment with those pale stone eyes, and tilted his head as if listening to something very far away.

Then he opened his mouth, and spoke in Zephyrine's own childhood voice.

*"I am the blade at the threshold. I am the word unspoken. What sleeps below, I hold below; what rises against the innocent, I answer. This I swear on my breath, my blood, and my name—"*

Zephyrine's knees nearly went.

It was *him*. Not an imitation—the thing itself, pulled up out of memory like a bucket from a well: seven years old, standing in the boathouse with his hand on a wooden practice sword, reciting the oath to Aldous for the first time, stumbling on *threshold*, getting it wrong twice, laughing. The cadence was exact. The slight lisp he'd carried until he was nine was exact. The god beneath the lake had reached into whatever it kept of him and drawn out the sound of a child swearing an oath he didn't understand, and it played that sound back to him now through the mouth of a drowned man on a quiet beach while everyone he loved stood watching.

"*—my breath, my blood, and my name,*" the old man finished, gently, and closed his mouth, and smiled at Zephyrine with terrible kindness.

No one else had heard it. He understood that instantly, the way you understand you're bleeding—privately, completely. To the others the dead man had only stood silent, studying the ferryman. But Zephyrine had heard his own small voice recite the oath of an order of god-slayers, and the message needed no translation:

*I know what you are. I have known since before you did. I remember your childhood better than you do.*

"Zeph?" Isolde's hand was on his arm. "What is it? What did it—"

"Nothing." His voice came out steady, which cost him more than screaming would have. "It's nothing. Let them be."

* * *

At dusk the dead walked back into the lake.

There was no ceremony to it. One moment they stood along the waterline holding their loaves; the next they simply turned, all together, and waded out, the water rising up their bodies without resistance, taking them inch by inch—the child last, pausing at the shallows to look back at Bramble and wave, a small bright gesture that Bramble would carry like a coal in her chest for days afterward—until the lake closed over them and the surface lay smooth as hammered tin.

The valley exhaled. The fog began, cautiously, to regather.

Zephyrine stayed on the beach long after the others went up to the inn. Isolde stayed with him; she had stopped pretending she wouldn't. They sat on the cold shingle with the water lapping a hand's breadth from their boots, and the first stars came out overhead—all of them burning again, all except nine, and the nine absences drew his eye the way a missing tooth draws the tongue.

"It spoke to me," he said finally. "The old one. Words only I could hear."

Isolde went still beside him. "What did it say?"

"It said my oath." He watched the black water. "In my own voice. From when I was a child learning it. Word for word." He laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow, a sound with nothing under it. "Do you understand? It didn't threaten me. It didn't have to. It just—showed me that it keeps what I was. Somewhere down there is a version of me at seven years old, saying his oath wrong and laughing about it, and that thing owns it, and it wanted me to know."

Isolde was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was very soft.

"That's not a herald's message, Zeph. Heralds announce. That was—" she searched, and he watched her arrive somewhere she didn't want to be, "—that was courtship. Or recruitment. It's telling you it remembers you kindly. It's telling you that when you come down—and it believes you'll come down—you'll be coming *home*."

"And if it's right?" He heard the crack in his own voice and hated it. "Isolde, I stood on this beach ready to draw on the dead. On a *child*. If it had moved wrong—if any of them had—I'd have spoken. I felt the word sitting in my mouth like a hot coal. And some part of me *wanted* to say it. Wanted to see what the lake does when I command it." He put his head in his hands. "What kind of man is flattered that a god remembers his voice?"

"The kind it's been waiting for." Isolde pulled her knees up, chin resting on them, staring out at the nine starless places. "My mother kept records, in the ward-house. Sealed files I was never supposed to read. There was a phrase that showed up in the oldest of them, about the thing under the Drowned Hollow. Not *prisoner*. Not *monster*." She turned to look at him, and in the starlight her face was pale and grave. "*Patient suitor.*"

Far out on the lake, where the water lay darkest, something vast turned over in its sleep—or in its waking; Zephyrine could no longer tell the difference—and sent a single slow swell rolling shoreward, gentle as a hand laid on a shoulder.

It broke at their feet without a sound.

And up the slope behind them, at the inn, a lamp was lit, and another, and another—Bramble lighting the windows against the dark, the way she did every night, ordinary and stubborn and alive—and Zephyrine watched those small lights tremble on the black water and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what the god had shown him on the beach.

It hadn't been threatening his past.

It had been showing him the price of his future.
