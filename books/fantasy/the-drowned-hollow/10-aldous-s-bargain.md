## 10. Aldous's Bargain

The heralds came at moonrise, walking up out of the shallows with the water sheeting off them like silk.

Aldous Vane had waited for them on the shingle below the drowned chapel, alone, because this was a thing that could not be witnessed. He had brought a lantern out of habit and then set it down behind him on a stone, because light felt like an argument he did not want to start. The lake was low—ten feet low since the boy had spoken—and it lay against the shore in long slow breaths, as if something vast beneath it were turning over in its sleep and dreaming of waking.

Three of them stood at the waterline. They had been villagers once; Aldous knew their faces from the old photographs in the chapel vestry, from the parish rolls he kept locked in his desk drawer like sins. A woman with riverweed braided through her hair. A man whose chest rose and fell without breath. And between them, smallest, a girl of perhaps nine who had been dead for one hundred and sixty years and who now looked at Aldous with eyes the color of lake-bottom silt.

They did not speak first. They never spoke first. That was courtesy, or hunger wearing courtesy, or both.

"You know why we've come," Aldous said.

The woman tilted her head. Water ran from her jaw and did not drip so much as return, sliding back toward the lake in thin silver threads, drawn home. "We come," she said, and her voice was the sound of a bucket hauled up a deep well, rope creaking, darkness rising, "because you called."

"I didn't call."

"You knelt at the shore every night for forty years." The man's mouth moved around the words slowly, as though speech were a language he was relearning, or unlearning. "You gave. We remember giving. We remember *you*."

Aldous's hands were steady. He had trained them to be steady across four decades of small betrayals—the lies to Zephyrine about omens, the letters burned unread, the ward-keepers' reports signed in a dead woman's name. But steadiness was not the same as calm, and inside the cage of his ribs something paced and threw itself against the bars.

"I've come to make a new arrangement," he said.

* * *

He had been twenty-three years old when he first fed the lake.

That was not how the ward-keepers had described it, of course. They had called it *tithe*. They had called it *maintenance*, *oblation*, *the quiet work*. There were forty-one words in the keepers' liturgy for what they did at the water's edge after dark, and not one of them was *feeding*, because feeding implied appetite, and appetite implied a thing with a mouth, and the whole architecture of the Hollow's founding rested on the fiction that the thing beneath the lake was not a thing at all but merely a pressure, a weight, a hazard to be managed like weather.

Aldous had learned the truth on his third night of service, kneeling where he knelt now, when the tithe he carried—a stoppered jar of memory-silt, harvested from the dreams of sleeping villagers, ground fine as flour—had slipped from his numb fingers and shattered on the stones.

The lake had *moved*.

Not waved. Not stirred. It had leaned, all of it, every acre of black water tilting toward him by a single degree, the way a starving dog leans toward a hand. And in that degree of leaning Aldous had understood three things in the space of one heartbeat: that the god was awake; that the god was hungry; and that everything the ward-keepers told themselves about mercy and stewardship was a story they told so they could sleep.

He had picked up the shards. He had scraped the silt from the stones with his bare hands. He had given it freely, whispering the words of offering, and the lake had settled, and he had gone back to the keeper's house and been sick into the drain until dawn.

Forty years. He had done the arithmetic more times than he could count. Forty years of tithes, each one a little larger than the last, because the god's hunger grew as its long imprisonment wore its patience thin. In the early years a jar a month had sufficed. Then a jar a week. Then, in the last decade, Aldous had stopped measuring in jars at all and begun measuring in what the silt cost the valley: old Marta Hale forgetting her wedding day and blaming her husband for the loss; the twins' grandmother dying with her mind already half-drained, her last words a confusion of names; whole seasons going soft at the edges, remembered wrong by everyone who'd lived them, because memory, once taken, did not leave a clean hole—it left a scar shaped like itself.

And none of them had known. None of them had consented. He had stolen their pasts one spoonful at a time to keep a god asleep, and he had told himself, every night for forty years, that this was love.

It was, he thought now, watching the heralds wait with their terrible patience, the most refined cruelty he had ever practiced. He had made himself the sole author of the valley's forgetting. He had decided, alone, unasked, which memories the Hollow could spare and which it could not, and he had never once put the question to the people whose lives he spent. Guardianship, he had learned, was just tyranny that had learned to kneel.

"Speak your arrangement," the drowned woman said.

* * *

"The god is waking," Aldous said. "You know this better than I. You are its waking."

The girl-herald smiled. It was the worst thing Aldous had ever seen—not malice in it, but recognition, the way a child smiles at a parent coming home. "We are its hands," she said. "Its reaching. It has been folded up so long, keeper. Folded in the dark under the weight of your wards, in the cold, in the *quiet*. Do you know what it is like to be quiet for six hundred years?"

"No," Aldous said honestly. "But I know what it is like to be alone for forty."

Something passed across the heralds' faces then—he would spend the rest of his life failing to name it. Pity, perhaps. Or the memory of pity, worn smooth by drowning.

"It is rising," the man said. "Slowly. It does not wish to break the valley. It wishes to *drink* it. There is a difference, but I do not think you will find the difference comforting."

"I know." Aldous drew a breath. Here it was, then. The bargain he had walked down to the shore carrying like a stone in his chest. "I offer a trade. Memories—freely given, all of them, the whole living store of this valley. Every recollection held by every soul between the ridgelines. Given willingly, with open hands, no theft, no tithe, no scraping of jars in the dark. A feast instead of forty years of crumbs. In exchange, the god stays down. Not chained. Not warded. *Fed*. Full enough to sleep the sleep of the satisfied rather than the imprisoned."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the lantern flame behind him to gutter and steady twice.

"You would empty them," the woman said at last. "Every one. The innkeeper, the children, the ferryman. They would wake tomorrow as strangers to themselves."

"They would wake tomorrow *alive*," Aldous said. "Memoryless, but alive, in a valley that still stands, under stars that still burn. I have watched the alternative. I have spent my whole life standing between this valley and the alternative. When the god rises unfed, it takes everything anyway—memories, minds, the valley itself, and then the world beyond the ridge, and then the war begins again, and the war is why there is a hollow here at all and nothing where the cities used to be." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. Let them hear the forty years in it. "I am offering the gentler ending. It is the only kind I have ever had to give."

"And the boy?" the girl asked.

Aldous went very still.

"We see him," she said softly. "Through the water. Through the cracks where your wards have failed. The swordspeaker. The oath-child. The little blade they built a valley to sheathe." She stepped forward, and the lake stepped with her, a tongue of water sliding up the shingle to lap at her heels like a devoted animal. "The god dreams of him, keeper. It dreams of him the way it once dreamed of the others, the ones with the singing swords. It remembers being fought. It remembers being *hated*. Would you spend the valley's memories and keep his? Would you feed it everyone's yesterdays and hold back the one life it wants most?"

"He is not part of the trade."

"He is the *center* of the trade. Everything turns around him. Your wards turned around him—you must know that by now, you must have read the stone at the bottom of the drowned village, you must have seen his name cut into the heart of it. This valley is a scabbard, Aldous Vane. And a scabbard exists for the sword." Her head tilted, silt-colored eyes catching the starless dark. "Give us the valley's remembering. Keep the boy's. But understand—" and here her voice dropped, became almost tender, became almost human, became the voice of a nine-year-old girl who had drowned before her mother could teach her to swim, "—understand that the god will take him anyway. Hunger does not honor bargains forever. It only honors them until it is hungry again."

"Then I will buy the valley time," Aldous said. "Time is the only currency I have ever had. I have spent forty years paying it out in installments. I am asking to make one final payment—one great payment—and buy them a generation. A lifetime. Long enough for the boy to choose, if choosing is possible. Long enough for someone to find a better answer than mine." He laughed, and it came out broken. "There must be a better answer than mine. I refuse to believe I was the best the world could do."

The heralds regarded him. Behind them the lake breathed. Above them the sky hung starless and enormous, nine holes punched in it where the pattern of the dead order had burned out, and Aldous thought: *even the heavens have been drinking from this valley.* Even the sky owed the god a debt.

"You loved him," the man said. It was not a question. "The ferryman-child. You raised him in the cage you helped build, and you called the raising love."

"Yes."

"And you lied to him."

"Every day."

"And you will stand at his funeral someday, if he has one, and weep genuine tears."

"Yes," Aldous said, and found, to his horror, that they were already standing at his eyes. "All of that is true. All of it at once. That is what I am. I am the man who loves a thing and imprisons it in the same gesture and cannot tell, even now, even here, which of the two acts is the real one." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, roughly, like a man doing chores. "So judge me if you like. But judge me *after* the trade. Mercy first. Judgment can wait—I promise you, it has waited forty years, it can wait a little longer."

* * *

The drowned woman looked at the other two. No words passed between them that Aldous could perceive—but the lake's breathing changed, slowed, deepened, and he understood that some vaster consultation was taking place beneath the shingle, in the flooded streets and the sunken chapel and the deep place where the god's body curled around the roots of the mountains like a hand around a stone.

When the woman spoke again, her voice had changed register. It was not entirely her own. Something older spoke through the drowned throat, and the water of the whole lake trembled with it, and the lantern behind Aldous went out as though bowing.

"*Terms,*" the god said, in a voice like a cathedral filling with water.

Aldous knelt. Not from the liturgy—from something older and more honest. "Speak them."

"*The valley gives. Freely. Every mind opens. Every yesterday pours out. No keeper's hand. No jar. No theft. Given as gift, or not at all—if one soul refuses, the bargain fails and I rise as I please.*"

"One soul," Aldous repeated. His stomach turned over. "One refusal undoes everything."

"*Freely given means freely refused. That is the price of the word freely. You of all people should understand, keeper. You never paid it.*"

The truth of it went through him like a sword. Forty years of tithes, and not one of them a gift. Forty years of taking, dressed in the vocabulary of giving. The god had been robbed for six centuries and it knew robbery the way a scar knows the knife.

"I accept the terms," Aldous said. "For the valley. For the children. For all of them, whether they forgive me or not."

"*And the swordspeaker?*"

"Is not mine to give."

"*No,*" the god agreed, and the tenderness in it was worse than any threat. "*He is yours to lose.*"

The presence withdrew. The heralds' faces emptied back into gentle blankness, three drowned villagers standing in the dark with lake-water running home off their chins. The girl looked at Aldous one last time.

"She liked you," the girl said—her own voice again, small and strange. "The one who founded the keepers. She said you were the only honest thief she ever met." A pause. The water lapped. "She says the bargain will be honored. She says: bring them to the shore at the turning of the tide, three nights hence. And she says—" the girl's brow furrowed, retrieving something vast through a child's small mouth, "—she says *tell the ferryman the lake remembers his mother's lullaby, and sings it to him still.*"

Then they walked backward into the water, unhurried, and the lake closed over them like a book.

* * *

Aldous stayed on the shingle a long time after they were gone.

He relit the lantern with hands that shook now that shaking was permitted, and sat down heavily on the wet stones, an old man alone with the largest crime of his long career—which was saying something, given the inventory.

Three nights. He had three nights to convince an entire valley to open their minds and pour out everything they were: Bramble's recipes and her grief for her husband, Fen and Sorrel's shared childhood, Isolde's hard-won selfhood freshly renounced and therefore all the more precious, and Zephyrine—

Zephyrine, who would have to watch it happen. Who would stand on the shore and see the people he loved become strangers to themselves, bought safe with the coin of their own pasts, and know that the man who raised him had arranged it all without asking anyone anything, exactly as he always had.

*They'll refuse,* Aldous thought. Some of them. Perhaps all of them. One refusal and the god rose, and everything ended in fire and flood and the old war waking with it. He should tell them the terms were impossible. He should tell them the god had left no room. He should—

He should tell them the truth. All of it. Forty years overdue, and due now, in full, before witnesses.

Aldous Vane got up, joints cracking, lantern swinging, and began the long climb from the shore toward the lights of the rebuilt inn—toward Bramble's kitchen, where the survivors of the fire were surely gathered, where the boy would be standing guard over the people he loved, where Isolde would be watching him with those sharp ward-keeper's eyes that missed nothing.

Let them hate me, he thought. Hate is a door too. It opens from the other side.

Halfway up the hill, he stopped. Below him the lake lay flat and patient, holding its new bargain like a coin under its tongue. And drifting up across the black water, faint as breath on a window, came a melody—an old lullaby in a language the valley no longer spoke, sung in a voice no living throat owned.

The god was already practicing its gentleness.

Aldous stood in the dark and listened to it, and wept, and climbed.
