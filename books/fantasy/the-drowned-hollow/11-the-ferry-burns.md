## 11. The Ferry Burns

Fen had always known the inn by its sounds.

Not the creak of the sign or the groan of the old beams—those were noises, and noises were everywhere—but the *sounds*: Bramble's ladle ringing the big pot twice before supper, once for warning and once for grace; Sorrel's boots on the stairs, always two at a time, always too fast; the ferry rope knocking against its cleat in a rhythm like a slow heart when the wind came down off the western ridge. The Drowned Hollow was a quiet valley, but the inn was never silent. It hummed. It breathed. It was the one place in Fen's whole life that had never once felt like it was waiting for something to go wrong.

He was counting the sounds that evening because Sorrel had dared him to. *A hundred sounds before the moon clears the ridge,* she'd said, sprawled on her stomach on the floorboards with her chin in her hands, watching him through the gap under the table where he sat pretending to mend a net. *Loser carries the other's wood for a week.*

*That's not fair,* Fen had said. *You'll just make up sounds.*

*I never make up sounds,* said Sorrel, who made up sounds constantly, who had once claimed to hear the lake singing and been right, which was the worst thing about her.

So Fen counted. Ladle, twice. Boots, two at a time. Rope against cleat. The tick of the stove cooling. Isolde's voice low in the back room with Zephyrine, words too soft to catch but steady as rain—the sound of people planning something they were afraid of. Aldous's absence, which had its own sound too, these last days: a silence shaped exactly like a man, sitting in every room he wasn't in.

Forty-one sounds. Forty-two: Sorrel whispering *you're losing.*

Forty-three—

The dogs started first.

All three of them at once, from the yard, not barking the way dogs bark at foxes or strangers but the flat, continuous, joyless roar that meant *run*. Fen's hand stopped on the net. Sorrel's head came up off the floorboards like a hound's herself. And then, underneath the dogs, a new sound arrived, and Fen would spend the rest of his life hearing it in the dark: a low rushing exhale, like the valley itself drawing breath through its teeth.

He looked up through the window over the sink and saw the boathouse burning.

Not smoking. Not smoldering. *Burning*, the way things burn when they've been helped along—flame standing up out of the roof in orange columns, already taller than the chimney, though there'd been nothing there a moment before, nothing, he'd have heard it, he counted everything, he would have *heard*—

"Fen." Sorrel was on her feet. Her voice did the thing it only did in dreams and floods, very calm and very fast at the same time. "Fen, get Bramble."

He didn't need telling. He was already moving, through the kitchen door into the common room, where Bramble stood at the taproom window with a dish towel still in her hand, her broad face lit orange, her mouth set in the line Fen knew meant trouble being measured and found manageable. She'd faced down floodwater and tax collectors and the winter the ferry iced over for six weeks. She was already turning toward the stairs, already shouting names—*Zeph! Isolde! Out, now, out the back—*

The front doors went in.

They didn't open. They went *in*, both leaves at once, slammed inward off their hinges by men in gray robes who filled the doorway wall to wall, and behind them more gray figures spilled into the yard, and above the yard, impossibly, impossibly, the night sky was *moving*—a shimmer like heat-haze standing upright, a dome of faint silver light settling down over the inn and the yard and the burning boathouse, and Fen knew what it was even though he'd never seen one. Everyone in the Hollow knew the stories. A ward-circle. They'd thrown a ward-circle over the inn the way you'd throw a net over a fish.

"Zephyrine Thackeray," said the man at the front, in a voice like a ledger being read aloud. He was old, gray-robed, gray-bearded, with a chain of small stone beads around his neck that Fen realized, with a lurch, were ward-stones. Dozens of them. Enough to build the dome alone. "By inheritance and covenant, we claim the oath-breaker. Yield him, and no harm comes to this house."

"He isn't here," said Bramble.

It was true. Fen knew it was true; he'd heard the back door bang two breaths ago, heard Zephyrine and Isolde slip out into the dark toward the shore. But the gray man smiled the way Aldous never smiled—Aldous's smiles were sad; this one had edges—and said, "Then the house has nothing to lose."

"Bramble," Fen whispered. "Bramble, the twins are—" and then his brain caught up with his mouth, because he *was* the twins, half of them anyway, and Sorrel was beside him, and they were standing in the open doorway of the kitchen in full view of nine gray-robed descendants of the founders of the Hollow, and the oldest of them looked past Bramble's shoulder directly at Fen and said:

"And what do we have here? Keepers' blood?"

"Nothing," said Bramble, and stepped backward, and put herself between them.

Fen had watched Bramble all his life. He knew her the way he knew the inn, by sound and habit: the way she kneaded bread with her whole arms, the way she swore at the stove, the way she'd once carried Fen and Sorrel both, one under each arm, out of the flooded millrace without dropping either or slowing down. She was the biggest person Fen knew. She was the safest place in the world.

She was also fifty-three years old and holding a dish towel, and there were nine of them, and the youngest moved like water.

"Last chance," said the gray man gently. "Yield the children to the circle's keeping and stand aside."

"They're my children," said Bramble.

"By paper," said the gray man.

"By *choice*," said Bramble, "which is worth more than yours," and then the youngest one moved, and everything after that happened the way disasters happen: completely, and all at once, and too fast to be believed until it was already true.

Fen saw it in pieces. A gray sleeve flicking outward, casual as brushing away a fly. Light crawling along the bead-chain. Bramble surging forward—not away, *forward*, arms wide, the way she'd stood in the millrace—and the air between her and the robed man going white and hard as the surface of the lake in January. The shockwave of it taking the kitchen door off its last hinge. Sorrel screaming his name. Himself, on the floor somehow, ears ringing, watching Bramble come down.

She didn't fall like a felled tree. She fell slowly, terribly, folding at the knees and then the waist, one hand going out ahead of her to keep her face off the boards, the dish towel still somehow in her fist, and when she tried to push herself up her left arm bent in a place arms don't bend, and the sound she made was worse than any scream Fen had ever heard because it was almost quiet—a wet, astonished grunt, the sound of a body filing a complaint it knows will be ignored.

"*No,*" said Sorrel, and launched herself at the gray men, and Fen caught her around the waist and dragged her down behind the overturned table as flame answered flame—the boathouse outside roaring higher, and now the dry eaves of the inn itself catching, embers walking up the old shingles like glowing spiders.

"You said no harm!" Sorrel was shrieking, at the world, at the dome, at the ancestors whose blood ran in her own veins. "You said yield him and no harm comes to the house!"

"The house," said the gray man, examining his beads, "is collateral. The covenant is the point. Children—" and his eyes found them again over the ruined table, patient as owls, "—will be raised correctly this time."

And Fen, crouched in the smoke with his sister shaking against his shoulder and Bramble breathing wrong on the floorboards ten feet away, understood something with a clarity that felt like a blade going in: they had never been afraid of Zephyrine. All those years of wards and cages and careful gray mercy, and they weren't afraid of the swordspeaker at all. They were afraid of *this*—of an innkeeper choosing, of twins existing unregistered, of a runaway ward-keeper's daughter burning her name, of ordinary people deciding for themselves whom to love. That was the crime the Hollow had actually been built to prevent. Zephyrine was just the excuse.

The smoke was getting thicker. Somewhere upstairs a beam cracked like a rifle shot. Fen got his shoulder under Bramble's good arm.

"We're getting her out," he said. His voice didn't shake, which surprised him. "Back door. Now. Help me or don't."

Sorrel helped. Of course Sorrel helped. Between them they walked Bramble—half-carrying her, her broken arm cradled against her chest, her boots dragging—through the kitchen, through the scullery, out into the yard where the cold hit them like a blessing and the boathouse was fully gone, just a skeleton of black ribs and a fountain of sparks climbing toward the stars.

Which weren't there.

Fen looked up and his stomach turned over. Above the dome of the ward-circle there should have been sky, and there was sky, but the stars over the Hollow were *out*—not clouded, not veiled, simply absent, nine dark holes in a pattern he didn't know but his bones did, some lesson Aldous had taught them by the fire years ago dressed up as astronomy. The pattern was complete now. Whatever the stars had been waiting for, they had finished waiting.

And across the water—at the shore path, at the tree line, at the edge of the lamplight where the world ended—stood Zephyrine.

He'd stopped running. That was the thing Fen saw first and would remember longest. He had Isolde's hand on his arm and forty yards of darkness behind him, a clear road south, away, gone—and he had stopped, the way a man stops when he hears his own name, and he was looking at the burning inn, at the gray dome, at Bramble sagging between the twins with her arm folded wrong.

Even at that distance, in that light, Fen could see his face change.

Zephyrine Thackeray had lived in the Hollow for as long as Fen could remember, and in all that time Fen had never once seen him angry. Careful, yes. Kind, watchful, tired in a deep old way, occasionally amused. Never angry. It had seemed like a feature of him, like his colorless eyes or the calluses on his sword-hand—just part of what Zeph was. Standing in the yard with the heat of their home on his face, Fen understood that it had never been a feature. It had been a discipline. Every day of every year, Zephyrine had chosen, deliberately, hour by hour, to be a man who did not get angry—and Fen was watching the choice end.

The lamplight reached him. The dome's silver glow reached him. And between one heartbeat and the next, the air around Zephyrine Thackeray *changed*—there was no better word Fen had for it. The smoke bent away from him. The sparks rising off the inn hung suspended, a hundred tiny orange stars refusing to fall. The lake, flat and black beyond the shore, shivered from edge to edge as if something enormous beneath it had rolled over in its sleep.

Isolde said something to him, urgent, grabbing his sleeve. He didn't seem to hear her. He took one step toward the inn, and the ground under Fen's boots trembled, and the gray men in the yard—all nine of them, even the owl-eyed elder—went very still, the way animals go still.

"Thackeray," the elder called out, and for the first time his ledger voice had a crack in it. "Think carefully. You are inside our circle. Your power is nothing against the covenant—"

Zephyrine spoke one word.

Fen didn't catch it. Later he would wonder if anyone caught it, if it was a word at all or just the shape a word leaves behind. What he caught was the effect: the ward-stone chain around the elder's neck flaring white and then cracking, bead after bead after bead, a sound like ice letting go in spring, and the dome overhead flickering—flickering like the stars had flickered nine nights ago—and the nearest wall of flame leaning *away* from the inn as if shoved by a giant's palm, buying seconds, buying breath, buying Bramble's life measured out in fire pushed back.

The dome held. Barely. The elder stood in the wreck of his necklace with blood on his knuckles and terror on his face, and Zephyrine stood at the edge of the light with the lake shivering behind him, and between them lay the burning inn and everything Fen loved.

"Get her to the mill," Zephyrine said. His voice carried across the yard effortlessly, the way thunder carries, and it was still his voice, still gentle underneath—that was the worst part, the gentleness underneath, like a riverbed under floodwater. "The stone cellar. Sorrel, your hands are steady—you'll need to bind the arm. Fen—" and here his eyes found Fen's, and for one naked instant the fury in them banked itself, deliberately, the choice remade, and what looked out at Fen was just Zeph, tired and human and grieving. "Keep counting sounds for me. I want to know how many I owe you."

Then he turned to face the nine descendants, and Isolde drew her shard of border-ward from her coat, and the lake began, very quietly, to recede.

Fen got Bramble moving. One foot, then the other, up the slope toward the mill, her weight heavy and alive against his shoulder, Sorrel sobbing and binding as they walked. Behind them the inn burned, and the dome strained, and a man who had spent his whole life being ordinary stopped pretending.

Fen kept counting. He couldn't help it. Sixty-one: the crack of another ward-stone. Sixty-two: Bramble's ragged breathing. Sixty-three: the lake, falling back from the shore with a long, whispering sigh, like something vast drawing breath to speak.

Sixty-four was a sound Fen had never heard before and knew instantly, the way you know thunder: the god beneath the water, waking up.
