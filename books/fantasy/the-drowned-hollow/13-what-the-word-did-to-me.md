## 13. What the Word Did to Me

The word is still in my mouth.

I wake with it there, lodged behind my teeth like a fishbone, and I lie in the narrow bed above the burned ferry's ruin—Bramble's sister's house now, a borrowed room in a borrowed life—and I do not say it. I do not move my tongue around it. I have learned that much in three days: the word wants to be said the way water wants to run downhill, and every time I let it even lean against the back of my teeth, something in me leans with it.

Three days since the shore. Three days since the ward-circle came apart like wet paper and Aldous went down with blood on his face and the lake dropped ten feet, ten feet, ten feet, exposing mudflats and drowned fence posts and the pale shoulders of a thing that had been sleeping under us for four hundred years.

Three days since I enjoyed it.

That's the part I can't stop circling. Not the power—I expected the power, feared the power, was raised on stories of the power. It's the *enjoyment*. The moment the circle shattered and the descendants scattered like startled crows, there was a half-second—a heartbeat, no more—when everything in me sang yes. When the world finally made sense because it was finally obeying.

I got up off the mud and I was smiling.

* * *

In the morning I go down to help Bramble's sister knead bread, because helping is what I do, helping is who I am, and I need to be someone this morning before I am anything else.

She says, "Fold it from the ends, love, like I showed you," and I hear—

I hear the *shape* of a command underneath it. Fold. From the ends. As if she were speaking swordspeech without knowing it, as if her ordinary voice were just a crude instrument playing a score that was always there, waiting for someone with ears to hear it true. And for one terrible instant I want to take the dough from her hands—not to help, not to learn, but to *show* her how it should be done, how it would be done if the world were arranged correctly, which is to say: by me.

"Zeph?" She's looking at me. Flour on her knuckles. A woman who lost her oven and nearly lost her sister-in-law in the fire, still standing here making bread for eleven people. "You've gone strange."

"Just tired," I say, and fold the dough from the ends, like she showed me, and hate myself gently, thoroughly, the way you hate an old familiar ache.

But all through the morning it keeps happening. Fen asks me to check the boat tie-lines and I hear *check them, boy, do as you're told*, and feel a flash of contempt so hot and so fast it scares me worse than the lake ever did—contempt for Fen, who held my hand at Aldous's bedside, who is sixteen and brave and has never commanded anything in his life except a pair of stubborn goats. Sorrel laughs at something and the laugh lands on me like a permission I didn't ask for, like the world saying *you may find this amusing*, and I want to scream.

The word isn't in my mouth anymore. That's the horror of it. The word is in everyone else's.

* * *

That night I dream of the lakebed.

I am walking on it, though it is dry, though the mud has hardened into pale flagstones laid in patterns too regular for water to have made. Drowned streets. The old village, the one Isolde and I swam down into when the world still made a kind of sense, except now I walk it upright and unhurried and the water stands aside for me in walls of green glass on either side, and behind the glass the fish hang motionless, watching, the way courtiers watch.

At the end of the street there is a throne.

It is made of lakebed. Of course it is. Silt packed and pressed into a high-backed seat, studded with the small bones of things that never learned to fear drowning, and set into it, worn smooth by centuries of sitting or centuries of waiting, a shape like a man. Like a chair built for exactly one person. Like a chair built for *me*.

I sit down in the dream. I don't decide to; my legs simply carry out the instruction my body has been given, and the silt takes my weight the way a hand closes, and from the throne I can see the whole valley spread out above me through the water—the inn, the rebuilt ferry landing, the little lights of the houses at dusk—and every light looks like a candle on a table. Something to be moved. Something to be arranged.

And beneath me, under the throne, under the flagstones, vast and slow and patient as geology, something turns over in its sleep and says—not in words, in pressure, in the way deep water presses on your ears:

*You sat. You are already mine. Say the rest.*

I wake up standing.

Standing in the middle of Bramble's sister's kitchen, barefoot, one hand raised in the air in the shape of a command, and the water in the washbasin is trembling, ringing against the ceramic in a thin high note, and outside the window every drop of dew on every blade of grass in the garden is shivering in perfect unison.

I put my hand down slowly. The water goes still. The dew goes still.

I stand there until the sky goes gray, and then gray-gold, and then ordinary morning comes, and I make myself count the cracks in the ceiling until I believe in them again.

* * *

Isolde finds me at the shore.

Of course she does. She has taken to finding me at the shore the way other people take to prayer—daily, quietly, without expecting an answer. She sits down on the mudflat beside me without asking what I'm doing, which is looking at nothing, which is listening to the lake listen back.

"You didn't sleep," she says.

"I slept fine."

"You're lying."

"I'm lying," I agree. Two liars recognizing each other—that was how we started, weeks ago, her bleeding on the ferry planks with a shard of the border ward in her fist. It seems like another life. It was another life. It was the life before the word.

She pulls her knees up. The exposed lakebed stretches out ahead of us, acres of it, cracked mud and dead reeds and the ribs of the drowned village showing like a whale's skeleton. Somewhere out there, past the flats, past the drop-off where the new waterline begins, the god lies full and slow and dreaming on the life I haven't fed it yet. My life. The one I promised.

"It's getting louder," I say.

"I know."

"You don't know. You can't—" I stop. Breathe. "Isolde. This morning I looked at Fen and felt contempt. Contempt. For *Fen*. He brought me soup last night and I heard his kindness as an order and I wanted to—to rearrange him. And yesterday Bramble's sister asked me to pass the salt and for half a second the only reason I didn't speak the word was that I don't know which word does what yet." I hear my own voice climbing, cracking. "Do you understand? The only thing between these people and whatever I am is *vocabulary*. I'm learning, Isolde. Every hour I'm learning faster."

She doesn't flinch. She never flinches. She looks out at the waterline for a long moment, jaw tight, and then she says, "Tell me about the ferry."

"What?"

"The ferry. Tell me about it. The day I arrived. Start at the beginning."

"Isolde—"

"Start at the beginning," she says, "or I'll start for you, and I'll get the details wrong on purpose until you correct me."

So I tell her. Because it's that or drown, and I know it, and I think she knows I know it.

* * *

"The morning you came," I say, "the fog was so thick the rope on the ferry landing disappeared two feet out. I remember thinking: good day to see nothing, bad day to be seen. I'd been ferryman six years by then. Seven? Six. I'd gotten so I could cross the Hollow blind—counting strokes, counting current. There's a shelf of rock under the narrows that makes the water stutter, did you know that? Little hiccup in the current, right over the deepest point. I used to time my strokes to it. Made the crossing feel like a conversation."

"A conversation with what?"

"With the water, I suppose." I almost laugh. With the water. With what was *in* the water. "Anyway. Mid-morning, fog burning off slow, and I hear something on the far bank that isn't a bird. And then you come stumbling down the reed-bank with your arm soaked red and a piece of the border ward in your fist like you'd torn it off God's own fence, and you look at me—you looked at me like you were deciding whether I was a door or a wall—and you say—"

"'How deep is this lake?'"

"How deep is this lake," I confirm. "First words. Bleeding all down your side, half-dead, and your first question is about the depth of the water."

"And you said—"

"'Deeper than it looks.' Which was truer than I knew."

"Truer than anybody knew," she says softly. "Go on. What did you do with my arm?"

"I stitched it. Badly. You told me I'd done it badly."

"I did. It was very bad. You tied it off like you were mooring a boat."

"I *was* mooring a boat. You were the boat." And there—it snags, the first real laugh in three days, small and surprised out of me, and the thing coiled behind my teeth shifts uneasily, displeased, because laughter is not obedience and it knows it. I hold onto the feeling like a rope thrown to a man going under. "Then I took you up to the inn and Bramble fed you stew you couldn't eat because you kept falling asleep in it, and Fen and Sorrel argued the whole evening about whether you were a spy."

"They were right."

"They were right," I say. "And Bramble put you in the room over the kitchen because it was warmest, and she charged you nothing, and when I asked her why—she'd known you five minutes—she said, 'Because she's somebody's daughter, and somebody somewhere is sick with worry.' That was Bramble. That was the whole of her philosophy. Everybody is somebody's daughter."

Isolde is quiet a moment. Out on the flats, a heron stalks the new shallows, hunting in territory that was lakebed for four hundred years. "Your turn's coming," she says. "Keep going."

"The next morning you were gone before dawn. I found you down here, on this exact stretch of shore, staring at the water. You said you'd dreamed about it. You said the lake dreamed back." I shake my head. "I thought you were feverish. I thought, poor girl, she's been hurt, she's talking nonsense. And I sat down next to you anyway, because—"

"Because?"

"Because you looked like a person who needed sitting next to." I rub my face. "Gods, Isolde. I remember all of it. The stew, the stitches, the fog. I remember the way Sorrel hid your boots as a joke and Fen gave them back and they didn't speak for two days over it. I remember Aldous teaching me knots when I was seven, his hands over mine, patient as tide. I remember—" My voice catches on something. "I remember all of it, and every day there's a little less of it that feels like *mine*. Like it belongs to a man I read about in a book. The word is eating the edges first. The edges of everything."

"Then we'll say the edges out loud," Isolde says. "Every day. All of them. Until the deep gets its meal, it doesn't get to eat you by inches too."

* * *

It becomes a ritual, though neither of us names it that. Naming things has gotten dangerous lately; I hear commands in names.

Each evening she sits with me and recites. The day Sorrel fell through the ice on the millpond and Fen screamed loud enough to bring half the valley running, and afterward the twins fought about whose fault it was and made up over stolen honeycakes. The night the nine stars went dark and the animals went silent and Aldous lied to our faces with such gentleness we thanked him for it. The dive beneath the lake, the ward-stone, my name in the old script at its center—she tells that one carefully, watching my hands, ready to catch me if I drift. The council. Her renouncing her name in front of the gray-robed descendants, her inheritance going up like smoke. "Say it back," she demands, and I say it back, all of it, and where my memory frays she mends it with hers, stitch after crooked stitch.

Some nights it works. Some nights I hold the whole of my life in my head like a basket of eggs and carry it safely to morning.

And some nights the dreams come, and the throne, and the streets, and I walk them in state with the water bowing on either side, and the voice beneath everything says *soon, soon, say the rest, you were made for this, the chair fits, the chair has always fit*—and I wake with my hand raised and the basin ringing and less of myself than the night before.

Last night the dream changed. Last night the throne wasn't empty-waiting. Last night I was already sitting in it, and I could not remember sitting down, and I could not remember the ferry, the fog, the girl with the ruined arm asking how deep the water was, and I felt—this is the part I will not tell Isolde, not yet, maybe not ever—I felt *fine*. Peaceful. Relieved. Like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying, glad to be rid of it, unable to recall why he'd ever carried it at all.

I woke up clutching the bedframe and whispering the ferry crossing to myself. Stroke count. Current. The stutter over the narrows. Over and over, like a prayer, like a rope.

* * *

On the fifth evening, Isolde doesn't come alone. She comes down to the shore with Fen and Sorrel trailing her, awkward, arms full—blankets, a lantern, a pot of Bramble's sister's stew—and they settle around me on the cold mudflat like it's a picnic, like the end of the world is a place you bring supper.

"We voted," Sorrel announces.

"Firmly against your objection," Fen adds, to Isolde.

"You said the recitations should be private," Isolde says flatly.

"And we outvoted you." Sorrel ladles stew into a bowl and pushes it into my hands. "You're doing the remembering wrong, Zeph. You're doing it like it's medicine. Medicine's bitter. Remembering's supposed to taste like stew."

So Fen tells the story of the ice and the millpond, but wrong—makes himself the hero, makes Sorrel scream like a kettle—and Sorrel corrects him loudly, and Isolde rolls her eyes, and I sit in the middle of them holding my bowl and laughing until my ribs hurt, real laughter, disobedient laughter, and the thing behind my teeth coils tighter and tighter because it cannot digest this. It can eat memories. It turns out it cannot eat being *told* them, badly, with corrections, over stew, by people who refuse to leave.

They stay until the lantern burns low. Then they pack up, and Fen claps my shoulder—claps it, casually, the way you touch a person, the way you touch a *man* and not a weapon—and Sorrel says, "Same time tomorrow. Bring your own blanket next time, you hog the big one," and they go up the bank toward the lights of the houses, arguing about the ice again, their voices fading into the dark.

Isolde lingers. She looks at me a long moment, reading me the way she reads wards—for flaws, for fractures, for the places where the pressure's building.

"Better tonight?" she asks.

"Better tonight," I say. And mostly it's true.

She nods once and follows the twins up the bank, and I sit alone on the shore with the stars overhead—nine of them still dark, a black constellation, a wound in the sky—and the lake breathing its slow breath in front of me, and I let myself feel, for one minute, one honest minute, like a man instead of a hinge.

Then the heron comes back.

It walks out of the shallows onto the mudflat not six feet from me, unafraid, which herons are not, and it stops, and it turns its head, and it looks at me with eyes that are not a bird's eyes—they are gray, and calm, and old, and terribly kind—and it speaks.

Not in swordspeech. In a child's voice. My voice, the one I had at seven years old, the one Aldous's hands once covered with knots.

*"Zephyrine,"* it says gently, *"your mother is asking for you. Come down to the water. She remembers you. She wants to give you back."*

And I am on my feet before I know I've moved, heart slamming, because my mother has been dead since I was six, and the god below has never once spoken to me directly—

—and out on the dark water, far past the drop-off, something vast begins, very slowly, to rise.
