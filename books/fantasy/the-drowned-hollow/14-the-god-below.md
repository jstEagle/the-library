## 14. The God Below

Isolde had been diving since she was six years old, in the cold rivers of the ward-cities where her father trained her the way other fathers taught children to pray: early, often, and with the understanding that one day it would be asked of her when she did not want to give it. She knew how to hold a breath until her vision went to silver. She knew how to let panic pass through her like weather. She knew, better than anyone alive, what it felt like to sink.

None of it prepared her for this.

The lake had dropped ten feet and kept dropping, and what it left behind was not mud. It was streets. Cobbled lanes running out of the waterline into open air, black with forty years of silt but unmistakably laid by hands, curving in the patient way that streets curve when they were built to be walked for centuries. Doorways gaped along them, doorways without doors, and through each one Isolde could see only dark water standing perfectly still, like panes of smoked glass set into the hillside. The drowned village. Not a village drowned *by* the lake—the lake was simply the last thing that had settled over it, the way snow settles over a house where everyone has already gone.

She stood at the water's edge in the gray hour before dawn, alone, because she had made sure of it. Zephyrine was sleeping at Bramble's, or lying awake pretending to; either way he was watched over, and that mattered more than anything else now. She had seen what the word had done to him. She had spent two nights reciting their days back to him like a rosary—*you burned the porridge on Tuesday, you laughed when Sorrel fell off the ferry roof, you hum when you think no one can hear you*—and each recitation pulled him a little further from the throne-of-lakebed dreams. But he could not come here. Not yet. The god below had already learned his name, and everything Isolde had seen in its heralds' gentle faces told her it would offer him something, and everything she knew about Zephyrine told him he might say yes.

So she came herself. Alone. With a rope tied to a dead tree and a knife at her belt and her father's oldest lesson written on the inside of her eyelids: *a ward-keeper who fears what she wards is already lost.*

The exposed street sloped down and down, past doorways, past a well with its bucket still hanging, past a shrine whose carved god had been defaced so long ago the chisel marks had softened into something almost like kindness. The air grew colder and wetter. Somewhere below, the true water waited—the deep heart of the lake, which had not receded at all, because it was not held up by rain or rivers.

It was held up by choice.

At the threshold of the last lane, the water began. Not rising gradually—it simply *was*, a wall of black glass from cobbles to sky, standing vertical as a cathedral door. Her lantern light bent against it strangely, and in the bending she saw movement: vast, slow, tectonic. Something turning over in its sleep, except it was not asleep. Nothing that had sent gentle dead men walking up a shore was asleep.

Isolde set down her lantern. Checked her rope. And then, because there was no ritual left in any book she trusted, she did the only thing that felt honest.

"I'm coming down," she said aloud. "Not to fight you. Not to ward you. To see you."

The wall of water opened.

* * *

Falling through it was like falling through night that had decided, just for her, to be breathable. Cold wrapped her whole body but did not crush; pressure should have flattened her lungs at this depth and instead her breath came easy, too easy, the water feeding her oxygen the way a mother feeds a child who cannot yet ask. That frightened her more than anything else could have. This thing was *caring* for her, carefully, the way you handle something small.

She sank past rooftops. Past a bell tower with its bell still hanging, green with age. Past windows where shapes moved—not the dead, she realized, but memories, impressions pressed into the water itself like fossils: a woman shaking out laundry, a man mending a net, children running a hoop down a lane that had been under forty feet of lake before Isolde was born. The god was not showing her these. They were simply *there*, the sediment of a life it had been holding for centuries, unable to put down, unable to let go.

And then the bottom.

There was no bottom. There was a shape, and the shape was so large that her eyes refused it at first, sliding off it the way they slide off the sun. Coils like drowned hills. A head the size of the inn, resting on the silt with its chin nearly touching the ruins of a market square, and eyes—

Eyes open. Ancient, lidless, the color of deep water over deep stone. Looking at her.

Isolde hung in the water three hundred feet above the floor of the world and looked back, and every ward-keeper instinct in her body screamed the same word: *malevolent.* Everything she'd been raised on said that what lived beneath a binding was hunger wearing patience, that gods chained under lakes dreamed only of breaking. Her father had drilled it into her across a thousand cold mornings. *They are not sad, Isolde. They are hungry. Never confuse the two.*

But the eyes looking up at her were not hungry.

They were starving, and those are different things, and she understood the difference suddenly and completely, the way you understand grief only after it happens to you. Hunger takes. Starvation *aches*. Hunger is a wolf at the door. Starvation is the wolf lying down outside it, too weak to stand, watching the light in the window.

*You're not going to attack me,* she thought, and the water around her shivered with something that might have been laughter, if laughter could be old enough to have forgotten how.

Then it showed her the war.

* * *

It did not use words. Words were too small for it, the way a cup is too small for a river. It pressed understanding directly into her, image after image, and Isolde hung in the freezing dark and wept without noticing, because the water carried her tears away before they could blur what she saw.

She saw the world before the war: green and loud with magic, magic in everything, in the wheat and the wind and the blood of ordinary people, so common it was barely noticed, the way fish do not notice water. And she saw the swordspeakers—young, radiant, terrible—walking through that world like drawn blades, their gift not a talent but an *office*, created for one purpose. There had been things in the young world. Old things. Vast things. Some of them cruel beyond reckoning, and the swordspeakers had been made—made, forged, *bred*—to speak the words that unmade them.

And it worked. For an age, it worked.

Then she saw the turn. Saw a swordspeaker—a woman with Zephyrine's exact stillness in her shoulders—stand before a god that had done nothing, that had harmed no one, that had simply grown too large and too strange for the world's new taste, and speak the word anyway. Because the order had been built to kill gods, and a tool that stops being used is a tool that gets questioned, and no order of executioners in history has ever voluntarily retired. She saw the war begin not because the gods rose but because the swordspeakers needed something to fall. She saw gods who had never raised a hand learn, very quickly, to raise them. She saw atrocities bloom on both sides like mold on fruit. She saw the old ones drown cities and the speakers unmake mountains, and somewhere in the middle of all that burning, both sides forgot entirely what the fighting had originally been about.

And she saw this one—the one beneath her, the one whose memory she was drowning in—put down its arms. Walk into a valley. Lie down in a lake and let the water close over it, because it was tired, because it had never wanted the war, because surrender seemed kinder than becoming the thing the enemy said it was.

And she saw the founders find it there.

They built the Hollow above its sleeping body. They sank a village onto its back to anchor the wards. They wrote bindings into the bedrock—not to kill it, killing was beyond them even then, but to starve it. Slowly. Over centuries. Because a starving god cannot rise, and a bound god needs no answer, and a problem sealed away is a problem that lets everyone sleep at night.

Her father's voice, in her memory, calm and certain: *they are not sad, they are hungry.*

*They chained it,* Isolde thought, and the thought went through her like a blade through silk. *We chained it. My line. My name. The gray robes, the ward-cities, the whole beautiful architecture of keeping—they built a prison around something that had already surrendered, and called it peace, and called themselves keepers, and taught their daughters that the prisoner was the monster.*

The god let her feel its agreement, and its agreement was worse than anger. It was tired. Four thousand years of tired, pressed into her chest like a hand.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, and had no idea if the words meant anything to something that old, and then felt the water warm around her by a single degree, and understood that they had.

* * *

It showed her one more thing before it let her go.

Zephyrine.

Not the man—she knew the man, knew the way he hummed and burned porridge and pretended contentment like a coat worn indoors. It showed her the *seal*. Showed her the night of his birth, the founders' descendants gathering in a room that smelled of lilies and iron, a screaming infant, and the great working that poured a living child's soul into the architecture of a cage. He had not been born in the Hollow by chance. He had not been hidden in the Hollow by luck. He *was* the Hollow's keystone, the final stone laid over the prison door, a boy raised to believe he was hiding a dangerous gift when in truth the gift was hiding him—and the cage had been built inward, around his heart, so that the god below would stay fed on his silence and the world above would stay blind to its own crime.

Both cages, she saw, were the same cage. The god's and the boy's. Built by the same hands, justified by the same fear, sustained by the same lie: *this is mercy, this is protection, this is the kindest possible arrangement for everyone involved.*

Everyone, of course, except the ones inside.

Isolde hung in the dark water and felt her whole inheritance rot in her chest. Her name. Her father's training. The shard of border ward she'd carried bleeding into the valley, the craft she'd fled and still loved, the entire gray-robed civilization of careful people doing careful cruelty and calling it stewardship. She had renounced her name in front of the council. She had burned her inheritance papers in the inn's hearth while Fen cheered. But renouncing was easy. What the god was asking of her now, silently, with four-thousand-year-old eyes, was harder:

*Tell them. Tell the boy. Tell all of them what I am, and let them choose what I deserve—and mean it. Mean it even if they choose the chain again. Even if they choose the sword. Be the first keeper in your whole rotten beautiful lineage who actually asks.*

The water began to lift her. Gently, irresistibly, the way the tide returns what it borrows. She rose past the bell tower, past the fossil-windows, past the frozen doorways, and just before the surface took her she looked down one last time into those vast patient eyes and gave the only answer she had.

"I'll tell him," she said. "All of it. And I'll fight for you—but I won't promise them you're safe. You're not. None of us are. That's the point."

The eyes closed. Not in dismissal. In something that looked, impossibly, like gratitude—or like rest, the first rest in an age, taken by something that had finally been *seen*.

* * *

She broke the surface gasping, though she hadn't needed to breathe, and dragged herself up the drowned street on limbs gone clumsy with cold and revelation. Dawn had come while she was under. The eastern ridgeline was burning gold, and the fog was lifting off the valley for the first time in living memory, because the wards were dead and the valley was learning, awkwardly, to be an ordinary place.

Zephyrine was standing at the top of the lane.

He was supposed to be asleep. He was wrapped in Bramble's spare cloak, barefoot on frost-slick cobbles, and his face was the face of a man who has been awake all night listening to dreams that aren't his. Behind him, small and determined, came Fen and Sorrel, and behind them Bramble, leaning on a crutch, her bandaged shoulder dark at the edge.

"You dove alone," Zephyrine said. Not angry. Worse. Afraid.

"Yes."

"What did you see?"

Isolde stood dripping on the ancient street, her father's daughter and no one's daughter, a runaway who had finally run toward something. She thought about how to soften it and decided, with the last of her courage, not to.

"I saw the war," she said. "I saw how it started, and it wasn't what any of us were taught. I saw what they did to you before you could walk. And I saw the god." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "Zeph—it's not a monster. It never was. It's a prisoner. We all grew up guarding its cell and calling it a temple."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. Then, from far below them—from beneath the cobbles, beneath the ruins, beneath the whole drowned weight of the valley—came a sound. Not a roar. Not a voice. A long, slow exhalation, rolling up through stone and water and bone, shaking frost from the grass along the shoreline.

The lake shuddered. And every drowned dead man standing motionless along the shore turned his head at once, gently, toward the sound of Zephyrine's breathing.

"They know you're awake," Isolde whispered. "And they're coming to ask you what we've decided."
