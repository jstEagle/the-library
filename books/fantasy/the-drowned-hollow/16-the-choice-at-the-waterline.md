## 16. The Choice at the Waterline

The lake had dropped so far that morning that the ferry stairs ended in mud.

Zephyrine stood at the bottom of them, boots sinking into silt that should have been ten feet underwater, and looked at what the water had uncovered: a road. An actual cobbled road running out from beneath the Hollow's shore and down into the dark, lined with drowned lampposts still holding their rusted lanterns, as if the village below expected to be walked again someday. As if it had only been waiting.

Behind him, the inn was a blackened skeleton. Bramble was alive—barely, held together by Sorrel's stitching and Fen's stubborn refusal to let her sleep too long—but the smell of char still hung over everything, and the descendants' gray robes were visible on the far ridge like a row of headstones.

He had done this. Not the fire—the fire was theirs. But the lake dropping ten feet when he spoke, the god stirring beneath, the dead walking the shore with gentle faces and empty eyes. All of it traced back to his voice.

"You're doing the thing," Isolde said, coming down the stairs behind him. "Where you stand very still and decide you're a weapon."

"I am deciding nothing."

"Exactly. That's the problem." She stopped beside him, arms crossed against the cold coming off the exposed lakebed. Her ward-shard hung at her throat, dim now, its light spent. "Aldous is dying in my father's old boathouse, and he asked for you twice, and you haven't gone."

"He'll tell me what he always tells me. That I'm a lock, or a key, or a knife. Something to be used."

"He told me something else," Isolde said quietly. "Something about the oath. But it isn't mine to give you." She turned to face him fully. "It can't be anyone's but yours. That's rather the point of what's coming tonight."

"What's coming tonight?"

She nodded up the shore, toward the flat stones by the old swimming hole where the whole valley gathered for midsummer bonfires. "Everyone's coming. Everyone who's left. And they're going to ask you to choose."

* * *

They came at dusk, when the memoryless dead had withdrawn to the waterline and stood there in their rows, patient as reeds, watching with faces Zephyrine almost recognized. The living came too—all forty-one souls who remained in the Hollow after the burning.

Bramble came wrapped in blankets, propped between Fen and Sorrel, her burned arm bound and her face gray with pain she refused to show. She had insisted on being carried down. "It's my inn that burned," she'd said. "I'll be at the meeting if I have to be brought in a wheelbarrow." She had, in fact, nearly been brought in a wheelbarrow, until Fen threatened to resign from wheelbarrow duty.

Old Marta came, and the Hollis brothers, and the widow Cade with her goats left tied above the tide line. Even three of the founders' descendants came—not the ones in gray robes on the ridge, but the young ones, the ones who had slipped away in the night, one of them carrying a ward-stone under her arm like a stolen loaf of bread.

They arranged themselves in a half circle on the flat stones, facing the water, and Zephyrine stood at the center of them with the lake's breath cold against his back.

Isolde spoke first. She laid it out plainly, the way she did everything, her voice carrying across the water without effort.

"Three roads," she said. "You've all seen what's happening. The lake is rising back, inch by inch, and every inch it takes, it takes something with it. Bramble forgot her daughter's face this afternoon. Fen and Sorrel—" she paused, and the twins' hands found each other, "—couldn't remember which of them broke the kitchen window when they were seven. Small things first. It won't stay small."

Nobody spoke. The drowned dead stood at the waterline, listening too, though Zephyrine couldn't have said how he knew they listened.

"So. Three roads." Isolde raised one finger. "First: we rebuild the wards. Re-ward the god. Chain it back down the way the founders did, the way Aldous did for forty years, feeding it scraps of memory to keep it quiet." Her voice hardened. "That road means someone becomes a cage-keeper forever. Someone becomes what Aldous was. Someone decides, alone, that their mercy gets to be another person's prison—and we've all just watched where that leads."

"The second road," said the descendant girl with the stolen ward-stone, and her voice shook. "Kill it. Full swordspeech. My family's records say a swordspeaker at full strength could unmake a god entirely." She looked at Zephyrine, and there was no accusation in her face, only fear—which was somehow worse. "And then there would be a swordspeaker at full strength standing in an unwarded valley. We've read what happens next. Every record ends the same way. He becomes the thing the oath exists to kill."

"That road has a shorter name," Isolde said. "It's called *tyrant*. We don't take it."

"And the third?"

Isolde looked at Zephyrine. It was his to say.

He had rehearsed this on the walk down, and the words still came out rough. "The third road is the one Aldous found at the end. A swordspeaker's oath can be reversed. Not redirected outward—given. Memories instead of power. A life instead of a leash." His hands were steady; he made them be steady. "The god isn't hungry for destruction. It's hungry because it's been starved for a thousand years, chained in the dark and fed drips. Feed it a whole life, freely given, and it settles. Not dead. Not caged. *Full.*"

"Whose life?" Old Marta asked.

"Mine," Zephyrine said. "All of it. Everything I remember. My childhood. Aldous. My name. The oath itself. I pour it into the deep, and I wake up on that shore tomorrow knowing none of it." He let that sit. "Loss without death. That's the road."

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere out on the black water, one of the drowned dead sighed—or the wind through the drowned lampposts made a sound like sighing.

Then Bramble moved.

It took both twins to help her, and she waved them off halfway and stood on her own, swaying, blanket slipping from her shoulders, and planted herself in front of Zephyrine like a woman planting a flag.

"No," she said.

"Bramble—"

"No. You don't get to do that. You don't get to stand there and offer yourself up like the last biscuit on the plate and expect us all to nod politely." Her voice cracked, and she pushed through it. "Forty-one people in this valley, and you're the only one who thinks this is your choice alone. You've been alone your whole life—that's what they built you for, that's what Aldous made you, one man in a cage calling it contentment. Well, the cage is open now, and I'll tell you what's in it. *Us.*"

Fen stepped up beside her. Then Sorrel. Then, one by one, the others—Marta, the Hollis brothers, the widow Cade, the runaway descendants—until the half circle had become a cluster around him, close enough that he could feel their breath in the cold air.

"We vote," Bramble said. "Not for you. With you. All in favor of the third road—of Zephyrine giving his memories, and us keeping him anyway—say aye."

"Aye," said Fen.

"Aye," said Sorrel, and her voice broke on it, and she said it again, louder.

"Aye," said Marta. "Aye," said the Hollis brothers, nearly in unison, the way they said everything. "Aye," said the widow Cade, and the descendant girl, and her companion, and then Isolde, last and clearest, looking at him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't dare try to.

Bramble took his hand. Her grip was fierce despite the burns. "Aye," she said. "And hear me, Zephyrine Thackeray. Tomorrow you won't know my name. Fine. I'll teach it to you. I taught six orphans to bake before you ever ferried a soul across that lake, and I can teach one more man how to live in my inn. You'll forget the oath? Good riddance. You'll forget Aldous?" She glanced toward the boathouse, and her jaw tightened. "We'll carry that one for you. Somebody should remember him kindly, and it might as well be people who saw him try, at the end."

"You understand what you're voting for," Zephyrine said, and his voice was not entirely steady anymore. "You're voting to lose me. The me that stands here."

"We're voting to keep you," Isolde said. "The rest was never really yours. It was built into you, installed, warded around. What's left when it's gone—" she smiled, small and crooked, "—that's the part we actually like."

Someone laughed—a wet, ragged laugh, maybe his own.

But the vote wasn't finished. Bramble turned to face the waterline, where the drowned dead stood in their patient rows, and raised her voice.

"And you!" she called. "You hear all this? You lot with the empty faces and the manners! This valley votes aye, and we're sending him down to feed your god properly, so you call off whatever it is you're doing to our heads, you hear me? Fen and Sorrel are *twins*, and they will stay twins, or so help me—"

One of the drowned figures took a step forward.

It was an old man, weed-hung, gentle-faced, and when he spoke, his voice came out layered, many voices braided into one, the way it had spoken Zephyrine's childhood oath back to him on the shore two nights ago.

"*Freely given,*" it said. "*Freely received. The deep accepts the terms.*" Its head tilted, and those many voices softened into something almost tender. "*Bring him to the heart. Bring him soon. The hollow places grow heavy, and we are so tired of taking.*"

It turned and walked back into the shallows, and the other dead followed, folding into the black water one by one until the shore was empty except for the living.

* * *

They gave him the rest of the night.

Zephyrine walked the valley he was about to forget. He went up through the orchard rows, apples hanging heavy in the dark, and touched the bark of the tree he'd climbed as a boy—except he hadn't, had he, there was no boyhood here, only the one Aldous had manufactured for him, and even that would be gone by morning. He sat in the ruins of the inn's kitchen and held one of Bramble's dented copper pots and tried to memorize the weight of it. He stood outside the boathouse a long time, not going in, saying goodbye to a dying man through a wall.

Isolde found him at the waterline near dawn.

"You didn't sleep."

"Did you?"

"No." She sat down on the wet stones beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched. "I've been thinking about tomorrow. About what I'll say to you when you surface and don't know me."

"What will you say?"

"I had a whole speech." She picked up a flat stone and skipped it out across the water—it still reached the water, barely; the lake was climbing again, slow and obedient, filling the bed it had emptied. "Something about how I'm a liar and a runaway and you shouldn't trust me, delivered in a tone that makes it clear you absolutely should. Very charming. Rehearsed it four times."

"And now?"

"Now I think I'll just say my name and see what you do with it." She was quiet a moment. "Zeph. If it goes wrong—if the god takes more than you offered, if you come up wrong—I want you to know there was a version of tonight where I begged you not to go. I decided against it. You deserve a door you chose, not a wall you were pushed through."

"That might be the kindest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it. I'm mostly terrible."

He laughed, and the sound went out over the water, and far below—so far down now that the drowned lampposts were submerged again, the road swallowed, the lake nearly at its old height—something vast shifted in the dark. Not rising. Settling. Waiting for its meal.

The sun came up gray and gold over the eastern ridge, and the gray-robed descendants on the hilltop had grown fewer in the night, some gone home, some come down, one of them already standing awkwardly at the edge of the flat stones holding a kettle, as if the siege might simply dissolve into breakfast.

Bramble appeared at the top of the ferry stairs, upright, refusing all assistance, with Fen and Sorrel flanking her like honor guards.

"Well?" she called down. "Are we doing this, or do I have to come down there and push you?"

Zephyrine stood. The water lapped at the third stair, calm, patient, waiting to receive him.

"One more thing," Isolde said, rising beside him. She pulled her ward-shard from around her neck and pressed it into his palm, closing his fingers over it. "So you go down carrying something of ours instead of going down empty. So the last thing you know about us is that we sent you with a light."

The shard was warm. It glowed faintly against his skin, the first magic he had ever held that felt like a gift rather than a sentence.

Together, without ceremony, the whole valley walked him down to the waterline—and the lake opened before him like a held breath about to be released.
