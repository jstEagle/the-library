## 17. March of the Memoryless

**Bramble**

Bramble had kept her daughter's face for thirty-one years, and she lost it in the time it took to pour a cup of cider.

She was standing at the hearth of what remained of the inn—the east wall gone to char, the roof half-open to a sky that still refused its stars—when the forgetting came. It did not feel like losing something. That was the horror of it. It felt like reaching for a thing that had always been exactly where she left it and finding smooth stone instead.

Maren. Her daughter's name was Maren. She knew that the way she knew her own hands. But when she closed her eyes and called the face up—round cheeks, the gap in the front teeth from the fall off the woodpile, the way one eyebrow arched higher than the other when Maren laughed—there was nothing. A warm blur. A shape where a girl should be.

"No," Bramble said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the kettle, to whatever vast patient thing beneath the lake was drinking her life through a straw. "No, you don't get that. You don't get *her*."

The kettle whistled. She couldn't remember why she'd put it on.

She gripped the counter until her knuckles went white and made herself say it out loud, over and over, like driving stakes into soft ground: *Maren Thistlewood, born in the spring of the long frost, hair like wet copper, laughs with her whole body, went south to marry a cooper, writes every month.* Writes every month. There were letters. Letters she could read, letters that would prove the face back into existence—

Except the letters were upstairs, and upstairs was burned, and even if they weren't, she could already feel the words going strange in her memory, the sentences unraveling into a sense that some paper somewhere loved her, which was not the same as knowing why.

From outside came a sound she'd heard only once before, on the night the stars went out: silence. Total animal silence. No dogs, no owls, no wind in the pines. And beneath it, faint as a pulse, a rhythm of footsteps—not many, then more, then more than should be possible—coming down from the high shore of the lake toward the village.

Bramble took down the iron poker, thought about it, and set it back. Then she went out into the fog to find Fen and Sorrel, because whatever was coming up the shore, her children were not going to meet it standing alone in the dark, forgetting things.

If she could still remember, by then, that they were her children.

* * *

**Fen**

Fen woke with Sorrel's elbow in his ribs and his sister's voice sharp with fear: "Fen. Fen, wake up, there's people walking on the water."

There weren't people on the water. There were people coming *out* of it—a long line of them, wading ashore through water that lay flat as poured glass, dozens of figures gray and dripping and unhurried, moving up the shingle with the mild purpose of neighbors coming in from a field. He knew some of their faces. Old Tam Wicker, who'd drowned before Fen was born hauling nets in a squall. A woman in a style of dress nobody wore anymore. Children. Gods, there were children among them, small and pale and serene, wringing lake water out of their sleeves as they walked.

They weren't rotting. They weren't hungry-looking. They looked—this was the worst part—they looked *kind*. They looked like people who had forgotten every hard thing that had ever happened to them, which, Fen understood with a lurch, was exactly what they were.

"Sorrel." He caught her arm. "We have to get to Zeph. We have to tell him they're—"

"Who?"

He turned. Sorrel was looking at him with polite, faintly puzzled attention, the way you look at a stranger who has addressed you by the wrong name.

"Fen," he said. "Your brother. I'm your brother."

"I know," she said slowly, and he watched her reach for it and find nothing, watched her face do the thing Bramble's face must have done in the kitchen—reach, grasp, close on emptiness. "I know that word. I know it means—" Her brow creased. "Why do I know it means something? Why does it hurt to not remember?"

"It doesn't matter." His voice cracked. "It doesn't matter that you can't remember. I remember enough for both of us. Come on."

He took her hand. She let him, wary as a cat, but she didn't pull away, and he decided—there on the hill above the shore, with the dead of the Drowned Hollow walking gently out of the lake—that he would hold onto her hand until one of them reached the deep or dropped dead trying, and that memory, this one, right now, he would guard like a coal in a gale.

Because he could feel it happening to himself too. Not faces yet, not names—but edges. The shape of his tenth birthday going soft. The taste of Bramble's plum pudding thinning to the idea of sweetness. Somewhere below the valley an enormous patience was sifting through everything he was, taking first what he'd miss least, and it would work up, steadily, inevitably, to the things he'd die without.

The drowned dead passed below them without looking up. All except one.

Old Tam Wicker stopped at the edge of the lane, turned his gray face toward the hill, and smiled at Fen with terrible gentleness.

"He remembers you," Tam said, in a voice like water finding a drain. "Down below. He keeps what he takes. Nothing is lost, little ferryman's boy. Nothing is ever lost. It's only his now."

Then Tam walked on, and Fen ran, dragging Sorrel behind him, down toward the smoke-stained ruin of the inn where Bramble stood waiting with a lantern and a face that was trying very hard to place them both.

* * *

**Isolde**

By the third hour of the pulling, Isolde could map the god's hunger by what people lost.

It wasn't random. That was the discovery that turned her blood to ice water. It worked outward from the shallow memories the way a fire works outward from kindling—first the trivial, then the treasured, then the structural. Marta at the mill forgot the words to a lullaby and then forgot she'd ever had a mother who sang. The cooper's widow forgot the road to the south towns and then forgot she'd ever wanted to leave. The god was not ravening. It was *methodical*, eating the valley the way a careful guest eats a meal, and Isolde understood with sickening clarity that it had been starving for four hundred years and had just been handed permission to stop pretending otherwise.

Aldous had fed it forty years of his own memories, drip by drip, a man bled white by his own mercy. Forty years against four hundred. It had never been enough. It had never even been a beginning.

She found Zephyrine at the waterline, standing where the lake had withdrawn another foot, staring at the exposed mudflats where the roofs of the old village showed like ribs.

"It's taking them," she said. "All of them. Everyone. It'll take us last because we're farthest from the water, but it'll take us, Zeph. And when it gets to you—" She didn't finish. When it got to him it would find either a swordspeaker's power or a swordspeaker's past, and either way the choice they'd all voted on at the waterline would be made for them, by absence.

"Then we go tonight." He said it flatly, no drama in it, and that flatness frightened her more than shouting would have. Since the shore, since the ward-circle shattered under his voice, something in him had been running hot and quiet, like a banked forge. "Before it reaches the middle of anyone. Before it takes—"

"Bramble's already lost Maren's face."

He flinched as if she'd struck him. Good, some part of her thought cruelly. Feel it. Let it move you faster.

"And the twins," she went on, relentless, because the clock was ticking in people's heads and someone had to say the numbers aloud. "Sorrel doesn't know Fen. Fen knows her, for now, but the tide goes both ways, Zeph. By morning there may be nothing left in this valley but strangers standing next to each other wondering why they're sad."

Zephyrine looked out at the water. The drowned dead were still coming ashore, in ones and twos now, unhurried, and farther out, where the lakebed fell away into blackness, Isolde could feel it—the attention of the god, vast and diffuse and terribly gentle, spread through the water like cold through a current.

"It's not attacking us," Zephyrine said quietly. "That's what I keep coming back to. If it wanted us destroyed, we'd be destroyed. It's just... hungry. And we're food."

"Then we feed it what we voted to feed it." She stepped closer, made him look at her. "You. Your memories. Your whole life, freely given, at its heart, before it strips this valley bare looking for less willing meals. The Deeping. Tonight, while there's still a valley full of people who remember why we're doing this."

"And if I forget why I'm doing it on the way down?" His mouth twisted. "Isolde, I can already feel it. It started the moment I spoke the word at the shore. My mother's face is—I had my mother's face yesterday. This morning it's a warm blur and I *know* it's a warm blur, which is worse somehow—"

"Then I'll carry it." She said it before she could think better of it, and meant it anyway. "Everything you lose between here and the deep, I'll hold. Every name, every day, every reason. I'll say them to you as we go, and you'll say them back, and if you can't say them back I'll say them twice. That's the whole job, Zeph. That's why I dove to your valley bleeding. Somebody has to remember the man while he saves everyone else."

For a moment he just breathed. Then he nodded, once, and turned toward the village, where lanterns were converging through the fog—Bramble's light, and Fen carrying Sorrel on his back though she could walk, and behind them a scatter of others, the whole stubborn remnant of the Hollow coming down to the shore.

"They're coming to see you off," Isolde said.

"No." Zephyrine picked up the oath-sword from the mud where it had lain since the burning, and slung it across his back, hilt forward, ready. "They're coming to walk me to the water. And gods help anything that tries to stop them."

* * *

**Sorrel**

Sorrel did not know the boy whose hand held hers, but she knew three things with absolute certainty, and she built herself out of them like a raft.

One: he was hers to protect. The knowing lived below memory, in the marrow, and no amount of drinking from below could reach it. Whatever the lake took, it could not take the fact of *mine*.

Two: the tall woman with the ward-shard scar was telling the truth, and truth tasted like cold clean water after weeks of dust.

Three: the man with the sword walking ahead of them toward the shore was going to give himself away, and everyone walking with him had agreed to let him, and Sorrel had agreed too, though she could not for her life remember agreeing—and that, more than anything, told her what kind of man he was. You did not walk into the dark to forget yourself forever for someone you wouldn't follow.

The drowned dead lined the shore now, hundreds of them, silent, watching the procession come down through the fog. They did not bar the way. They parted, in fact, opening a lane from the shingle down to the black water, and Sorrel understood that they had been sent as an honor guard, or a warning, or both.

At the waterline, Bramble stepped in front of Zephyrine and stopped him with one hand flat on his chest.

"I'm supposed to tell you something," she said. Her voice shook. "I wrote it down this afternoon because I could feel it going. It's from—" her jaw worked— "from someone. From me, I think. Before." She pressed a scrap of charred paper into his palm. "Read it before you go under."

Zephyrine read it by lantern light. Sorrel watched his face and saw him come apart, quietly, and put himself back together, and fold the paper into his breast pocket over his heart.

"What does it say?" she asked him.

"It says," he managed, "that somebody here loves me like a son, and that I'm to come back wrong or not at all, because a whole Zephyrine belongs to the deep now, and a new one belongs to us." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and steady. "I intend to come back wrong."

"Good lad," said Bramble, who had no idea why she was crying.

* * *

**Fen**

The last thing Fen saw before the water closed over them was the whole valley standing on the shore holding its lanterns up—dozens of lights, then hundreds, every soul the Hollow had left, arranged along the shingle like a constellation someone had nailed to the dark to replace the stolen stars.

*So we'll be your sky,* he thought. *Go on down. We'll burn here till you come back.*

Beside him, swimming strongly, Isolde kept up a low constant murmur—names, days, the smell of Bramble's bread, the sound of Sorrel laughing, the weight of the oath-sword, the color of the fog at dawn—feeding Zephyrine his own life one spoonful at a time as the lake drank the rest. And Zephyrine swam between them, repeating what he could, silent where he couldn't, descending past the drowned rooftops into streets that had waited four hundred years for someone to walk them again.

Below them, far below, something enormous turned over in its sleep and began, gently, joyfully, to rise to meet them.

And on the shore, Fen felt the lake's long fingers finally find the edge of what he'd guarded all night—the hand in his, the coal in the gale—and he gripped Sorrel tighter and shouted her name at the water until his voice broke, because if the deep wanted his sister's face it would have to take it out of his mouth first.

The water swallowed the sound. The lanterns burned on. And the descent began.
