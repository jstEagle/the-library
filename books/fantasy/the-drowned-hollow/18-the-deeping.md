## 18. The Deeping

The lake had dropped so far that its old shoreline was a ring of pale stone around the valley's throat, like the scar left by a ring pulled from a finger. Below that scar, the water hung in walls on either side of us—held back not by any craft I could name but by the god's own attention, parted like a curtain while we walked down into what it had swallowed.

Isolde went first. She had insisted, and I had let her, because the truth was that I no longer trusted myself to lead.

The stairs were original. That was the thing that kept catching in my chest as we descended—the steps were worn smooth in their centers, the way steps wear when generations of feet have loved them into softness. These had been streets once. A town. People had carried bread up these stairs and argued about weather and buried their grandparents on the hill above, and then the water had come, and none of it had been an accident. The founders hadn't flooded a valley. They had drowned a congregation and built their cage on top of its church.

"Chimney," Isolde said, pointing with her knife at a stone stack rising out of the murk ahead, its hearth still black with four-hundred-year-old soot. "Someone cooked dinner here. The night before."

"The night before what?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. The night before the water.

My gift had been whispering since the shore. That was the only word for it—a whisper that wasn't sound, that lived behind my eyes where words lived, and it spoke in my own voice, which was the worst part. *You could part this water permanently,* it said as we picked our way down a street whose cobbles were furred with white silt-bloom. *You could walk these streets dry. You could give them back their town.* And beneath that, quieter, sweeter: *You could take it for yourself.*

I counted my breaths. Four in, hold, six out. Isolde had taught me that on the second night after the inn burned, when I'd woken with my hand closed around a bedpost hard enough to crack the wood and no memory of reaching for it.

"How far?" I asked, because speaking aloud helped. It gave the whisper competition.

"The ward-stone was at the temple." She glanced back at me, and in the strange light—the sky above, filtered through fathoms of held-back water, came down green and trembling—her face looked carved from something older than her years. "But the god isn't at the temple anymore. The stone was a lock. The god is what was locked."

"So we go deeper."

"We go deeper."

*Deeper,* agreed the voice that wore my voice like a glove. *Yes. Come down. You've always been coming down. Every step of your whole careful little life was a step toward me.*

I said nothing to it. I had learned that much: it fed on acknowledgment the way the lake fed on memory.

* * *

The first ward-ward found us at the mouth of what had been a market square.

I felt it before I saw it—a wrongness in the air, a pressure like a hand flattening against my sternum. Isolde felt it too; she stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into her, and her free hand came up in the ward-keeper's gesture I'd seen her use only twice, fingers spread, thumb tucked, a shape like a door held half-shut.

"Descendants' work," she breathed. "They seeded the deeping before the council broke. I thought—" Her jaw tightened. "I thought we'd have until the bottom."

The ward-ward assembled itself out of the water-light. There was no better word. Gray light gathered the way frost gathers, creeping along the cobbles and up the ruined fronts of the market stalls, and where it gathered it took shape: figures, seven of them, tall and faceless, robed in the same gray as the council that had come to our shore with its offer of amnesty. Ward-wards. Not guardians. *Wardens*—the distinction Isolde had drawn for me weeks ago, back when I still believed wards were protection. A guardian stands between you and harm. A warden stands between you and the door.

"They can't be killed," Isolde said. "They're not alive. They're instructions wearing shapes. We go through or we go around, and there is no around—the square's the only way to the sunken causeway."

"Then we go through." My hand found the oath-sword's hilt at my back. I hadn't drawn it since the shore. Since the moment I'd shattered the descendants' circle and felt, for one blazing instant, exactly what I was made for—and enjoyed it. "Tell me what breaks them."

"Nothing breaks them. They're anchored to the border-ward lattice, and the lattice runs under the whole valley like roots." She turned to face me fully, and I saw she was afraid—not of the ward-wards. Of me. "Zephyrine. If you draw that sword down here, if you speak even one word of command in this place, you're feeding it. The god. This close to its heart, your power isn't yours. It's an echo of the thing that taught your order how to speak. Every word you say down here, it hears. Every word, it learns you better."

The whisper laughed. It didn't have lungs, but it laughed, low and delighted, and I understood suddenly that Isolde was right and that it didn't matter. Because the ward-wards were already moving, gliding across the square with the terrible patience of things that had never once been in a hurry, and behind them, far below, I could feel Bramble forgetting. I couldn't explain how I knew—I simply knew, the way you know your own hand is bleeding. Somewhere above us, in the inn's scorched shell where Sorrel sat keeping watch over her bandaged mother, the god was pulling threads, and Fen had asked his sister twice that morning who she was, and Sorrel had answered both times without crying until he'd gone outside.

We had hours. Maybe fewer.

"I'll speak as little as I can," I said. "One word, if I must. No more."

"One word," Isolde echoed. "Make it count."

I drew the oath-sword.

The sound it made coming free of the scabbard was not the sound of steel. It was the sound of a held breath released after four hundred years, and every ward-ward in the square stopped at once, seven gray faces turning toward me with the blind attention of compass needles finding north.

*There you are,* whispered the voice in my skull, warm as bathwater. *There's my good right hand.*

I did not answer it. I answered the ward-wards instead.

I meant to say *stop*. One word, clean, minimal, a door shut gently. But the swordspeech has never been a language you speak so much as a language that notices what you mean and improves upon it, and what came out of me was older and vaster than *stop*: a single syllable that contained stillness itself, the stillness at the center of a falling stone, the stillness between lightning and thunder, and the word went out across the market square like a bell struck underwater.

The ward-wards stopped. All seven. Mid-stride, mid-glide, frozen as statues in their gray robes, and the frost-light guttered along their edges and went out, and they stood there, empty instructions with nothing left instructing them.

And the lake heard.

I felt it hear. The whole mass of the held-back water shivered against its invisible walls, and from somewhere below—far below, below the sunken causeway, below the temple, below anything with a name—came a slow exhalation that moved the silt off the cobbles in a long silver breath. Not anger. Not alarm.

*Recognition.*

"Zephyrine." Isolde's hand was on my arm, and I realized I was swaying, and that blood was running freely from my nose, warm over my lips, tasting of iron and of something else, something green and deep. "Hey. Look at me. What did it cost?"

"Nothing," I said, and then, because lying to her had become impossible somewhere in the last month and I hated that and needed it: "It didn't cost. That's the problem. It *paid*. Like drawing water from a well that wants to be drawn from." I wiped my face with the back of my hand. "It liked being used, Isolde. I could feel it liking it."

She studied me for a long moment. Green light trembled across her face. Then she nodded once, sharp, and said, "Then we run for the causeway, and you don't speak again unless the alternative is dying," and we ran.

* * *

The sunken causeway was a road that had once led out of the drowned town toward hills that no longer existed, and walking it was like walking through the memory of a country. Houses stood open on either side, doors ajar, tables set. In one doorway a cradle rocked gently in the current of our passing, though there was no wind down here and no water either—only that held, attentive stillness, the whole drowned world suspended like a note sustained past all endurance.

The whisper grew stronger with every step. It had stopped pretending to be separate from my own thoughts, which was the most dangerous thing it had done yet. Now it came as *my* thoughts, arriving with my own cadence, my own doubts wearing finer clothes.

*You could raise this town,* it murmured as we passed a shrine whose little god-statue had been lovingly beheaded by someone's careful chisel, four centuries ago, in obedience to some founder's edict. *Not restore. Raise. New timber on old bones, roofs on the standing walls, and every family that ever lived here brought back to fill them. You've spoken death-words and binding-words. Speak a waking-word. You are the last swordspeaker. Nothing says you can't.*

"That's not raising," I muttered. "That's puppetry."

"What?" Isolde called back.

"Nothing. Keep walking."

But the whisper only smiled inside my skull. *Is it? Is it really? You've spent your whole life among people who love you. Ask yourself honestly, ferryman: if you could fill the Hollow with the dead of this town instead—with folk who owed you their breath, who would never leave, who would look at you the way Bramble looks at her twins—wouldn't a small, tired piece of you want that?*

And the horrible truth, the one I would carry if I survived to carry anything, was that a small, tired piece of me did want it. Not much. A splinter. But the god below didn't need much. It had been starving for four hundred years, and starving things learn to live on splinters.

The second ward-ward caught us at the causeway's broken span.

This one was different. It didn't assemble from frost—it was simply *there*, standing at the gap where the road fell away into darkness, and it was shaped like a person. Shaped like Aldous, in fact, gray-robed and gray-faced, and I understood with a lurch that the descendants had built it from his confession, from whatever record the council had made of the man who had raised me and lied to me and, in the end, bled out on the shore trying to give me the truth. They had made a warden wearing my dead mentor's face, and it stood at the broken bridge and said, in a voice like dust settling:

*"The swordspeaker turns back. The swordspeaker is re-warded. The swordspeaker sleeps safely forever."*

"No," I said.

*"The swordspeaker turns back,"* it repeated, patient as tide. *"The swordspeaker is re-warded."*

"It's stalling," Isolde hissed. "It's not strong enough to stop us—it's making us spend time. Zeph, the span—we jump it, or we swim the dark under it, or we *make* a bridge, but we do it now."

*I could make a bridge,* whispered my own voice, my own treacherous hope. *One word. Stone rises. Easy. Kind, even. You'd be building, not breaking. Building is allowed, isn't it?*

And that was the trap laid bare, and I saw it, and seeing it was the only thing that saved me. There would be no kind uses. Not here. Every word I spoke in the deeping was a coin paid into the god's purse, and whether I bought a bridge or a battlefield, the purse grew heavier either way.

So I did the hardest thing I had done since the shore. I put the sword away.

"Jump," I said, and jumped.

The gap was twelve feet of empty dark, and I was carrying exhaustion and grief and a nosebleed that wouldn't quit, and I nearly didn't make it. My hands slapped wet stone at the far edge and held—barely, fingernails screaming—and Isolde landed beside me a heartbeat later, rolled, came up with her knife out, and together we scrambled backward away from the gray thing wearing Aldous's face, which watched us go without moving, saying its three sentences to the empty air in its dust-and-settling voice until the dark folded over it.

We climbed. The causeway became stairs again, and the stairs became a descending gallery, and the gallery's walls began to change. The masonry got older. Older, and then older than old—stones fitted without mortar, stones cut by hands that had never known iron, and carved over every surface, floor to unseen ceiling, ran writing in the old script that I had no right to read and read anyway, fluently, the way you read the language of your own nightmares.

*WE DID NOT CHAIN IT OUT OF CRUELTY,* said the walls. *WE CHAINED IT BECAUSE WE WERE AFRAID. FORGIVE US. WE WERE AFRAID.*

And below that, older still, in a hand that shook:

*IT ONLY ASKED TO REMEMBER US.*

* * *

The threshold was a doorway.

After everything—the drowned town, the ward-wards, the miles of descending dark—I had expected a cavern, an abyss, a scale to make me feel like an insect. Instead the deeping ended at a doorway of plain black stone, tall as two men, standing freestanding at the head of a final stair, and beyond it there was neither water nor rock nor light but a presence, vast and slow and attentive, the way the ocean is present beyond a ship's rail at night.

Isolde stopped at the foot of the stair. So did I.

"This is where I leave you," she said quietly.

"No."

"Yes." She turned to me, and she was crying, though her voice never wavered. "It showed me. When I dove before. It doesn't want two of us—it wants *you*, and if I stand beside you at the threshold it will spend half its strength deciding between us, and you need every scrap of its attention on you, giving, not taking." Her hand came up and pressed something into mine: the shard of border-ward she'd carried since the night she stumbled into the Hollow bleeding impossible blood. It was warm. It hummed faintly, like a struck bell fading. "So it remembers what it's giving up. What the wards were. Carry it down."

"Isolde—"

"Don't." She laughed, wet and fierce. "Don't make a speech. You're terrible at speeches. Just—" She grabbed the front of my shirt and pressed her forehead to mine, and for a moment neither of us breathed. "Come back up. Even if you're someone else. Especially if you're someone else. I'll introduce myself. I'm good at beginnings."

Then she stepped back against the gallery wall, arms crossed tight over her heart like a woman holding a wound closed, and I climbed the last stair alone.

The whisper rose to meet me at the threshold, and for the first time it dropped all pretense. It stopped wearing my voice. It spoke in its own—which was not a sound at all but a pressure, a meaning that arrived whole, the way you understand falling without needing the word.

***LITTLE SWORD.***

The black doorway filled—not with a body but with attention, and the attention was ancient beyond bearing, and gentle, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing I had ever felt, because cruelty I knew how to refuse.

***YOU CAME DOWN ALL THIS WAY. LET ME SHOW YOU WHAT I HAVE KEPT FOR YOU.***

And it showed me.

It gave me my childhood back—not a vision, not a memory offered at arm's length, but the *thing itself*, poured warm into my mind: rain on the orphanage roof at Vell's Crossing, the smell of tallow and wet wool, Aldous young and laughing, teaching me to whittle, his big clumsy hands guiding mine. It gave me my first oath, spoken at fourteen with my palm bleeding on the altar-stone, and the pride in Aldous's eyes, and the taste of the celebration ale, and the name of the girl I'd danced with who died in the purge two winters later, whose face I had deliberately forgotten and now had again, freckles and all, and I sobbed aloud at the threshold of a drowned god because her name was Merrin and I had not thought of her in twenty years and now I would never be able to stop.

***ALL OF IT,*** the god said, tender as a mother. ***EVERY DAY. EVERY NAME. I HAVE THEM ALL. THE LAKE KEEPS WHAT FALLS INTO IT, LITTLE SWORD, AND YOUR ORDER FELL INTO ME PIECE BY PIECE. YOUR PAST IS NOT LOST. IT IS HERE. TAKE IT BACK AND KEEP IT AND NEVER AGAIN BE A MAN WITH A HOLE WHERE HIS LIFE SHOULD BE.***

The memories kept coming, and oh, it knew what it was doing. It gave me the war next—the true war, unchained from the founders' histories: the swordspeakers and the god, before the drowning, before the fear. It showed me that my order had not been executioners originally. They had been *speakers*—intercessors, the ones who talked the vast hungry things of the deep world into sleeping, into waiting, into patience. It showed me the bargain my order had broken. It showed me the first star going out, and whose hand had snuffed it.

***AND MORE THAN THIS,*** it said, and its attention leaned close, and the doorway's darkness rippled like heat. ***THE WAR CAN BE WON. NOT RESTARTED—WON. FINISHED, THE WAY IT SHOULD HAVE BEEN FINISHED BEFORE YOUR ORDER TAUGHT THE WORLD TO BE AFRAID OF ITS OWN DEEP HEART. STAND ASIDE. SPEAK MY RISING. I WILL MAKE THE VALLEY A GARDEN AND THE DESCENDANTS' GRAY TOWERS WILL COME DOWN LIKE SANDCASTLES AT TIDE-TURN, AND NO ONE YOU LOVE WILL EVER AGAIN BE HUNTED FOR LOVING YOU.***

And then, last, softest, the thing it had saved like a knife kept for the end:

***A CROWN.***

It showed me the crown. Not gold—stone, lakebed stone, worn smooth as the drowned stair-steps, set with nine stars returned to burning. It showed me sitting beneath it, and the terrible thing, the thing I will refuse to remember if I survive this, is how *right* it looked. How natural. The whisper had been telling the truth all along, in its way: every step of my careful little life had been a step downward, and the throne at the bottom fit me like a hand fits a glove, and the glove was already closing.

***YOU HAVE BEEN ALONE YOUR WHOLE LIFE,*** the god said, and there was no lie in it, and that was the sharpest edge of all. ***ALWAYS HIDING. ALWAYS ORDINARY ON PURPOSE. ALWAYS THE LAST OF SOMETHING DEAD. I OFFER YOU AN END TO THAT. NO MORE DISCIPLINE. NO MORE COUNTING BREATHS. EVERYONE BELOW WILL KNOW YOUR NAME AND TREMBLE, WHICH IS ONLY ANOTHER WORD FOR CARE.***

I stood at the threshold with my dead childhood pouring through me and a crown shining in the dark, and I thought about Bramble forgetting her daughter's face. I thought about Fen asking Sorrel who she was, twice, and Sorrel answering both times. I thought about Isolde against the wall upstairs with her arms crossed over her heart, promising to introduce herself to a stranger.

And I understood, finally, completely, what the god could not understand, because it had been chained in the dark for four hundred years and hunger had eaten its understanding away:

Everything it offered me was a cage. A beautiful one. A gilded one, wide as a kingdom. But the Hollow had taught me the difference between a home and a cage, and the difference was never the size of the walls. It was whether anyone inside was held there against their will—including me.

"You're starving," I said aloud. My voice sounded very small in the vastness, and very steady. "I know. I know that's all this is. You're not cruel—you're *empty*, and they chained you instead of feeding you, and everyone in this story has been wrong except maybe the ones who drowned." I lifted Isolde's shard, and it glowed faintly against my palm, remembering the shape of the walls. "But I won't take my past back and keep it like a hoard. I won't win your war, because your war has no winning—only sides, and I've seen what sides build. And I won't wear the crown."

The attention went very still.

***THEN WHAT, LITTLE SWORD?*** Something moved in the deep of it—hunger, or hope, or both wearing each other's faces. ***WHAT IS LEFT?***

What was left. Aldous's last lesson, dying on the shore, his blood cooling between us: *the oath can be redirected. A swordspeaker can give memories instead of power. Feed it a life instead of a leash.* He had told me expecting nothing. He had told me the way you leave a lamp lit in a window for someone who may never come home.

What was left was the one currency I had that the god had never been freely given.

"Not a crown," I said. "A life. Mine. Freely. Not chained down here—*carried*. Remembered by something that chooses to remember." I stepped forward, to the very lip of the threshold, and the dark leaned toward me like a listener. "You wanted to remember us. So remember *me*. All of me. Everything I am about to give you. And in exchange—"

Behind me, far above, faint as a bell through fathoms of held water, I heard Sorrel scream my name.

"—in exchange, you let them keep theirs."

The god was silent for the space of three heartbeats. Then, softer than it had spoken yet, almost wondering:

***NO ONE HAS EVER OFFERED. THEY ONLY EVER BUILT WALLS.***

"I know," I said. "I was raised inside one."

I stepped through the doorway, and the dark closed over me like water, and the last thing I heard from the world above was Isolde shouting my name—and the first thing I heard from the world below was my own childhood, all of it, waiting for me like a table set for supper.
