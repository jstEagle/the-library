## 20. The Man Who Woke Up

Isolde saw him first, because of course she did. She had not left the shore since the lake rose. Three days and two nights she had sat on the cold shingle with her knees drawn up to her chest, watching the water settle back into its old shape as though it had never dropped ten feet, never bared its drowned streets, never shown her the vast pale shape at the bottom that had looked at her—not with eyes, exactly, but with the whole of its attention—and *let her go*.

The others had gone home to sleep, to grieve, to bury what needed burying. Bramble had tried to make Isolde come too, had put a blanket around her shoulders and said, gently, "He gave everything so you could rest. Rest." But Isolde had shaken her head, and Bramble, who understood grief better than anyone in the Hollow, had not pressed.

So Isolde was there when the water changed.

It was dawn, or near enough—the fog thinning to gauze, the eastern ridgeline going from black to bruise to a thin gold line. The lake had been still all night, stiller than she'd ever known it, and then a ring spread out from the deep channel where the god's heart lay, wide and slow, like something exhaling after holding its breath for an age.

Then his hand broke the surface.

She was in the water before she knew she'd moved, boots and coat and all, the cold punching the breath out of her. He floated face-up, ten feet from shore, drifting on the new-calm water like a man asleep in a meadow. His hair fanned out dark around him. His eyes were closed. The oath-sword was gone from his belt—she would learn later what that meant, what the deep had done with it—and his hands, those hands that had spoken words that cracked ward-stones and dropped lakes, lay open and empty at his sides.

"Zephyrine," she said, and then, stupidly, louder, as if volume could reach him across whatever distance he'd traveled: "*Zephyrine.*"

His eyes opened.

She had prepared herself for many things in three days of watching the water. She had prepared for him not to surface at all. She had prepared for him to surface changed—eyes silvered, voice full of command, the corruption having kept some purchase even in defeat. She had prepared for a body, for the thing every part of her refused to name.

She had not prepared for him to look at her the way he looked at her now: politely, warily, the way a man looks at a stranger standing over him in waist-deep water at dawn.

"Easy," she said, when he flinched from her grip. "Easy. I've got you."

He let her haul him toward the shallows, mostly because he seemed too weak to resist, and she catalogued the wrongness as they went: he swam badly. Zephyrine had grown up on this lake; he swam like breathing. This man swam like someone being taught by his own limbs in real time. And when his feet found the shingle and he stood, swaying, dripping, he looked around at the shore—at the ferry inn's scorched skeleton, at the ward-stone's shattered stump, at the gray-robed descendants kneeling bound on the far bank under Fen's watchful crossbow—and his face held no recognition at all. Not grief. Not relief. Just the blank, careful attention of a man taking inventory of a place he has never been.

"I know you," he said finally. His voice was hoarse, silt-roughened. "Don't I? You're—" He stopped. She watched him reach for something and find nothing there, watched the small vertigo of it move through him like a current under ice. "I don't know your name."

"No," Isolde said. "You wouldn't."

He looked down at his own hands. Turned them over. Flexed them, once, twice, as if checking whether they belonged to him. Then he looked up at her again, and there was something underneath the wariness—not fear, exactly. A kind of dawning, terrible calm.

"There's nothing," he said. "There's nothing *in* here. I remember how to talk. I remember how to swim—or almost. I remember—" he gestured vaguely at the sky, the water, "—things. Words for things. But I don't remember *me*. Did I—" His throat worked. "Did I drown?"

"You gave yourself to the lake," Isolde said. "All of you. Every memory you had. It was the only way to feed it enough to make it stop pulling everyone else's."

He absorbed this the way a man absorbs a blow—slowly, in stages, waiting for the full weight of it to land. "I don't feel sad about that," he said at last, and sounded troubled by it. "Shouldn't I be sad?"

"You can't mourn what you can't remember losing."

"That's not true," he said quietly. "I think I'm mourning it right now. I just don't know what for."

Isolde's chest did something complicated and painful. She had rehearsed this moment for three days on the cold shingle, and in none of her rehearsals had he been *articulate about it*. In her rehearsals he had been frightened, or vacant, or weeping, and she had known what to do with all of those. She did not know what to do with a man who woke up already understanding the shape of his own absence.

Behind her, gravel crunched. She turned.

Bramble stood at the top of the shingle with Fen and Sorrel behind her, and Bramble's face—Bramble, who had carried the inn's grief and the twins' terror and her own burned hands without complaint—Bramble's face crumpled and reassembled itself three times before she got it under control. Her forearms were still bandaged from the fire. She came down the bank slowly, the way you approach a horse that has spooked, and stopped an arm's length away.

"Hello, love," she said.

Zephyrine blinked at her. "I'm sorry," he said. "I don't—"

"I know." Bramble's voice held steady, which cost her; Isolde could see it costing her. "I know you don't. That's all right. Knowing isn't the only way of loving someone." She held out her bandaged hands, palms up, an offering. "I'm Bramble. I kept the ferry inn. You hauled my barrels and argued with me about cardamom and taught Fen to skip stones off the north bend. You ate at my table most nights for eleven years."

"Eleven years," he repeated. He looked at her burned arms, and something moved through his face—not memory, but the ghost of the reflex memory leaves behind, the way a field remembers a harvest. "Your arms. Was that—was that because of me?"

"The descendants burned the inn to flush you out," Sorrel said from the bank. She was fourteen and furious about everything lately, and her voice cracked on the last word. "You didn't do it. They did. And then you fixed it. You fixed *everything*, and it cost—" She stopped. Fen put a hand on her shoulder, and she shook it off, and then, characteristically, put it back herself.

"It cost me," Zephyrine said. Not a question. He touched his own temple, lightly, like a man probing a missing tooth. "I can tell it cost me, even if I can't count what's gone. There's a—I don't know. An ache with no edges."

"Yes," Isolde said. "That's exactly what it is."

Fen came down next. He was fifteen and had spent the past week trying very hard to be a man about things, and he failed at it now, which was better. He walked right up to Zephyrine, close enough that they had to tilt their heads to keep eye contact—Zephyrine was taller—and stared at him with red-rimmed eyes.

"Do you know me?" Fen demanded.

"No."

"Do you know you taught me to skip stones? Off the north bend? Flat ones, gray ones, you said the gray ones fly best?"

"No."

Fen's jaw worked. "Well," he said, thickly, "you did. So." He thrust out his hand, formal as a treaty. "I'm Fen. Pleased to meet you. Again."

And Zephyrine—this stranger with Zephyrine's face and Zephyrine's scarred knuckles and none of Zephyrine's history—took the boy's hand and shook it gravely, and said, "Pleased to meet you. Again," and Fen laughed wetly and turned away fast to hide his face, and Sorrel grabbed Zephyrine's other hand and said, "Sorrel. I'm the smart one," and Bramble made a sound that was half sob and half laugh and pulled all of them into her arms at once, bandaged hands and all.

Isolde stood apart and watched, and let herself feel it fully for the first time: the grief of it, braided tight with something that was not quite hope but lived next door to hope and borrowed its light. Because this was what he had bought them. All of them. Bramble's daughter's face, which the god had begun to pull from her before the Deeping—Bramble had told Isolde, weeping, that she was losing it, that she could see her girl only in pieces now—and the twins' knowledge of each other, and the valley's whole bright store of itself. He had paid it down with his own past, freely given, and the god had settled, fed, gentle, and the stars had come back.

And now he stood in their circle, held by people whose names meant nothing to him, and the circle held him anyway.

That was the thing Isolde kept coming back to, in the days that followed. *Held anyway.*

* * *

They took him up to the inn—or what remained of it. Half the structure still stood; the fire had taken the east wing and the ferry shed, but the great room's stone hearth survived, and Bramble declared, in the voice she used for ending arguments, that they would rebuild around it. For now they rigged canvas over the burned rafters and lit a fire in the old hearth, and it was strange and holy, that first night: the ruined room, the five of them, the man who was not a man they knew.

Zephyrine ate like a starving man and apologized between bites, and Bramble told him not to be ridiculous, and he asked questions. That was the hardest part and the easiest part both—he asked about himself the way you'd ask about a distant cousin. *Was I any good at cards? Did I have a trade besides the ferry? Was there anyone—* and here he glanced at Isolde, quickly, and away, and did not finish the question, and Isolde looked into the fire and said nothing, and the silence had a shape she felt in her teeth.

Later, when the twins had fallen asleep in front of the hearth and Bramble had gone to check the stores, he found Isolde mending a net by lamplight, because her hands needed work or they'd shake.

"You were going to say something earlier," he said. "At the shore. When I asked if I knew you. You said, 'you wouldn't.' Not 'no.' *'You wouldn't.'* Like it was my fault for forgetting."

Isolde set down the net. She had decided, on the third night of the shingle vigil, what she would do if he surfaced. She had decided it against every screaming instinct, against the ache that wanted nothing so much as to tell him everything—*your name is Zephyrine, you hum when you cook, you lied beautifully and badly, you dove the drowned village with me and held my hand underwater and neither of us mentioned it after*—all of it, poured back into him like water into a dry field.

But she had seen what memories did in that valley. She had seen Aldous feed the lake forty years of stolen recollection and call it guardianship. She had seen the god take and take, and she had heard Zephyrine, at the threshold of the deep, refuse the crown and the war and the past offered back to him—and she understood, finally, why he'd refused. Some gifts are cages with the lock on the inside.

"He gave me a choice," she said now. "Before he went down. Well—he didn't ask. But he showed me. What it would mean." She met his eyes. "If you want your past back, it exists. The god holds it. Everything—your childhood, your order, your oath, every day at this inn. It remembers your life for you now. That was the bargain. And I think—I think some of it could be given back, if you asked. If I asked for you."

He was very still. The lamplight moved on his face.

"And if I don't ask?"

"Then you stay as you are. New. Empty-handed. Free."

"Free," he repeated. He looked at his hands again, and this time he turned them palm-up and studied them a long moment, as if listening for something that wasn't there anymore. "There's silence in them," he said wonderingly. "I don't know why I expected noise. But I keep waiting for these hands to—do something. Say something. And they're just *hands*." He closed them. Opened them. "Is that what it cost? Whatever I was?"

"You were the last swordspeaker," Isolde said. "The gift is gone with the memories. Both went into the deep together. You'll never speak a word of power again. You'll never be leashed, either. No one will ever build a valley around you again."

He nodded slowly. Then: "Tell me about you. Not—who I was to you. I'm asking about *you*. Isolde." He said her name carefully, tasting it; Bramble must have given it to him. "You sat on that shore for three days. Bramble told me. Why?"

Because I love you, she thought. Because I dove a drowned village with you and recognized a fellow liar and burned my inheritance in front of my own bloodline for you, and you don't remember any of it, and the not-remembering should make it smaller and instead it makes it enormous, because now I get to choose it again, every day, with no debt and no history forcing my hand—

"Because you'd do the same for me," she said aloud. "I know it like I know my own name. Even empty, you'd sit three days on a shore for someone. It's not in the memories. It's in the man."

He considered that. Outside, the lake lapped soft against the shingle, and above the broken roof the stars were out—all of them, every one, burning steady for the first time in weeks.

"All right," he said at last. "Then start there. Start with who you are. As if—" he smiled, tentative, crooked, a smile she had never seen on him before because every smile she knew had history in it, and this one had none, and it undid her completely, "—as if it were the first time."

Isolde set down the net for good. She wiped her palms on her knees.

"My name is Isolde Vane," she said. "My family built the wards that caged you—which I didn't know until a month ago, and which I renounced in front of all of them, loudly, with fire involved. I ran away from home with a shard of the border ward in my boot and a wound that shouldn't have been possible, and I fell into your valley, and a ferryman fished me out of the reeds and lied to me about omens, badly." She paused. "You lied badly. I want that on record. You're going to be terrible at it now too, probably. Some things survive anything."

He laughed—a startled, real laugh—and pressed a hand to his sternum like the sound surprised him. "Noted," he said. "I'll practice."

"Don't," Isolde said. "Practice honesty instead. You'll be worse at it, but I'll like you more."

"Will you?" he asked. Simply. Without weight, without history, without the accumulated gravity of everything they'd been to each other—and precisely because of that, it landed harder than any loaded version could have. *Will you like me.* A man with nothing asking a woman with everything whether there might be room.

Isolde looked at him across the lamplight, in the ruined inn that smelled of smoke and river stone, with the twins asleep and the stars restored and the deep holding a whole human life like a cupped hand holds water.

"Yes," she said. "Starting now. Hello, Zephyrine Thackeray. My name is Isolde. I think we're going to be friends."

He reached out and shook her hand, formal as Fen's treaty, grave as a vow.

"Friends," he agreed. And then, tilting his head toward the dark window and the water beyond it: "Whoever I was—he must have trusted you completely. I can tell. I trust you completely, and I've known you ten minutes."

"That," Isolde said, and her voice caught only a little, "is the first true thing you've said all night."

* * *

He slept in the loft that night, in the bed that had always been his, and Isolde lay in the next room listening to him breathe and doing the arithmetic of loss. Eleven years of inn suppers. A childhood under Aldous's careful, terrible tenderness. An order dead a century. A name he'd have to learn like a word in a foreign language. All of it sunk in the deep, feeding something vast and starving so that a valley full of ordinary people could keep their ordinary memories—could keep Sorrel knowing Fen, Bramble knowing her lost daughter's face.

It was a monstrous price. It was a clean mercy. She suspected, with the certainty of someone who had watched him choose it, that he would make the same choice again with the memories in hand—and that was exactly why he could never have them back. A man who would pay that price twice deserved to be spared the second payment.

Near dawn she heard him stir overhead, and then his footsteps on the stairs, careful on the treads he must have climbed a thousand times. He came into the kitchen where she sat wrapped in a blanket, and he was holding something: a wooden bird, whittled smooth, a wren with its wings folded, worn soft at the edges by years of handling.

"It was in the mattress seam," he said. "I nearly missed it. Did I carve this?"

"You did. You carved one for each of us. Mine's a kestrel. Fen's a pike. Sorrel's a fox. Bramble's a goose—don't ask."

"Why a wren?"

Isolde smiled. "Because you said wrens sing like they're defending the whole valley single-handedly, and you liked the idea of being small and loud about it."

He turned the bird over in his fingers. Something crossed his face—not memory, but the warm shadow where memory used to live, the way a wall holds heat after the fire's out.

"I'd like to see the north bend," he said. "Where I taught Fen stones. And the drowned village—is it still down there? Could I see it? Not remember it. Just... meet it."

"Soon," Isolde said. "There's time now. That's the whole point of what you did. There's time."

He nodded and set the wren on the windowsill, facing the lake. And it was there, in the gray first light, that Fen came pounding down from the ridge trail with his boots unlaced and his face white, skidding into the kitchen with a bundle clutched to his chest.

"Ferry's back," he gasped. "The ferry—it came across the water by itself, no one poling, and it's sitting at our dock like it never burned—and Isolde, there's something lying in the bottom of it. Wrapped in oilcloth. It's got a *hilt*."
