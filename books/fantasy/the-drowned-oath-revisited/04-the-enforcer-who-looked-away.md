## 4. The Enforcer Who Looked Away

Sefa had broken forty-one doors in her career as a Ledgerkeeper enforcer, and she could tell you the sound each one made. Oak sang. Iron wept. The cheap pine of the tenements in Saltrow simply gave up, the way everything in Saltrow had learned to do.

Tonight she was going to break Bram Vasquez's door, and she found she already knew how it would sound: like an apology.

The order had come down from Master Corvane himself, delivered in that voice he used for delicate work—warm as a hearth, sharp as the poker left in it. *Retrieve the map. Silence the courier's contact. Do it quietly, Sefa. You've always been my quietest instrument.*

Her quietest instrument. She'd carried that phrase like a medal for eleven years. Tonight it sat in her chest like a swallowed hook.

She stood in the corridor outside Bram's narrow room in the guild hall's east wing, lantern lowered, and listened. That was the thing nobody understood about breaking doors: you only broke them when listening had failed. And through the door she could hear scratching—the small, frantic scratch of a pen, and beneath it, barely, humming. Bram Vasquez hummed when they worked. Off-key, tuneless, a habit so ingrained they likely didn't know they did it. Sefa had stood outside this door on other nights, delivering summons, collecting ledgers, and had catalogued the humming the way she catalogued everything: without comment.

Forty-one doors. She had never once stood outside one and felt like this—like the wood between her and the person inside was the only honest thing left in the building.

*You've enforced two purges,* said the voice in her head that sounded like Corvane but was, annoyingly, her own conscience. *Do you imagine the third will feel different?*

The first purge had been the Vellum Street families. Nine households who had tried to leave the guild's debt-books by leaving the city. She had been twenty-three and certain that order was the same thing as mercy. She still remembered the youngest one—a boy with ink-stained fingers who had begged not for his life but for his notebook, because it held his mother's recipes. Sefa had burned it. She had told herself the ash was necessary.

She had never told anyone what she kept: the smell of it, filed away in the ledger of her own skull, cross-referenced under *costs*.

The humming stopped.

Sefa knocked. Softly. Twice.

The scratching ceased entirely. A long silence, the kind Bram Vasquez specialized in—the silence of a person who had made invisibility into a discipline.

"It's me," Sefa said quietly. "It's Sefa."

Nothing. Then, muffled: "If this is about the courier, I don't know anything."

"You're a terrible liar, Bram. It's why I've always liked you." She tested the handle. Unlocked. Of course it was unlocked; Bram locked nothing, because Bram had nothing worth stealing and knew it. "I'm coming in. Don't hum at me."

* * *

The room was exactly what she expected: a cell of a place, one candle, shelves of borrowed books, and Bram Vasquez sitting very still behind a desk with their hands flat on its surface, as if stillness could make them smaller. On the desk, half-hidden under a inventory list, lay a corner of something that was neither paper nor parchment—something that gleamed faintly, like skin dried in saltwater.

They both looked at it. Neither touched it.

"Corvane sent you," Bram said.

"Corvane sent me to retrieve the map and silence the courier's contact." Sefa set her lantern down, deliberately, where its light couldn't reach the desk. "I'm bad at my job tonight. I've decided to be bad at it permanently."

Bram blinked. In eleven years, Sefa had seen them flinch at loud noises, go pale at blood, and once drop an entire tray of trade-goods when Master Corvane raised his voice—but she had never seen them look *surprised*. Surprise required believing the world might hand you something unearned.

"Why?" Bram asked. Simply. No performance of gratitude, no trembling. Just the question, laid out like a line item awaiting entry.

And there it was: the question she'd been avoiding since the corridor. Sefa sat down on the edge of Bram's cot, which creaked under her like a confession.

"Because eight years ago I burned a notebook," she said. "Because a boy on Vellum Street begged me for it and I burned it anyway, and last night I dreamed he was asking again, except in the dream it was you, and in the dream I burned *you*, and I woke up knowing that the only difference between those two things is timing." She rubbed her face with both hands. "Corvane is going to do to you what the guild does to everyone who learns too much. He'll call it bookkeeping. It isn't. It's just burning notebooks with extra steps."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. Then, in the tone they used for reading aloud figures that didn't add up: "You'll lose everything. Rank, quarters, pension. They'll hunt you. I'm the least valuable person in this hall—I've checked the ledgers; there's literally a column—and you're trading all of it for *me*?"

"Yes."

"That's terrible arithmetic."

"I know." Sefa smiled, and it hurt her face, because she realized she hadn't smiled in weeks. "First lesson of leaving the guild, Bram: some debts aren't meant to balance. Some you just pay."

Bram looked at her for a long moment. Then they slid the salt-stained map from under the inventory list, folded it with the careful economy of someone who had handled precious things their whole life without owning any, and tucked it inside their coat.

"The flooded quarter," Bram said. "There's a woman there. Ilyen. River-witch. If anyone can read what this is, it's her." A pause. "Also, I should mention I have absolutely no plan beyond that sentence."

"Good," said Sefa, standing and taking up her lantern. "Plans are how the guild catches people. We'll go the way water goes."

* * *

The undercity had been drowning slowly for two hundred years, and the guild had never paid to stop it, because the drowned streets made excellent graves for inconvenient records. Sefa led them down through the cistern gates, wading thigh-deep through water the color of old tea, past doorways that opened onto nothing but green dark.

Bram followed close, one hand on the wall, breathing in small controlled sips. "How deep—" they started.

"Don't ask questions in echo places," Sefa said. "Rule one of fleeing."

"What's rule two?"

"Don't flee with accountants."

"I'm a *bookkeeper*."

"That's what makes it funny."

Bram made a sound that might, in a better-lit life, have been a laugh. Sefa found she wanted to hear it properly, and filed that want away under a new heading she hadn't needed before tonight.

They were three turns from the witch's stairs when the bell began.

Not the guild's hour-bell—this was the alarm, the low bronze moan that every child in the capital learned to dread before they learned their letters. It rolled down through the stone and across the black water, and somewhere above them, boots began to hammer on stairs.

"They've noticed," Bram whispered.

"They've noticed that *I've* noticed," Sefa corrected. "Which is worse. Corvane doesn't send one enforcer after a defection. He sends enough to make a point." She pulled Bram into a doorway alcove as torchlight bloomed far down the passage—three lights, then four, reflections doubling them on the water until it looked like an army. "Stay behind me. Whatever happens, stay behind me and keep hold of that map."

"Sefa." Bram's voice was very small. "Those are your people."

"No," Sefa said, and drew her iron bar—not to break a door this time, but to hold one shut, which was a kind of poetry she suspected Bram would appreciate later, if there was a later. "My people are ahead of us. We just haven't met yet."

The torches came on. The water shivered. And from the dark stairwell ahead—out of the flooded quarter where a river-witch waited, unaware or perhaps entirely aware that her night was about to be ruined—came a light of a different color entirely: green, cold, patient, rising up out of the water itself like a question.

"Oh," said Bram faintly. "Is that supposed to happen?"

"No," said Sefa. "But I'm starting to think it's on our side."
