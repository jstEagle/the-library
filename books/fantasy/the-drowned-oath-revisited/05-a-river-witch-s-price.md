## 5. A River-Witch's Price

Ilyen had lived on the river long enough to know that everything worth having came with a price, and everything with a price could be haggled over. Except the river itself. The river only ever took.

She was mending a net that didn't need mending—nets were for thinking, and thinking was for avoiding the thing in her second drawer—when she heard them coming down the towpath: two sets of boots trying very hard to be quiet, and one set of boots that had given up entirely. That last set belonged to a big woman walking like an apology, which was unusual. Enforcers of the Ledgerkeepers' guild did not walk like apologies. They walked like debts come due.

Ilyen set down the net and watched them pick their way along the frozen margin of the Vell. The small one first—a narrow-shouldered creature wrapped in a coat three sizes too earnest, clutching a satchel like it was a sleeping child. Then the tall one, gray-cloaked, moving with the careful economy of someone who had broken many things and was tired of it. And behind them both, the big woman, whose walk Ilyen recognized now with a cold slide in her stomach: Sefa Vane. Chief enforcer. The woman who had once stood on the quay at Corvane's right hand while the guild burned the houseboats of everyone who'd tried to leave its ledgers.

Well. Everyone had to be somebody's monster before they got the chance to be anything else. Ilyen herself had been the river's monster for a decade, and look how well-adjusted she was.

"You can stop pretending to fish," she called out. "The ice is too thick. Even the fish are pretending."

The small one startled so badly they nearly went into the water, which Ilyen noted with professional approval was at least a natural instinct around her dock. The tall one put a hand on something under her cloak. The big one—the chief enforcer of the most feared guild in the kingdom—said, "Please. We need your help," and sounded like she meant it, which was somehow more alarming than a knife.

"Everyone needs my help," Ilyen said. "It's the help that costs."

* * *

She made them sit in her kitchen because her kitchen was warm and her patience was not, and because people told truer stories when their fingers were thawing around a hot cup. She ladled out nettle broth without being asked and watched the small one—Bram, the tall one said, in a voice that suggested the name mattered to her—wrap both hands around the bowl as if it were the last warm thing in the world.

Which, given the winter, it might have been.

"The map," Ilyen said. "Show me."

Bram looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at the window, where the frost had grown inward instead of outward, which even a hedge-witch could tell you was wrong. Frost was supposed to reach for the cold. This frost was reaching for the warmth. Somewhere south of here, something enormous was drinking.

Bram unrolled the map on the table, and Ilyen forgot about the broth, the frost, the guests, all of it.

Salt-stained, yes. But salt was only the sea's way of signing its work. Beneath the stain, drawn in ink that shifted like current when she wasn't looking directly at it, was the capital—not the capital above, with its spires and its processions and its king who hadn't been seen in public since midsummer, but the capital below. The drowned vaults. The old city the founders had sunk on purpose, back when sinking things was considered architecture. She knew those streets. Every witch did. You didn't grow up on the Vell without learning what lay beneath the weirs: the sunken chapel, the flooded archive, the vault where the first oaths were kept in jars like preserves.

And at the center of the map, drawn in ink that hurt to look at, a crown. Hollow. Drawn hollow, deliberately, the way you'd draw a wound.

"It's real," Bram said quietly. "You see it too."

"I see it." Ilyen touched the edge of the paper and pulled her finger back fast. The map bit. Not maliciously—it was simply full, the way a jar packed too tight is full. "This isn't a map of a place, little ledger-keeper. It's a map of a *binding*. Whoever drew this wasn't showing the way to the Crown. They were showing the way the Crown is held together. Every line is a stitch."

Sefa leaned forward. "Then whoever holds the map holds the stitches."

"No." Ilyen laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "That's the joke of it. Nobody holds the stitches. The map holds *you*. Look at him." She nodded at Bram. "He hasn't let go of that satchel since he walked in. Hasn't eaten. Hasn't slept, by the look of his eyes. The map's already got its hooks in. It does that. It finds the person least able to say no and makes itself their problem."

"I noticed," Bram said, with the flat dignity of someone who had indeed noticed and resented it.

Something in Ilyen's chest went soft and traitorous. She'd spent ten years keeping people off her dock precisely so her chest would stop doing things like that.

"There's more," she said, because there always was, and because if she didn't say it now she never would. "You want to know how to open the way. How to get into the vault. The map will show you—but it doesn't work for free. Nothing works for free. Not magic, not rivers, not guilds, though the guild would like you to believe otherwise." She turned the map toward the firelight. "See these blank spaces? They're not blank. They're *paid*. Someone paid to draw this. Someone paid in the only coin the old bindings accept."

"What coin?" Sefa asked, though her voice said she already dreaded the answer.

"A memory," Ilyen said. "A cherished one. Willingly given. The old bindings don't take blood or gold or years—they take the things you love best. That's why the doors stay shut. That's why nobody's walked into that vault in three hundred years. Because the toll is a piece of yourself, and most people, when it comes to it—" she spread her hands, "—most people choose to keep themselves whole and let the world freeze."

The kitchen was quiet. The fire popped. Outside, the wrong frost crept another inch across the window glass.

"How much?" Bram asked.

Ilyen looked at them—that thin, stubborn face, the hands still curled around the empty bowl—and hated the next sentence with her whole heart.

"For a binding this old? A crown this deep?" She shook her head. "Not one memory. All of a kind. The map takes a whole thread of you. Everything you remember of—one person, one place, one life. A single color, pulled out of the cloth. You wouldn't know what you lost. That's the cruelty of it. You'd just be lighter, and you'd never know why."

* * *

They argued about it, of course. People always argued, as if arguing changed the price.

Sefa wanted to find another way. There was no other way; Ilyen had spent twenty years looking for other ways, mostly for clients with worse problems and deeper pockets, and other ways were the river's favorite lie. Bram wanted to know if the map could choose which memories it took. It couldn't. It took what it was owed, and what it was owed was whatever the bearer loved most—love was the coin's weight, and light coins bounced off the door.

"And you?" Bram asked finally, turning those too-careful eyes on her. "Why are you telling us this? What do you want?"

Ah. There it was. The question Ilyen had been dodging since she'd invited them into her kitchen, which she had done, notably, without deciding to.

"I want the winter to end," she said. "My river is dying under that ice. The fish are gone. The reeds are dead to the root. Whatever sits on that throne is drinking the year itself, and I am the last witch on this water, and I am *cold*, Bram Vasquez, in a way that has nothing to do with weather." She stood, and her knees cracked, and she decided to be dignified about it. "Also, you're going to die without me. The vault doors answer to water-tongues. Old water-tongues. Mine."

"We haven't asked you to come," Sefa pointed out.

"You were working up to it. You have the faces of people working up to it. I'm saving us all some time." She began pulling supplies from her shelves: dried nettle, tallow candles, the good iron kettle, the jar of river-salt she'd sworn never to spend. Her hands moved on their own, the way they did when the river had already decided something and was merely waiting for her to catch up.

Bram watched her pack. "What will you pay?" they asked softly. "At the door. If it comes to it. What's your thread?"

Ilyen's hands stopped.

Her eyes went, before she could stop them, to the second drawer. The one she never opened. The one holding a lock of hair tied in faded blue ribbon, and a name she hadn't spoken aloud in eleven years, and the memory of a morning on this same river when the mist was gold and someone had laughed at her terrible singing and meant it kindly.

"That," she said, "is between me and the door."

She slammed the drawer shut inside herself, the way she had eleven years of practice doing, and kept packing.

* * *

They left at dusk, four shadows on white ice: a defected enforcer, a runaway clerk, an exiled princess waiting somewhere north with a claim that would get them all killed, and a river-witch who had just realized, with genuine horror, that she was doing the bravest stupid thing of her long cowardly life.

The map rode against Bram's chest, and Ilyen could feel it from three paces away, humming like a struck bell. Patient. Hungry. Counting what each of them loved.

And far to the south, beneath the frozen capital, something hollow turned its attention toward the north road—toward the small stubborn keeper of impossible books—and began, with the slow delight of a creditor long unpaid, to wait.
