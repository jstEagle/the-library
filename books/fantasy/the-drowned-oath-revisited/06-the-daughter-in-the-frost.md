## 6. The Daughter in the Frost

The borderlands did not so much have winter as *be* winter. Three days north of the last living orchard, the world had gone white and silent in a way that felt less like weather and more like a held breath. The trees stood bare and glassy, their branches sheathed in ice that caught the pale light and threw it back in thin, wounded colors.

"Cheerful place," Sefa said, stamping her boots against the frozen road. "Reminds me of home."

"You're from the southern coast," Ilyen said.

"I said what I said."

Bram pulled their coat tighter and kept walking. In the guild hall they had catalogued a bottled lullaby once—a mother's voice, captured mid-hum, sold to a sleepless duke for the price of a vineyard—and Bram had thought then that they understood what cold was: the absence of warmth, a thing you could price. But this was different. This cold had intent. It pressed down on them like an auditor who suspected everyone of something.

The map, folded inside Bram's shirt against their skin, had grown strangely warm since they'd crossed into the borderlands. It tugged faintly at their ribs, like a cat deciding whether to trust you.

"It knows we're close," Ilyen murmured. She walked with her staff slung across her shoulders like a yoke, her breath curling out in ribbons. "Maps are made of wanting, little ledger-keeper. This one wants very badly."

"I wish it would want somewhere warmer."

"Warmth is rather the point of our errand, isn't it?"

They found the first sign of Averil's exile on the second morning: a village, or the bones of one. Doors nailed shut from the outside. A well frozen solid to its lip, which Ilyen said was impossible, and then looked at for a long time without saying anything else. And everywhere, pressed into the frost on every window and door, small handprints—melted through the ice as if someone had touched each surface with a burning palm, though nothing here had burned.

"People left in a hurry," Sefa said quietly. She was doing the thing she did when old guilt surfaced: standing very straight, hands loose at her sides, ready to be blamed. "Or were made to."

"The exiled princess is supposed to be out here," Bram said. "Somewhere past the last villages. The courier's notes mentioned—" They stopped. The courier's notes lived in Bram's memory now, and speaking them aloud still felt like handling someone else's teeth. "A woman who trades bread for stories. Who pays too much for both."

Ilyen smiled. "Then we follow the smell of overpayment."

* * *

They followed it to a shrine.

It had been a river-shrine once—Ilyen identified the drowned stonework with a homesick sound—but the river had frozen to its bed, and the shrine now stood in a valley of white silence like a tooth in a jaw. Someone had rebuilt its broken roof with ship timber. Smoke rose from a chimney in a thin, defiant line. And strung along the eaves, where a pious soul might hang bells or charms, hung dozens of small clay tablets, each stamped with a name.

"Averil," Sefa breathed.

"You know her?" Bram asked.

"I arrested her." Sefa's jaw worked. "Officially, I escorted her to the border. Unofficially—" She stopped, started again. "She was nineteen. She stood up in the throne room during the Oath Renewal, in front of every lord and the whole court, and said the king wasn't her father. Said the thing wearing his face had been wearing it for years. They called it grief-madness. Her mother had just died; it was convenient to call it that." Sefa looked at the smoke rising from the shrine. "I put her in the exile cart myself. She thanked me. That's the part I've never managed to forgive."

"Why would she thank you?"

"Because I was the only person in that entire hall who looked at her like she might be telling the truth."

Before Bram could answer, a voice came from the shrine doorway—dry, amused, and carrying the particular authority of someone who has been alone long enough to stop performing for anyone.

"Well," said Princess Averil. "The Ledgerkeepers finally sent someone. I'd begun to feel neglected."

* * *

She was not what Bram expected, which was perhaps foolish, since Bram hadn't known what to expect. Exile had made her broad-shouldered and brown as a nut; her hair was cropped short for practicality and her hands were a working woman's hands, scarred at the knuckles. She wore a patched fisherman's coat over what was unmistakably, absurdly, a royal sash, worn as a belt. When Bram stared at it, she shrugged.

"It's the only thing I took," she said. "Seemed rude to leave it behind after they went to all the trouble of embroidering it."

Inside the shrine it was warm—the first true warmth any of them had felt in days—and it took Bram a moment to understand why. The little stove burned ordinary wood, but around the walls, set into niches like votive candles, glowed dozens of small stones, each one radiating heat like a sleeping animal.

"Heartstones," Ilyen whispered. She crossed the room slowly, reverent. "River-stones that have held a hearth-fire for a hundred years. There aren't a dozen of these left in the world. Where did you—"

"I trade for them." Averil poured tea from a dented kettle into four cups, counting them out with the ease of a hostess who had decided, some winters ago, that ceremony was a tool and she could pick it up or put it down as she liked. "Refugees come through. Everyone fleeing south carries one thing they can't bear to lose. Sometimes that thing is a heartstone, and sometimes they need bread more than they need a hundred-year fire. I pay fairly." Her eyes flicked to Bram, sharp and gray. "And in exchange for the bread, they tell me what they saw. What the roads look like. What the capital looks like. So. You've come from the capital, or near enough. Tell me about my father."

Bram set down their cup. "Your Highness—"

"Don't." The word cracked like ice underfoot. "Don't do the title. Out here I'm the madwoman who bakes bread. Say what you came to say."

So Bram said it. About the courier dying on the guild's back step with salt on his lips and a map in his hands. About the razored pages in the oldest ledger, the trade made with a king centuries ago. About the Hollow Crown binding a dead oath to a living face. About the winters being traded away—one by one, ledgered and sold, the kingdom's warmth flowing out through some ancient bargain while the thing on the throne grew stronger with every frost.

Averil listened without moving. When Bram finished, she was quiet for a long moment, and then she laughed—not bitterly, but with a strange, exhausted relief, like a woman setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy.

"Seven years," she said. "Seven years I've been telling people, and the best response I got before today was a physician with very soothing eyebrows." She looked into her tea. "He stopped being my father when I was fourteen. I can't tell you the day. That's the horror of it—it wasn't a death, there was no body, no funeral. He simply... became less. Less warm. Less *present*. Like a man reading a letter he finds more interesting than his own daughter. And by the time I understood what I was seeing, everyone else had adjusted to him. People adjust. That's the most terrible thing about people." She looked up. "You say the Crown binds a dead king's oath to the living monarch. Then the thing on the throne is my father the way a glove is a hand. Something else is inside it, using him."

"Yes," Bram said gently.

"Then we go to the vault and we break it." She said it the way she might have said *then we bake the bread*, and Bram understood suddenly why refugees trusted her: she made enormous things sound survivable.

"Not so fast," Sefa said. "Breaking the Crown breaks the oath. The oath is the only thing holding the succession together. If it shatters, every claimant, every border lord, every general with an army—"

"Better honest war than a stolen kingdom."

"Easy to say in a warm shrine! There are millions of people between here and that throne, and most of them will die in whatever comes after, and none of them will ever know your name except as the girl who broke the world!"

"And what do they call me now?" Averil was on her feet. "The madwoman? At least if I break it, the madness will be *true*."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly—" 

"Please," Bram said, and something in their voice must have been odd, because both women stopped. Bram realized their hands were shaking. Not from fear. From recognition. "I'm sorry. It's only—you're arguing about whether to do your duty or save yourselves, and I've spent my whole life watching people make that exact argument, and it always ends the same way. The ones who choose duty die certain, and the ones who choose survival live ashamed, and nobody wins." They took a breath. "There's a third option. It's worse. But it exists."

Averil sat back down slowly. "Say it."

"We don't break the Crown and we don't leave it. We *unbind* it. The oath, the dead king, the bargain—all of it, unwritten. Not shattered like a thief breaking a lock. Released like a debt forgiven. The map leads to the vault. The witch says the map opens for a price. If we do this right, no war, no succession crisis. Just an empty throne and a very confused kingdom, which is how most kingdoms spend most of their history anyway."

Silence. Ilyen made a small approving noise into her tea.

"And the price," Averil said. "What price?"

Bram looked at Ilyen, who looked at the fire, who was being no help whatsoever.

"A memory," Bram said. "One you love. Given up willingly."

Averil was quiet. Outside, wind moved through the frozen valley with a sound like pages turning.

"My father teaching me to sail," she said at last, half to herself. "That's the one I'd lose. I've already decided. I decided seven years ago, actually—I just didn't know what I was deciding." She looked up, and her gray eyes had gone steady and bright. "Well, ledger-keeper? You've come all this way through my winter to find me. Was I what you expected?"

"No," Bram admitted. "I expected someone tragic."

"I'm tragic *and* excellent at bread. Exile is educational." She stood, and began wrapping the heartstones in cloth, one by one, with the tenderness of a woman packing away her whole life—which, Bram realized, she was. "We leave at dawn. The vault gates are beneath the capital, and the capital is a month's walk, and the winter is getting worse. I can feel it getting worse. Can't you?"

Bram could. Since dawn, the cold had changed key—deeper, more deliberate, like something on the throne had noticed them at last and turned its attention south.

And it was Averil, folding the last heartstone into her pack, who said the thing that turned Bram's blood to ice water:

"One more thing you should know, since we're being honest with each other. Two nights ago, a man came through the pass asking after a courier's map. Rich coat, silver tongue, paid for information in *memories*—the refugees remembered things afterward that never happened to them." She cinched the pack closed and met Bram's eyes. "He said his name was Corvane. And he said he was coming to collect what the guild was owed."
