## 7. Corvane's Bargain

Master Corvane had not slept in a winter and a half, which was, by his own accounting, a small price for what he intended to buy.

He sat in the upper study of the Ledgerkeepers' hall with the shutters closed against the cold that pressed at the glass like a beggar who would not be refused. Frost climbed the panes in patterns he recognized—patterns he had helped to draw, centuries ago, in a vault beneath a capital that had since learned to drown. He poured himself tea that had gone lukewarm and drank it anyway. Waste offended him. Waste of warmth most of all, now that warmth had become currency.

On the desk before him lay three objects: a ledger bound in eel-skin, older than the guild itself; a letter knife made from the rib of something that had once sung; and a single page, razored long ago from the guild's oldest book, which he kept in his breast pocket the way other men kept portraits of their mothers.

The page concerned a king. The king concerned an oath. The oath concerned Corvane personally, in the way that a noose concerns a neck.

And somewhere out in the frozen sprawl of the city—or beyond it, past the borderlands where even the crows had stopped flying—his youngest clerk was carrying a map that could undo all of it.

"Bram Vasquez," Corvane said aloud, tasting the name. "The boy who dusts the shelves."

He almost smiled. There was a genuine fondness there, which was the trouble with him and always had been. Corvane loved people the way a collector loved porcelain: tenderly, attentively, and with a clear understanding of what they would fetch on the open market.

* * *

He had watched Bram for eleven years, which was longer than most parents watched anything.

It had been Corvane's decision to keep the boy. The courier who delivered Bram to the guild's door—a transaction recorded, naturally, in the eel-skin ledger—had been paid handsomely and asked no questions, because the payment included forgetting how to ask them. The child had arrived small and silent and watchful, with eyes like someone reading a room for exits, and Corvane had thought: *there*. That is the making of a Ledgerkeeper. Not power—the boy had almost none, barely enough to light a candle without swearing at it—but attention. Attention was rarer than magic. Magic could be bought. Corvane bought it weekly.

So Bram had been given ledgers to keep and secrets to shelve, and had grown into the guild's quietest corner like moss into stone. Essential. Invisible. Perfectly safe.

Or so the design went. Corvane reviewed the design now, in the lamplight, and admitted its flaw: he had built a cage so comfortable that he'd forgotten cages were meant to hold things that wanted out. A courier dies at your back door pressing contraband cartography into your clerk's hands, and the clerk does not report it. The clerk hides the map inside a folio of shipping manifests, which was clever, and then fails to destroy it, which was sentiment. Cleverness Corvane could use. Sentiment was a leak in the hull.

Sefa's defection had stung more than he showed. His chief enforcer, seventeen years of unblemished service, gone down through the flooded undercity with the traitor-clerk at her heels—and Ilyen of the river waiting for them with her lanterns and her prices. Corvane had read that arrangement before it happened. He read everything before it happened; it was the occupational hazard of being very old.

What he had not read—what genuinely surprised him, and surprise was rarer to Corvane than honesty—was the princess. Averil, found alive in the frost country, walking willingly beside a bookkeeper and a broken enforcer. The girl was supposed to have died of exile years ago. Instead she was marching toward the vault with her father's name in her mouth like a drawn knife.

Which meant the game was no longer about a map.

It was about an heir.

* * *

"You're brooding," said Vess, from the doorway.

Corvane did not startle. Startling was for people whose hearts still accelerated. "I'm strategizing. Brooding is for men with fewer resources."

Vess, his second, crossed the room and stood at the window with her arms folded, silhouetted against the white glare. She had been with him sixty years and had never once asked what he was. He valued this the way sailors value ballast.

"The enforcers lost them at the Weir," she said. "Ilyen drowned two of ours in three feet of water. Don't ask me how."

"I never ask you how. It's our whole marriage."

"Ha." But her mouth stayed flat. "The roads are closing, Corvane. The real ones. The frost took the southern pass last night. Refugees are coming up from the river towns in numbers we can't feed, and the guild's stores are pledged to buyers who pay in summers-that-might-still-come." She turned. "Whatever you're planning to do about the boy, do it soon. Winter isn't waiting for your paperwork."

"Winter is my paperwork," Corvane said mildly, and enjoyed, as he always did, the flicker of unease that crossed her face. Even after sixty years, Vess preferred not to know precisely what her master was. Ignorance, in the guild, was a benefit package.

He rose and went to the tall cabinet behind his desk, the one Bram had dusted every Thursday for eleven years without ever being permitted to open it. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the thing he had carried since the century when carrying such things was fashionable: a key of black iron, unremarkable except that it opened one door in the world, and that door was beneath the capital, and behind it sat a crown on a dead man's brow, keeping a bargain alive that should have rotted with its signatories.

The thing wearing the king's face had begun, lately, to spend recklessly. Winters sold wholesale. Warmth traded for dominion over the cold. And every coin minted from that trade passed, eventually, through Corvane's hands—which was profitable, yes, but also precarious, because debts of that size are only ever collected one way, and Corvane had seen the collection notice coming for a hundred years. The Crown would consume its brokers last, but it would consume them.

Unless someone shattered it first. Unless a bookkeeper with almost no magic and an heir with every reason to hate it reached the vault ahead of schedule.

Corvane had decided, in the small hours, that he would help them.

Not out of goodness. Goodness was a currency he'd never managed to counterfeit convincingly. Out of arithmetic. If the Crown fell, Corvane needed to be standing next to the person who felled it, holding the receipts, positioned to inherit whatever order grew in the ruin. And if it did not fall—if the thing in the king's face won—then Corvane needed Bram's map surrendered quietly, before the vault opened and the old broker's name came due.

Either way, the path ran through a dusty clerk who liked tea and catalogued lullabies.

"Prepare the grey room," Corvane said. "Two chairs. Good fire. The decent biscuits, not the sawdust ones we give the tax collectors."

Vess raised an eyebrow. "You're inviting him here?"

"I'm inviting him home. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole negotiation." He tucked the black key into his coat. "Send word through the river channels. Ilyen will carry it; she owes me a favor from before her hair went green. Tell them there's a place where no enforcer can follow, and that I offer parley. Bram will come. Curiosity is the one debt that boy has never managed to service."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then I'll know the guild taught him nothing at all," Corvane said, "and I'll grieve, briefly, and then I'll do it the expensive way."

* * *

They came at dusk, three days later, and Corvane watched from the study window as they crossed the frozen canal toward the hall's side entrance: Sefa first, moving like a woman expecting the ice to betray her; then Ilyen, green-haired and luminous, leaving footprints that steamed; then Averil, wrapped in a borrowed cloak, chin up, every inch the daughter of a palace she'd been thrown out of; and last, small and hunched against the cold, a figure Corvane had watched for eleven years, clutching a satchel to his chest like a shield made of paper.

Bram looked up at the window. Even at this distance, even through frost-thick glass, Corvane felt the look land—that flat, careful, inventorying stare, the stare of a person counting exits. Eleven years of Thursdays, and the boy had learned to count.

Good, Corvane thought. You'll need it for what I'm about to offer you. And for what I'm about to take away.

In the grey room, the fire burned properly and the biscuits were the decent ones. Corvane received them standing, which for him was ceremony enough, and gestured them all into chairs before the fire. Averil refused hers and stood behind Bram's shoulder, arms crossed, radiating the particular hostility of royalty forced to negotiate in a room with carpet.

"Master Corvane," Sefa said. Her hand had not left her sword-hilt since the door. "This is either surrender or a trap."

"It's a trade," Corvane said pleasantly. "Everything is. Sit, Sefa. You know I don't trap people I respect; it's inefficient and it wrinkles the relationship." He let his gaze travel around the room and settle, at last, where it had always been going to settle. "Bram. Thank you for coming."

"You knew I would," Bram said. Their voice was steady. Their knuckles, on the satchel strap, were not.

"I knew you'd want to see what I keep in the cabinet you've dusted for a decade. People always want to open the drawer they've polished longest." Corvane lowered himself into the opposite chair and warmed his hands at the fire, unhurried, letting the silence do some of the work. Silence was a tool he maintained better than any blade. "I'm going to tell you a story. Then I'm going to make you rich. Then you're going to refuse me, and I'm going to tell you why that breaks my heart, and then—" he smiled, thin and genuine, "—we'll see."

"Get on with it," said Averil.

"Your highness. Still all edges. Your father used to be the same, before—" He caught himself, savored the catch. "Ah. But that's part of the story."

He told it plainly, because plainness disarms. How, four hundred years ago, a young king had come to a younger broker in terror of dying with his oaths unkept—an oath to protect his people, sworn on a crown hollowed for the purpose, binding his deathless will to whoever wore his face after him. How the broker had arranged it, for a fee, and taken as surety a page from his own ledger. How the bargain had held through plague and succession and the slow drowning of the vault beneath the capital, the broker growing old without aging, collecting interest, until the thing wearing the king's face began spending winters instead of gold.

"The guild didn't stumble into this," Corvane said. "The guild *is* this. Every impossible trade we've ever brokered flows downstream from that one contract. I am the Crown's oldest creditor, Bram. And I am calling the loan."

Ilyen spoke for the first time, soft as water finding a crack. "And the boy? What does the boy have to do with your old arithmetic?"

Corvane turned to Bram, and here he let the fondness show, because it was true, and truth was the sharpest thing he owned.

"Here is my offer. Bring me the map. Help me reach the vault first—not to save the Crown, but to renegotiate it, on terms where everyone in this room lives, including your father, your highness, in whatever shape remains to him. In exchange: full partnership. A seat at the table. Your name in the front of the ledger instead of the back. No more dusting. No more invisibility." He leaned forward. "And absolution, Bram. Whatever you think you owe the guild for eleven years of shelter—struck clean. Forgiven. That's not nothing. Debts are the only gods I've ever worshipped, and I'm offering to forgive yours."

The fire cracked. Sefa's jaw tightened. Averil had gone very still.

"And if I say no?" Bram asked quietly.

"Then I'll be sad," Corvane said, "and then I'll tell you the rest of the story, which is the part I've been saving." He drew the razored page from his breast pocket and laid it on the table between them, facing Bram, and watched the color leave the boy's face one shade at a time.

"Eleven years ago," Corvane said gently, "a frightened child was brought to this guild's door and purchased outright. Paid for, recorded, filed. Do you know what the purchase price was?" He tapped the page. "Your past. Your name, your family, your first snowfall, your mother's voice—all of it, sold by someone who couldn't afford to keep you, and bought by me, because I collect beginnings. You've spent eleven years feeling like a man missing a room in his own house. That ache isn't a flaw in you, Bram. It's a lien. And I hold the deed."

The silence in the grey room was total. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"So," Corvane said softly. "Partnership and forgiveness on one side. The story of who you were on the other. Or the map, and none of it. Choose."

Bram looked down at the page for a long moment. When they looked up, their eyes were wet and absolutely level, and Corvane—who had read kings, read monsters, read the slow betrayal in his own reflection—found, to his genuine astonishment, that he could not read them at all.

"No," Bram said.

And outside, far to the north, the sky went the color of a blade, and the winter leaned down upon the city like something that had just heard its name.
