## 8. The Long Winter Falls

Sefa had broken men before. She had never broken a road.

The north highway out of Vellharrow had been a river of people when they arrived at dawn—carts stacked with hens and grandmothers, children asleep in grain sacks, a man carrying his front door on his back because, he explained to no one in particular, "the rest of the house can fend for itself." Now the road was a river of ice, glazed black where breath and spilled water had frozen in layers, and the refugees moved across it like figures on a music box: slow, careful, turning and turning without getting anywhere.

"Third cart wheel this morning," Bram said, crouched beside a splintered axle, hands blue to the wrist. "I've fixed two with belt leather and one with a prayer. The prayer held better."

"You're not supposed to be good with your hands," Sefa said. "You're supposed to be good with books."

"I contain multitudes. Mostly cold ones."

She almost smiled. That was the thing about Bram Vasquez that no one at the guild had ever bothered to notice—the ledger-keeper who made themselves small enough to slip through a room unnoticed was also the person who could make an entire refugee column feel, briefly, like it might survive the day. Sefa had spent eleven years as the guild's chief enforcer learning how fear moved through crowds. She had never once learned what hope looked like until she'd watched Bram talk a farmer out of abandoning his goats.

The goats were still with them. So were the farmer and forty-seven other souls who had simply decided, somewhere along the frozen miles, that the quiet person with the salt-stained map was worth following.

Sefa did not correct them. She only walked the column's edges with her axe loose in its sling and her eyes on the treeline, counting the things that followed them.

Because something did follow them. She could feel it the way old soldiers felt weather in their scars—a pressure behind her ribs, patient and professional. Enforcers. Her former colleagues. Possibly her former friends.

*Later,* she told herself, as she had told herself every hour for six days. *Grieve later. Walk now.*

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a collapsed tollhouse, and Ilyen built a fire that burned green and gave off no smoke, which was either impressive witchcraft or a very confident lie. Around it, the party of five—six if you counted the goats, which Averil stubbornly did—shared a pot of barley soup that tasted mostly of melted snow and optimism.

Averil sat apart, as she always did, wrapped in a fur that had once been royal and now smelled of woodsmoke and goat. She was sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen. It was, Sefa had learned, how the princess prayed.

"The capital's gates," Averil said, without looking up. "Tell me again what we're walking into."

"A drowned vault under a drowned city," Ilyen said cheerfully, ladling soup. "Guarded by whatever's left of your father, bound inside a crown that eats oaths. Also possibly several hundred enforcers, depending on how fast Corvane's hounds run." She handed Averil a bowl. "But the soup is hot, so really, mixed news."

"You find this funny," Averil said.

"I find everything funny eventually. It's a discipline. Like knife-sharpening." Ilyen's grin faded into something gentler. "You laugh or you freeze, princess. And you—" she pointed her spoon at Averil, then at Sefa, then at Bram, hunched over the map by firelight, "—and you, and especially you, are all far too close to freezing already."

Bram looked up. In the green light their face was young and tired and terribly honest. "I keep thinking about the memory," they said quietly. "The price. What if I give up something and don't even know what I've lost? What if I pay and pay and there's nothing left of me to walk out?"

"Then we'll remember for you," Sefa said.

It came out before she'd decided to say it. Bram stared at her. Averil stopped sharpening. Even the goats went quiet, which was unprecedented.

"That's not how it works," Bram said.

"It's exactly how it works," Ilyen said. "Memory isn't a coin, little keeper. It's a thread. Cut it here and it's still knotted somewhere else." She tapped her own temple, then Sefa's shoulder, then reached across the fire to tap Averil's knee, dodging the knife with practiced ease. "Four knots. One thread. You'll lose the guild from your head. You won't lose it from us."

Bram looked down at the map for a long moment. When they spoke again, their voice was rough. "That's the nicest thing anyone's ever threatened me with."

"Don't get used to it," Sefa said. "I'm mostly terrible."

"She is," Ilyen confirmed. "It took me three days to teach her to say thank you. We're still working on please."

And for one hour—one whole hour—the winter let them alone. Sefa sat with her back against the tollhouse wall and listened to Bram explain, with escalating exasperation, why the king's daughter could not name a goat *Chancellor*, and she thought: *this. This is what I defected for. Not the crown, not the cause. This stupid, warm, impossible little fire.*

Then the wind changed, and she tasted iron in it, and she knew the hour was over.

* * *

"They're here," she said, waking Bram with two fingers pressed to their shoulder—the way you woke someone you didn't want to startle, though Bram startled anyway, every time, bless them. "River side. Four, maybe five. They've been circling since dusk."

Bram blinked awake and, being Bram, asked the worst possible question: "Which four? Do I know them?"

"No." Yes. Dain would be among them; Dain always volunteered. "Doesn't matter. Get everyone to the ice. The river's our road now—it's frozen solid to the capital gates, and they can't flank us on open ice."

"They can shoot us on open ice," Averil said, already pulling on her boots.

"Yes," Sefa agreed. "So let's not stand around admiring the view."

The frozen river was a mile of silver moonlight, wide as a cathedral and just as echoing. The refugees went first, carts sliding and cursing, the door-carrying man bringing up the rear with his door. Sefa took the point with her axe drawn, and behind her came Averil with her unnecessary knife, and Bram with the map strapped against their chest like a second heartbeat, and last—because she refused to be anywhere else—Ilyen, barefoot on the ice, humming.

"Why are you barefoot?" Sefa hissed.

"The river talks through my feet. Shoes make it mumble." Ilyen wiggled her toes. "Also I lent my boots to a child with wet socks. Don't make it a whole thing."

The first arrow came out of the dark like a thrown needle.

Sefa heard it—she'd spent eleven years hearing arrows—and turned, and caught the second one on her raised shield instead of in Bram's back, which she considered a fair evening's work. Then the treeline bloomed with torchlight and the night stopped being a night and became a battle.

"Enforcers!" someone screamed, uselessly, because everyone could see the black-clad shapes flowing onto the ice, and everyone knew what enforcers were. Half the refugees froze. The other half ran. Both halves were wrong.

"Hold the line!" Sefa roared, in the voice the guild had trained into her, the voice she'd sworn off using. "Ice holds in the middle! Move! MOVE!"

They moved. Gods forgive her, they moved, because she had spent years teaching people to obey that voice and now she spent it saving them instead of sorting them. Dain came at her out of the torchlight—young Dain, who'd once cried about a horse—and she broke his sword arm and put him down on the ice, alive, breathing, and hated herself efficiently and completely.

"Bram!" Ilyen's voice cut through everything, high and strange. "The river wants to help!"

"Can it?" Bram shouted back.

"It wants payment!"

"We'll owe it!"

The river took the offer literally.

The ice beneath the enforcers' line groaned—a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat—and heaved. Black water breathed up through new cracks, and the pursuing shapes scattered, and Sefa understood in one cold flash what Ilyen was doing: holding the passage open. Keeping the ice true under the refugees' feet and false under the hunters'. Holding both truths at once, on bare feet, in killing cold, while arrows fell around her like iron rain.

One found her.

Sefa saw it happen the way you see terrible things: too clearly, too slowly. An arrow took Ilyen high in the side, and the witch didn't fall so much as *lean*, one hand pressed flat against the ice, keeping the river's bargain with her whole body, blood steaming off the frozen surface in a long red ribbon.

"Ilyen!" Bram was running toward her, and Averil was running toward Bram, and Sefa was running toward all of them, and the world narrowed to torchlight and screaming and the green fire of a witch refusing to die on schedule.

They got her to the far bank together. Averil carried her—carried her, the exiled princess with the unnecessary knife, arms shaking, jaw set like her father's portrait—and Bram pressed both hands over the wound and said useless, desperate bookish things, *pressure, keep the pressure, I read about this once—*

"Stop," Ilyen whispered. "Both of you. Listen." Her lips were gray. Her eyes were bright as coins. "The river's paid. Passage holds to the gates. I just have to... stay touching it. Silly of me. Should've negotiated boots into the contract."

"You're coming with us," Bram said. It wasn't a plea. It was a ledger entry. *Owed: one witch. Payable: immediately.*

"Mm." Ilyen smiled, and it was the smile of someone doing arithmetic they didn't like. "Maybe. Keep walking. I'm very stubborn. Ask the river."

So they kept walking. Sefa carried Ilyen after that—Averil's arms were done, and Sefa's were not, because guilt makes a strong pack-frame—and the column strung itself out along the silver mile toward the capital, and behind them the torches of the guild thinned and guttered and finally, blessedly, went out.

Dawn came up the color of pewter. And there, rising out of the frozen mist where the river met the city, stood the drowned gates: twin arches of black stone half-sunk in the ice, carved with kings whose faces the centuries had eaten away, water sheeting down their insides in ropes of silver. Beyond them, the capital lay silent under its endless winter, spires white as bone, chimneys cold.

No smoke. No bells. A whole city holding its breath.

Sefa set Ilyen down gently against the gate's cold stone and knelt beside her. The witch's breathing was shallow but steady, and her hand stayed pressed to the ice, and the ice, Sefa noticed, had gone soft near her fingers—water welling up warm, unfrozen, alive.

"You held it," Sefa said. "All of them. Every soul on that river."

"Somebody had to." Ilyen's eyes drifted closed. "Don't tell the river I said thank you. It gets smug."

Bram stood before the drowned gates with the map in their hands, and the salt stains on it had begun to glow, faint and gold, like embers under ash. The carved faces above the arch seemed to turn—just slightly—toward the small figure with the map, as if the dead kings had been waiting a very long time for someone foolish enough to come.

"The way opens at moonset," Bram said quietly. "That's when I pay."

Above them, in the white silence of the capital, something wearing a king's face turned its attention toward the gates—and the temperature dropped so fast the air itself cracked.
