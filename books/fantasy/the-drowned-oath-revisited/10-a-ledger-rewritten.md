## 10. A Ledger Rewritten

The thaw came to the capital the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and all at once.

Bram stood at the window of a rented room above a chandler's shop on Tallow Street and watched the icicles let go of the eaves one by one. They fell into the gutters with small bright sounds, like coins dropped into a collection plate. Below, a man was sweeping meltwater off his doorstep with the grim satisfaction of someone who had spent four months losing arguments to the weather and had finally won one.

Four months since the vault. Four months since Bram had spoken the unbinding with a mouth full of salt water and a head full of holes, and watched the Hollow Crown come apart in their hands like a promise that had finally been allowed to break.

They remembered none of it. That was the price, and prices were the one language Bram still spoke fluently. What they knew of those days came secondhand now, told around fires and over meals by people who had been there: Sefa describing the drowned gates swinging open; Ilyen, half-healed and furious about it, describing the king's last true self rising from the dark like smoke deciding to be a man again; Averil describing her father's face at the end—*empty, and then not empty, and then free*—in a voice she kept very level until she couldn't.

Bram listened to these stories the way other people listened to music. They recognized nothing. But something in their chest leaned toward them anyway, the way a plant leans toward a window.

*You gave up every memory of the guild,* Sefa had told them, the first night, when Bram woke coughing riverwater in a safehouse and asked, reasonably, who everyone was and why they kept looking at Bram like that. *That was the toll. The map only opened for someone willing to lose something they loved.*

*I loved it?* Bram had asked.

Sefa had considered this for a long moment. *You loved being needed,* she said finally. *You hated everything else.*

Which sounded, Bram thought now, watching the gutter-ice fall, exactly like something they would have done. It was strange, grieving a self you couldn't remember. Like mourning a house you'd lived in but never seen from outside. There was an ache where the memories should have been—not a wound, exactly. More like a room in a familiar building whose door simply wouldn't open anymore, no matter how politely you knocked.

The guildhall had dissolved the way fog dissolves: thoroughly, and without anyone quite able to say when it started. Bram had gone there three weeks ago, out of some instinct older than their memory, and found the great doors standing open on an empty hall. The ledgers were gone—carried off or burned, no one could say. The elders had scattered like startled pigeons. Someone had left a broom leaning against Master Corvane's empty chair, which struck Bram as either an act of profound disrespect or profound hope.

Corvane himself had gone to ground. Averil's agents—the beginnings of a real intelligence service, though she insisted on calling it "a few friends who owe me favors"—reported sightings in three cities and confirmations in none. He was a broker, Bram reminded themselves. Brokers did not die; they merely changed markets. Somewhere, someday, a door would open and Corvane would be standing behind it with an offer too sharp to refuse.

But that was someday. Today the icicles were falling, and the bread on the chandler's counter smelled of actual yeast instead of frost, and Bram had a ledger to begin.

It was a plain thing—a shopkeeper's daybook bought for two coppers from a stationer who tried to charge four and wilted under Ilyen's stare. Bram sat down at the little table by the window, uncapped the ink, and stared at the first blank page for a long time.

In the old hall, Bram had catalogued impossible trades. A bottled lullaby. A debt paid in shadows. A grandmother's recipe for forgetting, sold twice. Every entry precise, every entry anonymous, every entry essential and unseen. Bram had been very good at it. They suspected they still would be, if the trade existed anymore. But the trade was dead, and its books were scattered, and the person who had kept them was scattered too—a few pages here, a few pages there, most of them lost to the water.

So this ledger would be different. This one would record things that actually mattered.

Bram dipped the pen and wrote, in a hand that surprised them with its neatness:

*Ledger of the People Who Stayed.*

Then, beneath it, the first entries.

*Sefa Vane. Chief enforcer, formerly of the Ledgerkeepers' Guild. Stayed when staying meant arrest. Carries my stories because I cannot. Frighteningly good at cards; cheats, but only against people who deserve it, which is a moral position I respect. Owes me: one explanation of what I was like before. I owe her: apparently several apologies, contents unknown. She says I'll understand when she tells me. I look forward to it with dread.*

*Ilyen of the Graywater. River-witch. Stayed when staying meant bleeding. Held the frozen river open so we could reach the drowned gates, and has not once let anyone forget it. Currently recovering, currently insufferable about it, currently teaching me which mushrooms will kill me and which will merely ruin my week. Says the river remembers me fondly, whatever that means. I have decided to be flattered.*

*Averil. Princess, formerly exiled; heir, formerly reluctant; daughter, always. Stayed when staying meant claiming a father the whole kingdom had stopped believing in. Argues with me about duty approximately twice daily and loses gracefully roughly half the time. Is rebuilding a kingdom out of thawed ground and stubbornness. Asked me yesterday if I regretted the price. I said I didn't know what I'd paid. She said that was the bravest answer she'd ever heard, and then she cried, and then she threatened me if I ever told anyone she cried. Entered here under seal of the ledger.*

Bram sat back and read it over. It wasn't guild work. It was warm and imprecise and full of jokes, and any elder of the old hall would have struck it through in disgust.

Bram found they liked it enormously.

* * *

They came at dusk, the way they always did now, drawn by the smell of the chandler's fire and the unspoken agreement that Bram's room had the best table.

Sefa arrived first, as always, stamping snowmelt off her boots even though there was barely any left to stamp—it was habit now, a ritual of the winter she refused to stop performing. She carried a paper-wrapped bundle that turned out to be meat pies, still hot, from a stall whose owner owed her his life and repaid it in pastry.

"Status report," she said, dropping into a chair. "The harbor ice broke this morning. First ships in five months. Also, a fishmonger tried to shortchange a widow and I made him cry, so all in all, a good day."

"You made him cry?"

"I looked at him."

"Ah," said Bram, who had been on the receiving end of the Look exactly once and had required a sit-down afterward.

Ilyen arrived next, walking with a stick she claimed was temporary and everyone else privately suspected was permanent, wearing it with the swagger of a woman who had wrestled a frozen river and won on points. She dropped a pouch of dried herbs on the table. "For your tea. The kind that heals. Not the kind that kills—I know you can't tell the difference yet, and honestly, neither can I after the incident."

"There was no incident," Bram said.

"There was a small incident."

"There was a *slightly bitter tea*."

"And yet," Ilyen said serenely, "here we are, both alive, which proves nothing either way."

Averil came last, hood up, two guards trailing at a distance she pretended not to notice. She'd taken to moving through her own capital quietly, learning it street by street, listening to people who spoke freely because they didn't recognize her. She set down a satchel heavy with documents and looked, for a moment, like a girl carrying the weight of a country—which, Bram supposed, was exactly what she was.

"The last of the relief granaries are open," she reported, pulling off her gloves finger by finger. "Father's—" She paused. She still stumbled on that word sometimes, reaching for it and finding it changed shape in her mouth. "*The king's* old councilors are cooperating, mostly because the alternative is me. And we found something in the palace archives you should see. Tomorrow, maybe. It's about the guild."

Bram felt the old reflexive flinch—the ghost of a fear they no longer had the memories to justify—and set it aside. "Nothing urgent?"

"Nothing that can't wait for pie." Averil took one, and for a moment the heir to a thawing kingdom was just a tired young woman eating dinner with her friends, and her shoulders came down from around her ears for the first time all day.

They ate. Sefa recounted the fishmonger story in full, with gestures. Ilyen disputed several details on grounds of narrative fairness. Averil laughed—really laughed, the unguarded kind—and Bram sat among them feeling the strangest sensation, which was that they belonged somewhere, and knew it, and were not afraid of it.

Once, Bram would have called that impossible. Once, Bram had traded in impossible things and never once put their name in the book.

* * *

Later, when the others had gone—Sefa to her watch rounds, Ilyen to her barge, Averil to a palace that was slowly becoming a home again—Bram sat by the candle and opened the new ledger to a fresh page.

There was one more entry to make. They had been avoiding it for weeks, the way you avoid the last bite of something good.

They dipped the pen.

*Bram Vasquez. Ledgerkeeper, formerly of nothing worth remembering. Stayed—though technically, I am told, I was carried. Paid a price I cannot itemize and do not mourn, because you cannot mourn what you cannot find, and besides, the exchange rate was excellent: I lost a guild and gained a family, which any honest broker would call a profit.*

*Note to future reader (me): if you are reading this and wondering who these people are and why they keep looking at you like that—stay anyway. Whatever you were before, whoever you were, they stayed. That is the whole of the accounting. Everything else is commentary.*

Bram blotted the page, closed the book, and blew out the candle.

And in the dark, in the space between one breath and the next, something surfaced—brief and bright and gone again, like an icicle letting go of the eaves. Not a memory. Nothing so complete. Just a feeling, worn smooth by the water: the sense of a small figure in a vast hall, invisible, essential, waiting for someone to look up from the books and say *your name matters too.*

Someone had. Eventually. Bram was almost sure of it.

They smiled at the dark, went to bed, and slept without dreaming of winters at all.
