## 2. The Child at the Threshold

The storm came down off the Spine like something with a grudge.

Cyrus Almeida had weathered forty years of mountain storms and knew their moods the way she knew the moods of customs officers: which ones could be bribed with patience, which ones meant to take everything. This one meant to take everything. It arrived an hour past dusk on the third day after she'd buried her uncle, arriving not as rain first but as a pressure—a change in the weight of the air against the guildhall's old stones, a low note in her bad ear that she'd learned to read as warning.

She was in the kitchen when it hit, because the kitchen was the only room left that still felt like the guild and not like a tomb. She'd lit the great hearth—the one big enough to roast a whole boar, back when there were thirty mouths to feed and reasons to feast—and she sat before it with her uncle's clay pipe unlit in her hand, turning it over and over, because lighting it felt like an admission and not lighting it felt like a lie, and she couldn't decide which she could afford.

The wind found every voice the hall possessed. The guildhall of the Almeida line had been built into the shoulder of Thornfell itself, its back wall raw mountain stone sweating cold, its front face shuttered against the valley road. In high wind the building groaned through its whole length, doors rattling in frames that no carpenter alive could true, and somewhere above the third landing a loose slate clattered its one-note complaint. Cyrus knew all these voices. She catalogued them without thinking, the way her uncle had taught her to catalogue footsteps on gravel outside a warehouse door—*that one is nothing; that one is trouble.*

So when a new sound joined the chorus, she heard it the way you hear your own name spoken across a crowded market.

A knock.

Three blows, small and spaced wrong—not the fist-slam of a traveler begging shelter, but the careful, deliberate knocking of someone who had been taught that doors were dangerous things. *Knock. Knock. Knock.* Then silence, as if the knocker were listening to see whether the door might answer back.

Cyrus set down the pipe. She did not move for a long moment. The fire popped. Rain began in earnest then, arriving all at once like a dropped bucket, hammering the slate roof with a sound like applause from a very large and unfriendly audience.

"No one knocks on this door," she said aloud, to the fire, to the empty hall, to the dead. "No one has knocked on this door in nine years."

Smugglers did not knock. Smugglers scratched, or whistled the guild's three-note signal, or simply came in through the cellar because the cellar door had never once been locked in two hundred years—that was the point of it. Honest people knocked, and honest people had no business at the end of the Salt Road in a storm, four miles from the nearest village, at a hall that officially did not exist.

The knock came again. Three blows. Same small hands, same terrible patience.

Cyrus took down the short knife from the mantel—the one with the bone grip worn smooth by four generations of thumbs—and went to the front door through a hall full of shadows. She passed the roster board where the guild's names had hung on hooks for two centuries, each member's tally-slate hanging beneath his name, and now only one slate remained, hers, swinging faintly in some draft she couldn't locate. She passed the spot where the ledgers' cabinet had stood before the fire that had eaten them, and the floorboards there were still scorched black in the shape of the cabinet's feet, two dark footprints burned into the wood like the marks of something that had stood there deciding.

She put her eye to the peephole—an iron tube bored through the door's thickness by some clever dead ancestor—and looked out into the dark.

Lightning chose that moment to split the sky, and by its light she saw the child.

A girl, perhaps seven years old, perhaps eight—small for either. Barefoot on the threshold stone, soaked to the skin, hair plastered flat to her skull in ropes. She wore what might once have been a fine dress, the kind of thing woven for a merchant's daughter in a city Cyrus had never visited, though the fabric had gone gray with road-dirt and the hem was torn to ribbons. She stood perfectly straight in the deluge, arms at her sides, face lifted toward the door as though she had been waiting there not minutes but hours, as though the storm were a formality she had already dismissed.

And here was the thing that made Cyrus's stomach drop through the floor and keep falling: there was no one else on the road. No lantern upstream or down. No horse, no cart, no tracks in the mud that the lightning revealed—only the girl, planted on the lintel-stone like something the flood had deposited, the way the sea leaves strange things on the sand.

Cyrus had smuggled contraband through eleven kingdoms. She had been shot at twice, knifed once, married briefly and badly, and betrayed more times than she kept count of anymore. She was not a woman who frightened easily, and she told herself, hand on the door bar, that what she felt was not fear. It was the other thing. The older thing. The feeling of standing in a room where the furniture has been moved an inch while you weren't looking.

She opened the door six inches, chain on.

"Whatever you're selling," she said, "we're not buying."

The girl looked up at her. Her eyes were the color of deep water over sand—pale, almost colorless, the eyes of someone who has spent a long time in the dark—and they did not behave like a child's eyes. They did not dart to the firelight behind Cyrus, or to the knife, or to the chain. They settled on Cyrus's face and stayed, with the fixed attention of something reading.

Then the girl spoke.

It was not any language Cyrus knew, and Cyrus knew a shameful number of languages, most of them learned for the purpose of lying fluently in them. She had the trade-tongues of the coast, the clipped mercantile dialects of the river cities, the border patois that mixed three grammars into one lie. She knew enough of the old church-speech to read tombstones. What came out of the girl's mouth was none of these.

It was low and liquid and strangely formal, each word rounded off complete, like stones worn smooth in a riverbed. There were sounds in it Cyrus's throat didn't know how to make—something between an *l* and a *w*, a click soft as a knuckle tapping glass. The girl said perhaps ten words, unhurried, and her voice never rose above the rain, and yet every syllable carried as though the air itself were leaning in to listen.

"I don't understand you," Cyrus said. "Do you speak Coast-Tongue? River? Anything?"

The girl tilted her head. And spoke again—the same words, Cyrus thought, or nearly the same, but slower now, the way you'd slow down for a foreigner or a fool. At the end of the phrase she lifted one small hand and pointed, not at Cyrus, not at herself, but *down*. Past the floor. Past the foundations. Down into the mountain at whose shoulder the hall leaned like a drunk against a wall.

Then she pointed at herself.

Cyrus's mouth had gone dry. "You're saying you came from below." She heard her own voice flatten into the tone she used for negotiating with men holding knives. "From under the fell."

The girl considered this. Then she shook her head—no—and pointed down again, and out, down and east, down and far, a gesture that traveled miles in the space of an arm's length, and made a sound that was unmistakably a name. Two syllables, drowned-soft. She said it the way you say the name of a home you expect to be scolded for leaving.

Wind gusted through the six-inch gap, spitting rain into the entryway, guttering the lamp on its bracket. Cyrus should have shut the door. Every instinct her uncle had drilled into her said *shut the door, bar it, wait until daylight, nothing good walks up to a door in a storm.* She had followed that instinct through forty years and it had kept her breathing.

But the girl was shivering now—Cyrus saw it finally, the fine relentless tremor of a body burning its last warmth—and her lips had gone the blue of deep ice, and she was seven years old, or eight, and barefoot, and whatever else she was, that was also true.

"Gods damn it," Cyrus said quietly, to whichever of her ancestors was currently wincing, and she slipped the chain.

* * *

The wards answered before the girl crossed the threshold.

Every smuggler's door on the Salt Road carried protections, and the Almeida hall's were old even by the standards of a family that measured age in centuries. Her uncle had renewed them every solstice with salt and iron filings and a recitation he'd refused ever to translate—"because," he'd said, "you don't thank a dog by learning to bark"—and Cyrus had done the rite herself last month, kneeling on the lintel-stone in the frost, feeling the familiar hum go up through her knees like the purr of an enormous cat. The wards were simple things, as wards went: they weighed intention. A friend passed through unnoticed. A thief with a blade felt a hand close gently on the back of his neck, and usually reconsidered. Something worse felt considerably more than a hand.

Cyrus stepped back to let the girl in, and the moment the child's bare foot touched the inner stone, the whole doorway *sang*.

There was no better word for it. The salt in the mortar above the lintel—salt pressed into the joints by hands two hundred years dead—began to glow. Not the hot white flare of alarm, which Cyrus had seen once, long ago, on a night she did not talk about. This was a slow gold light, warm as lamplight, spreading along the doorframe like dawn coming up over a ridge, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a sound: a chord, held and swelling, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass the size of a cathedral.

The girl stopped on the threshold and looked up at the glowing lintel. And smiled.

It transformed her face. Until that moment she had been eerie, uncanny, a doll left out in the rain. Smiling, she was just a child—gap-toothed, exhausted, lit gold by the light of a protection her presence had somehow woken instead of tripped. She reached up one small hand toward the shining salt and said a single word in that drowned, liquid tongue, and the word had the shape of a greeting. Cyrus would swear it to her grave: it had the shape of a greeting, the way a word can have a shape, the way *hello* and *goodbye* feel different in the mouth even before you know what they mean.

The chord faded. The gold dimmed to embers and went out. The girl lowered her hand and looked at Cyrus, waiting, dripping on the stone.

"Right," Cyrus said, to cover the fact that her heart was going like a drum in a parade. "In. Boots—well. Off, I suppose. You haven't any."

* * *

She got the girl dry the way you get anything done in a house that used to hold thirty people and now holds one: alone, improvising, talking to yourself. Old clothes of no telling whose original owner—a wool shirt that reached the girl's knees, socks rolled double at the toe. Hot barley broth from the pot Cyrus had been letting sit for days, too grief-dull to cook properly for one. The girl ate like someone who had forgotten food was allowed to be warm, cupping the bowl in both hands, and Cyrus sat across from her and watched and did not taste her own portion at all.

The fire had come back up. Outside, the storm worked itself into a fury, thunder rolling down the valley in long tectonic complaints, and inside, the kitchen was orange-lit and almost—almost—peaceful. Almost ordinary. A foundling on a storm night; such things happened; such things had always happened; there were laws about it, probably, and priests who handled it.

Except.

Except that when the girl set down the bowl, she looked around the kitchen slowly, taking in the copper pots on their hooks, the long table scarred by two hundred years of knives and coins and card games, the soot-blackened beam where generations of Almeidas had nailed up tokens of successful runs—and she nodded. Once. A small, satisfied nod, like a traveler confirming a landmark on a map drawn from memory.

And except that when a log shifted in the hearth and sent up a fountain of sparks, the girl flinched—not from the sparks, Cyrus realized, watching closely—but from the *sound the fire made*, as if she had expected fire to be quieter, or wetter, or otherwise differently behaved.

And except, above all, for the counting.

It started maybe an hour in. The girl had curled up on the bench near the fire, wrapped in a blanket, apparently asleep. Cyrus had finally lit her uncle's pipe, mostly to have something to do with her hands, and was staring into the flames trying not to think about the scorched footprints on the floorboards upstairs, when she noticed the girl's lips moving.

Silent. Rhythmic. Over and over, the same few words, in that low liquid speech, spoken the way children count—one, two, three—with the cadence of something practiced, something recited. But the cadence was wrong for counting. Counting runs forward. This ran *down*. Each repetition landed lower, heavier, like steps descending a stair, and at the end of each cycle there was a pause—a held breath—and then it began again, one step higher than before, working its way back up.

Cyrus smoked and listened and felt the hair rise slowly along her forearms, because she recognized the pattern. Not the words. The pattern. Her uncle had recited the ward-rite in exactly that cadence, every solstice, kneeling on the lintel-stone: a descent and a return, a going-down and a coming-back, over and over, like a man walking to the edge of a well and climbing out, walking to the edge and climbing out.

"What are you counting?" she asked.

The girl opened her eyes. They caught the firelight and gave nothing back, those pale water-colored eyes. She looked at Cyrus for a long moment. Then she raised one hand, pointed down—always down, everything about this child pointed down—and said a word.

One word. Two syllables, drowned-soft, the same name she'd named on the doorstep.

Then she closed her eyes again and went back to her silent descent-and-return, and within minutes her breathing slowed into true sleep, leaving Cyrus alone with the storm and the pipe and the growing, cold certainty that had been assembling itself in her chest since the moment the wards sang.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she had been refusing to say plainly even inside her own skull.

Wards weighed intention. That was how they worked; that was what her uncle had said, in his way, and every smuggler's lore confirmed it. They sang gold for friends and white-hot for enemies, and they did not sing at all for the innocent and the aimless. For the wards of the Almeida hall to wake—to wake *gladly*, gold as harvest-light, greeting like a host recognizing an expected guest—the child on the doorstep had to carry an intention the wards knew. An old one. A registered one. Something written into the salt two hundred years ago by hands that had expected her.

Expected *her*. Or expected something wearing her.

Cyrus got up, quietly, and went to the scorched footprint-shapes on the floor of the ledger room, and stood over them in the dark with the lamp turned low. The ledgers were ash. Her uncle was under fresh-turned earth. The guild was one woman and a knife, and now, sleeping by her fire, a barefoot girl who spoke the language of nowhere living and counted her way down into the dark like a clerk descending into a vault.

Something old has found us, Cyrus thought. Or—we were always its address, and it has only now come home.

Upstairs, faintly, through the floor, she could still hear it—or imagined she could—the silent rhythm of the girl's descent. Down, and back. Down, and back. Like something practicing its arrival.

In the morning, Cyrus decided, she would go up to the cold room where her uncle had hidden the things he hadn't trusted to the ledgers. He had always hidden *something*. Men who burned their books always kept one page back.

She just hadn't known, until tonight, that he'd be keeping it for her.
