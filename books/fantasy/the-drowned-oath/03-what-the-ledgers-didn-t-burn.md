## 3. What the Ledgers Didn't Burn

The ledgers had not burned well.

Cyrus stood in the guildhall's counting room on the third morning after burying her uncle, and she understood that at once, the way a smuggler understands a badly packed hold—by the way it sat wrong. Someone had made an effort. Ash lay in grey drifts across the floorboards, ankle-deep in the corners where the draft had pushed it, and the iron ledger-cage in the center of the room stood open with its door hanging crooked, one hinge sheared clean through. Not broken open. Sheared. Cut with tools and intent.

But paper is stubborn. Paper wants to survive.

She had found that out over two days of grief-work, kneeling in the ash with her sleeves rolled and a dust mask of wet linen over her mouth, sorting the dead guild's bones. Most of what remained was fragment: corners of pages, spines with their contents gone, the charred lacework of margins. The fire had been hot and thorough at the edges of the room and lazy at its heart, as fires are when they're set by men who want to leave before the smoke draws attention. It had eaten the recent volumes—the last forty years of cargo manifests, the debt schedules, the syndicate correspondence—and left the older things only scorched, because the older things lived deeper in the cage, in the iron boxes at the back, wrapped in oilcloth against exactly this kind of enthusiasm.

Cyrus worked by lamplight because the shutters stayed closed now. Thornfell was a town that watched a funeral house the way gulls watch a gutting boat, and she did not intend to be watched sorting through what her family had died protecting.

Her uncle Halvard had kept the keys. Her uncle Halvard had died with them still on his belt, and Cyrus had cut them free herself, because no one else would come to do it, because there was no one else. That fact sat in her chest like swallowed ballast. Forty years ago the Almeida guild had run eleven crews and owned three ships and a warehouse row on the salt-quay. Now it was one woman kneeling in ash, and a hall full of ghosts who hadn't even had the courtesy to stay for the burial.

* * *

She found the journal on the second evening.

It was wedged inside the false bottom of the oldest ledger-box—a box so old its iron had gone the color of dried blood, sealed with wax that had gone amber and then black and then something harder than either. She'd pried it loose with a chisel and a mallet, working slow, working careful, because her grandfather Torvald Almeida had been a man who booby-trapped his sentiment. Twice the chisel skipped and kissed metal that rang wrong, hollow-wrong, and both times she stopped and sweated and started again from another angle.

Inside: oilcloth, then leather, then a journal bound in grey calfskin gone soft as cloth with age. No lock. Torvald had apparently decided that if you got this far, you'd earned it.

The first page said, in a hand like barbed wire:

*If you are reading this, you are an Almeida, or you have killed one. Either way, sit down.*

Cyrus sat down on the cold floor among the ashes of her family's fortune, and read until the lamp burned low.

* * *

Most of it was practical. That was the thing that undid her, later, thinking back—how ordinary most of it was. Tide tables. Contact ciphers, three generations obsolete. A recipe for curing lamp-oil so it wouldn't freeze in the high passes. Notes on which customs officers in Saltmere could be bought with coin and which required flattery, and how the flattery differed, and how Officer Brann of the east gate liked to be told his wife's garden was the finest in the ward. Torvald Almeida, founder of the guild's modern fortunes, smuggler, patriarch, dead two hundred years if he was dead at all, and here he was fussing about lamp-oil and a customs man's wife's begonias.

She laughed once, alone in the ash-dark, and the laugh came out wrong, and after that she didn't try again.

It was near midnight when she reached the pages dated the founding year. The ink changed there. Older, browner, mixed with something that had made it bite deep into the page—she ran her thumb over it and felt the grooves, felt how hard the pen had pressed, as though the words had needed forcing out.

*Year One. We are rich, and I am afraid.*

*The salt-road contract came through tonight. Eleven years we starved and now in one season we hold more freight than the Vessar combine held in thirty. Men keep asking how. I let them believe it was nerve. Nerve is cheap and everyone has some.*

*Here is how it was. There is a court beneath Thornfell mountain. Older than the kingdom, older than the names of things. I did not believe it either. Go down the First Road and you will believe. They keep accounts the way we keep accounts, except theirs never close. I sold them a promise and they paid me in freight-passage and favor, and the favor has made us rich, and the promise—*

Here the writing stopped. Then resumed lower on the page, smaller, cramped into the margin like a man whispering:

*The promise is not mine to pay. That was the price. Not my soul—they did not want mine, they wanted one owed to me, one not yet born, one of my blood who would owe me everything. A soul-price. Paid in full at the signing. Owed in kind, at a date of their choosing. I asked when. The Oathkeeper said: when we call it due.*

*I signed anyway.*

*I signed anyway because eleven years of starving was enough and because I told myself the court forgets, the court sleeps, the mountain keeps its own counsel and maybe the debt dies with the mountain. I told myself my grandchildren's grandchildren would thank me. I am writing this at night and my hands will not stop shaking and I know—I know the way you know weather coming—that I have not bought a fortune. I have taken a loan against someone who cannot yet refuse.*

*If you are reading this and you are of my blood: I am sorry. Check the red box. The original instrument is there. Do not sign anything. Ever. For anyone. And if they come to collect—*

The page ended mid-sentence. The next leaf had been torn out. Not burned—torn, ragged at the gutter, torn recently enough that the exposed edge was still pale. Cyrus turned it over in her fingers for a long time. Her grandfather's handwriting had survived two centuries of damp and iron and fire, but that one leaf had been removed by human hands, deliberately, within her own lifetime. Her uncle's hands, most likely. Halvard, who never spoke of the old years, who went grey and silent whenever a client used the word *debt*, who had burned the ledgers and died in a chair facing the door.

He'd known. He'd known enough to tear out the answer and not enough—or too much—to write down what he'd done with it.

* * *

She slept four hours on the counting-room floor with the journal under her head like a pillow, and dreamed of water rising in the halls, patient and level, touching nothing, disturbing nothing, waiting.

In the morning she went looking for the red box.

Torvald's instruction said *check the red box* as if any fool would know which box, but Torvald also wrote tide tables in cipher, and Cyrus had grown up on his ciphers. The phrase meant the strongroom under the salt cellar—the one place in the guildhall built before the guild, built by whoever built the town, its walls fitted without mortar in courses too regular for human hands. Every child raised in the hall learned the story: don't go in the under-cellar, the cold comes up from below. Cyrus had gone in exactly once, at nine, dared by cousins long since scattered or dead, and remembered only the dark and the sense—not sound, sense—of being *counted*.

The strongroom door took her an hour. Three locks, and behind the third a wax seal bearing the guild mark, unbroken. Whatever was inside had waited untouched since before her birth.

Inside, on a stone shelf, wrapped in sailcloth gone stiff as bark: a box. Not red—red fades; whatever dye had named it in Torvald's day had surrendered two centuries ago—but the shape of a box built to be noticed, iron-banded, small enough to hold with both hands. Heavy as sin.

She carried it up into the light. She broke the seal with her thumbnail. Inside, on a bed of black wool gone grey, lay a single sheet of vellum, and a cord braided of three colors—salt-white, sea-green, and a black that drank the lamplight—and a strip of lead stamped with words in no alphabet she knew, though her eye snagged on them the way it snags on a face almost recognized.

The vellum was the contract. She knew contracts; she'd been reading them since she could read, had forged half of Saltmere's by sixteen. This one was different. The vellum itself was wrong—too smooth, cool to the touch, like skin that had never seen sun. The text was written in brown-black ink that she understood, with a lurch, was neither ink nor blood but both. And at the bottom, beneath lines of the drowned script that seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at them—

Her grandfather's signature. *Torvald Almeida,* in the same barbed-wire hand as the journal, pressed so hard the pen had torn the vellum. And beside it, in the same brown-black, a second line already filled: *paid in full.* And below that, a third line, blank. Waiting. Ruled and empty and patient as a grave plot, sized for one name.

*Soul-price, paid in full. Owed in kind.*

Dated Year One. The year of the founding. The year the starving ended.

Cyrus set the contract down on the counting table very carefully, the way you set down a sleeping snake, and stepped back from it, and stood in the cold morning light that came through the shutter cracks in thin blades, and thought about the girl.

The mute child who had walked out of the storm three nights ago, barefoot, unbothered by cold. Who had stood at the threshold while the old wards over the door—the wards nobody had renewed in fifty years, the wards everyone assumed were decorative—had lit green and bent toward her like grass in wind. Who had spoken, in her sleep, in a language that predated every kingdom's alphabet, and whose voice had sounded less like a child talking and more like something remembering how.

Who had appeared the same week the creditors began circling. The same week the syndicate's letters arrived using the word *assets*. The same week her uncle died with his chair facing the door.

*Owed in kind, at a date of their choosing. When we call it due.*

The blank line was child-sized. She understood that without knowing how she knew it, the way you understand a knife is for cutting.

"No," she said aloud, to the contract, to the room, to two hundred years of dead men who had signed things on behalf of people not yet born. "No. Find another line."

The vellum, of course, said nothing. But somewhere far beneath her feet—beneath the salt cellar, beneath the mortarless walls, beneath the roots of Thornfell mountain—Cyrus felt, with absolute certainty and no evidence at all, something shift its weight. Patiently. Like a creditor settling in to wait.

And from the room above, faint through the floorboards, came the girl's voice—not words, not yet, just a low hummed note in the drowned tongue, rising and falling like water finding its level.

It matched, note for note, the rhythm of the lead strip's inscription, when she finally worked up the courage to hold it to the light.
