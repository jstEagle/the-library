## 4. The Tongue Beneath Kingdoms

Cyrus had not slept in three days, and the child had not stopped talking in two.

Not to her—never quite to her. The girl spoke to the walls of the guildhall, to the hearthstones, to the rain that came down the chimney and hissed itself dead on the coals. She sat cross-legged on the floor before the great cold fireplace where Cyrus's uncle had once held court over maps and debts, and she talked in that language with no country, her small hands moving through the air as though she were shaping something invisible out of wet clay.

Cyrus had heard a hundred tongues in her years on the roads. Trade-cant from the salt ports, the clipped vowels of the mountain garrisons, the river dialects that turned every word into water. She had heard smugglers pray in six languages and curse in nine. This was none of them. It had no borrowed words, no recognizable bones. It sounded less like speech than like stone remembering it had once been liquid.

And the wards answered.

That was the part Cyrus could not stop turning over, like a coin she suspected was shaved. The old wards over the guildhall door—her grandfather's work, laid down so long ago that no living Almeida knew the words that had raised them—had been silent for forty years. Dead iron, the older smugglers used to say. Ornament. But last night, when the girl had wandered to the threshold and pressed one bare foot against the lintel-stone, the wards had *hummed*. A low, sweet note, like a finger run around the rim of a glass, and the salt packed into the doorframe had glittered as if lit from beneath.

The girl had looked up at the sound and smiled. Not at Cyrus. At the door.

Now Cyrus sat across the hall with her grandfather's journal open on her knee—the hidden journal, the one she'd pried out of the false bottom of the strongbox—and watched the child talk to a house that had been dying for a decade, and felt the first cold thread of understanding slide down her spine.

Because the girl kept saying one word over and over. A long word, heavy in the middle, that ended in something like a sigh. And Cyrus knew that word. Not its meaning—she didn't have its meaning—but its *shape*. Her grandfather had written it, or something very like it, in the margin of a page dated a hundred and ninety years back, beside a sketch of a tunnel mouth.

A tunnel mouth Cyrus had walked through herself, eleven years old, her uncle's hand on her shoulder, on her first run beneath Thornfell.

She got up. The girl didn't notice, or didn't care. Cyrus took a lantern down from its hook, checked the oil with fingers that were steadier than they had any right to be, and went out into the storm-washed morning to find whether a dead woman's margin could match a living child's mouth.

* * *

The tunnels under Thornfell had names. Most of them did, anyway—the ones the guild had cut or claimed in its two centuries of quiet commerce. The Corduroy, for the road they'd floored with split logs. The Widow's Stair, because of what had happened to the man who cut it. Saltcellar Deep, the Long Sigh, the Nine Sisters. Cyrus had learned them the way other children learned psalms: kneeling, in the dark, with an adult's hand heavy between her shoulder blades.

But there were older passages. Tunnels no one admitted to cutting, that predated the guild by nobody-knew-how-long, that the oldest ledgers referred to only as *the First Roads*, in handwriting cramped with either reverence or fear. Her uncle had forbidden her them. Her grandfather's journal barely mentioned them—except in margins, except in that one heavy word, repeated like a man worrying a loose tooth.

The passage behind the collapsed cistern was one of those. Cyrus hadn't been down it since she was nineteen. She stood at its mouth now with the lantern raised, listening to the drip and settle of the mountain, and said aloud, carefully, shaping her tongue around sounds it had never been asked to make:

*"Hallowen-ath."*

The word the girl kept saying.

The dark answered.

Not with sound. With *attention*. Cyrus felt it the way you feel someone enter a room behind you—a change in pressure, a prickle along the scalp, the sense of vast space suddenly deciding to be aware of her. The lantern flame leaned sideways, though no draft moved. Somewhere far down the passage, water that had been dripping in its slow rhythm for centuries faltered, skipped, resumed.

Cyrus stood very still. Her heart was doing something undignified against her ribs.

"Right," she said to the dark, in trade-cant, because profanity was easier than prayer. "So it's true."

She had half hoped it wasn't. Half hoped the girl was a foundling with a fever-tongue, some orphaned curiosity from a drowned coast, and the wards' humming a trick of wind in old salt. But the mountain had just flinched at a word spoken by a woman who didn't know what it meant, and hope was a luxury the Almeida guild had never once been able to afford.

She went back up. She had a child to watch, a journal to read, and apparently a language to learn.

* * *

In the dream, the girl is walking.

She does not know she is dreaming. Dreaming is a word in a language she has almost finished forgetting; she is down to perhaps four hundred words of the kingdom where people breathe, and this is not one of them. What she knows is this: the corridor is warm, and warm is good, and the walls are singing.

They sing in the old way, the under-way, the way that lives in stone the way blood lives in skin. She sings back. Her voice comes out small and clear and it goes down and down ahead of her like a lamp being carried by someone braver, lighting halls she has never seen but somehow remembers: a stair cut like the inside of a shell, spiraling into blue-black; a bridge over nothing with rails carved as reeds; a door tall as grief, banded in bronze gone green, waiting.

Behind the door, something patient turns over in its sleep.

She is not afraid. Fear is also a word she is losing. What she feels instead is older and simpler: the feeling of a key nearing its lock. Of a letter finally reaching the hand it was addressed to. She presses her palm flat against the bronze and the metal is cool and kind and it says her name—

—not the name the tall sad woman uses, the short human name, the coat they've put over her like a blanket—

—her other name. Her first one. The one made of water and weight.

She wakes with it still in her mouth and says it aloud into the dark rafters of the strange dry house, and somewhere below her feet, in rooms she cannot see, the stones say it back.

* * *

By the third night, Cyrus had filled eleven pages of a cheap schoolroom slate-book with transcriptions.

It was slow, maddening work. The girl spoke freely enough—she seemed to need to speak, the way a kettle needs to vent—but she spoke *at* things, not *to* people, and she had no interest in pointing at objects or repeating on demand. So Cyrus eavesdropped on a five-year-old's conversation with a fireplace and wrote down what she heard, sounding the syllables against her own memory of that single resonant word in the tunnel mouth, building a lexicon of maybe thirty terms she couldn't translate and didn't dare guess at.

Then, on the fourth evening, the girl pointed.

She was standing at the big map on the guildhall wall—the master chart, inked by three generations of Almeidas, showing every road, shaft, shrine, and bolt-hole within fifty miles of Thornfell. Cyrus had hung it crooked after the funeral and never straightened it. The girl reached up on her toes and put one finger flat against the map, west of the town, in the blank region beyond the marked tunnels where the cartographers had simply given up and left the vellum empty.

And she said: *"Hallowen-ath."*

Cyrus crossed the room slowly, afraid that hurry would break whatever spell made the child willing to share. "Show me again," she said, and hated how gentle her voice came out. "Where's that?"

The girl looked at her—directly at her, for the first time in days—and moved her finger. Not to another spot. Along a line. A route, traced through unmarked stone, sure as a signature: down past the cistern, under the flooded galleries, along a bearing no surveyor had ever drawn, to a point deep beneath the mountain's western shoulder where the map showed nothing at all.

Then she said another word. Shorter. Two beats, falling.

Cyrus's mouth went dry. She knew that shape too. Page forty-one of the journal, in her grandfather's hand, in a list he had titled—in cramped, apologetic script—*Names That Were Here Before Ours.*

She fetched the journal. She laid it open on the map table. And one by one, over the next two hours, while the storm came back off the fells and rattled the shutters, she matched the girl's vocabulary to her grandfather's margins, and the picture assembled itself with the terrible click of a lock accepting its key.

Every place the child named was real.

Not marked-real. Not on any chart drawn by living hands. But real in the way the First Roads were real—real in the old tunnels' geometry, real in the way certain passages curved around chambers that appeared on no plan yet existed, solid and echoing, whenever a fleeing smuggler needed them to. Cyrus had spent her whole life treating those anomalies as luck. Luck, or the mountain's odd mercy, or the simple fact that old stone hides more than maps admit.

The girl named them. All of them. The chamber beneath the Long Sigh that no survey had ever found. The stair that went down farther than the mine's deepest shaft and yet connected to nothing above. The place where the water ran upward, which Cyrus had seen once, drunk-blind with fear, and told no one, because telling would have meant admitting she'd been somewhere she shouldn't.

Thirty words. Thirty places. And the girl said them the way you say the names of rooms in your own house.

Cyrus set down the pencil. Her hands were shaking now; she let them.

"You know these roads," she said quietly. "You know them better than I do. Better than the guild ever did."

The child looked at her with those grey eyes, eyes the color of water over slate, and tilted her head. Then she reached out and touched the journal—touched the page with the list of names, the list titled *Names That Were Here Before Ours*—and said a sentence. A whole sentence, fluid and grave, in that language with no country.

Cyrus understood none of it.

Except that the girl's tone was the tone you use to correct someone. Gently. The way you'd tell a child that the story they'd been told was sweet, but wrong.

* * *

The dream changes, as dreams do, without transition or permission.

Now she is in the tall hall, the one with the ceiling like the underside of a wave frozen mid-break, and the court is seated, and she is late. She runs. Her feet make no sound on the floor, which is right, which is proper, which is how feet behave here, and the courtiers turn their faces toward her as she passes—faces like moonlight on the sea, beautiful and unreadable—and one of them extends a hand crowned in black coral and says, in the old tongue, the words every child of the under-way learns before any other:

*What is owed is remembered. What is remembered is owed.*

She nods, solemn, because that is the answer, that has always been the answer, and the courtier's face folds into something that might be approval, and the great doors at the end of the hall begin to open onto a light she has never seen but is, even now, in the dream, already beginning to forget—

She wakes.

The tall sad woman is asleep at the map table, cheek on her arms, the book of names open under her hand. The fire is nearly out. Outside, the rain has stopped and the world smells of wet ash and stone.

The girl gets up quietly, the way feet are taught to move in the under-halls, and stands at the window, looking east, toward the mountain. Toward home, or the road to it. Toward the door that opened onto light, once, long ago, before the debt, before the promise, before she was folded up small and sent ahead like a coin in an envelope.

*What is owed is remembered,* she thinks, in the old tongue, because the new one still fits her badly, all sharp corners.

*What is remembered is owed.*

Below her, deep beneath the foundations, the guildhall's oldest stone—stone the Almeidas never laid, stone they built on top of and called their own—shifts its weight by a fraction, the way a sleeper shifts toward dawn. And in the flooded dark of the First Roads, in the chamber beneath the Long Sigh whose name only she can say, the water rises one inch, and holds, and waits.

* * *

Cyrus woke at grey dawn with a crick in her neck and the immediate, animal certainty that something in the house had changed.

She found it in the entryway. The girl stood at the threshold, barefoot on the cold flagstones, looking out at the washed morning—and the wards were singing again. Louder this time. The salt in the doorframe glowed faint gold, and the old iron bell that hung above the gate, mute since Cyrus's grandmother's day, was trembling on its bracket without ringing, as if it wanted to.

Beyond the gate, the road climbed east toward Thornfell's shoulder, toward the mountain, and the morning mist lay along it in long white banners like roads of its own.

The girl turned. Her expression was not a child's expression. It was patient, and old, and sorry.

Then she lifted one hand and pointed—not at the mountain, not at the road, but down. Through the floor. Through the foundations. Through two hundred years of guild stone and however many centuries of older stone beneath it.

Down.

Cyrus followed the gesture with her eyes and felt, distinctly, beneath her boots, beneath the flagstones, beneath everything—a pulse. Faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. Slow. Enormous. Keeping time.

Something down there had noticed the child. Something down there was counting.

And the wards over the door sang on, sweet and terrible, like a welcome, like a warning, like both at once.
