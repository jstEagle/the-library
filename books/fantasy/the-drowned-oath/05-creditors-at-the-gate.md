## 5. Creditors at the Gate

The storm had passed in the night, and morning came up the valley like a slow tide, gray water-light spilling over the rooftops of Thornfell and pooling in the gutters where the cobbles still ran with last night's rain. Cyrus Almeida stood in the doorway of the guildhall with a cup of chicory going cold in her hand and watched three black-lacquered barges come down the river road on wheels, drawn by teams of horses that steamed like kettles.

She counted them the way her uncle had taught her to count everything—by what they cost, by what they carried, by how much trouble they could make. Six horses to a barge. Guards riding the rails, four apiece, in oiled leather the color of wet slate. No banners. The syndicate never flew banners; banners were for people who wanted to be remembered, and the syndicate preferred to be forgotten until the moment it knocked.

"Company," she said, without turning.

Behind her, small bare feet whispered across the flagstones. The girl had taken to following her through the hall like a shadow with a limp, always two steps back, always watching. She had slept last night in the alcove behind the hearth, curled around one of the old guild cloaks, and she had not made a sound—not even when the thunder cracked the sky open over the mountain. But when the first barge rounded the bend at Miller's Cut, she had gone rigid as a snared rabbit, and now she stood at Cyrus's hip, staring out at the river road with eyes the color of deep water under ice.

"You know them?" Cyrus asked.

The girl didn't answer. She never answered. But her hand came up and closed around a fold of Cyrus's sleeve, and her grip was stronger than anything that small had a right to be.

* * *

Cyrus had spent the morning doing what she did every morning since she'd come home to find the hall cold and her uncle dead in his chair and the ledgers burned to lace in the grate: she worked. There were barrels to be moved before the damp got into the salt, there was a lock on the east cellar door that needed coaxing, there was an inventory in her head that grew shorter every time she checked it. A dying guild was mostly maintenance. You kept the roof patched and the doors oiled and you pretended the silence in the corridors was restful rather than funereal.

She had been oiling the cellar lock when the knock came.

Not at the front door. At the lintel post—a single knock, knuckle on wood, delivered at shoulder height, which was the old courtesy of trade roads: *we know your customs, we claim the right of parley.* Nobody had used that knock in fifteen years. It belonged to a world of ledgers and tolls and men who shook hands like they were testing your grip strength for a coffin.

Cyrus put down the oilcloth. She took the short knife from her boot because habit was cheaper than armor, and she went to the door, and she opened it.

Three of them stood on the step. The one in front was a woman of perhaps fifty, dressed in traveling gray so fine it made the rain look dirty against it. Her hair was bound back in a knot tight enough to hurt, and her face had the particular smoothness of skin that had been expensive once and had decided to stop trying. Behind her, two men flanked the doorway with the patient stillness of furniture that could kill you.

"Mistress Almeida." The woman said it like she'd been practicing. "I am Sereth Vane, factor of the Meridian Compact. I hold instruments of claim against the estate of the Almeida smuggling guild."

"The guild has no estate," Cyrus said. "It has a roof that leaks and a cellar full of salt nobody wants. If your Compact wants salt, I'll sell you some at a fair price and we can all go home."

Something moved at the corner of the woman's mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, maybe, from long ago, before the work wore it off. "May we come in? The rain is unkind to my papers."

"It's stopped raining."

"All the more reason not to stand in it." And she stepped forward, and Cyrus—because the old law of the lintel said a factor who claimed parley could not be turned away without forfeiting the guild's standing, and because some part of her, God help her, was curious—stepped aside.

* * *

They sat in the great room, at the long table where forty smugglers had once eaten together and argued about routes and cheated each other at cards. Now it was Sereth Vane and her two silent men on one side, and Cyrus on the other, and the girl in the alcove behind the hearth, half-hidden by the chimney breast, watching with the fixed attention of something that had learned to watch before it learned to speak.

Vane unrolled her papers on the table with the tenderness of a priest laying out relics. There were many of them. More than Cyrus had expected. More, frankly, than the guild had ever been worth.

"You'll forgive the volume," Vane said. "Debts accumulate interest even when debtors die. Your uncle was... let me see..." She turned a page with one finger. "Remarkably disciplined about his own accounts and remarkably careless about his predecessors'. The Compact holds notes signed by your grandfather, your grand-uncle, two cousins on the Saltmarsh side, and a man named Ferro whom I believe died falling down his own stairs in—" another page-turn— "the Year of the Leaning Bell. Tragic. He owed us eleven crowns."

"He owed everyone eleven crowns," Cyrus said. "Ferro would have owed the tide if the tide lent."

"And yet the tide does not lend, Mistress Almeida, which is why institutions like mine exist. We are the tide's clerks." Vane folded her hands. "Here is the situation as the Compact sees it. The Almeida guild is functionally dissolved. Its charter lapsed with your uncle's death—you have filed no succession, you hold no quorum, and the hall itself stands as collateral against the principal debt, which currently stands at nine hundred and forty marks, six shillings."

"I've seen the numbers my uncle kept. They don't say that."

"Your uncle's numbers were written for your uncle's eyes. Ours are written for courts." Vane slid a paper across the table. "However. The Compact is not a cruel institution. We would prefer resolution to ruin. There is a settlement available."

"There's always a settlement available. That's how you people feed."

"This one is unusual." For the first time, Vane's voice changed register—dropped half a tone, slowed, became careful in a way that made the hair on Cyrus's forearms lift. "Most of these claims are negotiable. Marks can be paid, property can be seized, labor can be bonded. That is ordinary business, and I do ordinary business all day, every day, and sleep well after." She drew a second paper from the roll—this one different, heavier stock, sealed not with wax but with something dark and resinous that had been pressed while hot and had cooled into a surface like dried blood. "This one is not negotiable. This one is marked, in the original instrument, *non-negotiable, held below.*"

The fire behind the hearth popped. In the alcove, the girl made a sound—the smallest sound, a breath drawn in through teeth—and Cyrus heard it the way you hear a floorboard shift in an empty house.

"Held below," Cyrus repeated.

"That is the phrase as written. Two hundred years old, or near enough. The Compact acquired it in a consolidation sixty years ago—we buy portfolios, Mistress Almeida, whole portfolios, bundles of paper from dead banks and defunct houses, and most of it is worthless and some of it is gold and once in a generation, once, there is a note like this one." Vane laid the dark-sealed paper flat between them. "We have never been able to collect on it. The debtor named is not a person. The collateral named is not property. And the venue of collection—" her finger tapped the seal, once, lightly, as if it might bite— "is listed only as *below.*"

"Below what?"

"That is the question our archivists have asked for sixty years. Below sea level? Below the law? Below—" and here Vane smiled, briefly, without warmth, "—below the attention of respectable people, which is where your family lived and thrived, Mistress Almeida, and no judgment implied, because thriving is thriving. But three weeks ago, our archivists found the answer. Or rather, the answer found us. Word travels. A child appeared at this door during the storm. A mute child, barefoot, who walked past wards that the oldest records say were bought at terrible expense and have never once failed."

The room got very quiet. The kind of quiet that has weight, that presses on the eardrums. Cyrus became aware of her own heartbeat, and of the chicory cup she'd left on the sideboard, and of the knife in her boot, and of the fact that the two men by the door had not moved a muscle in ten minutes and their stillness had begun to feel less like discipline and more like patience.

"The Compact does not traffic in children," Vane said. "Let me be clear about that, because I know what you're thinking, and I'd rather you think accurately. Children are bad collateral. They grow, they die, they run away, they acquire inconvenient sympathies in the towns where they're held. We deal in paper. Paper doesn't cry." She turned the dark-sealed note around so Cyrus could read it. The script was old, formal, in a hand that had pressed hard enough to groove the page. Most of it was legal boilerplate gone strange with age—but one line stood out, fresh-cut into the old parchment as if someone had carved it recently, and the letters were wrong somehow, too angular, too many strokes, like writing done by a hand that had learned its letters somewhere very old and very cold:

*Collateral: one soul, lineal, to be rendered when it presents itself.*

Below that, in smaller characters, almost an afterthought: *It will present itself.*

"We are not here to take the child," Vane said. "We are here to inform you that the debt has come due, that the holder of record is not us, and that we intend to facilitate collection. Consider us..." she searched for the word, "...clerks. Again. Always clerks. The party below will send its own arrangement. We merely wish to establish, before they arrive, whether the Compact might negotiate terms of transfer. A finder's consideration. Access rights. There are precedents."

"Get out."

Cyrus hadn't planned to say it. It came out low and flat, from somewhere below thought, and it surprised her a little, the way her own voice sometimes did when grief took the wheel.

Vane regarded her without offense. She began, unhurried, rolling her papers back into their case. "You misunderstand the geometry of your position, Mistress Almeida, so let me draw it for you. To the south, the Compact holds your debts and can strip this hall to its stones within the month. To the north and east, every road out of Thornfell passes through territory where our factors keep friends, and those friends have been told to watch for a woman and a child. Above you is weather and law, both indifferent. And below you—" she tapped the dark seal one final time, and this time Cyrus saw that her fingertip came away faintly marked, a gray shadow on the pad of her finger that she wiped absently on her skirt, "—below you is the party that has waited two hundred years and is, I am assured, very good at waiting. You cannot fight us. You cannot buy us. You cannot outrun the geography. What you can do is choose which of us you'd rather deal with, and I promise you, having met the alternative's correspondence, you would rather deal with me."

At the hearth, the girl stood up.

She didn't speak. She walked—out of the alcove, across the flagstones, small bare feet silent as snowfall—and she stopped beside Cyrus's chair, and she looked at Sereth Vane with those pale eyes, and then she looked at the dark-sealed paper lying on the table.

And she reached out and touched it.

Cyrus grabbed for her—for her wrist, her collar, anything—but the girl was faster than she should have been, and her fingertip came to rest on the seal, on that dried-blood resin, and the temperature of the room dropped. Not a draft. Not imagination. The fire guttered; the chicory in Cyrus's abandoned cup skinned over with frost so thin it looked like breath on glass; and the girl's mouth opened, and what came out was not the drowned tongue, not this time, but words in plain Trade Common, accented strangely, spaced oddly, spoken with the cadence of someone reciting:

"*Non-negotiable,*" the girl said. "*Held below. Held below. Held below.*" Three times, like a bell struck three times, and on the third the dark seal cracked—cleanly, silently, straight through—and the paper beneath it yellowed and curled at the edges as though two hundred years had caught up with it all at once.

Then she stepped back behind Cyrus's chair and took hold of her sleeve again, and the room warmed, and the fire recovered, and everything was exactly as it had been except the broken seal and the smell—which lingered, faint and mineral, like the air off deep water, like the tunnels under the mountain where the old maps stopped pretending to know the way.

Sereth Vane sat very still. Whatever she had expected from this meeting, it had not been this. When she finally spoke, her voice had lost its polish, and Cyrus understood that underneath the factor's calm was a woman who read other people's debts for a living and had just watched one of them reach up out of the paper and touch her.

"Well," Vane said. "That simplifies certain questions of authentication."

"Get out," Cyrus said again, and this time both men by the door shifted, and Vane lifted two fingers and they went still again.

"We'll go," Vane said, rising, gathering her case. "But understand what happened here today, Mistress Almeida. Before this hour, the Compact could have claimed ambiguity. Could have sold the note onward, buried it in another portfolio, washed our hands of it. Authentication changes things. An authenticated instrument must be honored—or discharged—and the parties above ground who know how to discharge it are few, and the parties below who wish to collect it are patient, and you are standing in the middle holding a child who just countersigned her own debt in front of three witnesses." She paused at the door, and for one moment the professional mask slipped, and what showed underneath was tired, and old, and almost—almost—sorry. "My advice, for whatever a clerk's advice is worth: don't be here when the arrangement comes. They always send it at night. They always send it walking."

The door closed behind them. Cyrus listened to the barges creaking away down the river road, six horses to a team, wheels grinding the wet gravel, until the sound faded under the ordinary noises of the morning—a dog barking somewhere, the river muttering over its stones, the hall settling around its own emptiness.

Then she knelt down in front of the girl, whose hands had begun, very quietly, to shake.

"What did you touch?" Cyrus asked. "What was that?"

The girl looked at her. And slowly, deliberately, she raised one small hand and pointed—not at the broken paper, not at the door, but down. Through the floor. Through the cellars, through the salt, through the bedrock and the flooded galleries and the places where the oldest maps gave up.

Down.

Cyrus sat back on her heels. Nine hundred marks she could argue with. Syndicates she could cheat, outrun, outwait—she'd been born into a profession that treated all three as basic literacy. But the girl's finger stayed pointed at the floorboards, steady now, certain, and Cyrus thought of her grandfather's journal hidden in the chimney, of the phrase she'd found burned into her memory the way brands burn into hide: *soul-price, paid in full, owed in kind.*

The world had closed in from the south with paper and from the north with watchers. And now it was closing in from below, at its own pace, on feet that walked at night.

"Right," Cyrus said aloud, to the girl, to the empty hall, to whoever was listening under the floor. "Then we don't wait for it to walk to us."

She stood, and went to get the maps.
