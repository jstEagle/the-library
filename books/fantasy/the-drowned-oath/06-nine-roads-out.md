## 6. Nine Roads Out

There were nine roads out of Thornfell, and my grandfather had taught me all of them before I was tall enough to see over the guildhall's counting table.

*The Salt Stair*, he'd said, tapping the map with a pipe-stem gone soft as leather from years of handling. *The Weepers' Cut. The Hollow Bell. The Widow's Throat.* He'd named them the way other men named their children, and I had memorized them the way other children memorized prayers—without understanding, without questioning, because that was simply what an Almeida did. Nine roads, and every one of them built by hands three generations dead, cut through stone and salt and the roots of the mountain for purposes no one alive could fully explain.

Now I stood in the ruins of the guildhall with a mute child asleep against my coat and nine roads in my head, and I understood at last why my grandfather had made me learn them all.

It wasn't so we could leave.

It was so we'd know which ones would kill us.

* * *

The syndicate men came back at dusk on the second day. Three of them this time, walking up the cliff road openly, no attempt at stealth, which told me they wanted to be seen. Wanted *me* to see them. Behind them, down in the valley where Thornfell's few remaining houses huddled against the wind, lamplights moved in patterns that didn't belong to any household. Lanterns carried in sweeps. Search lines.

I watched from the loft window through a gap in the shutters, the girl's breath warm and even against my collarbone where she slept in the crook of my arm. She'd refused to let go of me since the storm night. I'd stopped pretending I minded.

The man in front was broad through the shoulders, wearing a grey oilskin that had never seen honest rain, and he stopped at the guildhall door—the door with the wards my family had paid blood and salt to lay—and studied it the way a butcher studies a carcass. Then he took something from his pocket and pressed it flat against the lintel.

The wards didn't flare. They didn't scream or burn him the way they should have.

They just... dimmed. Like a candle cupped behind a palm.

My stomach went cold. Whatever paper those men carried, it wasn't merely a claim on our debts. It was a key. Someone had sold them more than numbers out of a ledger. Someone had sold them the *grammar* of us.

"Time to go," I whispered, though she couldn't hear me, though no one could hear me but the house itself, and the house was dying.

She woke anyway. She always woke when I spoke, as if my voice were a bell rung somewhere inside her. Those pale eyes found mine in the dark, unblinking, patient in a way no child should be patient, and she reached up and touched the hollow of my throat with two fingers—the gesture she made whenever she wanted me to understand something she couldn't say.

*I know,* I told her silently. *I know.*

We went out through the cold cellar, past the racks where the last casks of salt-spirits sat gathering dust, and into the tunnel my great-grandmother had dug with her own hands when the crown's excisemen came sniffing. The Salt Stair. It descended ninety-one steps—I'd counted them a hundred times as a girl, racing my cousin Bren down in the dark—and emerged behind the waterfall on the north face of the gorge, where the spray hid the doorway and the roar hid the sound.

I paused at the top of the stairs and set my lantern down, and I listened.

That was the first lesson, and the only one that mattered: *listen before you trust a road.* Roads are like animals. They go quiet when predators are near.

The Salt Stair was silent. Too silent. In all my childhood it had never been silent—it dripped, it breathed, the mountain's groundwater sang through its walls in a thin mineral whistle. Now nothing. As if the water itself were holding its breath.

Someone had dammed the flow upstream. Someone who knew the road as well as I did.

*Not the Salt Stair, then.*

I picked up the lantern and led the girl back up, and she followed without question, bare feet making no sound on the stone, and I tried not to think about how easily she moved through these tunnels, how the dark seemed to arrange itself around her rather than against her.

* * *

The Weepers' Cut was next—a crack in the cliffside barely wide enough for a grown woman, running two hundred yards through rock that wept mineral tears year-round, emerging in the tumbled scree above the old quarry road. It was miserable going. It was meant to be. My grandfather said misery was the best guard a road could have, because misery couldn't be bribed.

Halfway through, wedged sideways in the dark with the girl ahead of me and the rock pressing against my spine like a hand, I heard the whistle.

Two notes, low then high. Repeated.

A signal. Syndicate standard—I'd heard it across a dozen border crossings in my smuggling years, the call that meant *position confirmed, hold and wait*. Which meant someone was waiting at the far end of the Cut. Which meant they weren't guessing about our roads anymore. They *knew* them.

I backed us out slow, one inch at a time, the girl moving with me as if we shared a single skeleton, and when we finally spilled back into the cellar's dark I sat down against a cask and put my face in my hands and let myself feel, for exactly ten breaths, the full weight of what was happening.

Then I stood up, because ten breaths was all grief gets in my family. We're economical people. We count everything.

Eight roads left.

* * *

The Hollow Bell nearly worked.

It ran under the riverbed itself, a brick-lined conduit from the days when Thornfell had been rich enough to move water instead of being moved by it, and it came up inside the ruined chapel of Saint Aldhelm on the far bank, where the roof had fallen in fifty years ago and the saints' faces had worn smooth as eggs. Nobody used the chapel. Nobody remembered the road. I only knew it because my grandfather had gotten drunk on my thirteenth birthday and drawn the whole route in spilled ale on the counting table, laughing, saying *this one's ours alone, Cy, this one not even your uncle knows*—

I got us through it in under an hour. The girl walked the flooded section without hesitation, water to her chin, her eyes open beneath the surface, and I tried very hard not to look at that too closely. There were things I wasn't ready to know yet. I could feel them queuing up behind my ribs like passengers at a toll gate.

We came up in the chapel as true dusk fell, blue light through the broken roof, and for one whole minute I let myself believe.

Then the dogs started.

Not hounds—not the baying of trackers on scent. This was worse: a low, rhythmic barking, evenly spaced, like a metronome. Working dogs. Dogs held on line, sweeping a grid. And beneath the barking, voices. Men calling positions to each other in the clipped cadence of professionals.

They hadn't found the Hollow Bell. But they'd guessed where it must come out, because there was nowhere else it *could* come out, and they were closing a net around the one place in the valley I'd thought was forgotten.

I pulled the girl down behind the fallen altar and pressed my eye to a gap in the masonry.

Four men in the chapel yard. Two dogs. And beyond them, on the ridge, lanterns—more than I could count, strung along the skyline like a constellation that hated me.

They weren't searching anymore. They were herding. Every road I'd tested had been watched or sealed, and now I understood that the testing itself had been the trap—they'd wanted to know which roads I'd try, and I'd shown them, road by road, like a child pointing out her hiding places to the thing under the bed.

One road left that they might not have thought of. The worst one. The one my grandfather had never drawn in ale, never spoken aloud, only once gripped my wrist hard enough to bruise and said: *If it ever comes to it, Cy, take the Widow's Throat, and don't stop for anything you hear down there. Nothing you hear is real, and none of it wants you to keep moving.*

But the Widow's Throat was six miles back the way we'd come, back toward the guildhall, back through the net.

Unless.

I looked at the girl. She looked back, and in the blue half-light her eyes caught what little color remained and threw it back silver, and I swear to you—I swear it on whatever's left of my family's name—she nodded. Before I'd said a word. Before I'd formed the thought fully myself.

She nodded like she already knew the way.

* * *

We went back through the Bell. Back under the river, through the black water, my lungs burning by the time we surfaced in the cellar among the dusty casks. And then out the front door.

Yes. The front door. The wards still hung above it, dimmed but not dead, and I gambled everything on a single truth: they expected rats in the tunnels. They did not expect the smuggler to walk out her own front door, down her own steps, into the open street, and turn west with the confidence of a woman going to market.

For four hundred yards it worked. Four hundred yards of ordinary evening, of smoke from chimneys, of a neighbor's cat slinking along a wall, of the girl's small hand swallowed inside mine. I had almost begun to breathe again when a voice came out of the alley ahead of us, mild as milk:

"Nowhere to be, mistress Almeida?"

He stepped out alone—young, maybe twenty-five, with a crossbow held low and easy and the bored expression of a man who'd done this a hundred times and found it dull. His partner would be behind us; I could hear him, boots on gravel, unhurried. They'd left the net loose right here because they'd known. Of course they'd known. The front door was the one road I'd never tested, and so it was the one they'd saved for last.

"You've got the wrong idea," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Guild's debts die with the guild. You know that. Your masters know that. This is theater."

"It's contract," he said, and shrugged, and raised the crossbow—not at me.

At the girl.

And something happened in my chest that I can only describe as a door opening onto a furnace. Not rage exactly. Rage is hot and loud and stupid. This was cold. This was arithmetic. This was the oldest ledger in my blood finally balancing itself.

His partner was eight paces behind us. The archer was twelve in front. The girl's hand tightened in mine.

I moved.

Not toward the archer—toward the partner. I heard the archer shout, heard the crossbow's string snap, felt the bolt tear through the shoulder of my coat and bury itself in the doorframe beside me, and then I was inside the partner's reach and my grandfather's skinning knife—the one I'd carried fifteen years and never once used on anything with a heartbeat—was in my hand and I put it into the soft place below his ribs, angled up, the way you gut a fish, the way my grandmother taught me to dress game when I was nine years old and thought it was only butchery.

He made a small sound. Not a scream. Smaller than that. A sound like a man remembering something.

I turned with his weight still on my blade and threw him sideways into the archer's second shot, and the bolt meant for the girl took the dying man high in the chest instead, and then I was running, dragging the girl west into the dark, and behind us the archer was shouting for the net, for the lanterns, for everyone, and the valley lit up like a festival.

* * *

We didn't stop until the Widow's Throat.

I won't tell you how we got there. Some roads you keep even from yourself. But we came down into it near midnight, that fissure in the mountain's flank where the air moves wrong and the echoes come back late, and I knelt at the entrance and washed my knife in the trickle of meltwater running down the stone, and I scrubbed, and scrubbed, and the blood wouldn't all come off, some of it had gone into the grain of the handle already, and I understood that it would never come off, that it was in the ledger now, written in a hand I recognized as my own.

The girl crouched beside me. She put her small fingers over mine on the knife's haft—not taking it away, just resting there, the way you'd rest a hand on a grieving friend's shoulder.

"He had a mother," I said. My voice sounded scraped. "Everyone does. That's the trick of it. Everyone you kill had a mother who taught them to tie their boots."

She tilted her head. And then, in that voice like water over drowned bells—the voice I'd heard only once before, in the shrine, saying *you owe me*—she said something new. Two words in the drowned tongue, soft, deliberate, and though I couldn't translate them, I felt their shape settle into me like stones into a wall.

I think they meant: *not your fault.* Or perhaps: *the debt moves.* With her, I was learning, those might be the same sentence.

Down in the valley, the lanterns were still swinging, still hunting, and somewhere behind us in the deep of the Throat, something exhaled—an echo that arrived a half-second after it should have, carrying a warmth the mountain's cold air had no business holding.

My grandfather's voice rose in my memory, drunk on nothing, sober with fear: *Nothing you hear down there is real, and none of it wants you to keep moving.*

The girl stood, faced the darkness, and smiled.

And the darkness, gentle as a tide, smiled back.
