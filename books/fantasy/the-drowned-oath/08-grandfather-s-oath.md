## 8. Grandfather's Oath

The journal did not want to be found.

Cyrus had known that from the first night, when her fingers had walked the guildhall's cold stone and stopped at a seam in the wall behind the old salt racks—a seam that had not been there when she was nine years old and hiding from her uncle's temper. The wall had grown a door the way a tree grows a knot: slowly, secretly, around whatever it wished to keep.

Now she sat cross-legged on the floor of the hidden room with a lantern turned low, because low light made the paper honest. Bright light flattered things. It gilded lies until they looked like scripture. But a guttering flame showed every water stain, every smudge of thumb-grease, every place where a hand had hesitated over the page.

The book itself was unremarkable. Calfskin gone soft as chamois, corners rounded by two hundred years of handling. No lock, no clasp, no title burned into the cover. Only the Almeida mark pressed into the leather at the center—a rope knotted through a keyhole—worn nearly smooth, as if someone had rubbed it for luck so often that the luck had finally worn out.

She had read it twice already. She would read it a hundred times more before she understood it, but even now, on this third pass, the shape of the thing was rising out of the ink like a body surfacing in dark water.

* * *

*12th of the Salt Moon, in the year of our founding.*

*We are seven men and one woman, and we are starving. The war has eaten the roads, the roads have eaten the caravans, and the caravans were all we had. Tonight I write by candlelight in the cellar of the drowned chapel, where no one prays anymore, to set down what we have done, so that if God or the mountain comes asking, there will be one honest page in all of Almeida.*

*Tomas says we should take the offer. Odile's grandmother—no, I get ahead of myself. There is a court beneath Thornfell. Every smuggler knows it the way sailors know the sea has a floor: you do not speak of it, you do not dig toward it, and you never, ever owe it anything. But Tomas went down the First Road last winter—I don't know how he found it open—and he came back changed. He came back rich.*

*"They deal fairly," he told us. "They only ask what we can spare."*

*I asked him what we could spare. He smiled at me with his new teeth and said, "Nothing yet. Something later. A promise is cheap tonight, brother."*

Cyrus stopped reading. Her breath had gone shallow without her noticing, the way it did in flooded tunnels when the air pocket began to thin.

*A promise is cheap tonight.* She could hear it in her grandfather's voice—or the voice she imagined for him, since he had died forty years before she was born and left nothing behind but this book, this guild, and this name she carried like a coin she'd never been allowed to spend.

Outside the hidden room, the guildhall creaked and settled. Wind worried the shutters. Somewhere above, in the loft where she'd laid her bedroll near the girl's, a floorboard shifted—the child turning in her sleep, murmuring in that drowned language that made the wards hum like struck glass.

Cyrus pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulders and read on.

* * *

*19th of the Salt Moon.*

*The contract is written. I have seen it. It is not parchment—it is something older, pressed flat and cured with salt, and the ink is blood, though whose blood I did not ask. The terms are these:*

*The court beneath Thornfell will open its roads to us. Not the smugglers' paths—we keep those—but the deep ways, the ones that run under borders and customs houses and the eyes of kings. Our cargo will move unseen. Our rivals will starve. In five years we will own the coast trade from here to the Salt Marches.*

*In exchange, we give them a soul.*

*I asked whose. Tomas said: "One of ours. Unborn. The court is patient; it will wait for the right one." I asked how we would know which child. He said the child would know. That it would come to us. That it would speak the old tongue without being taught.*

*I signed anyway.*

*I signed anyway, and my hand did not shake, because I was thinking of my mother's grave and the debt collectors who dug it too shallow, and I thought: let some future Almeida pay. They will be rich. Rich people can afford their souls.*

Cyrus closed the journal.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake. Instead there was a cold, precise anger settling into her chest, the kind she usually saved for men who cheated widows on cargo weights—an anger with edges, an anger that wanted names.

She gave it names. *Tomas*, first, with his new teeth and his easy smile. And then the others, the seven founders whose signatures she knew from the guild charter hanging—had hung—in the great hall, before the creditors' fire took it: *Almeida, Voss, Kettering, Marsh, the twins Ilse and Ryn, Odile's line, and old Bram Salt-Tongue*. Eight names. Eight hands. One blank space where a soul should go.

They hadn't even chosen a victim. They'd left it open. *Unborn. The court is patient.* They had sold a person who did not exist yet, who might have been anyone, any child of any generation—and then they'd built a fortune on top of that empty cradle and called it legacy.

And the name on the charter, the name passed down like an heirloom, the name she'd been proud of—

*Almeida.*

Her grandfather had signed first. Of course he had. His handwriting led the list, bold and slanting, the hand of a man certain history would thank him.

"History," Cyrus said aloud, and the word fell dead in the cold air of the hidden room.

* * *

She climbed the ladder, shuttered the lantern, and stood a while in the dark of the guildhall proper. The great hearth was cold ash. The long table where deals had been struck for six generations still bore the ring-stains of a thousand tankards. The ledger shelves stood empty, their contents burned by the syndicate's men—or so she had believed, until the hidden room revealed that her grandfather had kept a second accounting all along, a private one, written in the voice of a man trying to build himself a conscience too late.

She checked on the girl.

The child slept curled in the loft's corner with her back to the wall and her knees drawn up, the posture of someone who had learned young that doors could open from either side. In sleep she did not murmur. In sleep she was just a child—thin wrists, chapped lips, a bruise fading yellow on her shin from their flight through the passes. Cyrus had carried her half of the last mile. The girl weighed almost nothing. Souls, apparently, were lighter than they looked.

As Cyrus watched, the wards over the doorway below shivered once, faintly, like the surface of a cup of tea when a cart passes in the street. Nothing had touched them. Nothing needed to. The girl dreamed, and the old magic answered her dreams the way iron answers a lodestone.

"You knew," Cyrus whispered to her grandfather across two hundred years. "You wrote it down yourself. *It will come to us. It will speak the old tongue.* You described her. You built the trap and then you wrote down the bait, and then you died rich and let the rest of us inherit the bill."

The wind slammed a shutter somewhere. Cyrus didn't flinch.

* * *

*3rd of the Harvest Moon, fifth year of our founding.*

*We are rich. Gods forgive me, we are rich beyond what my father could have dreamed. The deep roads run true, the cargos arrive untouched, and the syndicates that laughed at us now pay us tolls. Tonight a man from the coastal houses offered to buy our routes outright for a sum I cannot write down without laughing.*

*I refused him. Of course I refused him.*

*But I confess something here, in this cellar, to this page, because the page does not judge: I have begun to listen for her. The child. Every barefoot girl who comes to the gate, I watch her mouth to see if the words come out wrong. Every storm, I stand at the threshold like a fool waiting for fate to knock.*

*Tomas tells me to stop. "You'll call her to you," he says, "by wanting her so loud." Maybe he's right. Maybe that's why I keep the journal in the old tongue sometimes, so that if she ever reads it, she will not understand what her family did.*

*No. That is a lie, and this page is supposed to be the honest one. I write it in the old tongue because I am ashamed. Because I want the debt to stay buried the way the chapel is buried, the way the court is buried, the way everything we bought with that signature stays buried under salt and stone and other people's forgetting.*

*Rich people can afford their souls. I wrote that once, and believed it.*

*I am not sure I believe it anymore. I am not sure souls work like coin. I am afraid we have not spent one soul but mortgaged every child who will ever carry this name, and that the interest compounds in the dark, below, where patient things count.*

Cyrus read that passage four times.

Then she got up, walked to the hearth, and stood looking at the cold ashes until her eyes burned—not with tears, she told herself, though the lie was a poor one—with the effort of not screaming in a house full of sleeping people.

*Mortgaged every child.* There it was. The truth under the truth. Her grandfather had thought he was selling one soul, one distant stranger, some hypothetical granddaughter three generations downstream. But oaths like this—she was beginning to understand, from the fragments the girl spoke, from the way the mountain's attention had settled over the guildhall like frost—oaths like this didn't end. They ran in the blood. Every Almeida born after that signature had been born into the ledger, marked, counted, held in reserve against a debt none of them had ever seen.

She had spent her whole life believing she was free. Free-born, road-sworn, answerable to no crown and no creed. She had sneered at bond-servants and debt-slaves and the syndicate's indentured runners, and all the while she had been collateral herself—her whole guild, her whole line, a fortune built on credit extended by something patient beneath the mountain.

And the girl. The mute, barefoot, storm-brought girl who spoke the drowned tongue and made the wards sing.

*It will come to us.*

She wasn't a stray. She wasn't an orphan the roads had washed up. She was the payment, delivered on schedule, walking up to the door her ancestors had built for her, and Cyrus had taken her in and fed her stew and started—gods help her—to love her, without knowing she was holding a bag of coin that something below considered already spent.

* * *

*Last entry. The hand is older here, shakier, the ink watery.*

*I am dying. Let the record show I know what I am dying of: not age. Age is what they will write on the marker. What I am dying of is arithmetic. Two hundred years of interest on one signature, and the principal has come due, and I can feel it coming up the deep roads toward my grandchildren's children like water rising in a mine.*

*I could burn this journal. I have held it over the candle three times now. But if I burn it, they will not know what they owe, and the not-knowing is how the court wins. Debts thrive in the dark. So I leave this page instead, and one instruction, and may whoever finds it have more courage than I did:*

*Find the original contract. It lies in the drowned chapel, under the altar, sealed in salt. Read the terms with your own eyes. And then decide—are you an Almeida, or are you a debtor? There is a difference. I waited my whole life to learn it, and learned it too late to matter.*

*Tell her I'm sorry. Whichever one she is. Tell her the man who signed her away was sorry, and sorry was worth nothing, and he knew it was worth nothing, and he was sorry anyway.*

*The candle is burning low. Below, I think I can hear counting.*

Cyrus set the journal down with exaggerated care, the way you set down a blade you very much wanted to throw.

The fury came then, finally, all at once—not the cold edged kind but a hot, roaring thing that filled her skull like floodwater filling a tunnel. Fury at Tomas with his new teeth. Fury at eight signatures. Fury most of all at the old man's trembling last hand, his *tell her I'm sorry*, his apology priced exactly what it was worth and offered anyway, as if contrition were legal tender, as if being sorry paid down principal.

He'd had a lifetime. He'd had the contract's location written in his own dying hand—*the drowned chapel, under the altar*—and he'd done nothing. Buried the truth in a hidden room behind the salt racks and died warm in his bed while the debt walked on down the generations toward a child who couldn't even speak to say what had been taken from her.

"He knew," Cyrus said to the empty hall. Her voice cracked on the second word. "He knew where it was, and he hid the map instead of using it."

Above her, the floorboard creaked again. Then small feet on the ladder-steps, soft as falling ash.

The girl stood at the edge of the loft, wrapped in the blanket Cyrus had given her, hair loose and wild, watching with those dark eyes that seemed to hold depth-perception for places much farther away than the room they were in. She looked at the journal in Cyrus's lap. She looked at Cyrus's face—at the fury there, and maybe at whatever else was underneath the fury, because children always saw that part.

Slowly, deliberately, the girl raised one hand and touched her own chest. Then she pointed down—down through the floorboards, down through the cellar, down through two hundred feet of salt-hard stone—toward the mountain's roots.

Then she said a word. Just one, in the drowned tongue, low and clear, and the wards over the door sang a single note in answer, and Cyrus felt the sound in her back teeth like a tuning fork pressed to bone.

She didn't know the word. But she was beginning to learn the grammar of this place, and the grammar of gestures, and the sentence assembled itself in her chest with terrible clarity:

*It knows you're reading.*

The girl held out her small hand—not for comfort, Cyrus realized. For the journal. For the pages her grandfather had tried to hide in the old tongue, the pages meant to be unreadable to her.

She had read them anyway. Somehow, she had always been able to read them.

Cyrus looked at the small outstretched hand, and at the drowned chapel waiting under the mountain with its salt-sealed altar, and understood that the next road—the only road left—ran straight down through everything her family had buried.

"We're going to need Odile," she said, and reached for the maps.
