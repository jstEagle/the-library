## 10. Flooded Mine, Burning Lantern

The mine was called the Widow's Throat, and Cyrus had sworn at nineteen never to go back into it.

She broke the oath on a Tuesday, in the rain, with a syndicate's hounds baying somewhere behind the tree line and a mute girl's hand locked around two of her fingers like a shackle made of warmth.

"There are nine roads out of Thornfell," she'd told the child that first night, when the wards over the guildhall door had flared silver at the girl's approach. "I've run six of them. The other three don't exist anymore."

That had been true, mostly. What she hadn't said—what she was only admitting now, standing at the mouth of the Widow's Throat with water already whispering somewhere below—was that one of those three roads didn't exist because it had been drowned on purpose.

* * *

They had come down out of the high passes three days ago, moving by night, sleeping in the hollows beneath fallen pines where the frost couldn't reach them. The girl—Sera, though she still gave no name aloud; Cyrus had chosen the name the way you choose a coat for someone else to wear—had not spoken since the shrine. Since *you owe me*, spoken clear as a bell into air that should have swallowed it.

But she watched. That was the thing about Sera that unsettled Cyrus most: she watched everything with the patience of something that had been watching for a very long time. When Cyrus checked their backtrail, Sera checked hers. When Cyrus counted lantern oil, small fingers tapped the count against her knee—one tap per measure, precise as an abacus. And always, always, her eyes went north. Toward the mountain. Thornfell proper, the old peak, the one whose roots went down further than any kingdom's claim.

Cyrus told herself it was fear. Children look toward the thing they're afraid of.

She was beginning to suspect it wasn't fear at all.

* * *

The Widow's Throat had been a copper seam once, before the Almeidas bought the mountain's permission to dig it. Her grandfather's journal—the pages she'd pulled from under the burned ledgers' ash, wrapped in oilcloth and carried against her ribs like a second heart—mentioned it exactly twice. Once as a route. Once as a warning.

*The Throat drinks,* he'd written, in his cramped, sideways hand. *We paid the water to let us pass, and the water remembers what we paid. Do not take the Throat unless the Throat is the last road.*

It was the last road. Vess had nearly taken them at the ford two nights past—a lantern swinging through the dark, a whistle splitting the rain, and Cyrus had put out her own light and waded upstream with the girl on her hip until her legs went numb, while behind them men who moved like wolves quartered the bank. After that, the open roads were dead to them. Every bridge watched, every pass staked. The syndicate wanted its collateral, and collateral, Cyrus was learning, was a word that meant *a person*.

So: the Throat.

She stood at its mouth now. It was less a mine entrance than a wound in the hillside—timbers rotted to black lace, a lintel stone carved with the old guild mark, the crossed hooks and the wave, worn nearly smooth. Rain ran down the rock above it in ropes. From inside came a sound she remembered from her nightmares: water moving in the dark, patient, enormous, the sound of a thing breathing through stone.

"Last road," she said aloud, and didn't know if she was telling Sera or herself.

The girl looked up at her. In the grey light her eyes were the color of deep ice, and there was something in them—not fear, not pleading—that made Cyrus think of the moment before a tide turns.

Then Sera stepped forward and touched the lintel stone, flat-palmed, the way you'd touch a horse to calm it.

The sound of the water changed. Just slightly. Just enough.

Cyrus's skin crawled. "Right," she said. "Let's not keep it waiting, then."

* * *

Inside, the world became the lantern.

She'd brought three. One burned now, hung from a hook on her pack's strap, throwing a coin of amber light that turned the tunnel walls into wet amber and shadow. The air tasted of copper and cold iron and something older underneath—mineral, mineral, and then, faintly, salt. Sea-salt, miles inland, miles underground. The Throat remembering the ocean it had been, a hundred million years before anyone swore anything to anyone.

They walked the main adit for an hour, boots splashing through shallows that rose from ankle-deep to mid-calf. Cyrus navigated by memory and by the journal both—her grandfather's map of the Throat was drawn in the margins of a page headed *routes the guild does not speak of*, and it showed a junction two hours in where the flooded gallery branched: left toward the old drainage adit, which surfaced in a ravine four miles east; right, down, toward workings no map had ever charted, toward the mountain's root.

Left, then. Always left. She said it to herself like a prayer. *Left, left, left.*

At the junction, the water was waist-high and moving.

Not flowing—moving. It slid past them in the dark with a slow, deliberate current, all of it heading right, down, toward the deep galleries. As if the mine were swallowing. As if somewhere below, a throat were working.

Sera stopped walking. Her grip on Cyrus's hand tightened—not in fear. In attention. The way a dog points.

"What is it?" Cyrus whispered, though whispering in a place like this felt like blasphemy. Sound carried strangely here; her voice came back to her off the water's surface, flattened, wrong.

The girl turned her face toward the right-hand passage. Then, slowly, deliberately, she shook her head.

"No," Cyrus agreed. "We're going left." She lifted the lantern. "Left, see? This way."

Sera looked at the left passage. Looked at the water. Looked back at Cyrus with an expression of such grave, ancient pity that Cyrus's stomach dropped.

And then the mountain groaned.

* * *

It started as a sound like a door too large to imagine being opened somewhere far below—a long, grinding exhalation of stone on stone. Dust sifted down through the lantern light, gold motes turning in the amber. The water around their waists shivered into rings.

Then the far wall of the junction—the wall between them and the left-hand passage—cracked.

Not cracked. *Exhaled.* A section of timbering the size of a barn door folded inward with a shriek of nails leaving wood, and behind it the packed rubble of a century slumped, slithered, and ran like sand poured from a fist. The collapse took thirty seconds. It sounded like the end of the world heard through a pillow.

When the dust began to settle, the left-hand passage was gone. Sealed. Four feet of mountain where a road had been.

Cyrus stood in chest-deep water holding a lantern with a steady hand, because her hands had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere very calm and very cold, and she heard herself say, "Well. That's decided, then."

Sera tugged her sleeve. Pointed right. Down.

"I know," Cyrus said. "I know. But listen to me—" She crouched in the floodwater, bringing her face level with the girl's, and made her voice the voice she used for frightened new runners on their first night run. Steady. Warm. Lying. "Whatever's down there, whatever's calling you—we're going to walk through it, and we're going to come out the other side, and I am going to buy you a coat with real buttons. Do you hear me? Real buttons. Brass ones."

Something flickered across the child's face. Almost a smile. Almost grief.

She pointed down again.

They went down.

* * *

The deep galleries were older than copper. Cyrus could feel it in the way the tunnels changed—the cuts grew cruder, then stranger, then not cuts at all but bores so smooth they might have been melted rather than mined, passages round as throats, and the guild-mark timbers gave way to pillars of living stone that her lantern light couldn't find the tops of. The salt smell thickened. The water rose.

By the time she understood how badly wrong the numbers had gone, it was at her sternum.

Her grandfather's journal had said the Throat drank. She'd thought it a figure of speech. Smugglers' talk, like calling a cliff the Widow's Tooth or a current the Devil's Comb. But the water in these lower galleries wasn't standing water. It was rising water, and it was rising fast—she could mark it on the tunnel walls, black tidelines climbing as they watched, and the current that had been sliding past them at the junction now pulled at her legs, dragging them both gently, patiently, irresistibly downward and inward.

Toward the mountain. Everything here flowed toward the mountain.

"Sera." Her voice came out thin. "Sera, I need you to hold on to me and not let go. Can you do that? Arms around my neck. Like crossing the ford. You remember."

The girl climbed her like a drowning sailor climbs rigging, which was, Cyrus reflected with a hysteria she kept firmly leashed, more or less what was happening. Lantern held high in her left hand, pack dragging her shoulders down, child clamped to her front, wading through water that reached her collarbones and was not stopping.

There would be air pockets. Old mines had them—domes in the rock where trapped gas gathered, where the water couldn't follow. Her grandfather's map marked three in the deep galleries. If she could reach one—

The tunnel ahead bent, and the water deepened, and the lantern flame shrank to a blue bead, and Cyrus did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do for ten minutes: the ceiling was dropping faster than the floor. There was no dome close enough. There was maybe forty yards of passage left before the gap between water and stone closed to nothing, and she was a strong swimmer, but not with a pack and a child and a current pulling the wrong way, and even if she made it, even if—

The lantern went out.

* * *

Darkness. Total. The kind of dark that has weight, that presses on the eyeballs like thumbs. The water took her fully as the tunnel floor fell away beneath her boots, and the current took her too, tumbling her end over end, and the last coherent thought she had was absurdly practical: *the journal's oilcloth-wrapped, the journal will survive, the journal will survive, the journal—*

Something hit her head. Stone, probably. Or the pack. The world went soft at the edges.

And in the softness, in the roaring dark, she heard—felt—singing.

No. Not singing. Speaking. The drowned tongue, the language no living kingdom used, pouring out of the small body pressed against hers in syllables she couldn't parse but her bones apparently could, because every bone she owned went quiet. The water's roar seemed to lean in and listen.

The current stopped.

Not slowed. *Stopped.* Mid-tumble, mid-drowning, Cyrus hung in water gone suddenly still as glass, and light bloomed around her—pale, sourceless, green-gold, the color of the wards over the guildhall door—and she saw, dimly, the child in her arms with her eyes closed and her small hands spread flat against the water itself, palms down, the way you'd calm a spooked animal. The way she'd calmed the lintel stone.

The water was listening to her.

Around them, in that impossible glow, Cyrus saw the tunnel as she'd never have seen it with a hundred lanterns: the bore smoothing into worked stone, worked stone smoothing into something carved, carvings of waves and hooks and figures with crowns of kelp, and everywhere, everywhere, the same mark her grandfather had signed at the bottom of the contract page. And the water—hundreds of tons of it, pressing in from every side—held itself apart from the girl like a crowd making room for royalty. It did not touch her. It curved around her body in a shell of empty space, and Cyrus floated inside that shell, inside the curve of the child's protection, breathing air that shouldn't have been there, and wept without deciding to.

*Up,* she thought, dazedly, at the girl. At whatever the girl was. *Please. Up.*

Sera opened her eyes. They glowed faintly, the same green-gold, and for one vertiginous moment Cyrus looked into them and saw depth—real depth, fathomless, the look-down-you-feel from a boat's edge—and understood with total, wordless certainty that the thing looking out of this child's face was old. Older than the guild. Older than the mountain's name. Something that had been promised back, and knew it, and had been walking toward the promise for a very long time.

Then the moment passed, and the eyes were a child's again, tired, and the light dimmed to a gentle luminescence clinging to her skin like frost, and she tugged Cyrus's collar. Up. Yes. Up.

They rose together through still water, the girl towing her with strength no eight-year-old body should have had, along the carved tunnel, up and up, until the ceiling broke into an air dome—her grandfather's third marked pocket, though no map had ever shown it glowing—and Cyrus hauled them both onto a shelf of dry stone and lay there gasping like landed fish, one arm locked around the child, the lantern dead beside her, the whole black cathedral of the flooded mine humming faintly around them.

* * *

She got the second lantern lit eventually. Hands shaking so hard it took three strikes of the flint. Its small warm circle of light was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

Sera sat beside her, wringing out her hair, pale as paper, exhausted in a way that went deeper than muscle. The glow had faded from her skin entirely. She looked, for the moment, like exactly what she appeared to be: a barefoot girl who had nearly drowned.

"You saved us," Cyrus said. Her voice sounded scraped. "Back there. The water—it stopped. It *listened* to you."

The girl said nothing. She was looking at her own hands, turning them over, studying the palms as if checking whether they still belonged to her.

"How?" Cyrus asked, and then, because silence answered nothing: "What are you getting *stronger* at, Sera? Because that's what this looks like. Back at the hall, the wards answered you. At the shrine, the water rose around you. Here—" she gestured helplessly at the still, listening dark beyond the lantern's reach— "here you held back a flood. Each time it's bigger. Each time we get closer to—" She stopped. She'd been avoiding saying it. Saying it made it a destination instead of a dread. "Closer to the mountain."

Sera looked up at her. And slowly, deliberately, she raised one small hand and pointed—not at the tunnel ahead, not at the water below—but *down*. Through the stone shelf they sat on. Through miles of it. Down.

Cyrus felt the meaning land in her chest like a dropped anchor.

"It knows we're coming," she said quietly. "Doesn't it. Whatever's down there. It can feel you getting closer, and you're—what? Filling back up? Remembering?" She laughed, a short, broken sound with no humor in it. "Two hundred years my family kept this ledger. Two hundred years of tolls and routes and secrets, and nobody ever wrote down what happens to the payment on the road to collection."

The girl reached out and touched Cyrus's wrist. Gently. Then she did something she had never done: she took Cyrus's hand, turned it palm-up, and traced a shape in it with one finger. Slowly, so it could be followed. Three strokes.

A wave. A hook. A wave again.

The guild mark. But drawn the old way—drawn the way it appeared in the carvings below, the way it must have been drawn before it was ever a smuggler's brand. Drawn as if it meant something else originally. Something older.

"That's ours," Cyrus whispered. "That's the Almeida mark. How do you know our mark?"

Sera held her gaze, and in the lantern light her deep-ice eyes were patient and sad and unbearably young all at once, and she tapped Cyrus's palm three times, right over the fading ghost of the traced sign.

Once for the wave. Once for the hook. Once for the wave.

Then she pointed down again, and this time, faint but unmistakable, rising through the stone, through the still black water, from the direction of the mountain's drowned root, Cyrus heard it too:

A knocking. Slow. Rhythmic. Patient as interest accruing.

Something far below had begun counting.
