## 11. A Name in the Drowned Tongue

There is a word for what I am, and it is not *girl*.

It comes to me at the bottom of sleep, when the fire has burned down to its red eye and the smuggler woman—Cyrus, Cyrus Almeida, who smells of tar and cold iron and something older underneath, something like wet stone—is breathing slow across the camp. In that hour between one dark and the next dark, the word rises in me like a bubble through black water. It has no letters. It is made of pressure and salt and the memory of doors.

I do not say it aloud. If I say it aloud, she will hear it, and then she will know what sleeps beside her fire, and she will run, or worse, she will stay, because that is the kind of person she is, and either way something will break.

So I hold the word behind my teeth, where it tastes of copper and tide.

* * *

I remember water the way other children remember their mother's voice.

Not drowning. Drowning is an ending, and what I remember goes on and on. I remember being *in* the water the way a coin is in a purse: held, carried, counted. I remember currents with names. I remember a river so old it had forgotten it was ever rain, and I knew its name, and when I spoke it the river shivered from bank to bank like a horse hearing its master's whistle.

That was before. Before the small hands. Before the mouth that only makes the shapes of words instead of the true ones.

Before they folded me up small and put me where I am.

* * *

Here is what I know about my body: it is borrowed, or it is new, or it is both, and I cannot tell which frightens me more.

The hands are small. They are chapped at the knuckles from the cold roads we have walked. When I look at them too long, they begin to seem wrong—not wrong the way a broken thing is wrong, but wrong the way a glove is wrong when you realize there was never a hand inside it, only your own hand pretending. The nails are bitten. There is a scar on the left palm, thin as a hair, that I do not remember earning, and some nights I press my thumb against it hoping it will open into a memory, and it never does, and I am almost grateful.

But under the skin, deeper than blood, there is a weight. It sits behind my sternum like a stone dropped down a well, and I can feel it falling still, always falling, never landing. When Cyrus sleeps, I put her hand over that place sometimes—just resting, just testing—and once, half-dreaming, she murmured, *cold*, and pulled her hand back, and turned away from me toward the fire.

She is not wrong. That place is cold. It is cold the way the deep places are cold, the places the sun has never argued with.

* * *

The dreams come in fragments now, and I have learned not to fight them, because fighting them only makes them sharp.

*A hall of pillars, and the pillars are not stone. They are the legs of drowned ships, grown over with white coral like candlewax, and between them swim the courtiers of the court beneath the mountain, and they are beautiful the way eels are beautiful, and they are singing.*

*I am standing among them. I am tall. My hands—my real hands, my first hands—are long-fingered and webbed at the base, and I am holding a ledger bound in grey leather, and every page of it is a name, and every name is a debt, and I am reading them aloud in the old tongue, and each name I read makes a bell somewhere toll once, low, like a heart deciding.*

*And a voice beside me—the Oathkeeper's voice, patient as erosion—says: read the next one. And I read the next one. And the bell tolls. And I understand, in the dream, that I have been reading for a very long time, and that the book is very long, and that this is my office, my honor, my chain.*

*Then the dream turns, the way water turns, and I see the last page. It is blank except for one line, written in a hand I now recognize because I have seen it in Cyrus's grandfather's journal, traced in her lantern light while she thought I slept:*

*Soul-price, paid in full, owed in kind.*

*And beneath it, a date. And beneath the date, a signature. And the signature is not mine.*

*But the name above it is.*

* * *

I wake with salt on my lips though there is no sea within a hundred miles.

Cyrus is already awake, feeding the fire, and she looks at me the way she does in the mornings—quick, assessing, guilty, fond, all four at once, which should be impossible on one face but she manages it. "Bad dreams?" she asks. Her voice is rough as rope.

I shake my head. It is easier than the truth. The truth would take the drowned tongue, and the drowned tongue is not for daylight, and besides—if I speak it, I will tell her things she is not ready to carry. She carries enough. I have watched her count her coins at night with lips moving, watched her touch the burned edge of the guild ledger like touching a wound to be sure it is still there. She thinks she inherited a debt. She does not yet know she inherited a *story*, and stories are heavier, and they bite.

"Eat," she says, and pushes dried fish at me, and I eat, because this body is hungry the way bodies are, and that hunger at least is honest.

* * *

In the drowned tongue, there is no word for *orphan*.

This took me days to understand, walking beside Cyrus on the high road, listening to the wind worry the pines. Every language I know—the small one, the surface one, the one with its soft round words—I learned after. But the drowned tongue I did not learn. It was poured into me, or I was poured out of it, and in it, a child without parents is called by a phrase that means, literally, *not yet claimed*. As if all children belong to someone by default, and orphanhood is merely a gap in the paperwork.

When I understood this, I had to sit down on a milestone and breathe until the shaking stopped, because I remembered—suddenly, wholly, the way ice remembers being water—that in the hall of coral pillars, there is a shelf. A long shelf, cut into the living rock, and on the shelf stand jars, hundreds of jars, glass gone green with age, and in each jar swims a small light. And the lights are children. Not-yet-claimed children. Children sold forward by grandfathers and guildmasters and frightened kings, payments promised against futures that had not happened yet, and the court keeps them the way a miser keeps coins: safe, dark, and counted nightly.

I stood on that shelf once. I think I stood on that shelf once.

I think I was taken down from it.

I think that is why I am here, in this small cold body, walking these bright loud roads with a smuggler who feeds me fish and teaches me which stars are lies.

* * *

Some nights I try to find my first name.

I lie still and go down into myself the way a diver goes down, past the smallness, past the hunger, past the borrowed heartbeat, down to where the well-stone falls and falls. And there, at the bottom, there is a door. The door is old oak, iron-banded, and it is underwater, and it does not open. But through the gap beneath it, light comes out—not warm light, but the pale green-gold of deep water where the sun has given up trying and something else glows instead.

Behind that door is my name.

I know this the way I know the scar on my palm. And twice—only twice—I have pressed against that door, and both times, from the far side, something pressed back. Gently. Patiently. The way the Oathkeeper waits. The way two hundred years can lean against a single plank without hurrying.

And a voice came through the gap, not in words but in pressure, in meaning, and it said:

*Soon.*

Both times I woke with my hands over my mouth and Cyrus's knife drawn in her sleep, pointed at nothing, pointed at me, and I lay in the dark loving her and fearing her and fearing myself more, and waiting for morning like a rope thrown to a drowning woman.

Which is funny, in the old tongue. There is no word for drowning, either. There is only *going home the long way*.

* * *

We come down off the passes into a valley where a river runs wide and brown and quick, and Cyrus stops at the crest and looks at it a long time, and I know why before she says it. The Ferryman. She told me about him on the mountain, in her flat smuggler's voice, the voice she uses for prices: he takes memories. That is his toll. Everyone pays, and everyone chooses what to lose, and everyone loses something they didn't mean to choose.

I looked at the river and the river looked back.

I know this river. I named it once—or it named itself through me, which in the old tongue is the same thing. Its true name is a sound like a gate swinging shut underwater, and when I let a little of that name rise toward my teeth, the river went smooth and silent from bank to bank, all its brown hurry stilled, and every bird in the reeds stopped singing at once.

Cyrus felt it. She didn't see; her back was to the water. But she went still the way animals go still, and her hand found her knife, and she said, quietly, without turning: "Don't."

Not *what was that*. Not *how did you do that*. Just *don't*. Because she is learning what I am piece by piece, and she is choosing, every day, to keep walking beside it anyway, and I do not know if that is love or stubbornness or the same thing wearing different coats.

"I'm sorry," I said, in the small language, and meant it, and the river started moving again, and the birds remembered their songs.

"It's not you I'm worried about," she said, which was a lie, kindly meant, and we walked down toward the ferry together, and I kept my mouth shut around the names.

* * *

But here is the thing I have not told her. Here is the thing that sits in me colder than the well-stone, colder than the shelf of jars, colder even than the door.

The night before last, in the fragment-dream, the hall of pillars changed.

The courtiers were not swimming idly. They were arranged—ranked, ranked the way creditors rank—and at the far end, past the throne of braided anchors and whalebone, the Oathkeeper stood before a great slab of slate, and on the slate was written a number, and the number was counting down. Not days. Deeper than days. Tides. Heartbeats of the mountain itself, the slow systole of stone, and each time the mountain breathed, the number grew smaller.

And the courtiers were not looking at the slate.

They were looking *up*. All of them, at once, heads tilted, eyes like drowned lamps, looking up through fathoms of rock and soil and root, looking along the roads, looking at—

Me.

They have known where I am since the storm. Since the wards over the guildhall door answered me like dogs answering their master's whistle. Since the night a barefoot child stood on a smuggler's threshold and the old stones remembered her, and Cyrus Almeida took her in out of pity, out of grief, out of that terrible kindness of hers, never guessing that the door had not opened for a stray.

It opened for a return.

*Promised back.* That is the phrase. It is in the journal she reads by lamplight, thinking I sleep; I hear the pages turn, I smell the old ink, and once I heard her whisper those three words aloud—*promised back, promised back*—and I lay in the dark and understood that she and I are reading the same book from opposite ends. She is reading forward, hunting the crime. I am remembering backward, being the price.

And the horror, the true horror, the one that makes the small hands shake at night where she cannot see: I cannot remember whether I mind.

There was a self once, tall and webbed and robed in the grey of offices, who read names from a ledger and tolled bells and did not weep, because in the drowned tongue there is also no word for *mercy*—there is only *the tide going out*, which happens whether or not anyone consents. Was that me? Is that me still, folded small, waiting? Or am I the child, the true one, the stolen one, and the tall reader is the mask they pressed onto my face?

I don't know. That is the worst of it. I reach for myself and find two shapes, and both of them fit my hand.

* * *

Tonight the fire is low and Cyrus sleeps with her boots on, as she has since the syndicate's riders crossed our backtrail, and I sit up watching the coals breathe, and I make a decision, or a decision makes me, I no longer know the difference.

I go down. Past the hunger, past the heartbeat, down to the door under the water. I do not push this time. I kneel at the gap and I put my mouth to it, to the cold light seeping through, and I whisper—in the small soft surface language, the language of fish and fires and dried apples and a woman teaching me stars—the only sentence I am sure belongs to *me*, whoever me is:

"My name is not yours to collect."

The silence on the other side stretches. Long. Longer. The patience of stone, the patience of ledgers, the patience of a creditor who has waited two hundred years and budgets eternity.

Then, through the gap, gentle as silt settling, the answer comes—not in sound, in *certainty*, laid into me like a seal pressed into wax:

*Child. We do not collect you.*

*We are collecting what was promised. And you were the promise.*

*Ask her grandfather's ghost what he sold. Ask the ledger what line your name sits on. Ask the woman who shelters you why her guild still stands, and on whose soul the interest is paid.*

*You have seven tides, little collateral. Then the mountain breathes in.*

The door's light gutters out. The well-stone lands, at last, at the bottom of me, and the impact rings through every borrowed bone.

Above, across the dead coals, Cyrus stirs and murmurs a name in her sleep—a man's name, old-fashioned, a founder's name—and her hand closes around the charred corner of the burned ledger she still carries like a relic.

And far below us both, beneath the roots of the mountain, I feel the first tide of the seven begin to turn.
