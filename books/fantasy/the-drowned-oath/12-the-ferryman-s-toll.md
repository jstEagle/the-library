## 12. The Ferryman's Toll

The river found them before dawn, the way rivers do in the high country—announced first by sound, a low grinding hush of water over stone, then by smell, cold iron and wet moss, and only last by sight: a black ribbon unspooling through the grey light, too wide to jump, too deep to ford, too honest in its noise to be anything but what it was.

Cyrus stood at the bank with the girl's hand wrapped in hers and felt something in her chest loosen and tighten at once. The Vell. Her grandfather's maps called it the Vell, though every smuggler who ever worked its banks had another name for it—the Long Toll, the Widow's Spine, the Road That Drinks. It ran out of the mountains like a vein let open, and everything it carried came from somewhere deep and old and unwilling.

"We cross here," she said, and heard how thin her voice sounded against all that water.

The girl looked up at her. In the days since the mine—since the water had parted around her small body like a congregation making way for a bride—she had not spoken again, not even in that drowned tongue that made Cyrus's teeth ache to hear it. But she watched the river with an expression Cyrus was beginning to know well: not fear. Recognition. The look of someone greeting a relative they did not like.

*You know this place,* Cyrus thought. *Some part of you has been here before.*

She did not say it aloud. There were things she was saving her voice for, and questions were among them.

* * *

The ferryman came when the sky went from grey to the color of old pewter, and he came the way the stories said he would, which was somehow worse than if he hadn't.

His boat was long and shallow, tarred black, with no lantern at prow or stern, and it did not so much arrive as become distinguishable from the river itself—a darker darkness resolving into planks and a figure standing at the stern with a pole that never seemed to touch bottom. He wore oilskins gone stiff with age, a hat whose brim dripped constantly though no rain fell, and when the boat slid against the bank, Cyrus saw that his face was the kind of face you forgot while looking at it. Not blank. Just unremarkable in the specific way of a man who had been paid, long ago, to have no features worth remembering.

"Almeida," he said. His voice was gravel rolled in wool.

Cyrus's hand went to her knife out of pure reflex, and just as purely, she made herself take it away again. "You know my name."

"I know the guild." He gestured with the pole, a slow sweep that took in the whole eastern bank, the hills beyond, the drowned mines two valleys back where they had nearly died. "Your people crossed here for six generations. Your grandfather crossed eleven times. I remember each crossing." A pause, and something moved behind the forgettable face—not quite a smile. "I remember everything. That is the trouble with me."

"The toll," Cyrus said. "Coin?"

He laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as a lock rusting shut. "Coin. Yes. Your guild paid coin once, generations back, when coin meant something to men like me." He shook his head, and water flew from the brim of his hat in a fine spray that smelled, faintly and horribly, of the shrine—that drowned chapel place where the girl had spoken her first clear words. *You owe me.* "The river took coin until the river learned there were richer things in a pocket than silver."

"What does it want now?"

"What it always wants." He stepped aside, one arm sweeping toward the boat's interior, and Cyrus saw that the benches were worn smooth in the shapes of many sitters, generations of sitters, their weight pressed into the wood like fossils. "Passage costs memory. Small crossings, small memories. The color of a door you loved as a child. The taste of a meal you'll never eat again. But—" and here the forgettable face turned fully toward her, and for one instant it was not forgettable at all, it was ancient and patient and hungry in the way of stone, "—you are not making a small crossing, Almeida. You are running. You are carrying cargo the mountain itself has marked. And the syndicate's hounds are no more than half a day behind you, because I have already told them where you slept."

The cold that went through Cyrus then had nothing to do with the river wind.

"You told them."

"I tell everyone everything. That is my function. I am not your enemy, smuggler. I am the road. Roads do not choose who walks them." He tilted his head. "But roads charge. And yours will be expensive."

Behind her, the girl tugged at Cyrus's sleeve. When Cyrus glanced down, the child was staring at the ferryman with that same unwelcoming recognition, and her lips were moving—shaping silent syllables in the drowned tongue—and the ferryman, for the first time, went still. Perfectly still, the way water goes still over something very deep.

"No," he said quietly. "Don't speak that here. Not on my boat. The river remembers that language, and it answers it, and I am too old to hold the banks closed if they start talking back."

The girl stopped. But she did not look away, and neither did he, and Cyrus understood that some negotiation had just occurred above her head, in a currency she could not count.

"How expensive?" Cyrus asked.

"For you? For the two of you, across, ahead of the hounds, with the wards of the far bank sealed behind you?" The ferryman held up one hand and began, horribly, to count on fingers that left faint trails in the air, like fingers drawn through fog. "I could take the storm your father carried you through when you were four. I could take the name of the first person you ever lied to successfully. I could take your grief for your uncle—fresh, still warm, excellent quality." Each item hung in the air between them, visible almost, tangible almost, goods on a shelf. "Or I could take something larger and leave the rest of you intact. One memory. Significant. Load-bearing. The kind a life is built around."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you swim. People swim. Some of them even reach the other side." He smiled again, that almost-smile. "The river keeps what it catches. You've seen what it keeps."

She had. She thought of the drowned shrine, the shapes beneath the green water that might have been statues and might not. She thought of the flooded mine, the hands she'd imagined in the dark water, reaching with such patience.

* * *

They walked the bank while she thought, because she would not decide this standing in front of him. The girl kept pace without being asked, bare feet silent on the stones, and after a quarter mile of silence Cyrus heard herself talking—low, half to herself, the way she talked to horses and to the dead.

"My mother used to sing when she worked the ledgers. Off-key. Terribly off-key. Uncle Brenn used to throw corks at her." She stopped walking. The river ground and hushed beside them. "I don't remember her face anymore. Isn't that strange? I remember the singing. I remember the corks. I remember her hands—there was a scar across the left thumb, from a canning knife. But her face—" She pressed her palm flat against her own cheek, as if the memory might be stored there, behind the skin, reachable. "It's like a room with the lamp out. I know the furniture's in there. I can't see it."

The girl watched her with those eyes that were too old and too young at once. Then, slowly, she reached up and touched Cyrus's cheek where Cyrus's own hand lay, and her fingers were cold as the river, and for a moment—only a moment—Cyrus felt something press against the inside of her skull. Not pain. Presence. Like someone standing at a door, asking politely to be let in.

And in that moment she saw it. The lamp lit. The room furnished.

Her mother's face.

Grey eyes, nearly the same grey as her own but warmer, shot through with amber near the pupil. A jaw a little softer than Cyrus had ever imagined for herself. Laugh lines already cut deep at thirty, because she had been a woman who laughed. A small mole beneath the right ear. And the expression—the expression was the worst and best of it, because it was not solemn, it was not tender in any photograph's way; her mother was looking at her the way you look at someone mid-joke, mouth tilting, eyes bright with the punchline she was about to ruin by laughing first.

Cyrus made a sound she had not made since the night she found her uncle cold in the guildhall. She knelt down on the riverbank stones and pulled the girl against her and held on, and the child held back with those strong, strange, cold hands, and the river went on grinding its stones beside them like a patient creditor.

When she finally stood, her eyes were dry. Mostly.

"That's the memory," she said, to the girl, to herself, to the waiting dark downstream. "That's the load-bearing one."

The girl shook her head, hard, twice. No. Her small hands came up and pushed against Cyrus's chest—not hard, but insistent—and then she pointed upstream, back toward the ferryman, and then at her own chest, and then she opened her mouth and produced one word, hoarse and cracked, in plain trade-tongue:

"Me."

"No." Cyrus said it fast, before the fear could finish forming. "No. Whatever you're offering, he doesn't want it. He wants *mine*. And I'm not trading you, girl. I'm not trading anyone. Not ever. That's the whole—" She stopped. Swallowed. *That's the whole point,* she had been about to say. *That's why we're running.* Somewhere beneath the mountain, a contract waited with a child's soul written into it as payment, signed by dead men who had traded someone exactly the way she was now refusing to trade. "That's the whole reason," she finished, quieter. "Understand me? Nobody gets sold. Not while I'm the guild."

The girl searched her face for a long time. Then she nodded once, slow, and Cyrus had the uncomfortable sense of having passed some test she hadn't known she was sitting.

* * *

The crossing cost more than she expected, because of course it did. Nothing in her family's history had ever cost what it was priced at.

"One memory," the ferryman agreed, when she named it. "Significant. Load-bearing." He extended his hand, palm up, over the gunwale. "Place it here."

"How?"

"You know how. Everyone knows how. You simply give it, the way you give a secret, the way you give a confession. The river does the rest." His palm waited, pale as fish belly. "I will warn you once, Almeida, because your grandfather paid me courtesy once, long ago, and courtesy deserves courtesy. What you give, you will not miss—you cannot miss what is no longer anywhere. But others may keep it. Others may carry it. And sometimes, on quiet nights, the river gives things back. Not always to the same people. Not always whole."

Cyrus looked at her hand. She thought of the room with the lamp out, and how, an hour ago, it had been lit—how she had seen the amber in her mother's eyes, the mole beneath the ear, the laugh arriving before the joke ended. She thought about how, in a few minutes, the lamp would go out again, and this time she would not even know there had been a room.

*Survival was never free,* her grandfather's journal had said, in a different context, in a hand that shook. *Someone always pays. Pray it is you and not the ones you love.*

"Well," she said aloud, to no one, to the river, to the dead. "It's me."

She took the ferryman's hand.

Giving it felt like exhaling after holding breath too long—one long, involuntary release, and something flowed out of her along her arm, warm and specific and shaped like a woman laughing. She felt it pass. She felt the space where it had been close up behind it, smooth and seamless, like water closing over a dropped stone, and she knew—with the eerie certainty of the newly robbed—that she would not feel the absence. That was the mercy and the horror of it both.

"There," the ferryman said softly, and folded his fingers closed around whatever he held. "Paid in full."

"Did you—" Cyrus stopped. Her own voice sounded distant to her. "What did I give you?"

"A memory," he said. "Of a face." And something in the way he said it—something almost gentle, almost grieving—told her that he knew exactly which face, and that he would keep it carefully, the way a man keeps the only flower ever given him.

The girl was crying. Silently, without changing expression, tears simply falling down her small face as she stared at Cyrus, and Cyrus wiped them away with her thumb and said, "What? What is it, little one?" and the girl only shook her head and climbed into the boat, and sat very straight, and would not look at the ferryman for the entire crossing.

* * *

The river carried them like a held breath.

There was no splash of the pole, no creak of timber, no sound at all except the water sliding past the hull and, far below, a deeper sound—rhythmic, vast, patient—that might have been the river's heart or might have been something the river flowed over. Cyrus sat with the girl tucked against her side and watched the banks slide by, dark walls of stone and pine, and tried to feel the hole where the memory had been.

She couldn't. That was the terrible elegance of it. She searched for her mother and found—nothing missing. A mother-shaped quiet, complete in itself, a woman who had sung off-key and thrown nothing and had no face at all, a woman who was somehow both present and erased, like a figure painted out of a fresco leaving only the outline of where she'd stood.

"She had a scar," Cyrus said suddenly, aloud, gripping the gunwale. "Left thumb. Canning knife. I remember that much—I remember—" But even as she said it, she felt the details going soft, going generic, the scar becoming *a* scar, becoming *some* knife, becoming a story about a woman instead of the woman. The river's interest accrued. Even what remained of her mother was being spent, drop by drop, on the crossing.

The girl caught her hand. Held it. And hummed—three notes, low, wavering, deliberately, achingly off-key.

Cyrus went still.

"Where did you hear that?" she whispered.

The girl shrugged, the smallest shrug in the world, and hummed the three notes again, and Cyrus understood that somewhere in that strange, drowned, layered mind was a copy of the song—maybe more than the song—and that the child had chosen, without being asked, to pay a tithe of her own. To keep one bar of it alive in the world, off-key, imperfect, but *hers*, so that Cyrus would lose the memory and not the music entirely.

Cyrus did not cry. She was a smuggler, and smugglers weep on their own time. But she held the girl's hand all the way across, and when the far bank rose up out of the dark—black rock, black pines, and above them, barely visible in the starless east, the vast blind shoulder of Thornfell mountain itself—she made a promise to the river, to the ferryman, to the painted-out woman in the fresco of her past:

*I'm coming back for it. Whatever it takes. I'm coming back.*

The ferryman poled them in without comment. As her boots touched stone, he spoke once more, quietly, not looking at her but at the mountain.

"You're walking toward the court, Almeida. Everyone who crosses here going east is walking toward the court, sooner or later. So I'll give you a freebie—courtesy for courtesy, as your grandfather would say." He finally turned, and his forgettable face was grave. "The river's been rising these past weeks. Not from rain. From below. Something down there has started counting again—it counts when it expects a delivery. And the counting is getting faster."

Behind him, in the boat's wake, the water shivered though there was no wind.

"It knows the child is coming," the ferryman said. "Walk fast."
