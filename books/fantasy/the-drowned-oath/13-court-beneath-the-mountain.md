## 13. Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain did not sleep. This was the first thing the deep understood of itself, and the last thing it ever forgot: that sleep was a thing for creatures with skins, with lungs, with names that ended. The mountain only *waited*, and waiting was a kind of hunger that had learned manners.

Far beneath Thornfell—beneath the flooded mines where Cyrus Almeida's lantern guttered and died and was relit, beneath the shrine that drank, beneath even the roots of the oldest road her family had ever cut—the court stirred in its long attention.

It had no eyes. It had never needed them. It perceived the way water perceives: by pressure, by weight, by the small displacements of things moving through it. And something was moving now, far above, two warm bodies and their little cargo of breath, descending along roads that had been built to be forgotten.

*The collateral walks,* said the stone, to itself, in the manner of stone speaking—which is to say slowly, and without punctuation, and with a patience that would have curdled the blood of anyone who heard it whole.

*The collateral walks home.*

* * *

The court had been old when the Almeidas were still farmers arguing about drainage. It had been old when the first king of Thornfell drove his banner into the mountain's shoulder and called the land his. It had watched kingdoms rise like scum on standing water and slide away again, and it had kept its accounts through all of it, because the court was, before anything else—a throne, a hall, a drowned chapel lit by light that came from nowhere—an accounting.

Debt was the mountain's native language. Older than the drowned tongue. Older than salt.

And so when the girl-child moved above, the court felt her the way a creditor feels a debtor crossing his threshold: not with surprise, but with the slow, sweet tightening of a hand around something long promised.

She had been promised twice over, this child. Once in blood and salt, in a contract signed by a man named Almeida who had believed himself clever. And once—this was the part the surface world never understood, the part that made the court's patience less mercy than trap—once by the mountain itself, or by whatever had worn the mountain like a coat in the years before men gave the mountain a name. There had been a soul belonging to the deep places, and it had been taken, and folded, and passed hand to hand like a coin too valuable to spend, until it came to rest in a body small enough to hold in two arms.

The court knew what walked above it. The court knew better than the child herself knew.

But knowing and telling were different economies, and the court kept its ledgers closed.

* * *

In the great hall beneath everything, where the ceiling was worked stone shaped into waves that had frozen mid-break two thousand years ago, the Oathkeeper stood at its post and listened.

It had stood there a long time. It had no memory of sitting down, because it had never sat; the Oathkeeper did not sit, did not eat, did not sleep, and had long ago stopped wondering whether these were privileges or sentences. It was tall in the way that dripstone is tall—not built upward so much as accumulated, layer upon patient layer, until its shoulders brushed the frozen waves overhead and its face had smoothed into something almost featureless, the way a coin wears smooth from passing through too many hands. Only its eyes remained sharp: two points of pale light, like lanterns seen through deep water, and they did not blink, because blinking was a thing bodies did when they expected the world to change.

The Oathkeeper did not expect the world to change. The Oathkeeper expected the world to pay.

"You feel it," said the throne.

The throne did not speak as men speak. Its voice arrived the way cold arrives through a wall—everywhere at once, already inside the room before you noticed it entering. The thing upon the throne had been a king once; the court remembered this the way an old house remembers its builder, fondly and without detail. Now it was mostly outline, a shape of shadow seated in a shape of shadow, wearing a crown of black coral that had grown into the skull beneath it, root and bone become one argument neither could withdraw from.

"I feel it," the Oathkeeper said. Its voice was dry and courteous, the voice of a clerk reading terms aloud. "Two heartbeats above. One large, one small. They travel the Third Road."

"The smuggler's road." Something moved in the shadow of the throne that might have been amusement, if amusement could fossilize. "Their grandfather cut that road. To carry our gold out and our silence back. And now the granddaughter walks it downward, carrying—" the pause was glacial, deliberate, savoring— "carrying what we are owed."

"She does not know she carries it."

"No," the throne agreed. "They never do. That is the elegance of it. You may tell a man he is damned and he will fight you. But let him inherit the debt quietly, let it arrive wrapped in a child's face, and he will carry it up the stairs himself and thank you for the honor." The crown of black coral creaked, very slightly, as the head tilted. "How far?"

"Nine days' walk, if they do not turn aside. Fewer if they are driven."

"They will be driven." The throne's voice cooled by degrees, the way deep water cools. "The surface-things smell it already. The ones who buy debts they do not understand. Let them come. Every hand that reaches for the child tightens our claim. Every greed confirms it. We have waited two hundred years; we can afford to let the living do our collecting for us."

The Oathkeeper considered this. Consideration, for the Oathkeeper, was a physical act—a settling, a shifting of mineral weight, a faint grinding somewhere in its chest like a mill turning without grain.

"There is a variable," it said.

"Name it."

"The smuggler. The last one. She has begun to read what was hidden from her." The pale eyes did not move, but something behind them did, some attention narrowing like a tunnel. "Her grandfather left a journal. Hidden pages. He was frightened, near the end. Frightened men write things down."

"Frightened men also lie," said the throne.

"Yes. But lies leave edges. And the child—" the Oathkeeper paused, and the pause had texture, like sediment settling— "the child is beginning to remember. Not us. Not yet. But the time before the taking. If she remembers fully, before she arrives—"

"Then she will know what she is." The throne was silent for a span that might have been a minute or a decade; in the deep, measurement was a courtesy observed for the benefit of visitors. "And knowing, she may refuse. And refusing, she may unmake two centuries of patient accrual with a single word spoken in her own defense." A sound moved through the hall then, low and vast, and it took the Oathkeeper a moment to identify it as laughter, because nothing about it was warm. "Yes. There is the variable. There always is. The living are so inventive about their own ruin—it is nearly their finest quality."

"What are your instructions?"

The shadow on the throne leaned forward. Where its face should have been there was a darkness with depth to it, a well-shadow, and at the bottom of the well two reflections of the Oathkeeper's own pale eyes looked back.

"Wake the toll-wards on the lower roads. Wake the ferryman; he has been idle too long and idleness makes debtors sentimental. Let the syndicate's hounds run—they serve us best as whips behind the fleeing. And you, herald—" the well-shadow brightened, just slightly, the way a trap brightens with bait— "you will go up. Not all the way. To the drowned chapel, where the contract sleeps. Prepare the room. Prepare the *line*."

"The line," the Oathkeeper repeated. The blank space at the bottom of the ancient contract, where a second signature had waited, unwritten, for two hundred years. Ink made of salt and expectation. Paper that grew no older and no softer, because paper awaiting a signature is not permitted to decay.

"A volunteer of equal blood may take her place. Those were the terms, and terms are sacred, and sacred things are the heaviest things in the world." The throne settled back, and the hall exhaled, and somewhere far above, in tunnels no map admitted to, stones shifted in their sleep. "We offer mercy, herald. That is what no one ever understands about us. We always offer mercy. It simply costs exactly what cruelty would have cost, and no one reads the price before they sign."

"And if the smuggler refuses to sign? If she takes the child and runs?"

The laughter came again, that vast cold grinding, and this time the Oathkeeper identified it more quickly.

"Then we will still be here," said the throne, "when she is dust. Patience is not our virtue, herald. It is our *nature*. Go and prepare the room. Our guests are coming whether they know it or not—and I find, after two hundred years, that I am nearly glad."

* * *

The Oathkeeper went down from the hall by stairs that had not been built but grown, spirals of flowstone slick as throat-muscle, past galleries where the bones of the first petitioners hung in nets of calcite like flies in amber. It passed the Bone Galleries without pausing, though the bones stirred faintly at its passage, the way dogs stir when the master's boots cross the yard. It passed the sunken chapel doors—twin slabs of green-black bronze, worked with waves and chains and, at their center, two hands clasped across a ledger—and laid its palm against the metal.

The doors recognized it. Everything in the mountain recognized the Oathkeeper; recognition was the one payment the mountain never failed to make.

Inside, the chapel waited as it had waited for two centuries: flooded to the height of a man's chest with water clear as conscience, lit by that sourceless glow that lived in the deep places and fed on nothing. At the far end, beneath a window of colored glass depicting no saint any kingdom worshipped, the contract lay open on its altar-stone, and the blank line at its foot shone faintly, patiently, like a held breath.

The Oathkeeper waded in. The water parted around it without touching it, the way the water in the drinking shrine above would soon part around a barefoot girl, though neither party knew of the symmetry yet, and the mountain kept symmetries the way misers keep coins.

It stood before the contract and read it through once more, though it knew every clause by heart—had known them since the night a frightened clever man had signed his family's future in exchange for gold enough to found a guild. *One soul, freely given, of the signer's line, delivered in the fullness of time.* The fullness of time. The Oathkeeper admired that phrase. It had been the Oathkeeper's own suggestion, offered in its capacity as herald, two hundred years ago, to a man whose hands shook so badly the ink had spattered.

*Freely given.* The clause was load-bearing. The whole edifice of the oath rested upon it, as a roof rests upon one pillar. And the Oathkeeper, who had watched the living for two centuries and learned their textures the way lichen learns stone, sometimes wondered—in the wordless, mineral way it wondered about anything—whether that pillar was as sound as it appeared.

The man had sold something. That much was certain; the gold had flowed, the guild had risen, the debt had accrued. But the Oathkeeper had been present at the signing, and it remembered—remembered with the perfect, terrible fidelity of stone—that the man had never once looked at the child he claimed to be offering. Not at her cradle. Not at her mother. His eyes had gone, instead, to the mountain walls themselves, and there had been something in his face that the Oathkeeper had filed, all those years ago, under *greed*, and had not reopened since.

Filing errors were rare in the deep. But they happened.

The Oathkeeper extended one finger of layered stone and touched the blank line. The salt-ink shimmered at the contact, eager, starving, two hundred years hungry for a name.

"Soon," it told the line, in its dry courteous voice, and the line brightened as if it believed him.

Then it turned, waded back through the water that declined to touch it, and began the long climb toward the upper dark—toward the roads where a lantern moved, and a child walked in her sleep speaking words that made the tunnels listen, and a woman followed her down into the earth carrying a grief she had not yet learned was also a key.

Behind it, in the chapel, the contract lay open on its altar.

And in the deepest gallery of all, the throne settled into its waiting, and the mountain drew its attention inward like a man drawing a blanket to his chin, and began—to use a word the surface would have understood, though none of its scholars would have dared apply it here—

to count.

Seven days. The mountain had decided on seven. It had always been fond of sevens; they fit so neatly into a week of human fear.

* * *

Above, in the dark of the Ninth Road, Cyrus Almeida slept badly against her pack with the journal's hidden pages pressed flat beneath her palm, and dreamed, without knowing why, of a hall with a frozen ceiling, and a voice like cold through a wall saying her family name with the tenderness of a hand closing around a coin.

Beside her, the girl did not sleep at all. She sat with her knees drawn up, watching the dark of the tunnel the way other children watch firelight, and far below—so far below that the sound arrived as feeling rather than hearing—she felt something begin to count.

She did not tell Cyrus.

Some prices, she had learned in a life longer than her body, are easier to pay in silence.
