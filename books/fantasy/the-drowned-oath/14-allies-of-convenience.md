## 14. Allies of Convenience

The city of Marrowgate smelled of tallow and old paper, and Cyrus hated it the way one hates a former lover—thoroughly, and with a great deal of memory.

She had come down out of the passes with the girl asleep against her back, wrapped in a smuggler's oilcloth that had once carried brandy and now carried something infinitely more dangerous: a child the world's creditors wanted delivered. Three days of cold rain had soaked through every layer she owned. Her boots had given up pretending to be waterproof somewhere above the tree line. And the girl—Wren, because Cyrus refused to keep calling her *the child*, and because the girl had answered to it once, shyly, as though tasting whether the name fit—had not spoken since the shrine.

*You owe me.*

Cyrus turned those three words over like a coin worn smooth. She had spent her whole life among people who spoke in prices. She knew what a debt sounded like when it was called in. The girl hadn't been angry. That was the worst part. She'd said it the way you say *the sky is grey*, or *the tide is coming in*. A fact about the shape of the world.

Marrowgate was where you went when you needed something the honest markets wouldn't sell. It sat in the crook of two rivers like a stone in a shoe, all leaning tenements and rope bridges and stairways cut into the cliff itself, and at its heart stood the Scriptorium—a converted salt warehouse where the city's archivists copied contracts, authenticated bloodlines, and, for a fee that would make a bishop blush, found the loopholes in both.

Cyrus knew exactly one archivist there.

She left Wren with a fishwife named Berta who owed the Almeida guild more favors than Cyrus could count and who asked no questions beyond *"Is she fed?"*—and walked alone into the Scriptorium's shadow.

* * *

Odile Vasseur had been the Almeida guild's keeper of records for eleven years before they threw her out for the crime of being right. Cyrus remembered the night of it. Odile had stood in the guildhall with a ledger open on her palm and told Uncle Bram that the numbers didn't balance—that there were entries going back generations marked only with a sigil no one would explain—and Bram had looked at her the way a man looks at a hole in a boat, and by morning her things were in the street.

Now she worked the third floor of the Scriptorium, in a cell barely wider than her desk, surrounded by other people's secrets, cataloguing them for strangers.

Cyrus knocked on the doorframe. Odile didn't look up from her copying. She was a narrow woman, ink-stained to the second knuckle, with spectacles that magnified her eyes into something owlish and unimpressed.

"If you're here about the Ferren inheritance," she said, "the answer is no, your grandfather's will is valid, and no, I won't forge an amendment."

"I'm not Ferren."

"Then you're either selling something or begging something, and I've no coin for the first and less patience for the second." But her pen had stopped moving. Archivists heard footsteps the way sailors hear weather. "Almeida," she said quietly. "I wondered how long the mountain would take to spit you out."

"You heard."

"I hear everything. It's the job." Odile set down her pen at last and removed her spectacles, and Cyrus saw that behind the professional boredom her eyes were red-rimmed, sleepless. "Guildhall burned. Ledgers burned. Old Bram dead in his chair with a cup of tea gone cold beside him, which at least means it was quick. Half the syndicate's factors are telling anyone who'll listen that the Almeida debts have come due, and the other half aren't talking at all, which frightens me considerably more." She folded her hands. "And a girl has gone missing from Thornfell way, except no one reported her missing, which means whoever wants her found doesn't want the finding official. Sit down, Cyrus. You look like a drowned cat and you're dripping on my floor."

Cyrus sat on the one stool, which creaked under her like a confession.

"I need to know about the oath."

Something shifted in Odile's face—not surprise. Confirmation. As if Cyrus had finally spoken aloud the thing Odile had been carrying for eleven years, waiting for someone to come collect it.

"Close the door," Odile said.

* * *

What followed took four hours, two pots of bitter chicory, and every candle in the room.

Odile talked the way she wrote—in tight, precise lines, no ornament, every clause load-bearing. She pulled ledgers from beneath her desk, from false bottoms in her shelves, from a loose brick behind the stove, and spread them across the desk until the surface disappeared under a century of paper.

"The guilds of this coast weren't founded on smuggling," she said. "That's the story we tell ourselves because it's a better story than the truth. They were founded on *guarantee*. Two hundred years ago, the courts below the mountains held the roads—the deep roads, the ones that don't appear on any king's map. Every tunnel, every flooded mine, every passage under the rivers. Nothing moved through the dark without their leave. Your ancestors didn't steal access to those roads, Cyrus. They *bought* it."

"With what?"

Odile's pen tapped the oldest ledger, its vellum gone the color of weak tea. "That's the question, isn't it. Coin doesn't interest the drowned court. What do they need coin for? They're below the water table. They can't spend it." She turned the page, and there it was—the sigil Cyrus had seen burned into the lintel of the guildhall, the one the wards answered when the girl spoke. A circle broken by a descending line. "They deal in continuance. In *duration*. And the only currency that lasts as long as they do—"

"Souls."

"Souls." Odile said it without flinching, which somehow made it worse. "Not bodies. Not lives taken. Souls *pledged*. A person promised to the court, to be delivered when the court calls. And here—" her finger stopped on a line of cramped script, "—here is the entry that got me thrown out of your family's good graces. Dated the founding year of the Almeida charter. One line. *Soul-price, paid in full, owed in kind.*"

"In full," Cyrus repeated. "Paid in full. So it was done. Someone was already given."

"That's what I thought for years. Paid in full—transaction complete, debt discharged. Clean." Odile pulled off her spectacles and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "But then why does the same page carry *owed in kind*? You don't owe something in kind if the account is closed. Unless 'paid in full' refers to the *promise*—the pledge made, sealed, irrevocable—and 'owed in kind' is the delivery still outstanding. A promissory note, Cyrus. Not a receipt. Your grandfather didn't pay the court a soul. He *promised* one. And promises, unlike payments, can name someone who doesn't exist yet."

The chicory went cold in Cyrus's cup. Outside the narrow window, gulls argued over the river refuse, and the sound seemed very far away.

"The girl," Cyrus said. "She's the payment."

"She's the *collateral*. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire architecture of the oath." Odile leaned forward, and now the archivist came fully alive, the way she must have been in the guildhall all those years ago, certain and unstoppable. "A collateral soul isn't owned outright. It's *held against performance*. The court holds the girl against the guild's continued good standing—the guild keeps the roads open, pays its respects, maintains the shrines, and the claim sleeps. Let the guild fail—" she gestured vaguely northward, toward Thornfell, toward ash, "—as yours just has—and the claim wakes. The court calls in its paper. And anyone holding that paper can collect."

"The syndicate."

"They bought the guild's debts at auction within a week of the fire. Everything Bram owed, everything your grandfather owed, rolled into one instrument. Except one entry they couldn't buy, because it isn't registered with any bank." Odile's voice dropped. "*Non-negotiable. Held below.* They can't own the claim on the girl. But if they deliver her to the court themselves—if they perform the collection on the court's behalf—they purchase something better than a debt. They purchase *standing*. Favor. The roads."

"And me running with her ruins their delivery."

"You running with her makes you the most hunted woman on this coast." Odile said it flatly, without drama, the way she'd once read out bad numbers to Bram. "Yes."

Cyrus sat with that. Through the window the light had begun to fail, going amber then bruise-colored over the rooftops, and somewhere below a bell rang the hour, and she thought of Wren asleep in Berta's back room with her bare feet and her drowned-tongue dreams, and felt the old stubbornness settle over her grief like armor over a wound.

"How do I break it?"

For the first time, Odile hesitated.

"There's a doctrine," she said slowly. "Old. Older than the guilds. It's in the deep law—the law the courts below recognize, which is not the law of any kingdom, but it has structure, Cyrus, it has *precedent*, because anything that survives two hundred years has lawyers, even if the lawyers are dead." She rose and went to the brick behind the stove, and drew out a slim volume bound in grey leather that looked damp no matter how long it dried. "Collateral can be reclaimed by substitution. If a greater price is offered—freely offered, knowingly offered, of sufficient weight—the holder may accept it in place of the original. The claim transfers. The soul walks free."

Hope rose in Cyrus's chest like water in a lock, sudden and lifting.

"What counts as greater?"

"That's the fine print." Odile set the grey book on the desk between them but did not open it. "Greater isn't a matter of size. It's a matter of *kind*. You can't offer gold for a soul; the currencies don't exchange. A greater price must be of the same nature as the debt—continuance, duration, selfhood. A life freely given might weigh against a life pledged. Years of a life. A name. An inheritance renounced. The court weighs these things according to rules no living person has fully read, because the full text of the deep law exists in exactly one place."

"Below."

"Below." Odile almost smiled. "Of course. The court keeps its own law books. We have fragments. Commentaries. Smuggled copies of smuggled copies, each generation of transcription losing something." She opened the book at last, and the pages inside were written in ink that shimmered faintly, like light through water. "And there's a worse condition. Read this clause."

Cyrus read. The script was archaic but legible, and she read it twice because the first time her eyes refused it:

*A substitution offered under compulsion, coercion, or necessity shall be void. The price must be free, or it is nothing.*

"It has to be voluntary," Cyrus said. "Truly voluntary. No one holding a knife. No one backed into a corner."

"No one whose alternative is death or ruin. Which—" Odile's owl-eyes fixed on her, gentle and terrible, "—describes precisely your situation, does it not? You're fleeing. You're desperate. Any bargain you strike at the foot of that mountain, with hounds at your back and the girl's life in the balance, the court could void with a word. It has waited two hundred years. Do you imagine it hasn't read its own contract? Do you imagine it doesn't know every loophole, because it *wrote* them?"

The lock-water hope drained away, leaving Cyrus on the mudflats of herself.

"Then what use is any of this?"

"Use?" Odile gathered her ledgers, stacking them with quick, practiced motions, and her hands were not quite steady. "Use is knowing the shape of the cage. Use is knowing that somewhere in the deep law there may be a door, even if I can't yet tell you where. Give me time, Cyrus. Give me a week. There are commentaries I haven't seen in twenty years, in collections I no longer have the standing to enter—but I know people who owe me, and some of them still fear me, which is nearly as useful." She paused. "There's also the matter of the original contract itself."

"The one my grandfather signed."

"Every oath has a root document. Sealed, witnessed, lodged with the court. If you ever stand before the court—which God forbid, but plans should be complete—you'll want to have seen it. Words matter in the deep law. A misplaced clause, a conditional phrase, a witness improperly sworn—any flaw in the instrument could be leverage." Odile met her eyes. "I'm telling you this as the woman your uncle ruined for saying true things: the odds are bad. The law is against you, the syndicate is behind you, and the court is beneath you. But the law has seams. I've spent my life finding seams."

Outside, the last light died. Somewhere across the city a watchman called the hour, and his voice cracked on the second syllable, and neither woman remarked on it, but both heard it.

* * *

Cyrus collected Wren from Berta's as the lamps were lit. The girl was sitting cross-legged on the pallet, awake, watching the door as if she'd known the moment Cyrus turned onto the street. When Cyrus knelt to check her for chills, the girl caught her hand—small fingers, cold as river stones—and pressed something into her palm.

A pebble. Grey, ordinary, river-worn. Except that when Cyrus turned it in the lamplight, she saw that lines had been scratched into its surface, fine and deliberate, in the drowned tongue.

"What's this say?"

The girl looked at her for a long moment. Then, in a whisper so soft the lamp flame barely stirred, she said the only words of common speech Cyrus had ever heard from her:

"*Road. Down.*"

Later, in the rented room above the chandler's shop, with Wren finally asleep and the shutters barred, Cyrus set the pebble on the sill and studied it by candlelight until her eyes ached. The script wound around the stone like a current around a rock. She couldn't read it. But she had spent three days watching the girl speak to doors and water and the dark places between trees, and she was beginning to understand that the child wasn't sending messages.

She was drawing maps.

Down. The road was down. It had always been down. Every clue, every ward, every word the girl spoke pointed the same direction—toward the mountain, toward the court, toward the root of the oath. The child wasn't fleeing her fate.

She was walking toward it, and pulling Cyrus along by the hand.

Cyrus blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the rain begin again on the roof, and made her plans the way her family had always made them: route by route, toll by toll, cost by cost. South through the salt marshes, where the syndicate's riders bogged down. Then east, along the old pilgrim ways, toward the mountain's shoulder. Seven days' travel if the weather held. Ten if it didn't.

She was nearly asleep when she heard it: footsteps on the stairs below, unhurried, deliberate, stopping at the landlady's door. A murmur of voices. Then silence—and the particular silence of coins changing hands.

Cyrus was on her feet with her knife drawn before the thought finished forming, and in the darkness the girl sat up too, eyes wide and luminous, and whispered a single word in the drowned tongue that Cyrus did not yet know—

but that the wards on the window, faintly, sadly, began to glow.
