## 17. The Second Ledger

The archive of the Saltwright Guild had not been opened in sixty years, and it smelled like it.

Odile Vane stood in the doorway with a lamp held high and let the dark look at her first. That was the rule her mother had taught her before she could read: *never enter a room of old paper without letting it know you've come.* Paper remembered. Paper held grudges the way stone held cold—passively, patiently, until some warm hand came along to wake it.

"Hello," she said to the dark, because she was forty-one years old and still obeyed a dead woman's superstitions. "I'm sorry it's been so long."

The air answered with dust and salt-rot and something sweeter underneath, something like dried flowers pressed between the pages of a funeral book. Her lamp guttered once, found its footing, and threw long shadows down the aisle of shelving where the Saltwrights' ledgers slept in their oiled wrappings.

She had come for one thing. She had told herself that on the road, and again at the gate, and again as she bribed the caretaker's widow with three silver marks and a bottle of plum brandy. One thing: the legal structure of a soul-oath. The wording. The clauses. The escape hatches, if any existed.

Odile was an archivist the way a drowning woman is a swimmer. She had been cast out of the Almeida guild for asking questions in the wrong tone—the tone that suggested the questions had answers someone didn't want found—and she had spent eleven years since then doing small, humiliating work cataloguing other people's inheritances, other people's debts, other people's carefully burned pasts. When Cyrus Almeida had come to her, mud-spattered and desperate, carrying a mute child who spoke in the grammar of drowned places, Odile had said yes before Cyrus finished asking.

Not out of kindness. Kindness was a luxury item, and Odile had never been able to afford it.

She said yes because she had seen a soul-price clause once before, years ago, in a ledger she should never have touched, and she had spent every night since telling herself she had imagined it.

Tonight she would find out whether she had.

* * *

The Saltwright ledgers were wrapped in waxed linen and tied with cord gone brittle as old cheese rind. She worked by lamplight, gloved to the wrist, unwrapping each volume with the tenderness owed to anything that had survived longer than the people who made it. The guild itself had died badly—a collapse in the eastern shafts, a lawsuit, a scattering—and no one had thought to burn these books. Small mercies. Or large ones. She wouldn't know which until she'd read them.

The early volumes were ordinary. Cargo manifests. Toll agreements. A bitter decade of correspondence about a rival family's poaching of the river routes. Odile read quickly, skimming for the shape of things, and the shape was familiar: a guild rising, a guild hungry, a guild reaching for wealth faster than honest trade could provide it.

Then, in the third ledger, dated the year the Saltwright fortunes turned, she found the page.

It wasn't hidden. That was the first horror of it. It sat in the middle of an ordinary accounting spread, between a shipment of tin and a dispute over warehouse rent, written in the same clerk's hand, in the same iron-gall ink, with the same bored precision:

*Received of the Court Beneath: one hundred crowns, paid in full.*
*Given in kind: one soul-price, to be named at birth, to be delivered at majority.*
*Terms perpetual. Witnessed under salt and stone.*

*Perpetual*, she read again. Not once. Not a single transaction. Perpetual meant generations. Perpetual meant a debt designed to keep paying forever, and there was only one way a debt like that kept paying.

Her hands were steady. She noted that distantly, the way you note the weather. Somewhere below her sternum, something had begun to scream, but Odile had been trained—first by her mother, then by the archivists' college, finally by her own disgrace—to keep her hands steady while everything else fell apart. Steady hands turned pages. Steady hands copied citations. Steady hands did the work that screaming hands could never finish.

She turned the page.

*Named at birth:* the next entry, twenty-two years later, in a different hand. *A daughter to Marta Saltwright. Delivered at majority per terms. Receipt acknowledged beneath.*

There was no copy of the receipt. There was only a line noting that one existed, filed somewhere else, somewhere *below*, and a small marginal mark in faded red—a circle with a line through it—that Odile recognized from her training as the archivist's shorthand for *verified against source*.

Someone had checked. Someone had gone down, or sent word down, or received confirmation up, and had verified that a living girl named at birth had been delivered on schedule, and had marked it *verified* the way you'd mark a delivery of tin.

Odile set the lamp down because her hands wanted very much to stop being steady, and she would not allow it.

* * *

She worked through the night.

By the second ledger-stack she had stopped being careful about the wrappings; by the third she had stopped being careful about anything except the count. She made it on a scrap of wrapping-linen, in charcoal, because ink felt too permanent for what she was writing.

Saltwright Guild: founded 1187. First oath recorded 1191. Deliveries confirmed: 1731, 1753, 1779, 1802, 1826, 1848, 1871. Seven children across two hundred years. Named at birth. Raised inside the guild, loved presumably, fed and taught and sung to, and then—at majority, always at majority, the court was punctual—delivered.

Seven children. And the Saltwrights were a middling guild, a minor power, a footnote in the trade histories. If they had paid seven—

She pulled the Corvane material next. She'd catalogued fragments of it years ago, during her brief and disastrous tenure with the Almeidas, when she'd been given access to seized records from a rival house's bankruptcy. At the time she had assumed the soul-price entry was a metaphor. Archivists are trained to assume metaphors; it is how we survive our own collections. *Soul-price* had seemed like colorful language for a blood-debt, a vendetta obligation, something grim but earthly.

The Corvanes had paid nine.

The Halloway river-clan, whose papers she'd seen in a magistrate's estate sale: five, and then the clan had died out entirely, and Odile now understood—with a lurch that bent her double over the reading table—that they hadn't died out. They had been *used up*. The last child taken, the line ended, the oath closed like a mouth finishing a meal.

She counted. She recounted. She checked dates against the founding years of each guild, and the pattern held with the terrible neatness of all true horrors: within four years of every founding, within four years of every sudden, inexplicable prosperity, there was the oath. One hundred crowns received. One soul-price given in kind. Terms perpetual.

Fourteen guilds whose records she could reach. Forty-one children whose names she could recover. And those were only the ones who wrote it down.

She thought of the girl—Wren, Cyrus called her, though the child had not yet agreed to the name—standing in the doorway of the Almeida hall with storm-water running off her and the old wards answering her voice like dogs answering a whistle. Cyrus believed Wren was the debt. The last payment on a single, monstrous transaction.

Cyrus was wrong, and the truth was worse. Wren was not a debt. Wren was a *harvest*. One ear of grain cut from a field the guilds had been planting for four hundred years.

* * *

Near dawn, her lamp burning low, Odile found the second ledger.

That was what she came to call it afterward, in the privacy of her own skull: not the guild ledgers, not the account books, but *the* Second Ledger—a slim volume bound in grey leather, unmarked, shelved apart from the rest on a high shelf behind a false row of tax records. It had been placed there deliberately. Hidden, but not destroyed. Hidden by someone who wanted it found eventually, by the right person, or hidden by someone too cowardly to burn it and too honest to leave it in the open. Both kinds of people existed in archives. Odile had been both.

Inside were not accounts. Inside were testimonies.

Handwritten statements, sworn and signed, dozens of them, spanning centuries. Guild-mothers and guild-fathers. Clerks. Two archivists—one of whom, judging by the hand, had written this very book's earliest pages. They had all done the same thing, generation after generation: they had written down what they knew and hidden it, because writing it down openly was death and destroying it was unthinkable.

*I, Aldous Saltwright, clerk of this house, do set down that I attended the delivery of the child Ede, aged sixteen years, to the door beneath the eastern shaft, and that she walked in on her own feet, and that the door closed behind her, and that her mother was paid one hundred crowns for her silence, and that I have not slept since.*

*I, Maren Corvane, do set down that my brother was taken in the year of the flood, and that when we went to the mountain to ask for him back, we were shown the contract, and the contract says GIVEN IN KIND, and the mountain does not return what is given in kind.*

*I, name withheld, do set down that the children do not die. This must be understood. The court does not kill them. The court keeps them. The couriers who have gone deepest speak of lights in the lower halls, and of voices, and of voices that know the names of the living above. What the court wants with them I cannot say. But they are not dead, and this makes the crime worse, not better.*

Odile read that last testimony twice, three times, her lips moving silently. *They are not dead.*

She thought of Wren speaking the drowned tongue. Of wards older than kingdoms bending toward her voice. Of the way the child had looked at locked doors—not with fear, but with recognition.

And she thought, with a cold that started in her teeth and spread downward: *if the court keeps them, then the court has been keeping them for four hundred years. A nursery of stolen centuries. A choir.*

What did a court need with a choir?

She looked at the blank back pages of the Second Ledger, waiting for her hand. Every contributor had added their testimony; that was clearly the custom, the ritual, the chain. Write it down. Hide it well. Trust the paper to outlive your courage.

Odile took up her pen. Her hands, steady all night, shook now, and she let them.

*I, Odile Vane, archivist, disbarred and disgraced, do set down that the oaths number at least fourteen houses and forty-one children whose names survive, and likely many more whose records burned or rotted. I set down that the pattern is uniform: prosperity purchased within four years of founding, a child named at birth, delivered at majority, terms perpetual. I set down that the current claim stands against the house of Almeida, last of the old guilds, and that the claimed child lives, and walks, and speaks the tongue of the court as if she learned it in her cradle—which perhaps she did, in some sense I do not yet understand.*

*I set down that I intend to find the flaw in the instrument. Every contract has a flaw. Contracts are written by hands, and hands are corruptible, and even mountains deal through intermediaries. If the court has been fed for four hundred years on stolen children, then somewhere in four hundred years of paperwork there is a lie, and I will find it, and I will bring it into the light where the law—if the law reaches that deep—must reckon with it.*

*If I do not survive to finish this, whoever finds this book: the children are not dead. Begin there.*

She signed it. She blotted it. She sat back in the grey half-light of dawn leaking through the archive's high slit-window, and she listened to the building settle around her, stone ticking as it warmed, and she felt—for the first time in eleven years—not like a disgraced woman doing humiliating work, but like an archivist. Actually one. The kind her mother had meant when she said *paper remembers*: the kind who makes the remembering count for something.

Then she heard the footsteps in the corridor above, and the low voices, more than one, and the particular cadence of men asking questions they already knew the answers to, and she understood that she had not been as alone in the world's attention as she had hoped.

* * *

She snuffed the lamp and stood motionless among the shelves, listening.

Three sets of boots, maybe four. Moving unhurried. Men who had time, which meant men backed by money, which meant the syndicate had traced her the way water traces a crack. Of course they had. She had bought passage under her own name—arrogance, sloppiness, the vanity of a woman who had spent eleven years believing herself unimportant enough to be invisible. Cyrus had warned her. Even the child had looked at her strangely when she'd signed the ferry manifest, as if the drowned tongue had a word for *fool* and the girl had been polite enough not to say it.

The voices stopped above her. A floorboard groaned. Then a new sound: the caretaker's widow, wheedling, frightened, and the flat reply of a man who paid for answers rather than begged for them.

Odile weighed her options the way she weighed everything: quickly, coldly, with an accountant's mercy. She could run—but the archive's only exit was up, through the corridor where the boots were. She could hide—but they would search, and searching men find things, and the Second Ledger could not be found. Not now. Not when it was the only proof in the world that forty-one children had names.

So she did the thing that made her an archivist and not merely a woman in a cellar. She took the ledger, wrapped it in waxed linen, and slid it into the false back of the tax-shelf where it had lived for who knew how many decades—then she took the pages she had copied, the counts, the dates, the fourteen houses, and sewed them into the lining of her coat, against her ribs, where a searching man's hands might hesitate.

Then she climbed toward the boots.

They were syndicate men; she knew the livery, grey-and-iron, the same colors that had sat outside the Almeida hall demanding debts. Their leader was a broad, patient-looking man with a clerk's eyes, and he smiled at her the way creditors smile, which is to say without warmth and without malice, arithmetic wearing a face.

"Mistress Vane," he said. "You've been busy."

"I'm an archivist," Odile said. "Busy is what I am."

"Mm." He glanced past her, down the stair, into the dark of the shelves, and his smile thinned by a degree. "We're told the Saltwright records are complete. Unburned. Rare, these days." His eyes came back to hers. "You know why records burn, mistress? Because fire is cheaper than truth. And you know why the syndicate pays so well for unburned ones?" He leaned in, and beneath the smell of road-dust and horse she caught something else on him, faint and wrong, like the air off deep water. Like the breath of a place that had no business clinging to a living man.

"Because we hold certain obligations," he said softly, "and we like to know exactly what we're holding. Now. You can tell me what's down those stairs, or you can watch us find out. One of those options ends with you walking out of here."

Odile looked at him—this instrument of the machine that had eaten forty-one children and intended to eat a forty-second—and she gave him the truth, or a truth, in the flattest voice she owned: "Tax records. Sixty years of grain levies and warehouse disputes. Take them all, if it pleases you. I'll even catalogue them for you. For my usual fee."

He studied her for a long moment. Behind him, one of his men shifted, and Odile saw that the man's hand, hanging at his side, was making a small repeated motion—thumb tracing circle after circle against his forefinger, unconscious, compulsive, like a man counting something he couldn't stop counting.

*Verified against source,* she thought, and her stomach turned to salt water.

The leader shrugged. "Search her," he said. "Then burn the stairs behind us. Whatever's down there, the Saltwrights kept it too long already."

They found nothing in her coat but a disgraced woman's spare shirt and a stub of charcoal. They missed the stitching. Archivists sew better than soldiers search. And while they tramped down into the dark with their lanterns to feed sixty years of paper to the flames, Odile Vane walked out of the Saltwright archive into the white morning, past the widow weeping in the yard, down the road, with forty-one names stitched against her ribs and one purpose stitched tighter than the rest:

Find Cyrus. Find the child. Tell them the truth Cyrus's grandfather's journal had only hinted at—that the oath was not one wound but a system, a farm, a feeding—and that somewhere in four hundred years of it, there had to be a lie.

Behind her, smoke began to rise from the archive windows, thin and grey against the dawn.

Paper remembers, her mother had said. But paper also burns, and that was why the living had to remember harder.

Odile turned south, toward the high passes, toward a smuggler and a stolen child, and walked as fast as her legs would carry the weight of what she knew.
