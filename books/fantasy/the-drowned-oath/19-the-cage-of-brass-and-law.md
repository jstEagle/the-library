## 19. The Cage of Brass and Law

The cage is brass because brass is honest.

That is what the syndicate's master believes, anyway. She heard him say it through the door on her first night below Salt Cellar Row, his voice smooth as tallow: *Brass holds what iron only bruises. Brass conducts. Brass remembers.* He said it the way merchants say holy things—reverently, and without understanding—and she pressed her palm flat against the bars and felt that he was wrong. Iron forgets. Brass remembers too well.

The bars are warm under her hand even now, in this cold cellar where nothing else has been warm in a hundred years. They hum faintly, a note so low she feels it in her teeth rather than hears it. And beneath that hum, deeper, patient as sediment, something else is speaking.

It speaks in the drowned tongue.

She does not answer it. Not yet. She sits cross-legged on the floor of her cage with her knees drawn up and her chin resting on them, and she listens the way Cyrus taught her to listen at crossroads—not to the loudest sound but to the oldest one—and she counts.

Because the stone beneath the brass is counting too.

*Seven,* says the mountain.

* * *

They took her boots. She had no boots—Cyrus never let her wear them, saying roads were read best barefoot—but they took her stockings, and the shawl with the salt-burned hem, and they put her in a gray smock with no pockets, as if pockets were where a child kept dangerous things. As if the dangerous thing were not already inside her, curled around her ribs like a second heartbeat, older than every pocket ever sewn.

Her cell is not truly a cage. The brass bars stand free in the middle of a stone room, a cage within a room within a hill, and beyond the walls of the room she can hear the sea doing its slow arithmetic against the cliffs. The syndicate built their countinghouse into the cliff itself, and the cliff was here long before the syndicate, and before the cliff there was the mountain's shoulder reaching down toward the water, and before all of it—

Before all of it there was the court.

She knows this the way she knows her own hands. Not learned. Remembered. There are two knowledges in her now, and they do not always agree. One of them is seven years old and likes the smell of woodsmoke and misses a woman named Cyrus with an ache like a bruised tooth. The other one is older than the guild, older than the ledgers, older than the word *guild*, and it lies very still inside her most days, listening.

Since they brought her down into the cliff, the still thing has stopped being still.

*Six,* says the mountain.

No—that is tomorrow's number. Tonight it says *seven*. She must be careful with the numbers. Numbers are how they will try to own her, both the men above and the thing below; numbers are the grammar of debt, and debt is the only language either of them respects.

But she is learning, slowly, in the dark, that numbers can be stolen back.

* * *

The woman who brings her food is called Maren. She is broad and tired and her hands shake when she sets the tray through the slot in the brass door, and on the third night her hands shook badly enough that the bowl slipped and spilled barley soup across the threshold, and she swore, and knelt to wipe it up, and looked at the girl through the bars.

"You're younger than they told us," Maren said.

The girl said nothing. She has learned that silence is a road too. People walk into it and get lost and start telling you things.

"They said you were a cargo." Maren's rag made small wet circles on the stone. "Manifest said *one unit, bonded soul, handle per schedule*. I've moved cargo twenty years. Crates don't look at you like that."

The girl tilted her head. Inside her, the old knowledge stirred and offered a word—the drowned tongue has forty words for looking, each one precise as a blade—and she chose the mildest one and gave it to Maren across the bars, soft as breath.

Maren went pale. She stood up too fast, dropped the rag, left the spilled soup cooling on the stones, and did not come back until the next night. When she came back, she would not meet the girl's eyes. But she brought honey with the bread.

Honey is a language too. The girl ate it slowly and understood: *I am afraid of you, but I am not your enemy.*

Noted, she thought, in the ledger behind her eyes. Everything goes in the ledger now. She keeps it the way Cyrus kept hers—in the body, in the memory, in places fire cannot reach. Entry one: the man with the ring of small keys sleeps on the north stair and snores in a rhythm she could set a clock by. Entry two: the brass conducts, which means it also carries, which means when the master stands outside the cage and talks to her about honesty, the bars whisper every word down into the stone, and the stone passes it along, and somewhere far beneath, the court takes minutes.

Entry three: seven days.

*Five,* says the mountain, correcting her, because the mountain counts differently—it counts nights remaining, and tonight is spent.

She lets it have its arithmetic. She is keeping her own.

* * *

On the fourth day—or the fifth night, depending on whose ledger—they bring her a visitor.

He comes down the stairs with a lantern held high, and he is not the master. The master smells of ink and pomade and fear wearing cologne. This one smells of road dust and lamp oil and something underneath that makes the still thing inside her rise up like a fish rising to feed: salt, and blood, and oath.

Vess.

She knows the name before he gives it, because the mountain has been murmuring about her for days. *The hound. The hound who is also chained. The hound who owes.* The court finds Vess interesting the way a moneylender finds a debtor interesting—with appetite dressed up as patience.

The girl sits very still against the far bars and watches Vess stop on the other side of the cage and hold up the lantern, and light moves across her face like water moving over rocks.

"So," Vess said. "You really do just sit there."

The girl said nothing.

"They told me you'd be raving. Screaming in that dead language. Scaring the clerks." Vess crouched, bringing her eyes level with the girl's, and there was something in her face that the seven-year-old knowledge recognized before the old one did: exhaustion. The specific exhaustion of someone who has carried a debt so long she has forgotten it is not part of her skeleton. "Instead you sit like you're waiting for a coach."

The girl considered her. Then, carefully, she reached out with the old knowledge the way Cyrus had taught her to test thin ice—one point of weight, gently, ready to withdraw.

What she found nearly made her flinch. Under Vess's skin, under the road dust and the lamp oil, ran a bond like a scar of light, a contract stitched into the meat of her, signed in her own blood years ago and renewed so many times the signatures overlapped into a smear. Debt-bonded. Indentured to the syndicate down to the marrow. The court saw such things clearly; the court was, in its way, the oldest creditor of all, and it recognized kinship when it looked upon another person owned.

And the girl understood, suddenly, with a clarity that hurt, why the mountain found Vess interesting.

She opened her mouth, and the drowned tongue came out of her like cold water from a deep well:

"*Your chain and my chain were forged in the same fire. Ask it who holds the mold.*"

Vess went absolutely still. The lantern flame leaned sideways though there was no draft.

"What did you say," Vess said quietly. It was not a question. It was a door being closed very carefully, or perhaps very slowly opened.

The girl leaned back against the bars and said nothing more. Silence is a road. Let her walk it.

But that night, after the footsteps faded, the mountain spoke to her directly for the first time—not through the stone's low murmur but clear as a bell rung underwater, filling the whole cage, the whole cellar, the whole girl:

*You showed the hound her collar. Why?*

She lay back on the cot they'd given her and stared at the ceiling, where the lamplight from the corridor made shapes like drowned branches.

*Because chains should frighten the people wearing them,* she answered, in the old tongue, in the privacy of her skull. *Maybe if enough of them are frightened, they'll stop making new ones.*

A long quiet. Then the mountain laughed—a sound like a landslide deciding to wait—and she felt it settle back into patience, and she hated how much the laughter sounded almost kind.

*Four nights,* it said, gentle as a lullaby. *Rest, little collateral. We are almost family again.*

* * *

The dreams are getting worse, or better. She has stopped trying to decide which.

In them she is not seven years old. In them she is tall, robed in weed-gray, standing in a hall where the pillars are the ribs of something vast and sleeping, and she is holding a lantern made of bone, and everyone in the hall is bowing to her, and she wants to scream *stop, you've got the wrong person, I'm nobody's heir, I'm just a girl who likes honey on her bread*—but her mouth in the dream opens and what comes out is judgment. Verdicts in the drowned tongue. Names read from a ledger of the dead. And every name she reads, in the dream, a lantern somewhere above ground goes out.

She wakes each time with salt on her lips, though there is no salt in the cellar except what the sea sends up through the rock, and she wipes it away and does not cry, because the old knowledge tells her crying is a toll and she refuses to pay this particular ferryman.

Except once. On the sixth night, she cries.

It happens because Maren brings the supper tray and, tucked beside the bread, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth, is a sprig of dried rowan with a red thread tied around it. A warding charm. Cheap, homespun, the kind grandmothers knot for children walking home after dark. Maren doesn't look at her when she sets it down; she looks at the wall, jaw tight, and leaves quickly, as if fleeing the scene of a crime.

And the girl holds the rowan in both hands, this tiny defiant green thing smuggled into a place built entirely out of ownership, and thinks about Cyrus teaching her stars in the high passes—*that one's the Ferryman, see the crook of his staff, follow him and you're going west*—and thinks about a shawl with a salt-burned hem, and the tears come fast and hot and she lets exactly four of them fall, and then she pays no further toll.

The mountain notices. Of course it notices.

*You grieve,* it observes.

*I'm practicing,* she says. *For when it's real.*

*Three nights,* it answers, and for once there is something in its voice that might, in a creature capable of kindness, have been gentleness. *Then you come home.*

"I'm not coming home," she says aloud, to the stone, to the brass, to the sea beyond the cliff. Her voice cracks on it, seven years old again, stubborn as a weed in a wall. "Home is a woman with burned hands and bad maps. Home is a guildhall that smells of tar. You've got the address wrong."

The stone does not argue. The stone never argues. It simply continues, patient as interest compounding, and she feels the countdown settle over her shoulders like a yoke:

*Three. Two. One. Then zero, and zero is a door.*

* * *

She spends the last full day doing inventory, because Cyrus taught her that fear shrinks when you give it a task.

Inventory of self: one girl, age uncertain (seven by body, some centuries by weight of memory), barefoot, unwashed, unbroken. Assets: the drowned tongue, fluent; the wards of the Almeida guildhall, memorized; three words of star-lore; one red-threaded rowan charm, hidden in the seam of the cot where the stuffing sags. Liabilities: everything else.

Inventory of captors: one master who loves brass and fears the mountain more than he admits even to himself—she has heard his footsteps pause outside her door at night, heard him breathe close to the bars, heard the little wet click of his throat when the mountain murmurs and the brass carries it up to him. He is not delivering her to the court. He is feeding her to it, the way a farmer feeds a bull, hoping appetite stays on the far side of the fence. He thinks the transaction ends when she crosses the threshold.

She knows better. She has been the collateral; she has also, in the dreams, worn the crown. Transactions with the drowned court do not end. They compound.

Inventory of allies: one cook named Maren, afraid and honey-handed. One enforcer named Vess, whose chain she has rattled and whose face she now sees in the corridor sometimes, lingering, watching, a question building behind her eyes like weather. One archivist named Odile, whose betrayal still sits in her chest like a swallowed stone—but Odile sold her location to survive, and survival is a debt everyone understands, and the girl has decided, in her private ledger, that debts can be forgiven once they are paid. Whether Odile has paid remains to be seen.

And inventory of the mountain itself: vast, patient, cheated—for that is the thread Cyrus went hunting for, the lie at the root of everything, the fraud that would unmake the whole economy of souls if it could only be proven before the throne. If Cyrus found it. If Cyrus is even alive. If the syndicate didn't take her too, or the mine, or the river, or the dark.

The girl presses her forehead against the warm brass bar and sends her voice down through it, through the stone, down the old roads her grandfather's grandfather helped carve, down toward the chapel where a contract waits with a blank line glowing like an open wound:

*Cyrus. Hold on. I am coming with a verdict in my mouth.*

Far below, something shifts. She cannot say how she knows—it is less a sound than a change in the quality of the darkness—but the mountain feels it too, and for the first time since she arrived, the countdown stutters.

*Two,* says the stone, uncertain.

And then, softer, from very far away, traveling up through bedrock and brass and bone, in a hand she would know anywhere because it is written the way Cyrus writes everything—fast, slanting, furious with purpose—comes a single line of feeling, not words, just pressure and intent, like a seal slapped onto a letter:

*Keep the roads open. I'm on one.*

The girl laughs. Four tears' worth of grief, converted in a moment to fuel. She sits back on her cot, barefoot and grinning in the dark like a small fanged saint, and begins, silently, in the drowned tongue, to rehearse the verdict she has been carrying since the night the wards first sang over a smuggler's door:

*This oath was sworn on stolen goods. And I have come to repossess.*

Outside, the tide turns. Somewhere above, a stair creaks—Vess, or Maren, or dawn. Below, the mountain resumes its count, but slower now, wary, the way a lender is wary when the debtor starts reading fine print.

*One night,* it says.

"Then zero," she agrees pleasantly, "is a door."

And doors, she thinks, closing her eyes and folding the rowan charm into her fist, doors open from both sides.
