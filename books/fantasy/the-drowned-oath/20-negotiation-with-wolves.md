## 20. Negotiation with Wolves

They took her boots first.

Cyrus noticed that before anything else—the small indignity of it, the way a man in a grey coat knelt at her ankles with the practiced tenderness of a farrier and drew her boots off as though they were evidence of a crime. Then her knife-belt. Then the flat iron key she kept sewn into her collar, the one that opened nothing anymore except the memory of a door. They laid everything on a table of black slate and covered it with a cloth, like mourners covering a face.

The room they brought her to was warm, which was its own kind of threat. After nine days of high passes and flooded roads, after the mine and the shrine and the ferryman's toll still sitting sour on her tongue where her mother's face used to be, warmth felt like a bribe offered too early. Braziers burned along both walls, coals banked low and red, and the air smelled of cedar smoke and tallow and, beneath it, faintly, of salt—salt carried up from somewhere deep, or perhaps only remembered.

The girl was not in the room. That was the first thing Cyrus made herself accept, because panic was a currency here and she would not spend it. The girl was elsewhere, held somewhere, alive—alive because the syndicate did not spend two hundred miles of hounds and blood-money to deliver a corpse to a court that wanted its collateral breathing. *Alive*, she told herself, and made the word a plank to walk on.

*Sit*, said the room, without anyone saying it. A single chair faced a long desk of dark wood, and behind the desk sat the master of the Vane Syndicate.

She had heard the name spoken in a dozen ports, always quietly: **Corvane**. Some said Corvane was one man; some said it was three brothers who never appeared together; some said it was a title passed like a poison chalice. The figure behind the desk resolved none of these rumors. It was an old woman with a face like a riverbed—dry, cracked, shaped by long water now gone—and hands folded on the desk with the patience of someone who had outlived every person who ever hurried her.

"Cyrus Almeida," the old woman said. Her voice was soft and gravelled, a hearth-voice, a grandmother's voice, and that frightened Cyrus more than shouting would have. "Last sworn smuggler of the Almeida guild. Do you know what your guild was famous for, in its day? Not the cargo. Anyone can move cargo. You were famous for *keeping accounts*. Your grandfather could recite a ledger from memory after twenty years. They said an Almeida never forgot a debt."

"My uncle couldn't remember his own birthday," Cyrus said.

"Decline is instructive." The old woman unfolded her hands and refolded them. "Sit down, child. You've been walking for days. Sit, and let us do this properly. I have admired you, you know. From a distance. You are the last of a line, holding a door that everyone else has abandoned, and you have done it with more spine than sense. That is a rare thing. Most people abandon doors early and call it wisdom."

Cyrus sat. The chair was deeper than she expected, cushioned, treacherous with comfort. She kept her spine off the backrest.

"I want the girl," she said.

"Yes," said Corvane. "I assumed you hadn't crossed my territory, killed my man at the weir, and cost me four days of hounds' wages to discuss the weather."

*My man at the weir.* The words landed in Cyrus's chest and stayed there, cold. She had not let herself think about him since—the young enforcer with the hooked pole, the way he'd gone into the water, the way the water had taken him and closed over him without comment. Survival's arithmetic. She had paid it once already this season and the ledger did not balance; it only grew.

"He came at me with iron," she said.

"He did. And you were better. I'm not here to weep over him—he knew the trade when he signed." Something moved behind the old woman's eyes, brief and unreadable, like a fish under ice. "But understand the shape of things, Almeida. Everything has a price with me. Even grief. Especially grief. So let us talk prices, since you came all this way to talk prices, and since you are, by blood and trade, a woman who respects them."

* * *

They brought tea. Cyrus did not touch hers. The cup was porcelain thin enough to see light through, painted with tiny blue ships, and she thought absurdly of the tin cup she'd drunk from as a child in the guildhall kitchen, dented on one side where her uncle had dropped it down the stairs and refused to replace it because *a dent is just a scar that holds more tea*. The memory arrived unbidden and she let it pass through her like weather.

"The girl," Corvane began, "is worth more than anything your guild ever moved. More than the salt runs. More than the war-year silk. Do you know why?"

"She's a soul-price. A payment owed below. I know."

"You know the shape of it. You don't yet know the weight." The old woman leaned forward, and the brazier-light caught the fine network of veins at her temples, and for a moment Cyrus saw not a merchant queen but something older and hungrier wearing one. "There are debts in this world that can be bought and sold like any other paper. And there are debts that are *anchored*. This one is anchored. It is held below—by the court beneath Thornfell mountain, which your family's pretty ledgers politely called 'the counterparty.' The court does not sell. The court does not trade. The court *waits*. And everything that waits beneath the earth eventually collects."

"Then why do you want her?" Cyrus asked. "If she's only good for delivery below, she's worthless above. You're spending blood and silver on a parcel you can't open."

For the first time, the old woman smiled. It was not a comforting sight. It was the smile of someone who has been asked a clever question by a student she intends to flatter and then ruin.

"Because the court rewards delivery," she said. "Two hundred years of patience, and the thing beneath the mountain has learned gratitude—or something shaped like gratitude. The last guild to discharge a debt in full was given passage rights through stone itself. Roads that don't appear on any map because they didn't exist until the court dug them overnight. My syndicate has spent three generations buying influence in six kingdoms, and I promise you, child, none of it weighs what one favor from below would weigh. Kingdoms fall. Mountains don't."

"So you'll take her below yourself. Deliver her. Collect the favor."

"I will. Unless—" and the old woman spread her hands, magnanimous as a stage magician, "—you give me a reason not to. You asked for this meeting. You sent word through my own pickets, bold as brass, offering a trade. So. Trade. What does the last Almeida put on the table?"

And here it was. The moment she had rehearsed against the dark for nine nights, lying in hollows and ruined shrines with the girl asleep against her shoulder, counting the guild's remaining fortune the way other people counted rosaries.

* * *

"The guildhall," Cyrus said. "Freehold, stone-built, three floors and the cellar levels, on the Salt Quarter's oldest street. Worth forty thousand crowns even gutted, and it isn't gutted—the wards alone are worth ten. The warehouse on the quay with its bonded cellars. Two revenue contracts still honored by the harbor trust, paying quarterly, another twelve thousand a year between them while the paper holds. And the name. The Almeida name, with its reputation for clean dealing—every syndicate in the coast kingdoms has tried to buy that reputation for a century and failed, because it wasn't for sale." She laid each item down like a card, voice level, and watched the old woman watch her. "It's for sale now. All of it. For the girl."

Silence. The braziers ticked and settled. Somewhere beyond the walls, muffled by stone, she heard the sound of the household moving—boots, a cart, a dog being quieted—and she fixed on those sounds to keep herself from listening for smaller ones, thinner ones, a child's voice crying out from some locked room below.

"That's a fortune," Corvane said at last, and there was no mockery in it, which somehow made it worse. "That is, genuinely, a fortune. Enough to buy a seat in any port council on the coast. Enough to make my grandchildren dull and comfortable." She turned one hand over, studying her own palm as if reading a ledger written there. "And I am going to refuse it, and you are going to hate me, so let me tell you exactly why, because you deserve precision."

Cyrus said nothing. Her heart was a fist striking a door that would not open.

"A soul-price is not property," the old woman said. "That is the whole horror of it, and the whole reason it's valuable. A ship can be sold twice. Land can be sold a hundred times. But a claimed soul exists in a state of—call it *escrow*. Held below, registered below, answerable to one creditor only. If I took your fortune and gave you the girl, I would be handing you a deed to nothing. She would walk out of my gate and the mountain's claim would walk with her, invisible, patient, and one day—next year, in thirty years, on her wedding day, on her child's christening—the ground would remember what it was owed. You wouldn't have bought her freedom. You'd have bought her a longer leash." The old woman's dry riverbed face tilted. "You of all people should understand. You found your grandfather's page. *Paid in full, owed in kind.* The grammar of it matters. The debt doesn't die. It waits for the kind."

"Then void it," Cyrus said, and heard her own voice crack on the second word, and hated the crack, and pushed through it anyway. "Contracts break. Contracts get broken all the time—that's half of what your syndicate does, I'd wager. Find the flaw. Sell me the flaw. Name a price for the flaw and I'll find a way to pay it."

"There is no flaw I can sell you." For a moment—just a moment—the performance fell away, and what looked out of the old woman's face was simply tired, anciently tired, a woman who had spent a long life learning the exact weight of things that could not be lifted. "Only the court can release a claimed soul. That is not syndicate doctrine, Almeida. That is the oldest law under the earth, older than my house, older than yours. Money cannot solve this. I know money. I have more of it than God and less use for it than a dead man, and I am telling you with the authority of both: *money cannot solve this.*" She paused. "What you're feeling right now—that's the lesson. Every rich fool learns it once. The ones who survive the learning are the ones who stop reaching for their purse."

"Then what do you reach for?"

"Delivery," Corvane said softly. "Or surrender. Those are the only two doors out of this room, and only one of them has you walking beside the girl."

* * *

They left her alone for a while. Perhaps an hour; perhaps less; time went strange in the warmth, in the cedar smoke, in the exhaustion that had been riding her shoulders since the ferryman's boat. She sat in the deep treacherous chair and did the only thing left in her kit: she thought.

She thought about the girl's hands. How they were always slightly cold, how the girl warmed them at every fire without ever seeming to notice she was doing it, cupping them toward the flames like a woman remembering a habit from another life. She thought about the drowned shrine, the water rising around them without touching the girl, and the first clear words the child had ever spoken: *You owe me.* Not cruel. Worse than cruel—factual. A statement of account rendered by a creditor eight years old.

She thought about her grandfather's journal, the careful slanted hand, the entry that had changed the shape of her whole life: the founding year, the desperate winter, the bargain struck with something beneath the mountain to keep the guild alive—and the price marked not in crowns but in *a soul of the line, payable when the court calls*. Her grandfather had signed it. Had signed it calmly, in good ink, and then built warehouses on the proceeds and raised children in the warmth of that stolen fire and never, in forty years of meticulous record-keeping, recorded one word of remorse.

*Fury at the dead,* she thought. *They signed my name before I existed. They signed hers before her grandmother existed.* And the fury was still there, banked like the coals in the braziers, but underneath it now lay something colder and more useful: understanding. Corvane was right about the shape of the trap. There was no purse deep enough. There was no road fast enough—the hounds had proven that at the weir, at the border shrine, in the flooded mine where the water had risen and the girl had calmed it with a word and Cyrus had felt, for the first time, reluctant awe instead of fear.

There was only below. Only the mountain. Only the court that waited.

Which meant the question was no longer *how do I keep her from the mountain?* The question—the real one, the one she had been circling for nine days like a moth around a lantern—was *what happens when we get there?*

She did not have an answer. But she had something she had not had at the start: direction. Every road her family had ever built ran toward Thornfell like veins toward a heart. The First Road, sealed since the founding. The drowned chapel where the original contract slept in blood and salt. If the court alone could release a claimed soul, then the court was the only door left, and Cyrus Almeida had spent her whole life opening doors that other people had given up on.

The door opened now—a different one, a lesser one—and the grey-coated man from before stood in it, expressionless, come to fetch her back to whatever cell they'd prepared.

"One more thing," Cyrus said, rising, and her legs ached and her voice held. "Tell your mistress I'll be making a counteroffer. Not today. But soon. And it won't be money."

The man's face didn't move. But behind him, at the end of the corridor, someone else was standing—a tall woman in a hound-master's leathers, arms crossed, watching Cyrus with an expression that was not quite hostility. Vess. The tracker. The one whose hounds had nearly had them at the border shrine, the one who had looked at the girl afterward like a woman who had heard something she couldn't unhear.

Their eyes met down the length of the corridor, torchlit and cold.

Vess looked away first—but slowly, the way a person looks away from a mirror when they don't like what the mirror is showing them.

And Cyrus, being marched back toward her cell with bare feet silent on the stone, smiled for the first time in nine days, because she had spent her life reading rooms full of wolves, and she knew a pack with a fracture in it when she saw one.

Somewhere below her feet—deeper than the cellars, deeper than the foundations, deeper than any syndicate had ever dug—she felt it again, faint as a pulse through a wrist: the mountain, waiting. Counting.

Seven days, the girl had whispered through the wall of her own cage, in the drowned tongue and then, for Cyrus's sake, in the common speech.

Six, now, she thought. Maybe five.

Whatever she was going to do, she would have to do it fast—and it would have to be worth more than money.
