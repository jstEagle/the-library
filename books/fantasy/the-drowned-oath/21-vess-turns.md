## 21. Vess Turns

The syndicate camp at the border shrine kept its fires low, the way soldiers do when they are ashamed of something.

Vess sat apart from the others with her back against a cold wall of the ruined shrine, and she did not sleep. She had not truly slept since the girl had looked at her across the trampled grass and spoken her debt aloud in a voice like water finding a crack in stone.

*Two hundred and eleven days,* the child had said—or rather, had not said, because the words had arrived inside Vess's skull the way a splinter arrives under skin, already deep before she felt it enter. *You were sold for two hundred and eleven days of your mother's fever. They bought the cure and they bought you with it. You have been paying since you were nine years old.*

Vess had put a knife to the girl's throat within the hour. It was what the syndicate paid her to do, and she was very good at doing what the syndicate paid her to do, and that had been the whole architecture of her life: a corridor of obedience with no windows, so well-lit from above that she had never once thought to ask where the light came from, or who held the lamp, or what it cost them.

Now she sat in the dark with her knife across her knees and counted the things she knew.

One: the girl's name, which the syndicate did not know, because the girl had never spoken it aloud in their hearing. *Sefa.* It meant something in the drowned tongue. Vess did not know what. She suspected she would learn.

Two: that the Almeida woman—Cyrus, lean as a whipstock, eyes like a ledger nobody could balance—had offered everything her guild still owned for one mute child, and had been refused, and had not wept, and had walked out of the syndicate master's tent with her spine straight as a plumb line. Vess had watched her go and had felt something shift in her chest like ballast sliding in a hold.

Three: that Master Corvane had sent word down the mountain roads tonight. Three riders, three sealed letters, three directions. The syndicate was no longer content to wait for the court's favor to be delivered. They were going down to take it themselves.

And four—the smallest thing, the thing that would not stop ticking against the inside of her ribs like a beetle trapped in a box—that the girl had not named Vess's debt to wound her. She had understood that only later, turning it over in the dark the way you turn over a coin found on a road, checking whether it is gold or gilt. The child had named it the way a physician names a sickness. Not to shame. To show her where the knife was already sitting.

*They bought the cure and they bought you with it.*

Vess's mother had lived. That was the arithmetic everyone in her village had celebrated: the fever broke, the woman recovered, the strange gray-cloaked men who brought the medicine asked nothing in return except that the daughter come work off the cost when she was old enough. Nine years old, and old enough. The village had called it charity. The village had called the syndicate benefactors for twenty years afterward, right up until the gray cloaks stopped coming at all, and then the village had called them nothing, because there was no one left in the village who remembered why the well water tasted faintly of salt.

She had spent nineteen years paying two hundred and eleven days. And the debt had never once gotten smaller. That was the thing about bond-debt that no one told you, the thing you learned only from the inside: interest ran backward here. Every year you served, you served *more*. Because each year of service was itself purchased on credit, and the credit accrued, and somewhere in a vault beneath the syndicate's counting house there was a page with her name on it that grew heavier instead of lighter, the way a stone grows heavier the longer you carry it, though the stone itself has not changed.

The girl had seen the page. Had read it through the wall of Vess's body like reading print through paper.

*It is not a debt,* the child had said, in the silence after, while the syndicate's hounds circled and Cyrus bled quietly from a split lip and Vess stood with her knife drawn and her hand shaking in a way it had never shaken in nineteen years of professional violence. *It is a chain wearing the clothes of a debt. Chains can be broken. Debts must be paid.*

Then the hounds had come and there had been no more time for speaking, and now here she was, third watch, cold wall, unsheathed knife, and the whole night holding its breath around her like a man waiting to hear which way a coin will land.

*

The camp slept badly. Even the guards. Vess heard them shifting at their posts, heard one of the young ones whisper a charm against drowning—a mountain charm, absurd, this far from any water deeper than a stream—and understood that the whole company had begun to feel what she felt: that the ground beneath them was listening. The shrine they'd made their camp in was old, older than the syndicate, older than the guilds, its god long nameless, and the stones had that particular patient quality of very old sacred places, as if they had seen every kind of bargain struck upon them and expected worse.

In the pen they'd built of hurdles and iron pins, the girl lay curled against the Almeida woman's side. Cyrus had refused the blanket they offered and taken the floor beside the cage instead, which Vess recognized, with a dull professional admiration, as exactly what she herself would have done: stay close, stay visible, make the guards nervous about hurting the prisoner because the other prisoner might be worth more than she appeared. Good tactics. Except that watching them now, in the blue hour before dawn, Vess could see it wasn't tactics. Cyrus's hand rested on the girl's hair the way you rest a hand on something you are afraid of losing, lightly, ready to withdraw, as if touch itself might be taxed.

Nineteen years, Vess thought, and no one had ever touched her like that. There had been hands on her shoulders—guiding, correcting, occasionally congratulating—but always with weight behind them, always with claim. The syndicate touched its people the way an owner touches stock.

She looked at her own hands in the dying firelight. Scarred knuckles. A burn scar along the thumb from a rope run too fast through a grip. A knife callus. Hands that belonged to the ledger, that had been signed away by her mother's gratitude and her own smallness, and that had done, in nineteen years—

She made herself count. It was a discipline; the syndicate taught it; accounting for your own violence kept you efficient. Eleven deaths she was certain of. Four more probable. A hundred beatings, deliveries, intimidations, burnings of shops whose owners had missed payments. A man in Lowmarsh whose fingers she'd taken joint by joint over a defaulted loan, who had begged, who had wept, who had thanked her at the end—thanked her—which was the worst part, worse than the screaming, because he had been so broken by then that mercy felt like grace and she had let him believe it was.

*Chains can be broken. Debts must be paid.*

What if it was neither? What if it was simply a choice, renewed every morning, that everyone pretended was a law?

Vess got up. She did not decide to get up; she noticed afterward that she had done it, the way you notice rain has started only when you are already wet. Her feet carried her past the sleeping guards, past the smothered fire, to the edge of the hurdle pen, and she crouched there in the dark looking at the girl through the lattice, and the girl was awake—of course she was awake—and looking back at her with those eyes that held depth without reflection, like water in a well shaft.

"I'm going to open this," Vess said. Her voice came out rough. She hadn't used it for anything but orders in months. "I don't know what happens after. I don't know if I'm freeing you or just moving my own chains onto different hooks. But I can't—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "He sent riders down the mountain tonight. Three letters. He means to go into the court's roads himself, with armed companies, and take what he decides belongs to him. Whatever's down there, it doesn't deserve him."

The girl tilted her head. And then she spoke—not into Vess's mind this time but with her actual voice, quiet and cracked with disuse, a real child's voice with a real child's hoarseness, and that undid Vess more than anything else that night:

"You're afraid."

"Yes."

"Good." The girl pressed her palm flat against the hurdles. "Afraid means the door isn't shut all the way yet."

*

Breaking the pen took less time than deciding to break it. Vess had built half the security of this camp herself, which was the particular irony of traitors everywhere: you are always best equipped to betray whatever trained you. She lifted the iron pins, folded back the hurdles, and stood aside, and for a moment neither prisoner moved, both of them watching her as if she might be a trap.

Cyrus rose first. Up close, unhurt and unhurried, she was even leaner than she'd seemed, all cable and shadow, and her eyes went over Vess the way a smuggler reads a customs post: cataloguing exits, weighing cargo, pricing the risk.

"Why," Cyrus said. Not hostile. Worse—genuinely asking.

"Because a child told me the truth about my life in four sentences, and I've had nineteen years to figure it out and never did." Vess heard her own voice shake and hated it and kept talking anyway. "Because I've spent my career collecting debts for people who invented them. Because I'm tired." She pulled the second knife from her boot and held it out, handle first. "Take it. You'll want it where we're going."

Cyrus didn't take it. "Where *we're* going?"

"The mountain road east of the shrine. I know which passes Corvane's riders took, which means I know which ones are empty. And when he finds his prisoners gone, he'll find me gone too, and he'll send the hounds, and the hounds answer to me before anyone else." Vess allowed herself one grim breath of almost-laughter. "Or they did. We'll find out how much standing I actually had."

"And when it runs out?"

"Standing always runs out. That's the lesson of my entire profession." She finally forced the knife into Cyrus's hand, closing the woman's fingers around the grip. "You owe me nothing for this. Understand? Nothing. I'm not buying anything with it. I'm—" The sentence collapsed. She looked away, at the girl, at the pale oval of the child's face in the dark. "I'm spending," she finished. "There's a difference. Ask your ledgers."

The girl reached out and touched Vess's wrist—just briefly, two fingers, feather-light—and the touch went through her like cold water through cloth, and when it passed, Vess felt something loosen in her chest that she had not known was knotted. Not gone. Loosened. Enough to breathe around.

"One hundred and ninety-three," the girl whispered.

"What?"

"That's what remains. Of the two hundred and eleven days. I took eighteen with me when I touched you." The child smiled, small and fierce. "Consider it a down payment. On the door being open."

Vess stared at her. "Can you do that for everyone? Just—reach in and take the debt?"

"It isn't taking." For the first time, the girl looked tired, anciently tired, older than her small body had any right to be. "It's carrying. Someone has to carry it eventually. That's what debts are." She turned toward the eastern dark, toward the mountain. "Come on. Dawn's coming, and dawn is when they'll check the pens."

*

They went out through the shrine's broken southern wall, single file, Vess leading, over stones worn glassy by centuries of pilgrim feet. Behind them the camp slept on, and Vess found herself cataloguing the sleeping shapes—the young guard with the drowning-charm, the cook who sang off-key, the quartermaster who cheated everyone including himself—and understanding, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she was walking away from the only family structure she had ever had, and that it had never once been a family, and that this fact had been available to her every day of nineteen years and she had needed a mute child to read it aloud.

The eastern pass opened ahead of them, black against the lesser black of the sky, and far up its throat, where the stars thinned, Vess could just make out the notch of the First Road's gate—a seam of darkness in the mountainside, sealed, according to every map she'd ever confiscated, since before her grandmother's grandmother.

Cyrus paused at the treeline and looked back the way they'd come. Vess watched her do it and understood she was not looking at the camp. She was looking south, toward Thornfell, toward a cold guildhall and a burned ledger and a grave she hadn't had time to dig properly.

"He'll come after us himself now," Cyrus said. "Corvane. Not the hounds. Him."

"You sound pleased."

"I sound *ready*." Cyrus turned, and in the last of the starlight her face was all angles and intent. "Every road he takes to reach us is a road I grew up in. He thinks he's hunting. He's walking into my house." She glanced at Vess, and something moved behind her eyes—assessment, yes, but underneath it, cautiously, the beginning of something else. "You know what he's carrying. The letters. What does the syndicate think the court will give them?"

"Favor. Passage rights. The old contracts." Vess shrugged. "Corvane believes debts are the only real currency. Down there, he'll try to buy a miracle with an invoice."

"Then he's about to meet the original creditors," Cyrus said, "and I promise you they don't honor his paper." She shouldered her pack and nodded at the pass. "Walk fast, walk quiet, and when I say drop, you drop."

They climbed. And behind them, faint across the miles, Vess heard—or dreamed she heard, or perhaps the mountain merely repeated it through the stone so that she would be sure to carry it down into the dark with her—a sound like a great bell underwater, tolling once, and then once more, as if something far below had marked the hour of their descent, and was counting.
