## 23. Roads My Family Built

The First Road did not want us.

I had known that from the moment the seal broke — known it in my teeth, in the way the air changed taste, going from stone-dust to something older and wetter, like the inside of a shell. But knowing a thing and feeling it are different debts, and by the second day below I had learned to feel this one in every step.

The girl walked ahead of me with her lantern held high, and the dark leaned back from her like a crowd making room for someone important. She had not spoken since we passed under the mountain's shoulder. She didn't need to. The tunnels spoke for her — or rather, they answered her, groaning open where she looked, sighing shut behind us where she didn't.

That should have comforted me. It didn't. It meant the road knew her name, and roads that know your name have opinions about what you owe.

* * *

My grandfather's journal had a section I'd read a hundred times as a girl and never understood: a page of diagrams that looked like nothing so much as a spider's argument with itself. Lines crossing lines. Little annotations in his cramped hand — *counterweight*, *false floor*, *sing here* — and at the bottom, in letters pressed so hard they'd nearly torn the paper:

**THE ROADS ARE THE OATH'S TEETH. FEED THEM OR THEY BITE.**

I understood now. Walking them, I understood with my whole body.

The first test came an hour after dawn-that-wasn't, in a corridor where the ceiling sloped down into a throat of black water. My grandfather's diagrams had shown this one. A flooded gallery, forty paces long, with a false bottom over a deeper channel — cross it light-footed or not at all, because the stones were balanced on levers old enough to have forgotten their own purpose, and any weight held too long would drop you into water that went down farther than rope.

I tested it the way he'd taught three generations of Almeidas to test things: with something expendable. I threw my spare boot.

It landed on the third stone. The stone sank a finger's width, held, and then — slowly, with the patience of machinery that had waited two centuries for exactly this — tilted. Water gulped around its edges. Somewhere below, something large turned over in its sleep.

"Light steps," I said. "Only ever one foot down. And don't stop moving."

The girl looked at the water, then at me, and her expression was the one I was coming to dread most: not fear but recognition. She knew this place. Not the way I knew it, from ink and inheritance, but the way you know a house you lived in before you could walk.

She crossed without a ripple.

I crossed with two, heart slamming, boots soaked to the ankle, and on the far side I sat down against the wall and laughed until it turned into something else and I had to press my sleeve against my eyes.

Two hundred years. Two hundred years my family had kept these traps oiled and honest, maintained them the way other families maintained shrines — and all of it, all of it, to guard a debt. To keep the court's things *in*. Or to keep oath-breakers *out*. Standing there wringing out my stockings in the dark, I honestly couldn't tell which side of the teeth we were standing on.

* * *

The second test was quieter, and it nearly killed us anyway.

We came up out of the flooded gallery into a long hall of pillars, each one carved — and this stopped me cold, because I had never heard of carvings in the deep roads; smugglers don't decorate, decoration is weight — each pillar carved with figures. Processions of them. Men and women walking downward, generation after generation, ring after ring around the stone, and every figure's face had been chiseled smooth.

Except one.

Near the top of the nearest pillar, at eye height, a single face remained: a man with heavy brows and a mouth set hard, and beneath him letters in the old guild cipher that I had to trace twice with my fingers before my heart would accept them.

**ALMEIDA. FIRST OF THE NAME.**

He stood apart from the procession. He wasn't walking down with the others. He was standing at the edge of the carving, looking back up toward the surface, toward daylight rendered as a few rough scratches at the pillar's crown.

"He sold them," the girl said.

I hadn't heard her voice in hours and it went through me like cold water. She was touching the pillar, her small fingers resting just below the smooth faces, and when she spoke again her voice had that doubled quality it sometimes took below ground — her own voice, and underneath it something older, resonant, like a bell struck inside a well.

"He sold them and he watched them go down. And he made the road so no one could follow and bring them back."

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, because the journal agreed with her. *The roads are the oath's teeth.* I had always read that as protection. Guarding the guild's secret. But teeth don't protect. Teeth keep things swallowed.

The hall of pillars had been built so that whatever the court sent up — if it ever sent anything up — would have to walk this corridor, past the record of everyone it had taken, past the founder's watching face. It wasn't a shrine. It was a gauntlet of guilt. My ancestors had built their shame into the architecture and called it security.

And then the floor asked its question.

There was no warning. One moment we were crossing the hall; the next, the stones beneath our feet began to hum, a low note I felt in my molars, and the pillars' shadows swung — though no light had moved — and pointed all at once toward the girl.

A voice came out of the floor. Out of the walls. Out of two hundred years of patient stonework, in the drowned tongue, and even without understanding I understood: it was asking who we were. Demanding a name. And I knew, with the certainty of blood, that the wrong answer — or silence — would open the floor.

The girl answered.

She said four words in the drowned tongue, and her voice cracked on the last one, and the shadows swung away from her and settled on me instead.

The hum climbed. The note sharpened. The floor wanted *my* name.

"Cyrus," I said aloud, uselessly, and then, because the dead don't listen to the living tongue, I did the only thing left. I knelt, put my palm flat against the stone, and spoke the words my grandfather had taught my father to teach me — the guild-words, the road-words, the ones we used over cargo and graves alike:

*"Almeida. Last sworn. The road is owed and the road is paid."*

The silence afterward lasted long enough for me to count eleven of my own heartbeats.

Then the humming faded, the shadows released me, and somewhere ahead, deep in the dark, a door I couldn't see unbarred itself with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

The girl was looking at me strangely. "You knew the words."

"I knew the words." I got up off my knees and found they shook. "I didn't know they'd still work."

"They work," she said softly, "because he built them to. He wanted the road to remember his family. Even the parts of the family he sold." Her hand was still on the pillar, on the smooth blank faces. "Cyrus. These aren't decorations. They're a list. He carved everyone who came down. So the court couldn't pretend it hadn't taken them."

I looked at the rings of faceless figures spiraling down into the stone, hundreds of them, more than I could count, and I thought about Odile's archive, about the other guilds, about children born into debt across two centuries and five kingdoms, and for the first time since the syndicate's hounds first caught my scent I felt something hotter than fear.

Good, I thought at the dead man's carved face. You wrote it down. Now I'm going to make it cost you.

* * *

*

The third test I never saw coming, because it wasn't a trap at all. It was a kindness, and kindness below the mountain is the cruelest thing there is.

We camped in a side chamber that my grandfather's journal marked with a symbol I'd assumed meant danger — a circle with a line through it. It meant shelter. Of course it did. The Almeida cipher ran backward through everything; we were smugglers, we hid truth in plain sight the way other people hid silver in flour barrels. The chamber had dry ledges, a vein of clean water seeping through the rock, and niches in the walls sized for oil lamps, still bearing two-hundred-year-old soot.

Someone had stocked this room once. Someone had meant for travelers to rest here.

I was laying out the last of our food — half a wheel of hard cheese, a fistful of dried plums, the heel of a loaf gone stone-stiff — when the girl made a sound. Small. I almost missed it under the drip of the water vein.

She was standing at the back wall, where the lamplight barely reached, looking at marks scratched into the stone. Tally marks. Hundreds of them, grouped in fives, marching along the wall and around the corner and out of sight.

Counting days.

Beside the tallies, in the same worn script, someone had written in the drowned tongue — and she read it aloud for me, translating haltingly, her finger under each word like a child at lesson:

*"Whoever rests here: you are not forgotten. We were many. We kept each other's count. If you can read this, add your days to ours, so the number stays true."*

The lamp guttered. Neither of us moved to fix it.

They had been prisoners here — or laborers, or sacrifices-in-waiting, I didn't know which was worse — and they had done the most human thing left to them. They had counted. They had left a message not of despair but of bookkeeping, because they were tunnel-people, road-people, and bookkeeping was prayer to people like us.

"Add yours," the girl said.

"I'm not—"

"You're resting here." She held out a shard of flint she'd found on the ledge. "So add your days. Or their counting stops being true."

My hands weren't steady. I scratched five marks beside theirs — one for each day since the guildhall went cold, since my uncle died with the ledgers burning, since a barefoot child walked through my door and the wards sang for her. Five days. It looked like nothing. It looked like everything.

Then, because the flint was still in my hand and the wall was listening, I added six more strokes in the guild cipher, quick and small, where only an Almeida would think to look:

**GONE UP. COMING BACK FOR THE REST.**

The girl watched me write it. She didn't ask what it meant. She touched the tallies once, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder, and came and sat beside me and ate her share of the plums in silence.

That night I dreamed of pillars. In the dream the faceless figures stepped out of the stone one by one and fell into step behind me, walking the way I walked, matching my stride, and none of them spoke, and none of them blamed me, and somehow that was worse than blame.

* * *

The fourth test was the last before the deep ways opened, and it was my grandfather's masterpiece, and I hated him for it.

The corridor forked. Both branches looked identical — same width, same slope, same damp breath of air from below. My journal-diagrams showed the fork too, and showed, in a margin note so bitter the ink had bled: **THE HONEST ROAD AND THE LIAR'S ROAD. HE WOULD WANT YOU TO CHOOSE RIGHT. HE NEVER SAID WHICH WAS WHICH.**

Above each arch hung a mechanism I recognized from the diagrams: counterweighted blades, salt-rusted but sound, poised to fall when the wrong traveler passed beneath. And set into the keystone of each arch, a scale. Not a metaphor. An actual balance, brass and bone, ancient pans swaying in the dark.

Under the left arch, the left pan held a small heap of salt.

Under the right arch, the right pan held nothing at all.

I stood there a long time. The girl stood with me, her shoulder against my arm, and I could feel her trying to understand it the way I was trying — reading the stone, reading the weights, reading two hundred years of a dead man's mind.

An honest road and a liar's road. Salt in one pan, emptiness in the other.

Salt was the guild's oldest word for value. *Worth one's salt.* Paid in salt. Salt on the lintel to keep the house honest. And the court below dealt in souls, and a soul weighed — Odile had told me that much, in her dust-smelling archive, tapping the old contracts — a soul weighed precisely as much as the life it owed.

If the salt represented payment given, then the empty pan was the debt unpaid, and the honest road was the one where the scales balanced — except they didn't balance, neither pair did, and that was the point. That was the whole vicious point. There was no honest choice at this fork. There was only the choice between admitting you owed something and pretending you didn't, and both roads led down, and both roads had teeth.

"He built this for oath-breakers," I said slowly. "Anyone coming down to renege. Anyone coming down to steal back what was sold. The scales weigh intention. They always have. Grandfather didn't build locks, he built confessions."

The girl stepped forward before I could stop her.

She stood between the two arches, lantern raised, and looked up at the empty pan, and then at the full one, and then she did something I will carry out of the dark places if I carry nothing else.

She reached into her pocket — the little pocket sewn into her dress where she kept her treasures, a river pebble, a moth wing, a button from my uncle's coat — and she took out the pebble, and she placed it in the empty pan.

The pan dipped. Held. The scales trembled and did not balance, because a pebble is not a soul, and then—

—and then the right-hand blade slid back into the stone above us, quiet as a closing eye, and the left-hand blade dropped and bit into the floor with a crack that echoed away down the liar's road, sealing it.

Not a confession. A *choice*. The scales hadn't weighed our guilt. They'd weighed whether we were willing to put something of ours into the empty pan — whether we came down to take, or came down owing and ready to pay.

The girl looked up at me. In the lantern light her face was very calm and very young and very old, all three at once, and I understood suddenly and horribly that she had known what the scales wanted. Of course she had. She was the collateral. She had spent her whole remembered life in the empty pan.

"Don't do that again," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I meant it. "Don't pay things for me. That's how this family got here. People paying for each other without being asked."

"It wasn't for you," she said. "It was for the road. Roads like to be acknowledged." A pause, and then, smaller: "Also it was a pebble. Cyrus. It was a pebble."

I laughed. Down here, under the mountain, in the dark my family had built and guarded and been damned by, I laughed until I had to hold the wall, and the girl laughed too, high and startled, like a bell rung by accident, and the sound went down the honest road ahead of us and something down there — far down, past the reach of any lantern — seemed to laugh back.

* * *

Beyond the fork, the character of the tunnels changed. The cuts became cleaner. Older. The tool-marks vanished altogether, replaced by surfaces that looked less carved than *grown*, flowing stone like frozen water, and the drowned tongue began appearing in the architecture itself — not inscribed but integral, letters swelling out of the walls the way grain shows in wood.

The girl slowed. Her lantern arm lowered. For the first time since the First Road sealed behind us, she looked afraid.

"What is it?" I whispered.

"This is where the family roads end," she said. "And the court's roads begin." She swallowed. "Your people built so well, Cyrus. Everything they made — the traps, the tests, the tally rooms — it all funnels here. Every road your family ever built leads down to one place." Her hand found mine in the dark, small and cold and gripping hard. "They didn't build a labyrinth to hide the way. They built a labyrinth to make sure anyone who came this far had no choice but to arrive."

Ahead, the passage opened — I felt the change in the air before the light confirmed it, a vastness inhaling — and far below us, impossibly far, I saw it: a glow. Not firelight. Not lamplight. A pale, patient shimmer, like moonlight that had gotten lost and decided to stay lost.

Water. A great deal of water, glowing faintly from beneath.

And rising out of it, half-drowned, half-remembered, the silhouette of a chapel.

The girl's grip tightened on my hand.

"There," she said, in the drowned tongue and in ours, both at once, and the word came out of her like something surfacing after a long time underwater. "That's where they keep the paper."
