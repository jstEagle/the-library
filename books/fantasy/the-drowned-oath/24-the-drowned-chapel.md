## 24. The Drowned Chapel

The water came first as a sound.

Cyrus heard it before she saw it—a hush beneath the mountain's silence, a breathing that was not wind. The First Road had narrowed for the better part of a day, its walls sweating brine, and now it opened without warning into a cavern where the ceiling vanished into blackness and the floor became, gently and without any line to mark the change, a lake.

It did not look deep. That was the first lie of the place. The water lay flat as poured glass, dark as tea left too long in the cup, and it gave back their lantern-light in a way that made Cyrus's skin crawl—because the reflection was not quite theirs. In the mirrored surface her lantern burned white instead of gold, and beside her the girl stood taller, older, robed in something that moved like weed and silk together.

"Stop looking at it," Cyrus said, and realized she was speaking the drowned tongue, the words coming out round and strange on her tongue. She'd been doing that for two days now. The girl's language was settling into her like silt into a riverbed.

The girl did not stop looking. She crouched at the water's edge, barefoot despite the cold, and pressed one finger to the surface.

Ripples spread outward—and then inward, against all sense, drawing toward her fingertip as if the lake were drinking from her touch.

"It remembers me," the girl said. Her voice in this place carried strangely, doubled, as though something below repeated each word a half-beat after she spoke it. "Not this body. The other one."

"The other one can wait." Cyrus shrugged off her pack and began unpacking rope, wax, the little clay seals Odile had pressed on her in the salt cellars what felt like a lifetime ago. *Seal your breath, seal your thoughts, seal your name.* "We didn't come here to be remembered. We came for the contract."

"It is the same thing," the girl said mildly, and stepped into the water.

"Wait—" But there was no stopping her. The girl walked out onto the lake as if it were flagstone, and where her feet fell the surface dimpled but did not give, holding her up the way ice holds a sparrow. She turned back, and her eyes in the lantern-light were the color of the water itself. "The chapel is under. It has always been under. You knew that when you chose this road—you just didn't let yourself know it."

Cyrus looked at the water. Looked at the maps in her head—the ones her grandfather had drawn, the ones Odile had reconstructed from fragments, the ones the girl had sung into shape with words no living kingdom used. Every version agreed: the original contract, blood and salt, signed by the first Almeida, rested in a chapel that had drowned when the mountain drank its own river.

She began sealing her breath with wax and will, the old smuggler's trick, and told herself the tightness in her chest was only the clay across her lips.

* * *

Underwater, the world went quiet in a way that was not silence.

Sound moved differently—slowed, thickened, arriving late. Cyrus swam down through water that warmed as it deepened, which was wrong, which was the second lie of the place, and followed the pale ghost of the girl ahead of her like a lamp following a moth. Below them, impossibly, light rose to meet them: a soft green-gold glow, sourceless, the color of shallow sea over sand.

The chapel resolved out of the murk all at once, the way things do in dreams.

It was small. That undid her more than grandeur would have. A humble vaulted nave, built by hands that had expected maybe forty worshippers, sunk whole and intact—pews still ranked, an altar still dressed, candles still standing in their sconces though they had been under for two centuries. And burning. Every candle burned, flame steady and upright beneath the water, shedding that green-gold light, and none of them had ever gone out.

The girl touched down on the flooded aisle and walked, her hair moving around her face like ink in oil. Cyrus landed harder, boots stirring up a whisper of silt, and stood a moment simply breathing through the seal, heart hammering, taking it in: the fish that drifted between the pews unbothered, pale and eyeless; the altar cloth rotted to lace; the font at the door still full, though the whole world was full around it, and somehow the font held its water apart from the water, a cup within a cup.

On the altar lay a book.

Not a grand thing. A ledger, actually—the same make and cut as the guild ledgers Cyrus had grown up keeping, the same ruled lines, the same oxblood leather gone soft with soaking. Of course. Her grandfather had commissioned his false books from the same bindery as his true ones. A man hides a thing best in plain sight, among its brothers.

The girl reached the altar first. She did not touch the book. She stood before it with her hands clasped behind her back, the way a child stands before a gift she has been told not to open, and said, in the doubled voice that the water echoed:

"This is where I was sold."

Cyrus came up beside her. "You weren't sold. You were promised. There's a difference, and I'm going to use it."

"You keep saying things like that." The girl glanced at her, and there was something achingly old in the look, some residue of the person she had been before she was small. "Things that are brave and not true."

"Brave is how untrue things get started," Cyrus said. "Open it."

* * *

The book opened to the middle, to a page that had waited two hundred years to be read, and the water did not blur the ink. Blood does not run, even underwater, when it is written in oath. The letters stood up off the page in brown-black relief, each one sharp as the day it dried, and Cyrus read aloud because reading silently felt like letting the words move unseen:

*"Let it be known beneath stone and above it, before the court that endures and the kings who do not: that Edran Almeida, called Salt-Hand, being master of the guild that bears his name, does sell and surrender unto the Drowned Court one soul, freely given, of his own line, to be delivered when born and kept until paid..."*

There it was. The founding of everything. The fortune that had built the guildhall, bought the roads, bribed the harbormasters, kept three generations fed and fat and safe—all of it purchased with a single line of credit drawn on a soul not yet born.

And there, at the bottom, the signature. *Edran Almeida.* She knew the hand. She had traced it a thousand times in the guild's true ledgers, learning her letters from his flourishes. The E with its long spine. The final a looped like a noose.

Beside it, in different ink—in the court's ink, which even now seemed to shift and swim like something alive—was the countermark. The court's acceptance. The terms of payment. And below all of it, ruled off neat and waiting, a blank line.

A blank line for a second signature. For confirmation. For renewal upon delivery.

Cyrus stared at it, and the trap of it closed around her so completely that for a moment she could not breathe past the wax seal. It wasn't enough, the court's law, that a dead man had sold a living child. The contract required the delivered soul—or its guardian—to sign acknowledgment. To accept the debt in person. To make the grandfather's crime into the granddaughter's consent.

That was why the syndicate wanted the girl delivered rather than dead. That was why the court had let them walk this far, had lit their road with patient candlelight. They hadn't come to take her. They had come to get her *signed.*

"They need my hand," Cyrus whispered. The words buzzed against the clay. "Or yours. The line isn't closed until one of us signs."

The girl nodded slowly. Water moved in her eyes without falling. "I tried to sign it once. Long ago. Before." She held up her small hands, turned them over, studied the palms as though expecting to find ink there still. "They would not let me finish. They said I must be delivered first. Grown again. Whole again. There are rules even for them." A pause, and then, quieter, in the voice of the child she appeared to be: "I ran instead. That is how I came to your door."

The storm. The threshold. The wards answering her. Cyrus had thought, all these months, that something old had found them. She understood now that it was the reverse: the girl had been running from this chapel since before the guild had a name, and Cyrus's door had simply been the first honest lock she'd found.

"We don't sign," Cyrus said. "We burn it. We take the page and we—"

"No." The girl's hand closed over hers, small and cold and strong. "Look."

Cyrus looked. At the bottom of the page, beneath the blank signature line, in the court's swimming ink, a new line was writing itself. Slowly. Letter by letter, as though a pen moved somewhere in the deep, patient as erosion:

*Delivered: one (1). Days remaining: six.*

Six days. The countdown from the cage of brass and law, still counting. And beneath it, forming now, a second line:

*Witness required: blood of Almeida, present and consenting.*

"They're not asking you to bring me," the girl said. "They're asking you to agree."

* * *

Cyrus had killed a man nine roads ago and remembered every second of it. She thought she had learned, that night, the full weight of what a life cost. She had been wrong. This was heavier. This was a man two hundred years dead reaching through ink and water to spend a child he had never met, and using Cyrus's own blood as the coin.

"I never signed anything," she said aloud, to the chapel, to the candles, to whatever listened beneath the mountain. "My grandfather had no right. My family had no right. And I am telling you now, before this court's own altar—I contest the debt."

The flames all bent sideways at once, though nothing moved.

In the silence that followed, the girl tugged her sleeve. Pointed down.

Beneath the altar, visible now through the lace of the rotted cloth, something else was written—scratched into the stone itself, hidden under the book, hidden perhaps for two hundred years from everyone except whoever finally stood exactly here, exactly at this angle, with the light of the ever-burning candles falling just so. Letters gouged deep, in a hurried hand. Not the court's ink. Not blood. Chisel-work, done fast, done in fear or fury.

Cyrus leaned close, wiped away a veil of silt with her palm, and read the words her grandfather—or someone—had carved into the altar's bones before the waters closed over them:

*THE SOUL SOLD WAS NOT OURS TO SELL.*

The candles guttered, every one, all together, and in the sudden dark the girl's grip tightened on her wrist, and from far above—through fathoms of wrong-warm water, through stone, through the long throat of the First Road—came a sound like a great door beginning to open.
