## 26. Seven Days, Six Left

The dark below the mountain does not count the way the sky counts. The sky counts in birds and light and the slow turning of seasons; the mountain counts in heartbeats, in drips of water striking stone, in the long patience of things that were old before anyone thought to name them old.

I count too. I have always counted. Even before I had a mouth that worked, even when my voice lived only in the drowned places where no one could hear it, I counted. Six days now. Six days left of the seven the court promised through the stone, and I can feel each one like a coin being taken from my hand by fingers I cannot see.

Cyrus is sleeping. She sleeps badly now — she has slept badly since the Bone Galleries, since Vess fell with three syndicate blades in her and did not rise again. Cyrus's breathing has a hitch in it, a small broken place, and every time it catches I wake a little more. We are camped in a hollow off the Second Road, a smuggler's pocket her grandfather carved for men who needed to disappear. There is a lantern turned down to a coal. There is a blanket that smells of salt and lamp oil and, underneath all of it, faintly, of someone who loved me without ever once saying so in any language I was born knowing.

I watch her hands in the coal-light. They are scarred across the knuckles — rope burns, blade scars, one white seam from a night she has never told me about. Those hands have carried me over rivers, pulled me out of drowning water, killed a man so that I could keep walking. And tomorrow, or the day after, or however many days remain, those hands are going to do something terrible, because she has already decided what the blank line in the contract is for.

She thinks I don't know. She thinks the drowned tongue is a thing I speak and not a thing that speaks *through* me, that the court's whispering stops at the edge of my sleep. But I hear everything the mountain says. I heard the Oathkeeper offer its terms: *the girl stays, or a volunteer of equal blood takes her place.* Equal blood. There is no equal blood. There is only Cyrus, who signed nothing, owes nothing, whose name was never written in any ledger — except that she will write it there anyway, in blood and salt, because that is what she does. She pays. She has been paying her whole life for debts other people made, and she has never once learned to stop.

So tonight, while the coal gutters and the mountain counts, I am going to do the hardest thing I have ever done with my new voice.

I am going to tell her to leave me.

* * *

I wait until her breathing deepens into something almost like rest. Then I sit up. The cold of the stone comes up through my legs and settles in my bones, and I let it, because I want to be sure. I want to be as sure as the mountain is sure.

"Cyrus."

Her eyes open instantly. That is a smuggler's sleep — one breath from waking, always. She looks at me the way she always does first: checking. Counting my hands, my face, listening for the court in my voice. Finding me whole, some knot in her shoulders lets go.

"You should be sleeping," she says. Her voice is rough. It has been rough since Vess. Everything about her has been rough since Vess, like a rope dragged over rock.

"I can't." I pull my knees up. "It talks to me at night. You know that."

"The court."

"The court." I look at the ceiling of the hollow, at the veins of pale mineral running through the black stone like frozen water. "It isn't cruel, exactly. That's the worst part. It's patient. It's been waiting two hundred years and it can wait six days like six minutes. It keeps telling me —" I stop. My throat closes. In the drowned tongue there are perfect words for this, words like water finding cracks, but in the language Cyrus gave me back, the language of kingdoms, I have to build the sentence plank by plank. "It keeps telling me how it ends. Both ways it ends. It wants me to choose the easy one."

"There's no easy one," Cyrus says flatly. "There's just the one where nobody signs anything, and we find another road."

"There is no other road. Odile said —"

"Odile also sold us to the syndicate." The bitterness comes off her like heat off a stove. "Forgive me if I've stopped building my hopes out of Odile."

"She came back," I say quietly. "She walked down alone, past the syndicate's dead, past the bone galleries, to bring us the loophole. People are allowed to be two things, Cyrus. You taught me that."

She doesn't answer that. In the coal-light her jaw works, the way it does when she is losing an argument with herself and refusing to admit it. I make myself go on, because if I stop now I never will.

"I heard you talking to yourself last night. By the lantern. You thought I was asleep."

The silence stretches. Somewhere far below us, water falls into water, counting.

"You're going to sign it," I say. "You're going to give yourself to the court and walk me out of here, and you've already decided, and you think that because you decided it quietly, it won't break anything."

"Cyrus Almeida breaks lots of things," she says at last, and tries to make it light, and fails, because her voice cracks in the middle like river ice. "It's practically the guild motto."

"Don't joke."

"It's how I pray."

* * *

I move closer to her. The blanket rustles. Up close I can see the gray coming into her hair at the temples, more of it than there was a season ago, as if the roads themselves have been bleaching her year by year. I put my hands in my lap so she cannot see them.

Because my hands are shaking.

They have been shaking since the chapel, since I saw the contract with her grandfather's signature dried brown in its lines and the blank space below it glowing like a held breath. They shook in the cage of brass and law while the court counted down through the stone. They shake now, and they will shake tomorrow, and I understand suddenly — with a clarity that feels like falling — that this is what fear is. Not the dark. Not the syndicate. Not even the court with its patient herald and its throne of salt. Fear is your own body telling you the truth your mouth refuses to say.

And still I say it.

"Leave me here."

The words come out small. Smaller than I meant them. I make myself say them again, louder, in the drowned tongue first and then in hers, so there can be no mistaking: *"Leave me. Go up the First Road. Take the maps. Refound the guild. Live."*

Cyrus goes very still. Stillness, on her, is dangerous — I have seen it come before violence, before hard choices, before the moment she steps off a ledge into black water because standing still is worse. But this stillness is different. This stillness is hurt.

"No," she says.

"You have to. Listen to me — please, listen, I've listened to you for a whole year, listen to me for five minutes." My voice is climbing and I can't stop it. "This is what I'm *for*. Do you understand? I was born for this. Before I was born, I was for this. Some girl in the drowned court, hundreds of years ago, promised back and back and back until the promise wore me like a glove. Every road we walked, every shrine, every toll — they weren't taking me somewhere. They were bringing me home. I felt it in the mine when the water went calm around me. I felt it in the chapel. The mountain knows my name, Cyrus. It knew my name before the guild did."

"That's what it *wants* you to think—"

"It's what I *know*." And here is the terrible part, the part I have been carrying like a stone in my chest for six days: "And it doesn't even hurt. That's the secret nobody tells you. When I stand close to the deep places, something in me goes quiet, like a door finally closing that had been rattling in the wind my whole life. Maybe that's the trap. Maybe. But maybe it's just true, and I was always going to end here, and the only choice I get is whether I waste your life on the way down."

"My life," Cyrus says, "is not yours to spend."

"Neither is yours mine!"

The words echo in the hollow and die against the stone. For a moment neither of us breathes. The lantern-coal pops. Far beneath us, so far it might be imagination, something vast turns over in its sleep and murmurs a number: *six.*

Cyrus reaches out and takes my chin in her scarred hand and turns my face to hers, gently, the way she did the first night on the threshold, when the wards answered me and she looked at me like a woman reading her own death warrant in a child's eyes.

"Listen to me," she says, and her voice is low and level and absolutely certain, which is how I know how much it is costing her. "Two hundred years ago, my grandfather sat in a warm room with wine in his cup and signed away a person he'd never meet, because it was easier than being poor. Everyone told him it was fate. Everyone told him it was necessary. He wrote it all down in his pretty journal with his pretty handwriting, and do you know what every page of that journal sounds like to me now?"

I don't answer.

"*Excuses,*" she says. "Every word. He dressed a betrayal up as destiny and called the dressing an oath. And I will burn every road my family ever built before I sit in a warm room and do the same thing and call it love." Her thumb brushes my cheek, rough and careful at once. "You were not born to be paid to someone. Nobody is. That's the whole lie, Wren. That's the lie under every ledger in every guildhall in every kingdom. That people can be *owed*."

Wren. The name she gave me, the small bird name, the name that means nothing to any court or ledger or oath. She uses it now deliberately, and it lands in my chest like a hand pressed over a wound.

"But the contract—"

"Is fraud. Odile proved it. The first Almeida sold something he never owned — he sold the mountain its own soul and pocketed the difference. The whole oath is built on a theft. And I will not finish a theft by adding myself to the scale like a coin." She leans closer. "You want to know what I'm for? Fine. I'll tell you what I'm for. I'm for getting you out. That's it. That's the whole of it. A year ago I was the last smuggler of a dead guild hauling somebody else's cargo, and then a barefoot girl stood in my doorway during a storm and the wards answered her, and for the first time in my life I was carrying something that mattered. I am not putting that cargo down six days from the door."

My hands are shaking so badly now that I fold them tighter in my lap, but she sees. Of course she sees. She sees everything.

"And you?" she asks softly. "What are your hands doing?"

"They're fine," I say.

"Wren."

"They're — " And then, horribly, the truth comes up out of me like floodwater through a floor drain, and I am crying, ugly and silent, the way I cried before I had words, and my shaking hands come up between us where she can see them and betray everything. "They won't stop. They haven't stopped since the chapel. I don't want to stay. I said I was meant for it, I said it didn't hurt, I said the door was finally closing — and it's all true and it's all a lie and I don't know which parts are which anymore. I don't want to be the court's. I want — "

I stop. Because the next word is the one I have never said, in either tongue, and saying it will make leaving impossible and staying unbearable and I do not know which of those I can survive.

Cyrus waits. She is very good at waiting. It is the smuggler in her — the patience of someone who has crouched in reeds for whole nights waiting for a patrol to pass.

"I want to learn the stars," I say instead, wrecked and quiet. "You promised. In the high passes. You said when this was done you'd teach me all of them, the sailors' ones and the liars' ones, and the road-names, all of them, north to south. You promised."

"I know," Cyrus says.

"So don't sign. Don't you dare sign. Find another way. Steal one. You're a smuggler — smuggling is just loving something enough to carry it past every gate they put in your way. So carry me past this one. Carry me past *this* one, Cyrus, please, please —"

She pulls me in. I end up against her shoulder with her arms around me, and she smells of salt and smoke and the long cold roads, and she holds on the way she does everything — like it costs her, like she'd pay double, like paying is the only prayer she knows.

"Okay," she says into my hair.

I go still. "Okay?"

"Okay. No signatures. No volunteers. No equal blood." Her chest rises and falls under my cheek, once, twice, and I realize she is steadying herself the way I steady myself, borrowing calm from the rhythm of another body. "We have six days. Odile's loophole is real — the oath was sworn on a lie, and lies rot, even old ones, even holy ones. We prove the lie before the court. That's the road. If the court won't take the proof—" her arms tighten fractionally, "—then we do what Almeidas do best."

"Which is?"

"We steal you back."

A sound comes out of me that is half laugh and half sob, and my hands, pressed between us, are still trembling — but differently now. Less like falling. More like a bird beating at a window it has decided, finally, to fly through.

* * *

Later, when the coal has burned to nothing and the dark is total and kind, I lie awake listening to her breathe. The hitch is still there. It will always be there now; grief leaves seams the way blades do. But she chose it, I think. She looked at the blank line and the easy mercy and the two hundred years of patient pressure, and she chose the hard road with a limping child on her back, because that is who she is, and no court and no contract and no grandfather's clever signature will ever change it.

Six days.

Far below, the mountain murmurs its number into the stone, patient as tide. But tonight, for the first time, I murmur something back — three words of the drowned tongue, the ones she taught me, the ones that mean *the road remembers* — and I would swear, lying in the dark with my shaking hands finally still, that somewhere far beneath us, something old and vast and cheated paused in its counting.

And began, very slowly, to listen.
