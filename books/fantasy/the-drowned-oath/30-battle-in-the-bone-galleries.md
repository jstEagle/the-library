## 30. Battle in the Bone Galleries

The dark below Thornfell was not empty. Cyrus had known that since she was nine years old, when her uncle first took her down the First Road and told her to walk softly, because the mountain listened. She had thought it a smuggler's superstition then, the kind of thing old men said to keep children quiet on the stairs.

She did not think it now.

The ossuary tunnels opened around them like the ribs of something vast and long-dead and not entirely dead at all. Walls of packed bone rose on either side—femurs stacked like cordwood, skulls set into the mortar of the seams so that they faced inward, every one of them, watching the road. The lantern light slid across them and they gleamed, yellow-white and wet-looking, as though the mountain had only just finished arranging them and had not yet had time to let them dry.

"Keep moving," Cyrus said. Her voice came out flat and small in all that architecture. "Don't look at them longer than you have to."

"I'm looking," the girl said.

Of course she was. She walked with her chin up and her bare feet silent on the bone-dust floor, and the skulls turned—not visibly, never visibly, but Cyrus felt it, the way you feel a room shift its attention—and the girl looked back at them without fear, the way you look at relatives.

Behind them, far back along the galleries, the syndicate's lamps were coming.

* * *

Vess heard them before she saw them: boots on stone, too many boots, and the wet click of the hounds' claws. She had put three hundred paces between herself and the smuggler and the child, and she had done it deliberately, walking backward down the gallery with her knife low and her lamp held high, because someone had to be the rear guard and no one else was going to volunteer.

Her bond-debt sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. It had sat there for eleven years, ever since she'd signed with the Meridian Syndicate to buy her brother out of a debt of his own, and she had told herself for eleven years that a debt was a debt was a debt, that the arithmetic of it made it clean. Then a mute child had looked at her in a border shrine and spoken the exact sum of what Vess owed, to the copper, in a language Vess didn't know and understood anyway, and the arithmetic had never been clean again.

The hounds came around the bend.

They were not dogs. They had been dogs once—the syndicate fed its debts into them too, she'd always suspected—but now they were long and low and jointed wrong, their hides stretched over frames like the bone walls themselves, their eyes lamp-bright and empty. There were six of them, and behind them came the enforcers, a dozen at least, crossbows cocked, and behind them, somewhere, the master's voice saying *the collateral, the collateral, nothing else matters but the collateral.*

Vess set her lamp on a skull shelf where the light would reach farthest. Then she drew her second knife.

"Hold," she called, and her voice echoed down the gallery and came back to her wearing the bones' own accent. "Contract dispute. I'm invoking clause nine."

One of the enforcers laughed. "There is no clause nine."

"There is," Vess said. "It's the one where the person holding the rear gets to choose what the rear costs." And she threw her lamp into the bone-oil she'd been pouring behind her for the last quarter mile.

* * *

The fire went up the gallery like a rumor.

Cyrus felt it before she saw it—a breath of heat against the back of her neck, a bloom of orange light chasing their shadows up the walls ahead of them, making ten thousand skulls flicker and grin. The girl grabbed her hand. Not for comfort. To steer.

"This way," the girl said, in the drowned tongue first, and then, catching herself, in the trade speech that sat strangely in her mouth, like a song translated by someone who loved the melody more than the words. "This way, this way—the road remembers us here."

"The road can remember whatever it likes if there's a wall in front of it," Cyrus said, but she followed, because the girl had not yet been wrong about the deep places, and because behind them the fire was roaring and Vess was somewhere inside it.

They ran. The gallery narrowed, widened, split; the girl chose at every fork without hesitating, left, right, left again, down a stair cut so steeply into the living rock that the steps were more ladder than stair. Bone gave way to bone—the deeper roads were older, and older meant stranger, and here the walls were not arranged but grown, ribs fused into arches, spines braided into pillars, whole ribcages hung from the ceiling like chandeliers that some enormous hand had forgotten to light.

Then the hand lit them.

Pale flames woke inside the bone chandeliers one after another as the girl passed beneath, cold green fire that cast no heat, and Cyrus understood that the mountain was lighting their way, and understood also what that meant: the court knew they were coming. The court had always known. Everything below was either an invitation or a trap, and down here the two words meant the same thing.

"They're past the fire," the girl said quietly. "Some of them. The ones who don't burn."

"How many?"

"Three. And the tall one."

Vess. Cyrus's stomach turned over. "And Vess?"

The girl didn't answer, which was an answer, and Cyrus ran faster.

* * *

Vess had killed four of them when the fire found its limit and died against a seam of wet stone, and the gallery went dark except for the burning bones of the hounds, which burned green and did not stop moving.

She had learned, in eleven years of enforcing other people's contracts, exactly how much punishment a body could absorb and keep standing, and she had spent most of that budget. Something in her left side was wrong in the way that things are wrong when they will not be right again. Her knives were gone—one buried in an enforcer's throat, one snapped off in a hound's jaw. She had taken a crossbow bolt through the meat of her shoulder and broken the shaft off rather than let it drag.

The tall one came last. The master's own shadow, the one they called the Auditor, who carried no weapon because he *was* one, who moved through the smoke like ink poured upward.

"You're in breach," the Auditor said. Its voice was mild. That was the worst thing about it. "Clause nine isn't real. You invented it."

"Yeah," Vess said. She spat blood, mostly teeth-adjacent. "I've been inventing things my whole career. Turns out I'm good at it."

"It changes nothing. The collateral comes below. The oath completes. You'll be entered into the record as an obstruction."

"Enter this," Vess said, and pulled the last thing from her coat—not a knife, not a lamp, but the contract itself, her bond-debt, the paper she had carried against her heart for eleven years because the syndicate required it and because she had never once dared imagine being without it.

She had seen what the child could do. She had heard her own debt read aloud in a voice like water finding a crack in a dam.

She held the contract to the nearest burning hound.

The paper caught with a sound like a scream played backward, and Vess felt it go—felt the stone lift out of her chest, felt eleven years of arithmetic come apart all at once, and with it came everything the stone had been holding down: her brother's face, the day she signed, the smell of the syndicate hall, the name she'd almost forgotten wanting. She laughed. It hurt more than the bolt.

The Auditor stopped moving. For the first time, it looked uncertain, and uncertainty on that face was the most frightening thing in the tunnel, and Vess found she wasn't frightened at all.

"That's not—" it began. "You can't void a bond by—"

"Watch me," Vess said, and charged it with her bare hands and her broken body and her brand-new, unencumbered, entirely free soul, and bought with all of it another hundred paces of road.

* * *

Cyrus heard the end of it.

There was no way to describe it honestly. Sound carried strange in the bone galleries; the mountain bent it, returned it, delivered it whole to whoever it wished. What reached her was a crash, a shout that might have been triumph or agony or both, and then—clearer than anything, in a voice that was not Vess's and seemed to come from everywhere, from the walls, from the skulls, from the drowned tongue itself:

*Paid in full.*

The girl stumbled. Stopped. Put her hand flat against the fused-rib wall as though steadying herself against a wave.

"What," Cyrus said. "What was that."

The girl lifted her head. Her eyes were shining, wet, wide. "She paid," she said. "The one who hunted us. She burned her own debt and the mountain took the fire instead and—" her voice cracked, young suddenly, terribly young, "—and it *thanked* her. It doesn't thank anyone. It thanked her."

Cyrus stood in the green-lit dark with her heart going like a hammer on a door, and grief arrived mid-stride, mid-motion, with no time to sit down under it. Vess. The woman who had tracked them across half the kingdom, who had put a blade within a finger's width of Cyrus's throat at the border shrine, who had wavered and turned and freed them in the night and paid for it with her standing and then, apparently, with everything else. Grief for an enemy. Grief for the only person in the world besides the girl who had ever understood what a debt was from the inside.

"We don't stop," Cyrus said, and her voice was rough and she let it be rough. "We don't stop and we don't waste it. She bought the road. We walk it."

"She's in the walls now," the girl said softly. "With the others. She's listening."

"Then let's give her something worth hearing."

* * *

The last three enforcers came at them in a gallery so narrow they could only come one at a time, and Cyrus, who had spent thirty years learning the mathematics of tight spaces, discovered that her body remembered everything even when her hands shook. The first one she took with a knee and a thrown elbow and the particular smuggler's trick of letting your opponent's momentum do the killing work. The second she shot with the pistol she'd been saving, the ball striking sparks off the bone wall an inch from his ear and convincing him, wisely, to reconsider his employment.

The third was the one who didn't burn.

It came out of the smoke behind them—not an enforcer at all, she realized, but one of the hounds that had worn a man's shape, joints folding the wrong way, green fire guttering in the sockets of its face—and it was fast, faster than anything had a right to be in a corridor that narrow, and Cyrus knew with a cold, clarifying certainty that she was not going to be fast enough.

The girl stepped between them.

"No," Cyrus said—

But the girl wasn't looking at the hound. She was looking *through* it, and she spoke one word in the drowned tongue, a word like a key turning, like a ledger closing, like a name being called home, and the hound stopped. Shuddered. The green fire in its eyes guttered, steadied, and then bowed—there was no other word for it—the whole wrong-jointed body bowing low, forehead nearly to the bone floor, in the posture of a servant before a magistrate.

"It forgot whose it was," the girl said. Her voice shook. "Down here, everything belongs to somebody. I reminded it." She touched the hound's smoking head, gently, the way you'd touch a tired dog. "Go home. Be bones."

It went. It simply came apart, kindly, into the dust it should have been all along, and the dust settled over the girl's bare feet like a blessing.

Cyrus looked at her—at this child she had pulled in from a storm, this soul-price, this collateral, this impossible, ancient, seven-year-old girl—and said, because there was nothing else to say, "Remind me never to owe you money."

The girl smiled. It was a small smile, and sad, and it did not reach anywhere near the fear in her eyes. Because she knew, and Cyrus knew, what waited at the end of the road: a throne, a king, a blank line, and a price that kindness could not pay.

* * *

The galleries ended the way rivers end: all at once, and wider than you believed possible.

They came out onto a ledge above a cavern that the lantern could not measure—vast, black, breathing. Far below, impossibly far, a pale light moved like something swimming under ice. Stairs descended from the ledge in sweeps of white stone, each step carved from a single femur longer than a ship's mast, and at the bottom, at the very floor of the world, stood doors.

Not doors like the guildhall's. Doors like a mouth deciding whether to speak.

And rising up the stair toward them, unhurried, patient as interest accruing, came a figure robed in salt-white, carrying no lamp and needing none, its face smooth as the inside of a shell.

The Oathkeeper. The herald. It had waited two hundred years, and it had stopped waiting.

"Almeida," it said, and the word filled the cavern the way water fills a hull. "You are expected. Bring the child. Bring your evidence. Bring your courage, if you have any left."

Cyrus put her hand on the girl's shoulder and felt the tremor running through her, and answered steadily, because Vess had bought this road and Odile had bled for the loophole and her grandfather's fraud had waited two centuries to be called what it was:

"We brought all three. Lead on."

Behind them, faint and far, the syndicate's lamps spilled into the upper galleries like a tide coming in.

The doors below began, slowly, to open.
