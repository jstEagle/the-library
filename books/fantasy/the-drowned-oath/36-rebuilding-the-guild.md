## 36. Rebuilding the Guild

The years above the mountain taught me that grief does not fade so much as it learns to live in the body quietly, the way water lives in stone.

I was eleven when I walked out of the First Road alone, and the mountain sealed itself behind me with a sound I have never been able to describe—not thunder, not a door, but something closer to a long breath drawn in and held. I stood in the thin gold light of evening with Cyrus's journal pressed against my chest, and I did not cry until dark came, because crying felt like leaving her twice.

Her name was Cyrus Almeida. Say it, Odile told me once, in the first winter after. Names are roads too. If you never say them, they wash out.

So I said it every morning for years. I said it over the cold hearth of the guildhall while I learned which beams were rotten and which would hold a roof. I said it when I learned to read her hand—the quick slanted script, the margins crowded with tolls and warnings, *the ferryman takes memories, do not pay him your name*, *the third shrine drinks but the fourth only listens*, *if the water rises without touching you, you are already known*. I said it when I slept in her old room because my own room smelled of nothing at all, and hers smelled of lantern oil and salt and someone who had chosen me.

That is where the years went: into the hall, into the journal, into the saying of her name.

* * *

The guildhall had been dead seven years by then, and it took four more to bring it back to something like life. I was not alone in the work, though for a long time I believed I was.

Odile came first, of course. She arrived on a gray morning in early spring with two mules loaded down with ledgers and an expression that dared me to comment on either. She had sold us once—to buy her archive's safety—and she had spent every year since trying to buy back what she'd spent. Guilt, I learned, can be a kind of engine. It burns dirty, but it burns.

"I'm not asking forgiveness," she said, standing in the doorway where I had once stood barefoot in a storm, the wards singing over my head. "I'm asking for work."

"The archive comes with you?" I asked.

"The archive *is* me," she said. "You may as well ask if my hands come with me."

So she moved into the east wing, the one with the deep shelves and the small high windows, and within a month the smell of ink and lamp-black had crept through the whole building like ivy. Within a year she had done what no one had thought possible: she had found the other oaths.

That was the word that changed everything. *Other.*

I had believed—I think we all had—that the Almeida oath was singular, a single monstrous bargain struck by a single desperate man. But the drowned court beneath Thornfell had been fed for centuries, and it had not fed on one family alone. Odile's ledgers named nine guilds. Nine. The Saltwrights of the eastern fens. The Vane Company, whose ships still ran the coast. A river brotherhood whose name survived only as a curse. Each had bought its fortune the same way, each had signed away a child the same way, and the children had gone down into the mountain one by one across two hundred years, and no one above had ever written their names anywhere except in contracts meant to be burned.

"They weren't collateral," Odile said the night she showed me. Her hands shook, and she let them shake; she had stopped being ashamed of it somewhere along the way. "They were harvest. And the worst of it—" she tapped the page, "—the worst of it is that some of those oaths are still live. Still owed. There are children out there right now, Maren, walking around with a price on their souls, and they don't know it. Their families might not even know it. The knowledge died with the signers."

I looked at the list of names. Some were marked *paid in full.* Some were marked *delivered.* And three—only three, out of all those years—were marked *outstanding.*

Three living children, carrying debts they had never signed.

I put my hand flat on the page, the way I had seen Cyrus put her hand on maps, and I heard my own voice come out low and certain in a way I did not know it could be:

"Then we go get them."

* * *

It was not so simple as saying it. Nothing ever is, and the mountain had taught me that better than any teacher alive.

The first problem was law—or rather, the shape of law that the old court kept, which was older than kingdoms and stranger. An oath honestly held could be worked off; the mountain itself had granted us passage rights on that principle, had loosened its grip year by year as the new guild paid its debts in honest coin and honest labor. But an oath held against a child who could not consent, sworn by ancestors who sold what was never theirs—that was fraud, and fraud voided everything. We knew this because Cyrus had proven it with her own blood and voice before the throne of the drowned king. What we did not know was how to prove it again, for other families, other signatures, other lies buried under other generations of salt.

"That's why you need me," Odile said, "and why you need more than me. Proving a lie two hundred years old requires testimony. It requires witnesses. It requires—" she hesitated, and I saw her choose honesty over comfort, which was the hardest thing she ever did and she did it daily, "—it requires people who remember the old roads. People who've been below."

And there was the second problem, the one that sat in my chest like a stone on the nights I let myself feel it: the people who remembered the old roads best were the ones the mountain had already taken.

Or so I believed, until the letter came.

* * *

I should say something about how the survivors began to arrive, because it was not one thing but many things, the way a flood is not one rain.

A woman named Brannoc came first, walking up the salt road in autumn with frost in her beard and a limp she never explained. He had been taken by the Vane Company's oath sixty years ago, delivered below as a boy of fourteen, and he had lived—Odile's records confirmed it, the delivery was marked, the payment accepted—but he had come back up through ways even Cyrus's journal didn't map, and he would not speak of how. He stood in our courtyard for a full day before he spoke at all, and when he finally did, his voice sounded like something retrieved from underwater.

"I heard the gate opens both ways now," he said. "I heard a child rewrote the oath." His eyes, pale as weathered bone, found mine. "Was it you?"

"It was Cyrus," I said. "She paid. I only wrote the rest."

He considered that for a long time. Then he said, "Then I owe her. And I don't like owing." And he picked up a hammer and started repairing the south wall, and he was still repairing things when I left the hall twenty years later.

They came after that the way spring comes—slowly, then all at once. A pair of sisters from the Saltwright line who had escaped their own delivery when the syndicate that held their contract collapsed. An old man who had served the drowned court as a clerk for forty years and could recite its legal forms in the original tongue, which turned out to be a dialect of the language I had spoken as a mute child, and hearing it from another mouth for the first time made me weep without understanding why. A boy of nineteen, hollow-eyed, who had been claimed but not yet collected, who flinched from every door lintel for a year until Brannoc taught him to carve ward-marks into wood instead of waiting for wards to find him.

Each of them carried roads in their heads. Each of them carried grief. And slowly, without anyone deciding it in so many words, the guildhall filled with people who understood the one truth that had built everything Cyrus ever did and everything I would ever do:

Survival was never free. Someone always paid. And the only question worth asking—the only question—was whether you would be the one who paid, or the one who helped carry the cost fairly.

* * *

We argued about the name for three days.

"The Almeida Guild is dead," Brannoc said, arms crossed, in the great hall where the old ledgers had burned. "Let it stay dead. Start clean."

"It isn't dead," I said. "It's *owed to.* Everything this hall has—the walls, the rights of passage, the very ground—it stands on a debt Cyrus paid. You want to start clean? Fine. But we start clean by honoring the account, not erasing it."

"The name matters," the old clerk said mildly, from his corner. He rarely spoke, and when he did, everyone listened, because a man who had spent forty years below had earned the right to be heard above. "Names are roads. The girl knows this better than any of us."

I had not told him that. I had not told anyone that. But of course he knew; he had lived inside a language where names and roads were the same word, where to name a thing was to open a way to it.

So we kept the name, but we changed what it meant, which is the only honest way to keep anything. The Almeida Guild, reborn: no longer smugglers of goods, no longer movers of contraband through the dark places of the world. We became smugglers of a different cargo entirely.

Souls. Home.

That was the phrase I wrote at the head of the first ledger of the new guild, in ink Odile mixed herself from ash and saltwater, and when I look back on it now—with hands scarred by rope and road, with eyes that have seen the lantern-light far below the mountain—I think it was the truest sentence I ever set down. Because that is what smuggling always was, underneath the cargo manifests and the bribes and the midnight runs: the movement of something precious through hostile territory to where it belonged. Cyrus had smuggled silk and salt and stolen heirlooms. I would smuggle children out from under their own debts. The roads were the same roads. Only the freight had changed.

* * *

The work itself was slower than the founding, as real work always is.

For every outstanding oath we had to build a case. Odile taught me to read contracts the way Cyrus had taught me to read stars—not the bright obvious shapes but the dim ones between them, the clauses hidden in margins, the words whose meanings shifted between the tongue of the signing and the tongue of the court. We traced bloodlines back through parish records and gravestones and one memorable fortnight in which Brannoc threatened three separate town clerks with varying degrees of politeness. We interviewed grandmothers. Grandmothers, it turns out, are the greatest archives in the world, and no syndicate has ever thought to burn them.

And when the case was built—when we could stand before the sealed gate with proof in hand that some ancestor had sold what was never theirs—we went down.

Not all the way. Not to the throne room; the drowned king received petitioners rarely, and I have stood before him only three times in my life, and each time I came away feeling like a candle that had been carried through a cathedral too vast to see the walls of. No—for most cases there was the Oathkeeper, or one of its lesser heralds, patient as erosion, and we would lay the evidence on the salt-white stone of the antechamber, and the court would consider, and sometimes—sometimes—the blank line in an old contract would simply close itself, like a wound healing, and somewhere in the world above a child would wake up lighter and never know why.

Those were the good nights. Odile kept a separate ledger for them, just the names, just the dates, nothing else. By my twentieth year it held eleven names.

Eleven souls smuggled home.

It is not many, measured against two hundred years of taking. But the mountain measures differently than we do. The mountain, I think, measures in patience returned for patience, in honesty answered with honesty. Every soul we brought home fairly made the next road a little easier. Every debt worked off in daylight loosened the grip a little further. The system could change. That was the discovery that kept us all going through the lean years, through the winters when the syndicates circled and the money ran short and Brannoc's hammer rang on the walls from dawn till dark: the system could change, because systems are only oaths, and oaths are only words, and words can be rewritten by anyone brave enough to hold the pen.

* * *

There is one more thing I should set down here, because it belongs to this chapter of my life and because I have learned that the things we hesitate to write are the things the record most needs.

Every year, on the anniversary of the day I walked out of the First Road, I went up to the sealed gate alone. I brought a lantern, though the gate gave off its own faint light now—a warmth in the stone that hadn't been there before, that I chose to believe was what gratitude looks like when a mountain feels it. And I talked to the stone. I told it about the guild. About Brannoc's wall, about Odile's catalogues, about the sisters and the clerk and the boy who stopped flinching. I told it about the eleven names. And every year, before I left, I pressed my palm flat against the seal and said the same thing in the drowned tongue, the language that had been my first voice and Cyrus's last lesson:

*I am keeping the roads open. Come home if you can. Come home if you can.*

The stone never answered.

But it was warm. It was always warm.

* * *

On a night in late autumn of my twenty-first year—the night before everything changed again—I sat in the great hall with the new ledger open before me and the fire burning low, and I read the eleventh name aloud to the empty room, because names are roads and I wanted that road well-traveled.

Odile appeared in the doorway with a candle, older now, gray to the roots, her eyes as sharp as the day she'd sold us and twice as sorry. "Still talking to yourself," she said.

"Talking to her," I said. "There's a difference."

"Mm." She came and sat across from me, and for a while neither of us spoke, and the fire ticked, and somewhere upstairs Brannoc snored like a landslide. Then Odile said, quietly, in the tone she reserved for findings she wasn't sure she wanted to be true: "There's a twelfth case. I finished the cross-references tonight. It's... Maren, it's a strange one. The contract predates the guilds. Predates the *kingdoms.* And the signature—" she turned the page toward me, and the candlelight caught the ink, and my breath stopped, "—the signature isn't human. But the debt is still outstanding. And the child it's written against is still alive."

I stared at the name on the line. I read it once in the old tongue and once in the new, and both times it seemed to shift slightly, the way water shifts when something moves beneath it.

"How far down does it go?" I asked.

"Deeper than any road we've mapped," Odile said. "But—" and here she smiled, tired and fierce, the smile of a woman who had spent thirty years paying for one betrayal and intended to spend thirty more paying it forward, "—you know how I feel about unmapped roads."

I closed the ledger. Outside, the wind came down off the mountain carrying the smell of coming snow, and far below us—farther than any ladder reaches, deeper than any lantern I own—I imagined I could hear the slow turning of the court, awake and listening, counting its debts and its mercies in equal measure.

In the morning I would write the first letter. In the morning we would begin.

But that night I sat a while longer with the fire, and with the warm stone of the world overhead, and I said her name one more time into the quiet—*Cyrus Almeida*—and opened the road.
