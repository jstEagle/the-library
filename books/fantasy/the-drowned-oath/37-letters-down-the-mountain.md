## 37. Letters Down the Mountain

*To Cyrus Almeida, who is below.*

I don't know how to start a letter to someone inside a mountain. Odile says there's no protocol for it. She says that in four hundred years of guild record-keeping, no one has ever written down before. We are inventing this as we go, which she says is either blasphemy or tradition, depending on which guild you ask.

The gate has been sealed for one hundred and nineteen days. I counted at first by scratching marks into the lintel, and then Odile caught me doing it and said, gently, in her archivist voice, that the stone remembers better than scratches, and that if I wanted to count something I should count what I was building instead. So now I count rafters. We have thirty-one.

The first snow came last week. It lies on the road like a sheet pulled up over a sleeping face. I stood at the gate for a long time with my bare hands against the rock, and I swear — I *swear*, though you would tell me not to swear things I can't prove — the stone was warm. Not warm like a hearth. Warm like a hand that had been holding another hand and only just let go.

Vess used to say that hope is a debt you pay in disappointment. She said it the way people say things they learned the hard way. But Vess also turned her back on everything she'd built to open a cage door in the dark, so I've decided her arithmetic was wrong. Hope isn't a debt. It's a road. You don't know where it goes until you walk it far enough to see around the bend.

So: a letter. The first of however many. I'll leave them at the gate the way travelers leave coins at shrines, and if no one ever takes them, then they will be prayers, and prayers are also a kind of cargo.

The roads are open. I'm keeping them that way.

— Wren

* * *

*Second letter. Winter proper now, the kind that makes the eaves groan.*

You would laugh at the guildhall. Or maybe you wouldn't laugh — maybe you'd stand in the doorway the way you did the day you came back from Thornfell and found everything cold, and your face would do that thing where it refuses to feel anything until it has decided whether the feeling is safe. I know your faces now. I catalogued them the way Odile catalogs documents. That's what living with someone does; you inherit their expressions along with their maps.

We have eleven members. Eleven! Odile, who pretends she joined out of guilt but who reorganized the entire archive in the first month because she couldn't bear the disorder. Tam, who was held below for nine years under an oath his grandmother signed, and who cried for two days when we told him he was free and then asked, very quietly, if there were others like him. There are others. We've found six so far. Six children born into other guilds' debts, scattered across three kingdoms, each of them carrying a language underneath their language the way riverbeds carry water under ice.

Tam is teaching me the northern dialects of the drowned tongue. Did you know it has tenses for things that happened underground? A whole grammar for beneath. There's a word that means *the sound a promise makes after everyone who made it is dead*. I'm going to use it in every letter until you answer me, just to be irritating.

The syndicate sent a man in autumn. He stood at our door with a document as long as my arm and read out all the debts the old guild still owed, and when he finished, Odile handed him a single page: the verdict of salt, sealed with the court's own mark, voiding every oath the mountain had repudiated. He read it twice. Then he asked, in a voice like a man stepping off solid ground, "And if the court changes its mind?"

"The court," I told him, "remembers kindness." I don't know why those words came out of me. They felt old in my mouth, older than me. But the man went pale and left without another word, and Odile wrote in the margin of the incident log: *Note — the child speaks with authority she does not appear to know she has. Investigate later. Gently.*

I am not a child anymore, Odile. I'm fourteen. I've decided.

Come up and argue with me about it.

— Wren

* * *

*Third letter. I'm writing this one by lantern light at the gate itself, because some things shouldn't be composed in comfort.*

Today I led my first run. Not a smuggling run — Odile flinches at the word, so we're calling them *passages* — but a real one, down the Second Road as far as the flooded shrine, with Tam and a girl named Sefa whose oath was bought by a salt guild two generations back. Sefa had never been underground since they took her as an infant, and she shook the whole way, and I put her hand on the tunnel wall and told her: *the mountain knows you're coming home, not being taken.* I don't know how I knew to say that. The words arrived in my mouth already warm, like bread handed over from someone else's fire.

At the shrine, the water rose around us the way it rose around us once before, without touching, and Sefa stopped shaking. She looked at the black water and said a word in the drowned tongue I didn't teach her, and Tam went very still, and then we all went still, because the word meant *thank you*, and none of us had said it yet.

The mountain talks to us, Cyrus. Or through us. I can't tell the difference and I've stopped trying, because Odile says the difference between a thing and its messenger is a distinction lawyers invented to avoid paying messengers.

I left this letter on the shrine's dry ledge instead of the gate. Call it an experiment. Call it superstition. Call it what Vess would have called it, which is a bet placed on a horse that died years ago.

But here is what I believe, and I believe it the way bedrock believes in pressure: you are still moving down there. I think the court doesn't keep you the way a jailer keeps a prisoner. I think it keeps you the way soil keeps a seed. And seeds are not lost. Seeds are *waiting*, which is a completely different entry in the ledger.

Write back. Scratch it on slate. Speak it into the stone — I've started sleeping with my ear against the cellar floor and I'm not ashamed.

Any road. Any sign. Anything.

— Wren

* * *

*Fourth letter, fifth letter, sixth letter — excerpts, because Odile says even grief must be archived efficiently.*

*Fourth:* The thaw came early. The river smuggler's ferry is running again and I paid him a memory to carry these letters downstream to towns where other freed children live, so they'll know someone is looking for them. He asked which memory. I gave him the smell of the guildhall kitchen in winter. It seemed fair. You can always build a new kitchen, but a person who thinks no one is looking for them will stay lost forever, and we are in the business of finding.

*Fifth:* Found another one today. A boy, seven years old, born owing a debt to a court under a hill in the east that no map names. His mother sold him before he could speak and then wept when we took him in, because she thought we were the collectors. It took four hours to convince her we were the opposite of collectors. What is the opposite of collectors? Odile says *librarians*. I laughed until I cried, and then I cried longer than I laughed, which is becoming a pattern with me.

*Sixth:* I dreamed of the drowned chapel again. In the dream the contract was blank — both lines, yours and mine, clean as fresh snow — and the Oathkeeper stood beside the altar holding a pen it never offered anyone. When I woke, my hands smelled of salt and blood, and under my pillow was a sliver of pale stone I have never seen before, smooth as a tooth. I didn't put it there. I'm keeping it anyway. Evidence, Odile says. Of what, she doesn't say.

* * *

*Seventh letter. Written fast, in bad light, with a hand that would not stop shaking — though not from fear. Odile made me note the date twice because she refused to believe it.*

I need to write this down exactly as it happened or I will spend the rest of my life deciding I imagined it, and I refuse. I refuse the way you refused to let the guild die. Stubbornly. With salt in the wounds.

It was the eve of the second spring since the gate closed. Two years. I had carried the seventh letter down the First Road alone — Tam wanted to come and I told him some cargoes have to travel unaccompanied, which is a lie I stole directly from you — and I set it in the crack of the sealed gate the way I always do, and I pressed my palm flat against the stone, and I said the old word for *open* without meaning to. It just came out. It comes out sometimes, the way tears come out.

And the stone —

Cyrus, the stone *breathed*. Not moved. Breathed. A warmth came up through my palm like a pulse, slow and enormous, the heartbeat of something that measures its days in centuries, and dust sifted down from the lintel, and somewhere far below — far, far below, deeper than the chapel, deeper than the bone galleries, deeper than any road you ever mapped — I heard a sound I will be hearing until I die.

A lantern. I heard a lantern. Which is impossible, because lanterns don't make sounds, except this one did: the small iron knock of a swinging handle against a wire frame, the exact sound your lantern made on every road we ever walked together, the sound I fell asleep to for a year of my life, the sound I would know blindfolded, deafened, drowned.

Then, drawn on the gate itself, in the dust that had fallen — drawn where no hand could have reached, on a surface sealed for two years, at the height of a grown woman's shoulder and not a child's — three words in your handwriting. Your actual handwriting, with the way you always crossed your sevens and hooked your r's like they were trying to escape:

**Keep the roads open.**

I sat down in the mud in front of the gate and I laughed and sobbed until Tam came running with a torch thinking the mountain had eaten me, and when he saw the writing he sat down next to me in the mud too, and neither of us spoke for a long time, because there is nothing to say in front of a miracle except *thank you*, and we had both already used that word on the mountain once, and it seemed greedy to spend it twice.

Odile examined the gate for six hours. She measured the dust, tested the seal, checked for scaffolding, for trickery, for syndicate hands, for anything. She found nothing. In the end she stood back, pushed her spectacles up into her gray hair, and said the sentence I think she had been avoiding for two years:

"There is no law of archives that accounts for this. Therefore it is not an archive matter. It is a faith matter." And then, quieter, almost to herself: "She answered. After two years, she answered."

Here is what I think, and I want you to correct me if I'm wrong, which means I want you to write back, which means I will keep writing regardless:

I think the court is not a prison. I think it never was. I think it is a ledger, and ledgers can be balanced, and debts honestly held can be worked off — you taught me that survival is never free, but you also taught me that a price paid willingly is not the same as a price stolen. You paid willingly. And I think the mountain knows the difference. I think it has known the difference for a very long time, longer than the guilds, longer than the oaths, longer than the fraud at the root of everything, and it has been waiting all this while for someone to pay honestly enough that the whole rotten economy of stolen souls could begin to unwind.

Maybe that's you. Maybe that's all of us together, every freed child, every opened road, every letter left in a crack in the stone.

Or maybe — and I barely dare write this, my pen is pressing hard enough to tear the page — maybe the mountain is not keeping you. Maybe it is *teaching* you. The way it taught me its language. The way it taught Sefa gratitude. The way it taught Vess, in the end, what her bond was really worth.

Keep the roads open. You wrote it like an order. I'm taking it like a promise.

The eighth letter is already drafted. The ninth is forming. I have ink enough for a hundred more and stubbornness enough for a thousand, and you know better than anyone alive — or below — that I get that from you.

The lantern knocked again tonight. Just once. Just faintly, through two fathoms of sealed stone, like a knuckle on a door.

I'm going to answer it.

— Wren

* * *

*P.S., added in a different ink, hours later, by lantern light at the gate where she has clearly gone back despite Odile's explicit instructions about sleep:*

Something has changed. I can't say how I know, only that the stone under my hand feels the way a held breath feels in the moment before it's released — and tonight, deep beneath the gate, far down the First Road where no light has reached in two hundred years, I heard footsteps. Not yours. Lighter. Many. Coming *up*.

And threaded through them, unmistakable, growing closer with every heartbeat: the sound of a lantern, swinging.
