## 1. The Thirteenth Turn

The mountain told me the storm was coming before the sky did.

I felt it in the seep of water through the limestone at the twelfth turn of the pass, a thin cold insistence where there had been only dry stone an hour before. The rock was drinking hard, the way it does when snowmelt runs high in the hidden country above, and somewhere behind that drinking was a weight of weather the sun hadn't yet thought to make. I put my palm flat against the wall and let the water speak. *Fast,* it said. *Cold. Coming down the Gullet by nightfall.*

"Storm," I called back along the line of mules. "We make the Salt Stair before dark or we sleep wet and sorry."

Maren Corvane, who owned more contraband than conscience, spat into the gravel and pulled her furs tighter. "You always know, Kavanagh. Twelve years I've hired the guild and you've never once been wrong about water." She squinted at me the way she did every season, that merchant's arithmetic running behind her eyes—*how does he know, what's it worth, can I buy it*—and every season I gave her nothing but a shrug.

"Long ears," I said, tapping one. "You hear a storm coming. I hear it leaving."

It wasn't a lie, exactly. It just wasn't the truth, and the difference between those two things is the whole architecture of my life.

Here is what Maren would have done with the truth: she'd have weighed it, priced it, and sold it to the first crown man who offered a purse. Because the truth is this—I don't hear storms. I hear water. All water. The slow conversation of aquifers under the pass, the pulse of the salt lake three hundred feet below us, the wet dark argument of blood moving through a body standing too close. My mother had it, and hers before her, back into whatever drowned country we came from. The guild calls it the tide-gift, when it calls it anything at all, which is never, because officially the Saltwrights are guides and road-wardens and honest keepers of the Thornfell passes, and nothing else. Unofficially, there are eleven of us in the high house at Greywater Bend, and every one of us can read water like priests read scripture, and every one of us has spent a lifetime pretending otherwise.

I was the youngest. That mattered. In the guild, being youngest meant being invisible, and I had made invisibility into a craft as fine as any smuggler's false bottom. Odile said once, drying her hands by the kitchen fire, "A gift you hide well enough becomes a rumor. A rumor no one can prove becomes nothing at all." She'd said it kindly. She'd said it like a blessing. I believed her for twenty years.

* * *

We came down off the Salt Stair as the light went the color of old pewter, and the first drops hit the canvas of Maren's wagons like flung gravel. By then I could feel the whole mountain drinking around us—the Gullet roaring somewhere below with snowmelt it had no business carrying yet, the deep cisterns under the waystation filling, the small blind lakes in their caverns shivering. And beneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat through a floor, the salt lake itself, turning over in its sleep.

I have never liked how it sleeps. It sleeps like something listening.

The waystation at the thirteenth turn was older than the guild, older than the kingdom if you believed Odile, a squat black fortress grown out of the living rock with a stable cut into the mountain's ribs. We got the caravan under cover as the storm broke properly, and the smugglers were still swearing and wringing out their cloaks when the dogs started up.

Not at the storm. At the road.

I knew before I looked. I'd felt them for a mile—six bodies coming up the pass, and in five of them the ordinary salt-and-iron music of blood doing its work. But the sixth was wrong. The sixth moved through water the way a stone moves through a song, taking no part in it, and every drop of moisture on that stretch of road leaned away from him as he passed. Even the rain seemed to fall around him rather than on him.

"Royal hunters," Maren said at my shoulder, very quietly. Her hand had found the knife under her furs without her face changing at all. "Three weeks early. They're not due till the spring census."

Six riders in the king's grey, hoods streaming, and the one in front wore no livery at all—just oiled leather gone dark with rain, and a captain's knot at the throat. He dismounted in the yard like a man stepping into his own house.

"I am Captain Vess," he said, to no one and everyone. "By order of Aldric, third of that name, King of the Thornward Reach and its waters." He unrolled a proclamation weighted with lead seals, and held it up against the rain, and read it in a voice pitched to carry into the stable, into the kitchens, into every ear in the mountain:

"All persons gifted in the ways of water, tide, or depth shall surrender themselves to the crown's hunters for assessment and culling. Shelter given to the gifted is treason. Silence kept of the gifted is treason. This by the king's mercy, that the realm be scoured clean."

*Culling.* The word dropped into the yard and lay there. I heard Maren stop breathing beside me. I heard the mules shift, and the rain, and beneath everything the water in the walls going very, very still, the way a household goes still when the wrong knock comes at the door.

And I heard something else. Under the captain's skin, where blood should have whispered its ordinary whisper, there was that same terrible silence—a dryness, a refusal, as though the water in him had been taught not to speak. Or replaced.

He rolled up the proclamation. His eyes crossed the yard, unhurried, counting. They passed over me the way a lamp passes over a wall, and stopped on the doorway of the great hall, where Odile Saltwright stood with her hands folded and her grey braid over her shoulder, wearing the face she wore for strangers: open, weathered, harmless.

"Guildmaster," said Captain Vess. "We'll want shelter. And we'll want your rolls. Every soul sworn to this house."

"You'll have both," Odile said, warm as fresh bread. "No one turns away the king's men in the mountains. Come in out of the wet."

* * *

They ate at the long table while the storm worked itself hoarse against the shutters. I kept to the shadows by the buttery with a pitcher I had no intention of pouring, doing what I do best, which is not being looked at. Bren caught my eye across the room and tilted his head toward the stairs—*later, we'll talk later*—and I nodded, and hated how much comfort I took from it. Bren had been my brother-in-arms since we were nine years old hauling rope on the Stair. Whatever came, there would be Bren.

Odile fed the hunters venison and dark beer and asked after the roads, and Captain Vess answered in short courteous pieces, and the water in him never once spoke. Around the fire the other five hunters laughed at the right moments. Their blood sang its ordinary songs. Only the captain was silent, and only I could hear the silence, and I stood in the buttery shadow holding my useless pitcher and understood, for the first time in my life, what it meant to be the only person awake in a sleeping house.

Past midnight, unable to bear my own bed, I went down for water I didn't need, and that was when I saw them.

The map room door stood open a hand's width. Candlelight lay in a thin gold line across the flagstones. Odile's voice came through the gap, low and level, stripped of all its bread-warmth.

"—the rolls will show twelve names. You'll take none of them."

"The proclamation—" Vess began.

"Is paper. I've watched proclamations come and go for forty years, Captain, and the guild has outlived every king who wrote one. You will take no one from this house." A pause. Rain. Then, softer, in a voice I had never heard her use, a voice almost afraid: "But the thing you serve—you may tell it the guild keeps its side. As always. Tell it the thirteenth turn is coming, and we remember what we swore."

Silence. When Vess answered, it was in a register no human throat should manage, a sound like water finding a crack in a dam, and every candle in the map room guttered though no window stood open.

"It remembers *you*, Odile. It asks if the youngest has come into his gift yet."

My heart stopped. In the dark of the corridor, with my hand pressed flat against the cold stone—and the stone full of water, and the water full of listening—I waited for her answer the way the condemned wait for the drum.

"No," Odile said. "There's no one like that here."

I drew my hand back from the wall as if it had burned me. Above me, the mountain groaned under the storm, and far below, three hundred feet down, the salt lake turned over in its sleep—and listened—to me.
