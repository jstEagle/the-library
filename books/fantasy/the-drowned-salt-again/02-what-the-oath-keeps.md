## 2. What the Oath Keeps

The tunnels below the Thirteenth Turn smelled of old rain and older stone, and Quentin followed the water the way other men followed lanterns.

He had not meant to come this deep. When Odile's voice cracked through the guildhall—*silence, and no one speaks to the crown's men*—he had done what he always did: made himself small, made himself furniture. But the hunters' captain had looked at him across the hearth-smoke with the flat patience of a man counting coins, and Quentin had understood that invisibility was a currency, and someone had been spending his.

So he had gone down. Down past the cisterns where the smugglers' brandy slept in the cold, down past the salt-scoured stair that even the oldest guild brothers called *the throat*, following a thread of moisture he could feel through his boot soles like a pulse in a wrist.

*There*, the water said. *Down. You were always going to come down.*

The tide-gift had never spoken in words, exactly. It was more like remembering something he'd never learned—the way water behaved in every place it hid. In granite it sulked. In limestone it wandered. And here, in the deep bones of Thornfell, it did something he had never felt it do before.

It *waited*.

Quentin stood at the mouth of a corridor that no pick had cut. The walls were smooth as riverbed, veined white with mineral bloom, and the air tasted of brine though the sea was forty leagues west. His skin prickled. Every drop of moisture in the dark was oriented—not toward him, but past him, deeper, the way iron filings lean toward a lodestone.

"Guild business ends here," said a voice behind him.

Bren. Of course Bren. Bren, who had taught him to throw a knife by candlelight, who had split the last of his bread with him on the worst night of both their lives, who stood now with a torch held high and grief already hardening on his face like wax.

"You were seen," Bren said. "Leaving the hall. Odile asked me to find you before they did."

"Before *who* did?"

"The captain's men are in the upper passes. Quentin—" Bren's torch wavered. "Give me your hand. We go back up, we say nothing, we wait out the proclamation like we've waited out everything else for two hundred years. That's what the guild *is*. Waiting."

"The captain looked at me, Bren. She told him my name."

Bren flinched, and that flinch told Quentin more than any confession could have.

"She wouldn't," Bren said. "She raised you."

"She raised me to be invisible." Quentin turned back toward the corridor, toward the waiting water. "I want to know what she's been feeding."

"Don't." Bren's voice broke open. "Quentin, some doors the guild keeps shut so the rest of us can sleep. Please."

But the water was pulling at him now, gentle and absolute, and Quentin Kavanagh—who had spent twenty-six years being no one's first choice, no one's worry, no one's son—walked into the dark because for the first time in his life, something down there knew exactly who he was.

* * *

The vault opened like a wound.

One moment he was walking a natural seam in the rock; the next, the seam widened into a chamber ringed with standing stones dragged down from the peaks above, each one carved with the Saltwright sigil worn nearly to nothing. In the center, on a plinth of black salt, lay a book bound in grey leather gone stiff as bark, and beside it, stacked in careful rows, decades of journals in Odile's narrow hand.

He opened the founding book first. The ink had faded to the color of dried blood.

*We were masons before we were oathmen,* the first page read, in a script like frost. *When the drowned thing came up the old riverbed and wore the king's grandfather like a coat, twelve of us swore what no priest would swear: to bind it beneath the salt lake and keep the binding fed. Not with blood. With truth. It eats small truths the way a lamp eats oil. One secret, freely given, each moon's turn, or the binding thins.*

*And when the truths run out?* someone had asked, in a marginal hand.

*They will not run out,* the founder had written. *Men are endless in their small dishonesties.*

Quentin sat down on the cold floor without deciding to. The chamber hummed around him—or rather, the water in the chamber hummed, and he felt it against his teeth, patient as sediment.

Every month of his life, a truth had gone down into this dark. He thought of Odile's private vigils, her monthly walks she'd claimed were inspections of the lower cisterns. He thought of how carefully she'd never asked about his gift—never once, in all his years, though she must have known, must have *always* known—and understood at last that she hadn't been protecting him from discovery.

She'd been saving him. A truth of that size—a gifted child hidden in the guild's own house—was worth months of oil. Worth years. She had been keeping him like a coin kept back for a famine.

He pulled one of her journals toward him. The earliest was dated thirty-one years past.

*It spoke to me tonight. It does not often bother with words; it prefers to let the binding speak for it, the slow leak of it into the lake's edge. But tonight it used the king's voice, and it said: the harvest is thinning. The gifted grow fewer. Give me something young.*

*I gave it the name of a smuggler who cheated us in the spring. It is not enough. I fear what I will have to give it next.*

Page after page. Year after year. Names, mostly. Small betrayals, catalogued in a hand that grew shakier with time. And then, near the end of the last journal, an entry fresh enough that the ink still shone faintly:

*The thing has left the lake. I don't know how—it should not be able—but the king's proclamation came down the pass today in a hand I know too well, and the captain of the hunters carries a token I last saw sunk in the shallows with the rest of the regalia. It is hunting the gifted itself now, wearing the crown as its mask.*

*I bargained. Gods forgive me, I bargained. The guild's lives for the guild's silence, and one name besides. It asked for the name of the strongest gift in Thornfell, and I am an old woman, and I gave it.*

*I gave it Quentin.*

The chamber seemed to tilt. Quentin gripped the plinth and the black salt bit his palms, and far above, muffled through forty fathoms of mountain, he heard the horns of the hunters sounding in the upper passes.

Not a general culling. A search. A narrowing circle, drawn around one name.

He had been invisible his whole life because invisibility was *storage*. Because a hidden thing keeps. Because Odile had looked at a terrified boy with water in his blood and seen not a child to raise but a debt to defer—and he had loved her for the very care with which she'd done it.

The water in the walls pulsed once, slow and satisfied, and Quentin realized the vault wasn't just a record.

It was a mouth.

* * *

Torchlight spilled down the corridor, and with it came voices—Bren's, high and pleading, and under it the measured tread of the captain's boots.

"—down here, I swear it on the guild's oath, he went this way—"

"Bren." Quentin didn't stand. There was nowhere to stand *to*. "You brought them."

"They said if I gave you up, the guild stands. Everyone else walks free. Quentin, they have Odile's seal, they have—" Bren's torch found him, and the light shook. "You were always going to be found out. At least this way it isn't all of us."

Quentin rose slowly. Through the stone under his feet he could feel them now, the hunters, a dozen heartbeats descending, and threaded through every one of those heartbeats, faint as a stain in snowmelt, the same signature he'd felt waiting in these walls.

It was already in them. It had been in the captain since the pass.

"I understand," Quentin said, and found that he did, and that understanding was the cruelest thing the mountain had ever given him. "You chose the many over the one. That's the oath, isn't it? That's all the oath ever was."

"It kept us alive two hundred years."

"So did the thing downstairs," Quentin said. "Ask it what it costs."

The captain rounded the corner with her crossbow level, and behind her visor her eyes were the flat grey of a lake before it freezes, and when she spoke, the voice that came out of her was layered, doubled, wet—

"There you are," it said, in a voice Quentin had only ever heard from criers in the market square, from proclamations nailed to guildhall doors. The king's voice. "Little reservoir. We have been *so patient*."

Quentin backed toward the far wall, where the oldest stone showed a seam of seeping water, cold and clean and singing of the surface—of the passes, of the salt lake, of the moon climbing toward its thirteenth turn.

He put his palm flat against the seam, and the whole mountain listened.

"Run," he told Bren quietly. "This time, be the many."

Then he let the water take the wall apart.
