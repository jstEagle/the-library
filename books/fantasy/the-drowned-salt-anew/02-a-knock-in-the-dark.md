## 2. A Knock in the Dark

The knock came at the hour when the mountain forgets it is a mountain—when snow and sky blur into one gray breathing thing, and the world shrinks to the size of a single lit window.

Bram Kavanagh heard it through three feet of stone.

That was the first wrongness. Her waystation had been built by smugglers who valued silence above comfort, walls of fitted granite that swallowed sound the way the sea swallowed ships. In ten years she had never once heard a knock from outside while sitting by her own fire. She heard avalanches as whispers, heard wolves as rumors. The mountain spoke to her only when it wished to, and it did not wish to often.

But this knock came through the stone like a fist through water, and it was followed—this was the second wrongness—by silence. No voice calling out. No second knock. Just that single hollow percussion, hanging in the air like a bell that refused to finish ringing.

She set down the knife she'd been sharpening and listened with more than her ears.

Storm-calling was not, as the Vigilant Order taught its recruits, a matter of shouting at clouds. It was listening. It was opening yourself until the pressure of the sky wrote itself on your skin, until you could feel where the wind wanted to go and persuade it, gently, to want something else. Bram had spent ten years refusing to listen. Ten years of keeping that door in her chest shut and barred, because every time she opened it even a crack, the sky remembered her name, and names traveled, and names got people burned.

But she could still hear weather the way other people heard music. She couldn't help it. It came through the walls whether she willed it or not, the way cold came through wool.

And what she heard now, beneath the ordinary murmur of the storm—the slow gray weight of it pressing down on Thornfell like a hand on a sleeper's chest—was nothing. A hole in the weather. A patch of stillness moving up the switchback trail toward her door, small as a child, silent as the grave, while all around it the wind bent and swirled as though flowing around an absence rather than a body.

Bram took the knife after all. She told herself it was for the firewood.

* * *

She had built her life the way masons build dry-stone walls: no mortar, just weight and placement, each piece chosen so that its absence would be noticed immediately. She rose before dawn and checked the snares on the eastern scree. She hauled water from the spring that never froze, two hundred paces downhill, because hauling it kept her honest. She cured meat, mended rope, carved decoy carvings for the smuggler caravans to hide their true cargo among, and once a month she walked four hours down to the Widow's Rest inn to drink one cup of bad wine and say perhaps forty words to men who knew better than to ask a forty-first.

Invisibility was not the absence of being seen. Anyone could hide; hiding was easy and it failed. Invisibility was an art, and its medium was tedium. You made yourself so reliably dull, so precisely what everyone expected, that eyes slid off you like rain off slate. Bram Kavanagh, guide, was a lean gray woman of thirty-odd years with a limp she exaggerated and a temper she performed in small, safe doses. She complained about her knees. She overcharged slightly and apologized for it. She was nobody, and she had worked very hard, for ten years, to be nobody well.

The Order's brand sat under her left collarbone, a circle with a broken line through it, the mark they gave storm-callers who would not kneel. She had not let anyone see her bare shoulders since the night they cut it there. Even alone, even in summer, she dressed to the throat. Some habits were load-bearing.

So when she unbarred her door at midnight with a knife in her fist and a lie ready on her tongue—*wrong house, traveler, nothing here but an old guide and his rheumatism*—she was, in every way that mattered, prepared.

She was not prepared for the mud.

* * *

The girl stood on her doorstep like something the river had given back.

She was small—eight years old, maybe nine, the age hard to read under the grime—and she was caked head to foot in wet earth, the kind of dark clay that lived in the river valleys of the lowlands, forty miles and a kingdom away from this altitude. Her hair was black with it, plastered flat. Her clothes had been good ones once; Bram could see the ghost of embroidery at the hem, fine work, southern work, now stiff with dried filth. And her feet—

Her feet were bare. Bare, in a snowstorm, on a mountain pass, at midnight. They were blue to the ankle and bleeding in places, and they were clean. That was the detail Bram's mind snagged on and could not release: the girl's feet were washed clean, as if the mud had refused them, as if she had walked through everything the lowlands and the slopes could throw at her and the clay had climbed her body and stopped, deliberately, at her ankles.

The girl looked up at her. Her eyes were the color of deep water over sand—not blue, not green, that shifting in-between that the eye kept trying to name and kept failing—and they were calm. Not the calm of shock, which Bram knew well, the glassy stillness of the frozen. This was the calm of someone waiting. Of someone who had knocked on this particular door, in this particular storm, because this was where she had been going all along.

"Wrong house," Bram said, in her guide's rasp, the performance already running. "Nothing here. Turn back down the—"

The girl spoke.

It was one word, maybe two, and it went through Bram like a fishhook through the soft part of the palm. The language was old. It was liquid and strange, full of sounds that seemed to come from behind the teeth and below the breath at once, vowels that opened like drowned doors, consonants that fell like water dripping in a cave no light had ever reached. Bram did not know it. No one alive knew it. She had heard fragments of it exactly once before, ten years ago, in a place she did not let herself remember, scratched into the wall of a flooded crypt by hands that had been dead for three centuries—and even then, even carved in stone, it had sounded less like writing and more like singing that had been interrupted.

The girl said it again, patient, as if the fault lay in Bram's hearing and not in three hundred years of erasure. Then she tilted her head, and the wind—which had been screaming down the pass all night—went quiet around the doorstep, folding itself close like a dog that has been told to wait.

Bram's hands were shaking. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

Every instinct she owned, every instinct the last decade had honed to a whetstone edge, said the same thing: *close the door*. Strange child, strange tongue, strange stillness in the storm. Trouble wore many coats and this was one of them, and the smart move, the survival move, the move that kept a traitor's heart beating for another quiet year, was to shut the door and bar it and tell herself in the morning that the girl had been a hallucination born of bad wine and lonelier nights than she'd admitted to.

The girl swayed.

It was small—a shift of weight, a blink that lasted too long—but Bram had spent ten years reading bodies on mountains, reading the exact moment exhaustion tipped into collapse, and she saw it happen. Saw the calm flicker. Saw the deep-water eyes lose focus, saw the small mud-caked frame list sideways like a boat taking water.

And Bram Kavanagh, exiled storm-caller, traitor to the Vigilant Order, artist of invisibility, discovered that her body had made a decision without consulting her.

She caught the girl before she fell. The child weighed almost nothing. She smelled of river mud and underneath it, faintly, impossibly, of salt—clean ocean salt, brine and kelp, though the nearest sea was forty miles off and the nearest ocean a lifetime. Her skin was cold as a gravestone and her heartbeat, when Bram pressed two fingers to the small throat, was slow and strong and steady as a tide.

"No," Bram said aloud, to herself, to the mountain, to whatever had arranged this. "No, absolutely not."

The girl's eyes half-opened. She said something else in the drowned tongue—soft, slurred with sleep, and somehow, though Bram understood not one syllable of it, she understood this completely: *it's all right now.*

* * *

She did not remember deciding to carry the girl inside. She remembered the firelight, and the weight in her arms, and the door swinging shut behind her on the storm's roar, and then she was kneeling on her own floor with a dying-warm child wrapped in her oldest blanket, and the knife was lying across the room where she had dropped it, and the door was barred, and there was no version of events in which she could pretend any of this hadn't happened.

She worked. Work was the shape her panic took; it always had been. She stripped the mud-stiff clothes—the embroidery again, southern court-work, a noble child's garment or a servant of nobles, either of which meant trouble—and rubbed the girl down with snow-melt and rough cloth, working warmth back into the blue feet, the blue fingers. The mud came away reluctantly and strangely: it flaked off in sheets, whole maps of riverbank lifting from the skin, and beneath it the girl was unmarked. Not a scratch, not a bruise, nothing. Children who crossed the Thornfell passes did not arrive unmarked. Children who crossed anything did not arrive unmarked.

Under the left side of her ribs, there was a birthmark. Bram froze with the washcloth in her hand.

It was small, the size of a coin, and it was not brown or red like birthmarks were. It was a deep ember-orange, and when the firelight shifted, it seemed to shift with it—to brighten, to gutter, the way coals breathe. Bram stared at it for a long time, and the mark, or her imagination, or something older than both of them, stared back.

She covered it with the blanket and decided, firmly, not to have seen it.

The girl slept by the fire like the dead sleep, which is to say totally and yet somehow watchfully. Bram sat across from her with her back to the wall and the knife back in her hand and watched her, and drank tea she didn't taste, and conducted the argument she'd been losing since the moment she'd opened the door.

*Turn her out at dawn,* said the voice of survival, the voice that had kept her alive for ten years. *Feed her, warm her, point her down the mountain, wash your hands. You know what children are. Children are witnesses. Children are questions with legs. You have spent a decade becoming nobody, and nobody does not harbor mysterious foundlings who speak dead languages.*

*She's eight,* said another voice, one Bram had thought starved out of her years ago. *She's eight and her feet are bare and she walked forty miles of winter to knock on your door specifically, and the storm went quiet when she asked it to, and you are going to sit here and pretend you don't know what that means?*

*I don't know what that means,* Bram told it bitterly. *That's the entire problem.*

Because she did know pieces. Storm-callers were not made, they were born—born thin-skinned to the sky, born with the door in the chest already ajar. The Order collected them young for exactly that reason. And the stories, the ones told low over smugglers' fires, the ones about the drowned kingdom that had been scraped off every map and out of every book three hundred years ago—those stories always circled back to the same impossible details. Salt where no sea should reach. Voices in the water. A fire that had gone into hiding when the kingdom fell, and had been hunted ever since by something that wore the crown of Vhalcrest like a glove wears a hand.

Bram had always filed those stories where she filed everything dangerous: under *not my problem.* She was a woman with one brand, one limp, one carefully dull life. Dragons and drowned kings belonged to ballads, and ballads belonged to people whose faces weren't nailed to Order notice-boards.

But the salt smell was still in the room. Faint, unmistakable, rising off the sleeping child like heat off stones, and the nearest coast was forty miles away, and Bram Kavanagh sat in her fortress of tedium and felt the dry-stone wall of her life shift, one stone grinding against another, somewhere near the base.

* * *

Near dawn, the girl woke all at once, the way animals wake—no groggy surfacing, just open eyes and total attention. She looked at the fire. She looked at the blanket. She looked at Bram, and Bram watched her take inventory: the room, the exits, the knife, the woman. A practiced inventory. An adult's inventory in a child's face, and it chilled Bram more than the bare feet had.

Then the girl said something in the drowned tongue. A question, by its rise and fall. When Bram only shook her head, the girl tried again—slower, different words, and Bram caught the shape of the same root repeated, *asking, asking*, patient as water.

"I don't understand you," Bram said. "Child, I don't—"

The girl frowned. Then she lifted one small hand, mud still in the creases of her knuckles, and pointed at her own chest.

"Wren."

Bram blinked. "What?"

The girl pointed at her chest again, firmer. "*Wren.*" Then she pointed at Bram, eyebrows raised, and waited.

A name. Her name, or the word she'd chosen, or the word she'd been given by whoever—or whatever—had sent her up this mountain. Bram heard herself answer before she'd decided to, her guide's rasp falling away, leaving something rawer.

"Bram," she said. "Bram Kavanagh."

The girl—Wren—tasted it. Her mouth moved around the sounds, testing them, and then she smiled, and it was the first unguarded thing Bram had seen her do, and it was devastating, a small sun coming out over a wreck. She said "Bram" back in her own language first, rolling it into those drowned vowels until it sounded like a wave breaking over a seawall, and then again in plain common speech, careful and clear, and something in Bram's chest that had been locked for ten years turned over in its sleep.

Wren pointed at the window, where the storm still leaned on the glass, and said a long phrase, questioning. Bram shook her head. Wren tried the window itself, pressing her palm flat to the frost-feathered pane, and murmured—there was no other word for it—murmured to the glass, and outside, for the space of three heartbeats, the wind died utterly. Total silence, that held-breath hush, in the teeth of a Thornfell gale. Snow hung in the air like a held note.

Then the wind resumed, and Wren turned back with an expression of mild frustration, as if the storm were being stubborn, as if this happened everywhere, as if it were nothing.

Bram set down her tea because her hands were no longer safe with it.

*Not my problem,* said the voice of survival, thinner now, thinner than it had ever been. *Walk her down the mountain tomorrow. First light. Give her bread, give her boots, give her to the Widow's Rest and the king's road and anyone else—*

Wren yawned. It cracked her whole small face open, and without ceremony or permission she shuffled over, dragging the blanket, and folded herself down against Bram's side with the boneless authority of a child who has decided, and leaned her mud-freckled head against Bram's shoulder, and was asleep again in under a minute. Slow tide-heart beating. Salt-smell rising. Ember-mark glowing faintly through the blanket's weave, like a coal remembering fire.

Bram sat absolutely still. Outside, the storm howled. Inside, the fire ticked and settled. And against her shoulder, in a language three hundred years dead, in her sleep, the girl began to sing.

It was soft. It was terrible. It was the most beautiful thing Bram had ever heard, a lullaby in the tongue of a drowned kingdom, and as she listened, half-hypnotized, she realized two things with the cold certainty of a mountaineer feeling the slope begin to move beneath her boots.

The first was that she was not going to walk this child down the mountain tomorrow. She had known it since the doorstep. Perhaps since the knock through the stone. The song only made it official.

The second was that the lullaby, though she could not understand a single word of it, was addressed to the sea. And the sea, forty miles away and climbing in her mind's ear like water up a stair, was answering.
