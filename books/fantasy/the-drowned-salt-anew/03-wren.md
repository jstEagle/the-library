## 3. Wren

The girl slept for two days.

Bram checked her breathing the way she checked a snare line—quickly, without sentiment, expecting failure. But the small chest rose and fell beneath the sheepskin with a stubbornness that surprised her, and by the second evening color had crept back into cheeks that had been the gray of old ice when Bram had carried her in from the doorstep. The mud was gone now, scrubbed away in a basin of water Bram had heated twice because the first went black. Underneath it the child was all sharp elbows and hollows, a bird's architecture of bone, with hair the color of wet river stones and eyes that opened—when they opened—like doors onto weather.

She did not speak common. That much Bram confirmed on the third morning, when she set down bread and honey and said, plainly, *eat*, and the girl looked at the bread, then at Bram, then at the window where snow-light came through like milk, and said something in reply that made the hairs on Bram's arms stand up under her wool.

It wasn't a word. It was a shape of sound, low and round at the start, rising into something almost like gulls crying over water. It had no edges. Common speech was hammers and nails; this was tide against stone, wearing things smooth. The girl said it again, patient, as if Bram were the one who was slow, and pointed at the bread.

"I don't know what you're saying," Bram said.

The girl's face did something complicated—frustration folded around a deeper thing, an ache—and she pressed her palm flat to her own chest.

"*Aelmarra,*" she said. Then again, tapping her sternum with two fingers, "*Aelmarra.*"

The word landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. Bram heard it fall a long way before it stopped.

Because she knew that word. Everyone knew it, the way everyone knew the shape of their own hands—the drowned kingdom, Aelmarrow, the pearl of the southern coast, taken by the sea three hundred years ago in a single night, so the story went, when the tides rose and did not stop rising until every tower and temple and market square lay under forty fathoms of green silence. Every map had erased it. Every schoolchild learned the lesson: pride, and the sea's long memory, and never build below the high-water mark. There were songs about it. Grim ones, sung in taverns to make children behave.

No one sang them in its language. No one alive knew its language.

"Where did you get that word?" Bram asked quietly.

The girl looked at her with those door-onto-weather eyes and answered in the same liquid syllables, a whole sentence of them, unhurried, as though explaining something obvious to someone standing very far away. And Bram—who had spent ten years teaching herself not to feel things at volume—felt the floor of her certainty tilt, just slightly, like a deck in a swell.

* * *

She named her Wren on the fourth day, mostly out of practicality.

"You can't keep calling you *girl*," Bram said, turning eggs in an iron pan while the child sat cross-legged on the hearthstone, watching fire the way other children watched festivals. "And I'm not calling you anything that sounds like a name out of some ballad. You're small. You're brown. You hide in cracks and startle easy." She slid the eggs onto a tin plate and set it down. "Wren."

The girl considered the plate. Considered Bram. Then she pointed at herself and tried the word, mangling it beautifully—*Wrehn*, a soft breath of a sound—and something happened in Bram's chest that she chose, firmly, to ignore.

"Wren," she agreed. "Eat your eggs."

Teaching her common was like trying to teach a river to run uphill. Not because the child was dull—Bram had known smugglers who could count cargo in six languages, and none of them had this one's quickness—but because the words themselves seemed to slide off her, as if her mouth had been built for different sounds entirely. *Water* came out as *wah-ter* and then collapsed into something else, something with a rolling middle that made Bram's teeth ache faintly, the way a struck bell makes nearby glass hum.

"No," Bram said, for perhaps the hundredth time. "*Wa-ter.* Two beats. Like footsteps."

Wren frowned with her whole body. Then she stood, went to the water bucket, dipped her fingers, and flicked droplets across the floorboards—and sang, actually *sang*, a falling two-note phrase: *a-may-a*. She pointed at the wet. Pointed at the bucket. Looked at Bram with the expression of a teacher whose student is hopeless but beloved.

"That's not—" Bram started, and stopped.

Because it was. Of course it was. *Amaya.* Water. The word moved the way water moved; she could hear it if she stopped fighting it, could feel how the sounds poured one into the next without seams, the way a wave isn't separate from the wave behind it. Common speech chopped the world into pieces. This language let the world stay whole.

"You're not learning my language," Bram said slowly. "You're teaching me yours."

Wren smiled. It was the first full smile Bram had seen from her, and it transformed her face from something wary and wild into something heartbreakingly young—a gap where a tooth should be, a smudge of soot on one cheekbone. She patted the hearthstone beside her, an unmistakable invitation.

Bram told herself she sat down because the bench was cold and the hearthstone was warm. She was a good liar, even to herself, but not a perfect one.

* * *

Over the following days, the lessons reversed themselves entirely.

Bram would hold up a cup and say *cup*, and Wren would say *cup*—she was getting better, the hard edges of common softening in her mouth but landing—and then Wren would take the cup, fill it from the bucket, and give Bram back a word like a gift wrapped in water: *selath*, or something near it, the *l* flowing directly into the *a* with no wall between. And Bram, who had not been anyone's student since she was nineteen and the Vigilant Order had finished with her, found herself repeating it. Found herself *wanting* to repeat it.

There was a system to it, she began to see. The drowned tongue didn't have separate words for many things common kept apart. *Selath* meant water, yes, but also rain, also tears, also the sea, also—when Wren said it while looking out the window at the snow, with a strange softening—something else, something Bram couldn't translate. Melting, maybe. Or grief. In Aelmarrow, apparently, these had all been the same word once, and no one had needed to explain why.

"They must have been a patient people," Bram murmured one evening, watching Wren stack kindling with geometric precision. "Or a sad one."

Wren looked up. She'd learned to catch tone even when the words escaped her, and something in Bram's voice snagged her attention. She said a short phrase, questioning, and touched her own cheek where a tear would go.

"I'm fine," Bram said automatically, and then, because the lie felt heavy in the small room, amended: "I knew a little of your kingdom. Secondhand. Ruins. Stories." She hesitated. "I've seen it. What's left of it."

That got her the full weight of those eyes. Wren set down the kindling with great care, crossed the room, and took Bram's hand—small fingers, cold even by the fire—and pressed it flat against her own sternum again. *Aelmarra.* But this time she added words after it, a slow cascade of them, her voice dropping into a register Bram hadn't heard before, older-sounding, formal, like a recitation. Like a list. Like a genealogy.

When she finished, she looked at Bram expectantly.

"I don't understand," Bram said gently.

Frustration flickered. Wren tried again, slower, and this time Bram caught fragments—not meanings exactly, but shapes. The word for *last*, she thought, or something like it, appeared twice. The word for *voice*. And at the end, repeated three times with increasing intensity, a word that sounded like a wave breaking: *vess-sara, vess-sara, VESS-SARA.*

Vessel. The word arrived in Bram's mind unbidden, in common, and she disliked it immediately.

"Who are you?" she asked. "Whose voice are you?"

Wren tilted her head. Then, deliberately, she reached up and touched Bram's throat—just briefly, just two fingertips against the pulse point, light as snowfall—and said one word.

It was Bram's name. Not Kavanagh, not storm-caller, not any of the names the Order had burned into her record. Just *Bram*, which she'd taught the girl only that morning, pronounced perfectly, in that impossible accent that made everything sound like it was being said underwater, from far away, across a great distance of years.

Then Wren withdrew her hand, went back to her kindling, and began stacking it again as though nothing had happened.

Bram sat by the fire for a long time after that, listening to the wind work at the eaves, feeling the ghost of two small fingertips on her throat.

* * *

On the ninth night, the dreams started.

Not hers—hers were the usual stock, the pass, the lightning, the faces of people she'd failed, all of it worn smooth by ten years of repetition. It was Wren who dreamed badly. Bram woke past midnight to small, choked sounds from the loft and climbed up to find the girl thrashing in the blankets, tangled, her fists clenched so tight the knuckles shone white.

"Wren." She shook her shoulder, gently. "Wren, wake up. You're dreaming."

The girl surfaced gasping, and the words that came out of her were pure flood-tide, a torrent of the drowned tongue, too fast to catch, her eyes open and seeing nothing of the loft, nothing of Bram. She was somewhere else. Somewhere deep. Somewhere with a great deal of water in it.

Bram did the only thing she could think of. She pulled the child against her chest, the way she'd once held a lamb through a night of thunder, and she spoke—not common, not sense, just sound, low and rhythmic, a lullaby cadence she hadn't used since she was a girl on the coast before everything. Her mother's cadence. Her mother's nonsense syllables, half song, half prayer, handed down from women who'd lived close enough to the old drowned shore that their cradle songs had salt in them.

Slowly, Wren stilled.

And then, into Bram's collarbone, the girl whispered a response. The same lullaby. The same nonsense syllables—except they weren't nonsense, Bram realized with a jolt that went through her like a current. They were the drowned tongue, worn down by generations of mothers who no longer knew they were speaking it, the way a coin rubbed smooth still keeps the shape of a face. Her family's cradle song was Aelmarrow's cradle song. Had always been. She'd been singing fragments of a dead language her entire life and calling it nothing, calling it home.

Wren's breathing slowed to match hers. Before sleep took her, the girl murmured one more word against Bram's collarbone, clear and distinct, and this time Bram understood it without knowing how—understood it the way you understand warmth, or the pull of the tide.

*Ammi.* Mother. Or close enough. Or the word for the person who holds you through the storm, which in a kinder language might be the same thing.

"Don't," Bram said softly, to the dark, to the child, to herself. "Don't go promising things."

But her arms stayed where they were until dawn.

* * *

In the mornings after that, something had shifted between them, subtle as a change in barometric pressure. Wren followed her through the chores—checking snares along the treeline, hauling water from the spring, mending the smokehouse door that the wind had been worrying all winter—and narrated everything in her own tongue, a running commentary that Bram gradually began to parse. *Snow* had a word that sounded like a sigh. *Rabbit* had a word with a quick hop in the middle of it. *Snare* had a word that was ugly and efficient, and Bram laughed the first time she recognized it, a rusty sound that startled a jay off the fencepost.

"What?" she said, when Wren looked at her in surprise. "Your people had honest words for traps. I respect that."

They fell into a rhythm, the two of them, exile and exile. Bram had been exiled from her country, her order, her name; Wren, it seemed, was exiled from time itself, a child carrying a kingdom three centuries dead inside her small chest, speaking for people who had no mouths left to speak with. Neither of them fit anywhere. But the waystation was small, and the winter was long, and two wrongnesses side by side can sometimes make a rightness, the way two broken halves of a seal can still hold water.

On the twelfth day, Wren drew.

She'd found Bram's charcoal stubs—the ones she used for marking trade routes on slate—and commandeered them without apology, kneeling on the floorboards with a sheet of wrapping paper salvaged from a crate of Vhalcrest brandy. Bram watched over her shoulder as lines grew under the small, fierce hand: a harbor, crescent-shaped. Walls in concentric rings. A citadel on a hill. Canals, dozens of them, laid out in a pattern too regular for accident—a pattern, Bram realized with a slow cold spreading through her stomach, that she had seen before. Seen it dark and drowned and drifting, forty feet down through green water, when she was younger and dumber and diving wrecks off the southern coast for salvage money.

She had swum through those canals. She had run her gloved fingers along those walls.

"Wren," she said, and her voice came out strange. "Where did you see this?"

The girl looked up, charcoal-blackened fingers poised, and gave her an answer in the drowned tongue—one word, calm and simple, the way you'd answer a question with an obvious answer.

*Here.*

She tapped her own chest. *Aelmarra.* Here. Inside. As if the city were a memory she carried, as if she'd walked those streets that morning, as if three hundred years of drowning were a night's sleep she'd only just woken from.

Outside, the wind turned, and for a moment—only a moment—Bram could have sworn the air coming down from the pass smelled of brine. Of low tide. Of a sea that had no business being within forty miles of her door.

Wren went back to her drawing, humming the cradle song, and the charcoal whispered on, laying down streets and towers and temples in a city that had been dead longer than any nation now living had been alive—and Bram stood in her kitchen, very still, listening to the salt-smell fade, and understood that whatever she had taken in from her doorstep that snowy night, it was not merely a lost child.

It was a door. And something on the other side of it was beginning to remember the way through.
