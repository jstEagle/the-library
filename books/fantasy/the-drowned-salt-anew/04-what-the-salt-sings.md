## 4. What the Salt Sings

The dream came to Bram the way storms came—first a pressure behind the eyes, then the smell of something wrong.

She woke in the black hour before dawn with her heart already hammering and her hand already closed around the iron poker she kept by the bed for wolves. The waystation was silent. Too silent. No small rustle from the loft where Wren slept, no restless turning of the child who had spent three weeks in her care and still made sounds in the night like a bird caught under eaves.

Bram lay still and listened.

Wind through the pines, the ordinary complaint of old timber, the river muttering over its stones a quarter mile below. And beneath all of it—a hum. Low, sustained, felt more than heard, as though someone had drawn a bow across the string of the world and was holding the note.

"Wren," she said aloud, and the word fell flat into the dark.

The loft was empty. The blanket lay thrown back, still holding warmth, and the ladder's top rung bore two small prints of mud that had not been there when Bram banked the fire. Mud. In midwinter, when the ground outside was iron-hard with frost and the snow lay knee-deep past the tree line. She stood at the foot of the ladder with the poker hanging forgotten at her side and looked at those prints for a long time, because there was nothing else her mind would let her do.

Then she moved. Boots, coat, the storm-lantern with its shuttered flame. She did not light it yet; her eyes were good in the dark, better than most, an inheritance from a line of people who had learned to read weather the way priests read scripture. She went out into the cold without barring the door behind her, which was a thing she had never once done in ten years of living alone above the Thornfell passes.

The night was clear and enormous. Stars in their cold thousands, the moon a thin paring of bone, and the mountains standing around her like patient elders. Frost smoked off her breath. She cast about for tracks and found them immediately—not because they were obvious but because they were wrong. Small bare feet had crossed the frozen yard in a straight line, unerring, unhurried, straight toward the river path, and where each footprint pressed, the frost had melted and refrozen into a skin of glassy ice that glittered faintly even under starlight. As though the ground itself remembered being warmer. As though something in the child's passing had whispered *sea* to the earth, and the earth, confused, had obliged.

Bram followed.

* * *

The river ran shallow and quick through its gorge below the waystation, a mountain stream that would eventually forget itself into one of the great lowland rivers and finally, forty miles and a lifetime later, into the sea. In winter it shrank to a third of its summer voice, running dark between shelves of ice, and Bram had lived beside it long enough to know every mood of it: the sullen grinding of the spring breakup, the flat obedience of deep summer, the brittle whisper of January.

It was whispering now. But under the whisper was the hum again, stronger here, rising out of the water itself, and Bram stopped on the bank with her lantern still shuttered and felt the hair rise along her arms beneath two layers of wool.

Wren stood on a flat stone at the river's edge, ankle-deep in water that should have taken the girl's feet off at the bone with cold. She stood perfectly upright, arms loose at her sides, head tilted back so that her face caught the starlight—and she was singing.

Bram had heard the child make sounds before. Cries, mostly, in the first days, and then fragments of that other language—the drowned tongue, she'd come to think of it, though she'd had no right to name it anything—soft syllables that rolled and broke like water over stone, full of vowels that seemed to open somewhere deeper in the chest than any language Bram knew. She had assumed it was nonsense. The private music of a mind that had lost whatever it had lost.

This was not nonsense. This was not even, quite, a child's voice.

The song came out of Wren in long, rolling phrases, and each phrase seemed to have weight, seemed to press against the air the way low thunder presses against the ears before lightning shows. It rose and fell in patterns too regular to be improvisation—an old pattern, a worn pattern, the way a stair is worn by centuries of feet. And the river answered. That was the only word Bram could find for it, standing helpless on the bank with frost climbing her sleeves: the water rose slightly where the girl stood, gathering against the current, leaning toward her like a crowd leaning toward a speaker, and the hum beneath the whisper swelled until Bram felt it in her back teeth, in the plates of her skull, in the old scar tissue over her ribs where a Vigilant brand had been burned and cut away and burned again.

She should have called out. Some part of her, the smuggler's guide, the survivor, knew that waking a sleepwalker badly could break something in them, knew that the correct procedure was gentle words and a guiding hand. But her body would not move. Her body understood, in some animal register older than procedure, that she was watching something vast wear a small shape, the way a flood wears a culvert, and that the proper response of small things was stillness.

So she stood, and she watched, and the song went on.

It must have been minutes. It might have been an hour. Time had gone strange, stretched thin as wire. The stars wheeled almost imperceptibly. Wren's breath did not steam in the cold, Bram noticed—her own breath plumed white with every exhale, but the child's breath came and went without clouding, as if the air around her were warm, or as if what breathed in her were not quite air.

And then the fish began to rise.

At first Bram thought it was the moonlight playing tricks, silver flashes along the black current. Then one turned over in the shallows not six feet from the girl's stone, and the starlight lay flat along its belly, and Bram saw that it was dead. Not dying—dead, and bloated, and pale as a peeled egg, pale as something that had been dead a long time, though the mountain trout of this river fought their way upstream fat and silver in every season and nothing in these waters bloated in the cold.

Another rose. Another. They came up out of the depths of the pools in ones and twos and then in dozens, rolling belly-up, drifting down the current past the girl's ankles in a slow silver procession, and the smell reached Bram a moment later and made her stomach lurch—not the clean mineral smell of dead fish in winter, which is bad enough, but salt. Brine. The heavy, green, tidal stink of a mudflat at low tide, of kelp rotting on a strandline, of the sea in August when the heat lies on it. Forty miles from the ocean. Nine thousand feet up. The smell rolled up the gorge so thickly that Bram could taste it on her tongue, coarse and mineral, and it left a film on her lips like dried seawater.

Wren sang on. Her voice had changed—it had dropped, or deepened, or something behind it had leaned closer to the surface, and now the syllables carried harmonics that no eight-year-old throat should hold, overtones that Bram felt in her sternum like a second heartbeat. The dead fish circled her now, caught in a slow eddy, dozens of them, turning in the current around the stone where she stood like mourners walking a bier.

And Bram, who had spent ten years teaching herself to be no one, who had buried her name so deep that some mornings she needed a moment to dig it out, felt the brand scars on her ribs begin to burn.

That was the thing that finally unlocked her limbs. Not fear for herself—she had made her peace with what the Order would do if they ever found her—but a sudden, blinding, physical terror for the child standing in the freezing water with her face turned up to stars that were not hers. Because Bram knew burning. She knew what it meant when old marks woke, and whatever answered in her blood answered to weather, to sky, to the ordinary violent machinery of storms—and even that small inheritance had cost her everything she had. Whatever this was, this tide-sound in a mountain river, this brine in the high cold air, it was not small, and it was wearing a girl who weighed less than a sack of barley.

"Wren." Her voice cracked on the first try. She cleared her throat and said it again, louder, stepping down onto the ice-skinned rocks. "Wren. Wake up."

The song faltered.

For one terrible instant the silence that rushed in behind the broken note was worse than the song—worse because the hum didn't stop, it only lost its voice, continuing on underneath the world like a struck bell that would not finish dying away. The dead fish stopped circling. All of them, at once, hung motionless in the eddy, facing upstream, facing the girl.

Then Wren's knees buckled.

Bram covered the last yards in a scrambling slide and caught her before she went under, hauling the child up out of the water, and the cold of her soaked nightshirt was shocking even through Bram's coat—river-cold, true cold, the ordinary killing cold of a Thornfell January, and somehow that was a relief. Cold she understood. Cold she could fight.

Wren's eyes were open. That was the worst of it, in the moment and after, in the retelling Bram would never permit herself: the eyes were open and looking directly at her, and they were not the child's eyes. They were not cruel, exactly. They were not anything. They were the eyes of someone looking out from very far away, from a great depth, the way a diver sees the surface as a bright trembling ceiling, and behind them, far down in the black, something was *looking back*.

Then the child blinked, shivered violently, and became eight years old again.

"Bram?" Wren's teeth chattered. Her voice was her own, small and hoarse. "Why—" She looked down at the water, at the stone, at the dead fish drifting past her legs, and Bram watched the understanding arrive in her face and do what understanding does to children, which is arrive as fear before it can become anything else. "I was dreaming about the water," Wren whispered. "There was a city in the water. Everyone was waiting. I was late."

"It's all right." Bram stripped off her coat and wrapped it around the shaking shoulders, lifted her, stood. The girl weighed nothing. She weighed nothing and she smelled of salt. "You were sleepwalking. It happens. Come inside, we'll get you warm."

But over Wren's head, as Bram turned up the bank, she looked back once at the river. The eddy had released its dead; the fish were scattering downstream, knocking softly against the ice shelves, going wherever dead things go. The hum was fading. The air tasted of pine and frost again, almost, except at the very back of the throat, where the ghost of brine lingered like a word you couldn't quite spit out.

Forty miles from the sea, Bram thought. And the sea had come to her door.

* * *

She got the fire up and the child into dry wool and sat her by the hearth with hot barley tea laced with the honey Bram kept for coughs, and Wren drank it in silence, hunched, watching the flames with an expression far too old for her face. Bram watched her back and turned the night over in her mind like a smuggler counting coin, looking for the false piece.

Three weeks. Three weeks since she'd found the girl mud-caked and half-dead on her doorstep in a thaw that shouldn't have been happening, speaking a language that made Bram's bones itch to hear. Three weeks of teaching her common speech, word by word, the way you'd teach a wild hawk to perch—and of learning, unwillingly, fragments in return. *Aelmarrow.* The child had said it the first night, pointing east, pointing down, and Bram had known the shape of the word before she knew why: it was the name of the kingdom that wasn't on any map anymore, the drowned kingdom, the one the histories called cursed and the sailors called simply *the Shallows*, because that was all that remained of it above water. Aelmarrow had gone under three hundred years ago in a single night, whole cities, whole coastlines, gone beneath the sea with such totality that even the maps had been redrawn, as if the world itself had agreed to forget.

Bram had seen the ruins once. Young, foolish, diving for salvage off a smuggler crew that hadn't asked questions—that had been before the exile, before everything. She had gone down fifty feet into green twilight and found streets. Found a doorway with a carved lintel, found a bell tower with the bell still hanging, found faces in the silt looking up at her with the patience of stone. She had surfaced vomiting seawater and hadn't spoken for a day, and the crew had laughed and said everyone came up like that the first time.

Now a child who spoke that drowned tongue stood in her kitchen smelling of tidewater, and the river forty miles inland had risen to meet her song.

"You dreamed of a city," Bram said quietly, in common speech, slow enough for the girl to follow. "In the water."

Wren nodded. Her hands were wrapped around the tea bowl, and Bram noticed for the first time that the child's fingertips were wrinkled, pruned white, as though she'd been soaking for hours rather than minutes.

"Everyone was waiting," Wren said. "They said I was late. They weren't angry. They just... waited." She frowned, searching for words in a language still new to her, and gave up, and switched—fluidly, naturally—to the other tongue, the rolling one, and said something short and plaintive.

Bram didn't know the words. But she knew the tune of them, now, God help her, because she had heard it by the river: it was a fragment of the song, a single phrase of it, the way a beggar whistles a scrap of a hymn he's heard from a church door.

"Sing me the rest," Bram heard herself say. "Of what you sang tonight. Slowly."

Wren looked up, and for a moment the far-away look flickered behind her eyes again—not possession, Bram thought, not exactly. More like memory. Like a room in the child's mind that she herself had never entered but whose door stood open.

"They're not my songs," Wren said. "I don't know where they come from. They come when I sleep." She hugged the tea bowl tighter. "Are the dead fish my fault?"

The question went into Bram like a fishhook. She thought about lying, about the smooth reassurance a child deserved, and found she couldn't do it. This child had walked barefoot through frozen mud to sing the dead up out of a river; she had earned better than comfort built on sand.

"I don't know," Bram said. "But we're going to find out. Together. That's the bargain—you and me. Whatever's in you, whatever's calling you, we look at it together, and neither of us pretends it isn't there. Agreed?"

Wren considered this with the grave thoroughness of the very young, then nodded once, a small formal dip of the chin, and Bram had the sudden absurd sense of having concluded a treaty with an ambassador of a nation that no longer existed.

"Sleep now," she said. "In my bed tonight. I'll take the loft."

Wren went obediently, pausing at the foot of the stairs. "Bram. The people in the city." Her voice was very small. "They weren't skeletons. They were waiting like—they were *standing*. Like they were going to walk somewhere, when I got there. Where would they walk to?"

Bram held her gaze and lied after all, just once, just gently. "Nowhere, little wren. Sleep."

* * *

The child slept. Bram did not.

She sat by the banked fire with a cup of tea gone cold in her hands and made herself think like the criminal she officially was, because criminals survived by noticing things early.

Item: the thaw on the night Wren arrived, out of season, localized, brief. Item: the frost melting to glass in the child's footprints. Item: breath that did not steam, water that leaned, fish that died bloated and ancient in a healthy river. Item: salt, forty miles inland, in the air, on the lips, in the smell of a sleeping child's hair when Bram had tucked the blankets close. Item: the brand scars on her own ribs, burning—not with the Order's old fire but with something answering, something recognizing, the way one tuning fork answers another across a room.

Her blood was storm-blood. Everyone in the old stories agreed on that much: the Kavanaghs had called weather for the kings of the coast before the coast became Vhalcrest, before the Vigilant Order decided that weather-calling was heresy and storm-callers were traitors, before the purges. Before her mother's sigil was scraped off doors and her family's name became a cautionary tale told to novices. She had spent ten years strangling that inheritance, keeping it down like a swallowed coal, and tonight it had *answered* something. Tonight, for the first time since her exile, the buried thing in her blood had stirred—not toward the sky, but toward the sea.

Toward the child.

Bram set down the cold tea and pressed her palm flat against her ribs, against the ridged scar tissue, and asked the silence a question she didn't have words for. Nothing answered. But the scars stayed warm a long time, like a hand resting on her from the inside.

Through the loft's open door she could hear Wren breathing, slow and even, a child's breathing, ordinary and dear. And beneath it—if she strained, if she let herself strain—Bram could almost hear the other sound again, the hum, very faint now, coming not from the child but from everywhere. From the ground. From the water table, maybe, from every hidden vein of moisture running down out of these mountains toward the distant sea. As if something far below had lifted its head, all along the coast of the world, and begun to listen.

*Aelmarrow,* the child had said, the first night, pointing east, pointing down.

Bram looked at the shuttered window, beyond which the mountains stood black against the stars, and beyond them, forty miles and nine thousand feet below, the invisible line where the land gave up and the sea began.

Three hundred years, she thought. Three hundred years the histories said the drowned kingdom had lain quiet.

Someone—or something—had apparently decided the quiet was over.

She banked the fire, checked the door bar twice, and took her father's old spyglass up to the loft window to watch the eastern sky until dawn. She told herself it was habit, a smuggler's caution, nothing more.

But when the sun finally rose, red and sluggish through the valley haze, she was still watching—and she saw, far off on the low road where it wound down out of the foothills, a single figure on a mule, traveling alone, stopping at intervals to kneel beside the frozen roadside streams as if listening to them.

Even at that distance, even through old glass, Bram could see the glint of the relic-hunter's brass instruments, and the white knot of a traveler's badge that was not quite a pilgrim's badge, and not quite a soldier's either.

It belonged to the third kind. The kind that paid.

Bram lowered the spyglass slowly, and her hand was steady, and her heart was not.
