## 5. The Order's Long Arm

The stranger came up the mountain on the third day of the thaw, when the snow went rotten and the pines dripped like leaky eaves.

Bram saw him first as a smudge against the white—a single figure on the low road where caravans had stopped passing weeks ago, moving with the deliberate economy of a man who knew how far it was between shelters and did not trust any of them. She watched him from the window of the waystation for the better part of an hour while Wren slept in the loft above, curled around the hearth-warmth like a cat around a sunbeam.

She should have turned him away. Every instinct she'd spent ten years sharpening said so: strangers were weather you didn't ask for, trouble that walked on two legs, and the mountain was full of both this time of year. But he was limping. And his left arm hung wrong, cradled against his chest, and there was something in the set of his shoulders—the particular hunch of a man who had been walking too long with pain and had stopped expecting anyone to care—that snagged on something old in her.

*You were nineteen once,* said the treacherous voice in her chest. *You knocked on doors too.*

She put water on to boil and told herself it was only because turning away a dying man in the thaw-mud would bring wolves down on her doorstep, and wolves were bad for business. The lie sat comfortably enough. Most of her best lies did.

By the time he reached the door he had resolved into detail: fifty-odd years old, grey-stubbled, dressed in the layered road-leathers of a traveler who had crossed more borders than he could name. A satchel rode his good shoulder, heavy and carefully packed, the straps worn shiny from use. His eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of glacial melt, and they moved over the waystation's exterior before they ever found her face—reading the walls, the roofline, the lightning-rod she kept disguised as a chimney brace, the iron stakes driven at the four corners of the property that any honest farmer would have called fence-posts and any storm-caller would have called what they were.

His gaze lingered on the stakes exactly long enough.

"Road's bad," Bram said through the door, before he could knock. "Shelter's full."

"It's just me." His voice was gravel over gravel. "And I'll pay. Coin, or news—whichever's worth more out here."

"We don't get lonely enough to need either."

"No," he agreed, and she heard the smile in it, thin and humorless. "I don't imagine you do."

Silence. The meltwater ticked off the eaves. Somewhere downslope, a crow said something rude to another crow.

Then: "The girl singing by the river last month," he said quietly. "Three days after the ice broke. Fish floating belly-up for a mile. Water tasting of brine forty miles from any sea." A pause, measured out like medicine. "And the night before that, thunder out of a clear sky, rolling backward, west to east, which doesn't happen anywhere in the world except—" he let the sentence die the way a man sets down something fragile, "—well. It happens here."

Bram's hand had gone cold on the latch without her permission.

"I could keep talking on your step," the stranger offered, "or you could let me in and we could talk like people who've both survived longer than we should have."

Every sane part of her said *shut the door*. She thought about Wren asleep in the loft, small and warm and speaking a dead language in her dreams. She thought about the fish. She thought about the word *here*, hanging in the cold air like a hook baited with everything she'd buried.

She opened the door.

* * *

He gave his name as Doss Marlowe, though the way he said it suggested it belonged to someone else—a coat borrowed so long ago he'd stopped noticing the fit. He drank his tea in careful sips, favoring the ribs on his right side, and set his satchel between his boots where he could reach it, which was not a threat so much as a habit, the way some men scratched their beards.

Wren watched him from the top of the ladder with the flat golden stare she gave everything new, half-hidden in shadow, silent as furniture. Bram had taught her nothing about hiding; the child had arrived already knowing how, the way she'd arrived knowing the drowned tongue. Marlowe glanced up once, nodded to Wren as one nods to a cat, and returned his attention to Bram—which was its own kind of message. He had decided the child was scenery. He was lying, and letting Bram see that he knew she knew.

"You're a relic-hunter," Bram said. Not a question. The satchel's shape gave it away—compartmentalized, padded, built for carrying things that other people wanted badly enough to kill for.

"Retired," Marlowe said. "Mostly. I dig where the Vigilant Order hasn't gotten around to digging yet, and I sell what I find to collectors who like their history older than the Order says history is allowed to be." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Which is how I know things the Order would prefer I didn't."

"The Order can prefer all it likes. Doesn't make you interesting."

"No. But this might." He reached into his coat—not toward the satchel, deliberately—and drew out a folded square of oilcloth, unwrapped it on the table. Inside lay a strip of lead, green with age, stamped with characters Bram hadn't seen since she was a girl kneeling in the archives beneath the Citadel, learning to read the script of a kingdom that no map admitted to.

Aelmarrow. The drowned kingdom. The salt-kingdom.

Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"Found it two seasons back, in a riverbed near Greywater Ford," Marlowe said. "Lead curse-tablet, pre-Drowning work. Somebody pressed it into the mud asking the deep water for protection." He looked up. "Protection *from* the deep water, if you read the grammar right. Which most people can't. But you could."

"Could I," Bram said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"You read it in under three seconds. Your eyes went left to right, then right to left, then stopped on the fourth character, because the mason botched it and everyone who actually knows the script flinches at the same spot." He wrapped the tablet back up with the tenderness of a man handling a sleeping snake. "I've spent thirty years around people who study Aelmarrow, Kavanagh. Scholars, grave-robbers, Order archivists. None of them flinch."

The name landed in the room like a dropped knife.

Bram did not move. Ten years of practice held her face still, held her breathing even, held the storm that woke in her chest—because of course it woke, of course it did, thunder gathering behind her sternum at the sound of the name she had scrubbed out of every ledger and register and memory she could reach—held it all behind her teeth the way she'd learned to hold it when she was nineteen and the sky itself had been entered into evidence against her.

"That's not my name," she said.

"No," Marlowe agreed. "Your name is Brannoc, guide and sometime cook, out of Thornfell, references available. Very thorough. I checked. You've been careful." He took another slow sip of tea. "But careful isn't invisible. Careful is just slow. And the thing about storms, mistress guide—even ones you never call—is that they leave signatures. Iron stakes at the compass points. A rod disguised as a chimney brace, which fooled me for about four seconds. And the weather around this valley bends. Has bent for ten years. Lightning strikes the same bare ridge twice a season and nowhere else. Hail falls in circles. Any Vigilant reader with a decade of fieldwork and a grudge could sit on that ridge for a month and write your confession from the clouds alone."

Outside, the meltwater ticked. Above them, faintly, the loft boards creaked—Wren shifting, listening. Bram prayed to no god in particular that the child couldn't understand common speech well enough yet to catch the shape of what was being said. *Kavanagh.* Traitor. Storm-witch. The girl who called lightning into the Grandmaster's own courtyard and ran before the chains cooled.

"What do you want?" Bram asked.

"To leave. Alive, preferably, and soon." Marlowe set down the cup. "And to give you something first, because thirty years of digging teaches you one law above all others: you don't leave debts sitting on tables in houses like this one."

"And what's that worth from a stranger?"

"Nothing. That's why it's free." He leaned forward, wincing at his ribs, and his washed-out eyes were suddenly very awake. "There's a price on your head. There has been since you ran. Two hundred crowns the year I heard it quoted, and that was eight years ago, and the Order revises its lists upward every spring like a merchant raising rents. They call it a redemption fee. Redemption of the storm-gift, they say—you know how they talk. As if the gift were a debt *you* owed *them*."

"I know how they talk."

"Then you know the list gets read aloud at every chapter-house, every garrison, every crossroads shrine from here to the coast. You know the readers are mostly bored clerks. But you also know that every few years, the Order sends out a Seeker—an actual hunter, not a clerk—to walk the wild places and refresh the list. Ask questions. Look at weather." He tapped the table once, softly, with one scarred finger. "A Seeker passed through Greywater Ford eleven days ago. Buying supplies. Asking after a woman living alone in the high passes. Asking, and I quote the man who sold him oats, after 'any strange child.'"

The fire popped. In the loft, the boards creaked again, and this time it did not stop creaking, and Bram understood that Wren was at the top of the ladder now, listening with her whole body, understanding more than Bram had ever guessed.

"How long," Bram said, "since Greywater Ford?"

"Eleven days. A Seeker travels light and fast, but these mountains in thaw are nobody's friend. Call it six days behind me. Maybe five." Marlowe shrugged with his good shoulder. "Maybe less, if he hired local guides who know shortcuts I don't. Maybe more, if the rivers are up. I'd plan for less. Hope is a luxury item and I've never been able to afford it."

Bram stood. It wasn't a decision so much as her body deciding for her, the old machinery of survival engaging gear by gear. She banked the fire. She checked the shutter-bars. She did all the small domestic things that meant *this conversation is ending* without ever saying so.

"You're leaving," she said.

"I'm leaving." Marlowe rose more slowly, gathered his satchel, settled it on his good shoulder. At the door he paused, and for a moment the relic-hunter fell away and she saw the man underneath—tired, hurt, and honest in the specific way of people who had made peace with being neither good nor safe. "For what it's worth, Kavanagh—I dug up a lot of Aelmarrow in my day. Walked its drowned streets with weighted boots and a lantern. And I'll tell you what every diver knows and no scholar admits: the city isn't angry. It's *waiting*. Thirty years I felt it down there, patient as stone. Whatever's coming out of that salt—and something is coming, don't fool yourself—it isn't coming for revenge." He pulled the door open onto the grey drip of the thaw. "It's coming to collect something somebody promised it. And the Order knows. The Order has always known. That's why the list still has your name on it, and why it always will. You're not a criminal to them, girl. You're a loose thread."

He stepped out into the mud. Then he turned back, one hand on the doorframe, and delivered the last of it gently, the way you tell a patient the wound is worse than it looks:

"They'll come up the low road, because the low road is the only road. They'll come in grey cloaks with the eye-and-anvil stitched at the shoulder, and they'll knock politely, and they'll ask about a singing child, and they will not leave until they've searched every board of this house." His pale eyes flicked upward, toward the loft, toward the small silence crouched there. "Whatever you decide to do with the girl—decide it tonight. Five days goes faster than you think. It always does."

Then he was gone, down the slope, a dark figure shrinking into the white and the dripping green, until the pines took him.

* * *

Bram stood in the doorway until the cold drove her back inside. Then she barred the door, threw the bolt, and dragged the heavy supply chest across it besides, which she had not done in ten years, and hated herself for doing now.

The loft boards creaked. Wren came down the ladder one rung at a time, solemn as a small owl, and stood in front of Bram and looked up at her with those gold-flecked eyes.

"Singing child," Wren said.

Her common speech was still broken, still assembled from fragments Bram had fed her across the winter, but she had caught it—of course she had caught it, children caught everything, children were nets woven too fine for anything to slip through.

"Yes," Bram said. "That's you."

"Bad men come." Wren turned her hands over, palms up, the way she did when she was searching for a word. "Bad men come for—" she pressed both palms flat against her own chest, over her heart, over whatever burned in there that Bram could feel sometimes on the bad nights, a heat like a coal wrapped in silk, "—for *this*."

"Not while I'm breathing," Bram said.

It came out harder than she intended, rough as a struck flint, and Wren flinched—and then, slowly, did the thing she had never done before. She stepped forward and pressed her forehead against Bram's sternum, right over the place where the storm lived, and stayed there. Trusting the thunder not to break.

Bram put a hand on the small head and looked out the window, past the iron stakes, past the bending trees, down toward the low road where the meltwater ran silver in the failing light.

Five days.

Somewhere down there, moving through the wet dark, a man in a grey cloak was reading the weather like scripture—and finding her name written in it.

She began, quietly, to pack.
