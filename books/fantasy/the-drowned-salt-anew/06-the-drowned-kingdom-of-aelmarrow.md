## 6. The Drowned Kingdom of Aelmarrow

The snows broke early that year, and with them came the thaw-melt that turned the river below the waystation from a whisper to a roar. Bram had lived through eleven such springs in Thornfell, and she knew their grammar: first the ice groaning at night like an old man turning in his sleep, then the brown water rising to lick the roots of the alders, then the smell of wet stone and rotting leaves that meant winter had lost its argument. She had learned to love the mountains the way one loves a difficult parent—warily, gratefully, never quite trusting them not to turn.

Wren loved them without reservation.

The girl had been with her since midwinter now, long enough for the hollows in her cheeks to fill, long enough for her hair—dark as kelp and just as tangled when wet—to grow past her shoulders. Long enough for Bram to stop flinching when small feet padded into her room before dawn. She still did not speak a word of common speech. But she had learned other things: how to hold an axe for kindling, how to keep her fingers out from under the mule's lips, how to sit very still on the roof beam and watch storms come down the valley like Bram watched them, with her whole body listening.

It was on one of those watching mornings that Wren began to draw.

Bram had left charcoal stubs by the hearth for her own use—sketching trap-lines, marking where the snow bridges rotted first. She came back from checking the snares above the treeline to find the floor around the fire black with lines. Not scribbles. Lines with intention. Curves that repeated, arcs nested inside arcs, a great ring broken into segments like a wheel seen through water.

Wren sat cross-legged in the middle of it, charcoal dust to her elbows, drawing with the fierce concentration of someone copying something she could see but no one else could.

"What have you got there, little bird?"

Wren looked up. Her eyes were that strange pale grey-green, the color of shallow water over sand, and they caught the firelight in a way that made Bram's neck prickle. The girl pointed at the largest of the drawings—a sweeping curve—and made a sound. It was the drowned tongue again, that language of open vowels and swallowed consonants, and though Bram could not parse it, she heard the shape of a word she'd heard before, in the child's sleep-singing by the river:

*Aelmarrow.*

She hadn't known until that moment that she'd recognized it as a name.

* * *

Bram had seen ruins once. That was the thing she could not shake, sitting there on her own floorboards while a mud-child drew a dead city in charcoal.

Nine years ago, in her second year of exile, she'd taken work with a salvage crew off the Greywater coast—the kind of work smugglers did when the passes were closed and coin ran thin. Diving the shallows off Cape Sereth, where the old charts said *unmarked shoals* in the careful handwriting of men who knew better than to write what was actually there. Bram had gone down three times in the leaded suit, breathing through the brass helmet while the world went green and silent.

The first dive she'd thought it was reef. Coral-shaped stone, ridged and branching. The second dive, in better light, she'd understood it was architecture—towers furred with barnacle and weed, their tops snapped clean as if something enormous had bitten the city off at the waist. And on the third dive, following a gully between two fallen walls, she'd found the arch.

It had been carved with waves. Waves, and above them, wings.

She'd surfaced screaming into the helmet, or so the crew told her after; she remembered only the cold closing over her like a mouth, and the certainty—absolute, animal—that something far below had turned its attention upward toward the pale flicker of her lamp. The captain had laughed and said every diver felt that. Bram had taken her pay and never gone under salt water again.

Now she crouched over a child's charcoal map and felt the same cold rise in her, forty miles from any sea.

Because the arch was there. Third drawing from the fire, small and precise: two pillars, a curve, waves beneath, wings above. Drawn from underneath, the way you would see it if you swam up through it looking back.

"Wren." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Where is this?"

The girl frowned at her, hearing only the tone. Then she seemed to understand the question behind it, because she crawled across the floorboards, swept the smaller sketches aside, and began a new drawing in the clear space near the hearthstone. She worked fast, sure-handed, and what emerged made the hairs on Bram's arms lift beneath her sleeves.

A coastline. A bay like a cupped hand. A river mouth. And beyond the coast, out in the blank space where the paper should have been empty, a city—drawn as if seen from above through clear water, streets and rings and towers all visible beneath a surface Wren indicated with a single wavering line.

Bram knew that coastline. Every guide in Thornfell knew it, because the charts knew it, because the toll-roads followed it. The cupped hand of the bay was Serevan Bay. The river was the Greywater. The city sat exactly where the charts said *unmarked shoals*, exactly where the careful handwriting of frightened cartographers trailed off into nothing.

Except the charts showed nothing. Empty water. Bram had sailed over that empty water. There had been nothing to see from above—not a tower-tip, not a shadow. Forty fathoms down or four hundred, the sea had kept its secret perfectly.

But this child, who had appeared out of a blizzard speaking a language dead three hundred years, drew it as though she had flown over it yesterday. As though the water were glass.

* * *

Over the next six days, the maps spread.

They covered the floor first, then the table, then—when Bram surrendered and tacked sheets of scrap vellum to the wall rather than watch the girl draw on the stones—the walls themselves. Wren worked with a feverish steadiness that worried Bram more than fever would have. She ate when reminded. She slept hard and dreamlessly, most nights. But waking hours belonged to the charcoal, and the charcoal gave up a kingdom.

Bram learned its name piece by piece, assembling meaning from repetition the way she assembled weather from cloud-shapes. *Aelmarrow* was the city—or the kingdom; the word seemed to hold both. There was a word for the harbor that Wren always sang rather than spoke, a low round note like a bell underwater. There was a word that made the girl's face go dark and tight, and Bram guessed it meant the drowning itself. There was a word that recurred on nearly every sheet, attached to towers, to gates, to the great ringed structure at the city's heart that Wren drew larger than everything else, shading it in until it looked less like a building than a wound:

A word that sounded, to Bram's ear, like *vhal*. Like *Vhalcrest*. The capital's name. The capital that had stood, fat and smug and dry, on the cliffs above Serevan Bay for as long as anyone remembered.

She stood before the wall-maps one evening with a mug of barley tea going cold in her hand and let the horror arrive slowly, the way cold water finds the seams of a boot.

The city beneath the bay and the city on the cliffs shared a name. Shared, if Wren's drawings were anything to trust, streets. The new city had been built on top of the drowned one—or the drowned one had been built beneath the new one—and somewhere in the archives of Vhalcrest, in the ledgers and the law-books and the histories taught to children, someone had decided that the lower city should be forgotten so thoroughly that even its name would survive only in the throat of a feral child who walked out of a snowstorm.

Three hundred years. The Vigilant Order was three hundred years old. The Hollow King's line was three hundred years old. Bram had spent ten years telling herself those numbers were coincidence, the way exiles tell themselves many things.

Now a child was drawing her the proof in charcoal, and the proof had wings carved over waves.

* * *

On the seventh night, Wren drew the dragons.

Bram had gone out to bring in the mule and came back to find the girl standing before the wall, swaying slightly, charcoal moving without her seeming to look at it. The figure taking shape under her hand was wrong in scale—too large for the sheet, spilling off the edges—and beautiful the way lightning is beautiful, all terrible proportion. A serpentine body coiled around the central tower of the drowned city. Wings like sails. A head with an eye that Wren shaded and shaded until the charcoal shone black as a well.

And around the dragon, in the drowned tongue, words. Many words. Wren wrote them in a flowing script Bram had never seen, and she wrote them quickly, urgently, her breath coming short, and when the last stroke was done she stepped back and stared at what she'd made with an expression Bram had never seen on a child's face.

It was grief. Old, worn-smooth grief, the kind that belongs to someone who has carried it a long time.

Then Wren's knees buckled, and Bram caught her before she hit the hearthstones.

* * *

The girl slept through the night and most of the next day, burning faintly, muttering in that liquid tongue. Bram sat beside her and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do.

She thought about the fish that had risen dead to the river's surface. About the brine-taste in the water, forty miles inland. About the way storms bent toward the waystation now, curious as dogs, and how the sky had begun to know her name again after ten years of her silence—and whether it was only her calling they answered, or whether something else in this house had a voice the weather also recognized.

She thought about the arch with the wings over the waves, and the eye Wren had rubbed black, and the fact that every map the child drew placed the drowned city directly beneath the seat of the Hollow Court.

Wonder and horror, braided. That was the truth of it, and Bram held both strands and could not separate them. Wonder: that a whole kingdom lay under the grey water, complete, lit in a child's memory, its bells and harbors and coiled guardians rendered in charcoal on the walls of a smuggler's shack. Horror: that kingdoms did not drown by accident. That forgetting on this scale required hands. That the child who remembered had come to her door half-dead, pursued by nothing Bram could see, and that the mountains around them had felt, lately, like a held breath.

Near dusk, Wren woke. She lay still a moment, orienting, then her eyes found the dragon on the wall and filled with tears she did not shed. She said something soft in the drowned tongue. It sounded like a farewell, or an apology.

Then she rolled onto her side, looked at Bram, and pointed—one small finger, steady—at the eastern window. Beyond it, past the black teeth of the pines, the lowlands stretched away toward Serevan Bay, invisible in the gathering dark but present as a weight.

She pointed, and then she pressed her hand flat against her own chest, over her heart, and said a single word. Not *Aelmarrow*. Something else. Something that made the fire dip in the hearth, though there was no draft.

Bram did not know the word. But she knew, with the same absolute certainty she'd felt thirty fathoms under the Greywater, that she had just been told what was sleeping inside the child—and that whatever it was, it was the reason the girl had walked out of the blizzard. It was the reason the dead fish had risen. It was the reason the maps poured out of her like water finding a crack.

Something in Wren was meant to go home. Or something in Wren was meant to wake. Bram could not tell which frightened her more.

She banked the fire, tucked the blanket higher around the small shoulders, and stood a long time at the eastern window, watching the dark lowlands where the sea waited with its secret.

Two days later, a stranger's horse stood tied outside her gate, and a man with road-dust in his seams and a relic-hunter's tools on his belt was studying her roofline with far too much interest—and asking, in the pleasant voice men used when they wanted you to think they were fools, why the storm-clouds over her valley gathered in perfect rings.
