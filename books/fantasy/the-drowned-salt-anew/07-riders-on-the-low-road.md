## 7. Riders on the Low Road

The riders came up the low road three days after the frost first silvered the junipers, and Bram knew they were coming before she saw them.

Not by storm-sign this time—there was no weather in them, no pressure in her teeth, no taste of iron at the back of the sky. She knew because Wren stopped singing.

For six days the child had filled the waystation with that drowned language, a low tide-pull of syllables that made the kettle hum and the lamp-flame lean sideways when she grew excited. Bram had learned to work around it the way one works around a creaking floorboard—half listening, half ignoring, telling herself it was only noise. But on the morning of the third day of frost, Wren went silent mid-word, standing at the window with her palms flat against the glass like a bird pressed to a pane, and Bram felt the silence settle over the cabin heavier than any snow.

"What is it?" Bram asked. She'd been mending a pack-strap, waxed thread pulled tight between her fingers. The child didn't answer. She never answered questions in common speech yet, though she understood more of it every week—a fact Bram tried not to think about too closely, because understanding meant learning, and learning meant the child was staying, and if the child was staying then Bram had let another living thing past the walls she'd spent ten years building.

Wren turned from the window. Her eyes were that strange grey-green, sea-glass over deep water, and for a moment Bram saw something in them she had no name for. Not fear exactly. Recognition.

Then, far down the valley, a hawk cried—or something that wanted to be mistaken for a hawk—and Wren said, in her own tongue, one soft word that made the fire gutter in the hearth.

Bram crossed to the window herself.

They were still two miles off, dark shapes on the pale ribbon of the low road where it wound along the river before climbing toward Thornfell's skirts. Six of them, riding in pairs, and even at that distance Bram could read them the way she read cloud-forms: the matched black of their cloaks, the way they sat their horses with a soldier's economy rather than a courier's hurry, the banner-furled lance carried by the lead rider. Not smugglers. Not relic-hunters. Not tax-men out of Dunmark Ford.

Court riders. Hollow Court.

Her hands went cold on the windowsill. Ten years, and the sight of those colors still reached into her chest and closed around something old.

Behind her, Wren made a sound—a small, swallowed thing, halfway between a word and a whimper—and Bram turned and did the hardest thing she had done since the night she'd found mud-caked footprints on her doorstep. She smiled.

"It's all right," she said, in the gentle voice she was still learning to use, the one that didn't fit her mouth right. "Nothing's going to touch you. Do you hear me? Nothing."

She didn't know if the child believed her. She wasn't sure she believed herself. But she set down the mending, banked the fire low, and began to move through the cabin with the particular efficiency of a woman preparing to lie for her life.

* * *

There was an art to hiding things, and Bram had mastered it the way other people mastered instruments.

It wasn't concealment—not really. Concealment was crude, a shuttered window, a locked chest, and every lock was an accusation. No: the art was arrangement. You hid nothing; you simply arranged the truth so that the shape of it told a different story. A smuggler's guide who kept a child would be asked why. A hermit who kept a child would be pitied, then suspected. But a grieving woman—a sister, say, or a widowed cousin—who kept her dead brother's daughter out of stubborn family obligation? That was not a mystery. That was a small sadness, and small sadnesses made men look away.

She had an hour, maybe less. She used all of it.

Wren's sleeping pallet behind the curtain became a storage loft; the child herself went into the root cellar beneath the floor stones, which smelled of turnips and cold earth and had a single knot-hole through which she could see the main room. Bram hated it—the darkness, the confinement, the memory it must press against whatever the child had fled from before the mountain—but there was no better place, and Wren surprised her by going down the ladder without being lifted, without protest, pausing only to look up at Bram with that unnerving steadiness.

"Stay quiet," Bram whispered. "However long. However frightened. Quiet." She pressed her own fist briefly against her sternum, then pointed at the child. *Quiet here.* It was the closest thing they had to a shared sign, invented over weeks of broken language, and Wren pressed her own small fist to her chest and nodded once.

Bram lowered the floor stone over her and dragged the loom across it, then scattered wool scraps and a dropped spindle around its legs as though the mess had been accumulating for months. Then she opened the shutters wide, put water on to boil, laid out two cups instead of one, and waited.

While she waited, she practiced.

This was the part no one ever understood about lying—the labor of it, the craft. Anyone could invent a story. What kept you alive was the rehearsal: walking through every question, every answer, every pause, until the lie wore grooves in you deep enough that the truth couldn't climb out. *Whose child is that? My brother's girl. Where's your brother? Dead—fever, two winters back, down in Marsh Hollow. Why bring her up here? Because there was no one else, and I'm the last of us, and the mountain's poor but it feeds what it has.* And if they pushed further—if they mentioned singing, if they mentioned the river, if they mentioned the fish—

But they wouldn't know about the river. They couldn't. Unless someone had talked, and the thought arrived like a splinter driven under a fingernail: *the relic-hunter.* The man who had passed through in autumn, all sharp eyes and sharper questions, who had looked at her door-lintel a moment too long, who had named the Vigilant Order's bounty with a smile he probably thought was friendly. He had ridden east, toward the lowlands. Toward Vhalcrest. Toward the Court.

*You've had weeks,* she told herself. *If he sold you, they've known for weeks. This isn't a hunt. It's a sweep.*

That should have comforted her. It didn't. Sweeps were worse than hunts. Hunts followed a name; sweeps followed a pattern, and patterns caught innocent people in their nets, and nets did not care how well you lied if the shape of your life fit the mesh.

Outside, the light changed. Shadows moved across the snow.

* * *

They knocked the way soldiers knock—one blow, flat of the fist, a sound that expected obedience rather than offered courtesy. Bram counted to five, so it wouldn't seem she'd been waiting at the door, then opened it.

Six riders. Five remained mounted in the yard, breath steaming from their horses in the bitter air, and one stood on her step: a woman, forty perhaps, with a face like a closed ledger and eyes the color of old pewter. Her cloak was good wool, court-black, cut for riding rather than ceremony, and at her throat she wore a small badge of tarnished silver—a crown with no jewels in it, just the empty settings, like a ring after the stone's been pried out.

Bram had seen that badge once before, from a great distance, on a day she had spent ten years trying not to remember. Her stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like a body rolling in deep water.

"Mistress Kavanagh." The rider's voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of someone who had never needed to raise it. "We're looking for directions, and perhaps a warm cup. May we?"

"You may," Bram said, and stepped aside, and hated how easily the words came. Ten years of practice. "Though my tea's pine-needle and my bread's two days old. Mountain hospitality."

"Better than the road." The woman came in, stamped snow from her boots with precise little movements, and let her gaze travel the room once—once, thoroughly, the way a customs officer reads a manifest. Bram watched her do it and kept her hands loose at her sides and her breathing slow. The gaze touched the loom, the wool scraps, the spindle. Moved on. Touched the two cups by the fire.

"Expecting company?" the rider asked.

"My own bad habit," Bram said. "I set out cups whether anyone's coming or not. Talking to empty chairs keeps a body sane up here." She smiled, crooked and self-deprecating, the smile of a woman slightly embarrassed by her own loneliness. It was a good smile. She'd built it years ago, stone by stone, out of true materials—because the best lies, like the best bridges, used real stone.

The rider's mouth curved in answer, but her eyes didn't join it. "Kavanagh," she repeated, settling onto the bench Bram indicated. "That's a Thornfell name."

"And a Dunmark name, and a coast name, if you go back far enough. We get everywhere eventually, like burrs." Bram poured the pine-needle tea, steam rising between them. "You said directions. You're a long way off the post road for directions."

"We're not looking for a place." The woman accepted the cup but didn't drink. "We're looking for a person. A child—a girl, eight years old, maybe nine. Dark-haired. Small for her age." She said it evenly, reciting from a list Bram could almost see hanging in the air behind her eyes. "There are rumors she came north through the hill country. Alone, or near enough. They say—" and here the pewter eyes lifted and fixed on Bram with mild, terrible attention— "they say she sings. Songs in no tongue anyone knows."

The fire popped. Somewhere under the floor, beneath the loom and the wool and two feet of packed earth, Bram knew, a small heart was beating fast enough to shake the whole cabin, though no sound escaped, and Bram loved her for that silence more fiercely than she had loved anything in ten years.

"Singing children," Bram said, and laughed—a short, rough laugh, genuine in its texture because it drew on real material, on the memory of every fool who had ever come up her mountain chasing stories. "Is that what brings the Crown's riders into the high passes? A song?"

"The song is unusual."

"All songs are unusual, coming out of children. Half the ballads in the lowlands started as some shepherd's brat making noise at sheep." She sipped her own tea, unhurried. "I'll tell you what I tell the relic-hunters and the pilgrims and every other soul the road washes up here: I see maybe twenty travelers a year, and I remember maybe five. A child alone in Thornfell in autumn wouldn't have lived a week. Wolves, weather, ravines. If a girl passed through, she passed through dead, and the mountain keeps what it takes."

"You live alone, then." It wasn't a question. The rider's gaze drifted again, casual as smoke, and snagged—Bram saw it snag—on the second cup.

"I live alone," Bram agreed.

"But two cups."

"Two cups." Bram set hers down and met the pewter eyes straight on, and here she made her gamble, the one she'd rehearsed in the hour of preparation without knowing she was rehearsing it. She let her face change—let the mask slip just enough to show the raw thing underneath, grief worn smooth by years but still wet at the center. "My brother's daughter lived with me. Two winters past. Fever took her in the spring—you can see her marker on the south slope, if you've a mind to dig through frozen ground to check. I keep setting out her cup. I know how it looks. I know what it sounds like, a woman alone up here talking to ghosts." She picked up the second cup and drank from it herself, deliberately, and her hand shook, and she let it shake, because the shaking was true—it just wasn't true about what they thought. "So yes. I live alone. Most days I wish I didn't. Now you've had your tea and your directions, which is to say none, and I've got wood to split before the light goes. Was there anything else?"

Silence. The rider studied her, and Bram endured the study the way a cliff endures rain—by being, entirely, what she appeared to be. Under the floor, she willed at the child: *still, still, still.*

"There is one more thing," the rider said at last, and rose, and from inside her cloak she drew a folded paper, and unfolded it, and held it out.

Bram took it.

A drawing, done in charcoal by someone skilled—a child's face, younger than Wren was now, rounder, softer, but unmistakably the same. The same wide-set eyes. The same stubborn line of the jaw. Beneath it, in a clerk's careful hand: words in the Court's formal script that Bram refused to read fully, because reading them would mean knowing them, and she wanted—needed—to be able to say, later, honestly, *I never looked closely.*

"She is not accused of anything," the rider said quietly. "She is sought. There are people who wish her well. There are allowances, a household, schooling. Whatever she has suffered on the roads, it ends when she is found." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat against skin. "And there is a reward. Substantial. Enough that a woman alone on a mountain might stop wishing for company."

Bram looked at the drawing for a long moment. Then she handed it back.

"If I find her," she said, "I'll bury her next to my niece, and I won't take blood money for a dead child. Good day to you."

* * *

They rode out at dusk, single file now, their shapes dissolving into the blue shadow of the valley. Bram stood in the doorway until the last of them rounded the river bend, and then a while longer, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

When she finally moved, she moved quickly—shuttingters, bar the door, drag the loom aside, lift the stone. Cold air breathed up out of the dark below, smelling of turnip and earth.

"Wren." Her voice cracked on the name. "Come up. Come up now."

The ladder creaked. The child rose into the lamplight, gray-faced, lips nearly blue with cold and fear, and Bram gathered her in before her feet had cleared the last rung—gathered her up the way you lift something both precious and breakable, one arm under her, one hand cradling the back of her head, and Wren's thin arms locked around her neck with a strength that eight years shouldn't hold.

"They're gone," Bram said into her hair. "Gone, do you hear me? Gone."

Wren was trembling. Then, muffled against Bram's shoulder, she spoke—not the drowned tongue this time, but common speech, halting, each word placed like a stone across a stream:

"Not... gone. Come... back."

Bram closed her eyes. Of course. Of course the child was right. Riders like that didn't leave; they circled, they reported, they returned with more. The lie had held today because Bram had held it, but lies were weather, and weather always broke.

She carried Wren to the fire, wrapped her in the good blanket, fed her broth spoonful by spoonful while the child watched the door with those sea-glass eyes. And somewhere between the third spoonful and the fifth, the fear in Bram's chest finished its slow transformation—the alchemy she'd felt beginning all week, dread hardening, condensing, taking on weight and edge—until what sat in her breast was not fear at all but something with a much older name.

Resolve.

Let them come back. Let them come back with twelve riders, with sixty, with the whole hollow weight of that jeweled nothing of a crown. She had given ten years to invisibility and it had bought her nothing but an empty chair and a second cup she drank from herself. She looked down at the small head drooping against her arm, at the chapped hands curled around the bowl, and understood with perfect clarity that she would burn the art of hiding to its foundations before she let them touch this child.

"They'll not have you," she murmured, not sure the girl heard, not caring. "Whatever they are. Whoever sent them. Not while I'm above ground."

Wren's eyes lifted. And slowly, solemnly, the child raised one small fist and pressed it against her own chest—*quiet here*—then reached out and pressed it, gently, against Bram's.

Outside, far down the valley, thunder rolled across a sky with no clouds in it, and Bram Kavanagh went very still, listening, as the storm answered a call she hadn't made.
