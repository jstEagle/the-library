## 8. Storm-Called

The riders came back on the third day, and this time they did not pretend to be lost.

Bram saw them first from the ridge above the waystation, where she had gone at dawn to read the sky the way other people read letters. The weather had been wrong for two days—too still, too sweet, the air holding its breath like a liar before a confession—and she had told herself it was only a mountain spring deciding what it wanted to be. But now she understood. The stillness had not been the mountain's. It had been theirs, riding up the low road with their banners furled and their horses shod for silence.

Six of them. Dark cloaks, dark horses, and the sigil stitched at the shoulder that she knew even at this distance: a crown inverted, its points driven downward like teeth. The Hollow Court did not send six riders across forty miles of Thornfell passes to ask twice about a singing child.

They had asked once already. She had lied well. She had been lying since she was nineteen years old, and lying was the one craft in which she had never needed magic.

But they had circled back.

She stood a long moment with the wind moving through her hair, feeling the pressure in the air the way an old hound feels a storm in its hip joints, and below her the waystation sat small and gray against the pines, smoke rising thin from the chimney. Wren was inside. Wren, who had slept badly for three nights, muttering in that drowned language, her small hands opening and closing like something trying to hold water. Wren, who had looked at Bram yesterday over breakfast and said—in careful, broken common, each word a stone carried up from some deep place—"They come for the song. I hear them coming in my sleep."

Eight years old, and she heard riders in her dreams before Bram's eyes could find them on a road.

*They come for the song.* Not for her. For whatever sang through her, whatever vast drowned thing moved behind her eyes when she spoke words no living throat should know. And Bram had stood in the doorway after the first visit, smiling her smuggler's smile, saying *no child here but mine own, and she's dumb as a post and half-deaf besides*, and the rider in the lead had studied her with pale patient eyes and said, *We'll look anyway*, and they had looked, and found nothing, because Wren had hidden herself in the root cellar without being told, curled among the turnips with her hands over her mouth as if she already knew what her voice cost.

Now they were back, and they had not come to look.

Bram counted again out of habit, though the number would not change. Six riders, spread in a crescent, coming up the switchbacks with the unhurried confidence of men who had decided the answer in advance. Behind them, far down the valley, the low road unspooled gray and empty. No wagons. No witnesses. No one to see what happened at a smuggler's waystation in the high country, where travelers went missing all the time and the mountains were blamed, and rightly so, because usually it *was* the mountains.

Usually.

She turned and went down through the pines, fast and quiet, her boots finding the deer trail by memory. Ten years she had walked these slopes without leaving more mark than snowmelt. Ten years she had kept the vow she'd made on her knees in the Vigilant citadel's judgment hall, blood running from her split lip onto marble older than any kingdom: *I will not call. I will not reach. Let the sky forget my name.*

And the sky had forgotten. That was the cruelest part, the part she had never confessed to anyone, not to the smugglers who trusted her weather-sense, not to herself in the long winter nights. She had thought exile would feel like freedom. Instead it felt like deafness. Like going mute in a house full of singers. Every storm that rolled through Thornfell passed over her like a stranger, and she could only stand at her window and watch lightning write letters she was forbidden to answer.

Inside, the waystation smelled of woodsmoke and pine resin and the salt-and-iron scent that clung to Wren no matter how often Bram washed her clothes in the cold stream. The girl was sitting cross-legged on the floor by the hearth, drawing in the ash with one finger—the maps again, always the maps, towers and canals and a great ringed harbor, a city drawn with the loving precision of someone sketching her mother's face.

"Wren." Bram crouched, took the small shoulders, made the girl look at her. "Riders. Six. They're coming up the goat path this time, not the road. Do you understand me? *Riders.*"

Wren's eyes went wide and dark. Ash-gray light from the window moved across her face. "The hollow ones," she whispered, and it was the first time she had used that word in front of Bram, and the word itself was wrong—not in common, not quite in the drowned tongue either, but in the space between, a word shaped like a hole. "They smell the song. They followed it up from the sea."

"Can you hide your voice? Like before?"

"I hid it before." Wren's chin trembled, and then firmed, and Bram saw something ancient move behind the child's face, something that did not belong to eight years old. "But they are closer now, and the song is louder, and I am tired, Bram. I am so tired of holding it shut."

Outside, faint but carrying, came the sound Bram had been dreading: hoofbeats on scree. Not hurrying. Not yet. Men who wanted their quarry to hear them coming.

Bram closed her eyes.

There was a version of this where she took Wren out the back and up into the high rocks, into the maze of stone and shadow she knew better than anyone alive, and they vanished the way she had vanished for ten years, and maybe it worked and maybe it didn't, and if it didn't—if the riders split up and quartered the slope, if one of them caught the sound of a child's breath in a crevice—then it ended with Wren in a cage on the low road, and Bram dead in the rocks with her vow intact and her name still forgotten.

There was another version.

She opened her eyes. The hearth-fire bent sideways in a draft, and beyond the window the pines stood motionless in that terrible held-breath stillness, waiting, the whole sky waiting, full to the brim with everything she had refused to touch for a decade.

"Bram?" Wren's hand found hers. Small fingers, cold as river stones. "You have a face like the sky has."

"The sky and I need to have words," Bram said.

* * *

She went out the front door, which surprised her a little; she had expected her body to choose the coward's exit and instead here she was walking into the open yard, boots crunching frost, putting herself between the treeline and the child behind the walls.

The riders came around the last bend in a loose crescent, and she saw them clearly now: hard-faced men in black-lacquered half-armor under the dark cloaks, swords slung for riding, and the lead rider with the pale patient eyes from before, and his eyes found her and he smiled the smile of a man who has stopped pretending.

"Mistress Kavanagh," he called, reining in twenty yards off. His voice was pleasant. Court-trained. "Or should we say the guide of the Thornfell roads, the woman of no name, the smuggler's compass. We've been asking about you in the valley towns. You're known, you know. Known and remembered. A woman who reads weather like scripture."

"Reading clouds is no crime," Bram said. Her voice came out level. Underneath it her heart was slamming like surf.

"No. But calling them is." He let the word sit there between them, delicate as a blade laid flat on a table. "Storm-calling is a Vigilant crime, and you are a Vigilant matter, and the Vigilant pays us a finder's fee that would make a bishop blush. So here is courtesy, since you extended it to us three days past: give us the child, and we ride away and report that the exiled traitor Bram Kavanagh died in the mountains years ago, as everyone prefers to believe. Refuse—" he spread his hands, genuinely regretful, which was somehow worse than menace— "and we take both of you, and the report writes itself differently."

Behind her, faintly, she heard the door latch click. Wren, obedient, hiding. Good girl. Stay behind walls of wood and stone while I do the thing I swore I'd die before doing.

"You have the wrong woman," Bram said.

"We really don't." The pale eyes flicked to the chimney smoke, to the yard, to her stance—feet planted, weight forward, the stance of a woman guarding a door rather than fleeing from one—and something knowing settled into his face. "Ten years ago the Order burned your name off its rolls and put a price on your head, and you went to ground so thoroughly that half our marshals believe you dead. But storms don't lie, mistress. Three nights ago there was weather over this valley that had no business existing. Pressure falling like a stone. Winds turning against the season. Something *reaching*. We felt it in our teeth, all of us, riding up from the coast." He leaned forward in the saddle. "The sky remembers you, Kavanagh. Even if you don't."

And that—that was the moment. Not fear, though fear was there, cold and bright. Not anger, though anger was there too, banked ten years and suddenly roaring. It was the phrase itself. *The sky remembers you.*

Because she had spent ten years telling herself the opposite. Telling herself the silence was mutual, that the sky had turned its face away as she had turned hers, that the severance was clean. And here was a Hollow Court functionary, a man who smelled of ink and horse sweat and old cruelty, telling her that all along, every night she had stood at her window watching lightning she would not answer, the sky had been watching back. Waiting. Keeping her name the way the sea keeps drowned things—intact, patient, ready to give them up.

Her hands were shaking. She looked down at them and watched the shaking stop, deliberately, the way she had learned to stop it at nineteen, kneeling on marble.

"Last chance," the rider said.

Bram lifted her face to the west.

It was all still there. Of course it was. The pressure that had been building for three days, the warm wet air dragging up from the lowlands against the cold spine of the range, the whole enormous engine of a spring storm idling just past the western peaks, held back by nothing but the ordinary indifference of the world. She didn't need to summon anything. She only needed to stop refusing. She only needed to open the door she had nailed shut, and everything on the other side came through.

*I will not call,* she had sworn, blood on marble. *I will not reach.*

*Forgive me,* she said—to the vow, to the girl behind her, to the nineteen-year-old she had been, to all of it—and reached.

The world opened.

There was no other word for it, though she had spent her whole life searching for one. It was not like shouting and it was not like prayer. It was like being a river that had forgotten it was water and then remembering, all at once, everywhere, the banks bursting. The pressure in the air became legible, became language, became *hers*: every gradient, every current, every trembling column of atmosphere from the valley floor to the roof of the sky, and threaded through it all, faint and unmistakable, something that answered her touch. Something that knew her. Not the Order's records, not the Court's spies, not the pale-eyed man with his finder's fee—but the wind itself, the old vast moving body of the sky, and it recognized her the way the sea recognizes a drowned sailor, and it *grieved*, and its grief was joy, and it came.

The western peaks went white.

Not with snow—with cloud, boiling up over the ridgeline in seconds, devouring the daylight, and the temperature fell so fast the frost on the yard crackled and the horses screamed. Lightning walked inside the cloud-bank like something pacing a cage, and thunder rolled down the valley ahead of the rain, a long continuous roar like the mountain clearing its throat after ten years of silence.

The riders' horses broke first. Animals know; animals always know. They wheeled and plunged and the crescent dissolved into chaos, six men suddenly occupied with staying mounted while the world turned upside down above them. The leader fought his horse around, shouting orders that the wind took and shredded, and Bram stood in the center of her own yard with her arms at her sides and the gale streaming her hair out behind her like a banner, and she laughed.

God forgive her, she laughed.

It felt like the first deep breath after drowning. It felt like speech after ten years mute. Power moved through her and she did not clutch it and she did not hoard it, she simply *shaped* it, gently, precisely, the way a mason shapes stone—here, a wall of wind along the switchbacks to turn the horses; there, a hammer-blow of thunder directly overhead to break their nerve; and the rain came then, sudden and vertical and warm, the first true rain of the storm, and she sent it in sheets, blinding, driving, until the six riders were six blind men flailing in a white roar on a mountain trail with their quarry standing calm in the eye of it all.

"GO," she said, and she did not shout it, she *put* it into the wind, and the wind carried her voice to each of them personally, pressed it against their ears like a lover's whisper: "Tell your masters the singing child is dead. Tell them the mountains ate her. And tell them—" and here her voice cracked, or the thunder covered the crack, she would never know which— "tell them the storm-caller lives."

Lightning struck the scree fifty yards from the lead rider, close enough to fill the world white and leave them all blinking afterimages, and that was the end of it. Horses bolted down-valley with their riders clinging or falling, and the storm chased them, harried them, roared at their backs all the way down the switchbacks until the trees swallowed them, and then Bram stood alone in her yard in the pouring rain and let go.

The letting-go was almost worse than the taking-hold. The power drained out of her like heat from iron and left her hollow and ringing, knees buckling, and she went down to one knee in the mud of her own yard with the rain gentling around her into ordinary spring weather, soft and gray and forgiving, and she knelt there gasping like a landed fish.

Ten years. Ten years of silence, and the sky knew her name again, and so did six riders of the Hollow Court, and by tomorrow the whole machinery of two powers would know it—the Court because she had defied them, and the Vigilant Order because storm-signs like the ones she had just written across the heavens were visible for a hundred miles, and somewhere in a citadel of gray stone, watchers whose whole lives were reading such signs would look up from their instruments and say to one another, in voices gone quiet with certainty:

*She's alive. Kavanagh is alive. Find her.*

She had traded invisibility for a child's safety, and she could not even regret it, and that frightened her more than the riders had.

* * *

Wren was in the doorway when she finally got her legs under her. The girl had not stayed hidden after all—of course she hadn't—and she stood framed in the light with ash still on her fingers and her eyes enormous, staring at Bram, at the dripping yard, at the wrecked and retreating sky.

"You called it," Wren whispered. In the drowned tongue, then, haltingly, in common: "You called it and it *came*. Like it was waiting. Like it was—" she searched, hands fluttering— "*lonely.*"

Bram laughed, or sobbed, or something between. "Come here, little bird."

Wren crossed the yard at a run and hit her like a wave, arms around her neck, small cold face buried in her shoulder, and Bram held her in the rain and felt the enormity of what she had done settle over her like the weather itself. There was no undoing it. There was no going back to the gray invisible life, the smuggler's compass, the woman of no name. By week's end there would be hunters on this mountain—Court hunters with warrants, Vigilant marshals with pardons and chains, and she did not know yet which frightened her more.

"They'll come back," Wren said into her shoulder. She said it without drama, the way children state terrible things, and Bram's blood went cold. "More of them. Bigger ones. The hollow ones never stop. But—" the girl pulled back, and her face in the rain-light was grave and old and certain— "someone else comes too. Before them. A woman. Iron and paper. She smells of the Order but her heart is not theirs. She comes in four days."

Bram stared at her. "How do you—"

"The song hears everything now," Wren said simply. "You woke it up. When you called the storm, you woke *everything* up." She took Bram's muddy hand in both of hers, and her grip was surprisingly strong. "Four days, Bram. What do we do in four days?"

Above them, far to the east, past the drowned kingdom sleeping under its salt, the sky was already beginning to clear—as if the whole vast weather had said what it came to say and was satisfied. Bram looked at the child, and at the mountains that had been her hiding place, and understood that hiding places, like vows, could burn.

"We get ready," she said.
