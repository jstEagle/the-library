## 10. Wren Runs

The fire had burned down to a red eye in the hearth when Ilsa Corvane said the word that ended everything.

*Exile.*

She said it gently, the way a surgeon says *blade*, and she laid her offer out on my table like cards: help us reach the Hollow King, and the Vigilant Order strikes your name from its ledgers. No more bounty. No more hunters on the low road. A life with doors that lock from the inside and open without fear.

I should have been listening for the trap in it. Instead I was listening to the stair.

There had been no creak. Wren had learned months ago to move through my waystation like smoke through a keyhole—bare feet finding the one silent board among the singing ones, small body flattening into shadows I'd taught her myself, back when the lessons were games and the shadows were only for hiding from bad weather. But I heard the door latch lift all the same, soft as a moth against glass, and by the time I turned from Corvane's marshaled face, the cold was already pouring down the stairs like something liquid.

"Wren," I said.

Nothing answered but wind.

I was up and moving before Corvane finished standing. The door stood open two fingers' width, snow sifting across the threshold in little gray tongues, and beyond it the night was a wall of white so complete it seemed the world had been erased rather than darkened. Her tracks were already filling. Three steps past the woodpile they were shallow dents; five steps past that, nothing at all.

"She heard us." Corvane's voice behind me, level as ever, but I caught the thread of alarm pulled taut beneath it. "Kavanagh—the temperature's dropping. That's not natural weather. Something's coming down off the high ice, and if the child is out in it—"

"Then we find her before it arrives." I was pulling my storm-coat off its peg, the oilskin stiff with ten years of disuse, smelling of cedar and old lightning. "You'll stay here. You've done enough."

"I can track."

"You can follow tracks. In this?" I jerked my chin at the blankness beyond the door. "You'll walk in circles until the mountain eats you. Stay. Keep the fire hot. If we're not back by dawn—" I didn't finish it. Some sentences you leave unfinished because finishing them gives them weight.

Corvane held my eyes for a long moment, and whatever she saw there made her step back from the threshold. "Bram. Bring her home."

Home. She'd said it like it was already true, like the child was mine to bring anywhere, and the word went through me sideways, under the ribs, where fear keeps its knife.

Then I stepped into the white.

* * *

A whiteout is not darkness. Darkness has edges; darkness lets you know where you are by telling you where you aren't. A whiteout is worse—it is the world refusing to confirm itself. Snow falling upward and sideways and every direction at once, sky and ground dissolved into the same blind pearl, until your own boots are rumors and your own breath is the only landmark left. Old guides die in whiteouts not because they lose the mountain but because they lose themselves, walking in great confident circles, certain of their bearing right up until the crevasse opens under their certainty.

I had survived three of them before I was twenty. I knew the tricks: rope yourself to something fixed, count your steps, trust the compass in your skull over the compass in your hand because the iron in these mountains lies. But none of the old tricks mattered tonight, because I wasn't navigating. I was hunting. And my quarry had left me a trail no snow could bury.

Because Wren sang as she ran.

At first I thought I imagined it—the wind here does things to sound, folds it, throws it, makes voices out of nothing. But then it came again, thin and high and threaded through the gale like gold wire through gray cloth: the drowned tongue, rising and falling in that cadence I had spent a winter learning, syllables that tasted of brine and deep water and a city whose name the sea had taken back. She was singing the way frightened children sing—to fill the dark, to call for help in the only language that had ever answered her.

And the storm answered.

Every time her voice rose, the wind rose with it. Every time it fell, the snow thickened, as though the mountain itself were leaning in to listen, hungry for words it hadn't heard since Aelmarrow drowned. I felt it in my teeth, in the old scar along my forearm where lightning had written its signature years ago: the pressure building, the air going strange and metallic, the storm-caller's sense waking in me like an old dog lifting its head. This wasn't natural weather. Corvane had been right about that. Something vast and cold was sliding down off the high ice fields, drawn perhaps by the same song I followed, and Wren—a child of eight, alone, half-blind with terror—was running straight up into its arms.

"Wren!" My voice tore away the instant it left my mouth. I sang instead—or shouted in rhythm, anyway, hammering her name against the wind in the cadence of her own tongue, the few words I knew shaped as best I could: *come back, come back, come back.* It was butchered, flat-tongued, a smuggler's accent on a dead kingdom's grammar. But between one gust and the next, the singing stopped.

And started again. Closer. Angrier.

She didn't want to be found. Of course she didn't. She'd stood on the stairs and heard a stranger in fine gray wool tell the only person who'd ever kept her that she was a thing to be bargained with—that her safety was currency, that the woman who fed her and named her and sat up nights when the nightmares took her was trading her away to reach a king. I would have run too. I had run, once, at nineteen, with a brand on my arm and the whole Vigilant Order at my back, and I remembered exactly what that running felt like: not escape but amputation, tearing free of everything warm because warmth had become the most dangerous thing in the world.

So I stopped shouting her name and did the harder thing. I went quiet, and I listened like a hunter, and I let her song lead me.

It led me up. Of course it did. Not along the valley floor where any rational fugitive would go, following the river down toward the lowlands and other hiding places—but up, toward the shoulder of Thornfell itself, toward the glacier fields where even in summer the ice never fully thaws. Children don't flee rationally. Children flee toward the shapes of their fears, trying to outrun them by climbing above them, and Wren had spent a winter hearing me talk about the high passes the way sailors talk about sea-serpents: with respect, with distance, with the unspoken agreement that some places belong to powers older than people.

By the time I realized where she was headed, the storm had closed around us both like a fist, and the temperature had fallen so far that my breath froze in my beard and the wet wool of my sleeves crackled when I moved. The singing had gone faint—not because she was far, but because she was tiring. I could hear it in the way the phrases shortened, the breath between them lengthening. An eight-year-old body can only run from terror for so long before terror starts running on its behalf, and then the body follows wherever terror leads, which is usually somewhere fatal.

I roped myself to the last stunted pine I passed—a blackened thing bent horizontal by a century of wind—and paid out line as I climbed, driving my axe into ice at every fifth step. The glacier field began without announcement. One moment stone underfoot; the next, that particular hollow resonance, the mountain's voice changing key, telling anyone fluent in it that the ground ahead was water pretending to be land, frozen fast but dreaming of falling.

Somewhere ahead, the singing stopped altogether.

I stood in the roaring white and felt the silence where her voice had been, and I have known fear in many forms—I have faced tribunals and storms and men with knives—but nothing in my life has ever matched that silence. The world went narrow. There was no storm anymore, no cold, no mountain. There was only the absence of a child's voice and the screaming question underneath it: *how long since you last heard her? Ten breaths? Twenty? How far can a tired girl wander in twenty breaths?*

"WREN!"

This time the wind carried it. Carried it and dropped it into a hole.

I heard it happen—heard my own shout fall away from me downward, taking on that hollow, swallowed quality that sound gets in deep vertical spaces, and my stomach dropped with it. Twenty feet ahead, maybe thirty, the snow bridge over a crevasse had given. Whether it had given under her or merely near her I couldn't know, couldn't see, could do nothing but move, axe in both hands, probing every step, the rope snapping taut behind me, the storm howling its indifference.

My boot broke through first.

Not fully—the bridge was thick, layered, a season's worth of snowpack—but the edge of it, enough that I dropped to my belly in an instant, old training overriding panic, distributing my weight across the surface like a man spreading himself over thin ice on a lake. I crawled. I crawled through the blinding white with my heart slamming against the glacier beneath me, until my probing hand found the broken lip of the hole and the cold air rising out of it, air that smelled of blue depth and ancient pressure.

"Wren." Quiet now. The storm stole loudness but gave back closeness, carrying a whisper further than a scream. "Wren, I'm here. I'm right here."

Silence. Then, from below, so faint I felt it more than heard it: a sound that was not crying and not speech but the terrible space between them, the sound a child makes when she has cried herself empty and there is nothing left but breathing.

I got my lantern lit with hands that shook—the flint sparking four times before it caught—and lowered it over the lip on a cord.

Blue light. Blue depth. And there, twelve feet down, wedged on a shelf of ice no wider than a coffin lid, mud-streaked and wild-eyed, her small hands bleeding where she'd clawed the walls: Wren. Alive. Looking up at me with an expression I will see until I die—not relief, not joy, but a fierce, defiant, exhausted anger, the anger of someone who has decided not to hope and is furious to find hope still possible.

"You heard," she said, in her own tongue, and then, in the common speech she'd been learning all winter, each word placed like a stone across a stream: "You *sell* me."

"No." The word came out of me before thought, before strategy, from somewhere older than either. "No, Wren. Never. Look at me. Look at my face. *Never.*"

She looked. The lantern swung between us, throwing our shadows huge and wavering up the blue walls of the ice, and whatever she found in my face must have been true, because the defiance cracked, and the crying came back, and this time it was the ordinary crying of a frightened child, which is somehow the worst sound in the world and the best, because it means the person making it still believes someone might come.

"Hold still," I said, and went to work.

Rope, anchored twice over to the pine below and backed with my axe driven deep into the glacier's shoulder. Harness made of the spare line, tied in the quick-release knots smugglers use because sometimes cargo has to be abandoned mid-river, except that this cargo was not going to be abandoned, not by me, not ever, and my hands knew the knots while my mind screamed at them to hurry. I talked the whole time—nonsense, mostly, reassurance in two languages, telling her about the spring coming, about the goats I'd buy her, about the proper way to gut a fish, anything, everything, a rope of words to hold her alongside the rope of hemp.

The descent took four minutes. It took four years. When my boots finally touched the ice shelf beside her, she hit me like a wave hits a seawall—all at once, everywhere, arms around my neck, face buried against my throat, shaking so hard her teeth chattered against my collarbone. I wrapped her up, all of her, and pressed my mouth to the top of her head, and said the thing I had been circling for a year without saying, the thing I'd told myself I couldn't afford, the thing exile had taught me was the one luxury a hunted woman must never purchase:

"I love you. Do you hear me? You're mine. Not the Order's, not the Court's, not the storm's. Mine. And I don't sell what's mine."

She pulled back just far enough to look at me, tear-streaked and furious and eight years old, and asked the question that undid me completely:

"Then why the gray lady? Why talk about *bargain*?"

"Because grown women are fools," I said, "and sometimes they forget what matters until it runs up a mountain in the dark to teach them." I wiped her face with my thumb, leaving a clean streak through the grime. "We're going home. Both of us. And tomorrow, you and I are going to decide together what happens next. No more secrets decided over your head. Sworn."

She studied me with those eyes the color of deep water, weighing it the way she weighed everything, and then nodded once, a small hard nod, a smuggler's nod, a deal struck.

Getting her out was its own battle—hauling her up the shaft hand over hand while she climbed the walls with her bleeding fingers, the storm shrieking above us like something cheated of its prey. But we came up. We came up into the white and the roar, and I got her strapped to my chest inside my coat, her face tucked under my chin, and I turned downhill and followed my own rope home through the blindness, counting steps, trusting the line, letting the mountain rage around us while we moved through it like a single animal, four legs, one heartbeat.

Halfway down, I felt it pass overhead—the thing Corvane had warned of, the pressure rolling down off the high ice, vast and slow and searching. It paused above us. For three breaths the storm went utterly still, the snow hanging suspended around us like a held breath, and I felt something look down through the whiteness at the small burning thing strapped to my chest.

Wren stirred against me and murmured, half-asleep, in the drowned tongue: *"It knows my name now."*

Then the pressure lifted, slid away eastward toward the lowlands, and the storm resumed as if nothing had happened.

When we finally stumbled through my door, blue-lipped and crusted white, Corvane rose from beside the hearth, and I saw her take in the rope burns on my palms, the blood on Wren's fingers, the way the child was fused to me like a barnacle to a hull. The marshal opened her mouth—to apologize, to explain, to negotiate—and I cut her off with a look.

"She stays with me," I said. "Whatever your bargain is, it includes her safety, her consent, and my presence at every step. Or there is no bargain, and you can explain to your Grandmaster why the Order lost both the storm-caller and the vessel in one night."

Corvane bowed her head slowly. "Agreed." Then her eyes went to the window, to the east, where the clouds had begun to glow with a light that had nothing to do with dawn—red as coals, pulsing like a heartbeat under the skin of the sky.

"But Kavanagh," she said quietly, "you should know that whatever came down off the glacier tonight... it isn't going back up. It's heading for Vhalcrest. And it's moving faster than we are."

Outside, far to the east, thunder rolled across the lowlands—though no storm had been called, and no lightning followed.

Only the light, pulsing. Waiting.
