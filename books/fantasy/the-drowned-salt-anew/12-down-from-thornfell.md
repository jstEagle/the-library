## 12. Down from Thornfell

They left before dawn, because Bram had learned long ago that grief and pursuit both travel faster by daylight.

She stood a moment on her own doorstep with the door open behind her, looking back at the waystation she had built plank by plank over ten years of silence. The hearth was banked. The kettle hung cold. On the table inside lay two cups and a bowl, unwashed, because Wren had wanted to leave them exactly as they'd been at breakfast — *so the house remembers us*, the child had signed, hands quick as birds, *and knows to wait.*

Bram had no argument for that. Houses remembered. She had spent a decade teaching hers to forget her.

"Close it," Corvane said quietly behind her. "A door left open reads as haste. Haste reads as guilt."

"I'm not guilty."

"No." The marshal adjusted the strap of her pack, and the grey light caught the Order's sigil at her throat — the watchful eye, half-closed now, its silver worn dull from years under a collar. "You're something worse. You're certain."

Bram shut the door anyway.

Wren waited at the treeline, wrapped in a coat cut down from one of Bram's old ones, sleeves rolled three times at each wrist. She looked small against the black pines, small and utterly unafraid, and when Bram joined her the girl reached up without looking and took her hand — a habit now, four days old, already older than memory. Her palm was warm. Too warm, sometimes, like a stone left too near a fire, and Bram tried not to think about what that warmth was made of, or who else could feel it from how far away.

*Down?* Wren signed with her free hand.

"Down," Bram said.

The word tasted like iron filings. Down meant the lowlands. Down meant the drowned fields and the villages finding seawater in their wells, forty miles from any coast. Down meant Vhalcrest eventually, if Corvane's bargain held, and the capital had teeth that had never fully let go of Bram Kavanagh's name.

But staying meant the riders coming back, and there would be more of them next time, and next time they would not circle and probe. They would come the way winter comes. All at once, and hungry.

So: down.

* * *

The pass out of Thornfell was called the Shepherd's Throat, and it earned the name. It dropped three thousand feet in six miles through country that seemed to have been folded while still wet — switchbacks stitched across scree, cliffs leaning close enough to breathe on you, and always the sound of water somewhere below, unseen, patient. In summer the smugglers ran salt-brandy and untaxed silk down it by mule-train. In autumn it iced over and killed the careless. In late winter, with the thaw just beginning, it did something worse than either.

It sang.

Meltwater worked into every crack in the rock, and the whole mountainside ticked and groaned and wept, and Bram had crossed it perhaps sixty times and still felt her scalp tighten every time the deep stone shifted beneath the melt. Today it sang louder than usual. Today, she suspected, it was singing for an audience.

She stopped the column at the second switchback and held up a fist. Behind her, Corvane stilled instantly — a soldier's obedience, trained so deep it had become manners — and Wren copied her, raising her small fist with grave ceremony, which nearly undid Bram right there on the edge of a cliff.

"What is it?" Corvane murmured.

Bram didn't answer at first. She was listening with more than ears. Ten years of suppression had not dulled the sense; it had refined it, the way a man starved learns to taste faint sweetness in plain bread. The air above the pass carried pressure the way a drumhead carries a hand about to fall. Somewhere south and east, weather was being *made* — not born, not grown the slow honest way, but pushed, shaped, driven along a line toward them like hounds given a scent.

Storm-callers could feel other storm-callers the way wolves feel wolves.

"They're working the sky," she said at last. "Two of them, maybe three. Pushing cloud off the eastern range, trying to build something ahead of us. Rain, probably. Rain swells the rivers, swells the melt, closes the lower ford." She glanced at Corvane. "Your Court hunts with weather now."

"The Court hunts with everything," Corvane said. "That's rather the point of a court." But her jaw had gone tight, and Bram understood why. If the Hollow King's riders had storm-callers attached, then this wasn't a patrol anymore. This was a hunt with resources. Someone very high up had decided a mud-stained child in the Thornfells mattered enough to move the sky for.

*Can you push back?* Wren signed.

The question landed in Bram's chest like a dropped coal. *Can I.* As if it were nothing. As if breaking a vow she'd kept for ten years — the vow that had bought her this quiet, this invisibility, this life — were a thing done lightly, twice in a week.

"Not here," she said aloud, and crouched so the girl could see her face. "Not in the Throat. You call weather in a place like this, you don't get rain, little bird. You get the whole mountainside deciding to come down and see what all the noise is." She tapped the rock beneath their boots. "Everything up here is waiting for an excuse."

Wren considered this with the terrible seriousness of eight years old, then nodded once, and pointed down the trail. *Then walk fast.*

Bram laughed — actually laughed, a rough sound she barely recognized as her own — and heard Corvane make a small startled noise behind her, as if the marshal had just watched a locked door swing open in a house she'd thought empty.

They walked fast.

* * *

They came down out of the Throat at dusk into a world gone wrong.

Bram had known it would be bad. She had told herself stories on the way down — spring floods, early thaw, nothing stranger — and had almost believed them. But the valley floor at the mountain's foot was a lake where no lake should be. The River Senn, which in her smuggling years had run knee-deep and friendly through this stretch, had climbed its banks and kept climbing, drowning the alder flats, drowning the shepherd cairns, lapping gray and silent at the doors of the lowest crofts. Water stood in the pasture grass in sheets, and the water was the wrong color — not snowmelt's clean green-gray but a bruised blue, and when the wind moved it, it smelled of salt.

Forty miles from the sea. Fifty. And the fields smelled of a harbor at low tide.

Wren stopped walking.

Bram felt it happen — felt the small warm hand go rigid in hers, felt the child's breath change, the way a sleeper's changes just before a nightmare takes hold. Wren stared out across the flooded flats, and her lips parted, and what came out of her was not a scream. It was worse. It was a single syllable of the drowned tongue, soft as a mother saying a name over a cradle, and the water answered.

Across the whole breadth of the flood, the surface shivered. Not wind-shiver. Something beneath, turning over in its sleep.

"Don't," Bram said, and knelt, and took the girl's face in both hands, gently, the way you handle anything that might be holding more than it can carry. "Wren. Look at me. Not the water. Me."

Wide dark eyes found hers. In them Bram saw something she had been refusing to see since the night at the river: fear. Not of the water. *For* it. The child looked at the drowned fields the way you look at a room full of sleeping people when you know your voice can wake them and you cannot stop speaking in your sleep.

*I'm sorry,* Wren signed, hands trembling. *I didn't call them. I don't call them. They just—* Her hands faltered, failed, fell. She pressed her palms flat over her own chest instead, over the sternum, over whatever burned there, and her meaning needed no language at all.

"I know," Bram said. "I know, little bird. It's not yours. None of it is yours." She said it firmly, the way you say things you have decided to make true. "And we're going to fix it. That's what the road's for. That's what all of this is for." She glanced up at Corvane, and made herself say the rest, because the marshal deserved to hear it plainly: "Every mile we take her toward Vhalcrest, the dead get louder. You understand that. We're carrying a bell through a graveyard."

Corvane stood at the edge of the flood, and the last light caught her face, and Bram saw her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic the Order had been doing for three hundred years, lives weighed in the balance, cold and necessary — and then she saw her set it down.

"Then we walk quietly," Corvane said.

* * *

They found shelter in a drover's bothy above the floodline, a dry-stone hut with a turf roof and a hearth that smoked until Bram reamed the flue. There was no talk of fires beyond a handful of peat; smoke drew eyes, and eyes were the one thing they could not afford. They ate hard cheese and oatcake and a strip of dried venison each, and Wren fell asleep between one bite and the next, mid-chew, the way children do, as though sleep were simply another country she wandered into without packing.

Bram sat with the girl's head in her lap and watched the door.

"You should sleep," Corvane said. She had taken the window, such as it was — a slot in the stone — and kept her vigil there with the patience of her order. "I'll wake you at moonset."

"I don't sleep well around salt water."

"That's superstition."

"It's experience." Bram smoothed Wren's hair back from her forehead. In the dim glow of the peat the girl's skin had a faint luminescence, or Bram imagined it did — imagined heat shimmering off her like off a forge-stone. "Ask me what I was doing the night the Vigilant Order came for my mother. Go on. You've read the file. Everyone in your citadel has read the file."

Corvane was quiet a moment. "You were on the coast road. Fourteen years old. You'd called a squall to cover your father's run past the customs cutters, and the squall didn't stop when you told it to."

"It didn't stop," Bram agreed, "because I couldn't tell it to. Because I'd never learned how, because the Order doesn't teach control, it teaches *fear*, and fear is a fine whip and a rotten bridle. The squall became a gale. The gale took three ships. One of them was my father's." She heard her own voice go flat and distant and let it. "And the sea that night smelled like this valley smells. Salt where no salt should be. I thought I'd imagined it. I spent ten years thinking I'd imagined it." She looked up. "What was in the water that night, Marshal?"

Corvane didn't answer immediately, and Bram marked that — marked the pause, the careful stillness of a woman deciding how much truth a traitor could be trusted with.

"We think," Corvane said finally, "that the Hunger Below has been leaking for a long time. Through cracks in the binding. Through... conduits." Her eyes went, briefly, involuntarily, to the sleeping child. "Strong blood calls to it. Storm-blood especially. Your family's blood."

"My family's blood," Bram repeated softly. The ferryman's words from years ago surfaced unbidden — *your people served Aelmarrow once, girl, ask your mother why she flinched at the sea* — and she pushed them back down. Not yet. One inheritance at a time.

Outside, the wind changed.

Bram felt it before she heard it — the pressure-sense again, the drumhead-hand, closer now, much closer, riding down out of the pass they had left that morning. Two callers, maybe three, and with them the particular cold discipline of Court riders moving at forced march. They had lost the trail in the high country. They had not lost it now. The flood had left mud, and mud leaves prints, and Bram had led her small company straight down the only pass there was, because there was only ever one pass there was.

"How long?" Corvane asked. She had read the shift in Bram's face the way soldiers read weather.

"Dawn. Maybe sooner if they push the wind at their backs." Bram eased Wren's head onto her rolled coat and rose, joints cracking, and went to stand beside the marshal at the window-slot. Below, the flood stretched black to the tree-line, and here and there on its surface, where the peat-light couldn't reach and shouldn't reach, pale shapes stood in the shallows. Still. Patient. Facing the bothy.

Facing the child.

"They're not hostile," Corvane murmured. "Report from the lowland wardens says they've harmed no one. They walk at night and stand in fields and at dawn they're gone. Villages are emptying out of terror, not wounds."

"Terror's a wound," Bram said. "It just bleeds slower." She watched the pale shapes and felt something twist in her that was not quite pity and not quite dread. Aelmarrow had been drowned three hundred years ago, erased from maps, its name struck from records by kings who feared what slept beneath it. And now its dead walked inland, drawn by the last vessel of its fire, standing in its fields — no. Standing in *someone else's* fields, someone else's drowned pasture, and Bram understood suddenly, with a lurch, that the dead were not haunting the living.

They were following their queen's heir home. And home, tonight, was a drover's bothy that smelled of peat and cheese.

"Wake her," Bram said. "We're moving now."

"In the dark? Through the flood?"

"The flood's the safest road we have. Riders won't swim it in the dark, and the dead—" Bram paused, testing the thought against everything she knew, and found it held. "The dead won't let anything touch her. Did your wardens report that? That nothing else comes near the standing shapes? No wolves, no riders, no wading men?" She had heard it in a crofter's account two days back and hadn't understood it until now. "They're not haunting her, Marshal. They're guarding her. Three hundred years, and they're still doing their duty."

Corvane stared at her. Then at the pale figures in the black water. Then, longest, at the sleeping child whose fire was guttering down toward nothing while an ancient hunger fed on it — and Bram watched the marshal arrive at the same shore she had arrived at herself, weeks ago, on a snowy doorstep.

"All right," Ilsa Corvane said. "In the dark. Through the flood." She began shrugging her pack straps tight. "But if we drown, I'm filing a complaint with your ghost."

Bram knelt and woke Wren with a hand on her shoulder, gentle, and the girl came up out of sleep already reaching for her — no tears, no confusion, just that instant gravity toward her, as if Bram were north.

*Riders?* Wren signed, blinking.

"Riders," Bram confirmed. "Behind us. So we're going to do something clever."

*What?*

"We're going to ask the dead for an escort."

Wren's face opened like a gate. And far out in the flooded dark, as if the words had traveled through the water the way sound travels through a hull, every pale shape in the shallows turned its head — slowly, together, like a field of flowers finding the sun — and began, without hurry, without splash, to wade toward the door.

Bram Kavanagh picked up her daughter-of-no-blood, shouldered her pack, and stepped down into the salt-smelling dark, toward the river that lay between them and the lowlands — the river where, if her mother's stories held one grain of truth, an old man with a pole and a debt three hundred years overdue was waiting to carry them across.
