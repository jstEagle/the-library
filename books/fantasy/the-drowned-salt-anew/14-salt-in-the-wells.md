## 14. Salt in the Wells

The first village was called Merrow's Bend, and it had no business tasting of the sea.

Bram knew it before they reached it. The wind coming down off the low hills carried a smell that did not belong there—brine and rot and something older underneath, like a cellar opened after a drowning. She had spent ten years learning to read weather the way priests read scripture, and this wind told her nothing about rain. It told her about distance. It told her that somewhere behind them, forty miles east where the coast lay gray and patient, the sea had begun to reach.

Wren rode in front of her on the mule with her knees drawn up and her chin on her arms, and Bram watched the back of the child's head the way she might have watched a fuse.

They had left Thornfell nine days ago, descending through country that grew softer and greener with every mile, and with every mile Wren had grown quieter. Not frightened-quiet—Bram had learned the difference by now, had catalogued the child's silences the way other people catalogued birdsong. This was a listening quiet. Wren sat with her whole body turned slightly inward, as if she were hearing something spoken very far away, or very deep down, and did not yet know whether she wanted to answer.

"Tell me what you hear," Bram said, for perhaps the hundredth time since the pass.

Wren turned half around. Her eyes were the color of shallow water over sand, and in certain lights—the wrong lights—they seemed to hold a flicker of gold at the center, a coal buried under silt. "Old words," she said. Her common speech had grown by leaps since the mountain; she strung sentences together now like a child stringing river-stones, uneven but holding. "Under-words. They are not for me. But they know my name."

"Do they say anything else?"

Wren considered this with the grave seriousness she brought to all questions, as though each one might be a trapdoor. "They say *coming*," she said. "But not angry-coming. Like—" she searched, hands moving, small fingers sketching shapes in the cold air, "—like when you call sheep and they come because your voice is the voice they were born to."

Bram felt the words settle into her chest like swallowed stones. *Your voice is the voice they were born to.* The drowned tongue did that, apparently. It called things home.

Merrow's Bend appeared around a bend in the road exactly as its name promised: a scatter of thatched roofs along a slow brown river, willows trailing their fingers in the current, geese complaining in the yard of the inn. Ordinary. Peaceful. Except that the smell hung over it like a shroud, and except that every villager Bram could see was standing in the street looking east.

Not running. Not shouting. Standing, in little clusters, faces turned toward the horizon as if waiting for a procession.

Corvane reined up beside her. The marshal had traded her Order grays for a courier's leathers on the third day out, and without the insignia she looked less like a blade and more like a tired woman of fifty who had ridden too many miles—which, Bram was beginning to understand, was closer to the truth than either of them liked to admit.

"They've been at the wells since dawn, I'd wager," Corvane said quietly. "Watch."

An old man came out of the nearest cottage carrying a bucket. He set it down in the middle of the road, knelt beside it, and simply looked into it for a long moment. Then he began to weep—not loudly, not dramatically, but with the exhausted, bewildered grief of a man whose world had stopped making sense overnight. A woman took the bucket from him, sniffed, recoiled, and poured it out onto the road. Even from thirty yards, Bram saw the water catch the light strangely. It foamed. It left a white crust on the packed earth as it drained, the way tide-pools do.

Salt water. In an inland well, forty miles from any coast, fed by hill springs that had run sweet since before anyone now living was born.

"The whole valley?" Bram asked.

"Every village on this road, if the pattern holds." Corvane's jaw was tight. "It started three days ago, according to the last courier report I read before we cut off the post roads. Wells turning brackish westward, mile by mile, following—" she glanced, deliberately casual, at the small figure on the mule, and did not finish the sentence.

*Following us,* Bram finished silently. Following Wren. The sea reaching inland like a blind thing feeling its way along a wall, hand over hand, toward whatever it had lost.

She urged the mule forward into the village, and the villagers' attention swung to them with the slow, collective weight of a flock changing direction. Their eyes went first to Corvane's swords, then to Bram's weathered face, then—and here was the thing that made the skin crawl up Bram's spine—to Wren. They looked at the child the way people look at a funeral bier passing: with pity, and fear, and the superstitious need to touch something of it. One woman actually stepped forward and made a sign against ill luck, an old gesture, thumb folded against palm, and then looked ashamed of herself.

"We need feed for the animals and bread if you have it," Bram said to the innkeeper, a broad woman with flour to her elbows. "We'll pay fair."

The innkeeper's gaze kept sliding past her to Wren. "You're taking it through," she said. Not a question. "Whatever it is. You're taking it on west."

"I am," Bram said.

"Then take it quick." The woman's voice dropped. "My husband's mother came from Saltmere, down on the coast, before the tides took the old shore-road. She used to say the sea never forgets what it's owed." She wiped her hands on her apron, leaving pale streaks. "Three nights now there's been folk standing in the barley field across the river. Standing all night, still as posts, facing east. My man went out with a lantern to tell them to move along, and—" she faltered, and Bram watched the memory move through her face like a fish under ice. "There weren't any tracks in the mud. Not one. And when the lantern-light caught them, they turned. All together. All at once. Like a shoal."

"And then?"

"Then they bowed." The innkeeper laughed, a short broken sound with no humor anywhere in it. "Bowed to the east, all of them, and walked back into the dark, and come morning there was salt on the barley. White as frost. It'll kill the crop if it keeps on."

Behind Bram, Wren had gone very still.

* * *

They took a room at the inn because the light was failing and the horses needed rest, though Bram would have preferred to push on into the dark. There was a part of her—a large and growing part—that wanted to put miles between this valley and the child sleeping in the bed beside hers, and a larger part still that was ashamed of wanting it.

Wren slept badly in lowland rooms. Bram had noticed it since the descent began; in the thin air of Thornfell the girl had slept like a stone, curled tight and breathing slow, but down here among the soft green hills she tossed and murmured, her lips shaping syllables of the drowned tongue, her fingers twitching against the blanket as if playing an instrument only she could feel. Tonight was worse. Tonight the murmuring had a rhythm to it, almost musical, and the window-glass—cheap glass, wavy with flaws—had begun to fog from the outside in, though the night was cold and dry.

Bram sat by the window with her sword across her knees and watched the moon rise over the eastern hills, and tried not to think about the word *vessel*, which Corvane had used once, early on, and never repeated.

Near midnight, Wren rose.

She didn't wake so much as empty—her eyes opened and there was nothing behind them, no Wren, just the shallow-water color and the buried coal-glimmer, and she stood and walked to the door with the smooth unhurried gait of sleepwalkers everywhere. Bram was moving before the second step. She caught the child's shoulders gently and felt the strangeness of her: warm, warmer than she should be, skin nearly fever-hot, and beneath her palms a faint vibration like a struck bell fading.

"Wren. Little bird. Stay with me."

The girl's head turned. For one terrible moment Bram looked into her eyes and saw the gold flare bright, bright as forge-light, and heard—not with her ears, but in the marrow of her bones, in the storm-blood her mother had passed down to her like a curse—a single word in a language dead three hundred years.

Then Wren blinked, and shivered, and was a frightened eight-year-old again, pressing her face into Bram's chest. "I was walking," she whispered. "I was walking in the water but I wasn't wet. There were so many of them, Bram. So many, and they were *happy*. They said the fire was coming back. They said—" her breath hitched, "—they said I'm late."

Bram held her until the shaking stopped, and did not sleep again.

* * *

In the morning the innkeeper met them in the yard, and her face told the story before her mouth did.

"It's moved," she said. "The standing folk. Last night they came over the river. Stood in the churchyard, all night, right up against the wall, and Father Aldous read the burial office at them through the window because he hadn't the nerve to go out, and no blame to him." She pressed a cloth-wrapped bundle of bread into Bram's hands and refused the coin. "They left before dawn. Westward. All of them, walking the road, in a line, like pilgrims." Her eyes went to Wren, who stood clutching Bram's cloak and staring at the ground. "Like they're following something. Or going to meet it."

"How many?" Corvane asked.

The woman's lips moved as she counted, failed, and gave up. "More than the parish has dead, Marshal. That I'll swear. More than the parish has ever buried." She hesitated, then added, lower: "Some of them were wearing old clothes. Old-old. My grandmother's grandmother's kind of clothes, the kind in the church windows. And one of them—" she crossed herself with the wrong hand, an old coastal gesture, pre-Vigilant, "—one of them had coral growing out of his cheek. White as bone. And he smiled at the window like he knew Father Aldous was afraid, and he wasn't cruel about it. He just smiled. Like a man glad the wait's near done."

They rode out of Merrow's Bend with the sun barely clear of the hills, and the whole village stood in the street to watch them go, and not one of them waved.

* * *

For two days the road west ran through country that grew stranger by the mile.

The wells were the least of it. Every stream they crossed ran faintly brackish; Bram tasted it once and spat, and after that made them fill skins from high springs only. Gulls appeared—gulls, forty-five miles inland, wheeling over plowed fields and crying down at furrows of black earth as though the soil were surf. On the second evening they passed a farmstead where the family stood in their own doorway, not watching the road but the ground, where a fine lacework of white crystals traced itself between the rows of winter wheat, spreading visibly in the dying light, glittering like a net being drawn.

And always, at dusk, the dead.

They saw the first of them properly on the third night, because until then Bram had let herself believe the innkeeper's tale was embellished—fear does that to stories, stretches them like taffy. But there was no mistaking what stood in the next field as the light went down: dozens of figures, motionless, spaced across the stubble with a strange evenness, like trees planted by a careful hand. The dusk made silhouettes of them, and the silhouettes were wrong in small ways—stooped where the living stoop differently, swaying faintly where nothing should sway, one figure missing an arm at the shoulder and not seeming to mind.

Wren stood at the fence rail and looked at them, and Bram stood behind Wren and put her hands on the child's shoulders, and felt the tremor running through the small body—not fear. Recognition.

"They're not ghosts," Wren said softly. It was the most fluent thing she'd ever said in common speech, and it landed in Bram's ear with terrible clarity. "Ghosts stay where they died. These walked. Out of the water, up the rivers, all this way. Do you know how long that takes, when you walk underwater? How heavy the miles are?" She shook her head slowly. "They don't mind. They've waited three hundred years. What's a few more days?"

"Wren." Bram crouched to bring her face level with the child's. "What are they waiting for?"

The gold flared in the girl's eyes, and when she spoke, the voice that came out was layered—Wren's voice with something vast underneath it, the way a shell holds the sound of the sea. "The binding is old and it is failing, storm-daughter. The Hunger gnaws at the last of the fire, and when the fire goes out, it will be free, and it will wear whoever stands nearest, and it will not stop wearing until everything wears it." The layered voice faded; Wren blinked, swayed, and Bram caught her. The child's own small voice returned, thin and frightened: "I didn't mean to say that. It says things through me when they get close. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, Bram, I'm trying to keep it shut—"

"Hush. Hush, little bird. You're not in trouble." Bram gathered her up, and the child weighed too much and too little at once, all sharp elbows and burning skin. Over Wren's head, Bram looked at the field of waiting dead, and they were all turned toward her now—every silhouette, every ruined face, oriented on the fence rail, on the child in her arms, with an attention that had nothing of menace in it and was somehow more frightening for that. They looked like pilgrims who had found their shrine. They looked like soldiers who had found their banner.

One of them, nearer than the others, raised its head. Coral crusted its cheek in white branches. Its mouth moved, and though no sound crossed the field, Bram read the shape of it easily enough, because she had seen the same word written in salt on a citadel floor, and heard it in dreams since.

*Come.*

* * *

They did not stop that night. Bram pushed them through the dark along the westering road, Corvane riding rear guard with her hand never far from her sword, the mule's hooves striking sparks of frost from the stones. Behind them, Bram did not look back, but she could feel it—the way she could feel weather coming, the way she could feel a storm gathering past the horizon. A pressure. A tide. Three hundred years of drowned patience flowing inland along every watercourse, drawn by the small burning thing asleep against her shoulder.

Near dawn they topped a long ridge, and Corvane drew rein beside her and pointed east with her chin.

Far below and behind, across the dark countryside, lights were moving. Not torches—nothing so warm. A pale, scattered glow, hundreds of points of it, drifting up the river valleys like foam drifting on a flood. And threaded through the glow, faint but unmistakable on the wind that reached them even here, came singing. Low, wordless, in a language Bram had never learned and understood anyway, the way she understood thunder.

Wren woke, and lifted her head from Bram's shoulder, and listened.

"That's the mourning-song," she whispered. "For the kingdom. They sing it every year on the last night, before the water comes." A pause. "They haven't sung it since the water won."

Corvane's face was gray in the starlight. "How long," she said, "before they reach Vhalcrest?"

Wren closed her eyes, listening to something Bram could not hear, and when she answered, her voice was very small.

"Sooner than we will. Unless we ride."

Bram looked west, where the road dropped away toward the distant smudge of the lowlands and, beyond it, the towers of the capital that had exiled her. Then east, at the rising tide of the patient dead. Then down at the child in her arms, whose chest glowed faintly through her shirt, a coal wrapped in skin, guttering.

"Then we ride," she said, and put her heels to the mule.
