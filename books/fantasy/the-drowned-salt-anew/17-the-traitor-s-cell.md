## 17. The Traitor's Cell

They came for her in the hour before dawn, when the citadel's bells had gone quiet and the corridors smelled of cold stone and tallow.

Bram heard them first—the particular cadence of boots that were not trying to be quiet. Six sets, heavy, unhurried, the walk of men who carried authority like a drawn blade and wanted everyone to hear it coming. She was out of bed before the knock landed, already dressed in everything she owned worth wearing: boots laced, storm-coat over her shoulders, Wren's carved gull-bone charm closed in her fist.

The child slept curled against the wall of their shared cell-room, if a room with a bolted door could be called anything but what it was. She had insisted on sleeping touching Bram's back, the way she had since the mountains, as though proximity were a rope and letting go meant drowning. Even now, asleep, her fingers had hooked into the hem of Bram's shirt.

The knock came again. Harder.

"Warden Kavanagh." A voice through the door, flat as slate. "You are summoned before the Tribunal of Doctrine. Immediately."

There was no tribunal meeting at this hour. There was no tribunal that would summon a guest of Marshal Corvane's escort in the dark, without Corvane present, without ceremony, without the seven days' notice that even traitors were owed by the Order's own charter. Bram knew all of this the way she knew weather—by the feel of it on her skin, by the pressure behind her ears.

She knelt beside Wren and touched her shoulder. The girl woke the way she always woke: instantly, silently, eyes open and fixed, one hand already reaching for the knife at Bram's belt. Eight years old, and sleep had never once been allowed to make her soft.

"Listen to me," Bram whispered. "Some men are going to come in. They're going to take me somewhere. You stay here. You do not follow. You sing nothing—not a note, not a hum, not under your breath. Can you do that?"

Wren's face did something complicated and terrible. She was learning common speech word by word, hoarding them like a miser, but some things did not need words. Her chin set. Her eyes went bright and furious.

"I come," she said. It was nearly the longest sentence Bram had ever heard from her.

"No." Bram took the child's face in both hands. "Wren. *No.* If they see you, they will take you where I cannot follow. This is how you protect me—by staying. Do you understand? Staying is the brave thing now."

That was a lie, or near enough to one, and Bram hated the taste of it. But the girl's jaw trembled, and after a long moment she nodded once, sharp, and pressed the gull-bone charm into Bram's palm with the fierce gravity of a queen bestowing a kingdom.

Then the door opened, because they had not waited for permission, and six wardens in the grey-and-silver of the hardline cloisters filled the frame. Their leader was a broad man with a face like a slammed door, and his eyes went past Bram immediately—to the small shape rising off the cot—and lingered there with an interest that made Bram's blood go cold and slow.

"The child stays," he said.

"The child stays," Bram agreed, and stepped between him and Wren as she said it, casual as adjusting her coat. "Where are we walking?"

"You'll see."

* * *

They walked her down through the citadel in layers, each one older and colder than the last. Past the warden barracks, where early risers watched from doorways and said nothing. Past the reliquary halls with their glass cases and their guttering votive lamps. Down stairs worn into shallow bowls by three centuries of feet, until the air stopped smelling of incense and started smelling of wet rock and old iron.

She knew these stairs. That was the cruelty of it. She had walked them once before, nineteen years old, wrists bound not with rope but with ribbon—the Order's particular obscenity, binding its own children in silk before it branded them. Ten years of exile, and her feet still knew every turning of the descent as if the mountain itself had memorized her and refused to forget.

At the bottom, the corridor opened onto a row of cells cut directly into the living granite of Thornfell's roots. No windows. Doors of banded iron with viewing slits like half-closed eyes. The Order called this place the Remembrance, because it was where they kept people they intended to remember forever, and Bram thought it was the most honest name in the whole grey fortress.

"In here," said the door-faced man.

"Charming," said Bram, and walked in, because the alternative was making six widows before breakfast, and whatever else she had become, she had not become that. Not yet.

The door boomed shut behind her. The bolt was enormous; she felt its fall through the floor, up through her boot soles, into her teeth.

The cell held a cot, a bucket, a candle stub in a wall niche, and darkness with a texture to it, thick as wet wool. Bram sat on the cot, closed her hand around Wren's charm, and began, methodically, to take inventory of herself the way a smuggler inventories cargo.

Body: intact. They had not searched her well enough—there was a sliver of iron filed to a point sewn into her left cuff, and a pinch of grave-salt in her right pocket, and neither had been found. Skills: storm-calling, suppressed but present, humming in her marrow like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. Allies: one marshal whose loyalty was about to be tested in ways Bram could not control, a handful of smugglers forty miles away who owed her favors, and a child of eight who spoke the language of the drowned.

Enemies: apparently everyone else.

She had known this was coming. That was the bitter joke of it. From the moment Corvane had brought them through the gates—*I can give you your exile back, Kavanagh, I can give you a pardon with a seal on it*—Bram had watched the hardliners watching her. Grandmaster Veylan's people, the ones in the deep-grey cloaks who kissed the relic of Saint Aldric's fingerbone each morning and spoke of purity the way other men spoke of profit. They had let her in because Corvane's rank made refusal costly. They had let her in the way a spider lets a fly in.

And now, in the dark, with the mountain pressing down like a held breath, Bram admitted the thing she had been refusing to say aloud since the gates closed behind them:

She had led Wren into the wolf's mouth and called it salvation.

* * *

Time in the Remembrance moved strangely. Without windows, without bells, the hours lost their edges. Bram measured them instead by her body—by hunger, by the ache in her hip when she lay too long, by the slow crawl of cold up from the stone floor into her bones. She slept in fragments. She talked to the dark in a low voice, telling it stories about the mountains, about the way snow sounded falling on pine, about a girl who had arrived on her doorstep caked in mud and speaking a dead tongue, and how Bram's whole life had cracked open around her like river ice in spring.

Somewhere above her—she was sure of it, sure the way she was sure of storms—Wren was doing the same thing. Staying. Being brave in the way Bram had asked her to be, which was the hardest way, which was the way that looked like doing nothing at all.

On the second day—or what she judged was the second day—the viewing slit slid open.

Light fell across the floor in a long pale bar. In it stood a woman Bram did not know: young, maybe thirty, with the close-cropped hair and ink-stained fingers of a doctrinal clerk, and an expression of careful, professional regret.

"Bram Kavanagh." The name came out like something read from a ledger. "You are accused under articles four, nine, and eleven of the Charter of Vigilance. Unsanctioned use of calling-gifts. Harboring an unregistered anomalous individual. And—" the clerk hesitated, and something human flickered behind the professionalism, "—consorting with entities designated hostile to the succession of Saint Aldric's line. Your trial is set for the third day hence. The Tribunal sits at full quorum."

"Does Corvane know?" Bram asked.

"The Marshal has been... engaged with internal matters," the clerk said, which was an answer, and a bad one. "You will be permitted counsel. Within limits."

"What limits?"

The slit began to slide shut. "The Tribunal's," said the clerk, and the light died.

Bram sat in the dark and turned the words over. *Engaged with internal matters.* Either Corvane was fighting for her—burning her political capital, spending favors, standing in council chambers shouting down men like Veylan—or Corvane had been outmaneuvered, boxed into some procedural corner while the hardliners moved with the smooth efficiency of people who had done this many times before. Or, said a small cold voice that sounded unpleasantly like her own mother, Corvane had decided that one exiled storm-caller was a cheap price for keeping her own position intact.

Bram lay back on the cot and stared at a ceiling she couldn't see.

Ten years ago, she would have raged. She would have hammered the door until her fists split, shouted curses in three languages, called the storm down on the mountain if she could have reached it. Nineteen-year-old Bram Kavanagh had burned hot and loud, and look where loudness had gotten her—a brand on her collarbone and a decade of exile.

This Bram did something harder. This Bram breathed, slowly, and planned.

If trial came, she would stand before the Tribunal and speak. Not plead—speak. About Aelmarrow. About the drowned city Wren drew from memory, street by street, tower by tower, matching ruins Bram had seen with her own eyes forty fathoms down. About the salt in the wells of villages a hundred miles from any coast. About the thing that wore the king like a glove and ate memory the way rot eats timber. Let Veylan's doctrinal clerks explain to a full quorum why the truth was heresy.

And if trial became sentence—if the brand came again, or worse—then the iron in her cuff and the salt in her pocket would have their say, and she would die moving toward Wren rather than sitting still in a hole.

It was not a good plan. It was a smuggler's plan: thin margins, bad odds, everything riding on nerve. But it was hers, and having it steadied her the way a compass steadies a man in fog, even a compass that points mostly at trouble.

She slept, finally, and dreamed of snow on pine, and of a small voice singing somewhere far above her, muffled by stone, patient as tide.

* * *

On the third day, they came to fetch her, and Marshal Ilsa Corvane was with them.

Bram knew it before the slit opened—knew it by the argument leaking through the iron, low and vicious, two voices she didn't recognize and one she did, that voice like a whetstone drawn across steel, controlled and cold and very tired.

"—the Tribunal does not require the Marshal's presence—"

"And the Marshal does not require the Tribunal's permission to witness proceedings concerning an operative under her own writ of passage. Read your charter, Warden. Article two. I'll wait."

The slit opened. Corvane filled it, in full dress greys, silver rank-bars at her throat, her face arranged into the expression Bram privately thought of as the fortress face—all portcullis and no gate. Behind her, the two hardliner wardens wore expressions of men chewing glass.

"Well," Bram said from the cot, not rising. "You found the place. Most people need a guide."

Corvane's eyes flicked over her—counting injuries, reading posture, doing in three seconds what a good officer does. Whatever she saw seemed to settle something in her jaw.

"You look terrible."

"It's the décor. Very austere. I'd leave a poor review."

Something almost like a smile crossed the marshal's face and was executed summarily. She turned to the wardens. "Alone."

"She is a prisoner of the Tribunal—"

"She is a prisoner of a *faction*, Hesk, and we both know it, and if you say the word 'charter' to me again I will spend the next six months making your life a lesson in its finer points. Out."

They went. Boots receding up the stairs, resentful as retreating surf. Then silence, and the pale bar of lamplight, and Corvane stepping inside the cell and pulling the door to behind her—not closing it fully. Leaving herself an exit, or leaving Bram one. With Corvane it was impossible to tell, and Bram suspected the ambiguity was deliberate.

"They moved fast," Corvane said. "Faster than I expected. Veylan convened the Tribunal the same night you were taken—no notice, no counsel of record, witnesses selected from his own cloister. By the time I got wind of it, the quorum was seated and the articles were drafted." She paused. "I've bought you a day. Maybe two. I'm spending everything I have to do it, and Veylan knows it, which means after this there's no version of events where he lets me keep my command."

"So don't keep it." Bram stood. "Walk out. Come with us. You've seen the wells, Corvane. You've seen what's coming down the coast. Your Order is arguing about procedure while the sea walks inland."

"Do not lecture me about my Order." The words came out with an edge that surprised them both. Corvane looked away, and for a moment the fortress face slipped, and underneath it Bram saw something she had not expected to see in Ilsa Corvane: doubt. Real doubt, the kind that eats. "You think I don't know what Veylan is? I've served under him eleven years. I've watched him bury inquiries. I've watched wardens who asked questions about the eastern houses get reassigned to postings so remote they might as well have been dead. I stayed. I told myself I could do more good inside than out, that reform comes from within, that—" She stopped. Her hand had gone, unconsciously, to the relic at her belt, the little silver case every senior officer carried, and Bram watched her realize she was touching it and watch her take the hand away as if the metal had bitten her.

"That's what he counts on," Bram said quietly. "People staying. People telling themselves useful lies. It's how the crown-thing works too, isn't it? It doesn't break its host. It just gives him reasons to stay."

Corvane's head came up sharply. For a heartbeat Bram thought she'd gone too far—that the marshal would call the wardens back and wash her hands of the whole affair. But then Corvane laughed, short and bleak, with no humor in it at all.

"Eleven years," she said, "and a smuggler I've known for a month hands me my own soul on a plate." She straightened. The fortress face returned, but differently now—as a choice rather than a hiding place. "Here is where we stand. The Tribunal sits tomorrow at midbell. Veylan has the votes unless something changes the arithmetic. I have perhaps a third of the officer corps willing to defy him openly, and most of those only if they can see a reason that costs them nothing. The rest are true believers, or frightened, or fed—which, before you ask, is not a figure of speech. I have evidence—fragments, ledger-shreds, testimony from a dead archivist—that souls have been delivered to the palace from inside this citadel. For decades. Under Veylan's seal."

The cell felt colder. Bram wrapped her arms around herself and found Wren's charm in her fist and held it.

"And the Tribunal tomorrow?"

"Is theater. The verdict was written before you were taken. Exile confirmed, the child remanded to doctrinal custody for 'examination.'" Corvane's voice went flat and careful, the voice of a woman handling something explosive. "Which is a word I have learned to translate. Examination, in Veylan's cloisters, means the vaults beneath the reliquary. It means instruments. It means they will try to find out what she carries by taking it apart."

The floor seemed to tilt. Bram heard her own pulse, loud and stupid in her ears. "Then we're done talking," she said. "Get me out. Now. Tonight."

"If I open that door tonight, everything I've built for eleven years burns, and I burn with it, and you and the child run into the mountains with half the Order hunting you and no allies inside the walls when you need them most." Corvane stepped closer, and her voice dropped to something raw and urgent. "One day, Kavanagh. Give me one day. Tomorrow morning, before the Tribunal sits, I move against Veylan in front of the full quorum—I put the archivist's ledgers on the table, I name names, I force the officers to choose with the evidence staring at them. If it works, Veylan falls, the Tribunal dissolves, and your trial dies with it. If it fails—"

"If it fails, I'm in this hole when they come for Wren."

"If it fails," Corvane said, "you'll find I am more useful broken than obedient. But I need the day. I need you to trust me for one day, which I know is asking a woman your history has taught to trust nothing, least of all the Order that branded her." She held Bram's gaze. "So I won't ask. I'll tell you what I told myself the night I decided to bring you through the gates instead of taking your head: I did not spend twenty years climbing this frozen pile of stones to hand it, at the end, to a man who feeds children to a crown."

Silence. Somewhere far above, faint as memory, the citadel's noon bell rang, and the sound came down through the stone and settled over them like ash.

Bram looked at the door. At the marshal standing in it, offering her everything she had on a single throw of the dice. At the darkness behind her, where ten years ago a younger woman had stood in almost exactly this spot and learned what the Order did to the inconvenient.

"One day," Bram said at last. "But hear me, Corvane. If they move on that child before your quorum sits—if I hear one boot on those stairs that isn't yours—I will not wait for rescue, and I will not be polite about it. There's iron in my sleeve and salt in my pocket and a storm in my blood that your whole citadel is built on top of. You want my trust? Earn it fast."

For the first time since the gates, Corvane smiled properly—thin, fierce, and entirely without comfort.

"There she is," the marshal said. "The storm-caller they were afraid of." She turned to go, then paused, one hand on the iron. "Kavanagh. The child. When they took you—did she try to follow?"

"She promised me she wouldn't."

"Mm." Something flickered across Corvane's face, too quick to name. "Then let us hope eight-year-olds keep promises better than marshals do."

She pulled the door shut, and the bolt fell, and Bram was alone with the dark and the counting of hours.

She counted four hundred of them, give or take, pacing the length of her chains of stone, before the sounds began.

First: the bell, ringing wrong—too fast, arrhythmic, the alarm pattern she remembered from her childhood in the Order's orphan-halls. Then boots, many boots, running, and shouts with panic in them instead of discipline. Smoke-smell threading down through the stairwell, acrid and wrong, oil-fed. And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, drifting down through three hundred feet of granite as if the mountain itself were carrying it to her—

a child's voice, singing in the tongue of the drowned.

Bram was at the door with the iron sliver out of her cuff before the first scream finished.
