## 18. Wren Taken

The bells woke her.

Not the citadel's measured hours—those she had learned in three days of captivity to sleep through, the way one learns to sleep beside a river—but the alarm bells, the ones rung in thirds, the ones that meant *breach*. Bram was out of the cell's narrow cot before her mind finished counting the strokes, bare feet on cold stone, hands already reaching for a storm that wasn't there. Ten years of exile had taught her body to reach for weather the way other bodies reached for knives.

The corridor outside was torchlit and wrong. Too quiet between the bell-strokes. A warden lay against the far wall with his helm still on and his eyes open, and there was no wound on him at all—just a fine rime of white across his lips, like frost, like salt.

Bram knelt, touched his throat. Nothing. She touched his lips with two fingers and brought them away wet, and when she rubbed thumb against forefinger it left a gritty shine.

Salt. Inland, in a mountain fortress, four days' hard ride from any coast.

*Wren.*

She ran.

* * *

They had put Wren two floors up, in the warden-children's dormitory, because Corvane had argued—and Bram would remember this later, would turn the argument over in her hands like a coin worn smooth—that no one would look for a child among children. The Order's hardliners had agreed not out of wisdom but out of spite: let the traitor's whelp sleep under wardens' eyes, and let the traitor know it. A kindness shaped exactly like a threat.

Bram took the stairs three at a time. The alarm bells kept their terrible arithmetic. Somewhere below, boots were hammering toward the gates, toward the walls, toward every direction except the one that mattered, because the Court did not come through walls. The Court came through cracks.

The dormitory door stood open.

Inside, six cots, five of them empty—the children evacuated, thank all the drowned gods, or perhaps simply elsewhere—and the sixth overturned. A blanket on the floor. One small boot upright beside it, laces still tied, which meant Wren had not fought long enough even to kick free of it, or had been carried sleeping, or—

No. Bram refused the third thought. She picked up the boot and held it and it weighed nothing and it weighed everything.

Two more wardens in this room. One slumped in the corner by the window, salt-rimed like the man below. The second lay in the center of the floor, and he was different. He had died fighting; his sword was out, its edge nicked, and there were bruises blooming on his throat in the shape of fingers that could not have belonged to anything human, too long, too many joints. His eyes were fixed on the ceiling with an expression Bram had seen once before, years ago, on a smuggler who'd gone overboard in dead-calm water off the Sere Coast: the look of a man who had seen the bottom of the world looking back up at him.

And on the flagstones, in a wide unhurried curve around the fallen warden, someone had written a single word.

COME.

It was written in salt. Coarse grey crystals, scattered by hand or poured from some vessel, each letter taller than Bram's spread hand, precise as scriptoria work. It caught the torchlight from the corridor and glittered, and Bram understood—with the flat, ringing certainty of a struck bell—that whoever had written it had taken their time. Had knelt here in this room of sleeping children and murdered men and arranged their message grain by grain while the bells were still silent and the citadel still believed itself safe.

She stood in the doorway with a child's boot in her hand and felt something in her chest go from broken to burning.

* * *

Corvane found her there. The marshal came down the corridor at a run, sword drawn, half-dressed, her greying braid coming loose—and stopped when she saw Bram's face.

"They took her." Bram heard her own voice as if from across a valley. Calm. Terribly calm. "Your wardens. Your walls. Your *wardens*, Corvane."

"I know."

"You *know*." Bram turned the boot over in her hand. "Sixty feet of granite. Warded doors. Men sworn to the Vigilant Order since before I was born, and they went through them like—" Her voice cracked along a fault line she hadn't known was there. "Like tide through a sandcastle."

"Three dead," Corvane said quietly. She had entered the room and was standing over the salt word, reading it the way she read everything, completely, without blinking. "Possibly four. There's a man missing from the east postern watch who isn't answering."

"A traitor inside your walls, then. Someone opened the way."

"There is always someone who opens the way." Corvane crouched and touched the salt without tasting it. "This is old craft. Older than the Court. This is how the Hunger's servants marked doors in the archives' oldest accounts—salt to show the path home, so the thing being carried doesn't forget where it's wanted." She looked up. "They're taking her to Vhalcrest. To the king."

"Then we ride tonight."

"Bram—"

"We ride *tonight*." The calm was gone now, torn away, and underneath it was something raw and enormous that Bram didn't recognize as her own voice until it filled the room. "You don't understand what you're saying wait for. You've read about the Hunger in your ash-dry archives, marshal. You've read accounts. I have *held her*. I have carried her fevered through a whiteout and listened to her talk in her sleep in a language older than your Order, and I have watched her wake from nightmares calling for a mother who has been dead three hundred years, and she holds my hand until she remembers where—when—she is. She is eight years old. She is *eight*, and she has been afraid every single day of her life, and the one place she finally slept without waking—" Bram stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum as if she could hold the burning in. "I told her the citadel was safe. I told her that myself. I looked her in the eye and I lied to her."

"You didn't lie," Corvane said. "You were wrong. Those are different things, and only one of them will help us now."

"Don't lecture me. Not tonight."

"Tonight especially," Corvane said, rising, "because tonight you are about to do something stupid, and I am the only person in this fortress with both the rank and the inclination to stop you—or failing that, to make it survivable." She sheathed her sword. "So listen. The Court agents are gone, hours gone, and they'll have used the pilgrim road down the western face because it's the only descent fast enough for a mounted party carrying a child. That's a day ahead of us, no less. But."

"But."

"But the Grandmaster has sealed the gates. Officially, the breach is contained and no one leaves until the intruders are 'accounted for.'" Corvane's mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely bitter. "Unofficially, the Grandmaster does not want witnesses leaving this mountain. Not after what happened in the archives. Not after what I've been asking questions about. If we wait for his permission, we will wait until Wren is on the Hollow throne and you are in a cell awaiting a trial he has already decided."

"So we go without permission."

"So we go without permission," Corvane agreed. "There's an old supply stair on the north face, cut for the quarry works, abandoned eighty years ago. It comes out above the tree line near a shepherd's track that meets the low road past the shrine of Saint Verel. Hard riding, but we'd lose half a day instead of two."

"*We*," Bram repeated.

"I told you in the lowlands that I don't break oaths lightly." Corvane met her eyes, and in the torchlight her face was carved into lines of someone who had spent thirty years holding a wall that everyone else insisted wasn't crumbling. "I also told you I serve the Vigilant Order. Tonight those are different things. Tonight I choose which one I meant." She turned for the door. "Arm yourself. There's a warden's cache two corridors down—I'll deal with the lock. And Bram."

"What."

"The salt." Corvane nodded at the word on the floor, still glittering, patient, obscene. "Don't touch it again. Don't step in it. It isn't a message for you alone—it's bait, and things that write bait in salt usually salt the ground around it too. Walk around it. Always walk around it."

Bram looked down at COME, at the small bright crystals catching fire from the torches, and understood that she was being summoned—not by the Court, but by something far older that wore the Court like a glove, something that had reached into a stone fortress on a mountaintop and plucked away the only thing she loved and then, with the leisurely confidence of a creature that had never once been refused, invited her to follow.

It should have frightened her. It did frighten her, a cold deep-water dread beneath everything else.

But above the dread, above the grief, the burning had organized itself into something with edges. Something with a direction. She had spent ten years learning invisibility, ten years folding herself smaller and smaller until the sky forgot her name—and now she stood in a room that smelled of salt and death, holding a child's boot, and every folded-away inch of herself unfolded at once.

Let the sky remember, then. Let it remember everything.

* * *

The warden's cache gave her a short sword, a knife, a quilted jack that fit badly across the shoulders, and a hooded traveling cloak the color of wet slate. Bram dressed mechanically, checking straps with a smuggler's hands while her mind ran the western descent—she knew the pilgrim road, had guided caravans down it twice, knew the switchbacks and the places where meltwater undercut the stone. A mounted party with an unconscious or bound child could not push faster than a canter on those grades. Half a day behind became a day behind became—

Became nothing she could afford to think. Thinking was for later. Later, when Wren was breathing somewhere within reach of Bram's hands.

She paused once, at the cache door, and did the thing she had not let herself do in the dormitory: she let the grief come, fully, for exactly ten breaths. Ten breaths of the image she'd been refusing—a small girl crying in the dark, calling for her, and Bram not there. Then she closed it away, banked it like a fire for the journey, because grief was fuel and fury was the flame and neither was useful unless she stayed alive to spend them.

In the corridor, Corvane was waiting with two horses saddled in the inner yard—Order bays, unmarked, chosen for stamina—and beside her, unexpectedly, a third figure: a young warden, barely twenty, with a bandaged hand and the haunted look of a man who had seen the breach and lived.

"Tomas found the east postern open," Corvane said. "And found the traitor."

"The traitor?"

"Dead. Salt in his mouth too. They use their tools and then they close the tool's mouth forever." Corvane swung up into the saddle. "Tomas knows the quarry stair. He'll take us down the first mile."

The young man wouldn't meet Bram's eyes. When he finally did, his voice was very quiet. "I saw them carry her out," he said. "From the wall. I was too far. I rang the bell too late." His bandaged hand flexed. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Let me help. Please let me help."

Bram looked at him—at his young ruined face, at his guilt, which was the same guilt she was carrying and would carry to her grave—and something in her that wanted to scream at the whole world instead said, "Then ride like she's your own blood, boy, and we'll call it even."

He nodded once, hard, and led them to the horses.

* * *

The quarry stair was a wound in the mountain's north face, a slot of cut stone descending through cloud, slick with night-melt, barely wide enough for the horses in its widened sections. They went down in the dark with no torches, Tomas leading by touch and memory, and the mountain breathed around them—cold mineral breath, the smell of pine far below, and threaded faintly through it all, impossible, unmistakable:

Salt.

Bram smelled it first at the fourth landing and told herself it was memory. At the seventh landing she could no longer pretend. The air itself tasted of brine, growing stronger with every turn of the descent, as if the sea were climbing the mountain after them, as if the whole drowned weight of Aelmarrow had risen from its grave and turned its face toward Thornfell.

"She's awake," Bram said. It came out of her without permission. "Whatever they used to carry her—she's fighting it. She's singing. That's why the air tastes like this. That's why the wards failed on the way in, maybe—she was already calling, and the salt answered."

Corvane was silent a moment. "If she's awake and resisting, the Court will need to stop and bind her properly. That costs time."

"Or they'll gag her and she'll suffocate on her own song, and they won't care, because a dying vessel still burns." Bram's hands were fists in the reins. "Ride faster."

Below them, far below, past the last veils of cloud, the lowlands spread black and starless under a moon the color of bone. And on the distant southern horizon—forty miles, fifty, too far to see, too far to be possible—there was a glow. Not dawn. Dawn was hours away and would come from the east.

This light came from the sea.

It pulsed, slow as a heartbeat, green-white and deep, the color Bram had seen once from a diving bell off the Sere Coast when she'd looked down into water too deep for light and seen something down there glowing anyway. Looking back up.

"It knows we're coming," Tomas whispered. His voice shook. "Marshal—it *knows*."

"It knows," Corvane said. She did not soften it, and Bram was grateful; lies were a luxury the night had priced beyond them. "It has known since the moment its servants wrote that word on the floor. Which means it expects us to come in fear, on its schedule, begging." She gathered her reins, and in the bone-light from the south her face was grim and steady and absolutely unafraid, and Bram thought: *this is what the Order was supposed to be, before it forgot.* "So we will come instead the way the Vigilant was sworn to come. Angry. Armed. And late only by half a day."

They rode down out of the mountain, three riders and a grief, toward the pulsing shore-light and the city where a hollow king sat on a stolen throne with a stolen child.

Behind them, on the wind off the heights, so faint it might have been meltwater, might have been memory, Bram heard—or dreamed she heard—a thread of song in a tongue no living throat should know.

She held it like a hand in the dark, and rode.
