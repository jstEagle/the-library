## 20. The King's Road

The old royal road had been built for processions. Bram could still see its bones beneath the mud and the moss—flagstones wide enough for six horses abreast, milestones carved with the sigils of a dynasty that no longer ruled anything but memory. Now it was a scar of broken paving running south through the lowlands, and the sea kept creeping closer to it every year, as if the water remembered the road too, and wanted it back.

They rode at dawn, when the mist off the flooded fields was thick enough to drink.

Bram led on a grey mare she'd bought from a farmer who hadn't asked questions, because the farmer had been dead in his kitchen and the mare had been standing in his yard with her saddle already on. She didn't examine that too closely. The drowned dead were walking, and the living were leaving, and along the whole breadth of the lowlands there was a silence like the inside of a held breath.

Behind her rode Corvane in Order grey, sword at her hip and her marshal's badge buried somewhere she'd stopped wearing since the Grandmaster's fall—since she'd learned what the badge had been paying for all those years. Behind Corvane rode Wren.

The girl sat a horse better than any child of eight had a right to, which told Bram something about Aelmarrow's last days that no archive had: they'd taught their children to run. Wren rode wrapped in an oilskin cloak three sizes too big, hood up, salt-pale eyes scanning the horizon the way Bram scanned weather. And behind Wren, tied to a lead line and trotting with the offended dignity of the young, came the dragon.

It was the size of a large hound now, wings still membrane-thin and useless for anything but dramatic gestures, scales the color of wet slate shot through with veins of ember-red. It had refused every name Bram offered and answered only to the sound Wren made—a soft rising note, like a question asked in the drowned tongue. So the note was its name, and Bram found herself using it, and found it stranger each time that she did.

"Road forks ahead," Corvane said, drawing up beside her. "Left runs to the coast road. Right goes over the ford at Marlow's Crossing."

"Right," Bram said. "Marlow's Crossing has a man named Doss Hackett who owes me his life twice over and his livelihood once. Left has nothing but drowned villages and the things walking out of them."

"You're certain he'll come?"

Bram watched the mist peel off a field where, two nights ago, forty figures had stood in perfect rows until dawn, faces turned toward Wren like sunflowers toward a sun that had drowned. "Doss Hackett once watched me call lightning down on a revenue cutter to save his cargo and his neck, and afterward he wept into his beer and swore a debt so solemn I thought he'd propose marriage. He'll come. Whether he comes sober is another matter."

Corvane almost smiled. It was a rare thing, and it changed her face from something carved to something merely human. "An army of smugglers," she said. "The Vigilant Order will be thrilled."

"The Vigilant Order can be thrilled from a distance." Bram nudged the mare forward. "You want the Hollow King unmade. I want Wren breathing. Doss wants his debts paid and his people safe. That's not an army, Marshal. That's a pile of kindling looking for a spark."

* * *

Marlow's Crossing had been a toll town once, back when kings collected tolls instead of heads. Its bridge still stood over a river gone brackish and strange, and its inn—the Drowned Mermaid, a name that had been a joke before it became a prophecy—still poured ale to the handful of families too stubborn or too poor to flee inland.

Doss Hackett was exactly where Bram knew he'd be: at the corner table, nearest the door, with his back to the wall and a cup in front of him that he wasn't drinking. He'd gone grey since she'd seen him last. His hands, always quick, lay flat on the table like he was afraid of what they'd do without orders.

He looked up when she came in, and Bram watched him go through recognition, disbelief, and terror in the space of a breath.

"Stormbringer," he whispered. Then, louder, with the desperate cheer of a man greeting a ghost: "Bram Kavanagh. Ten years, and you walk into my inn looking like you never left. Sit. Sit! Ale? We've got ale. It's terrible. You'll have some anyway."

"I'll have information first." She sat across from him, and felt Wren settle into the shadow behind her shoulder the way she always did, a small warm weight of presence. The dragon curled under the table and put one clawed foot on Bram's boot, possessive. "Doss. What do you know about the riders?"

His cheer drained out of him like ale through a cracked cask. "Court riders. Aye." He glanced toward the window, toward the bridge. "They came through four days back. Black cloaks, no sigils, but you don't need sigils when your horses are shod in silver. They asked after a child. Showed a sketch." His eyes flicked, involuntary, to the hooded shape behind Bram, and Bram saw him understand everything at once, the way smugglers do—whole ledgers balanced in a single glance. "Saints' teeth, Bram. That's her. That's the singing child."

"That's my daughter," Bram said, and heard her own voice go flat and hard as river ice. "And if anyone in this room remembers a favor I ever did them, they'll remember that word. *Daughter.*"

The inn had gone quiet. There were perhaps fifteen people in it—barge folk, a tinker, two old brothers who mended nets nobody needed anymore. Fifteen pairs of ears, and Bram let the silence stretch until it ached, because she'd learned in ten years of exile that silence was a currency and she'd rather spend it than coin.

Then Doss Hackett did the thing that made her love him, in the rough way you love people who've shared your roads. He stood up, turned to face the room, and said:

"You all heard the lady. Her *daughter.* Now. Who here drank free the winter the revenue men burned the quay at Saltmarsh? Who here had their warrants torn up by a storm that came out of a clear sky, hmm? Who here—" his voice cracked, not entirely from performance, "—who here watched her pull Tam Broadbelt's boy out of the floodwater at Colder's Bend, dead cold, and breathe him back?"

Silence again. But a different silence. The kind that leans forward.

An old woman by the fire spoke up, reedy and clear. "I remember Colder's Bend."

A bargehand: "I remember Saltmarsh."

The net-menders looked at each other, and the elder one nodded slowly, like a man agreeing to a price he'd known was coming. "We remember you, Stormbringer."

Bram stood. Her throat was tight, and she hated that, and she used the hate to make her voice steady. "I'm riding to Vhalcrest to take a crown off a king's head. I won't lie to you—it's a fool's errand, and most of us will likely die. But the thing wearing that king is why your wells taste of salt. Why your dead won't stay down. Why the sea's been crawling inland like it's owed something." She paused. "It *is* owed something. And I mean to pay it, before it takes the rest."

She didn't ask them to come. Asking was for merchants. She sat back down and ordered the terrible ale, and drank it, and it was terrible.

By morning, eleven of the fifteen had saddled horses, loaded barges, kissed children goodbye, or simply picked up whatever weapon lay nearest. Doss Hackett locked the door of the Drowned Mermaid and hung the key around his neck.

"For luck," he said. "We'll drink there again, or we won't drink anywhere."

* * *

They gathered as they went, the way rivers gather.

At the ford at Colder's Bend, a broad-shouldered woman named Maren Ostwick took one look at Bram and started laughing—a wild, disbelieving laugh—and then embraced her hard enough to crack ribs. Maren ran cargo on the river now; she had six boats and thirty cousins, and by nightfall thirty cousins had opinions, weapons, and no fear worth mentioning. At the ruins of the Saltmarsh quay, a one-eyed man called Finch emerged from a smokehouse full of contraband brandy, listened to Bram's speech for exactly four words, and said, "Say no more. Where do I stab?" At a crossroads shrine whose saint had been decapitated by iconoclasts a century ago, they found a dozen pilgrims sheltering from the rain—and among them, to Corvane's visible shock, three wardens of the Vigilant Order, junior men who had deserted when the Grandmaster's confessions came to light. They knelt to Corvane in the mud. She made them get up and carry supplies instead, which Bram judged a far better use of penance.

Wren watched all of it from under her hood, silent, taking in faces the way she took in everything—with that ancient, patient attention that sometimes made Bram forget she was eight years old. On the third night, camped in a ruined granary while rain drummed the roof, Wren touched Bram's arm and spoke in her careful, accented common tongue.

"They follow you because you gave them things."

Bram looked up from sharpening her knife. "Gave them things?"

"When storms came. When floods came. When the king's men came." Wren's brow furrowed, searching for words the way she searched for footing on ice. "In Aelmarrow, they followed the fire-keepers. Same reason. Not because strong. Because... *giving.*" She pressed her small fist against Bram's sternum. "Fire-keeper heart. They see it."

Bram's mother had said something like that once, before the Order's chains and the trial and the branding. She'd said the Kavanaghs weren't storm-callers first; they were keepers first, and the storms came after, the way smoke comes after a hearth. Bram had spent ten years trying to bury that inheritance under invisibility, and here was an eight-year-old dredging it up in broken common speech.

"Eat your stew," Bram said gruffly, and Wren smiled—the small, knowing smile of someone who has won an argument conducted entirely in silence—and ate her stew.

The dragon slept between them, radiating heat like a banked oven, and outside the rain fell, and somewhere far to the south the sea waited with its patient hunger.

* * *

On the fifth day, the road gave them their first sight of Vhalcrest.

It rose out of the coastal haze like a jaw of black stone—walls a hundred feet high, towers crowned with the Hollow Court's banners, and beyond them, glimpsed between battlements, the pale gleam of the palace on its hill above the harbor. The royal road ran straight toward the great south gate, and the gate was shut, and the fields before it were crowded.

Not with soldiers. With the dead.

They stood in the drizzle by the thousands—fishermen and farmers and fine ladies in rotted silk, salt-crusted and patient, ranks deepening by the hour as more came up from the coast. They did not attack the walls. They did not wail or claw. They simply stood, facing the city, waiting, and the sight of them emptied the bowels of courage faster than any charge could have.

Bram reined in on a low ridge half a mile out, her ragged company gathering behind her—smugglers and deserters and barge-cousins, maybe eighty souls against a kingdom. Below, the dead stood in their multitudes. Above, ravens circled the south gate, where she could just make out the glint of ballistae being cranked into position.

"They're not besieging it," Corvane said quietly beside her. She had a spyglass up, her jaw tight. "Look at their faces. They're not angry. They're..." She lowered the glass. "They're *homesick.*"

"Aelmarrow stood here," Wren said suddenly.

Everyone turned. The girl had slid down from her horse and stood at the ridge's edge, wind pulling at her hood, and her voice had that doubled quality it sometimes took—the child's voice with something vast moving underneath it, like a current beneath ice.

"This was the shore road. Before the water came. The kings of Aelmarrow rode this road to greet the fishing fleets." Her hand rose, pointing—not at the walls of Vhalcrest but past them, at the harbor, at the grey sea beyond. "The city is under there. All of it. And they have walked three hundred years to stand where their doors used to be." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "They are not waiting for the city, Bram. They are waiting for *me*. To finish the song. To let them sleep."

"And the Court knows it," Bram said. "That's why they took you. You're not just a vessel for the fire. You're the key to the dead—and whoever holds the key commands the tide."

Corvane was quiet a moment. Then: "There's another way in. Not the gate."

Bram looked at her.

"Flooded undercity," Corvane said. "Old Aelmarrow's harbors became Vhalcrest's cellars and sewers when the sea receded the first time. My order keeps maps. Smugglers keep better ones." She raised an eyebrow at Bram. "I'm told you know a man with a boat."

Bram turned. Down the slope, among the tents and moored barges of her unlikely army, Doss Hackett was arguing with Maren Ostwick about anchor lines, the key to the Drowned Mermaid glinting at his throat.

"Doss," Bram called, "how do you feel about sewers?"

He looked up, and grinned, and it was the grin of a man who had already decided to die gloriously and was pleased to have the details settled. "Stormbringer, I've smuggled brandy through worse."

Below them, the dead stood in their thousands in the rain, faces turned toward the sea, toward the child, toward the end of a three-hundred-year wait. And high on the walls of Vhalcrest, something in the tallest tower turned its tide-pool eyes south, and saw them coming.
