## 21. The Drowned Dead March

The first village they came to was called Merrow's Ford, and it was empty.

Not abandoned—empty, in the way a cup is empty after someone has drunk from it in haste. Doors stood open. A cradle rocked by a hearth where the fire had been left to burn down to embers. Loaves of bread gone grey on a windowsill, half-sliced, the knife still standing upright in the board as though its owner had simply set it down mid-thought and walked out into the cold.

Bram stood in the doorway of the baker's house and felt the silence press against her like deep water.

"They left in the night," Corvane said behind her. The marshal had dismounted and was reading the street the way Bram read weather—by signs, by pressure, by what was absent rather than present. "No struggle. No pack animals taken. They just... went."

"Where?"

Corvane didn't answer at once. She was looking east, down the long shallow valley toward the coast road, where the morning mist lay thick as wool. And Bram followed her gaze and understood, because she had felt it too for three days now: a pull at the base of her skull, faint and tidal, like the sensation of being watched by something patient.

Wren.

The girl sat on her pony in the middle of the empty street, small hands folded on the saddle horn, her face turned toward the sea she could not see. Her lips moved. Not speaking—Bram had learned the difference over these hard weeks of travel. When Wren spoke, her eyes were present, sharp, a child's eyes. This was something else. This was listening.

"Wren." Bram kept her voice gentle but let it carry. "Little bird."

The girl blinked. Came back. Looked around at the empty village with an expression that was far too old for eight years old, and said, in her careful, accented Common—the words Bram had taught her one by one through a winter beside a waystation fire:

"They are coming home."

* * *

They made camp that night in a shepherd's hut above Merrow's Ford, and Bram did not sleep.

She sat by the small fire with Wren asleep against her side, the girl's breath slow and salt-sweet even here, forty miles and more from any coast, and she listened to the dark. The mountains behind them were quiet in the old way—wind, stone, the occasional complaint of some night bird. But ahead, downslope, toward the lowlands and the river country and the distant grey gleam of Vhalcrest's walls, the night had a different texture. A fullness. As if the land itself were holding its breath.

Near midnight, Hesk the smuggler climbed up from the picket line where the horses were hobbled, moving carefully, the way men move when they don't want to admit they're frightened.

"Bram." He crouched by the fire. His face was hollowed out by its light. "You'll want to see this. Or maybe you won't. I don't know which."

She eased Wren onto her bedroll, tucked the blanket to her chin—an act so ordinary it felt obscene, given what waited below—and followed Hesk to the ridge above the ford.

The mist had lifted off the valley floor. Moonlight poured down the river's course like spilled milk, and along the banks, on both sides, as far as the moonlight reached and further—

They were walking.

Bram had seen drowned things before. She had pulled corpses from harbors in her smuggling years, had once watched a wrecked crew wash ashore at Greywater with their faces eaten soft. She thought she knew what death looked like when it moved.

This was not that.

They came up the river road in ranks—not shambling, not stumbling, but *walking*, with the unhurried gait of people going somewhere they had been a thousand times. Fishermen still in their oilskins, crusted white with salt. Women with their hair long undone, streaming in the current of their own passage. Children. She saw children, small shapes keeping pace between tall ones, holding hands that had been bones for three hundred years, and the sight of it drove the air out of her lungs.

They did not glow. They did not moan. Their eye sockets held only shadow and, deep in each shadow, a pinprick of pale light like the last of a candle drowning in its own wax. They made no sound at all. Hundreds of them, thousands, filling the road from bank to bank, and the only noise in the whole valley was the river talking to itself and the wind combing the winter grass.

"Gods below," Hesk whispered. "Gods below and above and every direction there is."

"Not gods," Bram said. Her voice came out strange, flat, as if someone else were using her mouth. "Aelmarrow."

He looked at her. "The drowned kingdom? That's a story. That's a *song*. My gran sang me songs about—"

"I know the songs." Everyone knew the songs. *Fair Aelmarrow beneath the foam, where the king keeps court and the ships come home.* Every child in the lowlands grew up on them, the way children everywhere grow up on the dead: sweetened, softened, made safe. "It wasn't a song. It was a city, and it sank in a single night, and everything in it drowned with the king's name still on its gates. And now—" she gestured at the silent tide flowing up the valley "—now it's coming back, because my girl called it home."

Hesk was quiet a long moment. Then he said, "Called it? Or pulled it?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. The one Bram had been circling for days, turning over in the dark hours while Wren slept against her side smelling of woodsmoke and brine. The dead were drawn to Wren—that much Corvane had confirmed, that much the archives' scattered pages had promised. Dragon-fire memory, the last living vessel of it, a beacon burning in the shape of a child. The risen dead converged on her the way iron filings converge on a lodestone.

But watching them now, Bram saw no hunger in their march. No menace. They walked like pilgrims. They walked like exiles finally permitted to turn around.

*History demanding its due,* Corvane had said, two nights ago, staring at the same horizon. Bram hadn't understood then. She was beginning to now.

"We keep moving," she said. "At first light. We stay ahead of them, we stay off the main roads, and we get to Vhalcrest before they do."

"And if the city won't open for us?"

Bram looked at the tide of the dead, at the little ones holding hands, and said nothing, because she did not know the answer, and lying to Hesk would have cost more than silence.

* * *

The second village was named Coldharbour, and it was not quite empty.

One man remained: ancient, bent double, sitting on his own doorstep with a blanket over his knees and a jug of something strong between his feet, watching the road with the serenity of a man who has made his peace or lost his mind, possibly both.

"You'll be wanting to turn around," he told them cheerfully, as they reined in. "Or go on through, if you're fools. Everyone else went south to my sister's. Took the chickens, even. Chickens, mind you, and left me, because I'm ninety-one and slow and mean."

"Why did they run?" Corvane asked.

He squinted up at her Order-grey cloak without much respect. "Why d'you think? The dead are walking the low roads. My grandfather's grandfather helped build the sea-wall at Vhalcrest, and he'd tell you: when the salt comes inland, you get uphill and pray." He took a pull from his jug. "But they don't hurt nobody. That's the strange part. Old Marta Tull stayed to watch them pass—stubborn as me—and she says they walked right through the barley fields and never broke a stalk. Never touched a soul. Just..." He waved a hand vaguely, searching for the word. "*Processing.* Like a funeral that takes a week."

Wren had slid down from her pony. Before Bram could catch her sleeve, the girl crossed the yard and stood in front of the old man, looking up at him with her head tilted.

"You stayed," Wren said.

He grinned, gap-toothed. "I did, little miss. Someone ought to see them off proper. It's rude otherwise, all this dying and coming back and nobody waving."

Something in Wren's face cracked then—a brightness, a wet shine—and she reached out and took his spotted, knuckled hand in both of hers. The old man went very still.

"You speak the old words," he breathed. "My gran used to sing a lullaby in the old words. Said her gran learned it from folk who came inland when the kingdom fell. I thought they were nonsense sounds. I thought—" His voice failed him. He pressed the child's fingers to his forehead like a blessing, or an apology. "Three hundred years we forgot you. Three hundred years, and we sang your drowning for entertainment at fairs."

"It's all right," Wren said—in the drowned tongue, first, and then, laboriously, in Common, the words Bram had given her: "It's all right. You remember now."

Bram turned away and busied herself with her horse's girth strap, because her eyes were stinging, and a guide who had spent ten years perfecting invisibility could not afford to be seen weeping in a stranger's yard.

* * *

They traveled east through emptied country for four more days, and the emptiness grew stranger the deeper they went.

Fields stood harvested and ungathered, grain rotting in shocks. A wedding party's ribbons still fluttered from a doorway, the feast abandoned on its trestles, jackdaws fat and drunken among the pies. At a crossroads shrine, someone had left a final offering—a wheel of cheese, a doll, a soldier's medal—and scrawled across the shrine's stone in charcoal: *WE ARE SORRY. WE FORGOT.*

That one stopped Corvane cold. She stood before it a long time, gloved hand resting on the inscription, and when she turned away her jaw was set in a way Bram had learned to read as grief under discipline.

"The Order taught us the Drowning was a judgment," the marshal said that night, over a fire built small and smokeless. "Divine punishment for Aelmarrow's pride. That's the doctrine. Pride, and dragon-pacts, and forbidden memory—that's why the archives were sealed, why your storm-calling was branded heresy, why every trace of the old kingdom was scrubbed from the maps." She fed a twig to the flames. "Standing here, in a country that fled its own homes rather than look at what it had buried—I begin to think the judgment ran the other direction."

"Aelmarrow drowned," Bram said. "Whatever sank it."

"Yes. And everyone who survived made themselves comfortable on the shore and agreed to forget. Comfortable, and grateful, and quiet." Corvane's eyes found Wren, asleep again, always sleeping more now, her flame guttering lower by the day; Bram saw the marshal note it too, saw the fear flicker behind her composure. "Forgetting is its own sin, Kavanagh. Perhaps the oldest one."

Across the fire, Wren stirred and murmured something in the drowned tongue—a name, Bram thought, or a place—and the wind coming up from the lowlands carried, unmistakably, the smell of the sea.

Forty miles from the coast. Thirty-five now. The wells of the last village they'd passed had tasted of brine.

* * *

On the ninth day out of Thornfell, they topped a long rise and saw Vhalcrest.

Even from this distance—even diminished by haze and distance and dread—it was a blow. The capital sat astride the River Vhal where it widened toward the delta, ringed by walls that had been raised generation after generation until they seemed less built than accumulated, a geological fact rather than a work of masons. The palace crown rose at the city's heart, white stone gone pearl-grey, its spires needling a sky that was the wrong color. Bram had seen many skies in ten years of reading weather like scripture, and she had never seen this shade: a bruised violet-green, heavy as a held breath, pressing down on the delta like a lid.

And below the city, spreading across the floodplain in every direction, silent as frost forming—

The dead.

They had come from the whole coast, from every estuary and harbor and sunken mile of the old kingdom, and they had gathered. Tens of thousands. A hundred thousand. They stood in the fields and on the roads and in the shallows of the widening river, rank upon rank upon rank, motionless, waiting, their pale candle-flame eyes all turned toward the city that had forgotten them. From this height they did not even look like people. They looked like weather. They looked like a change in the world's substance, like the moment before snowfall when the air goes still and every living thing falls quiet.

Except they were not entirely motionless. As Bram watched, a ripple passed through the host—outward from the river, inward toward the walls—and she realized with a lurch that they were turning. All of them. Slowly, in unison, like a field of flowers following a sun.

Turning toward her. Toward the small figure on the small pony at the crest of the rise.

Toward Wren.

"Oh," Hesk said faintly, beside her. "Oh, that's not right."

Wren raised one hand. Just that—a child's wave, guileless, the way she might wave to Corvane across a campfire. And across the whole flooded plain, a hundred thousand drowned dead bowed. A wave of bending figures rolling out from the rise like the wake of a stone dropped in a still sea, down and down and outward until the eye lost it in the haze, the entire host of Aelmarrow bowing to an eight-year-old girl in a patched coat.

The sound reached them a heartbeat later: not a roar, not a groan, but a sigh. One vast exhalation, drawn from a hundred thousand ruined throats, carrying across the plain like wind through a canyon. It sounded, Bram thought, like relief. Like the sound a burdened creature makes when the weight finally shifts.

Then the dead straightened, and turned back to the city, and resumed their waiting.

Nobody spoke for a long while. On the plain below, a flock of birds rose from the walls of Vhalcrest—startled, perhaps, by the sigh—and wheeled once against the bruised sky.

"Well," Corvane said at last, and her voice was steady in the way of a woman gripping a rail in a storm. "Now the Court knows we're here, too. There isn't a spyglass on those walls that missed that."

"Good," Bram said, and meant it, and was surprised by how much she meant it. Somewhere in the last nine days the fear had burned down to something harder and cleaner. Let them look. Let the Hollow King sit on his stolen throne and feel, for the first time in three centuries, the ground shift beneath him. "Let them count."

"Counting won't save them," Corvane said. "Or us. Those walls will be sealed, Kavanagh—sealed against the dead, sealed against the Court's enemies, sealed against *us*. The Grandmaster's writ runs there, and his writ says your head and that child both belong to the pyre." She turned her horse down toward the plain. "We need a way inside that doesn't use a gate."

Bram looked at the city a long moment—at the walls, at the river running dark beneath them, at the warrens of the old harbor district she had haunted as a younger smuggler, all the drowned cellars and sunken streets and forgotten watergates that respectable maps pretended didn't exist.

A smile touched her mouth, thin and cold and entirely her mother's.

"Corvane," she said, "you're riding with the best smuggler-guide in the Thornfells. There is always a way in that doesn't use a gate."

Behind her, Wren turned her pony to follow, and the whole waiting host of the drowned turned with her, a hundred thousand faces lifting toward the walls of Vhalcrest, patient as tide, inevitable as salt.

The city had forgotten them for three hundred years.

By tomorrow's dusk, it would remember.
