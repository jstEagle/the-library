## 22. Gates of Vhalcrest

They saw the walls of Vhalcrest at dusk on the third day, and Bram felt the city see them back.

It was a feeling she had no better word for, though she had spent ten years learning the grammar of places—the way a mountain pass held its breath before an avalanche, the way a frozen river hummed through its ice like a struck bell. Vhalcrest had always had a voice. In her girlhood it had been a roar: gulls and fishmongers and temple bells and the endless grinding argument of ten thousand carts on cobblestone. Now it was quieter than it had any right to be, and its quiet had teeth.

The walls rose out of the coastal haze black against a bruised orange sky—basalt and old mortar, forty feet of them, crowned with watchfires that burned too many and too close together. A city did not light itself like that in peacetime. A city lit itself like that when it expected to be eaten.

"They've sealed the gates," Corvane said. She stood beside Bram on the ridge with her spyglass up, her marshal's coat swapped for a pilgrim's gray wool that made her look like what she pretended to be—a woman grown old in service to some minor saint. "All six. Look at the harbor chain."

Bram looked. The great iron chain that could be raised across the mouth of Vhalcrest's harbor hung low and taut, and beyond it the masts of the Court's fleet stood at anchor like a forest of bare spears. Between the fleet and the shore, small boats moved in patient patterns. Patrols.

"Sealed against us," Wren said.

The child sat between them on the cold grass, knees drawn up, her chin on her arms. She had not spoken much since the dead began marching. When she did speak, it was often in both tongues at once, as if the drowned language had taken up residence behind her common words and leaned out through them. *Against us,* she'd said, and beneath it, faint as a second heartbeat: *against all of us.*

"Not against you," Bram said. It came out harder than she meant. She softened it by putting a hand on the girl's back, feeling the small architecture of ribs and spine, the strange warmth that never quite left her now—the dragon-fire's ember-glow, banked but present, like a hearthstone in winter. "Never against you."

"Then who is left?" Wren asked, with the terrible simplicity of children. "Everyone is against someone."

Corvane lowered the spyglass. For once she didn't correct the logic. "The city's sealed against the dead," she said instead. "And against the Court, though they'll never admit it. And against anyone coming off the low road, which is where we are." She turned the glass north, along the coast, and Bram followed her gaze to the thing they had been walking toward and refusing to name for three days.

The dead were visible even from here, even at this distance, even in the failing light. Not as figures—that would have been easier—but as a wrongness in the land itself. The low road ran down through fields that should have been stubble and fallow mud, and instead there was a darkness moving in them, slow and silent and vast, a tide that walked. Here and there a farmhouse lamp went out as its owners fled ahead of it. No screams carried. That was the worst part. The drowned dead of Aelmarrow did not moan or rattle or beg. They simply came, in their salt-stiff thousands, with the patience of water finding its level.

"How long until they reach the walls?" Bram asked.

"At their pace? Two days. Perhaps three." Corvane folded the spyglass away. "The Court will want to be inside before then. So will we."

"The gates are shut."

"The gates are shut for people who use gates." Corvane allowed herself the ghost of a smile, dry as old paper. "You told me you knew another way in. I confess I've been dreading hearing it since the ferry."

Bram looked at the city a long moment—at the smoke rising thin and frightened from ten thousand chimneys, at the temple spire where she had once been dragged in chains at nineteen years old while a hall full of strangers pronounced the word *traitor* over her like a sentence of weather—and felt the old claustrophobia close around her throat like a hand. Homecoming. They called it that, homecoming, as if a place could only hold good things.

"There's a door under the harbor," she said. "Smugglers call it the Drowned Gate. It opens into the undercity—the old cisterns and vaults below the temple district, from before the last expansion. Half of them flooded decades ago when the sea wall cracked. Nobody official goes down there. Officially it doesn't exist."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, it's how half the contraband in Vhalcrest gets past the excise men." She stood, brushing frost from her knees. "And unofficially, one of the men who knows the way owes me his life twice over and his sister's freedom once. His name is Doss Merrow. If he's still breathing, he'll get us in."

*If.* The word had been doing a lot of work lately.

Wren took her hand without being asked. Her grip was small and fierce. "I don't like cities," she said.

"I know," Bram said. "Neither do I. We'll only borrow it."

* * *

They came down to the coast after dark, avoiding the low road entirely, cutting through salt marsh where the ground sucked at their boots and the reeds whispered secrets in a wind that smelled wrong—smelled of brine and old stone, of things dissolving. Forty miles inland the wells had tasted of the sea. Here, at the world's salt edge, the wrongness was everywhere and nowhere, a flavor in the air like the memory of a drowning.

The smugglers' cove lay two miles south of the harbor, behind a screen of black rocks that the charts marked as shoals. Bram had memorized the approach twenty years ago and found, to her relief and something like grief, that her body remembered it too—the channel that opened only at half-tide, the rock shaped like a kneeling monk, the smell of tar and herring that meant shelter.

There was a boat waiting in the cove. There was also a crossbow aimed at them from the rocks, and a voice like gravel soaked in gin saying, "That's far enough, friend, unless you're fond of bolts."

"Doss Merrow," Bram said to the dark. "You still owe me for the Kestrel job, and I've come to collect."

A long silence. Then the crossbow lowered, and a lantern unshuttered, and a face swam out of the gloom that time had worked over hard—broader, redder, missing an ear—but that she knew. Doss Merrow stared at her the way men stare at ghosts, and then he laughed, one bark of it, and pressed a fist to his chest in the old smuggler's salute.

"Bram Kavanagh," he said. "Dead ten years and back with the Vigilant Order at her side. You always did travel strange company." His eyes flicked to Corvane, cataloguing the pilgrim's disguise and dismissing it—he'd seen marshals before; he knew the posture—and then to Wren, and stopped. Something moved across his face. Fear, Bram thought, and then pity, and then a strange kind of reverence, as if he'd looked at the child and seen the sea looking back.

"She's the singer," he said quietly. "Word's come down the coast. Half the taverns between here and Thornfell are singing about her, and the other half have been paid not to." He spat into the shingle. "Court riders were through this morning asking after her. Offering gold enough to drown in."

"And you told them?"

"I told them nothing they wanted to hear." He grinned, gap-toothed. "I told them the tide was bad and the fishing was worse, and that no child had passed my rocks. Which was true until tonight." He looked at Bram again, and the grin faded into something older and more serious. "You're going into the city. Through the Gate."

"If it still opens."

"It opens." He hesitated. "It shouldn't, is the thing. The cisterns have been flooding worse these past weeks—since the dead started walking, since the girl started singing, if you believe the talk. Water where there's never been water. My cousin runs cargo through the undercity twice a month and says whole galleries are under now that were dry at midsummer. The sea's coming up inside the walls, Bram. Like it's looking for something."

Bram felt Wren's hand tighten in hers. The child said nothing, but the warmth of her palm burned a little brighter, and Bram understood, and hated understanding. The drowned kingdom was waking, and everything connected to it—every cistern, every well, every crack in every old foundation—was remembering the sea.

"Then we go fast and we go shallow," Bram said. "Can you take us to the Gate tonight?"

"Tonight, tomorrow, whenever you like." Doss was already moving, waving them toward the boat, shouting soft orders to the two men crewing it. "But I'm coming with you as far as the door, and I'm telling you now—if the water's risen past the third marker, I turn back and you're on your own. I've got a sister alive in the Low Quarter and I mean to keep it that way."

"Fair terms," Bram said, and climbed into the boat, and pulled Wren in after her, and tried not to think about how much the black water of the cove looked like the water in her dreams.

* * *

The Drowned Gate opened off a sea cave a quarter mile south of the harbor wall, behind a curtain of rock that hid it from every patrol lantern in the city. Doss killed the oars a hundred yards out and let the boat drift in on the swell, silent as a coffin, and Bram watched the basalt walls slide past close enough to touch, wet and gleaming in the hooded lantern's glow, and felt the claustrophobia settle over her again—not the open dread of the city above, but something tighter, older, the fear of small dark places full of cold water that had lived in her since she was small.

She had nearly drowned in a cistern once, at eight years old, the same age as Wren. She had never told anyone. She thought about it now, with the roof of the cave pressing down and the water slapping hollow echoes off the stone, and decided that some fears were load-bearing. You kept them because they kept you careful.

"Here." Doss pointed. "See the iron ring? Below the barnacles, there's a seam. Push and lift. Mind your head."

The gate was a slab of dressed granite disguised with rubble, and it swung outward on greased hinges into a low tunnel that breathed cold air smelling of rot and lamp oil. Beyond it, the undercity waited in the dark.

Doss handed Bram a second lantern. "Three markers," he said. "Carved fish on the right-hand wall. First fish, water's ankle-deep, fine. Second fish, knee-deep, slow down. Third fish—" he grimaced, "—third fish, I said I'd turn back, and I will, and so should you. Past the third gallery the tunnels climb toward the Temple Ward cellars. There's a ladder up to a grate behind the chandler's row. You remember?"

"I remember."

"You always did." He caught her arm as she stepped onto the submerged stair, and his voice dropped. "Bram. What they're saying about the king. About the crown. Is it true?"

She looked at him—at the ear he'd lost to a press-gang, at the sister he'd mentioned, at the ordinary decent criminal courage of him—and lied the smallest lie she could.

"It's true that we mean to fix it."

He nodded slowly, accepting the shape of the answer without its substance, the way smugglers accept most things. "Then go quick," he said. "And Bram—when it's fixed, whatever it costs—" he glanced at Wren, huddled in the stern with Corvane's cloak around her shoulders, "—make sure there's a coast left for the rest of us."

* * *

The first fish was underwater.

Bram stood in the tunnel entrance with the lantern held high and read the carved stone the way she read weather: the fish's tail just broke the surface of water that stretched away into the dark, flat and still and black as poured ink. Ankle-deep, Doss had said. It was shin-deep already and rising, and it was not moving the way floodwater moved. Floodwater crept and gurgled and found its channels. This water lay perfectly still, perfectly level, as if it had been poured in deliberately and left to wait.

"The sea's coming up inside the walls," Doss had said. Looking at it now, Bram thought: no. The sea isn't coming up. Aelmarrow is coming back. The drowned kingdom was rising through every crack and cellar of the city built on top of its grave, reclaiming its foundations inch by inch, and the water in this tunnel was not floodwater. It was memory.

"Second fish will be under," Corvane said behind her. She had waded up close, her pilgrim's robe hitched to the knee, her voice flat and marshal-calm in a way Bram was beginning to recognize as its own kind of armor. "Which means we're past the point of turning back anyway. Good. I hate decisions."

"That's not what a marshal's supposed to say."

"I'm wearing a pilgrim's dress. I can say what I like." But there was no humor in it, really. Corvane's eyes were on the black water, and Bram saw her swallow. "How deep does it get?"

"Deepest gallery has a sump maybe chest-high on me. Usually." She lifted Wren onto her hip without asking—the child weighed almost nothing, all bone and ember—and Wren wrapped her arms around Bram's neck and put her face against her shoulder, and Bram felt the tremor running through the small body and the heat beneath it, the dragon-fire flickering like a candle in a draft.

"I can walk," Wren whispered.

"I know you can. Walk tomorrow. Tonight you ride."

They went in.

The undercity of Vhalcrest was a drowned cathedral of brick and stone—vaulted galleries older than the walls above, older than the kingdom, built by hands that had known Aelmarrow when Aelmarrow lived. Bram had run cargo through these tunnels for six years, and she had always felt the age of them, the weight of the drowned city pressing up from below like a sleeper pressing up against the underside of a dream. Now the feeling was not ambient. It was directional. It came from ahead of them, from the heart of the dark, and it came with a sound.

At first she thought it was water. Then she thought it was wind. Then, in the second gallery, where the second fish glimmered just beneath the surface and the water had reached their thighs and the lantern-light barely pushed the dark back an arm's length in any direction, she realized what it was.

Singing. Very far away. Very many voices.

Wren's head came up off her shoulder.

"You hear it," Bram said. Not a question.

"They're singing the gate-songs." The child's voice was thin and clear in the dripping dark. "The ones for coming home. My grandmother sang them. They sing them when a ship comes into harbor, so the drowned will know to make room." A pause. "They're making room."

"For whom?"

Wren didn't answer, and Bram decided she was grateful for that.

They waded on. The water rose—thigh, waist—and the galleries narrowed and the ceiling came down and the space closed around them like a fist, and Bram fought the old panic with every step, the eight-year-old screaming somewhere inside her, the taste of cistern water in the back of her throat. She counted her breaths. She watched the lantern flame. She kept Wren high on her hip and Corvane's gray shape close at her elbow and she did not look back at the black water behind them, because she had the sudden unshakeable certainty that if she looked, she would see faces.

In the third gallery the water reached their chests, and the third fish was gone, swallowed whole, and Doss's marker was a drowned god now, and there was no turning back because turning back meant swimming a quarter mile of flooded tunnel with a child in tow. Forward was the only direction left. Bram had been in corners like this before—in storms, in betrayals, in the long years of her exile—and she knew the truth of them: sometimes the only way out is through, and sometimes through is all you have.

The sump nearly beat them.

It was a low arch where two galleries met, and the water under it was deeper than her memories claimed—over her head by a foot, black and cold as a grave—and she stood at its edge with the lantern guttering and made herself say it aloud.

"I have to dive it. With her."

"I'll take her," Corvane said instantly.

"No." Wren's arms locked around Bram's neck. Small, fierce, immovable. "No. I stay with Bram."

"Wren—"

"She stays with me." Bram heard her own voice come out with storm in it, the old authority rising unbidden, and Corvane—marshal, veteran, a woman who had commanded hundreds—went quiet and nodded. Some orders outrank rank. "Breathe deep. Follow my bubbles. It's fifteen feet, maybe twenty. On the other side the floor climbs."

She filled her lungs until her ribs ached, felt Wren do the same against her shoulder, felt Corvane draw breath behind her, and dove.

The dark underwater was total and absolute. Cold closed over her like a mouth. She kicked for the far side with the child crushed to her chest, counting strokes, and somewhere in the middle of the count—somewhere in the blind, crushing, airless middle—she felt a hand.

Not Wren's. Not Corvane's. A hand in the water, large and cold and gentle, laid flat against her back between her shoulder blades, pushing her upward, helping her, guiding her through the black, and she did not scream because she had no air to scream with, and by the time her head broke the surface on the far side, gasping, hauling Wren up onto the rising slope of the gallery floor, the hand was gone and there was nothing in the water behind them but ripples.

Corvane surfaced beside her, coughing. "What—" she managed. "Something touched me. Something—"

"I know," Bram said.

"What was it?"

Bram looked at Wren. The child was soaked and shaking and her eyes were enormous in the lantern light, and they were not afraid. They were wet with something else entirely.

"He said sorry," Wren whispered. "The one who pushed us. He said he was a gate-warden, when he was alive. He said he's sorry they have to come through the city. He said—" her voice caught, "—he said they'll try to be careful."

The lantern hissed. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, keeping time like a patient clock. Corvane stood in the shallows with water streaming from her gray wool and her face gone the color of ash, and Bram put her free hand on the marshal's shoulder and felt her trembling.

"Welcome to Aelmarrow," Bram said quietly. "Come on. The ladder's close."

* * *

The grate behind the chandler's row opened onto an alley that smelled of tallow and woodsmoke and living human noise, and Bram emerged from the undercity into Vhalcrest like a diver surfacing—blinking, breathless, ears ringing with the ordinary roar of a city at night. Watch bells. Dogs. A drunk singing two streets over. Somewhere a woman shouting children indoors, sharp with fear.

Overhead, the sky above the walls glowed orange with too many fires, and the watch-bells were ringing a pattern Bram hadn't heard since she was a girl: the seal-pattern, the all-shut, the nobody-in-nobody-out. And beneath it, fainter, from the seaward dark, came the other sound—the one the whole city was pretending not to hear.

The dead were closer than two days now. Bram could feel it in her storm-blood, in the pressure drop along the coast, in the way the air itself seemed to lean east. A night, perhaps. Two at the outside. And the Court's fleet sat in the harbor like wolves at a door that was already buckling.

Corvane straightened her pilgrim's hood. "Temple Ward's half a mile," she said. "We move like pilgrims, we keep our eyes down, and we get to the Order's safehouse before the curfew patrol finds us. Then we plan."

But Wren had gone rigid at Bram's side. The child stood in the mouth of the alley with the firelight on her face, staring down the street toward the harbor, and the ember-warmth of her was suddenly blazing, sudden as a struck match, hot enough that Bram felt it through her soaked clothes.

"Bram," Wren whispered. "Look."

Down at the end of the street, where the lane opened toward the water, a figure stood in the torchlight. One figure, alone, where no one should be—past the sealed gates, past the patrols, past every wall the city had raised. It was tall and salt-white and it wore the rotted honor-guard livery of a kingdom erased from every map, and it did not move, and it did not threaten. It stood very still, the way a sentry stands, and as Bram stared, it raised one dripping hand—not in warning, not in hunger—and pointed, deliberate and unmistakable, toward the palace on its hill above the city.

Toward the throne room.

Toward the king.

"They're showing us the way," Wren said. Her voice was the drowned tongue and the common tongue at once, doubled and resonant, a chord rather than a note. "They've been carrying us through the water all night. They opened the roads. Bram—they're not just marching on the city." The child turned her face up, and her eyes held the green-black gleam of tide-pools, of deep places, of the last light going down into the sea.

"They're escorting us."
