## 23. The Hollow Audience

The pilgrim's robe itched like a lie.

Bram had worn many disguises in ten years of smuggling—toll-keeper's grey, a dead man's furs, once the vestments of a river-priest whose god she did not believe in—but none of them had ever felt this much like a costume. The undyed wool hung wrong on her shoulders, too clean, too new. Pilgrims wore their faith into the fabric; hers was borrowed from a Vhalcrest stall that smelled of dye and coin.

"Stop tugging at the hem," Corvane murmured beside her. The marshal had traded her steel cuirass for a pilgrim's hood and a walking staff, and she carried both with the same unbending posture, as though discipline were a garment that could not be removed. "You look like a woman wearing a robe. That is the point."

"I look like a smuggler wearing a robe."

"You look like every penitent who ever crawled up the King's Road to beg the throne for mercy." Corvane's eyes moved beneath her hood, cataloguing the crowd without seeming to. "Grief makes everyone anonymous. That is why the Court allows pilgrimage week. A city full of weeping strangers sees nothing, remembers nothing, and pays its devotions to a king it has never truly looked at."

*That,* Bram thought, *is precisely what frightens me.*

They came up out of the smugglers' tunnels at dawn through the mouth of an old drainage culvert behind a tannery, and the smell of Vhalcrest hit her like a hand across the face—tar and smoke and rotting fish, the reek of a harbor city pretending it was not drowning. Ten years since she had breathed it. Ten years since they had stripped her rank, burned her sigil, and put her on a cart east with nothing but the clothes she stood in and a brand of shame no one could see but everyone in the Vigilant Order knew how to read.

She had told herself she would feel nothing coming back. She had been wrong about many things in her life, but rarely so completely, so generously wrong.

The streets were full. Pilgrims flowed toward the palace district in a slow grey river, thousands of them, hooded and hollow-eyed, carrying candles that guttered in the sea-wind off the bay. And everywhere among them, quiet as frost forming, the Court's riders: black-lacquered armor, helms shaped like breaking waves, faces hidden. They did not push the crowd or shout orders. They simply stood at the corners, motionless, and the crowd bent around them the way water bends around stones.

"They know something's wrong," Bram said under her breath. "Look at the rooftops. They've doubled the watch since the fleet burned."

"The Grandmaster's death bought us three days of confusion," Corvane said. "No more. Half the Order believes he died of a seizure. The other half knows better and is afraid to say so. Either way, the Court's hunters are looking for a storm-caller, a disgraced marshal, and a child." Her voice did not change at all when she said *a child*, which was how Bram knew it cost her. "So we are none of those things today. We are two pilgrims from the salt marches, come to see the Hollow King and pray for the tide to turn. Can you weep on command?"

"I've had practice."

* * *

The palace of Vhalcrest had been built by men who wanted to say *we are not the sea*—white stone, high and dry on its bluff above the harbor, all sharp angles and proud towers. But the sea had been saying otherwise for three hundred years. Salt stained the lower walls in long dark tears. The gates were banded with iron gone orange with rust, and the moat—Bram had not known there was a moat—was full now, brimming black and still, though there had been no rain in nine days.

The pilgrims queued along the processional way in their thousands, and the queue moved at the pace of grief, which is to say barely at all. Hours passed. The sun climbed and the wind came off the water carrying that smell again, that wrongness, salt where salt should not be. Bram heard it in the crowd before she understood what she was hearing: people praying. Not the formal prayers of the temples, but small, frightened, private ones. *Let the wells run sweet. Let my father's grave stay shut. Let the dead sleep.*

A woman ahead of them held a candle that would not light. She struck flint five times, six, and the wick only smoked. When she finally looked up at Bram, her eyes were red-rimmed and terribly calm. "They won't take flame near the palace," she said, as if reporting weather. "Since the tides turned. The priests say it's nothing. The priests say a great many things."

Corvane's hand found Bram's arm. "Do you feel that?" she breathed.

Bram had been feeling it for an hour and had been telling herself it was memory, dread, the old city pressing on old scars. But it was none of those things. It was in her blood, in the storm-channels of her body that had lain silent for ten years and had woken screaming the day she called the thunder down on the Court's riders. It was a pressure, a vast slow suction, as if something enormous was breathing in and had not yet breathed out.

And threaded through it, faint as a hairline crack in glass—

*singing.*

Not aloud. Not with any throat. But Wren's song had lived in Bram's ears for months now, in the waystation and on the road, in the whiteout and the dark, and she knew its shape the way she knew her own heartbeat. Someone—or something—was making the child sing, and the song was being pulled thin, drawn out like thread from a spindle.

"She's here," Bram said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Under the palace. They're using her."

"That was always the plan," Corvane said quietly. "Ours and theirs. The only question left is who gets there first."

* * *

The Hall of Tides had been the throne room of living kings. Now the pilgrims filed through it in silence, down a corridor of pillars carved with waves, past braziers that held no fire, into a vast dim space where the ceiling disappeared into shadow and the floor was black stone polished by three centuries of shuffling feet.

Bram kept her head bowed as she had seen the others do. She let the crowd carry her forward, one step, another, a bead on a string of the grieving. And then, because she had come too far and lost too much to be a coward about it, she raised her eyes.

The throne sat on a dais of black basalt, and it was drowned.

Not metaphorically. Seawater sheeted down its steps in a slow, endless cascade that fell from nowhere and pooled nowhere, flowing and never rising, as if the room itself drank it. Salt crusts bloomed on the armrests like coral. And upon the throne, in the falling water, sat the king.

He had been a tall man once—the frame was still there, broad shoulders, a soldier's hands resting open on his knees. But he sat wrong. He sat the way a coat sits on a hook. His head was bowed as if in prayer, and his crown—a thing of black iron worked into the shapes of drowned hands, reaching up, always reaching—sat fused to his skull, the metal grown into the skin at brow and temple, roots and branches of it disappearing into his flesh behind the ears.

His eyes were open.

They were the worst thing Bram had ever seen, and she had seen the risen dead standing in wheat fields, patient as scarecrows. His eyes were tide-pools. Not like tide-pools—she searched for the comparison and failed, because there was none. They were actual pools, small round seas of grey-green water set in a man's face, and they moved. Things drifted in them. Once, as the line of pilgrims shuffled closer, something pale turned over in the left eye, slowly, the way a body turns over in deep water, and settled again.

Around the throne, motionless as the Court riders outside, stood twelve figures in black lacquer. The King's Own. Their helms were shaped like breaking waves, and beneath the helms, where faces should have been, there was only smooth dark glass.

And beside the throne, caged—

Bram's heart stopped. She made herself keep walking, made her face stay empty, made her breath come slow, because the King's Own did not move but the crown might see, whatever passed for seeing in that fused iron, and she could not—could *not*—give herself away.

They had built the cage of glass and black iron and set it on the dais at the throne's right hand, close enough that the falling water misted its bars. Inside, small and still, sat Wren.

They had washed her. That was the detail that broke something in Bram's chest and made her want to scream until the pillars fell. They had washed the mud and road-dust out of the child's hair and dressed her in a little gown of sea-green silk, and she sat with her hands folded in her lap like a doll arranged for a shelf, and she was singing.

Her lips moved. No sound crossed the cage. But Bram felt it in her teeth, in the marrow of her arms, in the silent storm-channels of her blood—that thread being drawn, and drawn, and drawn. The whole hall hummed with it. The pilgrims around her swayed slightly, eyes glazed, mouths slack, and Bram realized the song was doing something to them too, some low tide of compulsion pulling at every mind in the room, smoothing fear into obedience, and that only her storm-blood and Corvane's iron discipline were holding them upright against it.

Wren's eyes were open. They swept the hall, dull, distant—and then, for half a heartbeat, they found Bram.

Nothing changed in the child's face. Nothing could change; the Court's watchers were everywhere, and Wren had survived eight years alone in the mountains by knowing when to be stone. But her folded hands shifted, just slightly, just enough—one thumb crossing over the other, twice, twice again.

The signal they had invented on the road, in the dark of a smuggler's campfire, for exactly this: *I see you. I am here. Do not be foolish.*

Bram's eyes burned. She bowed her head with the rest of the penitents and let the grief pour through her and out, invisible, indistinguishable from worship.

*I'm here, little bird. Hold on. I am going to burn the sky down before I leave without you.*

* * *

They were herded past the dais, close—close enough that Bram could have touched the cage if the King's Own had not stood between, close enough to see that Wren's wrists bore thin marks where bindings had been, close enough to smell the salt rolling off the throne in cold waves, and then the moment came that Bram would remember for the rest of her life, however long the Court allowed that to be.

As the crowd's slow river bent around the dais, the king lifted his head.

It was a small movement. A dying man's movement, joints cracking like ship's timbers, the crown's iron fingers dragging at his skull. His tide-pool eyes rose from the floor and swept the line of pilgrims, and where they passed, people gasped and clutched their candles and some of them wept, feeling themselves *seen* by whatever wore their king.

The eyes reached Bram.

And stopped.

For three heartbeats—for five—the grey-green pools fixed on her hooded face, and in their depths the drifting pale things went still, all of them at once, like fish hearing a net. The pressure in the air tightened. The humming thread of Wren's song faltered, caught, and somewhere inside the glass cage Bram heard—not with her ears, with her bones—a small sharp intake of breath.

Then the tide-pools moved on. The king's head sank back onto his chest. The cascading water fell and fell and fell, and the line of pilgrims shuffled forward, and no one else in the hall had seen anything at all.

But Bram had felt it, in the instant those dead seas had looked at her: not the Hunger. Something else. Something buried so deep beneath the parasite's attention that it had needed the distraction of a thousand minds to surface for even a moment—a flicker, a drowning man's hand breaking water, and through it, fainter than memory, older than the crown:

*grief.*

The king had looked at her and grieved.

"He saw me," she whispered to Corvane as they passed out of the hall into the grey afternoon, into the crowd, into the noise and safety of numbers. Her hands were shaking. She clenched them. "Corvane. Whatever wears him—it didn't see me. *He* did. For one breath, he was in there, and he was trying to tell me something."

Corvane walked on steadily, staff clicking on stone, face forward. Only her voice betrayed her, low and rapid beneath the crowd's murmur. "Then the courtier's tale was true. The man beneath can still reach the surface—if the thing that wears him is distracted, or if it sleeps, or if—" She stopped herself. Drew breath. "We cannot plan around a maybe. We have the egg, we have the spark, we have perhaps two days before the Order's politics catch up with us and the Court stops searching and starts finding. The rite must happen in that throne room, and to reach the throne room we need—"

She stopped again.

This time it was not caution that stopped her. Bram followed her gaze and felt the bottom drop out of her stomach.

Across the processional square, at the head of a column of black riders, stood a figure in white and gold, and even at fifty paces, even hooded, Bram knew the silhouette: Grand Marshal Veyra, the Order's second blade, the hardliner who had voted for Bram's execution nineteen years ago and had wept with rage when the council chose exile instead. Veyra was scanning the pilgrim crowds with the unhurried thoroughness of a woman reading a page she intended to memorize.

And beside her, on a lead of silver chain, snuffling at the cobblestones, paced a lean grey hound with its nose to the ground.

Tracking. Not a scent of smoke or steel.

Storm-scent. The smell of rain called and thunder answered. The smell of Bram.

Veyra's head turned toward their side of the square.

"Walk," Corvane said softly. "Don't run. Walk, and pray with your face, and whatever happens—"

Behind them, across the square, a voice like a drawn sword rang out over ten thousand pilgrims:

"There. The woman in the grey robe. *Seize her.*"
