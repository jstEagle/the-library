## 24. The Man Beneath

The safehouse smelled of tallow and old blood.

Ilsa Corvane had chosen it herself—a cooper's loft above a shuttered chandlery in the Saltward district of Vhalcrest, where the streets ran narrow as knife-cuts and the harbor fog came up through the cobbles like breath through teeth. It was the kind of place the Vigilant Order kept in every city: unlisted, unpaid for on any ledger that survived an audit, known to perhaps four living people. She had been one of those four for eleven years, and she had never once needed it until now.

Now she stood over a dying man and wished she had never learned what she was about to learn.

His name was Aldous Renne. He had been Third Secretary to the Lord Chamberlain of the Hollow Court for thirty-one years, which meant he had spent three decades memorizing which silences meant danger and which meant merely boredom. He lay now on a pallet of sacking beneath the loft's round window, and the fog-light came down through the glass in a gray column and made him look already like something carved. His doublet—court silk, dove-gray, worth more than this whole street—was soaked dark from collar to hip where the Court's surgeons had opened him and the Court's assassins had closed him again, badly, on purpose.

"He'll last the night," Bram Kavanagh said from the doorway. She had her arms crossed and her weight on her heels, the stance of a woman who had decided not to care and was failing at it. "The blade went under the ribs but missed anything that kills quick. Whoever did it knew their work. They wanted him to walk home first. Wanted everyone to see how he walked."

"Why?" Corvane asked, though she suspected she knew.

"Because a man who bleeds slowly tells everyone who sees him that questions were asked." Kavanagh shrugged. "Smugglers do it with informants. Kings do it with secretaries. Same trade, better tailors."

On the pallet, Renne laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and thin, more cough than mirth, but it was unmistakably laughter. "The smuggler," he rasped, "understands the court perfectly. Madam Marshal. You shouldn't have come. If they learn you pulled me from the gutter—"

"They won't." Corvane knelt beside him. Up close, the man smelled of iodine and river water and the peculiar sweetness of a body beginning to fail. "You sent word to my dead-drop in the Weavers' Quarter. Three words, written in your own hand: *the king weeps.* I burned the note. Now you will tell me what it meant, and then I will decide whether you should have sent it."

Renne's eyes found hers. They were pale eyes, the color of watered milk, and they held the particular steadiness of a man who had finished being afraid some hours ago and was now simply curious how the story ended.

"You are Grandmaster Haleth's creature," he said. "Everyone says so. The marshal who does the ugly errands and asks nothing."

"I ask everything," Corvane said. "I just don't flinch at the answers."

"Then don't flinch at this one." He drew a breath that whistled. "The king weeps. Every night. In the west gallery, between the second bell and the third, when the guards change and the corridor is empty for ninety heartbeats. I have heard him. Thirty years I have walked that corridor after the household sleeps, and for twenty-eight of them there was nothing in the throne wing but cold and that smell—the salt smell, the tide-pool smell, the smell that gets into your clothes and never washes out. But twice this year—" his hand twitched against the sacking, "—twice, I heard weeping. A man's weeping. Old-fashioned. The kind they taught gentlemen in the old century, quiet, behind the teeth. And a voice saying a name over and over. *Lirienne.* Lirienne. Lirienne."

The name landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. Corvane heard Kavanagh shift behind her, heard the child—no. The child was not here. She had insisted on that; Wren slept two streets away under the guard of two wardens Corvane trusted with her life, which was to say, under guard she watched herself.

"Lirienne," Corvane repeated. "That is a name out of the archives. Out of Aelmarrow."

"It is the name of the king's daughter," Renne said. "Princess Lirienne Vael, born in the two-hundredth year of the Salt Throne, drowned at nine years old when the sea took the kingdom. Dead three hundred years, madam. And something inside the Hollow King remembers her well enough to say her name."

* * *

Corvane had read every surviving document concerning the Hollow Court. That was her work, her obsession, her particular damnation. She knew the official histories: King Aurelian Vael, last of the Aelmarrow line, had fled the drowning of his kingdom with whatever remnants of his court could fit aboard six ships, and had come to Vhalcrest as a supplicant and been crowned again by a frightened people who wanted a king who understood the sea's anger. She knew the unofficial version, the one the Order's founders had scratched into lead tablets and hidden behind a false wall in the third subvault: that Aurelian had arrived already wrong, already hollow-eyed, wearing a crown he would not remove even to sleep, and that within a decade the man who had begged Vhalcrest for shelter had become something the courtiers spoke of only in whispers—a presence that did not age, did not eat in public, whose gaze left men feeling wrung out like laundry.

Three hundred years. Eleven kings had reigned in Vhalcrest since. The thing on the throne had worn them all—or rather, wore one man stretched across centuries, because the Court's own genealogists, when pressed, could produce no credible record of succession. There was only ever Aurelian. Aurelian the Suppliant. Aurelian the Patient. Aurelian the Ever-Crowned. The portraits changed—the face grew subtly longer, the skin grayer, the eyes paler—but the seal, the crown, the name remained.

And now a dying secretary said the thing wept and called its daughter's name.

"That is not possible," Corvane said, and hated how much she wanted it to be possible.

"No," Renne agreed mildly. "It isn't. And yet." He turned his head with great effort, and the fog-light caught the sweat on his face. "Madam Marshal, I have served the Court since I was nineteen. I have falsified census rolls so the Crown could take children from the coastal parishes—I told myself it was conscription, that they lived. I have signed the orders that emptied villages. I have done everything a man can do and still call himself a servant rather than a monster. And I am telling you, with the little time I have left to tell anyone anything: there is a man under that thing. There has always been a man. I have seen him."

"Seen him." Kavanagh's voice, flat. "Through what, exactly? The throne room doors?"

"Through the king himself." Renne smiled, and it was the worst expression Corvane had ever seen on a human face—not cruelty, but wonder, arriving far too late to be any use. "Two winters ago I was summoned to attend him at night. A clerical matter—the tithe rolls from the Saltward, nothing. I was brought into the private audience chamber behind the throne, and he was standing at the window with his back to me, and the crown was off."

Silence. Even the fog seemed to lean closer against the windows.

"He set it down," Renne whispered. "On a cushion of black cloth, the way you'd put down something that bites. And for perhaps forty breaths, before the chamberlain announced me, I saw him without it. Do you understand? Without it. And he was—weeping. Standing at the window looking down at the harbor, and weeping, and his face—" the secretary's voice cracked, "—his face was *ordinary.* Tired. Middle-aged, or what would be middle-aged if a man had lived three hundred years. He looked like a father. He looked like a man remembering a child, and I thought, whoever you were, whoever did this to you, God under the salt forgive us all, because we let it happen. We fed it. Every soul the Grandmaster sends up those stairs, we fed it, and underneath it all there is a man who misses his daughter."

Corvane rose and walked to the window. It was habit, the marshal's habit—never stand still while thinking, never give a room your back entirely—and also she did not trust her face just then. Below, the Saltward lanes glistened. Somewhere a ship's bell counted the hour, and the sound came up muffled, like a voice through water.

"The archives," she said finally. "There is a rite. Fragmentary. The founders called it the Unbinding—separation of vessel from tenant. Every attempt to reconstruct it fails at the same point. The binding is not anchored to the crown alone. It is anchored to the *wearer*. To his consent, however coerced. You cannot cut a glove off a hand that will not open." She turned back. "If there is truly a man in there—if his will is not wholly gone—then the rite becomes possible. Not easy. Possible."

"And how," Kavanagh said, "do you propose to reach a man buried three hundred years deep? Knock politely? Ask the Hunger to step aside while we have a word?"

Renne laughed again, that dreadful bubbling laugh. "Oh, the smuggler is cruel. Good. Cruelty is honest." He struggled onto one elbow; Corvane moved to steady him and felt how little of him remained, bones wrapped in silk. "You reach him the way you reach any drowning man. You throw him the thing he loves. The girl."

Kavanagh went very still. Corvane saw it happen—the whole body tightening like a drawn bow, the storm-gray eyes going flat and bright at once.

"Carefully, secretary," Kavanagh said softly.

"I mean no harm to the child. Hear me out, because I am dying and I have no patience left for circling." Renne's breath rattled. "The girl carries dragon-fire memory. The Hunger feeds on it—that is why it wants her, why it has hunted vessels of the old blood for three centuries and only lately found one strong enough to matter. But memory runs both ways along that current. She dreams the kingdom's death because the fire remembers it. Which means she can carry things *into* the dark as well as out. Show the buried king his daughter—not the fact of her, the Order's histories have facts enough—but the *feeling* of her. Her voice. Her laugh. Let it pass through the girl's fire into the hollow where a man used to be, and see if he does not turn toward it. See if he does not remember that he once had hands, and hands can open."

"A child," Kavanagh said, each word laid down like a blade on a table, "as a key into the mind of the thing that is eating her."

"As a lamp," Renne countered, "carried toward a man lost in a cellar. Yes. And yes, there is risk, and yes, I am asking you to spend the last innocent thing in this story, and I will not pretend otherwise. I am a wicked old man asking brave women to do a hard thing. That is what courts make of everyone, in the end." He sagged back against the sacking, spent. "But consider the alternative, madam Marshal. Your Order's plan is to kill the vessel and hope the tenant starves. Kill the king, break the body, and pray the Hunger dissipates rather than leaps to the nearest warm throat. You know what the archives say about leaping."

Corvane did know. The Hunger Below had been bound once before, by the last dragons of Aelmarrow, at a cost that had ended their species. Binding was possible. Killing was not. The thing predated nations; it had worn priests before it wore kings. Destroy the vessel and it would simply wait, patient as tide, for the next open door—and there was no door in Vhalcrest more open than a child full of waking fire.

"There is another thing," Renne said. His voice had begun to fade, words coming slower, spaced like stones across a flood. "The reason they cut me. I found something in the household rolls. An entry, three hundred years old, copied forward by some dutiful clerk who never read what he copied. The princess Lirienne did not drown with the city." A pause; a struggle for air. "She died before. A fever, the winter the sea first turned strange. The king's grief was the door. Understand? The thing did not conquer him. It *comforted* him. It came to him in the shape of consolation—it promised him she was not gone, that memory could be kept alive forever, that he need never lose her if he only never took off the crown. And he was grieving, and he was a king, and kings are the loneliest creatures God ever made, and he said yes."

The fog-light had gone the color of pewter. Down in the lane, a drunk was singing something tuneless and far away.

"So when you reach him," Renne whispered, "when the girl's fire carries her down into that cellar—you will not find a prisoner who needs convincing to resist. You will find a mourner who must be persuaded to stop being comforted. That is harder. Grief is the oldest chain there is. But it is also the only chain the man forged himself. Which means it is the only one he can unlock."

He looked from Corvane to Kavanagh and back, and his milk-pale eyes were clear as a boy's.

"Tell her," he said. "Tell the child about the princess. Not the history—the girl. What she loved. How she laughed. Let the fire carry it true. And then get out of the way, and pray, if either of you prays, that three hundred years of drowning has not washed away the one thing worth saving in that palace."

* * *

He lasted until the fourth bell, as Kavanagh had predicted. At the end he asked for water, drank it in small careful sips like a man conserving something more precious than water, and then asked—almost shyly—for pen and paper. He wrote for ten minutes, in the beautiful clerk's hand of a lifetime's training: names, dates, the rotation of the throne-wing guard, the location of a postern stair behind the tapestry of the Ninefold Tide that the household had forgotten and the Order never known. Then he sealed it, addressed it to no one, and handed it to Corvane.

"For whichever of you lives longest," he said. "Burn it if you like. But I was a keeper of records, madam. Let me end as one."

He died a little before dawn, quietly, mid-breath, as if stepping between rooms. Kavanagh closed his eyes with two fingers, briskly, the way you'd close a gate.

They stood in the cooper's loft while the fog outside turned from gray to the faint bruised silver that comes before light, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

"He's right," Kavanagh said at last. Her voice was rough. "That's the worst of it. He's right, and I hate him for it, and I'd hate myself worse if I pretended otherwise." She turned, and in the half-dark her face was all angles and exhaustion. "You're asking me to walk my child into the mind of the thing that's killing her. Because there might be a man in there. Because grief might be a door instead of a grave."

"I'm not asking yet," Corvane said. "I'm telling you what a dying man told me. There's a difference, and I mean to keep it. Wren is yours, Kavanagh. Whatever the Order says, whatever the rite requires—she is yours first. If you say no, we find another road, even if the other road is worse. I'd have you know that plainly."

Kavanagh studied her for a long moment, the way she probably studied weather, reading what was coming in signs too small for anyone else to see.

"And if I say yes?" she said. "If I sit my eight-year-old down and tell her about a dead princess and ask her to dream someone else's grief? What happens to her, Corvane? What does carrying a drowned king's sorrow do to a child who already carries a kingdom's?"

"I don't know," Corvane said. It cost her to say it, and she let it show, because Kavanagh deserved truth over comfort. "But I know what happens if we don't. The flame gutters. The binding fails. And the Hunger walks out of that palace into a world with no dragons left to stop it."

Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor, and the first light caught the distant bulk of the palace on its rock above the waterline—black stone, black windows, and somewhere in its western gallery, if a dying secretary could be believed, a man three hundred years drowned was weeping his daughter's name into the dark.

Kavanagh looked at it a long time. When she spoke again, her voice was very quiet.

"She asked me yesterday," she said, "what the word *father* means. She's never had one to point at. So I suppose—" her jaw tightened, "—I suppose we start there. With the princess. With what she loved. Gods forgive us both."

Corvane nodded and turned to go, and that was when the knock came at the loft's rear stair—one of their wardens, white-faced, snow in his hair, breathing like a man who had run the whole way from the chandlery.

"Marshal," he gasped. "The girl. You'd better come. She's burning up—and the salt—there's salt coming out of her skin, and she keeps saying a name none of us knows. *Lirienne.* Over and over. Like she's answering someone."
