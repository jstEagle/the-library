## 25. Dragon-Fire Guttering

The candle in Wren's chest was going out.

Bram had no better way to think of it, though she tried, that first grey morning after the courtier's death, to find some other image less terrible. A lamp running low. A hearth banked for the night. But every metaphor failed her the moment she took the child's small hand in hers and felt what moved—or failed to move—beneath the skin. Warmth had lived there once, a heat like a held breath, like a stone pulled from a fire and wrapped in wool. Now it flickered. It stuttered. And when it stuttered, Wren's whole body stuttered with it, a tremor that ran from her fingers up through her thin arms and settled behind her eyes like a bird beating itself against glass.

They were hiding still, three days after the dying courtier Halvene had pressed his secret into Corvane's hands and his last breath into the dark of the safehouse cellar. The smugglers' war had begun in the harbor—Bram could hear it distantly even now, muffled by stone and street: the crack of burning pitch, the shouts of men whose loyalties had finally chosen sides. Somewhere above them, Corvane was turning half the Vigilant Order against its own Grandmaster. Somewhere beyond the walls, the drowned dead of Aelmarrow walked in their silent thousands toward a city that had forgotten them.

And here, in a cellar that smelled of tallow and old wine, the smallest person Bram had ever loved was dimming like a lamp left too long untrimmed.

"Tell me again," Bram said. "What it feels like."

Wren considered this with the grave patience she brought to all questions about herself, as if her own body were a country she was only beginning to map. She was sitting cross-legged on the pallet they'd dragged down from the storeroom, wrapped in two blankets because she could no longer make enough heat for one. Her hair had gone lank. Her eyes, which had always carried that unsettling depth—the tide-pool look, Bram called it privately—seemed shallower now, as if something were draining out of them from below.

"Cold," Wren said at last, in Common. Her Common had grown fluent these past months, quick and sly, but when she was tired she reached for the drowned tongue first, and Bram had learned to hear the shape of it under the words. "Not cold like snow. Cold like... when the tide goes out very far, and you stand where the water was, and the sand is wet but the sun is gone." She frowned, hunting. "The fire is being *drunk*."

*Drunk.* The word landed in Bram's stomach like a dropped anchor.

"Not burned," she said carefully. "Drunk."

Wren nodded. She lifted her hand, palm up, and for a moment—a heartbeat, no more—the old light came, that faint gold shimmer beneath her skin that Bram had seen on the night at the river, on the mountain, in a hundred small moments since. Dragon-fire memory, Corvane had called it. The last living vessel of a kingdom's burning heart. Then the shimmer guttered, thinned, and went out like a snuffed wick, and Wren's arm dropped into her lap as if the effort had cost her a mile of walking.

"It pulls," she whispered. "When I sleep, it pulls hardest. Like a mouth. Under everything. Under the sea." Her eyes came up, and they were frightened in a way that had nothing childlike in it. "It's learning my name, Bram."

* * *

Corvane arrived an hour past dusk, smelling of smoke and horse and other things Bram chose not to name. She came down the cellar ladder fast, no ceremony, and stopped dead when she saw Wren.

Bram watched the marshal take inventory the way soldiers do—the pallor, the tremor, the blankets—and watched the calculation behind her eyes arrive somewhere grim. Corvane had lost weight since Vhalcrest. The Order's civil war was writing itself on her face in lines that hadn't been there a month ago. But her voice, when she spoke, was level as ever.

"How long has the shimmer been failing?"

"Three days," Bram said. "Faster each time."

"Show me your hands, little one."

Wren obeyed. Corvane knelt, took the child's fingers in her gauntleted grip with surprising gentleness, and held them up to the lantern. Bram saw it too then, what she'd been refusing to see: faint traceries beneath the skin of the wrist, pale as frost-ferns, spreading upward from the pulse points. Where they touched skin, the warmth did not return.

"What are those?" Bram asked. Her voice came out flat and hard, smuggler-flat, the tone she used when the alternative was shouting.

"Frost-marks." Corvane set the hand down carefully, as one sets down something borrowed. "I've seen them once before. In the archives. On illustrations of the last binding rite." She paused, and Bram heard the pause for what it was—a soldier deciding how much truth a civilian can carry. "They appear when the Hunger feeds directly. Not through the crown. Through the vessel."

"Meaning what? Say it plainly, Ilsa. I've no patience left for mercy."

Corvane looked at her, and something in her face—the thing that was not quite kindness, not quite apology—cracked open a degree.

"The crown has been drinking dragon-fire through the king for three hundred years, slowly, the way a leech feeds. But a leech is patient. The Hunger is not patient anymore. It has Wren close now, caged beside the throne, and it is feeding *directly*. It doesn't need the king as an intermediary. Every hour, it takes more." She straightened. "At the current rate, the flame gutters out entirely in—" she calculated, visibly, hating the arithmetic, "—four days. Perhaps five."

"Four days," Bram repeated.

"And when it goes out," Corvane said, "the binding dies with it. The Hunger Below goes unbound. No vessel, no cage. It rises, and it does not rise alone—it takes the sea with it. Every coast from Thornfell to the Saltmarshes. Vhalcrest first. Everything the drowned dead have been marching toward will be underwater, and this time nothing will ever surface again."

* * *

Later, when Wren slept—badly, twitching, her breath coming in shallow catches as though something leaned on her chest—Bram climbed the ladder and found Corvane in the safehouse's narrow kitchen, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. The rhythm of it filled the silence so neither woman had to.

"Four days," Bram said.

"Five, if we're lucky."

"I don't believe in luck. I believed in weather, once, and look what that cost me." Bram sat across from her, laid her forearms on the table. The scarred wood was sticky with salt—everything in Vhalcrest was sticky with salt now; it came up through the stones, it frosted the windows, it lay on the tongue like grief. "So we go in four days. We take the palace, we start the rite, we pull her out."

"The rite requires the egg's fire already woken and in your blood," Corvane said. "You have that. It also requires proximity to the throne, an unbroken circle, and time. Time measured in hours, Bram, while the Court's remaining riders, the Grandmaster's loyalists, and whatever the Hunger sends up from the water all try to stop you. And you'd be doing it with the vessel already half-drained. The rite doesn't just need her present. It needs her *burning*. A guttering flame may not be enough to hold the separation. If the king and the crown come apart wrong—"

"Then what?"

"Then the king dies hollow, the Hunger escapes anyway, and Wren's death bought nothing." Corvane set the knife down. For a moment the marshal's composure slipped, and Bram saw underneath it the woman who had freed her from a traitor's cell against direct orders, who had stood in a burning gatehouse and chosen rebellion with her sword drawn. "There is another way. A worse one."

"Say it."

"If the flame cannot be preserved, it can be *moved*. Poured out of her before the Hunger drinks the last of it—into the egg, or into you, or into the earth itself at the binding-stone. It would save the binding. It might even starve the Hunger's feast short. But it would empty her. Whatever she is—the memories, the drowned tongue, the girl—all of it is woven through that fire. Pour it out and what's left sitting in that cellar is a child-shaped absence." Corvane met her eyes. "I won't order it. I'm telling you it exists, because you deserve to know the shape of every door before you choose one."

Bram sat very still. Outside, distantly, the harbor burned. Somewhere north, ten thousand dead walked without feet toward a city that owed them a burial. And in the cellar below, a child slept with frost-ferns growing up her wrists, dreaming in a language of a kingdom at the bottom of the sea.

"When I found her," Bram said quietly, "she was mud to the elbows and biting anyone who came near. Eight years old, alone on my doorstep in the worst storm of the winter, speaking words no living throat should know. Everyone since has wanted something *from* her. The Court wants her as a vessel. The Order wanted her as a weapon. The archives want her as a text." She looked up. "I'm the only person alive who wants nothing except that she eats breakfast tomorrow. So no. We don't pour her out. We don't spend her. We get her out *whole*, and we end this thing, and if the arithmetic says four days, then we move in three."

Corvane studied her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the nod of an officer accepting a plan she would have arrived at herself.

"Three days. I'll need until tomorrow night to finish turning the Order—the Grandmaster falls or the gates never open. Your smugglers?"

"Burning the fleet tonight. By dawn the Court has no ships and no way to bring its hunters home." Bram rose. "Which leaves the palace itself. The flooded vaults. My ancestors' bones. The egg." She flexed her right hand, and felt, deep in the marrow, the spark she'd carried since the undercity—the ember of true dragon-fire coiled around her storm-blood like a second pulse. It answered her attention with a slow, warm turn, patient as geology. "I'll be ready."

"One more thing." Corvane hesitated—an ugly word on her. "The frost-marks. There's a passage in the archives I didn't read aloud. About vessels near the end. When the flame runs low, the boundary between the vessel and the Hunger thins. The vessel begins to..." She chose the word with visible care. "*Hear* it. And sometimes, near the very end—to answer."

* * *

Bram went back down the ladder and sat beside the pallet until the small hours, listening to Wren breathe.

Near dawn, the child began to talk in her sleep. Not the restless muttering of ordinary dreams—this was fluent, rhythmic, terrible. The drowned tongue poured out of her in long cadenced lines, and though Bram knew only fragments, she knew enough now to catch the grammar of invocation. Wren was not dreaming. She was *answering*. Some vast attention below the world had spoken her name, and in her sleep, with her flame guttering low, she was speaking back.

Bram took both her hands. They were cold as riverbed stones.

"Wren." She kept her voice low and steady, the voice she used on spooked horses and green smugglers in rockfall country. "Wren. Come back. You're on a pallet in Vhalcrest, in a cellar that smells like bad wine, and I'm here, and I'm not going anywhere. Do you hear me? Whatever's talking to you, it doesn't get to have you. You're mine to look after. That was decided on a doorstep in the snow, and I don't renegotiate."

The cadence faltered. Stuttered. Wren's eyelids fluttered, and for a moment her eyes opened—not the tide-pool depth, just a child's eyes, bleary and confused and eight years old.

"Bram?" she whispered. "It was singing. Down under everything. It sang my whole name, all the names inside my name."

"I know."

"It said if I stop fighting, it will stop being hungry. It said everyone gets to rest if I just—" Her face crumpled. "It sounds so *kind*, Bram. That's the worst part. It sounds kind."

Bram gathered her in, blankets and all, and held her against the shoulder that had carried pack-straps and guilt and ten years of exile, and felt the guttering warmth of her flicker like a moth against her collarbone.

"Kind things that eat children aren't kind," she said into the lank hair. "They're just hungry with good manners. We're going to go down to the water where it lives, and we're going to give it something better to chew on than you. Storms and dragons and every debt my family ever owed. And then, little bird, we're going home up the mountain, and you're going to complain about my cooking for the next fifty years. That's the whole of the bargain. Nothing else gets a vote."

Wren made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and burrowed closer. Within minutes she was asleep again—truly asleep this time, dreamless, or at least silent.

Bram stayed awake, holding her, watching the frost-ferns creep another finger's width up her wrist.

Three days, she thought. Three days, and then the palace of tides, and the throne room, and the thing that wore a king like a glove. She thought of the egg sleeping in the dark beneath her ancestors' bones, of the spark coiled in her blood, of the vision of Aelmarrow's final night that still visited her sometimes between sleep and waking—screams translated into steam, a sky the color of an open wound.

Outside, far off, the sea sighed against the harbor walls. And beneath the sigh, so faint she might have imagined it, Bram heard it too: a song, patient and vast and terribly kind, singing a child's name into the dark.

She closed her eyes and began, silently, to count the hours.
