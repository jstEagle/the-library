## 26. The Smugglers' War

The harbor of Vhalcrest smelled of tar and rot and old salt, and Bram Kavanagh stood in the shadow of a fish-drying shed and thought: *I know this smell. It is the smell of every bad decision I have ever made.*

Below her, across a stretch of cobbled quay slick with herring guts, the Hollow Court's fleet rode at anchor in the grey morning light—eleven warships, black-hulled, their sails furled tight as fists, their rails hung with lanterns that burned a color no honest flame should burn. A cold green-white, like foxfire, like the underside of ice. The Court had never needed ordinary fire. It had something older to burn.

Eleven ships. Enough to carry three hundred riders down the coast, enough to chase a child to the edge of the world if the child ran. Enough, if the binding failed and the sea rose as the archives promised it would, to evacuate exactly the people the Court considered worth saving—which was to say, none.

"They've doubled the watch since last night," murmured Sef Hollis at her shoulder. He was a squat man with a smuggler's beard gone iron-grey and hands like knotted rope, and he had owed Bram a favor for nine years—a favor dating back to a night in the Thornfell passes when she'd called down just enough lightning to blind a customs patrol without cooking anyone. He'd never asked what she was. That was the thing about smugglers. They understood that everyone was something.

"Doubled isn't enough," Bram said. "Doubled means they're frightened. Frightened men watch the water and forget the land."

"And you're the land?"

"I'm worse than the land." She turned from the gap in the shed wall. "I'm the weather."

* * *

They gathered that night in the cellar of the Drowned Gull, a tavern so disreputable it had been raided twice by the harbor watch and once by actual pirates, who had left disappointed. Forty-odd people crowded among the beer barrels and rat-gnawed crates: smugglers, ferrymen, a widow who ran powder up the coast in flour sacks, two brothers who poached oysters off naval beds and knew the harbor bottom better than any admiral alive.

Bram stood on an overturned barrel and looked at them, and felt the weight of what she was about to ask settle onto her chest like wet wool.

These were not soldiers. These were people who lied for a living because lying was safer than the truth, who had spent their whole lives being small and quick and invisible—the same art she had practiced for ten years in the mountains. And she was about to ask them to stop being invisible. To stand up, in the capital itself, against a crown that ate souls.

"You all know why we're here," she said. "Some of you think I'm mad. Good. Mad men survive things sensible men don't." A ripple of rough laughter, quickly smothered. "Here's the shape of it. The Court's fleet sits at anchor waiting for orders to sail south—to hunt a child eight years old. While they wait, they're also the only boats that'll float when the tide comes in. You've seen the wells. You've seen the dead standing in the fields at night, patient as fence posts. When the sea rises—and it will rise—we will want those ships. Not them. Us."

"So we steal them," said the oyster-poacher, a woman named Maren with forearms like a dock crane.

"We can't steal eleven ships. We can *cripple* eleven ships." Bram unrolled a strip of oilcloth across a barrel head, a charcoal map of the harbor she'd drawn from memory and Maren's corrections. "Rudder cables cut below the waterline. Tar and lamp-oil packed into the bilges, set slow. Not fire that roars—fire that *creeps*. By the time they smell smoke, the hulls are already eaten through. And here—" she tapped the great chain that stretched across the harbor mouth, the boom-chain raised each dawn by capstan, "—the chain stays down. No ship leaves this harbor until we say."

Silence. Then Sef Hollis said quietly, "You're asking us to make war on the Crown."

"I'm asking you to choose," Bram said. "That's all anybody's asking anybody these days. The Court chose years ago—they chose to feed people to a crown. The Grandmaster of the Vigilant Order chose too; he's been shipping souls south in secret for decades. Everyone's chosen except us. So choose now, while choosing still costs something. Anyone who walks out that door tonight, I'll never speak your name to a living soul. That's a promise. But if you stay—" she looked around the lamplit cellar, at the seamed faces and scarred hands, "—then we do this together, and whatever comes of it, comes to all of us."

Nobody walked out. One young man near the back began to cry, silently, and stayed anyway. Bram would remember that longer than anything else about the night: not the oaths or the plans, but a boy of maybe nineteen weeping into his sleeve because he had decided, at last, to be someone.

* * *

It took four nights.

Four nights of Maren's people going over the seawall at slack tide with grease-bagged tools, working by touch in water cold enough to stop the heart, cutting rudder cables with saws wrapped in cloth so the sound wouldn't carry. Four nights of the widow's flour sacks—forty pounds of blackpowder and pitch apiece—rolling through the customs gate under the nose of a watch-sergeant who took his bribe and never once wondered why a baker needed so much saltpeter. Four nights of Bram herself walking the quays in a fishwife's shawl, hood low, listening—not with her ears but with the other sense, the one she'd spent ten years strangling. The sky over Vhalcrest was heavy and windless, a held breath, and beneath it she could feel the fleet like eleven hot stones in a cool stream. She could feel, further out past the harbor mouth, something else: a vast slow pressure moving along the coast, patient as tide. The drowned dead were still walking. Some nights, from the high streets, you could see them at the edge of the torchlight, standing in the fields beyond the walls, hundreds of them, facing the palace. Not attacking. *Waiting.* The city had stopped pretending they weren't there. Now it simply locked its doors earlier and prayed louder, and the temples of the Court grew rich on fear.

On the fourth night, everything went wrong, which meant it went right.

A watch patrol came early to the quay—six men with lanterns, checking mooring lines—and found Sef's youngest, a boy called Tam with powder-blackened fingers, halfway up a ladder on the *Stern Mercy*, the flagship. Tam froze. The lead watchman raised his lantern.

And every lantern on the quay went out.

Not blown out. *Taken*—flames bending sideways like grass in wind, then gone, six lights swallowed at once, though the night was dead calm. In the sudden dark, Tam dropped flat behind the rail, and the watchmen stood blinking in a darkness so complete it rang like a bell, and somewhere above them a voice like gravel said, in perfect bored harbor-slang, "Rats again. Big ones. Get the cats."

By the time the watch relit their lanterns from the brazier at the gatehouse, Tam was gone, and the watchman who'd raised the light swore forever afterward that he'd seen, for half a heartbeat, a woman standing on the quay with her hand raised and her eyes full of storm-light—though of course nobody believed him, because everybody in Vhalcrest knew the storm-caller was dead, exiled, a story mothers told.

Bram stood in the doorway of the fish shed afterward, shaking. Ten years she had kept the leash on. Ten years of swallowing every thunderhead, of letting rain fall where it pleased and lightning strike where it listed, saying *not mine, not mine* to a power that had been hers since before she could walk. And now she had reached out in front of six witnesses for one boy worth ten of her, and the sky had answered like a dog hearing its name.

"You're smiling," Sef said beside her.

"I'm terrified."

"Same thing, in my experience." He spat thoughtfully into the dark. "Tomorrow night, then?"

"Tomorrow night," Bram said. "Tomorrow night we burn their navy."

* * *

The fifth night was moonless, which the smugglers called a *smuggler's moon*, and it rose over Vhalcrest like a blessing.

Bram watched from the roof of the customs house, the highest point on the quay, with Maren and a signal lamp shuttered to a coin of light. Below, the harbor lay black and quiet. Eleven ships. Three hundred sleeping riders. Somewhere inland, Wren was hidden in a coal cellar with Corvane's most trusted warden, her dragon-fire guttering so low now that Bram could feel the absence of it like a missing tooth—she hadn't let herself visit in two days because seeing the child's grey lips made her hands shake, and shaking hands did not cut anchor chains.

"Chain crew's in position," Maren murmured. "Capstan's been greased and guarded. Nothing sails."

"Fire crews?"

"Maren's oysters"—that was the brothers—"are swimming. Give them till the third bell."

The third bell rang across the water, soft and bronze, and nothing happened for a long moment, long enough for Bram's heart to climb into her throat. Then, deep in the belly of the nearest ship—the *Salt Oath*, a black-hulled raider—there bloomed a glow like a coal breathed upon. Then another, in the ship beside it. Then another. Not roaring. *Creeping*, just as she'd promised, tar-fed and patient, spreading along keels and up into bilge-wood that had been drinking lamp-oil for two days.

The first shout went up from the *Stern Mercy*'s deck—a single voice, cracked with sleep, screaming about smoke. Then the harbor woke all at once: bells, boots, riders pouring from the barracks with buckets that would do nothing, officers shrieking for the chain to be raised, for the ships to be moved out into open water where they could burn apart from each other—

And the capstan crew, six of Sef's people who had volunteered for the most dangerous post of all, simply *sat down* on the capstan bars and refused. When the Court soldiers came with swords, the smugglers put their hands up and grinned, and one of them—an old poacher named Cobb, seventy if he was a day—said loudly enough for the whole quay to hear: "Raise it yourself, lads. Me back's gone. Been gone thirty years. Terrible shame."

They arrested him. He went laughing. Behind him, the chain stayed down, and eleven ships burned.

Bram lit the signal lamp: three flashes, pause, three flashes. *All crews home.* Around the harbor, shadows detached themselves from walls and doorways and slipped away into the warrens of the undercity, ordinary people going home to their ordinary lives with pitch under their fingernails and a new thing living in their chests. On the water, the *Salt Oath*'s mainmast went over the side with a groan like a dying animal, and the green-white Court-lanterns burst one by one in the heat, spilling light that hurt to look at, and the crowd gathering on the seawall—hundreds of them now, drawn from their houses by the glow—did not cheer and did not help. They just stood there, watching the Crown's navy cook in its own harbor, and Bram read their stillness the way she read weather, and what she read was: *the wind has changed.*

"They'll know it wasn't an accident," Maren said.

"It was never going to look like an accident." Bram shuttered the lamp. "That's rather the point. Let them know. Let the whole city know somebody chose."

* * *

Corvane came to them two hours before dawn, alone, mud to the knees, riding a horse nearly dead beneath her.

Bram met her in the coal cellar, where Wren slept curled against the warden's coat, and Corvane stood a moment looking down at the child—at the grey in her lips, the sweat soaking her hair, the faint flicker visible now beneath the skin of her sternum when she breathed, a light like an ember in a closing fist—and Corvane's face did something Bram had never seen it do. It broke, briefly, and reassembled.

"The fleet?" Corvane asked.

"Burning. Nine of eleven hulls lost, the other two flooded at their moorings. The chain held. Nobody sailed, nobody died but one man fished out of the water with burns." Bram studied her. "You didn't come through the city to tell me that. You have news."

"The Order has split." Corvane pulled off her gauntlets finger by finger, as if the motion helped her hold the rest of herself together. "I've spent three weeks building it—quietly, wardens I trusted, marshals who buried friends the Grandmaster sold south. Tonight, while your smugglers burned his fleet, forty-one sworn members of the Vigilant Order signed a declaration naming Grandmaster Aldous Renk traitor to the Order's founding charge. It's circulating the citadel in copies. Half the garrison has read it by now."

"And the other half?"

"Will kill us if we're wrong." Corvane sat down heavily on a crate, and for a moment the marshal armor of her seemed to rust straight through. "Bram, you should understand what Renk is. I served under him twelve years. He remembers every warden's name. He pays widows' pensions out of his own purse. He is *kind*—and he has fed four hundred souls to that crown, one petition at a time, telling himself each one was necessary, each one bought the coast another year. He isn't a monster. That's what makes him dangerous. Monsters you can fight. A kind man doing arithmetic with human lives—" she shook her head. "Tomorrow I go to him. Face to face, before the declaration reaches him secondhand. If there's anything left in him of the man who taught me the charge, I mean to reach it."

"And if there isn't?"

Corvane was quiet a moment. In the corner, Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured something in the drowned tongue—two soft syllables, rising, almost a question—and both women went still until the child settled again.

"Then one of us dies in that office," Corvane said, "and the other finishes it. Either way, the Order ends tomorrow—one way or the other. Pray it ends reborn." She stood, buckling her gauntlets back on, becoming a marshal again piece by piece. "Keep the child here. Keep her *dark*—no songs, no dreams spoken aloud. Her flame is guttering, Bram. I've seen this in the archives. When it goes, the binding goes with it, and the Hunger will not wait politely for our politics."

She paused at the cellar stair, her back to Bram.

"One more thing. Renk keeps a private ledger. Every soul, every payment, thirty years of it. If I fall tomorrow—it's in his office, third shelf behind the charter seals. Burn the fleet, burn the ledger, burn him. Promise me."

"I promise," Bram said, and hated how easily promises came now, and how much they cost.

Corvane climbed the stairs into the pre-dawn grey, and Bram sat down on the floor beside the sleeping child, and Wren—without waking, the way children do—leaned until her head found Bram's shoulder, and stayed there, warm and far too light, while outside the windows the east went slowly the color of banked coals, and the smoke of eleven burning ships rose over Vhalcrest in a column that did not scatter, that stood straight up into the windless sky like a warning written in a language even kings could read.

Bram watched it and thought: *Hold on, old man. Hold on, Ilsa. We are coming, and we are bringing the whole world angry behind us.*

Then Wren whimpered in her sleep—a small, cracked sound—and the ember-light beneath her ribs flickered twice and dimmed, and Bram's blood went cold, because the child had dreamed aloud in the drowned tongue, and this time Bram knew the word. She'd learned it from the maps, from the archives, from the ferryman's whisper.

The word was *father*.

And far away, in the citadel on the hill, a bell began to ring out of rhythm—fast, ragged, the alarm-pattern no warden used except for one thing:

The Grandmaster's tower was burning too.
