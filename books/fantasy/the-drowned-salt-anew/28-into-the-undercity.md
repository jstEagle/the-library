## 28. Into the Undercity

The sea had come for Vhalcrest the way a debt collector comes: patiently, and then all at once.

Bram stood at the lip of the flooded undercity with the water lapping black and patient against her boots, and she thought of her mother's hands—the way they had smelled of brine no matter how often they were washed, the way she'd hummed songs in a language she claimed was just an old fisherman's dialect from the coast. Lies, all of it. Inheritance dressed up as habit. The ferryman on the border river had known the sigil stitched inside Bram's coat collar before Bram herself had understood what it meant: a coiled serpent swallowing its own tail of flame. Fire-keepers. Wardens of the egg.

*Your family served Aelmarrow once,* the old man had said, and now here she stood, three hundred years and one drowned kingdom later, about to walk into the deepest room of that service.

"You don't have to do this alone."

Corvane's voice came low behind her, pitched under the groan of the palace settling above. They were in a smuggler's tunnel—one of dozens Rennick's people had cut through the city's bones over generations—and the air tasted of wet stone and rot and, faintly, impossibly, salt. Always salt now. The whole city breathed it.

"I know," Bram said. She checked the rope harness one more time, tugged the knots with fingers gone numb from cold. "That's why I'm doing it alone."

"Kavanagh—"

"The vaults are warded against anyone who isn't blood." She turned to face the marshal. Torchlight caught the gray in Corvane's cropped hair, the exhaustion carved around her eyes. Three days since the Grandmaster fell. Three days since Corvane had carried a dead man's confession out of a burning archive and hadn't slept since. "My blood, specifically. The Order burned every record of my line when they branded me traitor, but the wards don't care about paper. They'll know me or they won't. If they don't—" She shrugged. "Then I drown in my ancestors' house, and you tell Wren I went quickly."

"She'll want to be here."

"She's *here*," Bram said quietly. "She's the reason I'm going down there at all."

Because Wren was dying by inches. Because the fire in the child's chest guttered like a candle in a doorway, and the Hunger Below drank from it hour by hour through the crown on the Hollow King's head, and the archives had been explicit in their terrible arithmetic: when the last dragon-fire went out, the binding failed, and everything the sea had swallowed would come back up along with everything the sea wanted next. Days, not weeks. Maybe not even that.

And beneath the palace, in a vault her family had kept for twenty generations without knowing why, an egg waited. Dormant. Dreaming. Holding its spark like a held breath.

"If I'm not back by the turn of the tide," Bram said, "get Wren out of the city. Don't argue. Don't honor me. Just run."

Corvane looked at her for a long moment. Then she did something Bram didn't expect: she pressed her fist to her own sternum, the old Vigilant salute, the one they gave to marshals and martyrs alike.

"For what it's worth," Corvane said, "I think your ancestors would be proud. The ones who weren't traitors, anyway."

"Some of them were both." Bram pulled her hood up. "Keep the torch burning at the mouth of the tunnel. If the water starts rising fast, don't wait for me."

She stepped off the ledge into the dark.

* * *

The water took her like a cold hand closing around her throat.

She'd dived before—in the Thornfell rivers as a girl, in the wreck-strewn shallows off smuggler coves—but never into standing black water in a drowned building, never with the weight of three hundred years pressing down through the flood. The cold hit her chest and squeezed. Her storm-blood, that cursed gift she'd spent ten years strangling, woke immediately, singing along her nerves, tasting the water the way a tongue tastes wine. And the water tasted wrong. Old. Salted not by the sea but by grief, by centuries of a kingdom's dying steeped into stone.

She swam down the flooded stairwell with the guide-rope hissing through her gloved hand, counting turns the way Rennick's maps had promised. One flight. Two. The torchlight from above shrank to a coin, then a spark, then nothing, and she was alone in the dark with the sound of her own heartbeat and the whisper of the rope.

On the third flight, the water changed.

It wasn't colder—that would have been bearable, explicable. It was *aware*. The pressure shifted around her like a crowd parting, like something vast turning over in sleep to look at the small warm thing moving through its halls. Her skin prickled. Every hair on her arms stood up under the diving leathers, and the storm in her blood answered—not with fear but with recognition, the way a dog lifts its head when it hears a voice from long ago.

*You came,* the water seemed to say. Or perhaps she only imagined it. Perhaps the imagination was the point. The vaults had been waiting for a Kavanagh, and the vaults knew how to make themselves heard.

Her fingers found the carved lintel Rennick's map had marked—a doorway buried in silt, its arch worked with the coiled serpent sigil, worn nearly smooth by water that shouldn't have been able to reach this deep until recently. She pressed her bare palm against it, tearing off her glove because the map's final line had been specific: *skin to stone, blood to bone.*

For a moment, nothing. The cold. Her pulse hammering in her ears. The dark pressing in like a second ocean.

Then the stone warmed under her hand.

Not gradually—all at once, like a hearth uncovered after years, like a body remembered. Heat bloomed through the arch and down into the passage beyond, and Bram felt something move through the water around her, a current with intention, brushing past her like hands checking her face, her height, the shape of her jaw. Checking her against a memory. Twenty generations of Kavanaghs had pressed their palms to this stone, and the stone had kept their touch the way wax keeps a seal.

The doors ground open.

* * *

Beyond the threshold, the water was still there—but it let her breathe.

She discovered this the hard way, lungs burning, and then discovered it properly: the flooded vault beyond the doors held air pockets, pockets of trapped breath caught in the high vaulted ceilings, connected by passages where the water ran shallow along the floor. Not an accident. Engineering. Her ancestors had built this place to be survivable, to be *reachable*, by keepers who might arrive in any season, any catastrophe, any drowning age. The Aelmarrowans had known their kingdom could fall. They had simply refused to let the egg fall with it.

Bram hauled herself onto a submerged stair, gasping, and got her first look at the fire-keepers' hall.

Torch-brackets lined the walls, and when she fumbled flint to the nearest one, the stone itself caught—some alchemy older than the Order, some chemistry of the drowned kingdom—and flame ran along the brackets one after another, chasing itself down the corridor, lighting a hall so long she couldn't see its end. The walls were carved. Not decorated—*carved*, every inch of them, a continuous frieze that wound through the stone like a river through country.

She walked it slowly, dripping, and read her own history in bas-relief.

There: the founding. Ships with dragon-prowed hulls riding into a harbor city of white towers—Aelmarrow alive, Aelmarrow *whole*, rendered by hands that had loved it. There: dragons overhead, wings wide as sails, and below them human figures with arms raised, and from those figures' hands rose carved lines of flame—not the dragons' fire, but something smaller, humbler. Sparks. Embers passed palm to palm down a chain of figures that marched across the wall in procession.

Fire-keepers. Not warriors. Not mages. Custodians. People whose whole sacred office was to carry a spark and not let it die.

Her throat tightened. She thought of her mother humming in the kitchen, of the sigil sewn into coat linings, of a family that had fled north after the drowning and folded its duty away like a flag and told its children stories about fishermen's dialects. Twenty generations of quiet. And underneath all of it, this hall had waited, patient as seed-stock, for someone to come back.

The frieze darkened as she walked. The towers tilted. Water entered the carvings—actual sculpted waves, chiseled with such fury that the stone seemed to scream—and the dragons grew fewer, and the chain of fire-keepers grew longer, stretching further and further between each dwindling spark, until the final panel showed a single figure standing on a mountaintop holding a coal against the wind, and beneath it, in the drowned tongue that Wren spoke in her sleep, an inscription.

Bram couldn't read it. But she didn't need to. She'd heard enough of the language from Wren's midnight murmuring to catch its shape, and the shape of these words was a promise: *We hold it until they return.*

"They returned," she said aloud, and her voice cracked in the empty hall. "I'm sorry it took so long."

* * *

The guardian bones sat at the far end of the hall, and Bram stopped breathing when the light reached them.

They were arranged in a circle around a raised dais—not scattered, not heaped, but *placed*, seated cross-legged in a ring of maybe thirty skeletons, facing inward, their spines straight even in death, their fleshless hands resting on their knees in attitudes of meditation or vigil. Their armor had rotted to lace. Their weapons lay across their laps: not swords, but lanterns—iron lanterns, long extinguished, held in bony grips like the most precious cargo in the world.

Thirty fire-keepers who had stayed. Who had stood in this vault while the sea came down the stairs, and set their lanterns down—or rather, hadn't—and sat in their ring and waited to drown rather than abandon their post. The water had taken the city, but it had never moved them. Even now, three centuries on, not one bone had drifted from its place.

Bram stood at the edge of the circle for a long time, water streaming off her, and found she couldn't make her legs move. It wasn't fear. It was the weight of being *expected*. Somewhere above her, Wren was guttering; somewhere above that, a hollow king wore a hungry crown; and here, in the dark under everything, thirty of her own blood had died on duty so that a spark could survive for her to find.

"I'm Bram," she said finally, uselessly, to the bones. "Daughter of Maren, daughter of Sef, daughter of—" She stopped. She didn't know the names past her grandmother. The chain had been broken on purpose, hidden, denied. "I don't know all of you. But I'm yours. I think—I think I always was."

The lanterns lit.

All thirty of them, at once, with no flame she could see—just a soft gold kindling inside iron cages, light rising through rib cages and eye sockets until the whole ring of the dead glowed like a crown of embers. The light didn't flicker. It pulsed, slow and steady, and Bram realized with a jolt that it pulsed in rhythm with her own heart.

The ward. The last ward. Not a lock but a welcome, and it had been waiting three hundred years for a Kavanagh's heartbeat to answer it.

She walked into the circle. The dead did not stir, but the warmth of the lantern-light washed over her like sunlight through shallow water, and for one suspended moment she felt them—not as ghosts, not as voices, but as *attention*, thirtyfold and gentle, looking at her the way her mother used to look at her when she came in from a storm: checking that she was whole, that she was home, that she had brought the fire with her.

At the center of the ring, on the dais, the egg waited.

* * *

It was smaller than she expected. That was her first stupid, human thought—she had imagined something the size of a boat, a relic, a monument, and instead it was hardly bigger than a bread loaf, obsidian-dark shot through with veins of dull ember-red, resting in a cradle of fused glass that had once, she understood dimly, been sand melted by dragon-breath into a bed it could never burn through. The surface was cool-looking, matte, still.

But it wasn't still. As she knelt at the edge of the dais, she saw the veins moving. Faintly. Slowly. Light crawling through the dark shell like blood through closed eyelids, like a sleeper's dream made visible. Once every dozen heartbeats, a brighter pulse traveled the length of the egg and faded, and each pulse landed in Bram's chest like a fist closing gently around her sternum.

*Hello,* said something very old and very young, in a voice that was not sound. *Is it time?*

Bram's eyes stung. She was soaked to the bone, half-drowned, hunted, exiled, and kneeling in a tomb full of her ancestors' lanterns, and the thing she wanted most in the world was to answer a question asked by an unhatched creature.

"Not yet," she whispered. "Soon. There's someone up there who needs what you're carrying. A little girl. She's been holding a piece of the same fire for a long time, and she's tired, and she's running out." Her voice broke. She let it. "I've come to ask. Not take. Ask."

The pulse came again, stronger, and this time it didn't fade cleanly. It lingered, spreading, the ember-veins brightening across the whole shell, and the lantern-light of the dead ringed around her rose in answer, and Bram felt the vault begin to gather itself toward something—some transaction older than the Order, older than the kingdom, older than the word *keeper*.

The water in the hall began to rise.

Slowly, silently, without wave or ripple—it climbed the steps behind her, climbed the walls, climbing toward the ceiling with the smooth inevitability of a tide answering a moon, and Bram understood that the vault was sealing itself, that what happened next would happen underwater or not at all, that her ancestors had built this moment into the stone: *to wake the egg, a keeper must give it the sea's own depth.* Pressure. Surrender. The willingness to be small in the presence of something vast.

Above her, muffled through fathoms of stone, she heard the palace groan—the Court's riders, the fighting, the rising sea, Wren's fading light—and she thought of the child's face, mud-caked on a doorstep a lifetime ago, speaking a dead tongue to a woman who had forgotten her own.

Bram filled her lungs until her ribs ached.

"Hold it until we return," she said to the ring of the dead. "You did. Now watch me do mine."

She put both palms flat against the egg, and the world went white.

* * *

She was standing on a cliff above a harbor at night, and the sky was burning.

Dragons wheeled overhead—real ones, living ones, wings blotting out stars—and the city below was Aelmarrow as the frieze had shown it, white towers and ten thousand lamps, except the lamps were going out district by district as the sea came in. Not a wave. A *rising*, deliberate and total, called up by something beneath the seabed, something the last dragons had bound themselves against at the cost of everything. Bram watched a woman on the cliff press a glowing coal into a child's hands and speak words she somehow understood without translation: *Run north. Hold it until they return.* Watched the child run. Watched the woman turn back toward the drowning city and walk into the water with her lantern held high.

The vision folded. Time collapsed. Centuries compressed into a single held note, and at the center of the note, the egg's voice again—closer now, inside her now, curled against her storm-blood like a key finding its lock:

*You are the return. Take the spark, keeper. Spend it well. We have waited so long to be useful.*

The ember-veins split open like dawn cracking a dark horizon, and true dragon-fire—white-gold, ancient, alive—poured up through Bram's palms and into her chest, and the last thing she saw before consciousness left her was the ring of her ancestors' lanterns blazing like a sunrise at the bottom of the sea.

Somewhere far above, in a throne room drowning in tide and dead men, a child's guttering flame suddenly steadied.

And somewhere deeper than that, something ancient and hungry lifted its head, and for the first time in three hundred years, felt afraid.
