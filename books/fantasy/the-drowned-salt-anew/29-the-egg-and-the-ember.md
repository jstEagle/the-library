## 29. The Egg and the Ember

The water remembered her.

That was the only way Bram could describe it as she sank through the flooded vaults beneath the palace of Vhalcrest, past pillars furred with black silt, past the bones of her ancestors arranged in a ring around a stone cradle that had waited three hundred years for someone with the right blood to come looking. The water did not resist her. It parted around her like a congregation making way, and where her hands touched it, faint light bloomed and faded—storm-light, the color of the sky before thunder.

She had left Wren above, in the smugglers' tunnel behind the fish-market cistern, with Doss Marlow's crossbowmen standing guard and strict instructions delivered in the flat voice she used for orders she could not afford to have argued with: *If I am not back by the turn of the tide, take her up the coast road to Corvane. Do not wait. Do not look back.*

Wren had not cried. That was the worst of it. She had taken Bram's face in both small salt-crusted hands and spoken a single word in the drowned tongue—a word Bram had learned by now, because some words you learn whether you want to or not—and then she had let go.

*Come back.* It meant *come back*. Or possibly *return burning*. The language had never much distinguished between the two.

Now Bram hung weightless in the dark water, forty feet beneath the throne room of a hollow king, and looked at the egg.

It was larger than she had expected—larger than any bird, larger than a man's torso, its shell a deep storm-grey shot through with veins of dull ember-red, like coals banked under ash. It rested in the stone cradle at the center of the ring of bones, and though the water here should have been cold enough to ache, the space around the egg was warm. Not warm the way a fire is warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. Warm the way a hand is warm when you hold it too long and cannot bring yourself to let go.

The bones around it were Kavanagh bones. She had known it the moment her lantern found them—not from their faces, which the water had long since taken, but from the rings on their finger-bones, each one stamped with the sigil the ferryman had recognized on the border river: a mountain split by lightning. Her mother had worn that sigil. Her mother's mother. All the way back, apparently, to this drowned vault, where her family had kept the last fire of Aelmarrow the way other families kept heirlooms or grudges.

*Fire-keepers,* Corvane had called them, reading from archives that smelled of ash. *The Order's oldest records say the Kavanaghs were sworn to Aelmarrow before there was an Order. Before there was a Vhalcrest. They carried the binding south when the kingdom drowned.*

Bram had spent ten years believing her exile was punishment. Now, floating among the bones of women who had done the same work she had abandoned, she understood it had been inheritance all along. The Vigilant Order hadn't branded her traitor. They had simply been too young to remember what the brand meant.

Her lungs were beginning to complain. She had trained herself for long dives in the smuggling years—there were cargoes that moved only by underwater routes, debts collected from sunken wrecks—but even so, the cold pressed in now, patient as a creditor. She had perhaps four minutes of clear thought left, and what she intended to do could not be done halfway.

She descended the last few feet and settled onto the silted floor beside the cradle, boots sinking gently into three centuries of soft grey sediment. Up close, the egg's shell was not smooth but scaled, each scale no bigger than her thumbnail, and beneath each scale something pulsed—slowly, rarely, like a heart that had learned to beat once a minute rather than once a second, conserving itself against an endless winter.

Three hundred years of almost-nothing. Three hundred years of a spark refusing to go out.

Bram pulled off her right glove. The water bit at her bare skin, and she hissed a small curse into her breathing apparatus, then stopped, because it seemed suddenly important not to swear in this place. Her grandmother would have said something about that. Her grandmother had said a great many things that had sounded like superstition until this week.

"Right," she said aloud, and the word came out as a stream of silver bubbles. "I don't know if you can hear me. I don't know if you're a *you* at all yet." The bubbles rose. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of water and the palace's drowned foundations, she thought she heard the deep bell of Vhalcrest tolling—and tolling again, out of rhythm, which meant either the Court had noticed the sabotage in the harbor or the dead had reached another gate. There was no time. There had been no time since the salt word appeared on the citadel wall. "But my blood is the same blood that promised to keep you. And I'm calling in the debt."

She pressed her palm flat against the shell.

For a moment, nothing. The pulse beneath the scales continued its glacial rhythm, indifferent. The water held its breath. Even the distant bells seemed to hold theirs.

Then the storm in her answered the ember in the egg, the way two tuning forks answer across a room, and Bram Kavanagh's world went white.

* * *

She was standing on a cliff above the sea, and the sea was wrong.

Not rough—wrong. It stood too high against the land, a wall of green-black water taller than any tower, holding itself upright with the terrible patience of something that has been commanded. Behind her stretched the city of Aelmarrow in its final hour: white towers, copper roofs gone green, lanterns lit along every street as though light could argue with the ocean. And everywhere, rising from the streets and squares and temple steps, the people of Aelmarrow were singing.

Not screaming. Singing. Their voices rose together in the drowned tongue, thousands strong, and the sound went through Bram like a blade of ice, because she knew the song. Wren sang it in her sleep. Wren hummed it while mending nets. Wren had taught it to her syllable by syllable over a winter of fireside lessons, and Bram had thought it a lullaby.

It was not a lullaby. It was a binding. It had always been a binding—the whole city singing the Hunger Below back into its chains, generation after generation, until the night the sea came up to swallow the singers whole.

A woman stood at the cliff's edge near her, and Bram knew her without being told. She had Bram's build, Bram's stubborn jaw, hair the color of wet slate pinned back for work. On her breast was pinned the sigil of the split mountain. In her arms, wrapped in oilcloth against the spray, she carried the egg.

"You came," the woman said, and her voice was roughened by smoke and salt and shouting. She did not look at Bram; she looked out at the standing sea. "I dreamed you'd come. The egg dreams forward, sometimes. Shows us who's coming to keep the promise after we're ash." She shifted the egg in her arms. "You're late, though. By about three hundred years."

"The binding failed," Bram said. Her voice sounded strange here, thin against the choir of a dying city. "The crown broke. The thing wearing your king—"

"I know what it wears." Bitterness cracked the woman's voice. "We all knew, in the end. We sang louder to cover it, and it learned to sing back." She turned then, and her eyes were Bram's eyes, and they were full of a grief so old it had worn smooth, like sea glass. "Listen to me, keeper. We are going to die tonight. Most of us. The dragons bought us an hour with their burning and the hour is nearly spent. But the fire doesn't die with us. Fire doesn't work that way—it *passes*. It has always passed down the line, banked low, waiting." She held out the egg. "Take it south. Take it into the mountains, away from the salt. Wake it when the world needs it, and not one day sooner, because a woken flame burns fast and a sleeping one endures."

"I'm not—" Bram started, and the lie died in her throat, because she was. She was exactly the person this woman had been waiting for. Exiled, storm-blooded, carrying a child made of dragon-fire memory in her arms half a world and thirty years away. "There's a girl," she said instead. "Eight years old. She speaks your tongue. She sings your song. The crown-thing has been feeding on her, and I'm going to stop it, but I need—"

"Fire," the woman said simply. "You need fire. And fire needs a reason." She pressed the egg into Bram's arms, and it was warm, impossibly warm, warm as a living chest, and through the oilcloth Bram felt the slow coal-beat of the thing inside. "So I'll give you mine. Look. Look at what we loved. Carry it. That's the price of waking an egg—you carry the memory of why it must burn, and you pay it forward to whoever wakes next."

And the vision opened like a door.

* * *

Bram saw Aelmarrow alive.

Not the drowning—first the living of it, poured into her faster than she could drink. Fish-markets loud at dawn, gulls arguing over herring heads. Children racing paper boats down the canal-streets while their grandmothers shouted after them in the drowned tongue. A wedding on the seawall where the whole quarter danced despite the drizzle, because in Aelmarrow rain was considered good luck at a marriage and terrible luck at a funeral and nobody could ever quite agree which was happening. Scholars in the tide-temples arguing about the phases of the moon-tides. Dragons—she saw dragons, real ones, bronze and slate-grey, riding the thermals above the harbor with riders on their necks, and the people below barely glanced up, because what is miraculous becomes furniture in three generations.

She saw the king. Young still, in this memory, with his daughter on his shoulders—dark-haired, gap-toothed, shrieking with laughter as he galloped her through the palace gardens—and Bram felt her chest tighten, because she had seen that same face recently, hollowed and tide-pooled, enthroned in a hall of whispering courtiers. This was the man beneath. This was who the Hunger wore like a glove. He had been *this*: a father who let his daughter braid flowers into his beard and pretended not to notice the court laughing at him.

Then the memories turned, the way weather turns.

The daughter sickening—some wasting tide-fever the healers couldn't name. The king descending, mad with grief, into the deep temples where the old bindings were kept, asking questions no king should ask. *What lies below? What can give back what the sea takes?* And something below answering, patient and honey-voiced, offering comfort the way a spider offers silk. The crown passing to his head already poisoned. The first night the king's eyes went tide-pool grey, and the queen, watching, beginning quietly to sing.

The singing. Years of it. The whole city learning the binding-song again, harder, more desperate, as the king grew stranger—as harvests soured with salt, as wells turned brackish, as fishermen brought up fish with human faces and threw them back with shaking hands. The dragons growing thin and furious, feeling their ancient binding chewed at from below. The queen dying with the song unfinished on her lips. The daughter's grave by the sea-wall, visited nightly by a king who no longer slept.

And then the last night. The sea standing up. The dragons diving into the wave-wall with fire enough to boil a channel through it, buying the hour. The fire-keepers running for the vaults with the egg. The choir of thousands singing the binding one final time—not to save themselves, Bram understood now, but to buy the egg time to get away. To make the city itself into a lock.

The wave came down. The song went under. And the last thing the memory gave her, the very last, was the sound of the drowned tongue continuing beneath the water—thousands of voices, undrowned somehow, unbroken, singing in the dark for three hundred years. Waiting for a child to be born who could hear them. Waiting to teach her the song so that someday, someone might finish it.

* * *

Bram came back to herself gasping, and the gasp nearly killed her.

She tore free of the breathing apparatus, choked, coughed, and realized with distant surprise that she didn't need it. The water around her was warm now—warm as bathwater, warm as blood—and glowing. Faint amber light seeped from between the egg's scales, spreading outward through the flooded vault in slow pulsing rings, illuminating the ring of her ancestors' bones, turning the black silt to gold. The water tasted of brine and, beneath the brine, of woodsmoke.

The egg was cracking.

Hairline fractures spread across the storm-grey shell like lightning across a summer sky, and from each fracture leaked not fluid but light—true light, the first true light Bram had seen since the storm she'd called on the low road. The pulse beneath the scales quickened. Once a minute became once every fifty seconds, then forty, then ten, a heartbeat remembering its business.

"No," Bram said, and then, absurdly, "not yet, not *here*, you'll drown—" and then stopped, because the vision was still in her, still burning, and she understood. The egg wasn't hatching early. The egg had been waiting three hundred years for exactly this: a keeper with storm-blood and a reason, kneeling in the dark with the memory of Aelmarrow fresh in her chest. The woman on the cliff had told her the price. *Carry the memory of why it must burn.* Bram had paid it. The egg had accepted.

The shell burst.

It did not shatter—it opened, petals of scale folding back like a grey flower, and the hatchling inside was small, no bigger than a heron, slick with light instead of slime. Its eyes were molten gold. It looked at Bram the way newborn things look at the first moving shape they see, and Bram felt the look land somewhere deeper than eyes.

Then it breathed.

Not fire—something older than fire, something that fire was named after. A thread of pure ember-gold poured from the hatchling's open mouth and struck Bram square in the chest, and it did not burn. It *lit*. She felt it kindle along her storm-blood like a fuse finding powder, felt the two currents in her—sky and forge, thunder and coal—twine together into a single rope of heat that filled her from heel to crown. Her scars, the old whip-scars from the Order's judgment hall, warmed for the first time in twenty years. Her teeth ached with light. When she exhaled, her breath came out as sparks.

Along with the fire came everything the fire carried. Purpose, crystalline and cold-edged. She knew now—not believed, *knew*, the way she knew which way north lay in a whiteout—that the rite could be done. That the king could be reached. That the crown could be burned without burning the girl. That the dead of Aelmarrow could be sung home. Knowledge sat in her like ballast, steady and sure, and beneath it, gentler, the memory of paper boats and wedding dances and a king with flowers in his beard, given to her to spend.

The hatchling climbed into her cupped hands, weighed almost nothing, and chirped—a small, rusty sound, a bell unused for centuries.

"Aye," Bram said, and her voice came out smoke-roughened and certain. "I know. We've got a king to unmake and a girl to save, and precious little tide left to do it in." She tucked the hatchling inside her coat, against her heart, where it pulsed warm as a second heartbeat, and kicked for the surface.

Above her, through forty feet of glowing water, the drowned vault's ceiling trembled with distant thunder—and it was not natural thunder. It was rhythmic. Deliberate. The sound of something vast striking the palace gates above, again and again, keeping time.

The dead had reached Vhalcrest. And they were knocking.
