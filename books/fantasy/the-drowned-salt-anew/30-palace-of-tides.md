## 30. Palace of Tides

The sea came for Vhalcrest the way grief comes for the grieving—slowly at first, so gently you could almost pretend it wasn't happening, and then all at once, everywhere, without mercy.

Bram felt it before she saw it. She stood on the roof of the customs house with Corvane and a dozen of her smugglers, watching the harbor burn—the Court's fleet a garden of black blossoms, masts folding like burnt reeds—and beneath her boots she felt the stone begin to hum. Not the hum of fire or of falling masonry. A deeper note. The note of old things waking.

"The tide," she said, and her voice came out strange, flattened by awe. "Corvane. Look at the tide."

It was coming in wrong. She had spent ten years reading water the way priests read scripture, and she knew every lawful rhythm of the sea—its patience, its greed, its long memory. This was none of those. The water was rising as if summoned, climbing the harbor walls in sheets that did not break but *climbed*, hauling itself up the granite like a thing with hands. And it was not dark water. It glimmered faintly, faintly green, the color of the deep places where light goes to die, and in its shine Bram saw faces.

Not reflections. Faces.

"Saints above," whispered Doss, one of hers, a river-man who claimed he'd never feared anything that floated. His voice cracked on the word. "They're *in* it."

They were. The drowned dead of Aelmarrow had left their fields and roads three days past, a silent tide of them walking east through empty villages, and Bram had thought they marched on the city alone. She had been wrong. They had never needed to walk the whole way. The sea had carried them the last miles, held them in its green arms like children being brought home, and now it set them down—gently, so gently—on the quays and stairways and drowned streets of Vhalcrest, thousands upon thousands of them, salt-white and patient, their eyes full of the same faint glow that burned in Wren's chest.

"They're not attacking," Corvane said beside her. Her marshal's composure had gone thin as ice over deep water; Bram could hear the fracture running under every word. "Look at them. They're not attacking anything. They're *going somewhere*."

She was right. The dead moved with purpose but without violence, flowing up from the waterline in a slow braided river, and every current of that river bent toward the same point: the palace on its hill, the old royal road climbing through the terraces, the throne where something hollow sat wearing a dead man's face.

Toward Wren.

Bram's hand went to her chest, to the place beneath her ribs where the dragon-spark slept—a coal of borrowed fire she had carried since the flooded vaults, since the egg, since the vision of Aelmarrow's last night that still visited her in every unguarded moment. The spark pulsed against her palm like a second heartbeat, quickening, answering the dead below the way a mother wakes when a child cries in another room.

"She can feel them," Bram said. "And they can feel her."

"Then we have no more time." Corvane turned from the parapet, and behind her calm Bram saw the cost of the last two days written plainly: the ash smudge on her jaw she hadn't bothered to wipe, the bandage on her forearm from the Grandmaster's fall, the way her eyes went distant whenever the citadel's burning gates came up in conversation. Half the Vigilant Order had followed her out of that fire. The other half had stayed to bury what was left of it—or to hunt her. "The rite needs the spark, the child, and the crown in one room. We have two of three. Move."

* * *

They went down through the customs house into streets already ankle-deep and rising.

Vhalcrest in flood was a city Bram barely recognized, and yet recognized completely. She had been born six streets from the palace, in a smuggler's warren built into the bones of older buildings, older walls, older sins. She had run these alleys at nineteen with the Order's hounds behind her and her mother's sigil burning against her skin. Now she waded through them with salt water to her knees and the dead drifting past in the cross-streets like fish through kelp, and they did not look at her. That was the worst of it, in those first minutes—that they did not look. She was nothing to them. She was not what they had come for.

A woman in the rotted finery of some forgotten court drifted by an upper window, seaweed streaming from her sleeves like ribbons of black silk. A fisherman, his net still over one shoulder after three hundred years, stood knee-deep in what had been a market square, his face tilted toward the palace with an expression Bram could only call *homesick*. A child. There was a child too, small and salt-pale, holding the hand of a woman who might have been anyone's mother once, and Bram made herself look away because looking too long would stop her feet, and her feet could not stop.

"The undercity," she said, forcing her voice into the flat competence that had kept her alive for ten years. "The smugglers' ways. The flood will have opened doors that were sealed for a century, but it'll also have drowned the lower galleries. We go high and dry until the last possible junction, then we swim."

"Swim," repeated Doss, with feeling.

"With the dead in the water," added Ferro, who had lost an eye to a Court pike at the citadel and had been cheerfully fatalistic ever since.

"With the dead in the water," Bram agreed. "But here's the thing about them—" she lifted a hand, and the spark flared faintly between her fingers, green-gold, and the nearest dead turned their heads toward it as one, drawn like moths, "—they want what I'm carrying. Which means they'll follow it. Which means, my darlings, that tonight I am the bait, and you are the fishermen."

Ferro grinned with his one eye. "Always liked you, Kavanagh."

* * *

The smugglers' ways were exactly as her mother had described them in the maps she'd burned rather than let the Order find: a honeycomb of cellars, cistern-channels, and collapsed noble vaults threading beneath the terrace district like veins under skin. Bram led them by feel and by memory, by the chalk marks her mother's generation had left in hidden corners—a gull scratched twice, a knot tied left-handed—and the water rose around them the whole way, swallowing gallery after gallery behind them with a sound like slow applause.

At the third junction they found the first of the Court's soldiers.

Or what was left of them. Six men in royal livery, drowned—not fought, *drowned*, in a corridor forty feet above the new waterline, their bodies swollen and slack, their weapons unbloodied. The dead had passed through this place and simply... taken the air away. Taken everything away. One of the soldiers had died clawing at a wall where someone had scratched, in a shaking hand: *THEY SING*.

Doss crossed himself with two different gods' names and got them both wrong.

"Don't touch them," Corvane said quietly. She knelt by the nearest body and closed its eyes with a gentleness that seemed, in that drowned dark, impossibly expensive. "They were following orders. Someone sent them down here to hold the undercity against the dead." She looked up, and her face in the spark-light was grim. "Someone in the Court knows what's coming. Knows, and is spending soldiers like copper to buy hours."

"Then let's not waste theirs or ours," Bram said.

They climbed. The passages narrowed, rose, dried; the sound of water became the sound of water *elsewhere*, pressing at the walls, patient as siege artillery. Twice the spark in Bram's chest flared hard enough to hurt, and both times, seconds later, the whole passage shuddered—the dead passing overhead or beside them in the flooded galleries, hundreds of bodies moving through the city's drowned veins, and the pressure of their passage like the pressure of a train of storm-clouds dragging across open country.

The second time, Wren's name tore itself out of Bram's mouth before she could stop it.

"*Wren.*"

The spark answered. Faint—so faint, guttering like a candle in a doorway—but it answered, a pulse of recognition across half a palace's distance, and Bram pressed her fist to her sternum and breathed through the pain of it. *Alive. Still alive. Still burning, barely.*

"How much does she have left?" Corvane asked. She had heard the pulse too; everyone had. The smugglers were looking at Bram's chest the way sailors look at a barometer falling.

"Not enough," Bram said. "Hours. Maybe less. The Hunger's been feeding since they took her, and it feeds fast when it's frightened, and it has never been this frightened in three hundred years." She started climbing again, faster. "We are going to be late, and lateness is going to cost us everything."

* * *

They broke surface in the palace's oldest cellar—a wine vault from the first dynasty, its racks long emptied, its arched ceiling furred with white salt-bloom—and found the war waiting for them.

The great stair hall above had become a battlefield between two tides. The Court's remaining guard had formed a shield line at the top of the grand stair, pikes braced against the balustrade, and down the stair came the dead—not charging, never charging, just *coming*, wave after wave, absorbing pike-thrusts the way the sea absorbs stones. Where blades pierced them, seawater poured out instead of blood. Where they fell, others stepped over them without breaking stride. And behind the shield line, at the head of the stairs, Court battle-mages were working something ugly: a lattice of pale fire stretched across the stairway's width, and each dead thing that touched it burst into steam with a sound like a scream played backward.

"It's holding," Ferro reported from the shadows of the archway, having scouted ahead on his belly like the ex-siege-engineer he was. "For now. But there's a thousand of them out there and maybe sixty of the guard, and the water's at the third step already. When the water reaches the mage-line, the lattice shorts, and then it's arithmetic."

"So we don't use the stairs," Bram said.

She studied the hall with a smuggler's eyes and a storm-caller's ears. The grand stair was suicide. But palaces were built by the vain, and the vain built secrets: service ways, priest holes, the private stair of some long-dead mistress. Her mother's maps had marked three ways into the palace proper, and one of them—

"There." She pointed to the far wall, behind a rank of empty niches where statues had stood before some pious dynasty smashed them. "Behind the third niche. A door dressed as masonry. It comes up in the chapel gallery, one floor above the throne room antechamber. My mother used it to rob a duke. I intend to use it to save the world, which I think she'd find funny."

Getting there meant crossing thirty feet of open hall within sight of the shield line, and the fighting reached them before they were halfway—because the lattice failed early. Not from the water. From *within*. One of the mages simply stopped, mid-cantation, and turned around, and Bram saw his face and understood with a lurch that there was no one home behind it anymore: the Hunger's wraith-servants had been busy, wearing the Court's own people like gloves, just as they wore the king. The lattice flickered, sagged, and died, and the dead came up the grand stair like the sea coming over a sandbar.

What followed was not a battle so much as a drowning with edges. Bram called the spark up along her arms and it burned green-gold in the gloom, and the dead parted around her—not for love of her, but for hunger for what she carried, turning to follow her like iron filings following a lodestone, which cleared her path and doomed the shield line in the same motion. She hated that. She would hate it properly later, when there was a later. Corvane fought at her shoulder with a warden's halberd and a marshal's economy, killing only what had to be killed and grieving each one, and the smugglers moved through the chaos like the professionals they were—quick, quiet, unsentimental, alive.

The hidden door was where her mother's map promised. Of course it was. Her mother had never once lied to her about locks.

* * *

The chapel gallery ran along the upper wall of the antechamber to the throne room, and from its latticed screen Bram looked down into the heart of the Hollow Court and felt the bottom drop out of the world.

The antechamber was flooding. Black-green water swirled across the marble waist-deep and rising, and standing in it, motionless, were the wraith-servants—dozens of them, tall and wrong, shapes like people remembered by someone who had never loved anyone. They faced outward in a silent ring, guarding the great doors of the throne room, and the water around their feet did not ripple. It *avoided* them.

And beyond the doors—Bram could not see beyond the doors, but she could feel it, all of it, pressing against her ribs like a hand: the throne room, the king, the crown, and Wren. The spark in her chest strained toward the doors like a dog at the end of a chain. And underneath the strain, unmistakable now even to whatever untrained thing in her could hear such things, she heard singing.

Wren's song. The drowned tongue. Thin and exhausted and utterly unbroken, coming through three hundred years and forty feet of ancient oak, and every dead soul in the palace outside was singing it back.

The whole building hummed with it. The dead in the halls below, the dead in the flooded streets, the dead in the sea itself—they had all learned the words, and they sang them upward through the stone, and the stone remembered, and Bram understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water, what the song was.

It wasn't a summons. It was a *lullaby*. Three hundred years old, sung by mothers to children on the night Aelmarrow drowned, and the dead were singing it to the thing in the cage—to Wren, and to themselves, and perhaps to the king—to say: *we remember you, we are coming, you will not be alone when it ends.*

"Bram." Corvane's hand on her arm. Below, the wraith-ring had begun to move—not toward the doors, but toward the chapel stair. Toward them. Something had noticed. Something always noticed. "Whatever you're planning, plan faster."

Bram looked at the spark burning in her cupped palms—at the small, stubborn, borrowed fire of a dragon three centuries dead, entrusted to a traitor, a smuggler, a woman who had chosen invisibility and been handed a child instead. The spark pulsed. Once. Twice. In rhythm, she realized, with the song.

With Wren's heartbeat.

"I'm not planning anything," Bram said, and stood, and let the fire climb her arms like dawn climbing a mountain. "I'm knocking."

Below, every wraith-servant in the flooded antechamber turned its tide-pool eyes upward—and the great doors of the throne room began, slowly, to open.
