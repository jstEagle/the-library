## 31. The Unbinding Rite

The throne room of Vhalcrest had been built to drown.

Bram understood that now, standing knee-deep in black water that had come up through the marble floors like something exhaling after three hundred years of held breath. The architects of the old dynasty had raised their pillars from salt-veined stone, quarried—she realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold—from the ruins of Aelmarrow itself. The kingdom they erased had been ground into the very bones of the one that replaced it, and now the bones were waking, and the sea was coming home through every crack and seam.

Water poured down the grand stairway behind her in a silver sheet. Somewhere above, muffled by stone and flood, the Court's riders were still fighting Corvane's wardens—or what remained of them. The doors had been breached an hour ago. Maybe two. Time had gone strange since Bram had pressed her palm to the dragon egg in the flooded vaults below and felt a spark older than nations settle into her chest like a swallowed coal.

It burned there now. Not painfully—not exactly—but insistently, the way a fever burns, the way a name burns on the tongue when you are trying not to say it aloud.

*Say it,* the spark seemed to murmur. *Say the old words. I have waited so long to be said.*

"Not yet," Bram whispered, and waded forward.

The throne sat on its dais at the far end of the hall, and the king sat on the throne, and neither of them moved as the water climbed. He was not what she had expected. She had imagined something monstrous—the Hunger Below wearing flesh the way a butcher wears an apron, slick and red and grinning. But the Hollow King was terrible in the opposite direction: he was *absent*. A man-shaped room with all the furniture removed. His skin was the grey of driftwood long soaked. His eyes were tide-pools, dark and still, and when they turned toward her she saw nothing reflected in them at all. Not the torchlight guttering along the walls. Not her own face. Nothing.

Beside him, in a cage of black iron that did not touch the floor—that *floated*, half-submerged, swaying gently as though cradled by a current no one else could feel—was Wren.

She was small in there. Smaller than she had any right to be, knees drawn up, mud-brown hair plastered to her forehead, and her lips were moving. Even across the length of the drowning hall, even over the groan of settling stone and the hiss of water finding new paths, Bram heard her. Felt her more than heard her, the way you feel a bell through a wall.

Wren was singing in the drowned tongue.

And every note pulled at the coal in Bram's chest like a hand pulling a rope.

"Wren." Her voice cracked on the child's name. She forced it steady, smuggler-steady, guide-steady, the voice that had talked caravans past avalanches and lies past suspicious harbor-masters. "Look at me, little bird. Look at me."

The girl's head lifted. Her eyes found Bram's across the water, and for one heartbeat they were only Wren's eyes—frightened, fierce, eight years old—and then they rolled back and the song changed key, dropped low, became something with too many harmonies in it, something sung by throats that had been salt for centuries.

"No," Bram breathed. "No, no—you hold on. You hear me? You *hold on.*"

The cage drifted a handspan closer to the throne. The king's head tilted, following it, the way a man follows the movement of a fish beneath ice.

Behind her, at the base of the stair, steel rang against something that was not steel.

"Bram!" Corvane's voice, raw-edged, stripped of all its marshal's polish. "They're coming up the west gallery—I can't hold the whole floor!"

Bram turned. The gallery Corvane named was a colonnade running along the hall's upper reach, and it was full of them: the wraith-servants of the Hunger, the things the Court called the Crownsworn and Bram had already learned to call by plainer names. They had been courtiers once. Now they were silhouettes with too much depth, figures cut from black water given the memory of walking. Salt-crusted rags moved where robes should have been. Where faces should have been there were hollows, and in the hollows, faint as phosphorescence, the same tide-pool light that burned in the king's eyes.

There were dozens. They came down the gallery stairs without hurry, without splashing, though the water reached their waists, and where they walked the flood went flat and silent, as if afraid.

Corvane met them alone.

Ilsa Corvane had shed her marshal's cloak somewhere between the gates and this hall, and beneath it she wore the plain mail of a working warden, and she fought like a woman who had spent twenty years being told she was too blunt for promotion and had decided to make it a virtue. Her blade was Vigilant steel, quenched in consecrated ash, and each stroke tore a line of white fire through the wraiths' darkness. She did not fight elegantly. She fought *economically*, every motion a sentence with no wasted words, and around her feet the water churned and steamed.

"I said I'd hold the door," she called, not turning, driving two of the shapes back with a sweep that would have unhorsed a knight. "Nobody said anything about a *flood*. Whatever you're going to do, Kavanagh—do it fast."

"It's a rite, not a spell!" Bram shouted back, already wading again, hauling herself up onto the dais steps where the water shallowed to her shins. "It takes as long as it takes!"

"Then take less time!"

The dais rose in five broad steps, and each step was carved with words. Bram had seen them on her way in, disguised as a pilgrim, and had thought them decorative—a king's vanity, genealogies and victories etched in looping script. Now, with dragon-fire humming under her sternum, she read them properly and her blood went cold.

They were not Vhalcrestine. They were older. They were the script from Wren's drawings, from the sunken arches Bram had once swum through forty miles out to sea, from the ferryman's whispered stories of a family that had served Aelmarrow before Aelmarrow had a rival in the world.

*Binding-words.* The dais of the Hollow King was built on the last binding of the Hunger Below, and the kings of Vhalcrest had been sitting on it for three hundred years without knowing—or perhaps, some small traitorous part of her whispered, *knowing perfectly well.*

She knelt in the floodwater on the first step and laid both palms flat against the carved stone.

The spark in her chest surged toward her hands like a dog toward an open gate.

*Yes,* it said, in a voice that was not a voice but a heat, a pressure, a memory of wings vast enough to shadow a coastline. *Yes. Here. I know these words. I spoke them last.*

"You spoke them dying," Bram murmured. "That's different from speaking them living."

*All speech is a kind of burning,* said the spark, which had been a dragon once, or the last ember of one, and had opinions about the matter. *Begin.*

Bram closed her eyes. Found the rhythm Wren had taught her in the mountain winter, nights when the wind screamed and the child sang to keep the dark outside polite—syllables that rolled like surf, that tasted of brine and iron and old grief. She had thought she was learning nonsense sounds to comfort a homesick orphan. She had been learning the liturgy of a drowned kingdom, and its grammar was grief, and its verb tense was *remembering.*

She began to speak.

The words came out of her strangely, half her own voice and half something resonant underneath it, a second singer an octave lower, and where they fell on the carved stone the characters began to glow—a deep ember-orange, the color of the forge, the color of the egg-vault, the color of the last night of Aelmarrow as the spark remembered it and showed her unbidden:

*A sky the color of a bruise. Dragons wheeling over white towers, screaming in voices that cracked the clouds. The sea standing up out of its bed like a wall, and behind the wall, beneath the wall, inside the wall, a hunger with no shape opening mouths that were not mouths. And the dragons going down into it one by one, singing, singing, sealing it with their burning bodies, and the last one looking back at the shore—at a line of people on the cliffs holding lanterns, keeping the faith, keeping the fire—*

"—Kavanagh!" Corvane's voice, very close, very sharp.

Bram gasped and surfaced from the vision. The second step glowed. The water around her knees had gone warm as bathwater, and steam curled off it in slow ghosts.

"The third word-form is wrong," Corvane said. She had backed up the dais steps until she stood at Bram's shoulder, blade low, breathing hard. There was a gash above her eye painting half her face red. "I don't know the language. But the *shape* of it—the pattern in the carving repeats, look—" she pointed with two fingers, never taking her eyes off the hall, "—the spiral returns here, and here, and here. Your version doesn't spiral. It *stops.*"

Bram looked. Corvane was right. The binding-script wound inward like a shell, like a whirlpool, like the eye of the storm Bram had been born reading—and she had been pronouncing it linearly, common-speech fashion, start to finish, because that was how her own tongue worked.

"It's circular," she said. "It's a tide. It comes back on itself."

"Everything about this place comes back on itself," Corvane said grimly. "Fix it. They've stopped circling."

Bram looked up. In the flooded hall, the wraith-servants had halted, all of them at once, dozens of dark figures standing hip-deep in perfect stillness. Their hollow faces had turned—not toward Corvane, not toward Bram.

Toward the cage.

Toward Wren.

The song coming from the iron bars had changed again. The many harmonies had fallen away, and what remained was thin and young and fraying at the edges, a single small voice singing against an ocean—and Bram knew, with the sick certainty of a woman who has read weather her whole life, that she was hearing the last of the dragon-fire in Wren's chest. Guttering. Like a candle in a doorway with the storm coming in.

*It's feeding through her,* the spark said, and for the first time Bram heard fear in it. *The crown drinks the flame and pours it into the binding to keep itself sealed in its meat. That is why the vessel weakens. That is why it wanted her alive. Begin the third step, storm-daughter. The true way. Quickly.*

Bram set her palms on the third step and began again, and this time she let the words spiral, let them turn back on themselves the way a wave turns, the way grief turns, the way love turns—out and back, out and back—and the stone drank the fire and lit from within, and the heat of it rose up her arms and into her chest and she gave it everything, she gave it the loneliness of ten years of snowbound passes, she gave it the knock in the dark and the mud-caked girl on her doorstep, she gave it every frozen morning she had made breakfast for two and pretended it was for one—

The fourth step caught.

The fifth step, the last, lay directly beneath the throne. Beneath the king.

Between her and it stood six feet of drowned silence and a man-shaped absence whose tide-pool eyes had finally, slowly, begun to turn her way.

"Bram." Wren's voice. Small, cracked, *hers*—fighting up through whatever held her like a green shoot through pavement. "Bram, it's—there's a man in there. Underneath. He's so tired, Bram, he's so tired, he keeps saying her name, he keeps saying—"

"*Maren,*" said the Hollow King.

The voice was rust and rope and the creak of a hull, but beneath the ruin of it, buried deep, was a human timbre, and it broke on the name the way a heart breaks, quietly, from the inside.

"He remembers her," Wren whispered. "He never stopped. Bram—he's still *in there.*"

Bram's hands hovered over the final step, and the coal in her chest roared, and duty said *burn the binding, break the crown, end it, end it now while it's weak*—and love said *wait, wait, there is a man under there, there is a father under there, and if you burn blind you may burn him too.*

The water at the hall's edges began to rise faster. The wraith-servants took their first unified step toward the dais. Corvane planted her feet and raised her blade, and Bram heard her say, softly, almost to herself, in the accent of the border-farms she'd never spoken of in all their bitter miles together:

"Hold, then. We hold."

Bram looked at the king. At the cage. At the child inside it who had knocked on her door out of a snowstorm and taught her that a heart could be a country she hadn't known she belonged to.

She put her palms on the final step—and instead of the binding-words, she spoke the name.

"Maren's father," she said. "I need you to hear me. *Both* of you."

The tide-pool eyes fixed on her. Deep in their black, for the first time, something moved—something that was not the Hunger, something small and drowning and reaching upward, like a light seen through fathoms of sea.

And the crown upon the king's brow began, very softly, to laugh.
