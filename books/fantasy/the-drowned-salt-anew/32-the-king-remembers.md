## 32. The King Remembers

The throne room was drowning by inches.

Black water came through the great doors in slow, patient sheets, carrying salt and silt and the pale petals of drowned gardens that had not bloomed in three hundred years. It climbed the dais steps one by one, as if it too were coming to pay court. Above us the vaulted ceiling wept; somewhere far below, in the flooded undercity where I had woken an egg older than nations, something vast turned over in its sleep.

I knelt in the water with Wren's small body cradled against my chest, and I sang.

Not my song. Not any song of Thornfell or the smuggler roads or the storm-tongue my mother had beaten into me before she was taken. This was Aelmarrow's song — the lullaby Wren had hummed on my doorstep a lifetime ago, mud-caked and feral, speaking a language no living throat should know. I did not understand the words. I had never understood them. But I had heard them every night for two years, murmured into her blanket, into my shoulder, into the dark of our waystation while storms walked the passes outside, and grief has a melody that needs no translation.

Wren's voice came out of her like light through cracked glass.

She was not singing it herself. I knew that now, knew it the way you know a thing in your teeth. The Hunger Below had poured itself into her when the crown burned its last bridge, and what filled her was vast and cold and patient as the deep sea — but it had made a mistake, the same mistake all hungry things make. It had taken a vessel that remembered. And memory, once stirred, does not distinguish between what feeds it and what frees it.

So the lullaby rose out of her, in a voice layered strange — a child's voice with centuries underneath it, like a bell rung underwater.

*Hush, little gull, the tide is kind,*
*hush, little gull, the dark is only water—*

Corvane held the doorway behind me. I could hear her sword working, hear the wet shriek of things that had been courtiers once and were now only shapes the Hunger wore, hear her shouting words I would not let myself listen to because listening meant believing this could end here, with me kneeling in rising water, holding everything I loved against everything that had ever eaten a kingdom.

"Wren." I said her name against her ear. "Little bird. Listen to me."

Her eyes opened. They were wrong — tide-pool eyes, grey-green and depthless, with things drifting in them that had no business in a child. The Hunger looked out at me from inside my girl, and it smiled with her mouth, and the smile was the loneliest thing I had ever seen.

*You brought fire,* it said through her. *You brought the last spark of the last dragons into my house. Do you know how long I have waited to taste it? Three hundred years, storm-caller. Three hundred years of thin broth.* Her head tilted, birdlike, terrible. *And you love her. I can taste that too. Love is the sweetest thing there is. It is why I wear kings.*

"I know," I said.

And I did know. That was the thing Corvane had finally made me understand in the archives of ash, over candle stubs and forbidden pages: the Hunger did not hate. Hatred required a self, and it had none. It was only appetite wearing whatever shape fed it best. It had worn a young king named Aldric Vhalcrest because a grieving king was a door left open — his daughter dead of fever, his kingdom drowning, his prayers going up like smoke into a sky that answered nothing. Something below had answered instead.

*I can give you back your family,* the thing said, and Wren's voice shifted, softened, became for one horrible moment the voice of a woman I had buried twice — once in fact, once in exile. *Your mother, Bram Kavanagh. Your brothers. The sea keeps everyone. I keep the sea.*

The water around us shivered, and in it I saw them — faces in the flood, my mother's storm-grey eyes, my brother Tam's crooked grin, assembled out of brine and longing like a promise written in a language designed to be broken.

I had spent ten years learning to be invisible. Ten years of unlearning my own name, my own blood, my own sky. If ever there was a moment the old hunger in *me* should have risen — the hunger for home, for pardon, for the mother who died calling my name to come home — it was this one, kneeling in a drowning throne room with my heart's whole wish floating in the water at my lips.

"Come home," the water whispered in my mother's voice.

"No," I said. "She'd never ask it that way. She'd say *get up, you useless girl, and finish the work.*"

I kissed the top of Wren's head, right between the horns of shadow the Hunger had grown there, and I began the second half of the rite.

* * *

Corvane had taught it to me in the archives, her finger on a page so old the ink had gone the color of dried blood. *The binding was never chains,* she'd said. *Dragons don't chain. They remember. The last dragons bound the Hunger Below with memory itself — with the weight of everything Aelmarrow was. That's why it eats dragon-fire. Memory is the only food that starves it, and the only cage that holds it.*

*Then how do we free the king?* I'd asked.

*You don't pull a glove off a hand,* she'd said. *You remind the hand it isn't a glove.*

So I sang the lullaby, and I reached past the Hunger, past the tide-pool eyes, down into the drowned dark where a man had been buried alive inside his own body for three hundred years — and I gave him his daughter.

It was in Wren's voice, because it had to be. The Hunger had made one gift possible without meaning to: it had given the king a mouth again. Every word I shaped, Wren's throat shaped after me, the drowned tongue rolling out of her in syllables I could only mimic, trusting the child's borrowed memory to carry what my tongue could not.

*Aldric,* I said — the archives had given me his name, and names are doors. *Aldric Vhalcrest, son of the salt line, father of Maren.*

The water went still.

All of it. The sheets pouring through the doors, the flood climbing the dais, the black tide pressing at the high windows — everything stopped at once, the way a crowd stops when a name is spoken that no one expected to hear again.

*Maren,* I said again, in Wren's voice, in the drowned tongue. *Maren who was afraid of eels. Maren who hid your seal-ring in the flour barrel so you couldn't sign the treaty that would have sent her north. Maren who sang badly and knew it and sang anyway.*

*I never told anyone that,* Wren's mouth said — but the voice was different now. Thinner. Older. A man's voice worn down to a thread, coming up from very far beneath the sea. *How do you — who are you?*

"A friend," I said. "A keeper of fires. Your fire-keepers' daughters kept faith, king. Even in exile. Even branded traitor."

*Traitor,* he echoed, and something in the word hurt him; I felt it move through the water like a stone dropped down a well. *No. My fire-keepers stood at the gates on the last night. They stayed when everyone else ran. Where — * the thread of voice frayed, caught, pulled taut again — *where is Maren?*

And there it was. The wound the Hunger had worn like armor for three centuries. It had kept him alive on grief, fed him his own loss drop by drop, because a man looking for his daughter never looks up to see what's wearing his face.

I gathered Wren closer. Her body was ice-cold and burning at once, shaking with the effort of being two beings and a bridge between them, and I could feel the dragon-spark in her chest guttering like a lamp out of oil. Whatever I was going to do, I had moments, not minutes.

"She's here," I said gently. "Listen."

And I sang the lullaby again — but this time I changed it. I put Maren into it. I took every fragment Wren had ever taught me, every scrap of the drowned tongue I'd absorbed across two winters of fireside lessons, and I built a memory out of them the way you build a fire out of wet wood: patiently, stubbornly, feeding it everything I had.

*A king in a tower with his daughter on his knee,*
*a storm coming in off the salt,*
*and he tells her: hush, little gull, the tide is kind,*
*the dark is only water,*
*and the water remembers everything it loves—*

*Stop,* the king said. *Please. Stop. I know this. I know this night. She had a fever — no — that was later — this was before, this was the good night, the night the storm missed us, she fell asleep in my arms and I carried her to bed and I thought, I thought, nothing will ever touch her, I will burn the world first—*

"You did burn the world," I said softly. "Or something wore your hands while it did. But that night was real, Aldric. That night is still real. It's the realest thing left in this room. He can wear your face for three hundred years, but he can't have that night. He was never in it."

The silence that followed was the deepest I had ever heard — deeper than snowbound passes, deeper than the flooded vaults where the egg slept. It was the silence of a man standing at the bottom of himself, looking up.

Then the water began to weep.

Not rise. Weep. Salt tears moved through the flood like silver threads, and the tide-pool eyes overflowed, and Wren's small face — no, *his* face moving beneath hers, surfacing through hers like a drowning man breaking toward air — contorted with three hundred years of grief that had never once been allowed out.

*Maren,* he sobbed, in a voice that shook the silt off the rafters. *My gull. My little gull. I looked for you everywhere. I looked in the dark places. I looked so long I forgot which of us was lost.*

"I know," I whispered. "I know. Come up, king. Come up and see what he's done with your house."

*He — * the grief curdled into something harder, and I felt the change in the water, a shift of current, a gathering. *He showed me her face. All this time. He held her face just above me and said if I slept, if I stopped feeding him, he would let her drown again. Over and over. Three hundred years of almost-saving her.* The tide-pool eyes narrowed, and the things drifting in them scattered like fish before a shark. *There was never anything to save. There was only him. There was only ever him, and me, asleep at the wheel of my own soul.*

"Yes," I said. "And you're awake now."

*Am I?* And oh, the fear in it — the terror of a man who has been a prisoner so long he cannot trust his own waking. *What am I, storm-caller? What's left?*

I thought about how to answer. I thought about exile, about the ten years I had spent believing that what the Vigilant Order cut away from me was the truest part of myself. I thought about a mud-caked child on a doorstep who had become the whole architecture of my life.

"What's left," I said, "is the father who carried his daughter to bed. That's the part he could never digest. That's why he keeps you buried. Not because you're dangerous, Aldric. Because you're *kind*, and kindness is the one thing he cannot wear."

The king of a drowned kingdom, wearing the stolen body of a hollowed man, looked at me across three centuries — and laughed. It was a broken sound, a rusted hinge of a sound, but it was *his*, and the water rang with it.

*Kind,* he said. *My marshals called me soft. My wife called it worse. And now a smuggler kneels in my flood telling me it's the weapon.* His gaze — Wren's gaze, clearing now, the drifting shapes sinking out of the grey-green like sediment settling — moved down to the child in my arms. *What is she?*

"The last vessel of dragon-fire," I said. "And my daughter. In every way that matters, which is all of them."

*Then you understand,* he said, and the tenderness in it undid me more than the grief had. *You understand exactly what he will do to get her.*

"He already tried," I said. "He offered me my dead."

*And you refused.*

"My mother raised a stubborn woman."

Behind me, steel rang on something that was not steel, and Corvane's voice cut through, ragged but iron-hard: "Bram! Whatever you're doing — the walls are giving! I need you to be *done*!"

"Not yet," I called back. "One more thing."

I leaned close to Wren's ear, to the king beneath the Hunger, to the man surfacing through the child like light through water.

"She's dying, Aldric. The fire in her is guttering because he's drinking it. If you're still in there — if any part of you is still the man from the good night — then help me. Turn against him. From the inside. Now, while he's weak, while the binding's loose, while he's busy hating me. You've been his glove for three hundred years." I bared my teeth at the dark flooding through the doors, at the shapes Corvane was holding back, at the ancient patience coiled in my daughter's chest. "Clench your fist."

For a long moment, nothing. The water hung. The Hunger watched me through Wren's eyes with something that might have been amusement, might have been the first flicker of doubt an appetite had ever felt.

Then the king spoke one word, in the drowned tongue, in a voice like a mountain remembering it could fall:

*Maren's father says no.*

And the Hollow King's body — the man-shaped absence on the drowned throne, the vessel the Hunger had worn like a favorite coat for three hundred years — *moved*. Against it. Arms locking rigid, jaw clamping shut on the thing's rising howl, hands tearing themselves away from the armrests they had gripped for thirty generations and closing instead around the crown on his own head, as if to tear it off, as if to drag the parasite into the open air where fire could reach it—

The Hunger screamed with Wren's throat, and the scream was not a child's sound, was not any living sound, and every window in the palace burst inward at once, and the sea itself rose up against the walls of Vhalcrest like a fist.

"It's letting go!" Corvane shouted, bloody, blazing, magnificent in the doorway. "Bram — it's leaving him — *where is it going to go?*"

I already knew. I looked down at the girl in my arms, at the tide-pool eyes draining, clearing, filling with her own terror at last — and beyond her, at the egg I had woken sleeping in its vault below, full of the only fire in the world the Hunger still feared.

The water around us went suddenly, terribly warm.

"Oh no," I breathed. "Oh, little bird — *hold on to me.*"
