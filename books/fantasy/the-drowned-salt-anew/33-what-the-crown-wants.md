## 33. What the Crown Wants

The throne room was drowning.

Bram felt it before she saw it—water sliding across the marble in a black glass sheet, ankle-deep and rising, carrying with it the smell of a sea that had no business being forty miles inland. The dead of Aelmarrow stood in the flooded hall like reeds in a marsh, hundreds of them, salt-white and patient, their hollow eyes fixed on the dais where the king sat slumped in his crown.

And Wren—

Wren hung in the air above the steps.

Not floating, exactly. *Held.* Lifted by nothing Bram could see, her small body arched backward, arms out, hair drifting around her face as though she were suspended underwater rather than above it. From her chest came a light that was not light at all but memory—the dragon-fire, guttering now, its gold shot through with veins of hungry dark that spread like ink dropped in milk.

"No," Bram said. She did not remember starting to run. "No—*Wren!*"

Corvane caught her arm at the edge of the dais steps, and it cost the marshal something to do it; Bram could feel the tremor in the grip, see the blood sheeting down Corvane's left side from a wound she had taken holding back the wraith-servants in the antechamber. Behind them both, the doors they'd come through groaned against the press of bodies—not the dead, who did not push, but the Court's remaining guards, hammering, screaming, or simply gone silent in ways that were worse than screaming.

"Bram." Corvane's voice was raw. "You can't touch it while it's feeding. You'll give it exactly what it wants."

"Then tell me what I *can* do," Bram snarled, "because I am not standing here watching it drink my child."

The word was out before she could weigh it. *My child.* Ten months ago she had been a woman who guided smugglers through snow for coin and spoke to no one longer than a transaction required. Now the word came out of her like a tooth pulled—bloody, undeniable, hers.

Above the steps, the thing wearing the king made a sound.

It was not laughter. It was the sound the sea makes when it finds a hole in a seawall—a long, satisfied exhalation, wet and vast, drawn up from somewhere below the floor of the world. The king's body convulsed with it. Up close, in the storm-light bleeding through the shattered clerestory windows, Bram could see what three centuries of tenancy had done to him: he was less a man than an impression of one, features worn smooth as river stones, skin the gray of old oyster shell. Only his eyes remained vivid—tide-pool eyes, green-black and shifting, and in their depths things moved that had never been born.

The crown sat on his head like a barnacle on a hull. Black iron worked into shapes that might have been waves or might have been teeth, crusted with salt that wept continuously down his temples in slow mineral tears.

"*Kavanagh,*" the king said—or the thing said, using the king's ruined throat like a flute with a crack in it. "Storm-daughter. Fire-keeper's get."

"You know my line," Bram said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Somewhere behind her ribs, the spark she'd carried up from the undercity—the ember of true dragon-fire, woken from the egg among her ancestors' bones—pulsed like a second heart. It had been pulsing since the vault. It pulsed harder now, answering the dimming flame in Wren's chest the way one fire answers another across a dark room.

"I knew your grandmother," the thing said. "I knew her mother. Your family kept the binding for nine generations, little keeper. They fed the fire. They sang the salt to sleep." The tide-pool eyes rolled toward Wren's suspended body with something horribly like tenderness. "And then your line broke faith, and here we all are."

"My line didn't break anything," Bram said. "Your *court* hunted them down one by one and called it policy."

"Mm." The sound was almost amused. "The victors write the histories. Even the ones who are only borrowing the victory."

The water rose past their knees. Bram felt it tug at her, gentle as a hand on the hem—not pulling her under, she realized, but *inviting*. The whole drowned hall had the quality of a held breath. Even the dead of Aelmarrow had gone still, heads tilted, listening.

And then the Hunger let go of the king.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next. The tide-pool eyes went suddenly, terribly empty—the emptiness of a house whose owner has stepped out—and the king's body sagged forward on the throne like a coat slipping off a hook. For one naked instant Bram saw what remained underneath: an old man, impossibly old, worn thin as communion wafer, and on his face an expression she would carry to her grave.

*Relief.*

He mouthed something. No sound came. But Bram had spent ten years reading weather like scripture, and she read lips the way she read cloud-shapes, and she knew what he said.

*Thank you. Hurry.*

Then the darkness moved.

It poured out of the king's eyes and ears and mouth like smoke given weight, a column of hungry night that did not fall so much as *flow upward*, defying every law of water and light, and it crossed the space between throne and child in a single sinuous motion—and entered Wren through her open mouth.

The scream that followed was not Wren's. That was the worst of it. It came from everywhere—from the water, from the walls, from the throats of the dead—and it was the sound of something vast cramming itself into a vessel far too small, furious and ecstatic at once.

Wren's small body jerked like a hooked fish. The guttering gold of the dragon-fire flared, fought, dimmed. And then her feet touched the top step, and she straightened, and she turned her head, and Bram's heart stopped.

Her eyes were tide-pools now too.

"Wren—" Bram started up the steps and Corvane's grip became an anchor, hauling her back, and Bram nearly broke the marshal's wrist doing it. "*Let go of me—*"

"She's still in there," Corvane said, fast and low. "Look at her hands. Look."

Bram looked. Wren's hands hung at her sides—but the fingers moved, small restless patterns, the signing language they'd invented together over the winter, the one they used for words too big or too frightening for common speech. Over and over, the same sign: *wait. wait. wait.*

She was still fighting. Eight years old, and she was fighting something older than nations from inside her own stolen body, and she was telling Bram to wait.

Bram waited. It was the hardest thing she had ever done.

* * *

The thing spoke with Wren's mouth, and the wrongness of it was physical—a nausea that started in the teeth.

"Well," it said, and Wren's voice layered over itself into a chorus of drowned voices, "this is *cozy*."

The water had reached their waists. Around the hall, the dead of Aelmarrow had begun to move—not toward the dais, but into formation, ranks closing like a tide organizing itself. Corvane had her sword half-drawn; Bram put a hand over the scabbard and pushed it down. Not yet. Not while Wren's fingers still signed *wait*.

"What do you want?" Bram called.

"What I have always wanted." The child-thing descended two steps, unhurried, water parting around its bare feet. "To be full. To stop being hungry. Do you know what it is to be empty for three hundred years, Kavanagh? To wear man after man after man and taste nothing? Kings are *thin*. Thin broth. This one lasted longest because he had a daughter he loved more than his kingdom, and love has some substance to it, but even love runs out." The tide-pool eyes blinked with Wren's lashes. "But this—" a small hand pressed flat against the child's own chest, over the banked coals of the dragon-fire "—this is a *feast*. Memory of the first morning of the world. Fire that remembers being fire. I could live on this for a thousand years."

"And then you'll be hungry again," Bram said. "That's the shape of you. You eat everything and stay empty. That's not hunger, that's just—you. A hole wearing a hunger's name."

Something flickered across the borrowed face. Anger, maybe. Or recognition.

"But you didn't take me all the way down here to gloat," Bram pressed. She was guiding smugglers again, reading the current, finding the eddy. "Crowns don't negotiate. So say it."

The thing smiled with Wren's mouth, and Bram wanted to tear the smile off the world.

"Clever," it said. "Yes. I know what you want, storm-daughter. Shall I show you?"

It raised one small hand, and the water rose with it—and in the water, images.

Bram had seen scrying before. The Vigilant Order's diviners bent over their bowls like sick women; smuggler hedge-witches read futures in harbor foam. This was nothing like that. This was memory given flesh, rising out of the flood like corpses rising out of a wreck, and the first of them was her mother.

*Her mother*, standing in the kitchen of the house above Thornfell pass, flour to the elbows, laughing at something Bram's father had said—laughing with her whole body the way she had, head back, eyes shut, before the Order came and the fire came and the word *traitor* got carved into the family stone. Her father beside her, young, whole, his hand resting easy on the shoulder of a girl of nineteen who stood in the doorway with a pack on her back and a storm in her blood and no idea that she would never see either of them alive again.

"No," Bram whispered.

"They died because of the binding," the thing said gently, horribly. "Because your family fed the fire that starved me, and the Order's hardliners burned what the crown couldn't reach. Every death in your line traces back to the same root. Me. My hunger. My imprisonment." The images multiplied—her mother at the hearth, her father mending nets, the smell of bread she had not smelled in twenty years rendered so perfectly that her mouth flooded with it. "I did not kill them, Kavanagh. But I can *give them back*."

"That's a lie," Corvane said. "That's what it does. It wears the dead."

"It wears the dead *badly*," the thing agreed, unbothered. "A poor copy. But consider: a poor copy of your mother, or no mother at all. Which does the orphan choose?" The tide-pool eyes found Bram's and held them, and the terrible thing—the unforgivable thing—was that they were patient. Kind, even. The patience of a fisherman watching a hook. "And there is more. Your exile. Undone. Your name cleared in every archive of the Vigilant Order—I will wear the regency next, once these frightened men exhaust themselves; I will wear it for centuries, and the first decree of the new reign restores Bram Kavanagh, storm-caller, daughter of Thornfell, to her house and her honor. Your family's sigil flown again over the pass. The word *traitor* chiseled off the stone and the stone polished smooth."

The water showed that too: the house rebuilt, the pass green, a life with the sharp edges sanded off. And in the corner of the vision, a detail so small and so cruel that Bram's breath caught: her mother's garden, replanted, the rowan tree growing again that had been cut down for kindling the winter the Order came.

"I don't want copies," Bram heard herself say. Her voice sounded very far away. "I want—"

"You want what everyone wants. What was taken." The thing tilted Wren's head, and the gesture was pure child, pure Wren, and Bram understood suddenly how deep the danger ran—not that the Hunger could deceive her, but that it had learned, in three hundred years of wearing people, exactly how to be loved. "Kneel, storm-daughter. Swear the old oath your grandmothers swore, keeper to crown, fire to hunger, and I will release this child unharmed. I need only a vessel willing enough not to fight me from within. The kings fought. That is why they wore out so fast. But you—you understand necessity. You understand *cost*. You've been paying it since you were nineteen years old."

The water rose past their chests. Somewhere beyond the walls, the sea was coming for Vhalcrest, and the dead were coming with it, and the doors would not hold much longer.

"Kneel," the Hunger said, in Wren's voice, in her mother's voice, in a chorus of every voice it had ever eaten, "and have everything back."

* * *

And Bram—who had spent twenty years wanting exactly this, who had lain awake in cold waystations composing the letter of appeal she would never send, who had rehearsed her vindication in the dark like a prayer—found, to her astonishment, that the wanting had a floor.

She looked at the vision of her mother and saw, finally, what it was: flour without warmth. Laughter without the woman. A rowan tree grown from a seed of appetite, which would bear fruit that tasted of nothing, forever. The Hunger could copy the shape of everything she had lost and none of the substance, because the substance of her mother had never been the flour or the laugh—it had been the *choosing*, the thousand daily choices of a woman who loved her family freely, and a copy could not choose. A copy could only be worn.

Like the king. Like the poor worn-out man on the throne behind the thing, mouthing *hurry*.

"You're right about one thing," Bram said.

The tide-pool eyes narrowed.

"I do understand cost." She waded forward, one step, two, water dragging at her like hands. "I've paid it my whole life. And here's what the payment taught me, worm-in-the-crown: everything worth having is worth having *freely*. My mother's love wasn't real because someone gave me her shape back. It was real because she chose it, every day, until the day they took the choosing away." Another step. The ember in her chest was blazing now, answering the dying fire in Wren's, close enough to touch. "So no. I won't kneel. I won't swear your oath. And I'm going to take my daughter back."

For a moment the hall went utterly still—even the water, even the dead.

Then Wren's small body shuddered, and the fingers at her sides stopped signing *wait*—and began, frantically, desperately, signing something new.

*Now,* they said. *Now. Now. NOW.*

And Bram reached into her chest, took hold of the last dragon-fire in the world, and drew it out.
