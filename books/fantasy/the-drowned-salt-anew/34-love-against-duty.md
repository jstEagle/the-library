## 34. Love Against Duty

The throne room of Vhalcrest had become a drowned cathedral.

Water stood knee-deep across the marble, black and moving with a slow tidal breath, though the sea was a mile beyond the walls. Salt crusted the pillars in white weeping lines. The risen dead of Aelmarrow had broken through the eastern doors an hour ago—or perhaps they had simply walked through them, the way water walks through a net—and now they lined the walls in their hundreds, silent, patient, their salt-white faces turned toward the throne like flowers toward a sun that had gone out three hundred years ago.

Bram stood in the flood with lightning still crawling along her knuckles, and the Hunger Below spoke to her in her mother's voice.

*You are so tired, little storm.*

It came from everywhere—from the water, from the walls, from the hollow thing on the throne that had once been a king. The voice was warm. That was the worst of it. It was warm the way a hearth is warm, the way a bed is warm after ten years of sleeping in snowbound waystations with your boots half-laced in case you had to run.

*I can give it all back,* the voice said, and now it wore her father's timbre, roughened by pipe smoke. *Your name, unburned from every ledger. Your exile, ended. Your family—*

Bram's breath caught. She hated that it caught. She hated that some starved animal part of her leaned toward the voice like a plant toward light.

In the cage beside the throne, Wren hung against the bars, small and gray, and the fire in her chest—the last dragon-fire in the world, the ember that had kept the Hunger bound for three centuries—flickered like a candle in a draft. Each flicker dimmed. Each dimming made the water rise another inch.

*Your mother,* the voice crooned. *I remember her. I remember everyone. I could set her at this table again, pouring tea, scolding you for tracking storm-mud across her clean floors. Your father humming in his workshop. The house in Lowmarsh before the Vigilant Order came with its chains and its brand. I do not restore what was, Bram Kavanagh. I *restore*. There is a difference. What I make will never leave you.*

The water lapped at her thighs. Somewhere behind her, Corvane was fighting—a wet ringing clash of steel against things that did not bleed—but Bram could not turn to look. Looking away from the throne felt like looking away from a precipice edge.

"You're lying," she said. Her voice sounded small in all that drowned grandeur. "That's what you do. You wear dead men and speak in dead voices and you lie."

*Am I?* The thing on the throne shifted. Up close, in the witchlight of her own storm, the Hollow King was worse than any corpse: a man-shaped absence, robes hanging on nothing much, eyes like tide-pools where something vast and patient turned over in the dark. *Ask yourself what you would not give. You have already given everything else. Your name. Your country. Ten years of silence while the sky forgot you. What is one more refusal worth, when no one is left to see it?*

And there it was. The knife turned just so. Not temptation of pleasure—temptation of *rest*. Of putting down the weight.

Bram thought of the waystation. Of the first night, the mud-caked girl on her doorstep speaking a language no living throat should know, and how Bram had stood in the doorway with her hand on her knife and felt, instead of fear, something she hadn't felt in a decade: *needed.* Not as a guide, not as a smuggler's tool, not as a storm to be aimed. Needed as herself.

She thought of teaching Wren the word for *bread*, and Wren teaching her the word for *home* in a tongue that belonged to a kingdom under forty fathoms of cold water, and how the two words had somehow come to mean the same place.

"No," Bram said.

The word fell into the flood and the flood went still.

*No?*

"I know what you're doing." She waded forward, one step, two, water dragging at her legs like hands. "You offered Corvane her Order whole. You offered the Grandmaster his secrets kept forever. You offer everyone the shape of their own heart, because you learned long ago that hearts open easier than doors." Another step. Lightning ran down her arm and died hissing in the brine. "But here's what you don't understand about me, you ancient hungry thing. I spent ten years learning to want nothing. Do you know what that makes me?"

The tide-pool eyes regarded her.

"Free," Bram said. "You can't buy what isn't for sale. You can't threaten someone who already lost everything and built a life out of the losing. And you can't—" her voice cracked, and she let it crack, let him hear it, "—you cannot have that child. Not for my mother's voice. Not for my father's workshop. Not for a pardon written in gold on vellum. I chose exile once because duty told me to. I'm choosing *her* now because I choose it. That's the difference between us. Everything I do from this moment is mine."

For the first time in three hundred years, something wearing a king flinched.

Then the warmth went out of the voice, and what remained was the sound of deep water under ice, the sound of pressure, of things crushed slowly in the dark.

*Then burn with her.*

The flood rose.

It came up the walls, up the pillars, a black wall of seawater folding over them like the palm of a god, and Bram heard Corvane shout her name, heard steel ring somewhere far above, and then the world was green-dark roar and salt and the taste of Aelmarrow's drowning night. She was thrown against the dais steps. Her lungs burned. Through the churning water she saw the cage—saw Wren pressed against the bars, hair floating like weed, mouth open in a scream Bram couldn't hear but knew anyway, knew in her bones, in the storm-blood her grandmother's grandmother had passed down like a coal wrapped in cloth:

*Mine. She is mine. Mine to keep.*

Bram reached into her chest and took hold of the spark.

It was small. That was the terrible joke of it. Three days ago, in the flooded vaults beneath this palace, kneeling among the bones of her own ancestors—the fire-keepers, the keepers, the ones who had carried this duty generation to generation until the line forgot itself—she had woken a dragon egg older than maps, and it had given her a spark of true dragon-fire, and it had shown her Aelmarrow's final night in a vision that still woke her screaming. All that power, all that inheritance, and what lived in her chest now was no bigger than a grain of wheat.

But a grain of wheat does not ask the field's permission.

The spark answered her the way storms answer her—not as a servant answers, but as kin. It rose along her arm, under her skin, golden light veining her flesh like lightning trapped in amber, and the water around her began to boil and steam. She broke the surface gasping, hair plastered to her skull, and the whole drowned hall turned toward her—the dead, the wraith-things, the hollow king—all of them turning, because dragon-fire is the oldest light there is, and even the drowned remember it.

*Bram!* Corvane, bloodied, back against a pillar, holding three wraith-servants at bay with a marshal's blade that smoked where it touched them. *The rite—you have to finish the rite—*

"There's no time for the rite." Bram hauled herself up the dais steps, water cascading off her, each step lit gold. "There's only time for this."

*What are you doing?* The Hunger's voice had lost its music. Underneath it now was something rawer. Something almost like fear. *The binding requires the rite. The rite requires hours. You would waste the spark—you would spend the last fire on a single blow—*

"Yes," Bram said.

She reached the throne.

Up close, the Hollow King was dying—that was the thing no one had said plainly enough. Whatever the parasite had made of him, the man beneath was still in there, buried deep, and Bram had seen him for one moment in the audience hall: a flicker behind the tide-pool eyes when Wren sang. A man-shaped grief, three hundred years old, still trying to surface.

She put her hand flat against the crown.

It was colder than the water. Colder than the deep places. It pulsed against her palm like a second heart, greedy and slow, and through the contact the Hunger poured itself into her mind—not the warm voice now, not the lies, but everything: the hunger itself, aeons of it, the memory of feeding, of kingdoms worn like gloves and discarded, of dragons chaining it in fire and dying for the privilege. It showed her its age. It showed her its certainty. *Nothing burns forever, little keeper. Even your spark will gutter. I have waited longer than your species has had names.*

"I know," Bram said. "But I don't have to burn forever. I just have to burn *now*."

She thought of Wren's face when she'd first said the child's name—how Wren had tried it in the drowned tongue, *wren, wren*, tasting a new self. She thought of the crevasse in the whiteout, her hand closing on a small wrist, the moment she had stopped being a woman hiding from the world and started being a mother, though neither of them had used the word yet. She thought of the plan she'd whispered to Corvane that morning, the one with the rite, the careful rite, the rite that might save both king and child—

—and she let it go. Duty had drawn her the careful path. Love drew a shorter one.

"*Aelmarrow remembers you,*" she said in the drowned tongue, the words Wren had taught her, clumsy on her tongue and true anyway. "*Fire remembers longer.*"

She drove the spark into the crown.

The world went white.

Not light exactly—more like the memory of light, the first fire, the fire before fire had names, the heat at the center of the egg her ancestors had guarded through the fall of a kingdom. It went through her arm and out through her palm and into the crown, and the crown *screamed*. Every dead thing in the hall screamed with it, a soundless shriek that bent the air, and the black flood recoiled from the dais in a great retreating wave, and the wraith-servants tore apart like smoke in wind.

The crown burned. Black fire first—the Hunger's own essence, its hoarded centuries igniting—and then gold, dragon-gold, the color of the egg's heart, and the two fires did not mix. They *warred*. Bram held on. She could feel the spark spending itself, grain by grain, wheat by wheat, pouring out of her chest into the iron, and she could feel the Hunger tearing at her in return, flooding her with visions: her mother's kitchen, her father's hands, the house in Lowmarsh rebuilt and warm and *waiting*, all of it offered one last time, frantically, a merchant dumping his whole inventory on the table.

"Get out of my house," Bram said, and burned it all.

The visions went. The warmth went. What remained was only the thing itself, vast and furious and—for the first time in three hundred years—*wounded*, and Bram held the crown with both hands now, though the metal seared her palms, and she fed the fire everything. The storm-blood her line had kept hidden for generations. The loneliness of ten years. The fury of every village with salt in its wells. The grief of a drowned kingdom that had waited three centuries for someone to say *you may rest now.*

The crown split.

A crack ran through it, thin as a hair, singing—a pure high note like a struck bell—and through the crack poured light, gold and black together, spiraling up toward the vaulted ceiling in a column that punched through the roof tiles and out into the storm-dark sky above Vhalcrest. Every soul in the city must have seen it. Every dead thing on the roads outside the walls stopped mid-stride and turned toward it, salt faces lifted, waiting.

The Hollow King came apart.

Not violently—that was the mercy no one had predicted. The absence that wore him drained downward, out through the split crown, into the fire, and what was left standing on the dais was suddenly, terribly *small*: an old man in rotted robes, flesh like wet parchment, and eyes that were only eyes now, brown and human and drowning in tears.

He looked at Bram. He looked past her, at the cage—at Wren, who had dragged herself upright against the bars, gray-faced, her chest barely glowing at all now, the fire nearly spent.

"Is she—" the king's voice was a ruin, barely a whisper, but it was *his*, the first time in three centuries the mouth had spoken with its owner inside it. "Is she the one who sings? My daughter used to sing. Before. She used to—"

"She sings," Bram said. Her hands were burned to the bone and she could not stop smiling, or crying, or both. "She sings beautifully, Your Majesty. And you're free. Do you hear me? You're free."

The old king smiled. It transformed his face, that smile—it was the face from the coins, from the murals, young somehow despite everything, a father's face.

"Thank you," he said. "Oh—thank you. Let me—let me sit. Let me see the sky. I haven't seen—"

He sat down on the steps of his own dais, like a tired man at the end of a very long day, and looked up through the broken roof at the storm clouds churning over Vhalcrest, and Bram watched the life go out of him gently, between one breath and the next, a man dying free for the first time in three hundred years.

The crown lay split on the dais, dark now, cold, its fires spent. The column of light faded. In the sudden quiet, water dripped from the vaulted ceiling, and the dead of Aelmarrow stood motionless along the walls, and the flood had begun, very slowly, to recede.

"Bram." Corvane's hand on her shoulder, gripping hard, keeping her upright. "Bram, the girl—"

Bram turned.

Wren had slumped against the bars of the cage. The glow in her chest was a thread now, a filament, guttering. Her eyes found Bram's across the ruined hall, and they were frightened, and they were trusting, and they were asking a question no eight-year-old should ever have to ask.

Bram waded toward her through the draining flood, over the body of a kingdom's jailer, past the dead who bowed their salt-white heads as she passed—and the last of the dragon-spark flickered in her chest like a candle in a window, like a lamp left burning for someone coming home through the dark.

"Hold on," Bram said, reaching for the cage lock with hands that no longer had fingerprints. "Hold on, little bird. I'm getting you out. I'm getting you out, and then we're going home."

But the light was going out, and the cage lock was rusted shut, and somewhere far below the palace, in the flooded vaults where her ancestors' bones kept their long watch, something ancient and dormant had begun—very faintly, very patiently—to tap against the inside of its shell.
