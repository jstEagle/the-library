## 35. The Last Dragon-Fire

The throne room of Vhalcrest was drowning.

Not in water—not yet—but in something older than water, a pressure that pressed against Bram's eardrums like the deep places of the sea, like the moment before a storm breaks when the air itself seems to hold its breath. The tide had come up through the palace foundations behind her, black and patient, carrying with it the drowned dead of Aelmarrow, and now it stood ankle-deep across the marble floor, still as glass, reflecting the shattered dome above where lightning crawled like veins of fire across a bruised sky.

Bram knelt on the flooded stones with her hands buried to the wrist in dragon-fire.

It did not burn her. That was the miracle she would never be able to explain afterward, not to Corvane, not to the smugglers who owed her their lives, not even to herself in the long quiet years that followed. The spark she had carried up from the vault beneath the palace—the ember woken from an egg three hundred years asleep, guarded by the salt-white bones of her own ancestors—moved through her like recognition. Like a name spoken in a voice she'd been waiting all her life to hear. Her mother's blood. Her grandmother's blood. The fire-keepers of Aelmarrow, who had carried the last dragon's hope into exile and let it dim down through generations until only one woman remained, a traitor and a smuggler's guide, kneeling in a drowning hall with a child's life cupped between her palms.

*Hold it true,* the vision had told her, or something like it—the memory of Aelmarrow's final night, the dragons turning to face the Hunger Below, choosing to burn rather than be eaten. *Fire remembers what flesh forgets.*

The crown was screaming.

It sat upon the king's head—or what remained of the king—and it screamed without sound, a shriek that lived in the teeth and the marrow, in the floodwater shivering around Bram's knees. The thing inside it, the Hunger Below, the parasite that had worn three hundred years of kings like gloves worn through at the fingers, had finally understood what she was doing. It had abandoned subtlety. It had abandoned the king entirely, pouring itself out through the eye-holes of the crown in ropes of darkness that writhed toward Wren—

And found nothing to seize. Because Bram had already taken the fire from the child.

That was the terrible arithmetic of it, the bargain she had made in the space between one heartbeat and the next when the Hunger turned from its dying vessel toward the living one. She had reached into Wren's chest—not with hands, but with the storm-blood in her own veins, the inheritance she had spent ten years burying—and drawn out the guttering flame. All of it. Every last coal of dragon-fire memory that had kept the binding alive for three centuries, that had burned in a line of vessels from Aelmarrow's fall to this mud-caked girl who had knocked on a waystation door in the snow.

Wren lay crumpled at the foot of the dais, small and terribly still, her breath coming in shallow pulls that fogged the cold air and then didn't. Her eyes were closed. Her lips were gray. And where the light of the fire had always lived behind her face—that strange, sea-deep luminance that Bram had first seen in a dark doorway two winters ago—there was nothing now. Only a child. Only an eight-year-old girl who sang songs of a drowned kingdom and drew maps no mapmaker could read and called Bram by a word in the old tongue that meant, as near as Bram could ever translate it, *the-one-who-stayed.*

The fire in Bram's hands roared.

She drove it upward. Into the crown. Into the thing wearing the crown.

The world went white.

---

She had expected resistance. She had braced for the Hunger to fight, to coil around the flame and smother it as it must have smothered so many fires before, generation after generation of vessels ground down and devoured. But the dragon-fire did not fight the way fire fights. It fought the way grief fights. It fought the way love fights—without strategy, without retreat, with the whole of itself given at once.

The spark remembered Aelmarrow burning. It remembered the dragons rising from the harbor with their wings full of storm, remembered the last queen pressing her infant daughter's hand to an egg still warm, remembered every keeper since who had passed the ember down like a candle through a long dark corridor, each one watching it shrink, each one knowing they would not live to see it matter. Three hundred years of small stubborn flames. All of it was in Bram's hands now, and all of it went into the crown at once, and the crown—which had eaten kingdoms, which had eaten kings, which had drunk the memories of the dead like wine—could not swallow a fire that refused, finally, to be swallowed alone.

The darkness came apart.

Not all at once. It peeled away from the throne in sheets, in tatters, shrieking that soundless shriek, and where it tore the floodwater boiled and steamed and the marble beneath showed clean for the first time in centuries. Bram held on. Lightning fell through the broken dome and struck the dais and she rode it, she *was* it, storm-caller and fire-keeper both, the two exiled halves of her blood joined at last in the oldest work her line had ever known. She heard Corvane shouting somewhere behind her—heard steel ring against wraith-flesh, heard the marshal's voice crack on a prayer to an order that had never taught prayers—and she could not turn, could not spare anything, because the Hunger was dying and dying things are most dangerous, and part of it still clung to the man beneath the crown.

The king opened his eyes.

They were not tide-pools anymore. Whatever the parasite had made of them—those flat, depthless mirrors that had watched Bram across the throne room with the patience of geology—they were clearing now, like silt settling out of troubled water, like a sky after storm. And behind them, surfacing for the first time in three hundred years, was a face.

He was old. He should have been dust. But the face that emerged from under the crown was a young man's face, hollowed and wondering, the face of someone waking from a nightmare so long he had forgotten the shape of his own bed. His mouth moved. No sound came. His hands rose, trembling, and touched the crown as if touching a snake he had slept beside for lifetimes without knowing.

*Bram,* said a voice that was not a voice, everywhere and nowhere, vast and failing. *Bram Kavanagh.*

She knew that voice. She had heard it once before, in the vault beneath the palace, in the vision of Aelmarrow's final night—a king's voice, a father's voice, calling for his daughter as the sea came over the walls.

*You carry my kingdom's fire,* the dead-and-dying king said. *You carry my daughter's hope.* A sound that might have been laughter, broken and wet. *I had forgotten there was anything left to carry.*

"Hold on," Bram said aloud, and her voice came out wrecked, smoke-scraped, human. "The rite isn't finished. I can separate you—I can—"

*There is nothing left to separate, fire-keeper.* The king's hands fell away from the crown. He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Bram had ever seen, sadder than the drowned fields, sadder than the child lying gray-lipped at the dais's foot. *Only me. Only the shell it leaves behind. Finish it. Let me see the sky once with my own eyes before—*

The crown split.

It did not shatter. It *opened*, like a seed, like a fist unclenching after three hundred years, and the dragon-fire poured into the gap and filled it and the Hunger Below—what remained of it, the root of the thing, older than nations, hungrier than oceans—burned.

Bram felt it die. There was no other word. She felt the vast cold attention that had pressed against the world's skin for centuries contract, thrash, and go out, like a lamp dropped down a well, falling away from everything it had ever touched. The pressure in the air lifted. The floodwater around her knees sighed and began, slowly, to recede, sliding back down through the palace foundations toward the harbor, toward the sea, toward the drowned kingdom that would soon—at last, at last—be allowed to sleep.

And the king came off the throne.

He came off it the way a coat comes off a hook. What was left folded down onto the steps of the dais, a slight figure in rotted robes, and the crown rolled from his head and rang against the marble and lay still, cracked down the middle, smoking faintly, empty. Bram caught him before he struck the ground. He weighed almost nothing. Three hundred years had eaten everything except the last thin hour of himself.

His eyes found hers. They were brown. Ordinary brown. Eyes that had once looked out over a green kingdom at a daughter learning to walk.

"Thank you," the Hollow King whispered, and his voice was only a man's voice, rusted with disuse, breaking on the second word. "Thank you. It is so quiet." His hand twitched against her arm—trying, she realized, to grip. "The singing child. Where—"

"She's here," Bram said. "She's safe."

"Tell her—" The king's breath rattled. His gaze drifted past her shoulder, toward something no one else could see, and softened. "Tell her I remember the garden. Tell her I remember the garden, and the bells, and that I am sorry. Tell her her father kept faith as long as he was able."

"I'll tell her," Bram said, and meant it as a vow, the kind her family had sworn to Aelmarrow's kings before the maps were burned. "I swear it on the fire."

The king of a drowned kingdom died in a smuggler's arms in a flooding hall, free for exactly as long as it took to say thank you. It was not enough. It was more than anyone had been able to give him in three hundred years. Bram lowered him gently to the marble and closed his ordinary brown eyes, and above her the lightning stopped crawling and the clouds, far overhead, began at last to break.

---

Then she turned to Wren, and the victory curdled in her chest like blood in water.

The child had not moved. In the chaos of the burning—in the time it had taken to kill a god and free a king—Wren had lain where she fell, and now Bram crossed the flooded floor on legs that barely answered her and dropped to her knees and gathered the girl up, and the girl was cold. Not winter-cold. Not mountain-cold. Cold the way things are cold when the warmth has gone out of them entirely, when whatever burned inside has burned down to ash.

"Wren." Her voice cracked on the name. "Wren, look at me. Look at me, little bird."

The eyelids fluttered. That was all. A flutter, like a bird trapped behind glass, and then stillness again, and Bram pressed two fingers beneath the child's jaw and found a pulse so faint she had to count twice to believe it. There. Faint as a moth against a window. There.

"No," Bram said, to no one, to the hall, to the fire that had used her hands and left her empty. "No, you don't. You don't get to do this. Do you hear me? You held on through the snow, you held on through the riders, you held on through the citadel and the cells and the Court—you held on through *everything*, and you will not stop holding on now, not when it's finished, not when we've won—"

But she could feel it, the way she could feel weather coming, the way she could feel storms gathering beyond the horizon before any instrument or instinct gave her leave. The fire was gone from Wren. All of it. Bram had pulled it out whole to save her from the Hunger's grasp, and a vessel emptied of three hundred years of dragon-flame does not simply refill. The binding was renewed—the crown was destroyed—but the vessel that had carried the flame lay guttering in her arms like a candle in a doorway, and Bram Kavanagh, who had called storms and killed gods and walked out of her own exile in fire, had nothing left to give.

Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against Wren's chest anyway, over the small sternum, over the place where the light had lived, and poured everything she had into the contact—her storm-blood, her fear, her furious stubborn love—and felt it wash against emptiness and echo back. Like shouting into a cave. Like rain on salt.

"Corvane!" It came out as a scream. "CORVANE!"

Boots splashing. The marshal appeared through the steam, sword still drawn, her face streaked with soot and something darker, one arm hanging wrong at her side. She took in the scene—the dead king, the broken crown, Bram bent double over the child—and Bram watched understanding move across her features like a shadow across a field.

"The egg," Corvane said.

"What?"

"The egg, Kavanagh." The marshal dropped to her knees in the shallowing water, gripping Bram's shoulder with her good hand, hard enough to bruise. "In the vault. You woke it—it received your fire, it's been drinking your storm-blood for days—it isn't just an egg anymore. It's *ready*. It has been ready since you touched it. Fire goes to fire. That's the whole of the old rites, that's what the archives said—flame answers flame, life calls to life—"

"It's stone," Bram said. "It's been stone for three hundred years—"

"It was stone until a fire-keeper bled on it and spoke to it in lightning." Corvane's grip tightened. "I have read every account of Aelmarrow's fall that survived the burning. Do you know what the last queen wrote, the night before the end? *The egg sleeps, but it listens. And when it wakes, it will know its own.*" She hauled Bram upright by the collar, and her eyes were blazing, and for one moment she was not a marshal of the Vigilant Order but a woman who had lost a Grandmaster and half her wardens in a single night and refused, absolutely refused, to lose one more. "Go. Now. Before her heart stops. Run, Bram."

Bram ran.

She ran through halls running with retreating seawater, down staircases slick with weed and silt, past wraith-corpses dissolving into brine and warden-bodies she could not afford to look at, down and down into the vault beneath the palace where her ancestors' bones kept their long watch. The egg lay where she had left it, nestled in the basin of salt-white remains, and it was glowing.

Faintly. Softly. A pulse of gold beneath the shell, slow as a sleeping heart, and when Bram skidded to her knees beside it and laid her bare hands against the surface it was warm—warm as a living thing, warm as a cheek, warm as the doorway where a mud-caked child had once stood waiting to be let in.

"I'm sorry," Bram whispered, and did not know if she was speaking to the egg, to the bones, to the child dying three floors above her, or to every keeper in the long line between. "I'm sorry to ask this. I'm sorry it's so soon. But she held the fire when no one else would carry it. She sang your kingdom home. She is eight years old and she is dying and I am asking you—"

The egg pulsed against her palms.

Once. Twice. And then, from somewhere deep inside the shell, came a sound Bram had never heard before and knew instantly, the way she knew storms, the way she knew her own name: a tapping. Small. Determined. The sound of something that had waited three hundred years deciding, at last, to stop.

A crack spidered across the golden shell.

Above her, faint through the vault's throat of stone, Bram thought she heard Corvane shouting her name—and beneath that, impossibly, terribly, a silence where a child's breathing should have been.

"*Hurry,*" the bones seemed to say, in the voice of everyone she had ever lost.

The egg broke open in her hands.
