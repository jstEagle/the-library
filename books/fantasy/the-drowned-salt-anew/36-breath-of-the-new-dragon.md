## 36. Breath of the New Dragon

The throne room was still burning when the egg began to sing.

Not with flame — the fires Bram had loosed in her fight through the drowning halls had guttered to steam and smoke the moment the sea came in, hissing along the marble like a thousand dying serpents. What burned now was quieter than fire. It was a sound, low and resonant, rising out of the cracked vault beneath the broken dais where she had knelt among her ancestors' bones and given the sleeping thing her storm-blood. A sound like a bell rung underwater. Like a heart heard through a wall.

Bram knew that sound. She had heard it once before, ten years ago, in a dream she'd never told anyone about — the sound of something vast turning over in its sleep.

*No,* she thought, kneeling in the floodwater beside Wren's body. *Not yet. Not while she's still—*

Wren lay cradled against her chest, small and terrible in her stillness. The Hunger had wrung her nearly empty before it died; Bram had felt the child's flame go down to an ember no bigger than a candle-end, had felt it flicker, gutter, and — for one endless moment in the middle of the rite — go out. The king had died free in that same moment, whispering *thank you* with a voice three hundred years unused, and Bram had not been able to decide which grief to carry first. She carried both now. They sat in her chest like stones in a riverbed, worn smooth by the water still pouring through the shattered eastern wall.

The tide was receding. She could feel it going out of the palace the way blood leaves a face — the waterline creeping down the steps, the drowned dead of Aelmarrow sinking back into their long salt sleep somewhere beyond the walls, bowing as they went. Corvane was somewhere behind her, holding the doors against wraiths that were already dissolving. The world was ending and unending all at once.

And beneath the floor, the egg was singing.

"Bram." Corvane's voice, hoarse, from the doorway. "Bram, the floor—"

She looked down. The water around her knees was glowing.

It began as a faint gold thread, seeping up through the cracks in the marble the way light comes up through stormclouds — not bright, but *certain*, the way dawn is certain even on the worst morning of your life. The thread widened. Branched. The whole flooded floor became a map of veins, of roots, of rivers of light all flowing toward the broken vault beneath the dais, and the singing grew louder, and Bram understood with a jolt that went through her like lightning finding ground:

The egg wasn't waking because the binding broke.

The egg was waking because Wren's flame had gone out — and dragon-fire does not tolerate an ending. Dragon-fire calls to itself across any distance, through stone and salt and three hundred years of dark, the way a mother wakes at a child's cry in another room.

"Help me lift her," Bram said. Her voice didn't sound like her own. It sounded older. It sounded, she realized with a chill that had nothing cold in it, like her mother's voice, and her mother's mother's, all the way back down the line of fire-keepers whose bones she had knelt among. "Corvane. Help me carry her to the vault."

They carried her together — the exiled traitor and the marshal of the Order that had branded her, stumbling through knee-deep water lit gold from below, the dead king cooling on his throne above them with his face finally, finally at peace. Wren weighed nothing. That was the worst of it. She had always been a solid little armful, mud and stubbornness and sharp elbows, and now she weighed nothing at all, and Bram's arms knew the difference and hated it.

The vault stairs were flooded to the third step. Bram went down anyway, Wren held against her heart, and the water parted around them — not dramatically, not with any show, just gently, the way water parts around a swimmer who belongs there. The light rose to meet them.

The egg sat in the nest of her ancestors' bones exactly as she had left it, but it was no longer the grey, salt-scoured thing she had found. Its shell had gone translucent as sea-glass, and inside it something moved — something coiled and patient and impossibly old, pressing its snout against the inner curve of its prison, listening. Listening to the silence where a flame used to be.

Bram knelt. Laid Wren down on the lowest dry step, arranged the child's mud-stiff braid away from her face the way she did every night at the waystation, the way she had done for two years without ever saying aloud what it meant to her. Then she put her hand flat on the shell.

It was warm. After three centuries in the dark under the sea, it was warm as a loaf pulled from a hearth.

"I know," she whispered, in the drowned tongue Wren had taught her, the words coming easier than they ever had, as if the vault remembered them better than she did. "I know. She's still here. Bring her back."

The egg shuddered.

A crack ran across the shell from crown to base, fine as a hair, and through it came a smell Bram would know until she died: woodsmoke and rain and hot iron and, underneath all of it, something sweet she had no word for — the smell, she thought wildly, of the beginning of the world. Water poured into the crack. Light poured out. The singing became a keening, then a hammering, then a rhythm she recognized from the inside of her own ribs.

*Come on,* she thought, pressing her palm harder against the shell, feeling the small furious life within push back. *Come on, come on, you've waited three hundred years, don't you dare stop now—*

The shell burst.

It did not explode so much as *open*, petals of sea-glass peeling back like a flower deciding after a very long winter to try again, and the hatchling that emerged was small — hardly bigger than a marsh hawk, wet and wobbling and indignant — and it was the most beautiful thing Bram Kavanagh had ever seen in a life that had included storms seen from above the clouds and the drowned towers of Aelmarrow gleaming under forty fathoms of green light.

Its scales were the grey of storm-light on open water. Its eyes were molten gold, huge and new and unfocused, blinking against a brightness they had never been made for. It stood on the step above Wren on legs like folded umbrellas, spread wings that were mostly membrane and determination, and opened a mouth far too large for its head.

Bram had time to think, absurdly, *if it breathes fire on me I suppose I've earned it* —

The hatchling breathed.

But there was nothing to burn. No wood, no wick, no enemy. There was only a small grey body lying too still on the stone below, with a hollow in her chest where a flame used to live.

So the fire went where fire goes when there is nothing else: home.

It came out of the hatchling not as a gout or a roar but as a *breath* — a ribbon of gold-white light, soft as the last coal of a banked hearth, curling down the steps and over Wren's body like a blanket drawn up by careful hands. It sank into her skin. Into the hollow. And Bram watched — she would watch it every night for the rest of her life, behind her eyelids, in the space before sleep — watched the ember catch.

Wren's chest rose.

It was not a dramatic breath. It was small and ragged and accompanied by a cough that brought up seawater, and it was the single finest sound Bram had ever heard, finer than any storm, finer than the king's last free words, finer than the dawn chorus in the Thornfell pines in spring. Wren's chest rose, and fell, and rose again, and her fingers curled against the stone, and her eyes — those deep dark eyes that had looked at Bram across a waystation doorstep two winters ago and changed the shape of the world — fluttered open.

For a long moment neither of them moved.

Then Wren frowned, in the particular offended way she had, and said — hoarse, cracked, in accented but unmistakable Common, the language Bram had spent two years teaching her one stubborn word at a time:

"You're crying."

"I am not," said Bram, who was.

"You are." Wren tried to sit up, failed, tried again, and succeeded only because Bram's hands were suddenly there under her shoulders, hauling her upright, and then the child was against her chest and Bram's arms were around her and there was no pretending about anything anymore. Wren smelled of salt and smoke and herself, of river-mud and pine pitch and the particular wildness that no amount of civilization had ever managed to wash out of her, and she was breathing, she was *warm*, she was thumping one small fist weakly against Bram's shoulder in protest at being hugged too hard, and Bram held on anyway, and wept like the sky she had been named for, and did not care who saw.

Above them, the hatchling sneezed. A small curl of flame escaped its nostrils and set fire to nothing at all, and it looked enormously pleased with itself.

* * *

Later — how much later Bram could not have said; time had gone strange in the ruined palace, moving like tide through a channel — they climbed back up into the throne room, and the world had begun putting itself back together.

The water was gone. Not drained — *gone*, withdrawn past the horizon with the rest of the risen sea, leaving Vhalcrest dripping and listing and alive. Through the great shattered windows the morning was coming up clean and cold, the kind of washed-blue morning that follows the worst night of the age, and the city below was making sounds Bram had not heard in ten years of exile: bells. All of them. Every tower in Vhalcrest ringing at once, badly, out of sync, joyously.

Corvane met them at the top of the dais steps. The marshal looked as though she had fought a war alone, which, Bram supposed, in the ways that mattered, she had. There was ash in her close-cropped hair and a burn along one forearm bound in a torn pilgrim's sash, and when she saw Wren walking — *walking*, leaning on Bram's arm but walking, complaining in two languages about being hungry — the marshal of the Vigilant Order sat down abruptly on the steps of a dead king's throne and put her face in her hands.

"I thought," she said, muffled. "When the flame went out. I thought—"

"So did I," Bram said.

Wren, who had opinions about being discussed as though absent, extracted herself from Bram's side and marched over to peer up at Corvane. Then, with the gravity of a duchess bestowing a favor, she patted the marshal's knee.

"The little dragon is loud," she announced. "In my chest. Like a bird in a pot."

Corvane laughed — a rough, disbelieving sound, half sob — and caught the child's hand and held it, and Bram turned away to give them the moment and found herself instead face to face with the hatchling, who had followed them up the stairs and was now sitting in the middle of the throne room floor gnawing on a piece of the late Hunger's dissolved crown, which it appeared to find delicious.

"No," Bram said.

The hatchling looked at her.

"That is not food. That is three hundred years of evil. Put it down."

The hatchling swallowed it, burped a tendril of flame, and looked profoundly unrepentant. Bram pinched the bridge of her nose. Two years of raising a feral child had prepared her for precisely none of this and somehow entirely for this.

"What do we call it?" Wren asked, appearing at her elbow. "Dragons need names. Everything that breathes needs a name. You taught me that."

"I taught you that about people."

"It breathes." Wren shrugged, as if the matter were settled by simple logic, and perhaps it was. She studied the hatchling with her head tipped, and the hatchling studied her back, and between them passed some current Bram could feel on her skin like the air before lightning — the old current, the drowned-tongue current, the thing that had started all of this in a snowbound pass and a knock in the dark. Wren said something then, softly, in the language of Aelmarrow, three syllables that rippled through the ruined hall and made the standing water in the window-gutters tremble.

The hatchling chirped. Agreement, or acceptance, or simply delight at the sound.

"What does it mean?" Bram asked.

Wren considered how to say it. Her brow furrowed — the same furrow she'd worn at eight years old, wrestling with the Common word for *yesterday*. "Ember-that-remembers," she said at last. "Or... remembering-fire. In the old tongue, the word for remember and the word for keep-alive are the same word." She looked up at Bram, and her eyes were bright and wet and utterly alive. "That's what they believed. Aelmarrow. To remember something is to keep it alive. That's why the dead couldn't rest. Everyone forgot them. Everyone took them off the maps." She touched her own chest, where a dragon now lived. "We remembered."

Bram crouched down to her level. Out in the city the bells were still ringing. Somewhere below, in the vault of her ancestors, the empty eggshell lay among the bones of the fire-keepers, and Bram thought that tonight, when the crowds and the regency and the history-books had all had their fill, she would go down and bring the shell up into the light. Some things should be kept. Some things should be carried home to a mountain and set on a shelf above a hearth, next to a carved wren and a smuggler's compass and whatever other small holy junk two exiles accumulated between now and the end of their days.

"Wren," she said. "About what happens next—"

"I know." The child rolled her eyes with the weariness of someone four times her age. "The regency people want to look at me. The Order people want to ask questions. Corvane says maybe a house near the citadel, with guards." She scowled. "I don't want a house near the citadel."

"No," Bram agreed. "I didn't think you did."

"I want the mountain."

Something in Bram's chest — something that had been clenched since the moment the flame went out, since before that, since the day she'd walked out of this city with a brand on her name and nothing in her pockets — let go.

"Well," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Lucky for you, I know the way."

Wren grinned — gap-toothed, mud-streaked, magnificent — and threw her arms around Bram's neck, and the hatchling, offended at being excluded, flapped up onto Bram's shoulder with talons like a handful of needles and pressed its small hot snout against her cheek, and Bram Kavanagh, storm-caller, traitor, guide, fire-keeper, held her whole family in her two arms in the ruins of a palace that had tried to swallow them all, and laughed.

* * *

They were on the palace steps when the crowd saw them.

Bram heard it before she understood it — a hush rolling backward through the streets like wind before weather, street by street, the bells faltering one by one. The survivors of Vhalcrest had come out into the washed morning to count their dead and their living, and now they were looking up at the palace steps, at the woman in scorched smuggler's leathers with a dragon on her shoulder and a child's hand in hers.

Someone in the crowd began to kneel. Then a dozen. Then a street's worth, spreading outward through the city like frost across a windowpane, and Bram stood frozen on the top step with her heart slamming against her ribs, because she had spent ten years learning to be invisible and there was nowhere left in the world to hide.

"They're not kneeling at us," Wren said quietly. She was watching the crowd with the old sight, the drowned sight, and what she saw there made her voice go soft and strange. "Look. They're kneeling toward the sea."

Bram followed her gaze. Past the rooftops, past the walls, to the glittering line of the receded ocean — and on the wet sand where the tide had withdrawn, shapes were standing in the dawn light. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The risen dead of Aelmarrow, silent and salt-rimed, facing the land that had forgotten them. Not advancing. Not demanding.

Waiting.

At their center, one figure raised an arm — slow, ceremonial, a gesture Bram had seen carved on the ruins' oldest stones — and pointed, not at the city, not at the child, but eastward. Toward the deep water. Toward home.

Then, as one, the dead of the drowned kingdom turned and began to walk into the sea.

"Wait," Wren breathed, gripping Bram's hand hard enough to hurt. "Wait — they're waiting for *me*. They won't sleep until—" She looked up, and her eyes were wide with a grief and a wonder too large for her small face. "Bram. Someone has to sing them down. Someone has to tell them they were loved. That's the last thing. That's the *last* thing."

The tide was going out. The dead were walking into the dawn. And the last vessel of dragon-fire memory tugged Bram's hand and started down the palace steps, toward the sea, toward the ending that was also a beginning — and Bram, who had once chosen the mountain over the crown and the child over the duty, followed her down without hesitation, the hatchling clinging to her shoulder and crying its thin bright note into the morning like a bell rung for the newly born.

Behind them, the bells of Vhalcrest began to ring again.
