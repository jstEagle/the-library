## 37. Let the Dead Sleep

The silence after the burning was the loudest thing Bram Kavanagh had ever heard.

Not silence, exactly—the sea still spoke, the way it always had, a long gray murmur against the drowned steps of Vhalcrest's lower terraces—but the pressure was gone. For three hundred years, she understood now, there had been a second voice beneath the first, a hunger humming through the water like a note held too long in a struck bell. It had stopped. The bell hung quiet. The world rang with its absence.

She knelt on the flooded marble of the throne room, water to her waist, and held Wren.

The girl weighed almost nothing. She had always been light—eight years old and all elbows and salt-stiff hair—but now she felt hollowed, a reed with the wind gone out of it. Her skin was cold as harbor stone. Her lips had gone the blue-gray of deep water, and when Bram pressed two fingers beneath her jaw she found a pulse so faint she had to hold her own breath to feel it: once. Once again. A tide going out.

"No," Bram said. She said it the way you say things to gods and storms, because there is nothing else to say. "No, little bird. Not after all of it. Not here."

Wren did not answer. Her eyes were closed, and from her parted lips came a thread of smoke—not black, not choking, but a pale gold vapor that curled upward through the ruined air and dissolved where the roof had been torn away to show the sky. The dragon-fire, guttering. Bram could see it if she looked past the flesh, down into whatever place fire lived in a body, and what she saw was an ember in a hearth of ash, small as a fingernail, trembling at the edge of going dark.

Behind her, Ilsa Corvane stood hip-deep in the flood with her sword point-down and her hand still wrapped around the hilt, though nothing living or dead remained to fight. The wraith-servants of the Hunger had come apart when their master burned—unraveling like rope ends, like smoke drawn backward into a chimney—and what they left behind drifted on the water in tatters of shadow that were already thinning into ordinary gloom. Corvane's face was cut, her marshal's white gone gray with brine, and she was looking at Wren with an expression Bram had never seen her wear: helplessness, naked and unarmored.

"The egg," Corvane said. Her voice came out scraped. "Bram. The egg."

Bram had forgotten. That was the thing she would be ashamed of later, in the small hours, that in the whole of that terrible moment she had forgotten the only hope left—but grief does that, it eats the edges of the mind first. She turned, water dragging at her legs, and looked across the throne room to where the floor had broken open during the fighting, where the vault below gaped like a wound.

And the egg was hatching.

* * *

It came up out of the dark slowly, the way dawn comes, so that Bram could not have said when it began. First a light in the flooded vault, gold-green, moving like something swimming. Then the water itself began to shiver, rings spreading outward from the broken floor, and the temperature of the room changed—warmed, impossibly, the chill of three centuries of sea-drowned stone giving way all at once to a heat like a hearth-room, like a kitchen in winter, like every safe place Bram had ever imagined and never had.

The hatchling rose through the breach trailing sheets of luminous water.

It was small. That was the first absurdity, the detail her mind seized on because it could not yet bear the rest: a dragon no larger than a marsh heron, slick and new, its wings folded like wet leaves against its back, its scales the color of storm-light at evening—that shifting green-gold that has no name in any language Bram knew. Its eyes were enormous and ancient and utterly innocent, and they found Wren across the ruined throne room the way a compass finds north.

It did not roar. It made a sound like a kettle beginning to sing, high and sweet and questioning, and it paddled through the flood toward them with the graceless determination of every newborn thing, and Bram, kneeling in the wreckage of the world with a dying child in her arms, began to laugh. It was not a sane laugh. It was half sob, and it shook her shoulders, and Corvane stared at her as though she had gone mad, and perhaps she had, but the hatchling paid no attention to any of it. It climbed the last few feet of submerged dais, hooked its tiny claws over the edge of the step, and dragged itself out of the water directly in front of Bram and Wren.

Then it breathed.

Not fire—not the roaring annihilation of legend. What came out of that small throat was a single exhalation, warm and golden, smelling of hot stone and honey and something older, something like the memory of sunlight on the first morning of the world. It poured over Wren like water poured over a wilting plant. It sank into her skin. And the ember in her chest—

Bram felt it before she saw it. Felt the small body in her arms draw one long, shuddering breath, felt the cold begin to retreat from the girl's fingers like frost from a window in the sun. The ember flared. Not back to what it had been—it would never be that again, not wholly, not until years had fed it—but steady. Alive. A coal banked for the night rather than a fire dying.

Wren opened her eyes.

They were the same eyes. That was the mercy of it, the thing that undid Bram completely: the Hunger had worn this child, had reached into her and used her voice and her fire, and yet her eyes when they opened were still Wren's eyes, dark and fathomless and full of the drowned tongue's strange music. She blinked up at Bram. Her brow creased in the particular way it creased when she was searching for common words and finding them slow.

"Bram," she said. Just the name. Her first word of the day, hoarse as a gull's cry, and Bram pulled her in and held her so hard the girl squeaked in protest, and did not care, and held her harder anyway.

"You're all right," Bram said into her hair. "You're all right. I have you. I have you, little bird."

Wren patted her arm with the weary tolerance of the much-loved and much-smothered. Then her gaze slid past Bram's shoulder, and went wide, and she said something in the drowned tongue—a soft, rising phrase—and wriggled free.

The hatchling stood regarding her, head tilted, water dripping from its chin.

What passed between them, Bram never fully learned. Wren crouched, and the dragon leaned forward, and they touched foreheads the way old friends do, and the gold-green light of its scales brightened once, twice, like a heartbeat seen through skin. When Wren drew back she was smiling—the first true smile since the Court had taken her, since the salt-written COME, since everything—and she turned to Bram and said, carefully, in the common speech she had learned word by word through a mountain winter:

"Her name is *Ael*. It means *the last warmth of the hearth*. She says—" Wren paused, listening to something Bram could not hear. "She says she slept a long time. She says thank you for waking her." Another pause, and the smile turned shy. "She says she was dreaming about us."

* * *

They carried Wren up out of the throne room because she insisted on walking and then discovered, halfway up the great stair, that her legs had other opinions. Bram carried her then, the girl draped over her shoulder like a sack of smuggled wool, boneless with exhaustion and already half asleep. Corvane walked ahead with her sword drawn, though the corridors they climbed through were empty of everything but seawater and drifting silt and the small pale fish that had already begun, with the blind optimism of life everywhere, to explore the ruins.

The palace above was drowned to the second story. They waded through halls where tapestries hung heavy and black with weed, past statues crusted white with barnacle, under ceilings where chandeliers swayed gently in a current that should not have existed forty miles inland. And everywhere, everywhere, the dead.

Bram saw the first of them in a corridor near the eastern galleries, and her whole body went rigid, and she shifted Wren's weight and put herself between the child and the figure standing motionless in the flooded hall.

It was a woman. Or had been. She wore the rotted grandeur of Aelmarrow's last days—a gown gone to lace and shadow, a necklace of pearls fused to the bones of her throat—and her face was the face of the drowned: serene, emptied, patient. She had been one of the marching dead. Bram had seen hundreds like her on the road to Vhalcrest, the silent tide that had risen from the lowlands and converged on the capital, drawn by Wren's stolen song, wearing the faces of a kingdom erased from every map.

But this woman was not marching. She was simply standing, head bowed, hands folded, waiting.

And as Bram passed—heart hammering, Wren stirring sleepily against her shoulder—the drowned woman lifted her head. Her eyes were milk-white. They found Bram, and then found Wren beyond her, and something moved through the ruined face that was not hunger and was not command.

It was recognition. It was relief.

The woman bowed. Deeply, gracefully, the bow of a courtier to her sovereign, water streaming from her hair. And when she straightened, her lips moved, and though no breath had passed them in three hundred years, Bram heard—or dreamed she heard, she would never be sure—a single word in the drowned tongue, soft as sediment settling.

Then the woman turned, unhurried, and walked down into the darker water at the corridor's end, and the sea took her, and she was gone.

"They know," Corvane said quietly. She had stopped a few paces off, sword lowered, watching. "The Hunger is gone. Whatever held them up out of their graves, holding them like puppets on a string—it's burned. They're free, Bram. Free to finish dying."

* * *

They came out onto the western terrace as the sun went down, and Bram stopped, and stood, and forgot how to breathe.

The sea was leaving.

Not in violence—in the opposite of violence, in the gentlest withdrawal imaginable, the tide running out and out and out as though some vast hand had opened and let go. The flood that had swallowed Vhalcrest's lower city was sliding back between the streets, draining from doorways and windowsills, uncovering cobbles that had not seen air in living memory. Beyond the walls, across the drowned plain, the water was peeling back from the land mile by mile in the red light of sunset, revealing mudflats and channels and the dark ribs of a countryside that had been seabed for three hundred years.

And on the exposed flats, as far as the eye could see, the dead of Aelmarrow were lying down.

That was the sight Bram would carry for the rest of her life, the one that would wake her sometimes even years afterward, though by then it woke her gently, the way a beautiful ache wakes you. They lay down in families. That was what broke her—not the scale of it, though the scale was beyond reckoning, thousands upon thousands spread across the shining flats to the horizon—but the tenderness. She watched a man in the rotted uniform of Aelmarrow's guard kneel beside a smaller shape, a child-shape, and arrange himself around it as though taking up a watch he had abandoned mid-duty. She watched two women fold together like dancers ending a measure. She watched an old, old dead man lie back in the wet sand with his arms spread wide, facing the sky, mouth open as if to catch the rain.

They had waited three hundred years to lie down. Now, with the Hunger burned out of the world, they finally could.

Wren stirred against Bram's shoulder and slid down, and Bram let her, keeping a hand on the girl's back as she padded to the terrace rail. The hatchling—Ael—perched on the balustrade beside her, wings half-spread for balance, dripping.

Wren began to sing.

It was the drowned tongue, but not the song the Hunger had wrung out of her, not the pulling note that had raised the dead and marched them inland. This was slower. Softer. Bram recognized fragments of it from the winter nights in the waystation, from the lullabies Wren had taught her word by word over the fire—fragments, and now, hearing them whole, understanding bloomed in her like frost-flower on glass. It was a mourning song. It was also a blessing. In the drowned tongue, Bram realized, there might be no difference; a people who lived with the sea had learned that grief and peace shared a bed.

*Sleep,* the song said. *The water is done with you. The fire remembers you. Sleep.*

Down on the flats, the last of the standing dead bowed—to Wren, all of them together, a motion rippling across the miles like wind through wheat—and lay down among their kin. And the sea, having given back the land, came in again softly, tenderly, covering them by inches, drawing its gray-green blanket up over the kingdom it had taken. Not a drowning this time. A burial. A keeping.

By full dark it was done. The water stood where it had always stood, at the old tide line, lapping at the seawall as though nothing had ever happened—as though no crown had ever hungered, no kingdom drowned, no child had sung the dead awake. Only the mudflats remembered, and the shapes beneath the returning shallows, and the four living souls on the terrace who had seen it: a traitor, a marshal, a child, and a dragon no bigger than a heron, watching the moon come up over a sea finally at rest.

* * *

Later—much later, when Corvane had gone below to see to the wounded Order soldiers camped in the upper city, when Ael had curled herself around a brazier in the great hall and fallen asleep in a puddle of her own contented warmth—Bram sat with Wren on the terrace steps and shared a blanket against the night chill.

The girl was fighting sleep and losing. Her head kept dipping, jerking up, dipping again. Finally she gave in to honesty and leaned against Bram's side, and for a while neither of them said anything. Below them Vhalcrest flickered with cookfires; the city had opened its gates at dusk, cautiously, to the Order's marshals and the ragged army of smugglers who had burned the Court's fleet in the harbor, and the smell of bread baking somewhere in the middle town drifted up on the breeze. Bread. After everything, the world had resumed making bread.

"Wren," Bram said, when the breathing beside her had gone slow and deep. Then, when the girl mumbled: "I need to tell you something. You can sleep after."

"Mm."

"What you did—the fire you gave. To burn the crown." Bram looked out at the dark water, at the moon-path lying on it like a road. "It cost you. I felt it. And I need you to know that if it had cost you everything—if you hadn't opened your eyes—I'd have torn the world apart after you. Every sea. Every grave. Do you understand me?"

Wren was quiet long enough that Bram thought she'd fallen asleep. Then, without lifting her head, in the careful common speech that still came to her slowly, she said:

"I know. That's why I could do it." A pause. "Because you were holding me. Even when I was far away. Even in the dark place. I could feel where you were. Like—" she searched, gave up on common words, and offered the drowned tongue instead, a small round phrase that Bram had learned in the mountains and knew well.

*Home.* The word meant home, or hearth, or the fixed star sailors steer by—all three at once, the way the old tongue collapsed everything essential into a single sound.

Bram's throat closed. She got her arm around the girl and pulled her close and said nothing at all, because there was nothing left to say that the saying wouldn't diminish, and Wren sighed, and slept.

Bram sat awake a while longer, watching the sea.

Somewhere out there, beneath the returned water, a kingdom slept at last—kings and fishwives and soldiers and children, three hundred years of the drowned, resting in salt that was only salt now, that sang of nothing but itself. Somewhere behind her, in a ruined hall, the last dragon of Aelmarrow slept in the ruins of the throne it had been born to avenge or redeem, and had chosen redemption instead, because a child had asked. And somewhere ahead—she could feel it already, the way she could feel weather coming, the old gift stirring in her blood like a muscle remembering use—there was a mountain, and a waystation with a cold hearth waiting to be lit, and a spring coming on, and a life that was not invisibility and not duty either but something she had never had a name for.

Belonging, maybe. She'd learn the word properly on the road home.

The tide breathed in. The tide breathed out. And Bram Kavanagh, storm-caller, exile, traitor, mother-by-choice, closed her eyes at last and let the peace of the sleeping dead wash over her like the first warm rain of spring.

Above the middle town, faint on the night wind, someone began to bake bread for the morning—and from the great hall came a small, snoring rumble, gold-green and content, that sounded almost exactly like a purr.
