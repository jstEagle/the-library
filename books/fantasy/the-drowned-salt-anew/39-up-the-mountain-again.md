## 39. Up the Mountain Again

The snow was going the way of all old griefs—slowly, and then all at once.

Bram heard it before she saw it: a sound like a thousand small rivers waking under the crust, whispering through the pass in the grey hour before dawn. Meltwater. The mountain's long exhale after a winter of holding its breath. She stood at the mouth of the waystation with a tin cup of barley tea cooling in her hands and listened to Thornfell come apart and come alive, both at once, and thought that she had never once in ten years heard it make this particular sound. Every other spring she had been alone with it. This one had witnesses.

Behind her, inside, Wren was arguing with a dragon.

Not arguing, exactly. Negotiating. The hatchling—no bigger than a herding dog, all oversized wings and opinions—had decided that Bram's good traveling cloak belonged to him now, or at least to his nest, which he was building out of anything soft in the room. Wren stood over him with her hands on her hips, delivering what sounded like a full lecture in the drowned tongue, each syllable round and salt-flavored, words from a kingdom forty fathoms down and three hundred years gone. The dragon chirred back, unrepentant, dragging the cloak another foot toward the hearth.

"He says," Wren reported, switching to common speech with the careful precision of someone who had learned it within the year and loved it the way you love a borrowed coat, "that cloaks are for the cold, and he is never cold, therefore the cloak is wasted on us."

"Does he now."

"That is not what he says. That is what I say he says." A gap-toothed grin, quick as a fish. "He says the cloak smells like storms and he likes it."

Bram looked at the hatchling. He looked back at her with eyes like chips of amber held up to firelight, and there was something in them—something patient and old beneath the puppyish chaos—that made her certain Wren's translation was the truer one. *Storms.* Of course. She had carried a spark of true dragon-fire up out of the flooded dark beneath Vhalcrest, and the egg had woken to her blood as if her blood were a word it had been waiting three centuries to hear. Fire-keepers, the ferryman had called her family. Keepers, not owners. She was only beginning to understand the difference.

"Tell him," she said, "that when we reach the high valley, he can have every cloak I own."

Wren translated. The dragon made a sound like a kettle deciding to be pleased, and released the cloak, and immediately began dismantling the woodpile instead.

* * *

They left two days later, when the melt had firmed enough for the trail to hold a horse but not enough for the mud to swallow one. Corvane rode with them as far as the Shepherd's Stair—the last switchback where the lowlands still showed below, a green-brown wash of river country stitched with smoke from farmsteads—and Bram found herself grateful for the company in a way that sat strangely on her, like a coat cut for someone else.

Ten years ago she would have ridden this stretch in silence, counting the miles until she could be rid of any voice but the wind's. Ten years ago silence had been armor. Now it was just quiet, and quiet was better shared.

Corvane rode like a woman who trusted her horse more than most people, which Bram respected, and talked like a woman who had spent a career making reports and had forgotten how to make conversation, which Bram found oddly endearing. They spoke of small things. The state of the ford at Marrowgate. Whether the new regency's coin would hold its weight through summer. Whether smugglers made better neighbors than marshals (Bram said yes; Corvane said that depended entirely on the smuggler; neither of them pressed).

It was Wren who broke the surface tension, the way she always did, diving straight down to where the deep water lay.

"Why does Marshal Corvane keep looking at the mountain," she asked—not quietly, children of eight do not do things quietly—"like it owes her money?"

Corvane laughed. It was a rusty sound, an unused hinge, and Bram watched something in the marshal's face loosen around it. "Because it does, a little. Because I spent eleven years sending people into these mountains to find you, Bram Kavanagh, and none of them ever came back with anything but frostbite and excuses. And because I'm about to ride away from the only two people in the world who know what actually happened in that throne room, and I find I don't love it."

"You'll write," Bram said. Not a question. An anchor thrown ahead.

"I'll write." Corvane reined in at the top of the Stair and turned in the saddle to face them both. Below, the whole of the low country spread out gold-green in the morning light, the river a bright thread running toward a sea that had finally, finally given back its dead. Somewhere down there was Vhalcrest, half its palace drowned halls drying in the sun, its regents already arguing about who owned the miracle. Somewhere down there was a ferryman who touched two fingers to his brow when Bram passed, saying nothing, owing nothing, the old debt paid at last. "Letters, not summons. You have my word on it, and my word is the only thing I've got left that the Grandmaster didn't manage to ruin."

"The Order will want a report," Bram said. "On the girl. On the dragon. On me."

"The Order will get a report." Corvane's eyes were flat and grey and absolutely without apology. "It will say the vessel of dragon-fire memory has passed beyond recovery, the binding is renewed by means unknown, and the storm-caller Kavanagh was lost in the final action, body unrecovered. It will be signed by me, sealed by me, and believed by everyone, because no one wants to go looking up a mountain for a dead woman." She paused. "Dead women make excellent neighbors. No taxes. No trials. No one comes knocking."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "You're going to lie to your whole Order."

"I'm going to lie to the part of my Order that deserves it," Corvane said. "The rest will get the truth eventually, when they're ready, and not before. That's how institutions survive. Slowly, and by lying well." She swung down from the saddle, and before Bram could decide whether to allow it, the marshal had crossed the distance between them and gripped her forearm, hard, soldier-fashion. Her hand was warm and calloused and did not let go quickly. "I branded you traitor once. I'd like the record to show I've revised my findings."

"You didn't brand me. You read the verdict."

"I read it aloud. That's worse." Corvane's grip tightened. "I was nineteen years older than you and knew nothing. I know a little more now. Enough to know that duty is a road, not a destination, and I took a wrong turning somewhere around your trial and drove down it for twenty years." She released her arm and stepped back, and her voice went brisk again, the marshal reassembling herself piece by piece. "There's a widow in Marrowgate named Sela who sells the best honey-cakes on the river road. Tell her Corvane sent you and she'll charge you double, and tell her the marshal remembers the winter of the fever, and she'll charge you nothing. Small debts matter. Most of what matters is small, in the end. Nobody tells you that until it's too late to use it."

She mounted. Gathered the reins. Then, awkwardly, formally, she inclined her head to Wren—to the child, not the vessel, not the key, not the last ember of a drowned kingdom, but the girl.

"It was an honor," Corvane said, "to be frightened alongside you."

Wren beamed. "You're strange," she said warmly, "for a soldier," and Corvane laughed that rusty laugh again, and turned her horse down the Stair, and did not look back, because she was the kind of woman who understood that looking back was a thing you did with letters.

Bram watched her descend until the switchbacks took her, until the green swallowed the grey, until there was only the mountain and the meltwater singing and the child tugging at her sleeve asking if honey-cakes were real or a story soldiers told.

* * *

The high valley took four days to reach, and every mile of it was a homecoming Bram hadn't known she was owed.

Spring in Thornfell came like a rumor confirmed. The pines stood darker against snowfields gone soft and blue-shadowed; avalanche lilies pushed up through the last crust along the creek beds, white and stubborn; a marmot whistled at them from a boulder with the outrage of a landowner whose lease had been violated. Wren had never seen the mountains wake. She ran ahead on the trail with the dragon bounding after her—he could fly now, in short furious hops that ended in tumbles and indignant steam—and she named everything she met, first in common speech and then in the drowned tongue, as though the world needed to be entered into a ledger twice.

Bram walked behind and let her.

For ten years she had kept this range like a secret, moving through it silent and unseen, reading weather like scripture, making invisibility an art so fine that even she had begun to believe she wasn't there. She had told herself it was survival. Some of it had been. But some of it had been the older wound, the exile's arithmetic: *if I am small enough, quiet enough, gone enough, then what I am cannot hurt anyone.* Storm-blood. Traitor's blood. The blood of a line that had once kept fire for dragons and been punished for the keeping.

Now she climbed her own mountain with a child who sang the dead awake and a dragon who feared nothing except closed doors, and the arithmetic had come apart in her hands like wet paper, and what remained underneath was terrifyingly simple.

*I am here,* she thought, cresting the shoulder below the high valley, the wind coming off the snowfield cold and clean as a struck bell. *I am seen. I am not alone. And the sky knows my name, and has decided, against all precedent, to be gentle with it.*

The valley itself was everything she remembered and greener. A bowl of meadow ringed in granite, a stream braiding through it silver-bright with melt, an old shepherd's hut leaning against the slope like a tired man against a wall—hers, technically, though she had never once slept in it, having preferred the waystation lower down, closer to the passes, closer to escape. Escape routes. She had built her whole life out of escape routes, and standing in the meadow grass she realized she had stopped checking them. Somewhere between the throne room and the treeline, between the burning crown and the hatching egg, she had stopped planning how to leave.

The dragon approved of the valley immediately and completely. He galloped a full circle through the meadow, wings spread for balance rather than flight, scattering a dozen startled pipits, then flopped onto his back in the flowers with his legs in the air, radiating satisfaction. Wren collapsed beside him, breathless, laughing, and the two of them lay in the grass like a matched set of miracles, and Bram stood apart with the horses' lead ropes in her hand and felt something in her chest crack open along an old seam—not painfully, the way a scar splits, but the way soil splits in spring. To let something up. To let something grow.

"We're staying?" Wren asked. She asked it carefully, watching Bram's face the way she still sometimes watched it—reading for the flinch, the withdrawal, the door quietly closing. Eight years old and already fluent in the grammar of being sent away.

"We're staying," Bram said. "This is ours. All of it." She swept a hand across the valley—the hut, the stream, the granite walls going rose and gold as the sun dropped behind the western ridge. "No one's coming to take it. No one's coming to take you. If anyone tries, they'll have to explain themselves to the weather, and the weather and I have an understanding."

"What kind of understanding?"

"It leaves me alone when I ask nicely," Bram said, "and obliterates my enemies when I don't."

Wren nodded solemnly, filing this away among the other laws of the universe, and then the moment broke and she was a child again, hauling the dragon upright by one wing-joint to show him the stream, because he had never seen moving water that wasn't a bath, and his opinion on the subject was going to be loud.

* * *

They raised the roof beam of the hut on the third evening, Bram mortaring stone while Wren handed up whatever she was told to hand up and the dragon supervised from atop the woodpile, which he had declared his by right of conquest and which no one contested, because contesting a dragon is a poor use of an evening.

The work was slow and the work was good. Bram had forgotten—had trained herself to forget—how a place changes when you build it instead of merely hiding in it. Every stone she set was a sentence in a new language: *I intend to be here tomorrow. And the day after. And the day after that.* Her mother had taught her mortar and her father had taught her rafters, back before the Order and the exile and the long white silence, and their hands moved in hers now like ghosts who had finally been invited to sit down.

At dusk she lit the first fire in the rebuilt hearth, and the dragon crept close and breathed a thread of flame across the kindling, proud as a boy striking his first flint, and the fire caught gold and orange and threw their shadows huge and dancing up the fresh-plastered wall—one tall, one small, one winged—and Bram sat back on her heels and looked at those shadows for a long time.

Found family, Corvane had called it once, in a letter that had arrived impossibly fast, carried by some relaysystem of loyal wardens the marshal clearly maintained for purposes she declined to specify. *Most families are accidents of blood,* the letter said. *Yours is an act of will. In my experience the second kind holds better.*

Outside, the stars came up over Thornfell in their wheeling thousands, and far below, faint as memory, the sea murmured to itself about a kingdom it had agreed to stop talking about. The drowned dead slept in their salt. The crown was slag in a vault no one visited. The king had died free, and somewhere in the regency's archives a clerk was even now misfiling the truth of it, which was, Bram reflected, probably the best security the world could offer.

Wren fell asleep against her side mid-sentence, mid-song, a fragment of Aelmarrow dissolving into breathing. The dragon curled around the girl's feet like a banked coal. Bram stayed awake a while longer, the way she always did on the first night in a new shelter, listening to the mountain check its locks—the settling stone, the stream's cold arithmetic, the wind combing the pines—and found, for the first time in her adult life, nothing to guard against.

She was just closing her eyes when the dragon lifted his head.

He made no sound. But his amber eyes fixed on the northern sky, on a point above the pass where the stars stood thick, and his nostrils flared, and the ember-light under his scales brightened once, twice, like a pulse quickening—as if something on the night wind, something vast and distant and unmistakably *winged*, had answered the spark in his chest across all that dark cold air.

Bram followed his gaze and saw nothing. Only stars.

But she reached out, the way she reached for weather, the way she reached for the sea's mood, and felt—at the very edge of sense, at the border where the world thins into story—a pressure in the north. Old. Airborne. Awake.

"Well," she said softly, to the dragon, to the sleeping child, to the mountain holding them all in the cup of its hands. "Let's see what comes calling."

The dragon chirred, low and thoughtful, and laid his head back down—but he kept his eyes on the north a long while, glowing gently in the dark, like a lamp left lit for someone expected home.
