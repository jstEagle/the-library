## 1. Salt on the Wind

The snow came down like it had a grudge.

Lior Oyelaran stood at the head of eleven mules and nine liars and watched it fall, and thought—not for the first time—that the Thornfells in deep winter were the closest thing the world offered to forgiveness. Nobody looked at you in a whiteout. Nobody remembered your face. The mountain wrapped you up and made you anonymous as a stone, and for a woman whose voice could open throats at forty paces, anonymity was worth more than gold.

"Oyelaran." Brenn, the caravan master, a man shaped like a rain barrel and about as subtle, trudged up beside her. "You sure this is the way?"

"No."

"Good. Hate it when guides are sure. Sure gets people killed." He spat into the snow, considered it, and added, "You're not going to tell me where we are, are you?"

"We're in the pass," Lior said. "Everything else is decoration."

Brenn grinned. He was one of perhaps four people alive who knew her name, and he guarded that knowledge the way other men guarded coin—jealously, and without quite understanding why it mattered. To him she was just the quiet guide who never got lost, who read weather like a priest read scripture, and who had once, memorably, talked a customs officer out of searching the third mule by saying something to him in a language Brenn didn't recognize. The officer had wept a little and waved them through. Brenn had bought her a drink afterward and never asked a single question, which was the finest quality a man could possess.

"Third mule's complaining again," he said now.

"The mule or what's on the mule?"

"What's on the mule. Vess's silk. Says the cold'll crack it."

"The cold will crack *him* first if he keeps talking about it." Lior turned back to the trail. "Keep the line tight. Wind shifts after the narrows—we want to be through before it does."

She said it plainly, the way she said everything, and Brenn nodded and went back down the line bellowing at people who were already doing what she'd told them, because that was how caravan masters loved you: loudly, redundantly, and from behind.

Lior pulled her scarf higher and let herself be nobody for another hour.

* * *

It didn't last, of course. Nothing did.

The narrows were exactly as narrow as she remembered, which was to say two mules wide with a drop on the left that went down far enough to make even experienced men walk on the inside. The snow had buried the cairns. She found them anyway, by feel, by the small wrongness of drifts that lay too neat, and called the turns back down the line in a low voice that carried better than it should have. That was the trouble with her voice. It always carried. Words off her tongue had weight to them, like stones dropped in still water, and she spent her whole life choosing which pebbles to throw.

They were halfway through when the mountain spoke first.

Not words. Just a sound—a deep, grinding *hmm* somewhere above the trail, the sound of several tons of rock deciding, slowly, the way mountains do everything, that it was tired of standing.

Lior went cold all over, and not from the wind.

"Stop!" she shouted. "Everyone stop! Back to the last bend—now!"

Brenn, to his credit, didn't argue. The line reversed itself with the usual chaos of mules and curses, and Lior stood alone in the narrows looking up at the slope above, where the snow was beginning to slither and beneath the snow something dark was shifting its shoulder.

There was time. There was maybe enough time. If they backed out and went around by the river cut, they'd lose two days, and two days meant the Hollow Guard's patrol schedule would catch up with them at the ford, and Brenn's people would die for her caution. She'd done the arithmetic a hundred times in ten years. Caution had a body count too; it just wore quieter clothes.

Or.

Or she could do the small thing. The little indulgence. One word, aimed upward, no bigger than a whisper—and the mountain would listen, because everything listened to her, because her mother had bound her oaths into her bones before Lior had teeth enough to object.

*Don't,* said the part of her that had kept her alive for a decade. *You swore you'd stop. You promised yourself.*

*I promised myself I'd survive,* said the other part, which sounded annoyingly like her mother, and she hated that.

The rocks began to move.

Lior took off her glove, pressed her bare palm flat against the frozen cliff beside her, felt the mountain's old slow pulse under the ice, and spoke.

"*Stay.*"

One word. She put nothing extra in it—no edge, no command-structure, none of the architecture that could kill. Just the plain imperative, the way you'd say it to a dog, to a child, to yourself. But swordspeech wasn't volume. Swordspeech was truth given a place to stand, and the truth was that she wanted the slope to hold, and the slope heard her wanting and obeyed.

Above her, the slide simply... reconsidered. The grinding stopped. A few stones rattled down and settled, sheepish, into the drifts. The snow slithered once more and then lay still, and the whole mountainside held its breath around her word like a held note.

Then the cost arrived, as it always did, punctual as debt.

Frost bloomed across her tongue. Not pain, exactly—pain would have been simpler. It was cold, a bright aching cold that spread from the root of her mouth outward until her teeth ached and her eyes watered, and she knew without looking that when she spat later there would be blood-flecked ice in it, and that tonight her voice would be rough as gravel, and that tomorrow morning she'd find a thin white scar-line on her tongue that hadn't been there before, joining the others, a tally she couldn't show anyone.

Ten years, and she had maybe thirty such scars. She'd promised herself no more. And here was number thirty-one, blooming like frost-fern, all for a shortcut.

"You absolute fool," she told herself quietly. Her breath came out ragged. "Absolute—"

"Oi!" Brenn's voice, echoing up the narrows. "What happened? Sounded like thunder!"

"Rockslide," she called back, and was proud of how steady it came out. "It stopped. Move them through, quick, before it changes its mind."

He appeared around the bend, red-faced, delighted. "Stopped? How?"

"Snow settled wrong. We got lucky."

"Lucky!" He beamed at her like luck was a thing she personally manufactured, which, in fairness, was closer to true than he'd ever know. "That's why we pay you, Oyelaran. Luck with legs." He clapped her shoulder hard enough to stagger a smaller woman and went back down the line, hollering.

Lior stood alone a moment longer, flexing her jaw against the cold ache, watching the held slope with something between gratitude and resentment. Somewhere under that snow was a word she'd spoken, still standing guard, still costing her. Words off her tongue didn't come back. They lived where she planted them until the world wore them away, and she wondered—not for the first time—if the mountain would remember her voice in a hundred years, long after everyone who might have hunted her was dead.

Probably. Things she said tended to outlast things she did.

She pulled her glove back on and walked.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the treeline, a smuggler's camp so well-chosen it had probably been a smuggler's camp since before the Thornfell had a name. Fire small and shielded. Mules picketed close. The cargo—Vess's silk, mostly, and a crate of something Brenn refused to name in front of her, which meant it was either weapons or very good brandy—was distributed among bedrolls with the practiced ease of professionals.

Lior sat at the fire's edge, apart, as always. Close enough to share the warmth. Far enough that nobody felt obliged to talk to her.

This was the arrangement. This was the shape her life had worn itself into, smooth as a river stone: useful, invisible, alone. Armor and prison, and the trick of it—the thing she'd learned the hard way—was that they were the same wall seen from different sides.

"Here." Brenn dropped down beside her with two tin cups and the particular expression of a man about to commit an act of kindness. "Hot wine. Medicinal."

"I'm not sick."

"You sound like a gravel quarry with opinions. Drink."

She drank. It was terrible. It was warm. She drank more of it.

"Good work today," Brenn said, after a while. "In the narrows."

"Anyone would have—"

"Don't." He said it mildly, looking into his cup. "I've run this pass twenty years. Snow doesn't settle wrong and then un-settle. Whatever you did up there—" he raised a hand before she could speak, "—and I'm not asking, because asking is rude and also I don't want to know—I'm saying thank you. You saved us two days and maybe some necks. Including mine, which I'm fond of."

Lior stared into the fire. The frost-ache in her mouth throbbed in time with her pulse.

"It was a small thing," she said finally.

"Small things count double in winter." He drained his cup, stood, and pointed a finger at her with the gravity of a general. "Sleep. Tomorrow's the high trail, and the high trail's a bastard even when the sky's behaving, which it isn't."

He wandered off to bully someone about mule feed, and Lior stayed by the fire, turning the cup in her hands.

*Small things count double in winter.* She almost smiled. Almost. The trouble with Brenn's philosophy was that it worked both ways. Small indulgences counted double too. Every word she spent made the next one easier to spend, and she knew how that road went, because she'd watched her mother walk it—her mother, who had started with small mercies and ended with oaths laid on her daughter like chains, each link forged for love, every single one of them a lock.

The high trail tomorrow. Then the ford, then the low country, then Vess's warehouse and payment and the long anonymous winter after. She had a plan. The plan was: nothing happens. The plan was always: nothing happens. Ten years running, and it was a good plan, and she hated it the way you hate a coat that fits perfectly and itches everywhere.

She slept badly, as usual, and dreamed of water, as usual, though she'd never seen the sea.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, which in the Thornfells was less a mercy than a held breath.

The high trail climbed along the shoulder of the ridge, narrow as a shelf, with the world falling away to the right into cloud. It was beautiful in the way that only places trying to kill you can be beautiful—rime-feathered stone, ice-hung pines, light coming up silver through the passes. Lior led the way with her eyes moving constantly: trail, slope, sky, trail. Behind her the caravan strung out in a careful line, mules stepping delicate as dancers, the men silent because the altitude and the drop had knocked the chatter out of them.

An hour in, she smelled it.

Blood. Faint, under the pine and the cold iron smell of snow, but unmistakable—she'd grown up around wounds, in a house where her mother's work left stains that scrubbing never fully answered. She held up a fist and the line stopped behind her, obedient, and she stood very still and let the wind bring her the rest.

No smoke. No voices. No movement on the slope above. Just blood, and something else, fainter, threading underneath it—

Salt.

Sea-salt. Here, three hundred leagues from any coast, at the top of a frozen mountain, the wind carried the smell of brine and deep water, and every hair on Lior's arms stood up beneath her sleeves.

"Wait here," she said, and didn't wait for agreement.

She found him fifty yards on, past a shoulder of black rock, crumpled against the trailside like something the mountain had coughed up. A man—or what had been a man—in courier's leathers gone dark with frozen blood. Young, maybe. Hard to tell. His lips were blue-gray, cracked, and crusted white at the corners, and when Lior knelt and turned him gently, the smell hit her full in the face: seawater. His beard was stiff with dried salt, as though he'd crawled out of the ocean onto this trail, and his clothes were soaked through in a way that had nothing to do with snow.

His chest moved. Barely.

"Hey." She pulled off her glove, pressed two fingers to his throat. A pulse, thin and stumbling, like a drunk trying to walk a straight line. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

His eyes opened. They were the worst part—they were clear, terribly clear, the eyes of a man who knew exactly where he was and how long he had, and had already finished being afraid about it. He looked at her face, and something moved through his expression that she'd spent ten years making sure no one ever showed her: recognition.

"You," he rasped. Salt water ran from the corner of his mouth as he spoke, impossibly, steadily, as if his lungs were full of tide. "Swordspeaker."

Every instinct she had screamed at her to stand up, to walk away, to let the mountain finish what someone else had started. Nobody knew that word. Nobody living should know that word.

But his hand was moving, fumbling at his chest, and there was something in his face beyond fear—something urgent, pleading, the look of a man carrying a message that mattered more than his life, which was fortunate, because his life was nearly spent.

"Take it," he whispered. "Take—it has to—an Oyelaran—"

"How do you know that name?" Her voice came out low and dangerous, and she heard the edge in it, the faint ringing undertone that made ordinary men flinch. He didn't flinch. He smiled, actually, a small salt-crusted smile, as if the sound of it was a comfort.

"Knew your mother," he breathed. "Knew—what she sold. What they keep. Down under—" A shudder went through him, and more water came, and his hand closed around hers with shocking strength, pressing something into her palm: a folded square of oilskin, stained dark, damp with brine. "The ninth door remembers you. Tell them—tell the door—" His eyes fixed on hers, wide and drowning. "Tell it Lior is—"

He never finished. The strength went out of his hand all at once, and his head tipped back against the rock, and the clear eyes went vague, and then still, and the sea-smell rose off him one last time like an exhalation, and then there was only the snow, and the wind, and a dead man holding her fingers in a grip that was no longer his.

Behind her, distantly, Brenn was calling her name, his voice thin with distance and worry.

Lior knelt in the snow with a stranger's blood freezing on her knees and a salt-stained bundle clenched in her fist, and understood, with the cold certainty of a blade sliding home, that her plan—her beautiful, itchy, ten-year plan of *nothing happens*—had just died on the high trail along with the man who'd brought it here.

The wind shifted. Far below, faint but unmistakable, she heard the creak of harness leather and the rhythmic crunch of many boots on snow, coming up the pass behind them.

Someone had followed the courier. Someone was still following him.

Following *her*.
