## 2. The Dying Courier

The man was dying badly, the way men did when they'd been running too long and knew it. Lior had seen enough of it in ten years of mountain work to know the stages: first the denial, then the bargaining with whatever gods smugglers kept, then this—the terrible stillness of a body that had stopped arguing with itself.

He lay half-buried in the drift against the trail's outer wall, where the wind had stacked snow into a white rampart taller than she was. Only his boots had shown, one of them missing its lace, and if Lior hadn't stopped to retie her own—petty vanity, checking her bootlaces twice because the cold made her fingers stupid—she would have walked right past him and let the mountain keep him.

She knelt in the snow beside him and did the arithmetic every traveler on the high trails learned early. No pack. No weapon that she could see, though that meant little. Frost in his beard, which was black and full and wrong for a man who looked forty. And blood—not much, but enough—seeping from under his coat at the ribs, frozen to a dark crust that cracked when he breathed.

"You're a long way from anywhere," she said.

His eyes opened. They were brown, ordinary, and absolutely clear, which frightened her more than the blood did. Dying men were supposed to be cloudy.

"Water," he said.

"There's no water up here. There's snow, if you want to eat it slow."

"No." His hand came out of the drift and caught her wrist with a grip far stronger than it had any right to be. "You don't understand. I've been drinking it for three days."

Lior went very still. It was a habit older than her exile: when something was wrong, stop moving and listen. The wind moved instead, moaning along the pass, filling the silence where her answer should have been.

"Drinking what?" she asked, though she already knew, the way you know a thing before the knowing arrives, the way she'd known the rockslide yesterday would come down before the first stone sang.

"Seawater." He smiled, and there was salt drying white at the corners of his mouth, crusted in his beard, rimming his lips like frost. Salt. On the Thornfell high trail, four days' hard walk from any coast, three days' walk even from the brine springs in the lowlands that no sane person drank from. "It's all they gave me. Said it would keep me honest. Said the sea follows what's hers."

His grip tightened. His other hand came up clutching something wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone dark with handling, and pressed it against her chest with the blind insistence of a man handing off a brand.

"Take it."

"I'm not taking anything from a dead man," Lior said. "I'm going to splint your ribs, get you down to Vess's people at the waystation, and you can tell me who 'they' are over soup."

He laughed. It cost him; she watched the price of it cross his face like weather. "Splint my ribs. That's—" Another laugh, wetter. "That's kind. You're kind. She said you would be."

The wind dropped, just for a moment, the way it does between gusts, and in that pocket of quiet Lior heard the thing she'd been refusing to hear since she knelt down: gulls. Faint, thin, unmistakable, crying somewhere below the trail where there was nothing below the trail but four hundred feet of ice-glazed cliff and, beneath that, stone.

"What's your name?" she asked, because names mattered, because a name was the smallest true word there was, and she hoarded them.

"Tomasin." He coughed, and the cough brought up water—a thin trickle of it, gray-green, smelling of deep places, of kelp and cold silt and things that lived without light. It ran from the corner of his mouth and froze almost as soon as it touched the snow. "Tomasin Ferro. Courier. Third rank, coastal circuit." His eyes found hers and held them, and there was something in them now besides clarity. Pity, maybe. Or apology. "I was told to find an Oyelaran."

Every muscle in Lior's body decided at once that it wanted to be somewhere else. Ten years of practice went into keeping her face smooth as lake ice. "Never heard the name."

"Yes, you have." He wasn't cruel about it. He said it the way you'd tell someone their bootlace was dragging. "I was given a description. Red-brown skin, sword-calluses on a woman who carries no sword, and a habit of looking at doors like they owe her money." A ghost of the smile again. "And I was told you'd deny it, so I was told to say this instead: *the ninth door remembers you.*"

The words went through her like a fishhook through soft flesh. She didn't move. She didn't breathe. Somewhere behind her sternum, in the locked room where she kept everything she used to be, something turned over in its sleep.

"That means nothing," she said.

"It means everything. It's not mine—I'm only the bag it comes in." Tomasin's breath was shortening now, each one a little shallower, a little more expensive. He seemed to know it and to have made peace with it, which was its own kind of terrible. "Listen. Listen, because I don't have the time to say it twice and you don't have the time to pretend you didn't hear it. The map shows the drowned halls under Serein. There's a door there, the ninth one, and it doesn't open for force or fraud or faith. It opens for swordspeech. For *your* speech. Someone built it that way a long time ago and has been waiting ever since."

"I don't speak anything," Lior said. "I'm a caravan guard. I mind mules and count crates."

"And you do it beautifully, I'm sure." His hand slid down her wrist to the map, pushing it at her again. "Take it. Please. I carried it through the Guard cordon at Greywater with it sewn into my coat lining, and I drank seawater every day for three days because they put a compulsion on me—if I tried to give it to anyone but an Oyelaran, my throat would close. Do you understand? I couldn't give it away. I could only bring it to you. So every mile I walked, I was walking toward you, and every mouthful of the sea was telling me how close I was getting."

Lior looked at the oilcloth bundle. Small. Light, probably. The kind of weight that never left you once you picked it up.

"Why me?" she asked, and hated how small her voice came out. "Why does everyone suddenly remember my family after ten years?"

"Because the tide's turning," Tomasin said simply. "And things that sank are coming back up. Take it. Take it and go, because they're behind me, and they don't tire, and they don't eat, and they don't stop."

"The Hollow Guard." She said it flatly, testing the word for traps. Everyone in the passes knew the Hollow Guard the way children knew the shapes of things that ate children: by reputation, by silhouette, by the stories told quietly near fires. Riders in gray whose faces were their helmets. Who served the court at Serein, or claimed to, though no crown had held those mountains in living memory.

"They'll be here within the hour. Maybe less. I lost them at the ford but they don't lose things, they just... arrive." His voice was fraying now, thread by thread. "There's a route on the map. Your mother marked it. Only an Oyelaran can walk it—the doors won't open otherwise. She wrote it all down. She wrote everything down, God forgive her, she wrote it all down and sealed it and sent it out into the world like it was a letter and not a wound—"

He broke off into coughing again, worse this time, and the water that came up was darker, and Lior found herself doing the other arithmetic, the one she hated: how long, how far, whether she could carry him, whether carrying him would kill them both.

She was still doing it when he caught her hand and pressed the map into it, folding her fingers closed over the oilcloth with both of his.

"Take it," he whispered. "Let me have done one thing right."

And what could she say? What do you say to a man who has drunk the sea for three days to bring you a letter? Lior closed her hand around the bundle, and it was warm, absurdly warm, as if it had been sitting by a fire, and she felt the shape of rolled vellum through the cloth and thought, unhelpfully, *this is the heaviest light thing I have ever held.*

"The ninth door remembers you," Tomasin said again, softer now, his eyes beginning to lose their terrible clarity, glazing toward some horizon she couldn't see. "Remember it back. Remember it back, and maybe—" The sentence never finished. Maybe what. Maybe the whole world, maybe nothing, maybe just him, wanting his errand to mean something past the end of him.

"Tomasin Ferro," Lior said clearly, giving him back his name the way you give a coin to a ferryman. "Third rank, coastal circuit. You got it there. Whatever 'there' is—you got it there."

He smiled. This time it didn't cost him anything at all.

Then the wind came back around the shoulder of the mountain, and when it passed over him, he didn't shiver, and Lior knew that the cold had finally gotten its argument settled.

She stayed kneeling a moment longer than was safe. Then she took his hands and folded them on his chest, soldier-fashion, and said the small words her grandmother had taught her for dead strangers—the ones that weren't swordspeech, that were just human speech, just grief shaped into syllables—and stood up.

The map went inside her coat, against her chest, where she could feel its warmth like a second heartbeat.

* * *

She made it perhaps half a mile before she heard the horses.

Not hoofbeats—that was the wrongness of it. Hoofbeats have rhythm, effort, life. This was a sound like drums played underwater, muffled and regular and utterly without hurry, and it came up the trail behind her with the patience of something that had never once needed to run.

Lior stepped off the trail into the shadow of the snow rampart and went still, breathing shallow, the way Vess had taught her in her first winter: *you're not hiding, girl, you're becoming scenery. Scenery doesn't want anything. Wanting is what gets seen.*

They came around the bend below her, and her stomach dropped.

Three riders. Gray, from boot to helm—gray wool, gray leather, and helms that covered the whole face, smooth as river stones, with no eye-slits she could find and yet she knew, with the certainty of prey, that they saw her. Their horses were gray too, and wrong, moving with a gliding stride that left almost no print in the snow. Almost. Behind them, faintly, the snow held impressions like the memory of prints rather than prints themselves.

They drew level with the place where Tomasin lay.

One rider dismounted—or flowed down, was closer to it—and stood over the folded body for a long moment. The other two waited. Nobody spoke. Nobody needed to. The whole scene had the quality of a ritual performed so many times it had worn smooth.

Then the standing rider bent, and did something Lior couldn't quite see, and when it straightened, it was holding a handful of snow. It opened its gauntlet, and the snow fell through it as seawater, gray-green, smelling of kelp and deep silt, steaming faintly in the cold.

Her hand went to the map through her coat without her permission. She forced it back down. *Scenery,* she reminded herself. *Scenery doesn't want anything.*

The rider turned its head. Not toward her—toward the trail ahead, the direction she'd been walking, the direction of the waystation and Vess's people and the warm low road. It regarded that distance the way a man regards a ledger.

Then it raised one gauntleted hand, and from somewhere inside the smooth helm came a sound. Not words. A note—one long, sustained note, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and the sound went out across the pass and came back changed, and Lior understood with a lurch that it was calling, and that the call was not aimed at the trail behind or the trail ahead.

It was aimed at the mountain itself.

Far above, at the head of the pass, something answered. A crack, deep as a tree falling, then another. Snow began to move on the upper slopes, gathering itself, and Lior's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather, because she knew what was being done—it was the same trick she'd used two days ago to save the caravan, spoken backward, spoken large. They were going to bring the slope down and bury the trail, bury the body, bury anything and anyone the courier might have reached.

Bury her.

*Run,* said every instinct she owned. Run down-trail, outrun the slide, get ahead of it, get to cover—

But the slide wouldn't stop at the trail. It would run all the way to the valley mouth, and at the valley mouth, four miles down, was the caravan she'd just left. Forty people. Mules. Children in the supply wagons. Asleep in their bedrolls, trusting the pass to hold because the swordswoman had promised them the pass would hold.

Lior stood in the shadow of the rampart with a dead man's map warming against her heart and did the last piece of arithmetic, the one that always came out the same no matter how many times she ran it.

She could stay scenery. She could watch the slide take the trail and pray it stopped short of the valley, and spend the rest of her life finding out whether it had.

Or she could spend a little more of the thing she'd sworn never to spend.

"Oh, hell," she whispered, and it frosted white in front of her, and the frost hung in the air a moment too long, curling, almost reading itself.

She spoke then—quietly, barely louder than the wind, in the old tongue that was older than oaths, older than the court that had stolen from her, older than the lies the mountain was built on. One word, shaped like a lever, aimed at the slope above the riders:

*Enough.*

The mountain listened.

The slide died in its cradle. The gathering snow sighed, settled, and became merely snow again, and the silence that followed was so complete Lior could hear her own heartbeat, and under it, faint and rhythmic, the drum-muffled movement of three gray riders turning their heads—all three at once, unhurried, inevitable—toward the shadow of the snow rampart where a piece of scenery had just spoken.

The nearest one took a step toward her. Then another. From inside its helm came the glass-rim note again, softer now, almost conversational, and Lior felt the words forming behind her teeth, the cutting ones, the killing ones, the ones with frost-scars waiting on the far side of them, and she hated herself with a purity she hadn't managed in years, because she was going to say them, she could feel it, she was already choosing—

A horn sounded from the valley below. Long, ragged, human—a real horn, blown by real lungs, the caravan's evening signal, *all well, make camp.* The sound rolled up the pass and broke over the riders like a wave over stones.

And the riders stopped.

All three, mid-stride, as if the horn had been addressed to them. The lead one tilted its smooth head, listening to something Lior couldn't hear—some order, some accounting, some ledger being balanced—and then, without haste, it turned, remounted its impossible horse, and the three of them flowed away down the trail, past Tomasin's folded body, past the stain of seawater in the snow, growing smaller and grayer until the dusk took them and there was nothing left but the memory-of-prints fading behind them.

Lior stayed in the shadows until full dark. Her legs shook. Her mouth tasted of iron and old winters, and when she ran her tongue over her lower lip she found the first thin line of frost-scar already rising, pale as a scratch on glass. One word. One small word, and the bill was already arriving.

*They let me go,* she thought, huddled against the rampart with the map's warmth bleeding through her coat. *Or they were called off. Or—*

Or they hadn't needed to catch her. Because they knew where she was going. There was only one place a map of the drowned halls could lead, only one door it could open, and the ninth door remembered her, and now, apparently, so did everything else.

Below in the valley, the caravan fires were lighting up one by one, small and gold and utterly unaware. Lior pulled the oilcloth bundle out and unwrapped it just enough to see the edge of the vellum, and there, in faded brown ink, in handwriting she would have known anywhere—in handwriting she had spent ten years trying to forget—were the first words of a route only she could walk.

Her mother's hand. Her mother's map. Her mother's wound, delivered by a dead man's kindness to the daughter who had run.

Lior wrapped it again, tucked it away, and turned her face toward the valley lights, toward the waystation, toward the one place in these mountains where a woman could sit in a corner with a cup of something hot and decide, slowly, what in God's name she was going to do next.

Behind her, the snow was already filling in Tomasin Ferro's resting place, flake by patient flake, and the sea, wherever it was, kept its own counsel.
