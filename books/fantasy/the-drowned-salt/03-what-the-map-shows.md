## 3. What the Map Shows

The Drowned Gull was the kind of tavern that existed because no one had ever bothered to burn it down.

Lior had been coming to it for six years, back when she still came down from the passes at all, and it had not improved in her absence. The floorboards were soft as old bread. The hearth smoked more than it warmed. The innkeeper, a woman named Brannoc with forearms like ship rope and a face that suggested she'd personally disappointed every god at once, watered the wine so thoroughly that Lior suspected there was a grape somewhere in the cellar feeling deeply betrayed.

It was perfect. Nobody looked twice at the Drowned Gull, and nobody who did look twice was anyone worth fearing.

She took the corner table—the one with its back to the wall and a clear view of both doors—and ordered stew she didn't taste and wine she didn't drink, and then, with the particular care of a woman handling a sleeping snake, she unrolled the dead courier's map on the scarred wood.

The oilskin was stiff with salt. That was the first wrong thing. Salt crusted the edges like frost, and when she brushed it with her thumb, it didn't flake away so much as *cling*, reluctant, the way salt clung to things that had been underwater a long time and hadn't decided to come back up.

The second wrong thing was that the map was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not body-warm. It held a heat like a stone pulled from a hearth hours ago, faint and stubborn, and Lior found herself thinking—absurdly—of the hot springs beneath Mount Serein that her mother had once described to her, back before, when her mother still described things instead of selling them.

*Stop it,* she told herself. *Look at the map.*

So she looked.

The cartography itself was fine work, better than fine—every line drawn with the confidence of someone who had walked what they drew. The Thornfell range ran down the left margin in careful hatching. The smuggler's roads she knew by heart were marked in faded brown ink: the Kettle Pass, the Widow's Stair, the frozen ford where she'd learned that ice sings differently depending on how much weight is crossing it. All familiar. All safe. All the world she had chosen, inch by inch, over ten years of deliberate smallness.

And then the map kept going.

Past the last brown road, past the tree line, past everything Lior knew, the ink changed color—a deep blue-green, the shade of water seen from underneath—and Mount Serein rose on the right-hand side of the skin, drawn not as a mountain but as a cross-section, cut open like a fruit to show what slept inside.

Halls. Galleries. Stairways descending into stairways descending into dark. A whole architecture drowned in blue-green wash, each chamber labeled in a small, precise hand.

Lior stared at that hand for a long time before she let herself admit she recognized it.

She knew it the way you know a voice in another room. The way the *r*s curled slightly, like they were looking over their shoulders. The way every *t* was crossed with a single decisive stroke, no hesitation, no second thought. Her mother had written shopping lists with that hand. Had written Lior's childhood lessons with it. Had written, once, in that same confident script, a letter that said *I will explain everything when you're older*, and then had never explained anything ever again, because she had sold her daughter's soul to a drowned court and bound her oaths so tight the girl could barely whisper.

The labels were in Old Court glyphs, mostly—the formal script of the tide-priests—but the annotations were plain trade-tongue, and they were her mother's, and they were everywhere.

*The ninth door opens to blood-oath only.*

*Do not take the Gallery of Bells after the third watch. They listen.*

*Maren lies beautifully. Count her promises.*

Lior's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the table until the tremor transferred to the wine cup, which rattled softly against the wood like something small trying to get out.

"More stew?" Brannoc appeared at her elbow, ladle in hand, radiating the aggressive hospitality of a woman who charged for refills.

"No." Lior flipped the map closed too fast. "Thank you."

Brannoc looked at her the way Brannoc looked at everyone—as though cataloguing their weaknesses for future reference—and then, unexpectedly, sat down across from her with a grunt. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of a woman reading a letter from somebody dead." She tapped her own temple. "I've seen it. My sister got one once, from a husband lost at sea. Took her three days to open it and three years to forgive herself for waiting." She stood, collected Lior's untouched bowl, and paused. "Whatever it says—it already said it. Reading it slower doesn't change the words."

Then she lumbered off, leaving Lior blinking at the wisdom, which was an odd thing to find in the Drowned Gull, between the watered wine and the suspicious stew.

* * *

She waited until the evening crowd thickened—mercenaries arguing about dice, a fur-trader snoring into his cups, two sisters singing quietly and badly by the fire—before she opened the map again. Crowds were cover. Crowds were noise. If the map was going to do whatever maps like this did, better it did it where nobody could see her flinch.

Because the map *did* things. She'd confirmed it in the hour since Brannoc's interruption, testing it the way she tested everything now: cautiously, at arm's length, ready to run.

When she held it near the candle, the blue-green halls seemed to deepen, shadows moving in the flooded chambers like fish. When she turned it over, the reverse was blank—truly blank, blanker than blank, a white that made her eyes slide off it—and yet the whole time she'd carried it down from the pass, she'd felt it against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

And when she traced one particular route with her finger—the route marked in red, threading through nine sealed doors from the mountain's root to the drowned throne above it—the ink *warmed under her touch*. Not the oilskin. The ink itself. As if the route had been waiting for exactly this finger.

There was a note beside the first door, in her mother's hand, and Lior read it eleven times before the words stopped rearranging themselves into new cruelties:

*Only an Oyelaran can walk this road. I am sorry. I am so sorry. I built the lock and only our blood fits the key.*

"Our blood," Lior whispered.

An Oyelaran. Singular. Herself, presumably, unless her mother had other daughters hidden in other lives, which—given everything—was not a thought Lior could afford tonight.

She thought about the courier instead. The stranger on the high trail with seawater in his lungs, forty miles from any sea, pressing this thing into her hands with his last strength. *The ninth door remembers you.* He'd known her name? No—he'd known what she was. Someone had sent him to find a swordspeaker, or specifically her, and he had died getting the map most of the way, and the Hollow Guard had arrived minutes behind him, hunting it.

Which meant the court below knew the map was gone. Which meant they would come looking. Which meant—

Which meant the past she had spent ten years outrunning had not been chasing her at all. It had been *waiting*. Patiently. Under a mountain. Holding a door open with her mother's handwriting, keeping her seat at a table she'd never agreed to sit at.

Lior laughed, once, and it came out wrong, and the singing sisters glanced over, and she smiled at them until they went back to butchering their ballad.

Ten years. Ten years of smallness, of never speaking above a murmur, of biting back cutting-words until her mouth tasted of copper and frost. She had told herself she was hiding. She had told herself she was free. And all the while some drowned clerk beneath Serein had probably entered her into a ledger: *Asset: one (1) Oyelaran. Location: surface. Status: expected.*

Expected. Like a delivery. Like weather.

Her mother's annotations grew worse the deeper the route went. Near the middle doors, the handwriting changed—not the letters, but the pressure of them, strokes dug hard enough to nearly tear the oilskin:

*If you are reading this, I failed. Forgive me or don't, but go home, Lior. Go home and take back what's yours.*

*Home.* The word sat in Lior's chest like a swallowed stone. Home had been a drafty house in the foothills with a mother who hummed while she worked and went quiet whenever Lior asked questions. Home had been the day the oaths were laid on her, cold bands tightening around her throat that no one else could see, and her mother's face doing something complicated that ten-year-old Lior had read as grief and now, looking at this map, read very differently.

Protection. Betrayal. The same hand. The same ink.

"You're going to make me come down there," Lior murmured to the map. "Aren't you. You absolute—" She stopped, because there was no insult large enough, and because insulting her mother's handwriting in a public tavern was the sort of thing that got a woman talked about.

The fire popped. The snoring trader rolled over. Life continued being ordinary around her, aggressively, almost defiantly ordinary, and Lior sat in the middle of it holding a document that might as well have been addressed *To the Last Oyelaran, From Everyone You've Been Running From.*

She should burn it. That was the sensible move. Feed it to the hearth, walk out the back door, take the Kettle Pass north, start over in a town where nobody had ever heard the word swordspeak. She'd done it before. She was good at it. Disappearing was, if she was honest, the one art she had truly mastered these ten years.

But the route was warm under her fingers. And the ninth door remembered her. And somewhere under the mountain, in a bottle, in the dark, something with her name on it was whispering.

You couldn't run from a thing that was patient. She'd learned that the hard way; patience always caught up. The only question—the only question that had ever mattered—was whether you met it running or standing.

Lior looked at the map a long moment. Then she did the bravest cowardly thing available to her: she folded it small, tucked it inside her shirt against her heart, where it beat its quiet salt-warm beat against her skin, and ordered a second cup of the terrible wine.

"For courage," she told Brannoc.

"For courage costs double," Brannoc said, and poured it anyway, and lingered. "Guard's been asking at the crossroads inn. Two days back. Asking after a woman traveling alone out of the high passes."

Lior kept her face pleasant and empty, the practiced nothing-face. "Lots of those this season."

"Mm." Brannoc set down the jug. "These ones wore gray. No livery. Paid in new coin." She met Lior's eyes, and there was something almost gentle buried in all that granite. "Back room's empty. So is the cellar hatch, if it comes to it. Been meaning to oil those hinges anyway."

"Brannoc—"

"Don't thank me, it makes me itch." She stalked off toward the snoring trader, ladle raised like a weapon of last resort.

Lior sat very still. Gray cloaks. New coin. Two days' ride behind her, maybe less. The Hollow Guard didn't ask at inns because they'd lost the trail. They asked at inns because they were closing it.

She had until morning, perhaps. Less, if the snows held off and the roads stayed fast.

She drank the courage, which tasted like grape-adjacent regret, and spread the map open one final time beneath the table's shadow, memorizing the route in long, stubborn strokes: nine doors, red thread, drowned halls, her mother's desperate hand pointing the way down.

*I built the lock and only our blood fits the key.*

"Fine," Lior whispered—to the map, to the mountain, to the woman who had locked her and then left her the key. "Fine. But I'm coming for answers first, and you don't get to hide behind *when you're older* anymore."

The ink warmed under her fingertip, once, like a pulse acknowledging receipt.

She was folding the map away when the tavern door swung open and the cold came in wearing a man—a lean, hollow-cheeked man in a traveler's coat gone gray at the seams, with riverwater still dripping off his hem and the unmistakable bearing of someone who had been defrocked recently enough to still reach for vestments that weren't there. His eyes swept the room with professional thoroughness, missed nothing, and settled on her corner table with the grim satisfaction of a man who has finally found the last page of a very bad book.

He started toward her, and Lior's hand drifted, quite calmly, to her knife.

Behind him, out in the dark, somewhere on the snowy road toward the Drowned Gull, she would later learn, gray cloaks were doing the same arithmetic she was—and arriving, as she was, at the same answer.

There was nowhere left to run that wasn't *down*.
