## 4. The Priest Who Drowned Twice

Cassian Vael had been dead for six years, in the official records of the Court Beneath Serein. He had drowned twice—once literally, during his ordination, when the tide-priests held him under the black water of the Font until his lungs gave up their argument; and once figuratively, when they struck his name from the rolls and declared his soul forfeit to the deep. The first death had been an honor. The second had been a mercy he hadn't recognized at the time.

He was drinking bad wine in a tavern called the Salted Wound—which was, he felt, an honest name for an establishment, if not an appetizing one—when he saw the map.

He wasn't supposed to be looking at anyone's maps. He was supposed to be looking at his cup and minding his own ruin, which was what he'd done every night for three years in the border town of Greyhollow. But Cassian had spent twenty years of his life reading seals, and some habits drown slower than priests.

The woman at the corner table had spread it open just wide enough—a fold of oiled leather, a flash of blue wax—and there it was: the sigil of the Ninth Door, the coiled wave-and-key that no living hand outside the court was supposed to know how to draw. And beneath it, in handwriting Cassian knew better than his own sins, a route marked in faded ink.

His cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

The woman herself was unremarkable by design—hood up, travel-stained, sitting with her back to the wall the way soldiers and fugitives do. She ate like someone who'd learned hunger as an enemy rather than an inconvenience: efficient, watchful, nothing wasted. A sword lay across her knees under the table, which was either very careless or very confident, and Cassian suspected confident.

But it was the way she held her hands that caught him. Flat on the table, fingers spread, as though she were pressing the wood down to keep it from saying something.

Swordspeakers did that. His old mentor Maren had told him about them once, late, after too much consecrated brandy—the Oyelaran line, the oath-bound, words that cut. He'd assumed she'd invented them to frighten novices into silence. Maren had been good at that.

*Don't*, Cassian told himself. *You are a dead man drinking bad wine in a town with one road out of it. Dead men do not get involved.*

He got involved.

It took him two days to work up to it, which he considered a personal record for cowardice. Two days of watching the woman come and go, of confirming through the innkeeper—who owed Cassian money, and therefore liked him—that she had no companions, no schedule, and a name she gave only as "Lior." Two days of telling himself that the seal might have been forged, that the handwriting might be coincidence, that whatever lived beneath Mount Serein could continue living there without his assistance.

And then, on the third morning, he saw the Hollow Guard ride into Greyhollow, four of them, gray-cloaked and wrong-eyed, asking questions in voices like water running backward. They showed the innkeeper a sketch. The innkeeper, who owed Cassian money and liked him, lied beautifully about a woman who had passed through weeks ago heading south.

They weren't looking for Cassian. That was almost funny. Six years defrocked, and the Guard still didn't consider him worth a sketch.

But they were looking for her, and she had the seal, and the seal meant the ninth door, and the ninth door meant—

Well. It meant everything he'd drowned to forget.

* * *

He cornered her at the well behind the tannery, which was not where he would have chosen to conduct delicate diplomacy, but she was leaving within the hour and beggars, Cassian had found, cannot be choosers even when they're only begging for conversation.

"Before you reach for the sword," he said, hands raised and empty, "I should tell you I've already died twice, so threatening me is largely ceremonial."

She didn't reach for the sword. She did something worse: she looked at him properly, top to bottom, with the flat patience of someone deciding whether a thing was worth killing. She was younger than he'd expected. There was frost in the set of her mouth, the kind that doesn't melt.

"You've been following me for three days," she said.

"Yes."

"That's not a defense. That's a confession."

"I'm a priest. Confession is my native language." He winced. "Formerly. It's complicated. May I—"

"No."

"—say one sentence?"

Her eyes narrowed, but she nodded once, and Cassian understood this was the only door opening today, so he chose his sentence the way a drowning man chooses his last breath.

"The map you carry bears the seal of the Ninth Door of the drowned court, and I am the only man in Greyhollow who knows what's behind it, and I am begging you to take me with you."

Silence. Somewhere behind the tannery, a worker cursed at a vat. Lior's hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to rest on the pommel of her sword—not drawing it, just touching it, the way other people touch charms against evil.

"And why," she said, "would a former priest—if you are one—want to walk into the place everyone else spends their lives avoiding?"

"Because I was one of them," Cassian said. "A tide-priest. Under the mountain. And I know what the court does with the souls it harvests, and I know it's getting worse, and I—" He stopped. Honesty, he'd learned, worked best in small doses, like arsenic. "I left something there. Someone. My mentor. And I have spent six years telling myself she's beyond saving, and I don't believe it anymore, and I can't live with not believing it anymore. So."

Lior studied him for a long moment. Then, to his genuine surprise, she laughed—one short, dry note, without much humor in it but with some, buried deep, like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"You're begging passage," she said, "to a place that scares you, guarded by people hunting me, to save a woman who probably signed your excommunication."

"She did sign it. She used red ink. She always said black ink lacked conviction."

That got the second almost-laugh. Progress, Cassian thought. At this rate she'd trust him by midwinter.

"Why should I believe anything you say?"

"You shouldn't. Belief is for altars. I'd settle for a wager." He held up his hands again. "Take me as far as the mountain. If I'm lying, you'll know long before we're inside, and you can leave me in the snow. I'm told exposure is quick this time of year. If I'm telling the truth, then you need me, because the ninth door opens to swordspeech and the halls beyond it open to nothing else. The court has spent three hundred years making sure no one walks those halls by accident."

Something flickered across her face at *swordspeech*—there and gone, fast, but Cassian had spent twenty years reading faces across altar-fires, and he knew what guilt looked like when it flinched.

"Who told you the door opens to swordspeech?" she asked quietly.

"A courier," Cassian said. "About a week ago. He came down off the high trail half-drowned, full of seawater he couldn't possibly have swallowed, raving about a map and a swordspeaker and the ninth door remembering someone. We thought he was mad. The tide-touched often are." He paused. "Was. He died two days later. Before he went, he made me swear that if I ever met the person he described, I'd tell them the truth about what's under the mountain. So here I am. Telling."

He watched her go very still. He watched her decide not to ask what the courier had described, because she already knew.

"The truth about what's under the mountain," she repeated. "Say it, then."

So he did. Standing at a tannery well in the cold, with the smell of lye in the air and the Hollow Guard somewhere in town, Cassian Vael told the whole ugly story for the first time in six years.

* * *

"It's called the Long Tide," he said. "That's not poetry. That's its name, or the closest our language gets. Something older than winter, older than the mountain. When the court was founded, the first tide-priests found it sleeping in the deep water beneath Serein, and instead of waking it or fleeing it, they made a bargain. Feed it, and it sleeps. Let it starve, and it wakes."

"Feed it what?"

"What do you think?" He let the silence answer. "Souls. Not all at once—never all at once, that would be crude, and the court is nothing if not refined. A few each year. Criminals, at first. Then debtors. Then pilgrims who came down to marvel and never marveled back up. The nobles drink bottled light at their feasts and call it communion. The tide-priests call it tithing. And the thing below turns over in its sleep, satisfied, and the mountain stands another century."

Lior's face had gone carefully blank, which Cassian recognized as the expression of someone holding something heavy very still so it doesn't spill.

"And your mentor," she said. "She runs this."

"She perfects it. High Priestess Maren Voss. She could make drowning sound like a sacrament—I know, because she made it mine." He touched his own throat, an old habit. "When I was ordained, they hold you under the Font until you stop fighting. You die a little. They say what comes back up is purer. What came back up in me, apparently, was a man who kept asking how many souls a year was *enough*. Twenty years of service, and that question ended my career. They took my vestments, my name from the rolls, and my soul on paper—they filed it with the harvest, actually. Somewhere under the mountain there's a bottle with my name on it, waiting for the next tithe. Which means I am, technically, already court property." He smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere useful. "So you see, taking me along costs you little. I'm pre-forfeited."

For the first time, something in her posture shifted—not softening exactly, but a settling, like a weight finding a better place to sit.

"They bottle souls," she said. "Named. Labeled."

"Labeled. Catalogued. Some of them whisper, if the glass is thin. The older ones especially. Why do you—"

He stopped. Because she had gone white, and her hand was no longer on her sword but pressed flat against her own sternum, over the heart, the way you press a wound.

"Cassian," she said, and it was the first time she'd used his name, and she said it like a question she hated asking. "In your years there. Did you ever see a bottle marked with a name that wasn't harvested? A name that was... sold? Given freely, before the harvest began?"

The cold walked up Cassian's spine one vertebra at a time. He thought of the courier's ravings. *The ninth door remembers you.* He thought of the route on the map, drawn in handwriting he'd known since boyhood, because Maren had taught him his letters from books that bore the same slanted script in the margins.

He thought: *oh, you poor child, you don't know whose map that is.*

"I don't know," he said, which was true, and gentler than the truth he suspected. "But I know the vaults. Every shelf. And if you get me inside, I'll help you look for whatever name you're afraid of."

"I'm not afraid of a name."

"No," Cassian agreed quietly. "You're afraid it's yours."

She stared at him for a long moment, and he braced himself to be left standing at the well with nothing but his bad wine and his worse history. Instead she pulled her hood up and turned toward the stable yard.

"One condition," she said. "You walk behind me. You don't touch the map. And if you pray to anything under that mountain, you pray quietly."

"I haven't prayed in six years," Cassian said, falling into step beside her—behind her, correcting himself immediately, "but I promise to blaspheme silently."

At the edge of the yard she paused, glancing back at the gray line of the pass, where snow was beginning to fall in earnest, fat and unhurried, like the mountain settling in for a long conversation.

"Hollow Guard reached Greyhollow yesterday," she said. "Four riders. They'll find the innkeeper lied by tonight."

"Then we should ride now."

"We should ride now," she agreed, and swung up into the saddle, and Cassian mounted his mule—her spare, loaned with the same generosity she extended to everything, which was to say grudgingly and under threat—and thought, as they clattered out onto the north road, that he had finally found a use for a dead man.

Behind them, Greyhollow smoked gently in the morning cold. Ahead, Mount Serein stood against the sky like a held breath.

Somewhere beneath it, in a vault of singing glass, a bottle waited with a name on it. Cassian only hoped, with the small dishonest hope of a man who had drowned twice and still feared the water, that it wasn't the name he was starting to fear it was.
