## 5. Hollow Riders

The river came out of the mountains the way bad news did—quietly at first, then all at once.

Lior heard it before she saw it, a low grinding under the morning hush, and by the time they rounded the last shoulder of pine and scree the Greywash was running high and brown with snowmelt, loud enough to swallow conversation. The ford lay ahead: a wide, stony shallows where the water braided itself thin enough to cross, marked by a leaning post wrapped in faded red cloth that some long-dead smuggler had tied as a promise. *Shallow here. Safe here.*

She didn't trust promises made by dead people. She'd been making too many of her own lately.

"High water," Cassian said beside her, unnecessarily. He had a gift for stating the obvious with the gravity of scripture. "The melt's come early."

"You'd know," Lior said. "Being a priest of tides and all. Defrocked," she added, when he looked at her. "I haven't forgotten."

"I would hope not. It's the most interesting thing about me." He squinted at the river, and for once he didn't look like a man performing serenity; he looked like a man counting something. "It shouldn't be this high. Not yet. Something upstream is holding the melt back and letting it go in bursts."

Lior's hand drifted to her sword without her deciding anything. That was the thing about a decade of hiding—her body had learned to make decisions while her mind was still being reasonable. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning either the mountain is breathing strangely," Cassian said, "or someone wants the ford deep."

They crossed anyway. There wasn't another crossing for two days north or four days south, and the map under Lior's coat seemed to grow heavier with every hour she carried it, as though it were drinking from her. The horses went first, Vess's old pack mare complaining in the deep, rolling way horses complained—*I have done everything for you people and this is my thanks*—and then the two of them, thigh-deep in water so cold it felt less like temperature than opinion. The current shoved at their legs with big, blunt hands.

Halfway across, Lior stopped listening to the river.

That was her mistake, though she wouldn't understand it until later. She'd spent ten years learning every sound the Thornfell made—the whistle of wind through the passes, the tick of ice settling, the particular hush a hawk's shadow left behind—and somewhere in the last two weeks, walking beside a man who talked the way water ran, she had let the river fill her ears and called it safety.

What she didn't hear was hooves.

Cassian heard them. Or felt them—she never got the full account, because what happened next happened fast—but his hand closed on her arm, hard, and his face went through several expressions very quickly, like a man shuffling cards, and settled on one she'd learn to dread: recognition.

"Down," he said.

The arrows came out of the pines on the far bank, six of them, low and flat and fast. One took the pack mare through the neck. She screamed—that awful, human scream horses made—and went down sideways into the current, packs and all, and the river took her like it had been waiting all morning for an excuse.

Lior hit the water. Cold closed over her like a door. She came up coughing, dragged herself behind the leaning post with its dead red promise, and pulled Cassian down beside her by the collar.

"Hollow Guard," he said. Water ran off his nose. His voice was steady in the way that made her want to shake him. "Six, maybe eight. They'll hold the far bank and let us freeze rather than charge a ford. It's what I'd do."

"How comforting that you've thought about it."

"It's why you keep me around."

"I keep you around because you can read the map's seal and I can't." Arrows thudded into the post above them, one after another, patient as carpenters. "And because leaving you on a trailhead seemed rude."

Another volley. The red cloth shredded off the post in ribbons. Across the water, shapes moved among the trees—not men, quite. The Hollow Guard rode armored in grey-lacquer over grey mail, faces behind grey visors slit like letterboxes, and they didn't shout challenges or demand surrender. That was the thing everyone got wrong about them, Lior thought, pressing her cheek against wet stone. People expected cruelty to be loud. The Hollow Guard were quiet the way winter was quiet. They had been hunting her for ten years without ever once raising their voices, and there was something almost respectful in it, if you were sick enough to find respect there.

"We could swim for it," Cassian said.

"In this current? The river would do their work for them." She risked a look. Riders were moving along the far bank now, unhurried, spreading out. Two had dismounted and were wading in from downstream, flanking. "They're not trying to kill us. They're herding us back to our own bank."

"Why?"

Because they wanted her alive. Because whatever the dying courier had pressed into her hands, the court beneath Mount Serein wanted its property back, and property didn't get riddled with arrows. She didn't say any of this aloud. Saying it aloud made it real, and she had built a whole life on the principle that things stayed unreal as long as you kept your mouth shut about them.

The two wading riders were close now, forty feet, thirty, water to their knees, crossbows held high and dry. Behind the post, Cassian was murmuring something—a prayer, maybe, or an inventory of regrets. Lior found she was doing arithmetic instead. Thirty feet. Two crossbows. One sword, and a voice she hadn't used in ten years, coiled up under her tongue like a snake sleeping through winter.

There was a version of her—the smuggler, the ghost, the woman with no name worth printing—who waited. Who let them take her, even, betting that the court needed her breathing and that alive meant negotiable. That version had kept her breathing for a decade. That version had rules: *never spend more than a whisper, never say a word that cuts, hide the gift so deep even you forget where you buried it.*

The other version of her thought about the courier on the high trail, seawater in his lungs though he'd died a hundred miles from any sea, and the way he'd said *the ninth door remembers you*, like handing her a bill already overdue.

The wading riders reached twenty feet. One of them raised his crossbow, unhurried, certain.

Lior stood up out of the water.

She heard Cassian say her name—it was the first time he'd said it, actually, which was a fine time to start—and then she opened her mouth and broke ten years of silence with a single word.

She chose it the way you'd choose a blade from a rack: by weight and balance. Old swordspeech, older than the Thornfell, older than the oath that bound it. A word whose meaning was roughly *sever* and whose grammar required no object, because it cut whatever it was pointed at. She shaped it with the front of her tongue and the roof of her mouth and gave it breath, and the word left her and crossed the water faster than sound has any right to travel.

The world went white along a line.

Not a flash—more like the memory of one, an afterimage burned into the air itself. The nearest rider came apart at the waist with a sound like wet rope snapping. The second had time to turn his crossbow toward her, and the word—still going, words like that didn't stop for one target—took him through the visor slit and out the back of his helm, and he sat down in the river very politely, like a man deciding he'd waded far enough. The third rider, still mounted on the far bank, caught the tail end of it through the chest and fell backward out of the saddle, and his horse stood there afterward looking offended.

Then it was done, and the river ran brown and quiet again, carrying things downstream that it hadn't been carrying a moment before.

Lior stood in the shallows, chest heaving, and tasted blood.

No—not blood. Frost. Her tongue had gone numb, and when she worked her jaw, she felt it: a raw, cracked ache, as if she'd bitten through ice. She spat into the water and the spit came out pink-tinged and steaming oddly, and when she ran her tongue along her teeth she felt raised ridges there, hard and cold, like scars made of winter itself.

Frost-scars. Her mother's voice, ten years old and memory-thin: *Every word costs, little knife. The true ones cost true.* She'd thought that was poetry. Mothers said things like that. Nobody told you they meant it literally, that the price was paid in the flesh of your own mouth, carved there, permanent, a ledger written on the one part of you that couldn't lie about how much you'd spent.

Three riders. One word. Three scars.

On the far bank, the remaining Guard had gone very still. Then—one of them raised a hand, and the whole line withdrew into the trees, unhurried, silent, the way a tide withdraws. Not defeated. Recalculating.

"They'll be back," Cassian said. He had come up beside her without her noticing, which should have frightened her and mostly just exhausted her. He was staring at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere—not fear, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a story he'd only ever heard read aloud.

"Don't," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're about to. You have a face like a sermon warming up." She waded past him toward the bank, toward the drowned packs and the mare floating belly-up fifty yards downstream, and her tongue ached with every word, sweet and terrible. "Whatever you saw, put it away. I'm a smuggler with a good throwing arm. That's the story."

"That was swordspeech." He followed her, relentless as weather. "True swordspeech. Unbound. Do you know how long it's been since anyone spoke an unbound cutting-word? The archives say the practice died with the Oyelaran line. The archives say a great many things died." He paused. "The archives, apparently, need to get out more."

She laughed. It hurt, laughing; the frost-scars pulled. She laughed anyway, because the alternative was screaming, and she was saving her screaming budget.

"The Oyelaran line," she said, "is a dead woman in a grave outside Thornhollow who was clever enough to die under the wrong name. Whatever you think you saw—"

"Lior." And there it was again, her name in his mouth, second use, and this time it landed like a hand on her shoulder. "You killed three Hollow Guard with one syllable and your tongue is bleeding frost. You are standing in a river that someone is using as a weapon, heading for a drowned court that sold your soul before you could walk, following a map written in your mother's hand. I think we are well past the part of the journey where we pretend you are a smuggler with a good throwing arm."

She stood in the shallows and let the river push at her, and for a long moment she said nothing, because everything she might say cost her, now, in cold and pain and permanence. Silence had been armor for ten years. It turned out silence also had a weight, and she'd been wearing it so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her body.

"My mother taught me," she said finally. "Before she sold me off piece by piece, apparently, she taught me. Said words were the family trade." She climbed the near bank, wrung out her coat with two sharp twists. "I swore I'd never use them. Not once. Ten years. Today I spent my whole vow on three men I never learned the names of, and now there are scars in my mouth that will never heal, and the Guard knows what I am, and—" she gestured vaguely upstream, at the river, at the whole treacherous arrangement of the world, "—and the mare's dead, and the map was in the pack that sank, and I am having what I believe is called a day."

Cassian had gone pale. "The map was in the mare's packs?"

"No." She tapped her chest, where the oilskin bundle rode dry against her ribs, because some habits survived even revelations. "The map's fine. The brandy, tragically, is gone."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Then he looked back across the river, where the pines held nothing but grey shade and the bodies the water hadn't taken, and his voice dropped into something quieter and worse.

"They withdrew," he said. "Hollow Guard don't withdraw. They regroup, and they report, and within a day every road south of here will have grey riders on it asking quiet questions. We cannot go back the way we came. We can't stay on the high trails—they'll sweep those with dogs. Which means—"

"Don't say it."

"—we need help," Cassian finished, "and there is exactly one person in the Thornfell who can hide us, move us, and hates the Guard enough to try. Your fence. Vess."

Lior closed her eyes. Of all the debts she'd sworn never to incur again, Vess topped the list—not because Vess was cruel, but because Vess was kind, and kindness from a fence always came with interest calculated in ways you didn't see until the collection date.

"She's going to make me pay for this," Lior said.

"Probably."

"With something awful."

"Almost certainly."

"And she's going to be smug the entire time."

"Oh," Cassian said, with the serene confidence of a man who had known Vess for two days and already grasped the essentials, "unbearably."

Lior looked back once at the ford—at the red ribbons drifting off the broken post, at the brown water closing over everything—and turned north, toward Vess, toward the drowned halls waiting below Mount Serein, her scarred tongue silent in her mouth and the map against her ribs beating like a second heart.

Behind them, far upstream, the river rose another inch. Something in the mountain had let go of the melt again.

Someone, she corrected herself, and started walking.
