## 6. The Smuggler's Debt

Cassian had served eleven years among the tide-priests of Serein, and in that time he had learned that the most dangerous rooms in the world were not the ones full of knives but the ones full of ledgers. Knives only wanted your blood. Ledgers wanted everything else.

Vess's counting room, behind the Drowned Rat tavern in the river town of Marrowgate, was full of both.

"Sit," said the woman behind the desk, without looking up.

They sat. There was a bench against the wall, worn smooth by the backs of a thousand desperate people who had all been told *sit* in exactly that tone, and Cassian suspected every one of them had obeyed as quickly as he just had.

Lior did not sit. She stood near the door with her arms crossed and her hood back, watching Vess the way a cat watches a dog it has decided to tolerate rather than trust. Ten days on the road since the ford — ten days since Cassian had watched her speak three words that killed three riders and left frost-scars blooming up her own tongue like white lichen — and she still moved like someone expecting the floor to betray her.

"You look terrible," Vess said, still reading her ledger. "Both of you. The priest especially. Priests always look terrible when they've left their temples. It's the absence of incense. Goes to the skin."

"I was defrocked," Cassian offered.

"That would do it." Vess finally looked up. She was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, with grey-streaked hair cropped short and a face built for scowling at customs officers. Her eyes went to Lior first, and something in them softened by a degree so small Cassian might have imagined it. "You're thinner than the last time I saw you. And you're standing in my doorway like you're about to apologize for it."

"I'm not staying," Lior said.

"You're absolutely staying. You're staying until I say otherwise." Vess closed the ledger. "Because you came to me, which means you're out of options, which means you'll listen to terms. Sit down, Lior Oyelaran. You too, priest. There's tea. It's terrible. Drink it anyway."

* * *

The tea was, indeed, terrible. Cassian drank it anyway. It tasted of bark and iron and possibly a coin someone had dropped in the pot for luck, and he found himself oddly comforted by it. Holiness, he had learned, mostly smelled of incense; goodness smelled of whatever was actually in the pot.

"So," Vess said, settling back. "The Hollow Guard burned through Marrowgate two nights ago. Six riders. Asking after a woman with a sword and a mouth they're afraid of, traveling with a man in priest's grey with the collar torn off." Her gaze flicked to Cassian's throat, where the torn collar still showed as a pale line against his sunburn. "That's specific enough to be expensive. Which brings us to business."

"We need a week," Lior said. "A safe room, food, no questions—"

"No," Vess said pleasantly. "Try again."

"A safe room, food, and answers to whatever questions you already have."

"Better. But not good enough." Vess turned the ledger around and pushed it across the desk. On the open page, in a neat merchant's hand, was written: *One map. One copy right. One truth.* "Here are my terms, and I'll thank you not to interrupt, because I rehearsed this on the stairs and I'll lose my nerve if you talk over me.

"One: I shelter you. Both of you. Food, beds, a locked room, and my people keep their mouths shut, which costs me more than you'd think — Tamir alone would sell his grandmother for a bottle of plum wine, and his grandmother is the one who taught him to haggle.

"Two: the map. I want copy rights. Not the original — I know better than to ask, and I know you'd sooner eat it than hand it over. I want the right to have it copied, once, by my own hand, and to sell that copy when this is done. Whatever's under Mount Serein is worth more than this whole town, and I have spent thirty years building a business on knowing what things are worth before other people do."

Lior's jaw tightened. "The route is only walkable by—"

"By an Oyelaran. Yes. I can read, Lior, I just can't read *that*." Vess tapped the map where it lay wrapped in oilcloth against Lior's hip. "I don't want to walk into your drowned mountain. I want to be the fence who sold the map of it. Do you understand what that's worth? Every treasure-hunter, every scholar, every bored noble with more money than sense will pay handsomely for a map they can't use. It's perfect. It's beautiful. It is the single most profitable piece of uselessness I will ever own."

"And the third thing?" Cassian asked quietly.

Vess looked at him for a long moment. Then back at Lior, and the merchant's polish dropped away like a coat taken off at the end of a long day.

"The truth," she said. "Why does the Hollow Guard track *you*? Not swordspeakers generally — there haven't been any of those in a decade, the world made sure of it. You. By name, by face, by description detailed enough that six riders crossed a frozen pass in winter to find one woman. I've known you nine years, girl. You've never told me what you are. You've never told me why you flinch when priests walk past. And now the Guard is burning towns behind you, and I am about to stake my life and my livelihood on you, and I find that I would very much like to know what I'm buying."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough that Cassian counted his own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

He understood, in an abstract way, what Vess was asking. He understood it less abstractly than most, because he had spent the last ten days watching Lior decide, over and over, how much of herself to spend. A word here to turn a rockslide. A word there to kill men who would have killed them. Each time, the frost-scars on her tongue spread a little further, and each time she paid without complaint and hated herself quietly afterward, and Cassian had begun to suspect that the Guard hunting her was only the outside edge of a much older debt.

But Vess didn't know any of that. Vess knew a smuggler who'd appeared in Marrowgate nine years ago with a sword she never drew and a silence she never broke, who paid her debts promptly and asked no favors and told no stories, and who had just walked back into her life trailed by dead men and a defrocked priest.

"I can't," Lior said.

"You can," Vess said. "You simply don't want to, and I sympathize, because neither do I want to hear it. But those are the terms."

"Vess—"

"I chose the price carefully," Vess said, and for the first time her voice roughened. "Do you think I want your secrets? I want to sleep tonight. I want to know that when the Guard kicks my door in — and they will, if I shelter you — I'll know it wasn't for nothing. That the woman I'm bleeding for isn't going to get my people killed for something rotten." She spread her hands. "So tell me the worst version. Whatever it is. I promise you I've heard worse. I fenced goods for the Salt Baron's own steward for six years. There is nothing you can say that will shock me."

"There might be," Cassian murmured.

"Then say it, priest, and let's find out."

* * *

Later, Cassian would reflect that Lior told it badly — flatly, quickly, staring at the middle distance over Vess's shoulder, the way people recite things they've rehearsed precisely so they won't feel them. She told Vess about swordspeech. About words that cut deeper than steel, that could unmake stone and stop hearts, that left their scars on the speaker. About the oath-bound order she'd been born into, the last of its line. About the oath itself, sworn in her mother's name before she was old enough to refuse it, binding her voice to silence.

And then — because Vess had asked for the worst version, and because some part of Lior, Cassian thought, had been waiting nine years for someone to ask — she told her about the courier on the high trail. The seawater in his mouth. *The ninth door remembers you.* The map in her mother's handwriting. The drowned court beneath Mount Serein, and the bottled soul bearing her own name.

"My mother sold it," Lior said. "Or so the map implies. My soul, to the tide-priests, when I was a child. I don't know why yet. I don't know if it's even true. But the Guard isn't chasing a fugitive, Vess. They're chasing property. They're retrieving something that was sold, and I'm the packaging."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, one of Vess's people dropped something heavy and swore with real artistry.

Vess sat very still. Then she reached for the teapot, refilled all three cups with the terrible tea, and drank hers in one long swallow, which Cassian recognized as a ritual he had seen grieving families perform.

"Well," she said. "You're right. That's the worst version."

"I warned you."

"You did." Vess set down the cup. "And now I'm going to say something, and you're both going to let me finish, because I've earned it." She pointed at Lior. "Nine years ago a half-starved girl walked into my back room carrying stolen temple silver and a lie about where she got it. Worst liar I ever met. Sweating like a butcher. I bought the silver anyway, and I gave you work, and I never asked why a girl who could afford honest bread preferred stolen it. I told myself it wasn't my business." Her voice caught, just slightly, and she covered it by straightening papers that didn't need straightening. "It was my business. I knew something was wrong with you, Lior. Anyone with eyes knew. And I took the easy money and kept my questions behind my teeth, because that's what fences do, and I have regretted it every year since. So no. I'm not taking your soul-story as payment. I'm taking it as an apology nine years late, and we're calling ourselves even on that account, and *now* we'll discuss the actual debt."

Lior blinked. In ten days of travel, Cassian had seen her face fear, grief, fury, and frost-scarred pain. He had not, until this moment, seen her look genuinely disarmed.

"The copy rights stand?" she managed.

"The copy rights stand. One copy, my hand, sold after this is finished or after you're dead, whichever comes second." Vess's grin returned, sharp as a filleting knife. "Though between us, I hope it's the first. Dead clients are terrible about paying their debts."

"And the truth?"

"Was worth exactly what I paid for it." Vess closed the ledger. "Which is to say: nothing, and everything. Welcome home, girl. Both of you. Tamir will show you the room. Mind the fourth stair; it bites."

* * *

The room was small and clean and above the counting house, with one window overlooking the frozen canal and two beds that had clearly been argued over and recently aired. Cassian set his pack down and discovered, folded on the pillow of the smaller bed, a clean shirt in priest's grey — new collar intact — and beneath it a note in a firm merchant's hand:

*Not charity. It goes on your account. — V.*

He held the shirt for a while without putting it on.

Downstairs, faintly, he could hear the two women talking. Not arguing — talking, low and continuous, the particular rhythm of old friends catching up on nine years of omissions. Once he heard Vess laugh, a great unguarded bark of it, and once he heard Lior say something too quiet to catch, and then quiet laughter from her too, rusty, like a gate opening after a long winter.

He put on the shirt. It fit almost perfectly, which meant Vess had guessed his measurements from across a room, which meant she had decided to keep him before she'd decided anything else.

He was still marveling at this when the knock came — three raps, quick, the kind that meant trouble wearing politeness. He opened the door on Tamir, Vess's nephew, a young man with his aunt's build and none of her composure, breathing hard from the stairs.

"Priest." Tamir glanced past him into the room, then lowered his voice. "Aunt says come down. Now. Quietly." He swallowed. "There's a rider at the toll bridge asking after you. Grey cloak, torn collar. And—" he hesitated, visibly deciding whether to soften it, and decided against, "—and she's got a writ. Sealed with the Hollow Guard's mark. She's offering fifty crowns for word of where you're sleeping tonight."

Cassian felt the cold arrive in his chest, familiar now, almost companionable.

"Fifty crowns," he repeated.

"Fifty-five, by now. She's haggling with the ferryman." Tamir grimaced. "And Aunt Vess says — her words — 'tell the priest the Rat's back door still works, and tell him if he breathes a word of gratitude I'll dock his account.'"

Cassian went downstairs. At the bottom of the steps, Lior was already waiting in the shadows by the back door, map wrapped tight against her hip, sword loose in its sheath, and beside her Vess pressed a small purse into her hands and was saying, fast and fierce, "—the ferryman owes me, you'll cross at the weir instead, and Lior — *Lior.* Listen to me. Whatever's under that mountain, whatever your mother did — you come back up. That's not a request. That's a term. I don't collect on dead friends."

"I know," Lior said. "You'll invoice me."

"Damn right I will." Vess pulled her into a brief, crushing hug, then shoved her toward the door. "Go. Ninth door, was it? Then go open it. And priest—" she jabbed a finger at Cassian, "—keep her alive. That also goes on your account."

The back alley was blue with early dusk and smelled of snow coming down off the Thornfells. As they slipped out along the frozen canal, Cassian heard, far behind them at the toll bridge, the ferryman's voice rise in cheerful negotiation, selling a lie about two travelers headed south.

Three days north, Cassian knew, lay the foothills of Mount Serein. Three days, and then a hidden entrance that opened only to a voice like hers — which meant someone down in the dark had been waiting, all these years, for an Oyelaran to come home.

He only hoped, watching Lior set the pace ahead of him, quick and silent and terribly alone even with company, that it was waiting to welcome her.

He was beginning to fear it was waiting to collect.
