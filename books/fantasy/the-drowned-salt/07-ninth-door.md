## 7. Ninth Door

The mountain had been waiting for them for three days, and it was not subtle about it.

Lior felt it first as a pressure behind her eyes, the same dull ache she got before a rockslide or a lie told too close to her face. By the second morning it had settled into her teeth. By the third, when they crested the shoulder of Mount Serein's lower slopes and the wind died all at once—died, like something had put a hand over its mouth—she stopped pretending it was indigestion.

"It knows we're here," she said.

Cassian, hauling himself up the last of the scree with the grace of a man who had been drowned twice and never quite learned to walk on dry land again, looked at her with that expression he wore when he was deciding whether to be honest or comforting. He usually chose honest. It was one of his more irritating virtues.

"The mountain doesn't know things," he said. "The court knows things. And yes. It has known since the courier died on your trail. Possibly since before that." He paused, catching his breath, and added, "I'd have mentioned it sooner, but you were sleeping so peacefully and I'm a coward."

"You're not a coward. You're a priest. There's a difference, and it's mostly paperwork."

He laughed—a short, startled sound, like he'd forgotten how—and Lior found herself absurdly pleased by it. Three days of climbing will do that. Three days of frozen rope and shared rations and Cassian's endless, nervous commentary on the theology of tides, and even the most guarded heart starts keeping company with the people beside it.

She hated that a little. Company was a debt, and debts were how the Hollow Guard found you.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a granite shelf that some ancient hand had carved into steps—steps leading nowhere, worn smooth by water that hadn't flowed in centuries. Lior built no fire. Cassian built one anyway, small and defiant, cupping it in a hollow of stone.

"If they're tracking us," he said, "they're tracking us. A cold camp won't save us. A warm one might at least save our fingers, and I need my fingers for praying."

"Do you? I thought defrocked priests just made it up as they went."

"All priests make it up as they go. The frock is what makes it official." He fed the fire a twig. "Lior. Can I ask you something, and will you answer it honestly, or will you do the thing where you turn a question into an insult so you don't have to feel anything?"

"I'll consider both options carefully."

"Why did you come?" He looked at her across the flames, and the firelight did him the disservice of making him look young. "Not why are we going to the mountain—I know why. The map, the soul, all of it. But you could have sold the map. You could have run further west, changed your name again. You've done it before; Vess told me, and Vess tells everyone everything eventually. So why walk toward the thing you've spent ten years running from?"

Lior turned her hands toward the fire. The frost-scars on her tongue had faded since the ford, but she could still feel them when she spoke too long—the ghost of every cutting word she'd ever used, stacked up inside her mouth like coins in a miser's purse.

"Because it's mine," she said finally. "The soul. Whatever my mother did, whatever bargain she made—she sold something that belongs to me. Not to the tide-priests. Not to the Long Tide, whatever that is. To me." She flexed her fingers. "And because I've spent ten years being careful, and careful is just another word for buried alive. I'd rather die on purpose than live by accident."

Cassian was quiet for a long moment. Then: "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me since I was expelled."

"What did you do? Before. When they threw you out."

"I told the truth," he said simply. "At the wrong time, to the wrong people, about the wrong harvest. They call it defrocking. It's really just drowning with extra steps." He smiled, and it didn't reach his eyes. "Sleep, swordspeaker. Tomorrow we find your door."

* * *

The door found them first.

It was midmorning, and the snow had gone strange—not falling, not lying flat, but standing, in places, in thin white pillars that leaned against nothing. Lior had seen weather behave badly in the Thornfell before, but this was different. This was weather that had been *arranged*.

"There," Cassian said, and pointed.

Set into the mountainside ahead of them was a wall of black stone, seamless except for a single line—a vertical seam, thin as a hair, running from the snowline down into the rock itself. No handle. No hinges. No inscription.

Except there was an inscription. Lior just couldn't see it with her eyes.

She could see it with everything else.

The words rose off the stone like heat off a summer road, and they were written in swordspeech—the old grammar, the deep grammar, the language that lived in the space between a word and its meaning. Her mother's alphabet. Her mother's cadence. She knew it the way you know a voice calling from another room: not the words themselves, but who was speaking.

*Only the oath-bound may enter. Only the bound may pass.*

"Oh," said Cassian softly. "Oh, that's—"

"Shut up." Lior stepped forward. Her heart was doing something complicated and unpleasant. "That's swordspeech. That's *high* swordspeech. Nobody writes high swordspeech anymore. Nobody's spoken it in—" She stopped. Recounted. Felt the number land in her chest like a dropped stone. "In two hundred years. Since the Oyelarans were sworn."

"Which means?"

"Which means someone carved this door expecting one of us." She turned to look at him, and her breath came out ragged. "Cassian. My family hasn't been oath-bound in ten years. My mother unbound us herself. Whoever wrote this door wrote it for a family that doesn't exist anymore."

"Or," Cassian said gently, "for a family that was always going to come back."

The wind stood still around them. The snow-pillars leaned. Somewhere far below, deep in the mountain's bones, Lior thought she heard water moving—warm water, salt water, water that had no business being warm this high up a frozen mountain.

She raised her hand to the door.

"Don't," Cassian said. Then, immediately: "No, do. Obviously do. I just wanted to say don't first, so that later, when everything goes wrong, I can point out that I said don't."

"That's very reassuring."

"I'm a priest. Reassurance is sacramental."

Lior pressed her palm flat against the black stone. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything about this mountain was warm in the wrong places, like a body with a fever.

She spoke.

Not loudly. Swordspeech didn't need volume—it needed truth, and truth needed only enough breath to carry it. She gave the door the oldest greeting in the oath-grammar, the words her mother had taught her before teaching her to hide them, the words that meant roughly *I am the blade and I am the hand that holds it and I have come home.*

The seam split.

Light came out—but not light, exactly. Warmth. A long exhalation of air that smelled of brine and old candlewax and something sweeter underneath, something that made her think of her mother's kitchen, of salt cod and honey, of a childhood she had spent a decade trying to forget. The black stone swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, and beyond it lay stairs.

Stairs descending into water.

Black water, warm and utterly still, filling the stairwell from the tenth step down into darkness. No current. No ripple. It simply sat there, patient as a held breath, waiting for her foot.

"Well," said Cassian, behind her. His voice had gone very quiet. "That's new. The flooded halls aren't supposed to start until the third gallery. This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This is the mountain making room for you. Or making room for something it thinks is you."

Lior stood at the top of the stairs and felt fear arrive in her chest—not the sharp animal fear of the ford, not the dread of the Guard, but a slow, cold, patient fear, the kind that comes from understanding exactly where the point of no return is and finding yourself already standing on it.

Behind her: the pass, the snow, the roads she knew. Every road she knew, actually, laid out behind her like a hand of cards she'd already counted. She could walk away. She could go back to Vess, back to the smuggling runs, back to the small careful life of a woman who was nobody, which was the safest thing a woman with her gift could ever be.

Ahead of her: warm black water, and a stolen soul, and a mother's handwriting on a map that had been waiting ten years to be read.

"Lior," Cassian said. "We can still—"

"No," she said. "We can't. That's rather the point of doors."

She took off her boots, tied them to her pack, and started down the stairs barefoot, because if she was going to drown today she intended to do it with dry boots waiting for her on the other side. The water reached her ankles at the tenth step. It was blood-warm. It was salt. And as it closed around her skin, soft as a hand, she heard—faintly, from far below, from the drowned halls where the tide-priests sang their auction songs—

her own name.

Spoken in her mother's voice.

Lior Oyelaran stood in the dark water on the ninth step of the ninth door, and made her choice, and kept walking down.
