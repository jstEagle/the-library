## 8. The Court Beneath

Cassian had attended a great many ceremonies in his life as a tide-priest, and he had learned early that the secret to surviving them was to look like you belonged. Belonging, he had told his novices once, was ninety percent posture and ten percent not asking questions. He had been defrocked for many reasons since then, but never for that particular heresy.

So when Lior handed him the rough gray pilgrim's cloak and said, "Try to look devout," he did not argue. He simply arranged his shoulders into the shape of a man who had spent his life kneeling, and found—to his mild disgust—that the shape still fit.

"You've done this before," Lior observed, watching him settle the hood.

"I was clergy for twenty years. Devout is my native language." He tugged the cowl lower. "The trick is to walk slowly and appear mildly disappointed in everyone around you."

"That's just your face."

"My face is a gift to the liturgy."

She almost smiled. It was a small thing, barely a crack in the ice of her, but Cassian had spent three weeks walking beside this woman through snow and silence, and he had learned to treasure small things the way a drowning man treasures bubbles. Three weeks ago she had been a stranger bleeding out on a trail; now she was a stranger who slept with her back to a wall and flinched at bells. Progress, of a kind.

They stood at the mouth of the pilgrims' gallery, where warm air rose from below like breath from a sleeping animal. The water began here—a black canal running down into the mountain's heart, wide enough for the flat-bottomed barges that ferried the faithful. The smell was salt and something sweeter underneath, like flowers left too long in brine.

"Pilgrims come down in waves," Cassian murmured, eyes forward. "Between the low bell and the high bell there's a gap. We go with the third wave, stay in the middle of it, and we do not speak above a whisper."

"And if someone speaks to us?"

"Then I talk. You have the conversational warmth of a drawn blade."

"I'm aware."

He glanced at her. "That wasn't an insult."

"It wasn't taken as one."

* * *

The barge took them down.

Cassian had expected cold. What he got instead was warmth—the deeper they sank, the warmer the water became, until the air itself felt like bathwater, thick and soft against the skin. Lanterns hung from chains along the gallery walls, burning underwater without going out, their light bending strangely through the flooded arches. Fish the color of candle flames drifted past the windows cut into the rock. Somewhere far below, music.

Lior sat rigid at his side, her hand resting on the wrapped hilt beneath her cloak, and stared at the water with the expression of a woman composing insults she could not yet afford to say aloud.

"Breathe," Cassian said quietly.

"I am breathing."

"Like a woman being strangled politely."

Her jaw worked. "It's wrong," she said finally, so low only he could hear. "All of it. The warmth. The light. Water shouldn't be kind."

"No," Cassian agreed. "It shouldn't." And that was the heart of what he could not yet bring himself to tell her: that kindness was precisely what frightened him. The drowned court did not need to be cruel. Cruelty was for places that had to hold power by force. This place held its people the way honey holds a fly—gently, sweetly, permanently.

The barge grounded against a dock of white stone, and they stepped off among forty other pilgrims in gray cloaks, and Cassian looked up at the drowned court of Mount Serein for the first time in eleven years.

He had prepared himself for ruin. He had prepared himself for grandeur. He had not prepared himself for beauty.

The hall opened above them like the inside of a pearl. Vaulted ceilings of pale coral-stone arched up into darkness where chandeliers of living jellyfish pulsed slow colored light. Galleries ringed the space, tier upon tier, and from every balcony hung gardens—kelp braided into patterns, anemones blooming like fireworks frozen mid-burst. Stairs of mother-of-pearl spiraled upward toward a throne dais lost in the soft gloom. And everywhere, everywhere, the people: nobles in robes of woven silver drifting through the water as easily as air, their hair floating around their faces in dark halos, their throats working gently as they breathed the flood.

"They're breathing it," Lior whispered. Her voice had gone strange. "Cassian. They're *breathing* the water."

"The blessing of the deep," he said. "Old rite. The lungs learn salt." He kept his tone dry because the alternative was awe, and awe was how this place ate people. "Don't stare. Pilgrims stare, but pilgrims stare *down*. At the floor. Humility."

"What are you staring at?"

"The architecture. I'm allowed. I'm a disappointed former professional."

A noblewoman drifted past them close enough to touch, laughing at something her companion had said, and the sound came through the water warped and lovely, like a song heard through a wall. She was beautiful. They were all beautiful. That was the thing no ballad ever mentioned about the drowned court—that it was gorgeous, that it had been designed by people with impeccable taste and no consciences whatsoever, that corruption down here wore pearls.

* * *

The auction was held in the Shell Court, a smaller chamber off the main hall where the ceiling dipped low and the acoustics gathered every whisper and returned it threefold. Cassian steered them to the back, behind a pillar furred with soft red coral, and from there they watched.

A tide-priest stood at a lectern of abalone shell, robed in blue so deep it was nearly black, and before him, on tiers of velvet that should have rotted decades ago but hadn't, sat the bottles.

There were hundreds of them. Small ones, wine-flask sized, and great ones like funeral urns, each stoppered with wax the color of moonlight, each holding a curl of light that moved. Not flickered—*moved*, the way a trapped moth moves, pressing against the glass in slow, patient circles.

Souls.

Cassian had known, intellectually. He had told Lior as much on the road: the court harvests souls, the court feeds the Long Tide. Knowing a thing and watching a priest hold a bottle up to the light while a crowd of elegant, water-breathing aristocrats leaned forward with expressions of connoisseurship were two very different educations.

"Lot forty-one," the priest intoned, and his voice filled the chamber like poured oil. "Harvested fresh from the eastern valleys, a shepherd, thirty years in the body. Note the color—amber, unclouded. A simple soul, but honest. Honest souls burn longest."

A noble raised one webbed-fingered hand. Another countered. The bids climbed in graceful gestures, and the bottle on the lectern pressed its light against the glass faster, faster, like a heart in panic.

Beside him, Lior had gone very still. Stillness, in Lior Oyelaran, was not calm. Cassian had learned to read her stillnesses the way sailors read weather. This one was the stillness that preceded lightning.

"Don't," he breathed.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're doing everything. Breathe. Look at your feet. Be a pilgrim having a humble thought."

She looked at her feet. Her knuckles stayed white around the sword-hilt under the cloak.

Another lot. Another. A child's soul, sold by a father gambling away debts—"a bright little flame, gentlemen, rare purity"—and the room murmured appreciation, and a woman in a gown of fish-scale silk won it with a lazy lift of two fingers, smiling, *smiling*, and tucked the bottle into a carrying case lined with velvet as though it were a perfume.

"Gentle humor," Cassian muttered to himself, "is not going to survive this chapter of my life."

"What?"

"Nothing. Liturgical phrase."

And then the next lot came up, and Cassian stopped thinking about Lior entirely, because the priest at the lectern turned to receive the bottle from an attendant, and the light caught his face, and Cassian knew him.

High Cantor Aurel Voss. His mentor. The man who had taught him the opening litanies, who had held his head above water—literally, once, during his first immersion rite, when panic had seized him at thirteen and he'd swallowed half the baptismal pool. The man whose voice had been the most beautiful sound in Cassian's childhood, who had told him, hand warm on his shoulder, *the sea keeps what loves it, boy, and I love the sea.*

Eleven years since the disgrace. Eleven years, and Aurel Voss stood in the Shell Court selling souls by lamplight, and he had not aged a day.

"Aurel," Cassian whispered. The name escaped him before he could catch it, and Lior's head turned sharply.

"You know him."

"He was my teacher." The words tasted like seawater. "He was—Lior, he pulled me out of the pool when I was drowning. He carried me on his shoulders through the festival processions. He taught me that the Tide was mercy." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I keep waiting for the version of him I knew to step out from behind that face."

"People don't do that," Lior said. Not unkindly. Just factually, the way she said most things. "There's no version behind the face. There's just the face, and what it does."

On the dais, Aurel lifted another bottle to the lantern light, admiring it, and smiled—and it was the same smile. That was the worst of it. The same warm, crinkling smile that had once made a frightened novice feel chosen. Corruption wore beautiful faces, and some of those faces had once been kind to you, and there was no ritual in any canon for that particular wound.

"Lot fifty-eight," Aurel called, gentle as a lullaby. "A swordspeaker's discipline-soul. See how it burns white. See how it fights the glass. Oh, this one has *spirit*, friends. Who will give me spirit a home?"

The bottle writhed. The light inside it threw shapes against the wax seal—brief, furious, articulate shapes, almost like hands, almost like writing.

Lior made a sound beside him. Very small. Cassian had heard her make it exactly once before, on the trail, over the courier's body.

"We're getting it," she said.

"We are absolutely not getting it. We have no money, no standing, and no exit strategy beyond 'run.'"

"It's fighting the glass." Her eyes hadn't moved from the bottle. "Cassian, it's trying to *spell something*."

And it was. Now that she said it, Cassian could see it—the light pressing shapes into the glow, again and again, patient, desperate, a message hammered against the inside of the world. He squinted through the water-warped light, reading the way he'd been trained to read the Tide's currents, letter by luminous letter.

His blood went cold, colder than the Thornfell passes, colder than anything the drowned court could manufacture.

The soul in lot fifty-eight was spelling out a name. Over and over. Pressing it against the glass for anyone of the old blood to read.

The name was *Oyelaran*.

"Lior," Cassian said carefully, "how much do you know about your mother's dealings with this court?"

But she was already moving toward the bidding floor, hood back, eyes fixed on the bottle, and every beautiful face in the Shell Court was beginning, slowly, to turn and look at her.
