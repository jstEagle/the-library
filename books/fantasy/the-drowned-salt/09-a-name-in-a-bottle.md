## 9. A Name in a Bottle

The vault of souls did not look like a vault. It looked, Lior thought as she drifted through the doorway Cassian had sworn was unguarded at this hour, like her mother's pantry.

That was the horror of it, really. She had expected something grand and terrible—a cathedral of glass, a prison of iron and salt, chains and screaming. Instead there were shelves. Neat ones. Carved from pale coral-stone and arranged in long rows that receded into the green-black water until the lantern-light gave up and let them be dark. And on those shelves, thousands upon thousands of bottles, each one stoppered with wax the color of dried blood, each one glowing faintly, softly, the way a banked fire glows.

Souls. Bottled souls, filed away like preserves.

*Plum jam on the third shelf,* her mother used to say, tapping the jars in their kitchen above the pass. *Everything has its place, little bird. Everything kept safe is a kind of love.*

Lior's hand tightened on the hilt of her sword, though she had not drawn it. Drawing it would make noise. Noise would make corpses, and she was trying very hard, this week, to be a person who did not make corpses.

"You're staring," she breathed to Cassian, who hovered at the doorway behind her, dressed in the borrowed vestments of a minor tide-priest that made him look like a drowned heron.

"I'm praying," Cassian whispered back. "Badly. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"In my experience, no." He glided forward, robes whispering against stone even underwater—everything here whispered; the whole mountain seemed to have learned to speak in whispers—and held his lantern up to the nearest shelf. His face did something complicated. "By the Drowned Mother. I knew they took souls. I wrote my thesis on the theology of soul-harvesting. I argued it was metaphorical."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking at four thousand metaphors with wax stoppers."

Lior moved down the row. The bottles were small, no bigger than her fist, and each one held a curl of light that moved when she looked at it. Not light exactly. More like the way you could see wind if the wind passed through wheat. Some of them pressed against the glass as she walked by, the way dogs press against kennel bars. She made herself keep walking. If she stopped for every one of them, she would never leave this place, and neither would they.

She had come for one bottle. One name.

Cassian had found the ledger in the priests' scriptorium three days ago—a great salt-crusted tome listing acquisitions, dates, prices paid. Most entries were numbers. But one entry, eleven years old, had a name instead: *Oyelaran, L. Acquired by private arrangement. Vault location: Ninth Shelf, Third Alcove, Under the Sign of the Eel.*

Under the sign of the eel. The drowned court apparently organized its stolen souls with the same bureaucratic whimsy as any customs house, which somehow made it worse.

Third alcove. She counted as she went, past shelves where souls turned lazily toward her warmth like sunflowers toward sun, past an alcove marked with a carved leviathan, past one marked with a fish so ugly it could only have been carved by someone who hated it personally.

And then the eel.

The alcove beyond it was smaller than the others, tucked into a corner where two walls met, and the shelf there held only a handful of bottles. Kept apart. Special. Lior's heart was doing something embarrassing and arrhythmic, and she told it sternly to wait until she actually saw the thing before falling apart.

She lifted her lantern.

Row by row, she read the tags. Little slips of vellum, inked in a fine hand: names. *Maren of the Third Tide.* No—that couldn't be right, that Maren, surely. *Aldous Vane.* *Sera Ilves.* And then—

*Lior Oyelaran.*

Her own name looked back at her from a slip of yellowed vellum, tied to a bottle with a thread of silver wire, in handwriting she knew better than her own heartbeat. Her mother's handwriting. The same hand that had written *plum jam* on the pantry labels. The same hand that had signed the oaths binding Lior's words, one by one, year after year, until her voice was a locked room and her mother held all the keys.

The bottle itself was unremarkable. That was the cruelest part. It sat there among its neighbors, modest and dusty, holding a curl of pale gold light that didn't move the way the others moved. The other souls reached for her. This one didn't reach at all.

It just waited.

"Well," Cassian said softly, beside her. He had come without being called, which she was beginning to suspect was either devotion or a bad habit. "There it is."

"There it is," Lior agreed, and discovered her voice worked only barely.

"Do you want me to take it down?"

"No." The word came out sharper than she meant, frost-edged, and she saw him flinch—the tiny flinch he always tried to hide, the one that reminded her what her words did to the air. She gentled herself with effort. "No. Thank you. I need to—I should be the one."

"Of course." A pause. "For what it's worth, I've been thinking about what happens after. When you have it. There are rites for reclamation, old ones, pre-court ones. I remember fragments. We'd need—"

"Cassian."

"Yes?"

"Later." She reached out, and her fingers were steadier than she had any right to expect, and she closed them around the bottle.

It was warm.

Not body-warm. Hearth-warm. Kitchen-warm. The specific warmth of a room where someone has just baked bread and stepped out to fetch more wood, and the fire is still going, and you are alone in it but not lonely, because the room remembers who usually stands here.

Lior stood in the flooded dark of a vault beneath a drowned court, holding eleven years of theft in one hand, and felt homesick for a kitchen that had burned down when she was nineteen.

"Oh," she said. Just that. Just a small, stupid syllable that escaped before she could catch it.

"Lior?" Cassian's voice came from very far away.

Because the whisper had started.

*Little bird.*

Her mother's voice. Not remembered—remembered voices don't have texture, don't have the slight catch in them when they're tired, don't have the way she'd say *bird* with the second syllable half-swallowed, a habit from her own mother's dialect, from a village Lior had never seen. This was live. This was present. This was coming from inside the bottle, or through it, or from whatever place bottled things go when they think no one can hear.

*You came. I knew you'd come. You were always stubborn about doors.*

Lior's mouth opened. Nothing came out. Ten years of discipline, ten years of keeping her words behind her teeth where they couldn't cut anyone, and now she couldn't have produced a word if the Hollow Guard had been marching up the aisle.

*Don't cry. Salt water everywhere down here already, we'll flood the mountain.* A pause, and then, wry, warm, unbearably familiar: *Listen to me. Joking. Eleven years in a bottle and I still joke. Your mother's daughter.*

"You're not—" Lior managed, barely air. "My mother is alive. You're my soul. Souls don't talk."

*Don't they? What do I know. I'm a soul.* The light curled, almost a shrug. *Or I'm your mother. Or I'm both, which would explain why I can never find my spectacles. Or—*

The light dimmed, flickered, and when it brightened again the warmth in the voice had gone strange. Careful. Measured.

*Or I'm exactly what the tide-priests built me to be. A lure, little bird. Bait shaped like everything you miss.*

The cold that went through Lior then had nothing to do with the water.

"That's true," she whispered. "That could be true."

*Yes. It could. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. You were raised too carefully for that—your mother taught you never to trust a gift you didn't ask for.* Another pause, longer. *Did she? Did she teach you that? Tell me something only she would know. Test me. Please. I'd test me too.*

"The oath-words," Lior said. Her throat hurt. "When she bound my first word. What did she—"

*She cried afterward. In the privy, where she thought you couldn't hear. She thought binding you was saving you, and she hated herself for it every single day, and she did it anyway, because she loved you more than she loved being good.* The light pressed against the glass, urgent now. *She was wrong, little bird. About the binding. Maybe about all of it. But she was wrong the way people are wrong when they're terrified, and terror isn't an excuse, it's just a reason.*

Lior's eyes were burning, which was absurd, because she was submerged in water and tears should have been architecturally irrelevant, and yet.

"It knows things," Cassian murmured. He had retreated a respectful distance, but not far enough to be useless; she was learning that about him. "But Cassian—the priest I told you about, the one who trained me—he says the harvesters interrogate souls before bottling. Everything it knows, it might know because they made it tell."

*I know,* the voice said, gentle. *I heard him. I hear everything near my glass. Ask me the thing you haven't asked anyone. The question you've been carrying since the courier died in your arms.*

Lior went still.

"The map," she said. "Why me. Why does the route only work for an Oyelaran. Who arranged—"

*Ask the real question.*

"Why did she sell me." It came out flat and terrible. "Eleven years ago. Why did my mother sell my soul to these people."

The light in the bottle went very quiet. When the voice came again, it was small.

*Because I asked her to.*

"What?"

*Not tonight.* Urgent suddenly, sharp. *Not here. The brine, Lior—the singing brine on the wires, every bottle is strung with it, if you lift me off the hook without cutting the wire first it will sing, and everything in this mountain will know exactly where you're standing. Do you have a knife? Good. Small knife, under the wax, cut the silver wire, don't touch the—yes. Yes, like that. You always were quick with your hands when your mouth was busy.*

Lior drew her belt knife. Her hands, notably, were not steady anymore. The silver wire was finer than hair and cold as regret, and it ran from the bottle's neck through a tiny ring set into the shelf, and somewhere along its length—it was hard to see in the lantern light—there would be a filament of singing brine, the alarm-stuff the court grew in vats, the stuff that harmonized when disturbed and carried the song through every wire in the vault.

*Slowly,* her mother's voice murmured. Or the lure's voice. Or the thing wearing both their shapes. *Steady. There's no hurry. There's always no hurry right up until there's—*

The knife slipped.

It slipped because the bottle chose that exact moment to flare—bright, sudden, gold-white, whether in fear or eagerness or some third thing Lior had no name for—and the light blinded her for half a heartbeat, and half a heartbeat was enough. The blade kissed the wire a fraction too deep, and the wire sang.

Not broke. Sang.

One clear note, high and sweet and appallingly loud, a note like a wineglass struck in a cathedral, and then the note caught, spread, leapt along the silver threads strung shelf to shelf, bottle to bottle, and the whole vault began to sing. Four thousand souls shivered in their glass, and the brine filaments caught their shivering and turned it into harmony, rising, layering, a chord built from a thousand stolen voices swelling up through the coral shelves and out into the flooded halls of the drowned court.

Every soul in the room was now, functionally, an alarm bell.

"Oh," said Cassian, with the calm of a man whose day has finally matched his expectations. "Oh dear."

*Run,* said the voice in the bottle—her mother's voice, or the lure's, and Lior no longer had time to care which. *Run, little bird, run NOW—*

Lior stuffed the bottle into the inner pocket of her coat, against her chest, where it beat warm as a second heart, and ran.

They went up the aisle between the shelves with the chord howling around them, and behind them the vault door—which had stood open and indifferent—began, with the unhurried malice of all institutional architecture, to close.

"The door!" Cassian shouted, robes streaming. "The door doesn't—"

"I see it!"

Lior did the arithmetic in three strides: distance, closing speed, the drag of water on a man in heavy vestments who had spent thirty years indoors. She could make it. He couldn't. She could leave him, and the arithmetic would be clean, and she would spend the rest of her life doing sums with that number in them.

She spun, grabbed his collar, and threw him.

He was lighter than he looked, or fear was generous, or perhaps swordspeech had leaked into her muscles over the years the way dye leaks into cloth—either way, Cassian flew through the gap with a squawk of pure undignified terror, and Lior dove after him, shoulder-first, the closing door kissing her boot heel with a boom that she felt in her teeth.

Then silence. Then, muffled through the stone, still singing: the vault, four thousand strong, telling the entire drowned court exactly where they had been.

Cassian lay on the wet flagstones, wheezing, vestments askew, looking up at her with an expression that was equal parts gratitude and grievance. "You threw me."

"You're welcome."

"I was going to dive."

"You were going to hesitate."

"...I was going to hesitate," he admitted. "Thank you."

Against her ribs, the bottle pulsed. Warm. Patient. And beneath the fading alarm-song, so soft she might have imagined it, her mother's voice—or the lure's—humming a lullaby she hadn't heard since she was six years old, from a kitchen that no longer existed, in a house that had burned, on a road she could never walk again.

Somewhere above them, boots were hitting stairs. Many boots. Moving fast.

"Cassian," Lior said quietly. "How much do you remember about those corridors?"

"Not enough," he said, scrambling up. "But I remember the direction the guards *don't* come from, and I suggest we become extremely interested in it."

They ran.
