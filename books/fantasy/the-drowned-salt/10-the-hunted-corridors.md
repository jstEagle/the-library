## 10. The Hunted Corridors

### Lior

The singing brine followed her like a debt collector.

Lior ran through the flooded gallery with the bottle clutched against her ribs, and behind her the alarm went on and on—a high, sweet ringing that came out of the walls themselves, out of the water, out of the coral grown through the drowned masonry. It wasn't a bell. Bells could be silenced. This was the mountain *singing about her*, telling everyone where she was, and it had a voice like a choir of disappointed aunts.

"Left," she hissed at herself, and took the left fork, because the right was chest-deep and she'd learned in the first hundred paces that deep water made her splash loud enough to be heard in the next county.

The gallery opened into what had once been a ballroom, or a temple, or both—drowned courts never saw much difference. Pillars rose out of black water, furred with pale growth that glowed faintly when her lantern passed, like the room was blushing at having guests. The ceiling had collapsed somewhere above; a shaft of mountain cold came down through it, and snowflakes drifted into the warm air and died midair, turning to rain.

She stopped in the shallows, breathing hard, and did the arithmetic she hated.

The way they'd come in—the pilgrim's stair, the ninth door—was now the worst possible direction. The Guard would seal it first. They'd sealed everything else; she'd heard the doors booming shut behind them since the vault, one after another, patient as a man closing a house for winter. Whoever ran this court didn't chase. They *herded*.

Which meant the smart move was to find another way up.

Which meant leaving Cassian.

He'd shoved her through the side passage himself—"Go, I'll misdirect them, I'm very good at being disappointing"—and she'd gone, because going was the only thing she knew how to do, and now the arithmetic sat in her chest like swallowed stone. Cassian, who had drowned twice and come back both times out of sheer stubbornness. Cassian, who talked too much precisely so no one would notice he meant every word.

*He knew the risk,* said the practical voice, the smuggler's voice, the one that kept her alive.

*You left him,* said the other voice, which sounded annoyingly like Vess, and which she usually managed to drown out with work.

The bottle whispered against her ribs. Not words. Not quite. A warmth, a pressure, like someone leaning their forehead against her shoulder blade from inside the glass. Her own soul, if it was hers. Her mother's voice, if it wasn't. Either way it wanted her to keep moving, and she was beginning to suspect its advice and the court's advice were suspiciously aligned.

"No," she told it quietly. "We're not doing what you want either."

Somewhere behind, through two galleries and a collapsed colonnade, she heard the sound she'd been dreading: not the Guard's boots—they barely made any, which was somehow worse—but water displacing. Something large moving through the flooded halls without hurry. The wardens. She'd glimpsed one in the vault's antechamber: a shape like a diver's suit that had forgotten to include a person, filled instead with dark water and old intention.

Lior looked up at the shaft of falling snow. Cold air meant surface. Surface meant—

A door boomed shut somewhere above her, far away, and dust sifted down through the snow-shaft.

Surface meant closed.

Her fury arrived all at once, clean and total, the way it always did, and she let it have her for exactly three breaths before folding it up and putting it away, because fury was fuel and fuel was for burning in one direction only: forward. Deeper. Through whatever lay below this ballroom, down toward the heart of the court, toward Maren and the rite and the thing the tide-priests were feeding. If every road up was closing, then she'd stop trying to leave. She'd go down and break something important enough that they'd have to open the doors themselves.

It was a terrible plan. Vess would have hated it. That was how she knew it was hers.

She waded toward the far archway, where the water ran deeper and the glow-growth thinned, and just before the dark took her she heard, distantly, unmistakably, Cassian's voice—raised, theatrical, carrying the way it did when he wanted to be heard by everyone except the person he was actually talking to:

"—I'm telling you, the swordspeaker went *east*, toward the old cisterns, I saw her myself, why would I lie, I'm a defrocked priest, lying is the only sacrament I have left—"

Her heart cracked neatly down the middle, and she ran.

### Cassian

The trouble with lying professionally for twenty years, Cassian reflected, was that eventually you lied to professionals.

The three wardens herding him along the Gallery of Tithes were not fooled for a moment. He could tell because they weren't listening. Hollow Guard listened—that was their whole horror, the way they leaned toward your words like plants toward light. Wardens simply waited. They were the court's patience given legs, and patience, Cassian knew from long experience, was the one audience no performance could hold.

So he talked anyway. It was who he was. A man should die as he lived, and he intended to live considerably longer, but the principle stood.

"—and furthermore," he said, backing along the gallery with his hands raised, water sloshing at his knees, "if you examine the theology of the situation—and I know, I know, nobody examines theology anymore, it's all tides this and Long Tide that, nobody reads the *commentaries*—you'll find that harboring a fugitive is explicitly forgiven under the Rite of Mercy, third recension, provided the fugitive is pursued with sufficient vigor by others. I am pursuing her. Vigorously. In my heart. Ask anyone."

The nearest warden raised an arm that ended in something between a hand and a trident's bad dream.

"Yes, well." Cassian sighed. "Worth a try."

Behind him, the gallery ended at a junction he remembered from his first life, forty years and one drowning ago: three passages, one dry-ish, one flooded to the ceiling, and one—the middle one—leading down toward the Vault of Names, the way Lior had gone. The way he needed them to believe she *hadn't* gone.

He put himself squarely in front of the middle passage, spread his arms like a particularly unimpressive gate, and gave them the smile he'd once used on congregations of four hundred.

"Gentlemen. Ladies. Whatever you are under there. I'll tell you what I told my ordination board: I am extremely difficult to move."

They moved him.

That was unfair, really—he got one good moment, one genuine flash of the old training, catching the first warden's strike on his forearm and driving his palm into the seam of its helm where a face would be if the court hadn't economized. The thing staggered, water slopping from its collar, and for one absurd heartbeat Cassian thought: *I still have it.*

Then the second warden hit him like a wave hitting a sandcastle built by an optimist, and the floor came up kindlier than expected because there was a foot of warm seawater on it, and the third warden knelt beside him with terrible gentleness and began, in a voice like water finding cracks, to ask questions.

Cassian, cheek against submerged stone, tasting salt and blood, thought about Lior running east—or west, or wherever he'd sent her, anywhere but here—and felt something he hadn't felt since his second drowning, which had been less literal than the first.

Pride. In someone else. It was disorienting. He resolved to complain about it at length, later, to her face, assuming there was a later and a face.

"The route," the warden said. "Where does she go."

"You know," Cassian said, spitting water, "in the old days we'd at least share a meal first."

The warden's hand closed on his collar, and the dark water inside its helm turned, slowly, like something considering.

And Cassian, defrocked, disgraced, twice-drowned, liar, coward by his own careful accounting, looked up into that empty glass and told the truth for possibly the fourth time in his life:

"She goes where you can't follow. Down. All the way down. And when she gets there, everything your pretty court is built on is going to hear one true word, and I would pay money—I would pay *considerable* money—to watch."

The warden tilted its head. Somewhere above, the singing brine changed key.

Then the dark water reached out of the helm and took him, and the last thing Cassian thought, with the gallery lights smearing overhead like candles seen from underwater, was:

*I hope she's angrier than she is guilty. Guilt makes people run home. Anger makes them run forward.*

*Be angry, child. Be furious. I'll be furious with you.*

### Lior

She heard the singing change.

She'd been moving through the deep galleries for what felt like hours—down worn stairs, across bridges over lightless water, past niches where bottled souls hung like lanterns and watched her pass with small colored fires—and she'd learned the alarm's grammar without wanting to. One note, sustained: intruder in the halls. Two notes, alternating: intruder near something valuable. But this new sound was different. Lower. Almost gentle. Three notes, descending, repeated, spreading outward through the water like a rumor.

Then, faint and far behind her, cut off.

Lior stood on the bridge with her fist pressed to her mouth and understood, in the wordless way she understood rockfall and river ice, exactly what she'd heard.

They'd caught him.

The bottle pulsed against her ribs—*go, go, go*—and for the first time she agreed with it, though not for its reasons. Every instinct she'd spent ten years sharpening said *run, survive, mourn later*, the same instincts that had carried her from her mother's house at fifteen and kept her alive through a decade of passes and betrayals. Run. Go deeper. Find the rite. Break it.

But underneath the instinct, something older and less sensible had woken, and it sounded like a man saying *lying is the only sacrament I have left*, and it was not going to let her spend his capture cheaply.

"They don't kill him," she said aloud, to the bottle, to the dark, to whoever was listening. "Not yet. They want me more than they want him dead. Which means he's leverage, and leverage means he's *alive*, and alive means there's time."

The bottle's whisper shifted—warmer, almost approving—and she hated that it felt like her mother's approval, and hated more that some starved corner of her leaned into it like a plant toward light.

"Not yet," she told it. "When I know whose voice you really are. Then maybe."

She ran on, down, following the pull of the deep places, and the galleries around her grew older and stranger. The masonry changed from dressed stone to something fitted and vast, blocks the size of carts, carved with script she almost recognized—swordspeech's grandmother, maybe, or its drowned ancestor. The water rose past her knees, then her waist, warm as blood, and the glow-growth brightened until she hardly needed the lantern, and everywhere the three descending notes echoed, softer now, almost a lullaby.

Almost an invitation.

At the bottom of the last stair, the corridor opened into a hall so large her lantern couldn't find its ceiling, and across the far darkness, doors of pearl and iron stood half-open around a dais where figures moved in procession—priests, robed in kelp-white, preparing something. Preparing *him*, maybe. Preparing a rite. Preparing the end of everything, depending on whom you asked, and the only person she trusted to ask was currently a prisoner.

Lior Oyelaran stood in the warm black water at the edge of the drowned court's heart, soaked, hunted, alone, holding a stolen soul and a stolen map and nothing else worth the name.

"Right," she said, and drew her sword, and the glow-growth flared at the sound of steel like the whole hall was drawing breath. "Let's go make some noise."

From the shadows of the dais, unhurried, a woman's voice answered—kind, resonant, terribly certain, the voice of someone who had already won and was merely waiting for the guest of honor:

"There she is. Come in, child. Your mother and I have been waiting such a long time to talk."
