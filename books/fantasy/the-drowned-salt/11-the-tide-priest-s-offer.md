## 11. The Tide-Priest's Offer

The singing brine had stopped singing, which was worse than when it had been singing.

Lior crouched in the dark of a side gallery where the water came up to her shins and the walls wept salt in long white curtains, like a room that had been crying for three hundred years and saw no reason to stop now. Behind her, the vault of souls. Ahead of her, according to the map pressed against her chest beneath her coat, a service stair that climbed toward the pilgrim galleries and, eventually, air.

In her satchel, wrapped in her spare shirt, the bottle whispered.

Not constantly. That was the thing she hadn't expected—the mercy of it. It whispered in fits and starts, the way a fire crackles, and always in her mother's voice, and never anything useful. *Lior.* Just that, mostly. Her name, over and over, the way her mother had said it across a supper table, the way she'd said it the morning she'd braided Lior's hair for the oath-binding, fingers steady, face doing whatever a face does when it is about to sell its child's soul to a drowned court.

*Lior.*

"Stop it," Lior muttered at the satchel. "You don't get to say my name. You lost naming rights."

The bottle said nothing, which felt like agreement, which made her angrier.

She gave herself ten more breaths in the weeping gallery. Ten breaths was smuggler's discipline: enough to let your heart come down off the ceiling, not enough to let it convince you the ceiling was safe. On breath nine she heard footsteps.

On breath ten she understood why the brine had gone quiet.

* * *

High Priestess Maren did not walk through flooded halls so much as arrive in them, the way weather arrives. One moment the gallery held only Lior and the drip of salt; the next there was a woman standing on the water itself—not floating, *standing*, the surface dimpled under her bare feet as though it were sand—and six tide-priests fanned out behind her in a crescent that was either ceremonial or very politely a trap, and knowing courts, it was both.

Lior's hand went to her sword. Her sword, notably, was words, and her words were currently hoarse from the ford, frost-scars still stinging on her tongue like burns from a too-hot spoon. She could probably kill two of the six before they took her. She did the arithmetic anyway, because fear likes arithmetic, and then she stopped, because Maren raised one hand and every priest went still as furniture.

"You're bleeding," Maren said.

"I'm what?"

"Your tongue." Maren's voice was low and warm, the voice of someone who has talked a great many frightened people down from a great many ledges. "From the cutting-words at the ford. Three riders. I'm told the third one took a full minute to die, and spent it apologizing." She tilted her head. "That's an Oyelaran word, that kind. The old ones. I've read about them."

"They're in books," Lior said. "I'm told that's where most people keep them."

Something moved at the corner of Maren's mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile's well-dressed cousin. "And yet here you are, using them on my Guard, in my mountain, carrying—" she let the pause stretch, delicate as a hairline crack, "—a bottle with your name on it."

The water in the gallery seemed to lean closer. Lior kept her hand on her hilt and her weight even and said nothing, because the first rule of smuggling was that silence cost nothing and every sentence had a price tag.

"You ran past four exits to get here," Maren continued. She began to walk, slowly, along the water's surface, parallel to the gallery, unhurried as a woman strolling a garden. "Do you know how I know you're not a thief? Thieves run *out*. You ran *down*. Deeper. Toward the vault, toward the rite, toward everything heavy and old." She stopped. "You came home, Lior Oyelaran. Badly disguised as an invasion."

"This isn't home."

"No?" Maren spread her hands, and the gallery lit—gently, without drama—as though someone had turned up the sun through forty feet of stone and sea. And Lior saw where she stood for the first time: a corridor of white coral grown through black basalt, arches carved with oath-script, and along the walls, set in niches like candles, bottles. Hundreds. Thousands. Each glowing faintly, each murmuring, a congregation of whispers, and the sound of it was almost tender, was almost a lullaby, was the worst sound Lior had ever heard.

"We keep them safe," Maren said softly. "That's what no one above ever understands. Every soul in this room was sold, abandoned, or sentenced to death up there. We give them a shelf and a song. We are the only ones who ever called them *precious*."

"And the Long Tide eats them."

"The Long Tide *holds* them." For just a moment the warmth thinned and Lior saw the thing underneath: something vast and patient and cold as the bottom of the sea. Then it smoothed over again, and Maren was warm once more, and somehow that was worse. "But you didn't come all this way for theology. You came for answers. So let me offer you one, free of charge, priestess to thief: ask your bottle who bound your oaths."

"My mother did."

"Yes. And who taught your mother the binding?"

Lior's mouth opened. Closed. The frost-scars on her tongue prickled.

"Ask it," Maren said gently. "It will tell you. It remembers everything. It's *you*, after all—or the part of you that was never allowed to be. Ten years you've been half a woman walking around the mountains, Lior, flinching from your own gift, hating yourself for every word. Do you know why the oaths hurt? Because they're seams. Someone stitched you shut and left the thread in." She drifted a step closer, bare feet never disturbing the water. "We can take the stitches out."

"I'll pass."

"You haven't heard the offer."

"I've heard the sales pitch. The offer's always the same underneath. Give me the bottle."

Maren laughed—a real laugh, brief and startled out of her, and for one absurd moment Lior thought, *oh no, I like her,* which was exactly the sort of trap this whole mountain was built out of. "Direct. Your mother was direct too, before the court taught her manners." She folded her hands. "Very well. Here is the bargain, plainly, because I find plainness disarms people better than threats ever did."

She gestured, and one of the priests behind her stepped forward bearing a second bottle.

This one was larger. Older. The glass was green-black, fogged with age, and inside it something moved—not light, not liquid, but a shape, a posture, a way of holding shoulders that Lior knew better than her own heartbeat. Even before the glow steadied, even before the figure inside turned its head, Lior knew.

Her mother looked out of the bottle at her.

Alive. Not whispering, not a memory, not a ghost in a stolen voice. *Alive*, suspended in green glass, eyes open, mouth moving—saying something Lior couldn't hear, hands pressed flat against the inside of the glass, and the gesture was unmistakable, was the oldest one Lior knew:

*Go back. Go away. Don't.*

"She's been with us eleven years," Maren said quietly. "Since the day she brought us your soul and asked, in exchange, not for gold but for sanctuary. She's been in the deep halls ever since. Fed, sheltered, cared for. The drowned court honors its debts, whatever else you believe about us." She watched Lior's face with the attention of a surgeon. "Surrender the bottle in your satchel. Walk out of this mountain with your friend the defrocked priest—we know he's alive; my wardens are thorough, not cruel—and your mother walks out with you. All three of you. Above the snowline by dawn. I will personally see to it, and the Hollow Guard will forget your faces."

The gallery dripped. Somewhere far below, something enormous shifted in its sleep, and the water trembled against Lior's knees.

"That's it," Lior said. "No catch."

"One small catch." Maren's smile returned, apologetic. "The Rite of the Long Tide begins tonight. Earlier than planned—you've inconvenienced us wonderfully—but it proceeds regardless. If you surrender the soul willingly, it goes into the Tide as a *gift*, freely given, and gifts bind gentler than thefts. The mountain wakes fed instead of starving. No flood. No drowning of the pilgrim towns on the coast. No winter that doesn't stop." She shrugged, elegant, reasonable. "Or refuse, keep running, and we take it from you in the rite chamber anyway, and the taking is ugly, and the waking is hungry, and everyone downstream learns what the Long Tide's appetite costs. Same ending, Lior. Only the amount of blood differs. I'm offering you the version with less."

*Same ending.* Lior turned the phrase over and found the flaw in it the way her fingers found a bad coin—by feel, by years of practice. "If the ending's the same either way, why bargain? Why show me my mother? Why stand on the water making speeches?" She lifted her chin. "Because it's *not* the same. Because a freely given soul binds different than a taken one. You need me to hand it over. Which means I have something you can't take."

For the first time, Maren went still.

It lasted half a breath. But Lior had spent a decade reading customs officers and caravan bosses and men with knives, and she knew a flinch when she saw one, even on a woman who could stand on water.

"Cassian," Lior said. "Where is he?"

"Safe."

"That's a priestess answer. Where. Is. He."

"In the cells beneath the choir hall, being questioned by someone he used to love. Which is its own kind of mercy, if you think about it." Maren resumed her slow drift along the water, composure restored, but Lior had seen the seam now and would not unsee it. "Your friend is not part of the bargain. He walks free either way. I keep my terms clean; it's why people trust me."

"People trust you because you drown the ones who don't."

"People trust me," Maren said, "because unlike every other power they've ever met, I tell them exactly what I want and exactly what it costs. Consider it. Truly consider it." She nodded toward the large bottle, and inside it her mother pressed harder against the glass, mouth working, and Lior caught—lips alone, no sound—one word repeated over and over.

*Run. Run. Run.*

Eleven years in a bottle and the first thing she spends them saying is *run*. Not *save me*. Not *take the deal*. Lior felt something crack in her chest, some wall she'd built at fourteen and mortared with a decade of resentment, and hated it, and hated her, and wanted, with a ferocity that scared her, to hear her mother's actual voice just once more—even angry, even lying, even selling her out again.

"There's a condition of my own," Lior said.

Maren arched a brow. "Thieves negotiate. How refreshing."

"I give you the bottle at the rite. In person. Before the Tide. Not before—I know what happens to couriers who hand over the goods early. At the rite, in front of your whole court, I put it in your hands myself. And my mother comes out of hers *then*, and stands next to me, and the three of us walk out together while your priests watch. You get your gift given publicly, your gentle binding, your less-blood ending. I get proof of life until the last moment." She shrugged. "Smuggler's terms. Nobody pays before delivery."

Maren considered her for a long moment. The water lapped. The thousand souls in their niches murmured their lullaby, and Liro realized with a lurch that some of those voices were children's.

"You drive a hard bargain for someone ankle-deep in my mountain," Maren said at last. "Done. The rite chamber, moonrise. Come armed if it makes you feel brave; my wardens will be armed too, and better dressed." She turned, then paused, and glanced back over her shoulder with something that was almost kindness, which was the most frightening expression Lior had seen all night. "One kindness, freely given, since you value the currency: when you speak with your mother after—all three of you, walking out together, as promised—ask her what she was running from when she sold you. Ask her what the prophecy actually said. Not the version she told herself. The real one." A beat. "And then decide whose mercy you'd rather have. Mine costs you a bottle. Hers cost you everything."

The water swallowed her. Not dramatically—she simply walked into the dark of the gallery and became part of it, and the six priests followed, and the great green bottle with its living cargo went with them, and her mother's lips were still moving, still saying *run*, until the glow faded and the corridor was only coral and salt and the small sounds of a mountain breathing underwater.

Lior stood alone in the flooded hall for a long time.

Then she laughed. It came out cracked and slightly hysterical and echoed off the arches, and she clapped a hand over her mouth because laughing in a soul-vault seemed disrespectful to the tenants.

"Oh, that's good," she whispered. "That's really good. She's got me. She's actually got me."

Because here was the arithmetic fear wouldn't do, the sum she'd been avoiding since the green bottle appeared: Maren had shown her mother alive. Shown her *proof*. And either it was true—in which case Lior had just agreed to hand over the only piece of herself that mattered, to a tide-priestess, on promises—and the mountain was full of promises the way the sea was full of salt—

—or it was false, and the woman in the bottle was a forgery, a lure, a puppet of brine and memory, in which case Maren had miscalculated badly, because Lior had spent the entire conversation watching her mother's hands.

And her mother's hands had been wrong.

Small things. The scar on the right thumb—burned, when Lior was six, from a kettle—absent. The way her little finger curled inward, an old break badly set. Straight. And the pressing gesture, palms flat against the glass: her mother had never once pushed people away in her life. She pulled. She gathered. She held on with both hands like the world was a door slamming.

Whoever was in that bottle knew Lior's grief. They didn't know her mother.

Which meant the deal was a lie, which meant the rite chamber at moonrise was a trap, which meant Cassian was in the cells being interrogated by someone he used to love, which meant—

Lior wiped her face with a wet sleeve, checked the satchel, and turned down the dark corridor toward the choir hall, moving fast, water slopping around her knees, talking to herself because there was no one else to talk to.

"Right," she told the dripping arches. "New plan. Same plan, but louder."

Far below, something older than winter rolled over in its sleep, and the mountain groaned like a ship changing course, and the first notes of the rite began to rise through the water—low, sweet, and terribly patient.

Moonrise was three hours away.

Lior intended to be extremely rude about the schedule.
