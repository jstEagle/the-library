## 12. Prisoner of Salt

The cell was dry, which Cassian found almost insulting.

He had spent the better part of two days in the drowned halls of Mount Serein, breathing water that tasted of old prayers, watching nobles drift past on currents of pale light like moths who had decided the moon was a buffet. He had expected, when the wardens finally took him, to be thrown into something properly aquatic. A kelp-choked dungeon. A cell where the walls wept. Something with atmosphere.

Instead they had put him in a small stone room above the waterline, with a bench, a bucket, and a single lamp that burned a slow blue flame and gave off no heat at all. The air was cold and clean and smelled faintly of nothing. It was, he realized after an hour of sitting with his back against the wall, the most comfortable prison he had ever occupied.

That was the point, of course. Comfort was a tool. He'd used it himself often enough, back when he'd had a rank and a robe and the authority to make people comfortable right up until they told him everything he knew.

"They're softening you," he said aloud to the empty room. "Don't fall for it."

The room did not answer. Rooms rarely did, though in this mountain he was trying not to rule anything out.

His wrists still bore the marks of the salt-cuffs—thin white lines like frostburn, itching maddeningly. They had taken his coat, his knife, his prayer-beads, and the little tin of dried figs he'd been carrying since the ford, which hurt more than he cared to admit. He had eaten one fig every evening since Vess's tavern as a private ritual of hope. Now some warden was probably eating his figs. He hoped they choked. Politely. He was, whatever else had changed about him, still a priest at the root, and even his curses came out with table manners.

* * *

They came for him on what he judged to be the second day, though time below the waterline moved strangely, pooling in the corners of hours.

The door opened without a sound, and High Priestess Maren's voice arrived before she did, drifting down the corridor like incense smoke: "Still awake, Cassian? You always were a poor sleeper."

Cassian stood, because there were habits deeper than disgrace, and because his knees were stiff and standing hid it.

She filled the doorway the way tide fills a harbor—not rushing, simply arriving, inevitable. She looked exactly as she had eleven years ago, when she had laid her hand on his head and pronounced him cast out: silver-streaked hair coiled like a wave about to break, robes the grey-green of deep water, eyes the color of a sea before weather. Not one day older. That was the thing about the court beneath the mountain. Corruption kept beautifully.

"Maren." He inclined his head, neither bow nor refusal. "You haven't aged."

"The water is generous." She glided in and settled onto the bench as though it were a throne, arranging her robes with the unconscious precision of a woman who had never once sat down carelessly. "You look terrible."

"I've been having a very educational week."

"Have you?" She smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the seminary years, warm at the edges, and he hated how much of him leaned toward it like a plant toward a window. "Sit down, Cassian. This isn't an interrogation. Interrogations are for strangers. This is a conversation between colleagues."

"We were never colleagues. You were my teacher."

"And I remain so. Sit."

He sat. On the floor, deliberately, because if this was going to be a conversation between colleagues he preferred it to be an absurd one.

Maren sighed with theatrical patience. "Eleven years," she said. "Eleven years since I cut your stole in half and put you out, and do you know what I have regretted? Nothing. Because here you come swimming home anyway, through the ninth door, in the company of an Oyelaran swordspeaker, carrying yourself straight into my cells as though the tide itself delivered you. The water is generous," she repeated softly, "and it has a sense of humor."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know you were defrocked for selling the route of the Winter Pilgrimage to salt-smugglers. I know three pilgrims drowned when the smugglers' greed collapsed the ice road. And I know that a man who has sold one secret will sell another, given the right price." She folded her hands. "So let us discuss price."

The words landed where she meant them to, in the soft place under his ribs where the guilt lived. He kept his face still. He had learned stillness in these halls, once, from her.

"What secret could I possibly sell you now?" he asked. "I've been in your cells for two days. My whole route is *here*."

"Not your route. *Her* route." Maren leaned forward, and the lamplight moved in her eyes like something alive. "Lior Oyelaran. The last swordspeaker. Do you understand what walked into my mountain wearing a smuggler's coat? There is a bottle in my vault with her name on it, and a soul inside it older than her grief, and a rite coming that requires both. And you, Cassian—you found her, you guided her, you know how she thinks, where she'll run, what she loves, what she fears. The Guard caught you buying her time at the singing brine. Generous of you. Foolish, but generous." Her voice gentled. "Tell me how she'll move through the lower galleries, and everything you lost comes back to you. Rank. Robe. Name. The water remembers its priests, Cassian. It would take you back like a river takes a fish."

* * *

He made himself think about figs.

It was an old discipline, one of the first Maren herself had taught him, back when he was nineteen and all elbows and hunger: *when temptation speaks, count something small and true.* Figs. The tin in his pocket. One each evening. The way Vess had rolled her eyes at it—"you ration joy, priest, that's the saddest thing I've ever seen"—and then quietly started stocking them behind the bar, telling him they'd over-ordered.

Vess would be furious with him for getting captured. She'd say something dry and devastating about how he couldn't even drown properly in a drowning mountain. And then she'd start planning how to get him out, because that was what Vess did with fury; she converted it to logistics.

And Lior—

Lior had watched him get taken and had gone *deeper*. He'd seen it in her face in the half-second before the wardens closed around him: not relief, not calculation. Guilt. She was going to blame herself, and guilt made Lior Oyelaran predictable in exactly one direction—forward, toward danger, alone. If Maren wanted to set a trap, she needed only to describe one, and Lior would walk into it out of sheer remorse.

Which meant the most valuable thing Cassian possessed wasn't knowledge of her route. It was the ability to keep his mouth shut while knowing exactly how she'd break herself trying to rescue him.

"Cassian." Maren's voice had gone softer, which was worse. "I watched you pray for six hours once, over a stranger's body pulled from the shallows, hoping for a miracle. You believed, then. You believed in the Tide, in the court, in me. What happened?"

"You happened," he said. "I saw what the tithes bought. I saw the vaults. I saw souls in bottles being sold to nobles who'd already forgotten their own names, and I asked questions, and you didn't answer them, and then the pilgrimage broke and I answered my own question the worst possible way." He looked up at her. "I sold a secret, Maren. One. And three people died of it. I've had eleven years to learn the exchange rate. Your offer is not enough."

"It is more than enough. It is *everything you were*."

"Yes," he said quietly. "That's the problem. I've met the man I was. We're not on speaking terms."

* * *

Something flickered across Maren's face then—fast, quickly mastered, but he had spent his youth learning to read her, and he caught it. Not anger. Something wearier. Almost sadness.

"You think I don't know what the vaults are," she said. "You think I sleep well among the bottles."

"I think you stopped sleeping decades ago and called it devotion."

A laugh escaped her, brief and genuine and horrible. "Oh, you *were* my best student." She rose, smoothing her robes, and the warmth drained out of her by degrees until only the office remained. "Then hear the rest, colleague. The Rite of the Long Tide comes early—the winter above is sealing the passes, and the Deep One stirs whether we are ready or not. When it wakes, it will need feeding, and the feeding will need a vessel, and the vessel has a name written in her mother's hand on a map you helped carry. You can refuse me. Many do. But refusal only changes *your* part in what comes. The rite happens either way." She paused at the door. "There is a version of the next ten days where you walk out of here restored, and a version where you drown slowly in a cell that is currently quite comfortable. Both versions end with the Long Tide rising. Choose which man you want to be when it does."

The door closed without a sound.

Cassian sat in the blue lamplight for a long time.

Then he laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, because she had made one mistake, his teacher, his brilliant, terrible mentor. She had told him the truth. The rite was early. The passes were sealing. Which meant the clock above and the clock below were running together, and somewhere in the flooded galleries Lior Oyelaran was already moving, alone, armed with nothing but words, walking straight toward a trap Maren had probably described to him in detail just now so that his silence would feel like consent.

He pressed his palms flat against the cold stone floor—the old posture, the first one every tide-priest learns—and did the only thing left to a defrocked man in a dry cell.

He prayed. Not to the Long Tide. Not to the court. To whatever listened underneath all of it, older than oaths, older than lies.

*Keep her stubborn,* he prayed. *Keep her angry. And if she comes for me—*

The lamp guttered. Somewhere far below, through fathoms of stone and black water, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the cell wall hummed against his hands like a struck bell.

*—then at least let me be worth rescuing.*

Outside the door, boots were approaching again. More than one pair. Moving fast.

And beneath the sound of the boots, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the mountain's bones: singing. Brine-singing. An alarm.

Someone had touched the vault.
