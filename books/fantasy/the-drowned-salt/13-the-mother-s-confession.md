## 13. The Mother's Confession

The bottle sat in Lior's hands like a sleeping bird—warm, impossibly warm, considering everything else down here was cold enough to ache in the teeth.

She had carried it through three flooded galleries and one corridor that sang when you stepped wrong, and the whole time it had whispered to her in her mother's voice. Not words, exactly. More like the memory of words. The particular cadence of *Lior, come inside, your hands are ice* from a kitchen that had burned down before Lior turned seven.

Now Maren stood across the brine-pool from her, hands folded, patient as tide. The High Priestess of the drowned court did not look like a monster. She looked like someone's grandmother who happened to own the ocean.

"You have my price," Lior said. "I have yours. Let's not make a ceremony of it."

"Everything here is a ceremony," Maren said pleasantly. "That is rather the point of us." She gestured, and two wardens drifted forward—not swimming so much as *arriving*, the way water arrives everywhere at once. "Set it on the stone, child. Gently. Souls bruise."

"Do they."

"They do. Yours more than most." Maren's smile was kind in the way that made Lior want to put her back against a wall. "You've been carrying it against your heart. Sentimental. It will have picked up your habits."

Lior set the bottle down on the wet stone between them. The whispering stopped—or rather, it changed, became something like a held breath. She felt the absence immediately, the way you feel a tooth that's finally been pulled.

"The meeting," she said. "My mother. Alive. You said."

"I said, and I keep my word. It's the only currency this court has never debased." Maren tilted her head. "Though I warn you, Oyelaran. Mothers are not what we remember them to be. They are what they had to become."

"I've had ten years to adjust my expectations."

"No," Maren said softly. "You haven't. That's precisely why I'm letting you do this."

* * *

They took her down.

Down past the auction hall where bottled souls glowed like lanterns at a festival nobody should ever hold. Down past the throne room, where she did not look, because looking would mean thinking about chapter nineteen of her life, and she wasn't there yet. Down to a door of black coral that opened onto a small round chamber lit by nothing at all—the light simply was, sourceless and old, the way light is in dreams.

Her mother sat on a bench of white stone, and she was smaller than Lior remembered.

That was the first cruelty of it. Not the chains of pale salt crusted around her wrists, not the gray in hair that had been river-black. The smallness. In Lior's memory, Sefeya Oyelaran filled every room she stood in—a woman whose oaths could bend a border dispute, whose silences could end friendships. This woman looked like she'd been folded up and stored.

"Lior." Her mother's voice cracked on the second syllable, which was somehow worse than if she'd wept outright. "You got tall."

"That's what you're opening with."

"It seemed safer than the alternatives." A ghost of the old dry humor surfaced and sank again. "There were so many alternatives. I drafted several speeches over the years. In my head. There wasn't much paper down here."

Lior stayed by the door. Ten years of smuggler's instinct said never stand within arm's reach of anyone who has lied to you, even—especially—if they share your face. "Maren says you asked for me."

"I asked for you the day you were born. I've been asking since." Her mother's hands turned over in her lap, palms up, an old gesture of surrender that Lior had seen her use exactly once before—at the trial of a man she'd sworn to protect and failed. "You have questions."

"I have one question. Everything else is decoration." Lior heard her own voice go flat and cold, the swordspeech voice without any sword in it. "Why?"

Sefeya closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked older than the mountain.

"Because they told me what you'd be," she said. "And I believed them."

* * *

The story came out in pieces, the way confessions do—her mother kept stopping to apologize for the shape of it, and Lior kept saying *just tell it*, until Sefeya laughed once, wetly, and said, "You were always impatient with stories. You wanted the ending first. You'd flip to the last page of every book."

"I still do."

"Yes. I know. I know what you are now, Lior. I've known for twenty-two years."

The prophecy, her mother called it. Spoken by the tide-oracles before the court fell under its own weight, before the Long Tide began to drink, back when Mount Serein's priests still pretended their bargains were holy. A child of an oath-line. A speaker whose true word would either bind the deep thing forever or open it completely—and the oracles, being honest men in a dishonest profession, admitted they could not tell which.

"They showed me both futures," Sefeya said. "In the water. Two visions, side by side, like reflections in a well. In one, a woman with your face stands on the drowned throne and speaks, and the Long Tide sleeps for another thousand years. In the other—" Her breath caught. "In the other, the same woman speaks, and the sea comes up out of the mountain and does not stop coming. Every vision ended the same way, Lior. With your mouth moving. With the world ending or being saved, and no way to know which word you'd choose."

"So you sold me."

"So I *hid* you." Her mother leaned forward, chains dragging, eyes fierce suddenly—the old fire, banked but alive. "Do you understand what I was offered? Maren came to me herself, before you could walk. She said: give us the soul, and the gift goes dormant. No oaths. No words. No prophecy. A quiet life. She swore the soul would be kept safe, unspent, until—" She stopped.

"Until they needed a vessel."

"Until they needed a vessel," Sefeya whispered. "Yes. I knew that part. I told myself—I *told* myself—that keeping it from you was protection. That a woman who cannot speak cutting-words can't be used as a weapon. That you'd live and grow old and die a smuggler in the mountains, and the bottle would sit in the dark forever, and everyone would be safe." Her laugh was terrible. "I was very good at telling myself things. It's how I survived selling you."

Lior found she was gripping the doorframe hard enough to hurt. "You didn't sell me. You sold *it*. My soul. Which is me."

"Is it?" Her mother looked up, and there was genuine anguish in it, the anguish of a woman who had spent two decades turning the same question over like a stone. "I don't know. That's the truth, Lior. I don't know if what they took was a piece of you or all of you or none of you. I only knew that the oracles' visions both began with a swordspeaker, and I thought—if there is no swordspeaker, there is no vision. If I break the chain at the first link—" She spread her hands. "Protection and betrayal. I have never been able to find the seam between them. I looked for twenty years."

"And instead you gave them exactly what they wanted. A vessel, preserved. Ripening." Lior's voice shook now, and she hated it. "Every year I didn't die, every year I stayed hidden and whole, I was doing their work for them. You didn't break the chain. You *polished* it."

"Yes." No deflection. No excuse. Just the word, laid down like a blade on a table. "When I understood what the court had become—what Maren intended—I tried to undo it. That's why I'm in these chains. I sent the map. I sent couriers. Nine of them died getting it up the mountain." Her chin lifted. "The tenth was meant to reach you. I'm sorry he reached you dying. I'm sorry for all of it. I will say it as many times as you need, though I know it buys nothing."

Lior's throat worked. Somewhere above them, distantly, she thought she heard singing—the alarm of singing brine had been hours ago, but the court's music never really stopped. It just changed keys.

"Why tell me the prophecy now?" she asked. "If you wanted me dormant and safe, why hand me the reason to speak? Why not let me walk away with the bottle and never look back?"

"Because Maren doesn't need your cooperation anymore." Her mother's voice dropped, urgent. "The Rite can be performed with an unwilling vessel. It's slower. Crueler. But the Long Tide is waking whether you consent or not, Lior—the blizzards above aren't weather, they're its breathing. I told you the truth because you deserve one true thing from me before the end. And because—" She hesitated, and then, with the air of a woman stepping off a cliff: "because the visions were both *you*. Both futures. Your mouth, your word. Which means somewhere in you is the choice itself. And I would rather you choose knowing than sleep through it innocent."

The silence after that was long. Water moved somewhere in the walls, patient as everything down here.

"You could have told me when I was twelve," Lior said finally. "You could have told me anything. Instead you bound my oaths and vanished and let me spend ten years flinching from my own voice."

"Yes."

"That's it? Yes?"

"What would you like me to add?" Sefeya smiled, broken and real. "A justification? I've written a thousand. None of them survive contact with your face." She rose, chains whispering, and crossed the small chamber until she stood close enough that Lior could see the frost-scars on her mother's tongue when she spoke—old ones, faded, the marks of a swordspeaker who had once spoken true words of her own. "You asked why. Here is all the why I have: I loved you more than I respected you. It is the great sin of parents, and I committed it thoroughly, and I am sorry, and I would do it differently, and I might do it the same. All of those things are true at once. Welcome to being my daughter."

Against every plan, every wall Lior had built, something in her chest cracked sideways.

"You're infuriating," she managed.

"I'm told it's hereditary."

And Lior laughed—one short, wrecked sound, half sob—and her mother caught her hands, and for three seconds neither of them was a priestess's prisoner or a smuggler or a vessel. They were just two women in a small room, holding on.

Then the light in the chamber flickered, and the distant singing changed key again, and Sefeya went rigid.

"What is it?"

"They've moved the Rite forward." Her mother's grip tightened. "It was meant to be at midwinter. It won't be. Maren felt you come down the mountain, Lior. The moment an Oyelaran walked through the ninth door, the Tide began to rise. You weren't lured here." Her eyes were wide and horrified and certain. "You were *invited*. The whole hunt, the Guard, the corridors—it was theater. They only needed you inside the walls before the water finished rising."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, came a sound Lior had only ever heard in nightmares: the mountain exhaling.

The blizzard had begun.

* * *

Lior backed toward the door, mind already running routes, counting wardens, doing the arithmetic of escape that had kept her alive for a decade. Behind her, her mother pressed close to the bars of salt that sealed the alcove.

"Lior. Listen to me. One thing more." Sefeya's voice dropped low and fast. "The visions were twins, yes—but the oracles lied about one detail, and I only learned it after they chained me. The word that binds the Tide and the word that frees it are the *same word*. Spoken by the same mouth. What decides which future falls isn't the word at all." She smiled, fierce and proud and terrified. "It's whether the speaker believes she deserves to speak it."

"And do I?"

"I don't know," her mother said. "But I believe it for you. Until you can carry it yourself."

The door behind Lior swung open onto warden-lit water, and Maren's voice floated down the coral stair, gentle as drowning:

"Come along, child. The Tide is impatient to meet you."
