## 14. Winter Finishes Falling

Cassian had spent eleven years as a tide-priest learning to read water, and four years since learning to read weather instead, and he would have told anyone who asked that the second education was the more honest one. Water lied. Weather only ever told you the truth early.

The truth it was telling him now came down the mountain in a grey wall, moving like something with an appointment to keep.

He stood at the mouth of the smuggler's gallery—a crack in the rock wide enough for two people and their regrets—and watched the blizzard come down the Thornfell passes. It did not fall so much as arrive. One moment the peaks above Mount Serein were visible, white and indifferent; the next they were gone, swallowed whole, and the wind arrived a heartbeat later carrying snow sideways and a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

"Ah," Cassian said, to no one, because Lior was forty paces back down the corridor checking the map against the carvings, and there was no one else to say it to.

*Ah* was perhaps insufficient. He tried again. "That's the whole winter arriving at once."

Still nothing. He was talking to stone. This was, he reflected, not entirely new for him; he'd given sermons to congregations of drowned things for a decade, and at least the stone didn't heckle.

He turned back into the corridor. The drowned halls beneath Serein breathed warm air up through their stairwells—the mountain's old blood, heated by whatever slept at its root—and where that warmth met the storm above, the entrance passages wept. Water ran down the walls in long silver threads. Somewhere below, faint as a pulse through a wrist, he could feel the tide-chanting already beginning: the low, rhythmic drone of priests at their stations, hours ahead of any proper schedule.

That was the part that made his stomach fold over on itself. Not the storm. Storms were honest. It was the chanting starting *early*. Rites began early when the celebrants were afraid, and tide-priests were only ever afraid of two things: their god waking, and their god's dinner escaping.

Lior looked up from the map as his boots splashed toward her. She had the bottle wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, against her heart, which Cassian thought was either very brave or very foolish, and which she would have said was neither, it was simply where it fit.

"The pass is gone," he reported. "All of them, I'd guess. That's not weather, that's winter finishing the job. We're sealed in."

"I know." She rolled the map with the crisp efficiency of someone who had already done the arithmetic. "I felt the pressure change an hour ago. My ears have been popping like Vess counting coins."

Despite everything—despite the cells below, the Guard above, the thing at the mountain's root turning over in its sleep—Cassian smiled. "You've been spending too much time with that woman."

"One of us has to be funny. You're busy being tragic."

"I'm being *accurate*. There's a difference, and the difference is that accuracy keeps us alive."

"Does it?" Lior tucked the map away. "Because from where I'm standing, accuracy just told us every road out of this mountain closed at once."

Cassian opened his mouth to argue and found, to his considerable annoyance, nothing there. He closed it again. In the priesthood they'd trained him in rhetoric, in the gentle art of making despair sound like doctrine, and Lior Oyelaran dismantled him in six words without raising her voice. He'd have resented it if he weren't so grateful.

"So," she said. "Options."

"We go down faster than they expect and out through the pilgrim tunnels before the rite completes." He heard how it sounded even as he said it. Thin. A rope bridge made of hope and wet paper.

"And if the rite starts early?"

"It *has* started early. I can hear it."

Lior tilted her head, listening. Cassian watched her do it—the small unconscious grace of her, chin lifted, eyes half-closed, like a woman hearing music through a wall. Ten years of hiding had taught her to hear everything and admit to nothing.

"That's not a normal rite cadence," she said. "It's too fast. Too many voices."

"Because they're frightened," Cassian said. "Maren moved the timetable because of us. Because of you. Every hour we spend hiding in these galleries is an hour she spends pouring souls into—" He stopped. He always stopped there. Naming the Long Tide aloud felt like knocking on its door.

Lior named it anyway. She'd been doing that since the vault, saying the words flatly, daring them to mean more than they meant. "Into the Long Tide. Fine. Then we don't hide anymore. Hiding is what got Cassian captured and what nearly got me cornered by Maren in the first place. We move now, tonight, while half the court is kneeling in the deep hall with their faces in the water."

"You want to walk into the rite."

"I want to walk *through* it. There's a difference, and the difference is that walking through keeps us alive." She gave him his own sentence back with interest, and Cassian laughed despite himself—a short, startled bark that echoed down the warm black corridor and came back sounding smaller.

"Gods help me," he said. "You've been spending time with me too."

* * *

They descended.

The galleries changed as they went deeper, the way a house changes as you approach the room where the argument happened. The smuggler's passages were rough-cut and practical, all tool-marks and drainage channels, but the older ways beneath them were carved by hands that had expected to be admired. Pillars rose out of standing water, fluted and furred with pale growth. Murals swam past in the lamplight: processions of crowned figures walking into the sea and walking back out wearing the sea like a cloak. Cassian had studied these images in seminary copies, inked by careful scholarly hands that had never smelled the salt, and he remembered thinking them beautiful.

In person they were beautiful the way a snare is beautiful. Every crowned figure in every mural had the same face. He'd never noticed that in the copies. The copyists had smoothed it out, or hadn't seen it, or hadn't wanted to.

"Your church," Lior observed, "has a type."

"My former church. And yes." He touched the wall lightly, and the water running down it beaded around his fingers like it recognized him and disapproved. "We called it the Face of the Tide. Doctrine held that the drowned court all shared one countenance because they shared one purpose. Unity made visible." He wiped his hand on his coat. "It never occurred to any of us to ask why unity required everyone to stop having their own face."

"And you preached this."

"For eleven years. With conviction, which is the embarrassing part. Doubt is respectable; conviction is what gets people hurt." He stepped over a channel of brine that glowed faintly, a vein of the mountain's slow light. "I keep waiting for you to throw that back at me properly. You've been very restrained."

"I'm saving it," Lior said. "For when I need you to do something stupid and guilt is the fastest lever."

"That's fair. That's honestly fair."

The chanting grew. It came up through the water now, through the soles of their boots, a vibration more than a sound, and Cassian felt his old training wake in him unbidden—his feet wanting to find the liturgical rhythm, his breath wanting to match the drone. He bit down on it. Eleven years of habit sat in the body like ballast, and no amount of defection emptied the hold.

He must have made some face, because Lior glanced at him. "You're doing the priest walk."

"The what?"

"You get this look. Chin down, shoulders set, like you're about to forgive somebody whether they want it or not. You've been doing it for the last hundred steps."

"I am *not*—"

"You are. Vess does an impression. It's very good. There's a bit where she holds a ladle like a censer."

"I have known that woman for three days and she's building a repertoire about me."

"She's had longer than that. She's been doing the impression since the ford. You killed three Hollow Riders and she started rehearsing that night." Lior's voice was dry, but underneath the dryness Cassian heard the other thing, the thing she carried whenever Vess or the ford or the frost-scars came up: the memory of what her voice did when she let it off its leash. Three riders. Frost-scars on her tongue that still hadn't faded. She talked about it like weather. She carried it like bodies.

He let the humor lie where it had fallen and didn't step on it further. Some silences were a kindness.

They reached the Gallery of Tithes—an avenue Cassian knew from his pilgrimage years, though it had been dry then, ceremonial, lined with empty niches where offerings were displayed during high feast. Now it stood flooded to the knee, and the niches were not empty.

Bottles. Hundreds of them, racked in rows down both walls, each one glowing with its own small interior weather—blue, green, gold, one furious orange that flickered like a trapped argument. Souls in storage. The court's larder.

Lior stopped dead.

Cassian watched her look at them, and understood that she was not seeing hundreds. She was seeing one. The oilcloth bundle shifted slightly against her chest—not the bottle moving, he was fairly sure, but her breathing changing around it. Since the vault she'd been different with it: not protective exactly, or not only. Attentive. Like a woman sharing a cell with a stranger who might be her sister.

"Don't listen to them," he said quietly.

"They're not saying anything."

"No. But you're listening anyway. There's a difference, and—" He caught her expression and abandoned the construction. "Sorry. I'll retire that joke."

"Never. It's the only thing keeping me sane." But she made herself walk on, wading, eyes forward, jaw set, and Cassian followed and did not point out that her hand had come up to rest flat over the hidden bottle, the way you'd steady a wound.

At the far end of the gallery, where the avenue opened onto the great descending stair to the deep hall, the chanting resolved into words. Cassian's stomach dropped through the warm water and kept going.

He knew this part of the liturgy. He'd sung it. *From the nine doors, the gathered light; from the gathered light, the opening eye.* It was the third movement of the Rite of the Long Tide—which meant Maren wasn't beginning early. She was already a third of the way through.

"How long?" Lior asked. She'd read his face; she was getting frighteningly good at reading his face.

"If they started at dusk—and the cadence says they did—the third movement takes until moonrise. Maybe five hours. Then the pouring begins." He looked up the stairwell, where the light of ten thousand souls bled upward through the water like dawn seen from underwater. "Five hours, Lior. And the passes above us are a white death, and the pilgrim tunnels flood at the second movement—they open the sluices to fill the deep hall, it's part of the choreography—"

"So the exits close as the rite proceeds." Lior said it calmly, the way she said most terrible things, and Cassian realized with a strange floating clarity that this was what courage actually looked like up close: not fire, but bookkeeping. A woman counting doors as they shut and deciding, door by door, that she would make a new one.

"Yes," he said. "By moonrise there will be exactly one way out of this mountain."

"Which is?"

"Through."

She nodded slowly. Above them, up the long throat of the stair, the chanting swelled, and somewhere in its depths Cassian could hear—fancied he could hear—a single voice leading it, clear and cold and certain, a voice he'd knelt to for eleven years. Maren, conducting the drowning of a world.

And beneath the chanting, so far down it might have been his own pulse, something answered. A long, slow stirring, like a whale turning over under ice. The mountain's root shifting in its sleep, dreaming of waking.

Lior's hand was still flat over her heart, over the bottle, over the whispering thing in her mother's voice. She looked up the stair into the soul-light, and Cassian saw fear in her face, real fear, and saw her pick it up and put it in her pocket like a tool she intended to use later.

"Well," she said. "Then we'd better not be late."

* * *

They made their camp in the hollow behind the Gallery of Tithes, in an alcove the smugglers' maps marked as *dry-ish, mostly, don't tell anyone*, and Cassian took first watch because Lior hadn't slept properly since the vault and arguing with her about it was a battle he'd already lost twice.

He sat with his back to the warm stone and listened to the mountain.

The chanting went on. The sluices groaned open somewhere below, and the water around their camp rose an inch, then another, patient as arithmetic. Up top, muffled through a thousand feet of rock, the blizzard pressed its shoulder against the gates. He imagined Vess up there somewhere—he'd sent no word, there had been no time, no way—but imagining was a habit he couldn't break, and so he pictured her in some high tavern, dry and profane and safe, inventing new material about his priest walk, and the picture hurt less than the alternative.

An hour into the watch, Lior spoke from the dark without opening her eyes.

"Cassian. If it comes to choosing—the bottle or the rite—which do I choose?"

He was quiet long enough that she opened her eyes.

"You're asking me?"

"You're the one who knows what the rite needs. I'm the one who can't think clearly about—" Her hand moved toward the oilcloth bundle and stopped. "So. Which."

Cassian looked at her across the dim glow of the brine channels, this woman who had lost her name to a bottle and her mother to a bargain and her whole life to an oath sold before she could speak, and he found that the honest answer was also the only answer, and that it had been sitting in him since the ford, since he'd watched her word cut three riders down and seen what it cost her tongue.

"Neither," he said. "Choose the door nobody's guarding yet. There's always one. I spent eleven years in a church built on the belief that every door was locked, and the whole faith was wrong, Lior. That's why it works—that's why it *eats*—because everyone inside believes the doors are locked." He leaned forward. "One isn't. There never is. We just haven't found yours yet."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then, quietly, in the tone of a woman filing something away to survive on later: "That's the first hopeful thing a priest has ever said to me."

"I was defrocked. It doesn't count."

"It counts."

Below them, the chanting changed key, and the water rose another inch, and far beneath everything, the Long Tide turned toward waking—and Cassian kept watch, and counted doors.
