## 15. Breaking the Bottle

The hiding place Lior found was a chapel, or what had been a chapel before the sea came in and made its own decisions about worship.

It sat off a flooded gallery two turns below the vault of souls—a small domed room where the water stood waist-deep and utterly still, so clear she could see the mosaic floor beneath her: waves, fish, a drowned woman with her arms spread wide who might have been dancing or drowning or both. Salt crust climbed the walls in white lace. Somewhere above, light filtered down through a crack in the mountain's bones and turned the whole chamber the color of green glass.

No one had come here in a long time. The singing brine's alarm had faded behind her through six locked doors and one very narrow chimney of stone that had cost her a strip of skin from her ribs and most of her dignity. For now, at least, the court was searching elsewhere. She could hear them—distant bells, the wet echo of wardens' voices calling to each other through the flooded halls—but they were voices without direction, like gulls arguing over something already eaten.

Lior waded to the edge of the dome, where a ledge of dry stone curved like an old man's shoulder, and sat down on it. Her boots dripped. Her hands were shaking.

She took out the bottle.

It was smaller than it had any right to be, for something that was supposedly the whole of her. No longer than her forearm, blown glass gone milky with age, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. Inside, instead of liquid or light or anything sensible, there was a slow silver turning—like fog thinking, like a fish made of moonlight circling the same small sea over and over. And around the neck, tied with rotted cord, hung a tag of lead foil stamped with letters she knew better than her own heartbeat:

*LIOR OYELARAN.*

Her mother's handwriting. Of course it was her mother's handwriting. Everything terrible in Lior's life came wrapped in that careful, slanted script—the oath-bindings, the rules, the long years of *don't speak, don't speak, don't speak* pressed into her like flowers into a book.

"Well," Lior said aloud, because the chapel was empty and empty rooms deserved honesty. "Here we are."

The bottle did not answer. It never answered when she wanted it to. It only whispered—had been whispering since the vault, since her fingers closed around it and the singing brine began to scream—in a voice like wind through a keyhole. Not words. Not yet. Just the sound of something on the other side of a wall, trying to remember how doors worked.

She should smash it.

That was the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was simple. Maren wanted this bottle. The whole drowned court wanted this bottle; she'd seen the price souls fetched at auction, and hers would fetch more than all the rest together, because hers wasn't just food for the Long Tide—it was a *key*, Cassian had said, though he'd refused to say more until they were somewhere safe, which was a priestly way of saying *never*. If she shattered it here, in this forgotten chapel, the soul inside would scatter like breath on cold glass. The tide-priests could pour nothing. The Long Tide would stay hungry and asleep. Whatever prophecy her mother had sold her daughter to escape would drown quietly in the dark, unfinished.

And Lior would go back to being what she had been for ten years: a woman with a gift and no self underneath it. A swordspeaker hollowed to the blade. She'd survive. She'd done it before. You could live a long time as a knife, if nobody asked you to be a hand.

She raised the bottle over the mosaic floor, where the drowned woman danced her endless dance, and thought about how easy it would be. Glass breaks. That's practically its profession.

*Do it,* said the part of her that had guided caravans through snowbound passes and hated every word she spent doing it. *Do it and be free of them. All of them. Mother, priests, prophecy, Guard. Free.*

Her arm trembled.

Not from the weight. The bottle weighed almost nothing. From the whispering—which had changed. She noticed it the way you notice a room going quiet: suddenly, completely, wrong.

The whispering had stopped.

And then, from inside the glass, in a voice that was not wind through a keyhole at all but warm and low and unbearably familiar, someone laughed.

Lior nearly dropped it.

She caught the bottle against her chest and held it there, heart slamming, staring at the slow silver turning. The laugh hadn't been cruel. That was the worst of it. It had been the laugh of someone who has just watched a friend do something ridiculous and loves them for it—and Lior knew exactly four people in the world who laughed like that, and three of them were dead, and the fourth was Vess, and Vess was not currently a soul in a bottle, whatever Vess claimed after too much plum brandy.

"Who's there?" she asked the bottle. Then, feeling foolish, more precisely, in the old tongue her mother had used for oaths: "*What* is there?"

The silver turned. And turned. And then it showed her—

Snow.

Not Thornfell snow, mean and sideways. This was snow falling soft and straight over a city she had never seen but knew instantly, the way you know a face in a dream: white walls, blue roofs, lanterns strung across a frozen canal, and a girl of maybe eight running along the ice with her arms out like wings, laughing, chased by a woman whose braid was coming loose and whose laughter was bigger than the girl's.

The woman was her mother. Younger than Lior had ever known her. Younger than grief.

And the girl—the girl had Lior's own face. Not approximately. *Exactly.* The same stubborn jaw, the same crooked left eyebrow, the same gap between the front teeth that Lior had spent a childhood being teased about and an adulthood pretending didn't exist.

"That's not real," Lior said. Her voice cracked on *real* and she hated it. "That never happened. I grew up in the passes. I grew up in *Thornfell*. There was no city, there was no canal, there was no—"

But the memory kept going, indifferent to her objections, the way memories are.

The girl fell through the ice.

Not far—shallow water, a canal drained for winter, the kind of fall that bruises pride and shins. But the mother's fear was total, absolute, a bell tolling through the whole memory, and she pulled the girl out and held her and held her, and Lior felt the arms around herself, felt the wet wool and the hammering heart that wasn't hers except that it *was* hers, and heard her mother say, muffled into her hair, half-laughing, half-crying:

"My brave fool. My brave, breakable fool. What would I do if the world broke you?"

And eight-year-old-Lior, gap-toothed and dripping, said something that made the memory blur and stutter, some child's nonsense, but Lior felt the shape of it anyway, felt it the way you feel a word in a language you almost speak:

*I'm not breakable. I'm yours.*

* * *

Lior sat on the cold ledge for a long time after that, holding the bottle like a bird with a broken wing, and the water in the chapel lapped gently against the mosaic woman, and she did not cry, mostly, which she counted as a professional victory.

Because here was the problem, laid out plain as smuggler's arithmetic: those were not her memories.

She had checked. She had gone through everything, every year, the way you count coin twice when the sum looks wrong. There was no city with white walls. There was no frozen canal. Her mother had never braided her hair by a window overlooking water; her mother had braided her hair in a waystation at the top of the world, fast, in the dark, always glancing at the door. The first time Lior had ever seen the sea she'd been nineteen and had thrown up from the smell of it.

And yet the memory fit her like her own boots. The gap-toothed grin was *hers*. The fear in her mother's voice was *hers*—she'd heard that exact fear once, at fourteen, when Lior came home late from a pass with frostbite and a broken promise. The love in it was hers too. That was the unbearable part. You can fake a lot of things, but you cannot fake the specific weight of a mother's arms, and Lior's body remembered it even though her mind insisted it had never happened.

"Which means either I'm mad," she told the bottle conversationally, "or you're lying, or—"

Or the third thing. The third thing she'd been circling since the vault, since the tag with her name, since the whisper started. The third thing was the one Cassian wouldn't explain and Maren wanted so badly and her mother had sold away in some salt-stained transaction ten years ago:

That the soul in the bottle was not a piece of her.

It was the rest of her.

Ten years ago, her mother hadn't bound her oaths. She'd *split* her. Cut the whole cloth of her daughter in two and kept the dangerous half under wax and lead—the half that laughed in cities, the half that ran on ice with her arms out, the half that loved without checking the exits first—and sent the other half, the careful half, the blade-half, up into the mountains to hide. To survive. To be small enough that no prophecy could find purchase in her.

*Don't speak,* her mother had said, all those years. *Don't speak.* And Lior had thought it was protection. It was amputation. Every word she'd swallowed, every cutting-word she'd rationed like bread in a famine, every night she'd lain awake hating herself for the small indulgence of a single spoken syllable—that hadn't been discipline. That had been a woman bleeding slowly from a wound she couldn't see, keeping herself alive on half a heart.

"No," Lior said. "No, that's—" She laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin, echoing in the dome. "That's a story. That's the kind of story Vess tells to get a better price. People don't get *cut in half*, people don't—"

The bottle showed her another one.

A kitchen. Warm bread. A man with flour on his forearms—her father? Did she have a father? Had there been a father with flour on his arms and a tuneless humming habit and a way of saying her name like it was the punchline to a joke only he got? The memory was shorter, just a flash: him lifting her onto his shoulders while she shrieked with joy, and the joy was enormous, unguarded, *stupid* with happiness, the joy of a child who has never once considered that loving people is a liability—

Lior tore her mind out of it, gasping, and the chapel came back: green light, still water, salt lace on the walls. Her face was wet. She wiped it with the heel of her hand and pretended it was spray.

"You're doing this," she accused the bottle. "You're showing me what you want me to see. That's what the priests do, isn't it? That's the lure. Show the thief a paradise, get her to carry you wherever you want to go." Because that was possible too, and she had to hold on to it, she had to—Maren had let her find the vault. Maybe. Maybe the whole theft had been permitted, arranged, a soul walking itself home to the mouth of the Long Tide with its own legs. Bottled souls whispered. Cassian had said so. Bottled souls *begged*.

But the memories didn't feel like a lure. A lure would show her power. A lure would show her the throne, the gift unbound, words that cut mountains. These memories showed her bread. A father's shoulders. Falling through shallow ice and being loved furiously afterward. Nobody baits a trap with a kitchen.

Unless they know you've been starving for ten years. Unless they know that a kitchen is worth more to you than a kingdom.

"Oh," Lior said quietly. "Oh, that's cruel. Whoever built you learned cruelty from the best."

The bottle turned its slow silver turn, and for a moment—just a moment—she saw something else in it. Not a memory. A reflection. Her own face, bent over the glass, and beside it, overlapping it like two images on wet paper, another face. Hers. Hers, but smiling. Hers, but *whole*—eyes that had never learned to check the exits, a mouth that had never learned to swallow a word.

The other Lior looked at her. Across ten years and a quarter inch of enchanted glass, the stolen half of her soul looked at the half that remained, and Lior felt her do it—felt the attention like sunlight through a window, like a hand pressed flat against the inside of a door.

Then, distinctly, in her mother's voice, in the tone her mother had used for the oldest, smallest oaths, the bedtime ones, the ones that meant *I am here, sleep now*:

*"Not yet. Hold on. I'll find the way back to you."*

The chapel went silent. The silver turned. Somewhere far above, the alarm bells changed their rhythm—from searching to something faster, something organized, and Lior understood with a cold slide in her gut that the search had stopped being a search and become a convergence. They'd found the chimney. They'd found the trail of wet bootprints she'd been too shaken to think about covering.

She stood up on the ledge, bottle clutched to her chest, and looked down at the mosaic woman with her arms spread wide, dancing or drowning, and finally understood why the old builders had put her here. She was both. She'd been both the whole time. That was the whole sermon.

"I can't," Lior said—to the bottle, to the chapel, to the mother-shaped ghost in her own head. "I can't break you and I can't trust you and I can't give you back to them, and I have maybe fifty feet of corridor between me and the Hollow Guard, and I have never in my life hated a choice less and more at the same time."

The bottle whispered—not words now, just warmth, just presence, the way a sleeping person fills a room.

Lior tucked it inside her coat, against her heart, where it beat faintly out of rhythm with her own, and stepped down into the green water, and went looking for a way out that did not exist yet.

Behind her, in the still water of the dome, the mosaic woman kept her arms open. And far below the chapel, deeper than the vault, deeper than the drowned throne, something older than winter rolled over in its sleep—and smiled, because it could feel the key coming closer, carried willingly, in the chest of the last Oyelaran.
