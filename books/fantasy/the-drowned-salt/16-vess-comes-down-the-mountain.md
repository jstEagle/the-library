## 16. Vess Comes Down the Mountain

Vess Amaran had made a great many bad decisions in her forty-one years, and she had a private ledger of them, kept the way other people kept accounts of debts owed. Most of her bad decisions had paid out eventually—smuggling was, after all, simply the art of making bad decisions profitably. But standing at the mouth of the Kettle Pass with snow coming down like the sky had finally given up on subtlety, looking at a wall of collapsed rock that hadn't been there three days ago, Vess admitted to herself that this one might not pay out at all.

"Tell me again," said Dree, her second, who was built like a door and about as talkative, "why we're doing this."

"Because," Vess said, "a girl who once carried my entire inventory of Thornfell poppy-resin up a cliff face with her teeth, metaphorically speaking, is currently underground in a mountain full of fish-breathing aristocrats, and I owe her."

"You don't owe anyone enough for this."

"That's where you're wrong. That's where everyone's always been wrong about me." Vess pulled her collar up against the wind. "I keep excellent books, Dree. You know this. And in my books, Lior Oyelaran is owed more than I can count, which means the debt is infinite, which means—" she gestured grandly at the avalanche-choked pass, the blizzard, the general apocalypse of weather, "—this is just the interest."

Dree looked at the rockfall. Then at Vess. Then back at the rockfall, as if hoping it might have become something less like a wall and more like a suggestion.

"It's closed," Dree said.

"Everything's closed if you're a coward."

"I'm not a coward. I'm a professional. There's a difference, and the difference is I'm still alive."

Vess grinned at her. It was, she knew, an infuriating grin—the grin of a woman who had talked her way past three customs officers, two bandit kings, and one very confused bear—and she deployed it now with the precision of long practice. "Good thing I brought professionals, then."

She turned and waved down the trail, where eleven figures picked their way through the snow behind her: smugglers, every one, drawn from the scattered network of fences, runners, and knife-happy romantics Vess had spent twenty years cultivating across the Thornfell. Old Tam with his rope and his terrible singing. The Ferro twins, who could squeeze through gaps no honest person would attempt. Marisol, who'd once outrun a Hollow Guard patrol on a mule—a *mule*—and still told the story wrong on purpose because the correct version sounded too much like bragging.

They were battered, frostbitten, and armed to the teeth, and they had all come when Vess sent word, without asking what the job paid.

That was the part that kept catching in Vess's chest, like a bone in the throat. Nobody had asked what the job paid.

* * *

It had started, four days ago, with a boy named Pip arriving at her safehouse in Lowmark soaked to the ribs and half-mad with cold, carrying a message from Cassian—the disgraced priest, the one Lior had come down off her mountain with, the one Vess had taken one look at and privately filed under *trouble, imported*.

The message was short. Lior had gone through the ninth door. The Hollow Guard had followed. The court below was waking something, and the passes were closing, and by the time anyone read this, winter would finish falling.

There was a line at the bottom, in different handwriting—Lior's handwriting, cramped and hurried, the letters of a woman who wrote the way she did everything else, as though someone might catch her at it:

*Don't come. This isn't your fight.*

Vess had read that line eleven times. On the twelfth reading she'd started laughing, which had alarmed Pip considerably.

"She says don't come," Vess explained, wiping her eyes.

Pip nodded slowly. "So we're going."

"So we're going." Vess was already pulling maps from under the floorboards. "Honestly, you'd think after ten years she'd know me better than to write *don't*. That's not a warning, that's an invitation with extra steps."

The plan, such as it was, had come together the way all her plans did: badly at first, then all at once. The ninth door opened onto the mountain's eastern flank, above the Kettle Pass, and the Kettle Pass was buried under a rockslide that the Guard themselves had triggered to seal the mountain behind them. No way over. No way around.

But Vess had spent two decades learning every crack and seam of the Thornfell, and she remembered something most people had forgotten: the old miners' drain. A flood-tunnel cut into the pass's foundations two hundred years ago, back when men were fools enough to dig sideways into Mount Serein for silver. It ran beneath the rockslide. It came out—according to a map she'd bought off a dying prospector for the price of a funeral—forty yards below the ninth door.

"It'll be flooded," Tam had said.

"Partially flooded."

"It'll be *collapsed*."

"Partially collapsed."

"It'll be full of things," said one of the Ferro twins, who had a gift for pessimism.

"Everything's full of things. That's what makes the world interesting." Vess had rolled up the map. "We go through the drain, we come up behind the Guard, we open the door from their side, and we get our girl out. In, out, home by supper."

"And if the thing they're waking wakes first?" Dree had asked.

Vess had paused, only briefly, in the act of buckling on her knife-belt. "Then," she'd said, "we improvise. Which, if you think about it, has been the plan all along."

* * *

Now she stood before the drain's entrance—a black archway half-swallowed by scree, breathing out air that smelled of wet stone and older, stranger things—and delivered the speech she'd been composing since Lowmark.

"Right, you beautiful idiots. Listen up." She counted heads. Eleven. Good. "Through here is maybe six hours of crawling through a hole in the world. It will be dark, wet, and deeply unpleasant. Some of you may die. If you do, I'll say lovely things at your funerals and steal nothing from your estates, which for me is basically sainthood."

A ripple of laughter, thin but real. Cold fear, warmed by company. That was the trick of it, the whole trick, the only trick Vess had ever known: nobody walks into the dark alone if you make sure they don't have to.

"The priest's message said the rite happens soon. We don't know how soon. So we don't stop, we don't sightsee, and if anyone finds treasure down there, we discuss it *after* we're not all dead. Questions?"

Marisol raised her hand. "Why are we really doing this? And don't give me the speech about books and debts. I've heard the speech."

Vess considered lying. She was good at lying; it was most of her trade. But these were her people, standing in a blizzard because she'd asked, and some debts deserved payment in true coin.

"Because ten years ago," she said, "I was a small-time fence with a price on my head and nowhere left to run, and a girl of nineteen hid me in a cave for a whole winter. Fed me. Never asked my name until spring. When I finally gave it, she said—" Vess stopped. Cleared her throat. The wind chose that moment to howl, which she was grateful for. "She said, 'Everyone's running from something. Yours sounds heavy.' And then she taught me three words of swordspeech so I could open locked doors, and she never once used her gift on me, though gods know I gave her reasons."

She looked at the black archway.

"She's spent ten years being nobody so the world wouldn't notice her. Well, the world noticed. And I am not—" her voice came out harder than she intended, "—I am *not* letting the world have her."

Silence, except the wind. Then Tam shouldered his coil of rope and said, "Well. Someone's got to save the sentimental speeches for the funeral," and walked into the dark, and the rest of them followed, and Vess went last, the way leaders do, so no one could see her face.

* * *

The drain was everything promised and worse.

They moved by lamplight in single file through water that started at the ankle and ended, two hours in, at the waist. The tunnel groaned around them—actual groaning, tectonic and personal, the sound of a mountain shifting its weight in its sleep. Twice they waded through chambers where the ceiling had come down in the old days and the passage narrowed to a crawl, and both times the Ferro twins went first, wriggling through gaps that Vess measured with her eyes and refused to measure with her hope.

At the third hour, Tam found the bones.

Not human—or not recently human. They were long, jointed strangely, furred with mineral growth, arranged along the tunnel wall as though something had lain down there to die and the mountain had decided to keep it. The lamplight caught the skull's empty sockets and threw shadows that moved wrong.

"Old mining accident?" Dree asked.

"Old *something* accident," Vess said. "Don't touch anything. Don't lick anything. Marisol, I'm looking at you specifically."

"I licked one thing! One time! Years ago!"

"And you've never lived it down, and you never will. Keep moving."

But it was after the bones that the water changed. Vess felt it before she understood it—a warmth seeping into the floodwater, gentle as bathwater rising, utterly wrong at this depth in this season. And with the warmth came a sound, faint, threading up through the stone: singing. Distant, layered, many-voiced. Not human voices. Not quite.

Tam stopped walking. "Tell me you hear that."

"I hear it."

"What is it?"

Vess listened. The song rose and fell like breathing. Somewhere in its weave she thought she caught a melody her grandmother had hummed—a lullaby, or the wreck of one—and that frightened her more than the bones had, because the mountain had no business knowing her grandmother's lullabies.

"I think," she said carefully, "that whatever they're feeding down there is dreaming. And I think dreams leak." She lifted her lamp higher. "Which means we're close, and which means we hurry, because I have met exactly one person alive who can argue with a dream, and she's down there losing the argument right now."

* * *

They came up out of the drain at dusk—if dusk meant anything inside a storm—and emerged through a fissure in the hillside into driving snow, forty yards below a shelf of black rock where a door stood open.

Open. Unattended. Around it, the snow was churned and bloody, marked with the boot-prints of a dozen soldiers and the drag-trails of bodies hauled away. A Hollow Guard patrol had passed here, recently, and gone in—but something had happened at the threshold first. Vess crouched over the churned snow and read it the way she read everything: like a ledger.

"There was a fight here," Dree said. "Guard against—"

"No." Vess touched a scorch-mark on the stone beside the door, a handprint burned into rock, five-fingered, rimmed in frost. Her stomach dropped through the mountain. "Look at the spread. One side. One person, holding a whole patrol." She stood, and her breath came out ragged. "That's her. That's Lior's work. She fought her way *in*."

"Then the Guard's already inside after her."

"Then we're not early." Vess unslung her pack and started handing out the goods: lantern-oil, climbing line, the short wicked crossbows she'd bought off a nervous arms-dealer last autumn and never expected to be grateful for. "We're late. Standard operating condition. Move."

They climbed the shelf in the storm's white blindness, ropes and curses, and reached the ninth door—a slab of green-black stone carved with waves that seemed, in the lamplight, actually to move. Beyond it: stairs descending into warm black water, and the singing louder now, gorgeous and horrible, and the smell of salt and incense and something underneath both, something vast and sleeping.

Vess planted herself in the doorway and looked down into the drowned dark, and for one uncharacteristic moment she let herself feel the whole size of what they were doing—eleven smugglers marching into a fairy-tale kingdom to steal one soul back from a god's dinner table.

Then she got over it.

"All right," she said, loud enough for the tunnel to carry it down to whatever waited below. "You've had our girl for days now, you fish-faced gentry, and I expect you've been terrible hosts. So we're coming down to collect her, and fair warning—" she drew her knife and grinned into the dark, the grin that had survived customs officers, bandit kings, and one very confused bear, "—my books always balance."

Behind her, eleven blades came free of eleven sheaths, a soft chorus of steel.

And far below, through the warm black water, the singing stopped.

In the sudden silence, Vess heard something else instead: footsteps, many, hurrying upward. Coming toward the door. Toward *them*—or running from something deeper in.

Dree nocked a bolt. "Friends of yours?"

"In my experience," Vess said, leveling her crossbow at the stairwell as the first shapes broke the surface of the dark water below, "anyone running downhill in a panic toward an exit is either about to be very useful or very inconvenient. Let's find out which."
