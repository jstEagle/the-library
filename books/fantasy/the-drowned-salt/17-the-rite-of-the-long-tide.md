## 17. The Rite of the Long Tide

The drowned court sang.

Lior heard it before she saw it—a sound like a thousand wet bells rung underwater, rising up through the flooded galleries of Mount Serein. It was beautiful in the way a trap is beautiful: all its teeth hidden behind light.

She crouched on a ledge of black coral above the great hall, water lapping at her boots, and looked down at what her mother had sold her soul to keep her from becoming part of.

The hall had been a cathedral once, maybe, or a throne room—time and tide had worn away the difference. Its vaulted ceiling had collapsed into open water above, so that moonlight from some fissure far overhead fell through fathoms of sea-green dark and came down broken into pillars of silver. The court stood in rows upon the flooded floor, nobles in their finery, their hair drifting like weed, their gills fluttering at their throats as they breathed the water that would have drowned any living thing. They were lovely. That was the worst of it. Ten years of hiding had taught Lior to expect monsters to look like monsters, and here was a whole civilization of them wearing faces like painted porcelain, smiling up at the altar with the rapturous patience of people waiting for a wedding.

The altar was a dais of white stone at the hall's center, and on it stood High Priestess Maren.

She had changed out of her pilgrim's gray into robes the color of deep water at night, and she held in both hands a bottle no bigger than a fist. Even from the ledge Lior could see it glowing—that soft, wrong, candle-through-flesh glow she'd seen in the vault, in the bottles racked by the hundred, each one holding a stolen soul. Maren lifted it high, and the singing swelled, and every face below turned toward it like flowers toward a lying sun.

*They're going to pour them in,* Lior thought. *All of them. Every soul they've hoarded for a century, poured down that thing's throat like wine.*

And beneath the dais—she felt it more than saw it—the Long Tide waited.

It was hard to look at directly. Her eyes kept sliding off it, the way they slid off the sun off open water. But there was a shape under the mountain's roots, vast beyond sense, coiled in a flooded dark older than the halls built over it, and it was *listening*. She could feel its attention the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth. For ten years it had slept. Tonight, fed, it would wake.

Her hand went to the bottle at her breast.

It had whispered to her all the way down from the vault—her own name in her mother's voice, over and over, patient as dripping water. *Lior. Lior. Come home.* She had stopped listening. Mostly. The whisper was quiet now, as if the soul inside were holding its breath along with her.

Below, Maren began to speak. Her voice carried through the water without distortion, clear as a bell rung in a silent room, and Lior realized the priestess wasn't speaking to the court at all. She was speaking to the thing beneath.

"Older than winter," Maren said. "Older than the oath that bound us to your hunger. We kept faith when faith was foolish. We fed you when the land forgot you. And tonight—" she raised the bottle higher, and the light of it caught every upturned face "—tonight we pay the debt entire, and wake you, and drown the world that abandoned us."

A cheer went up from the court, and it was the ugliest sound Lior had ever heard, because it was made of beautiful voices.

*One priestess,* she thought. *One altar. A few hundred water-breathing nobles who have never fought anything that could fight back. And me.*

Well. And whatever she was about to do with words.

She had spent a decade rationing herself. A cutting-word here, a turning-word there, each one paid for in frost-scars across her tongue, each one hated because using the gift meant admitting the gift existed, meant her mother hadn't been entirely mad, meant the oaths and the prophecies and the sold soul were all real. She had hidden because hiding hurt less than being hunted.

But Cassian was in a cell somewhere below, and Vess was bleeding somewhere above, and three hundred souls were about to be drunk by something that had been hungry since before winter had a name.

Rationing was over.

* * *

Getting down was the easy part. The smuggler's passes had taught her how to move through vertical places without disturbing so much as a drip, and the court was too busy singing to watch the shadows. She descended the coral ledge hand over hand, slipped into the flooded gallery behind the altar, and waded chest-deep through water that was blood-warm and tasted of salt and old grief.

That was when she found the cages.

They hung from chains off to one side of the dais—six of them, iron and salt-crusted, half-submerged. Five were empty. The sixth held Cassian.

He was slumped against the bars, soaked to the bone, his defrocked priest's collar gone, his face bruised along one side. His eyes were closed. For one horrible heartbeat Lior thought he was dead, and then he cracked one eye open, saw her, and managed—of course he managed—a smile.

"You're late," he whispered. "I had a whole speech prepared about divine intervention. You've ruined the timing."

"Shut up," she whispered back, and meant it fondly, which was new.

"Keys are with the guard. Big fellow. Third row back. He took them off me personally, very smug about it." He peered past her at the altar, at Maren, at the gathering light. "Lior. Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm not planning anything. Planning is for people with time."

"That is somehow both reassuring and the most frightening thing you've ever said to me."

She got the keys the way she'd gotten everything for ten years: quietly, from behind, with an elbow and a prayer. The guard folded into the water without a splash. Lior caught him, lowered him gently—it seemed rude, somehow, to let a man drown twice—and turned back to the cage.

The lock was salt-welded shut. Of course it was.

She could pick it. It would take four minutes she didn't have. Or—

Or she could stop being small.

Lior put her mouth close to the lock and spoke a word that had no meaning in any living tongue but meant *open* in the language swords had spoken before men learned to forge them. The word left her lips trailing cold, and the lock didn't break—it simply remembered that it had once been unmade iron, and rusted apart between one heartbeat and the next.

Frost bloomed across her tongue. She was getting used to the taste of it. Copper and winter.

Cassian stepped out of the ruined cage, rubbing his wrists, staring at her with an expression she couldn't read. "You've been doing that the whole time," he said softly. "Hiding that. In passes and taverns. Smuggling *salt*, Lior."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the last time someone knew what I was, she sold me." She said it flatly, without heat, and watched him flinch. "Ask me again after we survive."

Above them, Maren's voice rose to a crescendo. There was no more time for whispers at all.

* * *

Lior walked out of the shadow of the gallery and into the light, and the first thing she did was the thing she'd rehearsed exactly zero times: she threw her head back and shouted, in plain trade-tongue, loud enough to crack against the vaulted dark:

"HEY! You're doing it wrong!"

The singing faltered. Three hundred porcelain faces turned toward her. Somewhere in the ranks, a noblewoman dropped her fan—it drifted down through the water like a dying moth—and the silence that followed was so complete Lior could hear the Long Tide breathing beneath the stone, slow and vast and interested.

Maren looked down at her from the altar, and smiled, and it was the smile of a woman greeting a delayed guest at her own wedding.

"The Oyelaran girl," the priestess said. "You brought it back to us. How dutiful. Your mother would be proud."

"My mother," Lior said, walking forward, water streaming off her, sword drawn though everyone present must have known steel was a formality, "sold my soul to a bunch of fish-priests in evening wear. I'm here to file a complaint."

Someone in the crowd gasped. Someone else—she'd swear on anything—laughed.

"You are one girl," Maren said, descending a step, the bottle still glowing in her hands, "with a sword, in our hall, under our mountain, surrounded by our faithful. What precisely do you imagine you can do?"

Lior grinned. It surprised her, arriving on her face; she hadn't grinned in years, and it fit worse than her old invisibility and better than anything she'd worn in a decade.

"Words," she said.

Then she stopped rationing.

The first word she spent was *fall*. She aimed it at the great coral chandelier above the eastern ranks—not at the nobles beneath it, she wasn't ready to be a killer of crowds, not yet, maybe never—and the word hit the ceiling like a hammer of frost. A hundredweight of black coral tore loose and crashed down into the flooded floor, and the court scattered screaming, and the beautiful order of the ceremony broke like glass.

The second word was *blind*, and she gave it to the darkness itself, and every lantern-glow in the hall guttered out except the light of the souls.

The third word she saved.

Because Maren hadn't scattered. Maren stood on the altar with the bottle raised, and she was pouring it—tilting the neck over the white stone, letting the first thread of soul-light spill down into the cracks where the Long Tide slept—and the thing beneath the mountain *shuddered*. Lior felt it in her bones, in her teeth, in the scarred flesh of her tongue. Stone groaned. Water displaced in a wave that knocked a dozen nobles off their feet. Far above, through fathoms of sea and rock, snow began to fall into the mountain's open veins.

"No!" Lior lunged for the dais, and the Hollow Guard came out of nowhere.

There were eight of them—they'd been standing among the nobles all along, disguised, patient as knives—and they moved like nothing human, because nothing human was left in them. Their armor was empty. Their swords were salt-white. And they had been waiting, she understood in one flash of clarity, for exactly this: for the last Oyelaran to walk into the open and stand still long enough to be taken.

The first one's blade came for her throat and she caught it on her crossguard and spoke *shatter* into the space between them, and his sword exploded into frost and powder. The second cut her arm—shallow, burning—and she gave him *kneel*, and his legs buckled so hard he drove himself face-first into the stone. The third and fourth came together and she ran out of cleverness and spent raw power instead, a word like a scream, *away*, and the pressure of it hurled them both across the hall into the ruins of the chandelier.

Frost-scars crawled up her tongue and into her jaw. Her mouth was bleeding. She could taste winter at the root of every tooth.

*Worth it,* she thought wildly. *Ten years of silence, and this is what the silence was saving.*

She reached the dais. Maren had emptied the bottle—all but a single drop of light clinging to the glass—and the priestess turned to face her with an expression of genuine sorrow, which was somehow worse than hatred.

"It's too late," Maren said gently. "It has tasted. One soul wakes the appetite. The rest will follow. You cannot stop a tide by shouting at it, child."

"Watch me," Lior said. "Tides are just water. And water," she raised her sword, and the blade rang with a note that was almost speech, "has always done what I tell it."

But even as she said it, the mountain shuddered again, deeper, and this time the sound that came up through the stone was not stone at all. It was a voice. It had no words. It was older than words, and it was waking up hungry, and the drop of soul-light Maren had spilled was spreading through the cracks below like dawn through a keyhole.

And in the chaos at the edge of the hall—she saw it only because she was looking—Cassian was climbing the altar steps toward her, soaked and limping and alive, waving his arms like a man trying to flag down a runaway cart.

"Lior!" he shouted. "Lior, listen to me—I know what feeds it! I know what it eats!"

* * *

The Long Tide opened one eye beneath the mountain, and the water everywhere in the hall went still, and every soul-bottle in the vault far above began, all at once, to sing.
