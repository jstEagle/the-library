## 18. One True Word

Cassian had been a prisoner of the drowned court for less than a day, and already he had learned the three great truths of captivity beneath Mount Serein.

First: the cells were not cruel. They were worse than cruel—they were *comfortable*. A dry shelf to sit on, water warm as bathwater lapping at his ankles, a soft phosphorescent glow that made everything look kinder than it was. The court did not torture its prisoners. It *accommodated* them, the way a spider accommodates a fly.

Second: time moved strangely underwater. His wounds from the corridor chase—the gash along his ribs where a warden's hook had caught him—had stopped bleeding almost immediately, sealed by salt that knitted flesh with unsettling eagerness. The sea wanted him whole. The sea wanted everyone whole. That was rather the problem.

Third: he could hear it. The Long Tide.

Not with his ears. With the old training that Maren had beaten into him thirty years ago and that no defrocking ceremony had ever truly scraped out. It was a sound like a heartbeat heard through fathoms of water, vast and slow and patient, rising up through the floor of his cell with every passing hour. And every hour, it was a little louder.

*It's waking up,* he thought, pressing his palm flat against the wet stone. *And we gave it a spoonful of souls to help it along.*

He thought about Lior—wherever she was, whatever gallery she was hiding in with that bottle clutched to her chest like a stolen infant—and he prayed, in the faithless, embarrassed way of a man who had lost his god and kept the habit, that she was still breathing air.

The door opened on the fourth hour. Or what he judged was the fourth hour. Time being what it was.

Maren's acolyte stood in the doorway, a young man with kelp-pale hair and eyes like polished abalone. "The High Priestess summons you," he said. "You are to be given the courtesy of the dry room."

"The dry room," Cassian repeated. "How festive."

"You will be permitted to breathe air for one hour. The courtesy is extended because you were once of the tide."

"Once," Cassian agreed, hauling himself up off the shelf. His legs protested; the salt had healed him but not fed him. "Once is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, isn't it?"

The acolyte did not smile. None of them smiled. Cassian had forgotten that about the court—in the years since his exile he'd built himself a memory of it full of warmth, of candlelit hymns and shared bread, and standing here now he understood he had been remembering a painting, not a place. The real court breathed water and never laughed.

*Like a church,* he thought grimly, following the acolyte up a spiral stair. *Just like a church.*

* * *

The dry room was a dome of black stone at the top of the priests' tower, open at its crown to a shaft that rose toward the mountain's roots. Real air moved down that shaft—cold, thin, smelling of snow and iron—and Cassian drank it in like a man coming up from a dive too deep and too long.

Maren waited for him at the center of the dome, seated on a chair of driftwood that had been polished by centuries of reverent hands. She looked exactly as he remembered her: silver-streaked hair coiled like a wave about to break, robes the blue-black of deep water, her face carrying that particular serenity that Cassian had spent ten years learning to distrust in all religious persons, including, on his worst days, himself.

"Cassian." His name in her mouth sounded like absolution. That was her gift, and it had always been her gift, and it had ruined better men than him. "You look terrible."

"I've been having a difficult week."

"So I understand. You came down my mountain with a thief and a swordspeaker and you stole from the vault of souls." She said it without heat, the way one reads a weather report. "Sit. Breathe. We have an hour, and I would rather spend it honestly than spend it the way the Guard would prefer—with hooks."

He sat, because sitting was free and dignity was expensive. "You're offering me honesty?"

"I'm offering you a choice. It's more than you offered me, when you left."

"I didn't leave, Maren. You defrocked me. There were *proceedings*. There was a document with a wax seal. I keep it framed, actually—it's very handsome."

Something flickered across her face—not quite amusement, but the ghost of where amusement used to live in her. "You asked questions," she said. "That was your crime then, and it is your crime now, and I confess I have missed it. Everyone else in this court has learned to stop asking. It makes for very quiet dinners."

"What do you want, Maren?"

She leaned forward. Up close, in honest air, he could see what the water hid: she was tired. Not the tiredness of a night poorly slept but the tiredness of decades, worn down to the grain like a stair tread.

"The rite begins tomorrow," she said. "When the blizzard seals the last pass. The Long Tide will wake, and it will need feeding, and the feeding will take everything the vault holds—every bottled soul we have harvested in two hundred years. Including, if she hasn't destroyed it by then, the Oyelaran woman's."

"And after?"

"After?" Maren spread her hands. "After, the mountain opens. After, the tide goes out into the world and does what tides do. What it was always promised to do."

"And what was it promised, exactly? Because I've read the founding texts, Maren. All of them, back when you still let me into the archive. And there's something—" He stopped. Something had been scratching at the back of his mind since the vault, since the auction, since the moment he'd seen the nobles floating in their flooded halls with their beautiful vacant faces, and now, in the cold honest air of the dry room, it finally broke through.

"There's something wrong with the arithmetic," he said slowly.

Maren went very still. It was the stillness of a woman who has spent two hundred years making sure nothing in her presence ever went still unexpectedly.

"Explain," she said.

"The Long Tide sleeps. Fine. Old things sleep. But it doesn't sleep the way a bear sleeps or even the way the dead sleep. It sleeps the way a *debt* sleeps—unpaid, accruing interest. Every soul you pour into the vault buys it another century of quiet. Two hundred years of harvests, Maren. Thousands of souls. And it's still hungry. Still waking. Still growing louder." He leaned forward, matching her. "A debt like that doesn't shrink. Which means the original payment—the founding bargain, whatever oath put it to sleep in the first place—was never actually made. Or it was made falsely. Or it was broken before the ink was dry."

The silence stretched so long that Cassian could hear the snow moving somewhere far above them, sifting down through the mountain's bones.

"You've known," he said quietly. "Haven't you. You've known for years."

"I have served this court for a hundred and sixty years," Maren said, and her voice was perfectly level, which told Cassian everything. "I have harvested souls from villages that would have starved anyway. I have told myself it was mercy—quick sleep in a bottle instead of slow sleep in the ground. I have told myself the bargain was sacred and the price was just." Her hands folded in her lap, white-knuckled. "And forty years ago, in the deep archive, I found the founding oath itself. The original contract between the first tide-priests and the thing below."

"And?"

"And it is *blank*, Cassian." For the first time, her serenity cracked, and what showed underneath was not cruelty but terror—old, calcified, long-practiced terror. "The page where the oath should be written is blank. Not erased. Not worn away. Blank, the way a page is blank when the words written on it were never true. Words that are spoken falsely don't bind. They never bind. The founders made their bargain with a lie in their mouths—promised the Long Tide an eternity of service, or worship, or feeding, some vow they never meant to keep—and because the oath was false, it held nothing. The Tide was never bound. It was only ever *bribed*. And bribes run out."

Cassian sat back. The cold air of the dry room suddenly felt very cold indeed.

"That's why it takes more every year," he said. "That's why the harvests grow. You're not paying a debt. You're servicing a lie, and lies compound."

"Yes."

"And you've known for forty years, and you said nothing, and instead you *accelerated the harvests*."

"I accelerated the harvests because the alternative was telling four hundred priests and nine thousand courtiers that their entire world was built on a broken promise!" Maren's voice cracked like ice. Then, immediately, the serenity reassembled itself, smooth as water closing over a dropped stone. "Do you know what happens to a court built on a false oath when the oath is named? It doesn't reform, Cassian. It *dissolves*. Every noble in those halls breathes water because the founding bargain grants them breath. Every priest holds rank because the founding bargain grants authority. Name the lie, and the water goes out of their lungs and the authority out of their hands, all at once, in the same hour. Nine thousand people who cannot remember how to be anything else."

"So you feed it instead."

"So I feed it," Maren agreed softly, "and I tell myself it is mercy, and I am running out of souls to tell myself with."

Cassian looked at her for a long moment. Thirty years ago he had loved this woman the way novices love their mentors—totally, uncritically, with the specific blindness of the young. He had assumed her fall from grace, when it came, would be dramatic. Instead it had been this: not wickedness, but arithmetic. Mercy calculated until it stopped being mercy at all.

"You're telling me this," he said, "because you want something."

"I'm telling you this because you came down my mountain with a swordspeaker." Maren rose, drifting to the edge of the dome where the cold air fell. "An Oyelaran. Do you understand what that means? The Oyelarans were the ones who *wrote* oaths for kings. Their words don't describe truth—they *enforce* it. One true word from a swordspeaker can unmake a false one. It's why the old kingdoms feared them. It's why someone sold that girl's soul before she could ever learn what she was."

The pieces came together in Cassian's mind with an almost audible click, terrible and clean, like a lock turning.

"You want her to speak," he said. "You want her to stand in front of the Long Tide and say the words your founders couldn't. You want her to make the bargain *true*."

"I want the debt paid," Maren said simply. "One way or another. If she binds it truly, the court survives, the harvests end, and I die knowing I bought nine thousand lives with a few thousand bottles. If she refuses—" the shrug she gave was small and awful— "then it wakes, and none of this matters anymore."

"And Lior? The girl whose soul you people stole? Does she get a vote in this arrangement of yours?"

"She gets a vote the same as anyone. She gets to choose whether to be a sacrifice or a weapon. It's more choice than most of us got."

"No," Cassian said, standing. His legs shook and he ignored them. "No, Maren, you've made an error, and it's the same error your founders made, and I'd laugh if I weren't so frightened. You think her power works like yours. You think she can stand before that thing and *promise* it something, and the promise will hold because her words hold. But that's not how swordspeech works. I've seen it. Three days ago I watched her kill three Hollow riders with a single word, and I'll tell you what the word was. It wasn't a curse. It wasn't a command. It was a *statement*. She said 'you are already dead,' and the world agreed with her, because it was true. That's the whole art, Maren. Swordspeakers can only speak what's true. That's why they're rare. That's why they're feared. And that's why your plan is doomed—because you can't pay a false debt with a true word unless the word names the falseness."

Maren turned. In the phosphor-light her eyes were the color of the deep sea, and for a moment—just a moment—he saw hope in them, raw and ugly and desperate, and it was the most frightening thing he had seen all week, and he had been chased through flooded galleries by things with hooks.

"Then tell me," she said. "What does she say?"

* * *

They gave him back to his cell, because Maren was not yet desperate enough to keep her promises in the open, and Cassian sat on his dry shelf with the warm water lapping his ankles and did the work.

That was the thing about questions—you couldn't unask them, and once asked, they demanded answers, and answers demanded structure, and structure demanded that a disgraced priest with salt-healed ribs and no god sit alone in the dark and *think*.

The founding oath was blank. False. Never kept.

The Long Tide was therefore not bound but bribed, and the bribe was failing, and the bribe was souls.

A swordspeaker's word, spoken truly, unmakes falsehood. Names the lie, and the lie dies, and everything resting on the lie falls with it.

Everything, he thought. The nobles' water-breathing. The priests' authority. The vault's sanctity. The Guard's commissions. Nine thousand identities, dissolved in an afternoon. Maren was right about that part. It would be a drowning in reverse—a whole civilization pulled up into air it had forgotten how to breathe.

But the alternative was the Long Tide awake, and Cassian had heard it through the floor, and he knew with a certainty that bypassed theology entirely: nothing that hungry should be allowed to finish sleeping lightly enough to wake.

So the word had to be spoken. And it could not be a promise—the founders' error, promising the Tide something, trading one oath for another, building a new lie atop the old. No. The word had to be a *naming*. Simple. True. Unarguable.

*The bargain was never kept.*

Five words. He counted them twice, the way he'd once counted the beats of the litany. Five words, and the whole rotten architecture comes down.

The trouble—the enormous, glaring, catastrophic trouble—was what it would cost *her*. He'd seen the frost-scars climbing her tongue after three riders. One word, and her mouth bled cold. What would five words do? What would the *truest* word she'd ever speak do, spoken at the heart of the thing her whole life had been stolen to serve?

It might burn the gift out of her entirely. It might burn more than the gift.

And she would have to choose it. Freely. That was the other rule of swordspeech, the one the archives whispered and the kingdoms learned too late: a true word coerced was just another lie. She had to walk to that drowned throne with her eyes open, holding her own soul in her hands, and *choose*.

"Well," Cassian said aloud, to the cell, to the water, to the vast slow heartbeat pulsing up through the stone. "That's settled, then. All I have to do is escape, find her, convince her, and get her to the throne room before tomorrow's apocalypse. Easy. Practically a morning's errand."

The cell, naturally, did not answer. But far above, muffled through fathoms of stone and water, he heard a sound that froze him colder than any frost-scar ever could.

Singing brine. The alarm.

And under it, faint but unmistakable, carried on currents older than the court itself: the ring of steel, the shout of wardens, and—one voice, cutting through the water like a blade through silk, saying something short and true and lethal—

Lior. Somewhere above him, Lior Oyelaran had stopped hiding.

Cassian got to his feet, pressed both palms against the cell door, and grinned like a man watching a storm come in over a burning ship.

"There you are," he said. "Now we're getting somewhere."
