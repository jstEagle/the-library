## 19. The Drowned Throne

The water came for the throne room the way grief comes—slowly at first, so you could pretend it wasn't happening, and then all at once, everywhere, undeniable.

Lior felt it before she saw it: a pressure change in her ears, a shiver running up through the flooded galleries behind her like a struck bell. The Long Tide was waking. She had seen it stir in Maren's rite—a vast pale shape coiled beneath the mountain's roots, drinking souls the way a drunkard drinks wine, without tasting, without stopping—and she had run from it with Cassian's blood on her hands and Vess's smugglers holding the ninth door open somewhere far above.

She was not running anymore. She was climbing. The drowned throne sat at the top of Mount Serein's hollow heart, up a grand stair that had once been dry marble and was now a waterfall in reverse, water pouring *upward* toward the court above, as if the mountain itself were inhaling.

"Tell me again," Vess gasped beside her, hauling herself up a broken balustrade with the grim competence of a woman who had climbed worse things for less money, "why we couldn't have taken the *servants'* stairs."

"Because the servants' stairs are guarded."

"Everything is guarded! That's rather the point of guards!"

"Then we go where the guards aren't expecting us to be stupid enough to go." Lior pulled her up onto the landing. "You wanted adventure when you were young. You told me. Drunk, at midwinter, but you told me."

"I said I wanted *money*," Vess said. "Adventure is what happens when the money goes wrong. This—" she gestured at the impossible waterfall, the singing brine still echoing through the halls where Lior had tripped it hours ago, "—this is money going extremely wrong."

Behind them, three of Vess's smugglers held the stair's mouth—Bram with his crossbow, the twins Ferren and Odile with knives and the particular serenity of people who had already made peace with their odds. Somewhere below, cut off when the water rose, Cassian was either dead or captured or clever, and Lior could not afford to know which. Not yet. Grief was a luxury for people with time, and the mountain was spending hers faster than she could earn it back.

"The throne room is through there." She pointed at doors tall enough to admit a ship's mast, carved with waves that seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at them. "Maren will be there. The Guard will be there. My mother may be there."

"And your soul?"

Lior touched the bottle at her throat. It was warm. It had been warm since the vault, since the night she'd nearly shattered it against a pillar and the whispering thing inside had shown her—not memories of a life stolen, but memories of a life *waiting*. A girl who spoke without fear. A woman who laughed with her whole chest. A self assembled and shelved, patient as salt.

"It's coming home," Lior said. "One way or another."

* * *

The doors opened at a word.

Not a cutting-word—she'd learned that lesson bleeding frost-scars into her own tongue—but a true one, spoken soft: *open*, in the old swordspeech, the language her mother had taught her in fragments and then forbidden her to use whole. The doors remembered Oyelarans. Someone had expected her. Someone had built this entire drowning cathedral around the shape of her arrival, and Lior was done pretending that didn't frighten her.

The throne room was a lie told in beautiful architecture.

It should have been underwater—it *was* underwater, black seawater standing waist-deep across a floor of white coral tile—but it burned with light, hundreds of lamps floating unmoored on the surface like a sky turned upside down. Nobles of the drowned court lined the walls, breathing water with the ease of long habit, their gills fluttering silver at their throats, their faces the kind of beautiful that makes you check your purse. Tide-priests stood in ranks before the throne, robes heavy with wet, chanting in a low tide-rhythm that Lior felt in her back teeth.

And on the dais, beneath a canopy of woven kelp and bone, sat the Drowned Throne itself—driftwood and pearl and the ribs of something enormous—and upon it, High Priestess Maren, hands folded, waiting.

"You came by the front door," Maren said. Her voice carried over the water without effort. "I confess I hoped you would. There's a certain honesty in it."

"I've been told I lack subtlety." Lior waded forward, sword drawn, though they both knew the sword was decoration. Her real weapon hung in the air between her teeth. "Where is she?"

"Your mother?" Maren tilted her head, and the lamplight moved on her face like light on the sea, which is to say it showed everything and revealed nothing. "Nearby. Watching. Mothers usually are, in the end—even the ones who sold their daughters' souls at a discount."

"It wasn't a discount. It was the market rate." The voice came from the left rank of priests, and then Sera Oyelaran stepped out of it, hood back, gray-streaked hair plastered to her skull, and ten years fell away and landed on Lior all at once. "Hello, little wave."

"Don't." Lior's voice cracked on the word, and she hated it, hated that the first word she gave her mother after a decade was a refusal. "Don't call me that. Don't stand there like you're proud of me. You don't get to be proud."

"No," her mother agreed quietly. "I don't suppose I do."

"Hollow Guard!" Maren's voice rang out, bright as a bell over the chant. "The swordspeaker is to be taken alive. The vessel must not be damaged. Everything else—" her gaze slid past Lior to Vess, to the smugglers at the doors, and her smile was gentle as drowning, "—is negotiable."

They came out of the water.

Lior had fought the Hollow Guard twice before, in the passes, in the dark, and both times she had won by being smaller and meaner and more desperate than they were. But here, in their own element, they were terrible and graceful, moving through the flood like herons through reeds, and there were thirty of them, and the water slowed every step she took.

So she stopped walking and started speaking.

"*Break.*"

The word left her mouth and became a wall of force that took the first four riders off their feet and cracked the coral tile beneath them. Frost bloomed across her tongue; she tasted copper and winter. The second word—"*shatter*"—caught two more mid-lunge and dropped them screaming, their hollow armor ringing like dropped pots. Vess was shouting something behind her, Bram's crossbow thrumming, and Lior waded into the gap she'd made because there was no other direction that led anywhere but down.

Her mother fought beside her.

That was the part Lior hadn't prepared for. Sera Oyelaran moved through the Guard like a rumor, using no words at all—just a knife and twenty years of knowing exactly where bodies were weakest—and when a rider's blade came for Lior's exposed back, her mother took it on her forearm and broke the man's wrist for his trouble.

"I don't need your help!" Lior snarled.

"No," her mother said, parrying, bleeding, "but you're getting it anyway. Consider it interest on what I owe."

"I considered it! For ten years! The interest is *catastrophic!*"

Despite everything—the water, the wounds, the chanting priests and the waking thing below them shaking dust from the ceiling—her mother laughed. It was a terrible, wonderful sound, and Lior felt it land somewhere under her ribs and stay there.

Then the tide-priests changed their song, and the water rose.

* * *

It happened between one verse and the next. The chant deepened, found a note that lived in the floor, and the flood answered—waist-deep becoming chest-deep becoming a rising black wall, and through it Lior heard the Long Tide turning over in its sleep far below, a sound like a continent clearing its throat. The nobles along the walls began to smile. They knew what was coming. They had bought tickets.

"Lior." Her mother grabbed her arm, and for the first time since the vault, since the confession, since *you were the price of my mercy*, her voice shook. "The throne. Maren sits the throne, but the bargain lives in the throne—that's where the founding oath is kept, the false one, the one Cassian figured out. Get to the throne and everything else can burn."

"And you?"

Her mother smiled, and it was the smile from Lior's earliest memory, the one from before the oaths, before the selling, from a kitchen in a house that no longer existed, flour on her hands and morning in her hair.

"I'm going to hold the door," she said. "Someone has to. And I have ten years of apology to work off, and only one way to pay it."

"Mother—"

"Sera," she corrected gently. "You stopped calling me Mother years ago. I'm asking you to stop being right about why." She pressed something into Lior's palm—small, salt-worn, a whistle carved from whalebone that Lior had lost when she was nine and had mourned for a year. Her mother had kept it. All this time, in all this dark, she had kept it. "Go be the thing I was too afraid to let you be."

Then Sera Oyelaran turned to face the Hollow Guard pouring through the eastern arches, drew her knife, and spoke her daughter's name like an oath:

"*Lior.*"

And the word detonated.

Lior understood, in the half-second before the shockwave threw her bodily toward the dais, that her mother had spent decades hoarding swordspeech the way misers hoard coin—one word saved, banked, buried—for exactly this. The blast tore through the Guard's first rank, blew the chanting priests off their feet, and hurled Lior up the last steps of the dais in a spray of black water, where she landed on her knees before the Drowned Throne with her ears ringing and her heart breaking in a rhythm too fast to count.

Behind her, her mother stood alone in the ruined doorway, knife low, blood threading pink through the water around her feet, facing thirty riders and smiling.

"Maren!" Sera called out cheerfully, in the tone of a woman greeting an old rival at a funeral. "You'll want to watch this part!"

Lior wanted to scream. She wanted to turn around. She wanted ten years back and a different mother and a kitchen that still existed. Instead she gripped the whalebone whistle until its edges bit her palm, turned to the throne, and put her hand on the driftwood armrest.

The false oath was there, just as Cassian had said—a knot of old lies braided into the wood, humming, *we kept our word, we kept our word, we kept our word*, the founding bargain that fed the Long Tide, that filled the bottles, that drowned a court in comfort and called it grace. Beneath her palm it pulsed like a second heartbeat.

Above her, Maren rose from the throne, water streaming from her robes, and looked down with something almost like pity.

"You can't speak it yet," the priestess said. "Your soul isn't bound to you. Your words are borrowed, girl—they'll crack you before they crack us. Give me the bottle. Take your mother. Walk out. Be *kind* to yourself, for once in your life."

Below, her mother's blade caught lamplight. The Guard closed in. The Long Tide turned beneath them, closer now, hungry now, and the water at Lior's waist trembled with its breathing.

Lior looked at the bottle in her hand. Warm. Waiting. Whispering in a voice that was hers and not hers: *now, little wave, now, while the door is held.*

She lifted it toward her lips—

—and the throne spoke first.

"Not yet," said the driftwood, in her mother's voice, in her own voice, in a third voice older than both, and the false oath tightened around her wrist like a hand. "The bargain isn't broken. The bargain was never *kept*—and the one who proves it must say so with everything she has."

The water rose past her chin. Behind her, her mother screamed—not in pain, Lior realized with a horror beyond horror, but in effort, holding a door that was closing—and Lior understood that whatever she was about to do, she would do it drowning, and she would do it now.

She opened her mouth to speak.
