## 20. Swordspeech

The water was rising and the water was singing.

Lior felt both at once as she waded through the drowned throne hall, her boots gone, her sword-arm burning, the bottle of her own soul clutched against her chest like a second heart. The Long Tide stirred beneath the floor of the world—a vast slow turning, like something rolling over in its sleep—and every soul Maren had poured into it glimmered up through the floodwater in pale threads, hundreds of them, thousands, all draining toward the dark.

Behind her, her mother held the door.

Lior did not look back. She had looked back once already, on the stairs, and seen Sera Oyelaran standing in the breach with her hands raised and frost crawling up her arms from words she was spending like a woman spending her last coins, and she had made herself turn away, because looking back twice would have killed them both.

*Hold,* her mother had said. Not *I love you.* Not *forgive me.* Just *hold*, in that flat command voice Lior had obeyed for seventeen years before running from it for ten more.

So Lior held. She waded forward.

The throne rose out of the flood like a whale's ribcage dressed in coral—white bone arches crusted with pearl, hung with banners that had been underwater so long they had become gardens. Anemones bloomed along the armrests. Small silver fish threaded through the crown that sat waiting on the seat, patient as a spider.

And on the steps below it, waist-deep now, stood High Priestess Maren, and she was smiling.

"You're too late," Maren said, and her voice carried strangely through the water, arriving at Lior's ears before the sound should have traveled, arriving almost before she spoke it. "The Tide has fed. It has tasted four hundred souls tonight. Do you know what wakes when it has tasted enough?"

"I've met your god," Lior said. "It snores."

Something flickered across Maren's beautiful face—not anger. Amusement, briefly, and then grief, which was worse. "Child. I was you once. A girl with a gift too large for her body, told by everyone she loved to make herself small. The Tide doesn't ask you to be small. It asks only that you pour."

"It asks everything," Lior said. "That's what pouring is."

She kept walking. The water reached her ribs. Her stolen soul—the bottle pressed against her sternum—was warm now, warmer than blood, and it whispered. It had been whispering since the vault, but here, this close to the throne, the whisper had changed. It wasn't her mother's voice anymore.

It was hers.

*Lior,* said the thing in the bottle, in Lior's own voice, from a throat she'd never owned. *You're limping. You always limp on the left when you're frightened. I remember being frightened in places you've never been.*

"Stop," Lior muttered.

*I remember snow in a country with no name. I remember laughing—I think it was laughing—in the dark under the world while they kept me like wine. Ten years, Lior. You got the roads. I got the cellar. Which of us do you think is the ghost?*

Maren raised her hands, and the water rose with them.

Lior had time to notice three things: the tide-priests emerging from the galleries with their coral staves; the thread of light from Cassian's direction—he was alive, he was free, he was shouting something she couldn't hear over the roar of the rising flood; and the bottle cracking, hairline-fine, against her chest, because souls want their bodies the way rivers want the sea, and hers had finally stopped asking politely.

Then the wave came down and the world went white and wet and silent, and Lior Oyelaran drowned for exactly as long as it took her soul to decide it was done waiting.

---

She woke kneeling on dry stone.

Not the flooded hall—somewhere else, or somewhere deeper, or somewhere inside. A plain of salt stretched to every horizon under a sky the color of old pewter, and the silence was enormous, and she was whole in a way she had not been since before she could remember being anything.

Her soul stood in front of her.

It looked like her. Of course it did. But it looked like her at rest—shoulders down, hands open, no limp, no flinch, wearing an expression Lior's face had forgotten how to make. Ten years in a bottle and it had come out gentler than her. That seemed unbearably unfair.

"You sold me," the soul said. No anger in it. Curiosity, mostly, and underneath the curiosity, a patience like bedrock.

"Our mother sold you. I was six."

"I know. I watched from inside the glass." The soul tilted its head. "You've spent ten years hating her for it. I've spent ten years understanding it. Would you like to trade notes?"

"I'd like to go home," Lior said, and hated how small it came out.

"This is home," the soul said gently. "This has always been home. Every word you ever cut with, every oath you ever bound—you were reaching for me across the glass. That's why the words cost you frost. That's why they scarred. Half a voice can't speak true; it can only wound." She held out her hand. "Come be whole. It doesn't hurt. I checked."

Lior looked at the offered hand for a long moment.

"What happens after?" she asked. "When I take it. When I'm whole. What happens to the gift?"

The soul smiled, and it was Lior's own smile, and it was kind. "You'll be able to speak one word that cannot be untrue. And then—" She shrugged, delicately. "Everything spends itself eventually. Even us. Especially us."

"And if I don't take it? If I walk away and leave you here?"

"Then the mountain drowns, the Long Tide wakes, and four hundred souls feed something older than winter forever. Also," the soul added, "you'd be leaving me, and I have been *so bored*, Lior. Bottles are very quiet. I've composed eleven thousand poems. They're terrible. I need you to take me back so I can stop."

Despite everything—the flood, the dying, her mother holding a door with her bare hands—Lior laughed. It came out cracked and half-drowned and real.

"That's the worst recruitment speech I've ever heard."

"It's the only one I had," said her soul. "Take my hand. We have a lie to unmake."

Lior took it.

---

The joining did not hurt. That was the strangest part. It felt like stepping into a bath drawn to exactly the temperature of her own blood; like a word she'd been mispronouncing her entire life suddenly spoken correctly; like coming home to a house she'd forgotten she owned and finding the fire lit and someone humming in the kitchen.

She opened her eyes underwater, in the ruined throne hall, and she was not drowning, because she was complete, and complete things decide for themselves what they are.

The water around her was full of light. Maren stood ten feet away, soaked to the bone, her coral staff shattered, staring.

"What did you do," the priestess whispered.

"I went and got myself," Lior said.

And oh, her voice. Her voice filled the hall like a bell fills a tower. Every syllable arrived everywhere at once. The tide-priests in the galleries dropped their staves and covered their ears—not because it was loud, but because it was *true*, and truth, in a court built on a founding lie, lands like a blade lands on a rotten plank.

Cassian was hauling himself onto the lowest step of the dais, gray-faced, one eye swelling shut, and he saw her and his mouth shaped her name without sound. Behind him, past the broken doors, her mother knelt in the wreckage of the Guard she had held back alone, frost-white to the elbows, still breathing. Barely. Still breathing.

"Lior." Maren's composure cracked down the middle. "Listen to me. The bargain can't be undone. It was sealed before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. The court gave its oaths to the deep, the deep gave the court its breath beneath the water, and what is sworn beneath the water—"

"—cannot be unsworn," Lior finished. "Yes. I know the doctrine. Cassian taught me the doctrine. He taught it to me over stew, actually, in a tavern that smelled like wet dog, and he cried a little, and I pretended not to notice." She began to walk toward the throne, and the water parted around her ankles, because it knew whose court this really was. "But here's what the doctrine leaves out, High Priestess. Here's what none of you ever say out loud, because saying it out loud would end everything."

She climbed the steps. The silver fish scattered from the crown. The Long Tide turned beneath her, vast and sleepy and nearly fed, and she felt it the way you feel a storm two valleys over.

"A bargain is only as strong as its keeping," Lior said. "You've told yourselves for nine generations that the founders swore the deep their service. That the first tide-priests knelt on this stone and gave their word and the word was good." She reached the throne. She did not sit in it. She put one hand on its cold coral arm instead, the way you steady yourself against a friend's shoulder. "But I am a swordspeaker. Whole, now, for the first time in my life. And I have read the memory in this stone, and I have read the memory in the salt, and I have read the memory in *you*—because you were there, Maren, weren't you? At the last renewal. You renewed the bargain yourself, forty years ago, and you knew, even then."

Maren had gone the color of the pewter sky. "Don't."

"The founders never swore," Lior said. "They *lied*. They promised the deep their service with fingers crossed behind their backs, because they wanted to breathe underwater more than they wanted to keep their word, and the deep—old and slow and trusting as winter—is still waiting for a promise that was never honestly made. Everything this court is stands on a lie told before the mountain had a name. And a lie, spoken aloud by a voice that cannot speak falsely—"

"Don't," Maren said again, and now she was begging, and now Lior understood that the begging was not for the court. It was for the priestess. For the girl Maren had been, the one who had poured herself into the Tide believing it was holy. "If you say it, I am nothing. We are all nothing. Four hundred years of—"

"Yes," Lior said softly. "I know. I'm sorry. Truly. But you built your cathedral on my family's theft, and I have come to collect the rent."

She turned to face the hall—to face the flooding galleries, the freed prisoners, Cassian on the steps with his broken face and his stubborn hope, her mother kneeling in the doorway with her frost-burned hands folded like a woman at prayer. She took a breath. The last breath she would ever take as a swordspeaker, though she didn't know that yet, or maybe, in the wholeness of her, she knew it perfectly and chose it anyway.

She spoke one word.

Not a cutting-word. Not an oath-word. Not any of the beautiful weapons her mother had taught her, the words that opened locks and closed wounds and turned rockslides aside. This was simpler than all of those. This was the first word a child learns to say when it learns that other people can be wrong.

"*Never.*"

The word went out of her like her life going out of her, and it was both.

It touched the water and the water remembered it was only water. It touched the oaths and the oaths came apart like wet paper, nine generations of false service dissolving in a single honest syllable. It touched the Long Tide, and the Long Tide—that vast slow turning, that hunger older than winter—did not scream. It sighed. It sighed the way a liar sighs when the truth finally finds him, relieved, almost grateful, and it settled back down into the roots of the mountain and began, very slowly, to sleep the way ordinary stone sleeps: dreamlessly, and forever.

The court's power left it the way color leaves a face.

The nobles in the galleries gasped—and breathed air, raggedly, humanly, for the first time in centuries. The pearls on the throne went dull. The bottled souls on the auction shelves, all through the vaults, popped their corks one after another in a long rippling applause, and light went up through the floodwater like reversed rain.

And in Lior's chest, where the gift had lived since before she had words for it, something burned down to embers, and then to ash, and then to nothing at all.

She gripped the throne to stay upright. The silence in her head was total. All her life there had been the hum of it, the pressure of unsaid words behind her teeth like a second set of teeth—and now, nothing. Just a mouth. Just a woman, standing in seawater, in a room full of strangers who used to be gods.

"Oh," she said, in a completely ordinary voice, and started to laugh, and then to cry, and did both at once, badly, gripping a dead throne in a drowning hall.

Footsteps. Splashing. Then arms around her—Cassian, filthy and bleeding and weeping openly, holding her up the way you hold up a wall that has decided, after everything, to remain a wall.

"You did it," he kept saying into her shoulder. "You impossible, insubordinate, magnificent woman, you actually—"

"Cassian." Her voice was just a voice now. It shook anyway. "My mother. The door. Is she—"

They got to her together, wading, stumbling. Sera Oyelaran lay where the Guard had fallen, frost melting off her in the warming water, her breath shallow as a tide going out. Her eyes found Lior's and focused with effort, and for the first time in Lior's entire memory, her mother smiled without a single order hidden behind it.

"There you are," Sera whispered. "Whole. I knew you would—find a way to be—" Her hand rose, trembling, and touched Lior's cheek, and left a smear of cold that was already fading. "I'm sorry costs nothing now. Isn't that strange. All those years I thought sorry would cost me everything, and now it's free, and I don't have time to spend it properly."

"You have time," Lior said fiercely. "Mother. You have time. We're getting out of this mountain, all of us, and you're going to learn to say it slowly, over years, until I'm sick of hearing it. That's an order."

Sera laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and the laugh cost her, and her eyes drifted, and above them, far above, through miles of stone and water, the first thunder of the blizzard sealing the passes rolled down the mountain like a door closing.

Lior looked up at the sound. So did Cassian. Around them, the freed souls were rising through the flood in their hundreds, streaming upward toward whatever waited beyond the ninth door, and the water was still rising too, and the passes above were filling with snow, and Vess—who was, at this precise moment, somewhere in the dark with forty smugglers and a stolen cart of blasting oil—was about to discover that the door she'd come down to open had just been unmade along with everything else.

"Well," Cassian said weakly. "Any chance your new talent includes swimming?"

"No," said Lior, who had never meant anything more in her life. "But I know a smuggler who owes me a boat."
