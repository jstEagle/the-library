## 21. After the Water

The first thing Cassian felt was cold.

Not the cold of the deep halls—that had been a living cold, a cold with intentions—but the honest, stupid cold of snow on his face, and he nearly wept for the plainness of it. He opened his eyes to a sky the color of old pewter and the taste of pine smoke somewhere far off, and he lay there in a drift up to his ears and laughed until his ribs hurt.

"You're awake," said Vess, who was crouched over him with the expression of a woman deciding whether to slap him or feed him soup. "Good. I owe you a punch and you were making it awkward."

"Please," Cassian croaked. "Punch gently. I've been interrogated by my former mentor in the last two days. My face has opinions about additional violence."

Vess punched him anyway, though gently, as requested—a soft cuff against his shoulder that was, he suspected, the smuggler's equivalent of an embrace. "That's for going down that mountain in the first place."

"And the soup?"

"The soup's for Lior. You get the dregs." She hauled him upright by the collar, and the world swung around him: the mouth of the ninth door behind them, half-buried already, weeping meltwater; the scattered survivors of the drowned court sitting or lying among the rocks like dolls someone had set out and forgotten; and everywhere, everywhere, the light.

The freed souls rose off the snow in slow spirals, hundreds of them, thousands—bottled years and stolen names unwinding upward into the grey morning. They made no sound. They needed none. Each one brightened the air around it briefly, the way a struck match warms a whole room for a moment, and then it thinned and spread and was gone, and the snow where it had rested seemed, absurdly, greener.

Cassian watched one pass close enough to touch. It carried a feeling with it, faint as perfume on a coat sleeve: the smell of bread baking, a memory not his own, offered freely and then withdrawn. His throat closed.

"They're going home," Vess said quietly. "Or wherever home is, now. Lior says some of them were in bottles longer than they'd been alive."

"Lior says." He looked around. "Where is—"

"There." Vess nodded toward the ridge above the door's mouth, where a figure stood apart from the others, small against the enormous sky, watching the souls go up. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she did not brush it away. "She's been standing there since we surfaced. Three hours. I brought her tea twice. Both times she held it until it went cold and then drank it anyway, which tells you everything you need to know about how she's doing."

Cassian climbed.

His legs complained the entire way—the cells beneath the court had taken their toll, and his mentor's parting gift, a broken finger on his left hand, had been splinted by one of Vess's smugglers with a fishbone and a strip of sailcloth, which he chose to find funny rather than humiliating. The climb took longer than it should have. Lior did not turn when he reached her, but she shifted slightly, making room on the rock beside her, which from Lior Oyelaran was roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

They stood together in silence while another soul rose past them, this one trailing the sound—real sound, heard only at the last instant—of a child laughing.

"I keep thinking I should know what to say," Cassian said finally. "I was a priest for twenty years. Rites of mourning, rites of joy, rites for the naming of boats. There is no rite for this."

"No," Lior agreed. Her voice sounded strange to him, and it took him a moment to understand why: it was only a voice now. No weight under it. No frost. Whatever she had spent on the drowned throne, she had spent all of it. "You could try 'it's over.'"

"It's over."

"Say it like you believe it."

He considered. Below them, the last of the court's survivors were being sorted by Vess's people with brisk, unsentimental kindness—the nobles who breathed water huddled in blankets, blinking at air like men newly landed; the tide-priests who had chosen surrender kneeling in a row under the eye of a smuggler girl who couldn't have been older than nineteen and clearly relished the assignment. Somewhere down there, Maren sat bound and silent, staring at nothing, her bargain with the deep dissolved along with everything else she had built.

"It's over," Cassian said again, and this time it came out steady. "The Long Tide is unmade. The founding lie is spoken through. The thing under the mountain—" he paused, testing the truth of it, "—isn't sleeping anymore. It's gone. Whatever it was, whatever it fed on, there's nothing left to feed it. It starved in the space of a single word."

"One true word." Lior almost smiled. "My mother spent my whole childhood teaching me that words were weapons. She never told me the most dangerous one would be *no*."

The grief in it was quiet and bottomless. Cassian had watched her mother die holding the Hollow Guard back in a flooded gallery, sword-words blazing from a throat that hadn't spoken freely in twenty years, and he had watched Lior watch it, and neither of them had had time then for anything but the next step, the next door, the next breath of water. Now the time arrived, as it always does, without asking whether anyone was ready.

"She saved you," he said. "Both things were true at once. That's the worst part. That's the part I can't make a rite for either."

Lior was quiet a long moment. Then she bent, took a handful of snow, and pressed it slowly against her own forehead, the way tide-folk once blessed their dead—an old gesture, older than the court, older than its lies. Clean water. Clean grief.

"She'd have hated this weather," Lior said at last. "She was born on a coast. She used to say mountains were just the sea having a tantrum." A breath that was almost a laugh. "I think I'll take her home. What's left. There's a cliff above Serein harbor where the oath-binders used to stand. It seems right."

"I'll help you carry her."

"You don't have to—"

"Cassian," she said, and then stopped, because he was shaking his head before she finished.

"I'm staying," he said.

She turned to look at him properly for the first time. Her eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and entirely clear. "Staying here? There's nothing here. There's a dead mountain full of flooded rooms and a village three valleys over that thinks this whole range is cursed."

"That's the appeal," Cassian said. "You should know—I've given this considerable thought, mostly while tied to a chair." He held up his splinted hand as evidence. "I spent twenty years serving a church, and then ten more hating it, and both of those were jobs, if you understand me. Positions. I wore the vestments and I said the responses and underneath it I was always waiting for something real to be required of me. And then something real was required of me, and I sold a secret instead, and a woman died because of it, and I've been drowning ever since. Twice, technically. Three times counting yesterday."

"You didn't drown yesterday."

"I came very close. It's the coming-close that counts." He looked out at the dispersing light, the last few souls rising thin and gold into the pewter sky. "Someone has to write down what happened here. Not the court's version—not the beautiful version with the noble drowned lords and the sacred tide. The true one. The harvest, the bottles, the thing under the mountain. Someone has to sit in those flooded halls and copy out every record before the water takes them, and sort the true priests from the liars, and decide—with actual mercy, not the kind Maren practiced—what happens to the ones who surrendered." He shrugged, and winced, because his shoulder had also been in a cell. "I'm defrocked. I'm disgraced. I have exactly one skill set and it turns out to be precisely suited to cleaning up after a catastrophe of faith. If that isn't a calling, I don't know what is."

Lior studied him for a long moment. "You could come back with us. Vess would take you. She pretends she wouldn't, but she punched you gently."

"I know." He smiled. "And maybe someday, when the records are copied and the halls are drained—or filled in properly, whichever the mountain prefers—I'll walk out of these passes and impose myself on your campfire and complain about the wine. But not yet. Redemption that catches the first coach out of town isn't redemption. It's tourism."

"That's very wise."

"I learned it from a priest. Terrible man. Sold secrets."

The laugh escaped her before she could stop it—short, cracked, real—and Cassian counted it among the day's miracles, filed alongside the snow and the surviving and the fact that his legs still worked.

* * *

By midday the camp had organized itself into something resembling order, mostly through the application of Vess, who ran a refugee column the way she ran a smuggling route: with lists, threats, and an underlying current of fierce decency that she would deny under torture.

Cassian found her at the fire, distributing soup from an enormous dented pot with the gravity of a queen bestowing knighthoods.

"Dregs," she said, handing him a bowl.

"I've earned the good soup. I was imprisoned."

"You were imprisoned *stupidly*. Who walks back into a temple that excommunicated him?"

"A man with a plan."

"You had a *map*. Lior had a map. You had a face the Guard knew." She jabbed the ladle at him. "You're lucky you're useful with paperwork, or I'd have left you in the cells on principle."

He sat down heavily on a crate beside the fire, and for a while neither of them spoke, which was its own kind of conversation. Around them the camp murmured: a water-breathing nobleman discovering, with visible wonder, that snow melted on his tongue; two former acolytes arguing softly over whether they were still priests now that their god-thing had starved; the nineteen-year-old guard feeding broth to Maren herself, expressionlessly, because someone had to and cruelty was a luxury the living couldn't afford.

"Vess." He turned the bowl in his hands. "The night you came down the mountain. Through the collapsed pass, behind the Guard, with—how many was it?"

"Eleven. Twelve counting the mule, and the mule earned his place."

"Why?"

She didn't answer immediately. She stirred the pot. Steam rose between them.

"Because eight years ago a swordspeaker walked into my camp with a stolen horse and a forged bill of sale so bad it was practically a confession, and instead of turning her in, I taught her the trade," Vess said. "And for eight years she never once used what she could do on me. Never lied to me with it. Never even let me see it, really. Do you know what that's worth, in my line of work? A person who could cut you open with a word and chooses, every single day, not to?" She shrugged. "You don't leave that on a mountain."

"No," Cassian said. "You don't."

"Also the map was worth a fortune and I wanted my investment back." She handed him a heel of bread. "But mostly the first thing."

Across the fire, Lior had come down from the ridge at last and was moving through the camp—quietly, as always, but differently now. People turned to watch her pass, and she let them. A water-noble caught her sleeve and pressed something into her hands, bowing; an acolyte simply touched her arm and moved on. She bore it the way she bore everything, straight-backed and unreadable, but when she glanced across the flames and found Cassian watching, she gave him a look that said, plainly: *this is unbearable, rescue me.*

He raised his soup bowl in salute. *No chance. You made this bed. Lie in it.*

Her mouth twitched. It was, he thought, the first almost-smile of the new world, and it felt like spring thaw arriving early.

* * *

Evening came down blue and bitter over the pass. The fires burned high. The last soul rose sometime after dark—a small one, hesitant, circling the camp twice as though memorizing it—and then the sky held nothing but stars, sharp and saltless, and Cassian stood at the edge of the firelight and felt the silence where the Long Tide had been.

It was a good silence. It would take getting used to.

Behind him, boots crunched. Lior joined him at the treeline, wrapped in a smuggler's cloak several sizes too large, and together they looked back the way they had come: the black shoulder of Mount Serein, the buried door, the whole drowned country sleeping under snow at last, honestly, the way snow was meant to.

"The villagers three valleys over," Cassian said. "The ones who think the range is cursed. Someone will have to tell them it isn't. Or rather—" he rubbed his splinted hand absently, "—that it was, and isn't now, and here are four hundred pages of documentation regarding the previous curse, please direct all complaints to my office."

"You're really going to enjoy this, aren't you."

"Immensely. I've already designed a seal." He paused. "It has a lighthouse on it."

"Of course it does."

They stood a moment longer. Then Lior reached into her cloak and drew out a folded square of heavy paper, creased white with handling. He recognized it instantly: the courier's map, the one that had started everything, salt-stained and blood-marked and walked-through-fire. She turned it over in her hands, showing him the blank reverse—the empty side, the side that had never been written on.

"Vess asked me what I'd do now," Lior said. "With no oaths, no power, no name worth hunting. I told her I didn't know." She smoothed the blank paper against her knee. "But I've been thinking. Every road I ever took was drawn by someone else. My mother. The oaths. The map." Her thumb traced the empty margin, slow, deliberate. "This side's never had anything on it. I thought... maybe it was waiting."

Cassian looked at her—at the last of the swordspeakers, powerless and upright, holding a blank page up to the starlight like a window—and felt something in his chest settle that had been unsettled for thirty years.

"Well," he said. "For what it's worth, I hear the roads east of here are terrible. Unmapped. Completely undocumented."

"I know," Lior said, and this time the smile came all the way. "Isn't it wonderful?"
