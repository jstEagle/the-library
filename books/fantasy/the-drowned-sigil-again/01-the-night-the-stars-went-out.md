## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The god had been silent for ten years, and Ines Sandoval had learned to patrol for it anyway.

She walked the valley's northern rim as she had walked it every seventh night since she was nineteen — lantern hooded, boots wrapped in wool to muffle their strike on the old paving stones, her breath pluming white in the cold. The border wards hummed beneath her feet, a vibration so low it was less a sound than a pressure, like standing near a struck bell that never finished ringing. Her mother had called it the god's breathing. The ward-scholars called it residual resonance. Ines had stopped calling it anything at all around the time she'd realized that praying to something that didn't answer was just talking to yourself with better posture.

Still she walked. Not out of faith. Out of habit, and because the valley had nothing else to give her, and because someone had to be awake when the world finally noticed this place.

The stars were wrong tonight.

She'd felt it before she saw it — a thinning in the air, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere far off. She stopped at the boundary stone, the one carved with the sigil worn nearly smooth by ten centuries of rain, and looked up.

The stars over the valley were going out.

Not clouding over. Not dimming behind weather. Going *out*, one by one, in no order she could read — first the bright ones, then the faint, like candles snuffed by an unseen hand moving across the ceiling of the world. She counted without meaning to. Seven. Twelve. Twenty. The dark between them spread like ink dropped in water, and where the sky should have held the Hunter and his dogs, there was nothing. Where the Sisters leaned together over the eastern ridge, nothing. A blackness so complete it seemed less like absence than like something laid over the sky, a lid, a hand pressed flat against the night.

Ines stood very still and discovered that her hands were shaking.

Ten years of silence, and now this. She had imagined a hundred endings for the valley — plague, fire, the armies of some southern king finally remembering they existed — but never this. Never the sky itself closing like an eye.

"Say something," she whispered, to the wards, to the stone, to whatever slept beneath the river. "If you're still in there. Say *something*."

The wards answered.

They screamed.

It came up through her boots and into her teeth, a rising shriek of power that had no business existing in a system this old, this neglected, this *dead*. Light burst from the boundary stones in a chain running north along the ridge — blue-white, then gold, then a color she had no name for, something that hurt to look at directly, that left afterimages burned into her vision even when she turned away. The paving stones under her feet cracked. Frost bloomed up out of summer-warm earth. And above her, visible now through the blacked-out sky as though the darkness itself had been peeled back for the occasion, the wards rose into view: vast interlocking rings of light arcing over the entire valley, a cage seen from inside for the first time in living memory.

A cage. The thought arrived cold and unwelcome. Wards that size weren't built to keep things out.

They were built to keep something in.

The rings shuddered. Held. Shuddered again — and broke, all at once, with a sound like the world's largest bell being struck and shattered simultaneously. The shockwave knocked Ines off her feet. She landed hard on her shoulder among the frost-killed grass and lay there, ears ringing, watching the last fragments of light drift down like luminous snow and gutter out against the stones.

Then silence. Real silence, deeper than any she'd known. The hum beneath the earth was gone. For the first time in her life, the valley was just a valley — dirt and rock and river, ordinary, unguarded, alone under a starless sky.

And Ines Sandoval, who had spent a decade telling herself she stayed out of duty, felt something rise in her chest that she refused to name.

Relief.

*It's over,* said the part of her she didn't like listening to. *Whatever this is, it's not your watch anymore. The prison door is open. You're free.*

"Shut up," she told herself aloud, and got up, and checked her blade out of pure reflex, because relief that arrives on the heels of an apocalypse is usually a lie.

* * *

She smelled blood before she saw the man.

He came stumbling down the old pilgrim road from the north, out of the dark where the stars used to be, moving with the lurching rhythm of someone who has forgotten how legs work and is relearning them one fall at a time. He went down twice between the tree line and the boundary stone, and both times he got up again, which told Ines more about him than his face did. His face told her the rest: forty or fifty, gaunt, gray-skinned in the way of men who have lost too much blood to be afraid of anything anymore. His fine riding coat was scorched black down one side. Beneath the scorch, she could see the embroidery of a royal house — though whose, she couldn't say, and the fact that she couldn't say frightened her more than the wounds did. She'd grown up on heraldry. Everyone in the valley had. It was practically scripture here.

"Stop there," she said, sword drawn, because ritual mattered even at the end of the world.

He stopped. He swayed. And then he smiled at her, a terrible, grateful smile, and held out his hands as if offering her something precious.

"You're real," he rasped. "Gods be thanked. I was afraid I'd dreamed the whole — " He coughed, and the cough was wet and wrong. "The valley. The wards. They said the wards would hold until — "

"The wards are broken." She kept her voice flat. "Who are you? How did you get past the perimeter? Nothing crosses the wards."

"Nothing did." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; he folded forward, caught himself on the boundary stone, right on the worn sigil, and Ines watched his fingers curl around it like a drowning man around flotsam. "That's rather the point. We didn't cross them. We *broke* them. Or — no. No, that's not — " His brow furrowed, and for a moment he looked less like a dying courier and more like a scholar losing his place in a lecture. "We followed the crack. There was a crack, in the crown-ward, and the king — "

He stopped. The change that came over him was instantaneous and awful, like a lamp being blown out. He stared at her with sudden, total clarity.

"You're a warden's daughter," he said. "I can see it. The way you stand. The way you hold the blade low. Oh, thank every god there ever was, even the dead ones, even *him* — " He fumbled at his chest, at a cord around his neck, and dragged something out from beneath the ruined coat. "Take it. Take it before I — it has to go deeper into the valley. It pulls. You'll feel it pull. Don't let the soldiers take it. Don't let anyone — "

"What is it?"

He pressed it into her hands.

Silver. That was her first impression — silver, heavy, warm as a fever despite the frost-nipped air. Her second impression was that it was *wrong*, the way a snapped bone is wrong, the way a word cut off mid-syllable is wrong. It was a sigil, a great disc of worked silver the size of her two palms together, and it had been broken — not cracked, not dented, but *broken*, sheared clean through in four places, its pieces held together by the cord and by some stubborn remnant of purpose. The design on its face showed a figure standing in a river, arms raised, holding up a sky full of stars.

Her god's sigil. The Drowned One, the Silent Brother, the god beneath the river who hadn't spoken in ten years. Every shrine in the valley bore this image. Every one of them showed it whole.

Except this one wasn't a shrine copy. This was old. This was *original* — old the way the boundary stones were old, old the way the wards had been old. And it was pulling at her, exactly as the stranger had promised, a gentle insistent tug behind her sternum, pointing south, toward the river, toward the deep places.

"Where did you get this?" she breathed.

"He gave it to me." The man slid down the boundary stone until he was sitting in the frost-killed grass, and the smile came back, softer now. "He couldn't carry it himself. He can't carry anything himself, not anymore, not for — how long has it been? Ten years? He lost count. He wants you to know he's sorry about the silence. He wants you to know he was *listening* the whole time." His eyes were glazing, but his grip on her wrist tightened with surprising strength. "Listen to me. Listen. The king is not the king. The thing wearing the crown locked him away inside his own body, and it locked the god in with him, and the wards — the wards were never protecting the valley, girl, the wards were the *lock*. And tonight the lock started to fail, and when it fails completely — "

"When it fails completely, what?"

But the man was fading. She could see it happening, the light going out of him the way the stars had gone out of the sky, orderly and unstoppable. She knelt, pressed her hands against the worst of the burns, knowing it was useless, hating that it was useless.

"Name," she said. "Give me a name. Who do I tell? Who sent you?"

His lips moved. Blood on his teeth. The words came out in pieces, spaced by breaths that took more than they gave:

"Aldric. Tell them — Aldric is still — "

"Aldric," she repeated. "King Aldric? The king is *dead*. He's been dead for — "

"No." Fierce, suddenly, a spark of the old urgency. "No, he's *in there*. He's been in there the whole time. Screaming. Ten years of screaming, and only the god could hear him, and the god couldn't answer, and now — " His hand found hers and squeezed, once, with everything he had left. "Now neither of them can scream anymore. Something worse is coming to listen."

He died between one breath and the next, quietly, almost politely, his head tipping back against the boundary stone. His eyes stayed open, fixed on the black sky, and Ines found she couldn't look away from them, and couldn't close them either, not yet.

The sigil pulled at her sternum. South. Toward the river.

Above her, in the starless dark, something moved — or seemed to. A shape crossing the place where the moon should have been, vast and slow, there and then not there. Far to the north, faint as memory, she heard horns. Many horns. An army's worth of horns, coming down out of a country that no longer had any stars to steer by.

Ines Sandoval sat in the frost beside a dead man, holding a broken god in her hands, and understood with perfect clarity that her exile was over, and that she had wanted it to be, and that wanting it made her complicit in whatever came next.

"All right," she said, to the corpse, to the sigil, to the silent river waiting in the dark below. "All right. I'm coming."

She stood, slung the silver across her back, and began to walk south into a valley that was no longer a prison — or no longer only a prison — while behind her, patient as winter, the horns grew louder.
