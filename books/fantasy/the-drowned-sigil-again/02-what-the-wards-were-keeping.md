## 2. What the Wards Were Keeping

Odile had spent thirty years learning the grammar of wards the way other scholars learned scripture, and in all that time she had never once been afraid of her own work. Fear was for soldiers and farmers, for people who lived at the mercy of things they could not read. She could read anything. That had been the whole point of her life.

She stood now in the wreckage of the border stones with ash on her sleeves and a dead man's blood drying under her fingernails, and she understood, finally, what it meant to be illiterate.

The stranger had died an hour after dawn, in Ines Sandoval's arms, whispering a name over and over like a coin he couldn't stop spending. *Aldric. Aldric.* Then his chest had gone still, and the shattered silver thing he'd carried — half a sigil, its broken edge still glowing faintly like a coal raked from a fire — had rolled from his fingers into the mud.

Odile had knelt beside him because no one else would, and because a dying man's last word was data, and data was the only prayer she knew.

Now she worked.

* * *

The ward-stones had not exploded. That was the first wrongness, and it took her two hours of circling the border to admit it. She had assumed shattering — some external force striking the valley's defenses, magic hurled against magic until the lattice gave way. It was the obvious theory. It was the theory every refugee stumbling down the mountain road was already telling each other: *the wards broke, something got in.*

But the stones hadn't broken. They had *opened*.

She found the first one intact enough to study behind the old waystation, half-toppled into a bramble. The sigil-work carved into its face was familiar — she had copied these very glyphs as a girl, on her first field expedition, ink-stained and triumphant. The Valley Wards. The great work. Three centuries of scholarship built on their interpretation.

Her master, old Vessia, had taught her the canonical reading in her first year: *a circle of exclusion*. The glyphs of holding arranged outward-facing, the valley sealed against the wild magic of the world beyond. Keep the sorcery out. Keep the valley safe. Every text agreed. Every commentary, every gloss, every marginal note by six generations of ward-scholars agreed.

Odile pressed her palm flat against the stone and read it again, slowly, the way you reread a sentence that has stopped making sense.

The glyphs of holding were not outward-facing.

They were inward-facing. They had always been inward-facing. She checked three more stones to be sure, scrambling up the hillside with her skirts torn and her breath ragged, and all three told her the same thing with the mute patience of carved granite: the valley was not a fortress.

It was a cell.

"No," she said aloud, to no one. "No, that's — the orientation is a transcription error. It has to be an error."

But it wasn't. She knew it wasn't, because if it were an error, it would be an error repeated identically across forty miles of border stone, maintained through three restorations, and no restoration crew in history had ever been sloppy in *exactly the same direction*. The wards faced inward because they were built to face inward. The valley's people had lived their whole lives inside a lock and called it a wall.

And the stars had gone out last night, which meant the door had opened, which meant whatever the lock had held —

She sat down hard in the brambles and did not care about the thorns.

* * *

By midday she had carried the stranger's body back to the waystation, laid him out on the long table where customs officers had once inspected grain carts, and begun the only autopsy available to her: the reading of everything he'd touched.

His clothes were court-made. That was the second wrongness. Fine wool, capital dye-works, the cut of a king's household — mended badly, recently, by someone unaccustomed to mending. His boots were palace boots worn down to the sole by walking, an enormous distance, in a straight line. He had come from the capital to the valley, alone, wounded, carrying half a sigil made of star-silver.

Star-silver did not exist anymore. The texts were unanimous: the last of it had gone into the founding of the Valley Wards themselves. Which meant this fragment had been *part of the wards*, once, or part of whatever the wards had been forged to hold.

Odile turned the broken sigil in the grey light and felt her certainty crack another degree.

There were inscriptions on it. Microscopic, spiraling, the kind of work done by light rather than chisel. She fetched her lens from her pack with hands that would not quite steady, and read.

It was a binding formula. Not a ward — she knew wards, she had *invented* two of them — but a binding, the older and crueler cousin. Wards kept things out. Bindings kept things *in*, folded small, folded silent, folded asleep. And this one was addressed not to a demon, not to a spirit of storm or plague, but to something the inscription named only by function:

*the voice beneath the crown.*

She set the sigil down. Her fingers were cold. The waystation was cold. Everything was cold, suddenly, the whole world a draft through a broken window.

Because there was a crown. There was exactly one crown that mattered, and it sat on the head of King Aldric, who had ruled for eleven years, ten of them since the night the god of the valley went silent — since the night Odile's own prayers had stopped being answered, though she had never admitted to praying, because scholars didn't pray, scholars *observed*.

Ten years of silence. Ten years of a king whose portraits grew steadily stranger, whose eyes in the later paintings looked painted-over. Ten years of wars of conquest launched from a throne that had once belonged to a man remembered as gentle.

And ten years of wards facing inward around a valley where everyone believed they were being protected.

"We weren't the treasure," Odile whispered. "We were the *guard*."

The valley had never been sealed to keep magic out. It had been sealed to keep watch — a garrison of ordinary lives arranged around a sleeping prisoner, generations of farmers and shepherds and ward-scholars standing sentry without ever knowing they stood. Her life's work, her master's life's work, three hundred years of reverent scholarship, and the entire discipline had been a jailer's manual written in a language the jailers couldn't read.

She laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, closer to the sound the stranger had made when the light left him.

* * *

Ines Sandoval returned at dusk, smelling of woodsmoke and the road, and stood in the doorway of the waystation looking at the covered body with the expression of a woman deciding whether to ask a question she already dreaded.

"He talked," Odile said, before she could decide. "Before he died. You heard him."

"Aldric." Ines came inside, shut the door against the dark, and leaned on it as if the wind might follow her. "The king's name. I thought he was delirious."

"He wasn't." Odile spread her notes across the table — the glyph transcriptions, the orientation diagrams, the rubbing she'd taken of the sigil's binding formula. Her hands moved with scholarly precision even while everything beneath the precision screamed. It was the only way she could keep working: treat the end of the world as a footnote. "Captain Sandoval, I need you to listen to me carefully, because I am going to say several things I have spent my career proving impossible."

"Scholars say that a lot," Ines said. "Go on."

"The wards didn't fail. They were released. There's a difference — a failure breaks, a release *opens*, and these opened from the inside of the formula, along seams that were built to open. Someone ended them. Deliberately. Last night." She tapped the diagram. "And the wards were never keeping anything out of the valley. Read the orientation. Every stone faces in. We weren't behind a wall, Captain. We were around a cage."

Ines looked at the diagrams for a long moment. Odile watched her — the mercenary was not a scholar, but she was not stupid either, and Odile had learned to respect the speed with which soldiers reached ugly conclusions.

"You're saying the god we've been praying to for three hundred years—"

"Was never listening from outside." Odile's voice stayed level. She was proud of that, in a distant, disconnected way. "It was here. Under us. Asleep. The valley is a prison and we were the bars, and last night someone opened the cell door, and the sky went black because whatever was locked in the dark just walked out into it wearing—"

She stopped. The next words wouldn't come easily, so she made them come anyway, the way she made herself read any painful passage twice.

"Wearing a crown. The sigil calls it *the voice beneath the crown*. And the dying man came here from the capital, from the king's own household, carrying a piece of the original binding." She lifted the star-silver fragment and let the firelight catch its broken edge. "He whispered Aldric's name. Captain — I believe he was trying to tell us where the prisoner is. Or who."

"That's madness," Ines said. But she said it the way people say things when they are already halfway to believing them, and her eyes had gone to the sigil and stayed there.

"It's worse than madness. Madness doesn't have footnotes." Odile pulled the binding rubbing toward her and traced the final line of the formula, the clause she had translated four times hoping to be wrong each time. "This binding was consented to. Do you understand? The grammar requires it. A binding this deep can't be forced — the bound must *agree*, must speak the acceptance themselves. Someone took this god into themselves willingly. Voluntarily. As an act of—" she searched for the word and hated the word when she found it, "—sacrifice. And then something else locked the door behind them both."

"Both?"

"The binder and the bound. If the god sleeps in the crown, then whoever consented sleeps too. Or screams. I don't know which is worse." Odile closed her eyes briefly. Thirty years of certainty lay in pieces around her, and underneath the pieces was a question she had been avoiding since dawn, because it changed everything, because it meant the enemy might be a victim and the victim might be a prison:

*Who woke up?*

When she opened her eyes, Ines was watching her with an expression Odile had never seen on that hard face — something almost like pity, offered awkwardly, like a tool handed handle-first.

"You spent your life on those wards," Ines said. Not a question.

"I spent my life serving a lie and calling it devotion." Odile began stacking her notes with brisk, unnecessary neatness. "So I intend to spend the rest of it correcting the citation. The sigil is half of a key, Captain. Half. Somewhere in this world is the other half, and somewhere beyond that is a king who may be the oldest prisoner in this war. I'm coming with you."

"I haven't asked you to come anywhere."

"You will." Odile tucked the star-silver fragment into its wrapping, and her hands, at last, were steady. "Because whoever broke those wards knew exactly what they were doing, and they'll come looking for the missing half. And when they do, they'll find us — or we'll find them first. Either way, Captain Sandoval, you're going to need someone who can read the dark."

Outside, the starless night pressed against the windows like a face. And far off down the valley road, faint but unmistakable in the silence, Odile heard hoofbeats — many horses, moving fast, riding toward the waystation out of the black.

Ines's hand went to her sword. "Company," she breathed. "Ours or theirs?"

Odile looked at the broken sigil glowing softly in its wrappings, and understood that the answer no longer mattered. The cell was open. Everything in the world was coming to see what had been inside.

"Theirs," she said. "It was always going to be theirs."
