## 3. A Company of Strays

The dead man's sigil weighed less than a knife and cost more than a kingdom. Ines felt it through her pack straps all the way down the mountain road, a cold that had nothing to do with weather, as if she were carrying a shard of winter against her spine.

She'd buried the stranger at dawn, under a cairn of river stones because the ground was too hard for digging and he'd come from the water anyway. She hadn't known his name. He'd died whispering *Aldric*, over and over, like a man calling for someone across a wide dark room.

Ines didn't believe in gods. Ten years of silence from the valley's drowned shrine had cured her of that early and thoroughly. But she believed in silver that hummed, in wards that shattered like glass heard three valleys away, and in the black sky where stars should have been — and all of it pointed downhill, toward the lowlands, toward people.

She hated pointing toward people.

*Keep moving,* she told herself. *Deliver the thing or bury it deeper. Either way, alone.*

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It lasted until noon.

* * *

The refugee column found her before she found it — a brown river of them pouring out of the valley passes, carts and goats and children on shoulders, an old woman carrying a door on her back because apparently even the end of the world wasn't reason to leave a good door behind. They flowed around Ines where she stood at the roadside, and some of them recognized the mercenary grey of her coat, and some of them asked her things.

*Which way is safe? Are the soldiers behind us? Did you see what happened to the sky?*

She answered none of it. She'd stopped being anyone's answer years ago, when the last company she led came home one man short and that man had been her fault by every honest accounting. She'd sworn it at his grave with a flask in her hand: no more leading, no more following, no more people who trusted her face.

Then a boy stepped on her boot.

He was maybe ten, thin as a whip handle, hair the color of river mud, and he looked up at her with eyes so old they made her stomach turn — the eyes of a man who has stood at too many deathbeds, wearing a child's face like an ill-fitting mask.

"You're going down," he said. Not a question.

"Everyone's going somewhere."

"To the capital." He said it with terrible certainty. "You've got something that pulls. I can feel it from here. Like a hook behind my teeth."

Ines's hand drifted to her pack strap. "You're mad."

"I drowned once," the boy said, conversational, horrifying. "Not here. Not this body. I remember the water going into my lungs and the light above me going green, and I remember hands binding my wrists with silver wire while I drowned, and everyone singing like it was a wedding." He tilted his head. "Your silver thing sings the same song. Wrong verse, though. Broken verse."

The cold along her spine went colder. Around them the refugee river kept flowing, oblivious, and the starless noon sky pressed down like a lid.

"What's your name, boy?"

"Tam." Then, after a pause, as if consulting some inner ledger: "It used to be something else. I don't say that one anymore."

"Well, Tam." She crouched to his level, which was a mistake, because up close the oldness in his eyes was worse. "I work alone. Find your mother."

"I don't have one. I have a column, and the column's going east to my aunt's, and my aunt is a story my mother told me so I'd stop crying." His chin lifted. "You're going to need me. You just don't know it yet."

"Everyone needs something. That's not my problem."

"No," Tam agreed placidly. "But I'm going to follow you anyway, and I'm very quiet, and I know which berries kill you. So really you should decide whether you want me in front of you or behind you."

Ines stared at him for a long moment. Somewhere in the column a baby wailed; somewhere else a cart axle screamed. The boy waited with the patience of a stone.

"Behind," she said finally, hating herself with a purity she hadn't managed since the grave-side vow. "And if you slow me down, I tie you to the next fence post."

"That's fair," said Tam, and fell into step behind her like he'd been doing it all his life.

* * *

They made eight miles before the road gave them Bramwell.

He was sitting in the wrecked shell of a tollhouse with a knight's sword across his knees and a deserter's beard on his face, and he had the look of a man deciding between two kinds of dying. When Ines stepped into the doorway with her hand on her blade, he didn't even reach for the sword. That, more than anything, told her what he was.

"Mercenary," he said. His voice was gravel left out in rain. "If you're here to collect the bounty, you'll want my head. The rest of me's no use to anyone."

"I'm not here for you at all." She stayed in the doorway. Behind her, Tam peered around her hip with open, unembarrassed curiosity. "Road's choked with refugees. Why aren't you among them?"

"Because I ran *from* an army, not toward safety." He laughed, short and ugly. "Ser Bramwell Hale. Ninth Lances. Or I was, until four days ago, when my commander told us what we'd be riding to do and I discovered my stomach had opinions my oath couldn't overrule."

"What did he tell you?"

Bramwell looked at her then, really looked, and she saw the calculation: how much truth could this stranger be trusted with. What decided him, she thought later, was Tam — nobody lies elaborately in front of a child.

"He told us the king is awake again," Bramwell said. "Truly awake, after ten years of sleeping through his own reign. And that the first order of the awakened king is fire. Three free cities, burned to ash, as an offering." His jaw worked. "He said it smiling. Commander Veyre has always smiled at the wrong moments. I told myself that for six years. That day I stopped telling myself anything and took my horse north until it died under me."

"The usurper," Ines said quietly. The word had been traveling ahead of her for a decade, attached to rumors: a king who ruled well enough, brilliantly even, but whose eyes in portraits had gone strange, whose edicts had begun to rhyme like scripture. "You think it's true? That Aldric's—"

"I think something wears that crown," Bramwell said, "and it isn't the man who was crowned. I carried his standard for six years. A man doesn't change his handwriting overnight. His does." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Why do you care? Mercenaries don't care."

"This mercenary is carrying something the usurper wants destroyed." She surprised herself by saying it. She'd planned to say nothing. But the sigil's cold was seeping through the pack into her skin, and the boy behind her was humming a broken verse, and the sky overhead was a lid on a pot coming to boil, and suddenly the arithmetic of *alone* refused to balance anymore. "I need to get south to the capital. Fast, quiet, and past outriders who will be looking for exactly what I'm carrying."

"And you want an escort." Bramwell's mouth twisted. "A deserter. There are bounties on my head in three provinces."

"There'll be no heads at all if we don't move. Bounty's a future problem." She held out nothing — no hand, no warmth, just the flat fact of it. "I can't pay you."

"Good." He stood, joints cracking, and slung the sword across his back. "Every coin I ever earned bought something I regret. Consider this charity." He looked down at Tam. "Whose is this?"

"His own," said Tam. "I belong to myself. It's a recent arrangement."

Bramwell blinked. "Gods help us. It talks."

"It remembers drowning," Ines said. "Among other things. Don't ask. Walk."

* * *

They walked.

By the second day she had stopped pretending she wasn't leading them. It happened somewhere on the ridge above the Ashford ford, when outriders appeared on the southern horizon — six lances, black pennons, moving with the unhurried confidence of wolves checking a treeline — and Ines found herself giving orders before her mouth had consulted her vows. *Off the road. Into the alder stand. Tam, you're smallest, crawl up and count them.* And they obeyed. Both of them. Without question, without irony, as if she'd been leading them for years instead of two days.

Six riders became nine by evening. The usurper's army was fanning out across the lowlands like ink dropped in water, and every road south was a throat slowly closing.

They camped that night in a hollow under dead oaks, no fire, sharing dried apples and half a loaf Tam had charmed off a refugee baker two miles back. Bramwell cleaned his sword by starlight that wasn't there — the sky stayed black, a darkness with texture now, and Ines caught both her companions looking up at it with the same expression, the look of people checking whether the world was still nailed down.

"The sigil," Bramwell said quietly. "The thing you're carrying. It's why the stars went out, isn't it? Whatever broke in the valley — it's connected."

"I don't know what it is." True, and insufficient, and she knew it. "A dying man brought it out of the valley. It hums. It pulls south. The boy says it sings."

"I said a broken verse," Tam corrected, mouth full of apple. "It's missing a piece. Something was supposed to be joined to it and got torn away." He swallowed. "When I find what it's missing, I think I'll remember the rest of my drowning. I'm not sure I want to."

Ines looked at him across the dark — this small grave-eyed creature who had appointed himself to her company without asking permission, this deserter sharpening a sword beside him, this broken god-piece humming against her spine — and felt the old vow crack another degree. *No more leading anyone.* She could almost hear her own voice at the graveside, drunk and certain.

"You should both turn back," she said. "Tomorrow there's a fork. East goes to the coast, ships, safety. South goes where I go."

"East is where the army isn't yet," Bramwell said. "'Yet' is doing a lot of work in that sentence. No. I deserted one master. I won't kneel to the next one that comes along. If you're walking at the thing that broke the sky, I'll walk behind you."

"Behind," Tam echoed, pleased. "I called it first."

Ines lay back against her pack and stared up at the starless dark, and the sigil hummed its broken song, and far to the south — so faint she might have imagined it, except Tam sat bolt upright at the same instant, hand pressed to his chest — something answered.

A voice. Not in the ear. Under it. Old as riverbeds, cracked as a bell with a flaw in it, saying a single word that neither of them would repeat aloud:

*Come.*

Tam turned to her in the dark, and his ancient eyes were shining wet.

"It knows I'm coming," he whispered. "It says it remembers me. Ines — it says it's sorry about the drowning."

* * *

They reached the edge of the drowned country at dusk on the fourth day, where the land sank into marsh and the air smelled of green rot and old stone, and the sigil's humming rose to a whine that set Ines's teeth aching. Ahead, half-sunk in black water, a shrine roof broke the surface like a whale's back, carved all over with the same sigil-shape, whole and unbroken, glowing faintly beneath ten feet of fen.

The pull in her pack leaned forward like a dog at the end of a rope.

Tam stood at the marsh edge, small and still, watching the water with the expression of a man looking at his own grave — which, Ines realized with a chill, was exactly what he was doing.

"It's down there," he said. "And it's been waiting for me a long time."
