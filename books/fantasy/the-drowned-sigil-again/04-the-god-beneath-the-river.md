## 4. The God Beneath the River

Tam knew the sigil was pulling them somewhere before anyone else did.

It wasn't a sound exactly. It was more like the feeling of standing near a door in winter — that thin cold breath of air against your ankles, telling you there was another world on the other side. The broken silver piece that Ines carried wrapped in oilcloth hummed against her hip, and Tam could feel it in his teeth, in the scar tissue at his throat where no scar should be.

"It's getting louder," he said on the third morning, when they stopped to water the mule at a stream gone brown with autumn silt.

Ines glanced back at him. She had a way of looking at people like she was measuring them for a coffin — quick, professional, already done. "Louder how?"

"Like someone calling from underwater." He said it before he could decide not to. "You hold your breath and they're shouting your name and you can't answer because if you open your mouth you'll die."

The stream went quiet around them. Even the mule stopped drinking.

Odile looked up from her notes, ink-stained fingers frozen mid-word. Bramwell's hand drifted toward his sword out of old habit, then away again, ashamed of it.

"That's very specific," Odile said carefully, "for a boy who claims he doesn't remember much."

"I remember drowning," Tam said. It came out flat, the way he'd practiced saying it so people would stop flinching. "I remember it every night. Just not whose body I was in when it happened."

* * *

They followed the pull south along the river for two more days, through country that grew greener and stranger as the wards' long shadow fell behind them. The trees here were old, their roots knuckling up out of the banks like the hands of buried giants. Moss hung in curtains. The light came down green and broken, and everything smelled of wet stone and something sweeter underneath — crushed flowers, maybe, or old incense.

On the second evening, the sigil's humming turned into a keening.

Tam doubled over at the edge of a ravine with his hands over his ears, though that didn't help, because the sound wasn't coming through his ears. It was coming up through the ground, through the soles of his boots, through the bones the way sound travels in water.

"Ines," he gasped. "It's here. It's *here*, it's right below us—"

Ines was already unwrapping the oilcloth. The shattered sigil lay in her palms — half a wheel of silver, its spokes snapped, its center hollowed out as if something had been scooped from it — and it was glowing. Not bright. A drowned kind of glow, the green-gold of sunlight filtered through ten feet of river.

Below them, at the bottom of the ravine, the river bent around a hill of stones, and in the bend stood a shrine.

Or what was left of one. Its roof had fallen in centuries ago; moss had eaten the walls down to suggestions. But the shape of it was still legible — a circular hall, ringed with pillars, sunk halfway into the floodplain so that the river ran straight through its heart. Water poured silver through the broken doorway and pooled in the sunken floor, and in that pool, the light of the sigil answered itself, glowing up from beneath the surface like a lamp sinking into deep water.

Nobody said anything for a long moment. Then Ines slung the sigil's cloth over her shoulder, checked her knife out of habit, and started down the ravine.

"Ines—" Bramwell began.

"It talked to me for ten years through a wall of silence," she said without turning. "You think I'm stopping at a little water?"

* * *

The shrine swallowed sound.

That was Tam's first thought as they waded through the flooded doorway, water shin-deep and cold enough to make his breath catch. Their footsteps made ripples but almost no noise. The river went quiet at the threshold, flowing smooth as glass, and inside the ring of pillars the air had a pressure to it, a held-breath quality, like the moment after lightning and before thunder.

The pool at the center was perfectly round, ringed with a mosaic of tiles that might once have spelled something. Most were broken now. Some were missing entirely, pried loose by patient hands a hundred years dead. But enough remained that Odile dropped to her knees at the edge and went pale.

"This is a ward-circle," she whispered. "A binding circle. This is — Tam, this is the same geometry as the valley wards. The same *exact* geometry."

"I know," Tam said.

And he did know. That was the terrible thing. He knelt beside her and looked at the tiles and his body remembered them the way his body remembered drowning — not as images but as sensations. The cold of tile under bare knees. The taste of river water in a mouth that was screaming. Hands pressing him down, gentle hands, sorry hands, hands that loved him and were killing him anyway.

"Tam?" Ines's voice, sharp with an alarm she'd never admit to. "Your nose is bleeding."

He touched his upper lip. Red. He hadn't even felt it.

"It's fine," he said. "It remembers being done here. To someone. Maybe to me." He looked up at her, and the green light from the pool moved across her face like the surface of water. "Ines, this is where they bind gods into things. This is the place they learned how."

* * *

She drew the sigil out over the pool because none of them could stop her, or perhaps because none of them wanted to.

The broken silver half-wheel trembled in her hands. The glow beneath the water rose to meet it, climbing, climbing, until the whole pool burned like a coin of captured sky, and then—

The voice came.

It didn't come from the pool. It came from everywhere — from the pillars, from the water, from the wet stone under their feet, from inside their own ribs. Ten years of silence broke all at once, and the god's voice was not what any of them expected. It was not vast. It was not thunderous. It was *fractured*, a voice spoken through a cracked bell, each word arriving in pieces, some of them worn smooth as sea glass, some of them jagged:

**—hear—**

**—hear me—**

**—so long—so long in the dark—**

Ines made a sound Tam had never heard from her, small and stripped, the sound of a woman hearing a voice she'd mourned. "I'm here," she said. "I'm here. Where are you? What happened to you?"

**—bound—** The word shuddered through the stone. **—bound and carried. Not dead. Never dead. Sleeping in a crown—**

"A crown," Bramwell repeated hoarsely.

**—the king took me in. Willingly. Willingly, willingly — he opened himself and I poured in and we meant to save them, we meant—** The voice splintered, caught, began again from a different angle, like someone swimming through wreckage. **—but there was another thing waiting in the crown. Old. Hungry. Patient. It closed its hand around both of us. King and god. One prison, two prisoners. Ten years—**

"Ten years," Ines whispered. "Ten years you went silent, and it was because you were *taken*—"

**—and the thing wearing my silence has been wearing him too. Wearing his face. Wearing his armies.** Something moved in the pool — shadows, many shadows, the shapes of marching men seen from very far above. **—it will burn the free cities to feed. It will burn everything. And I cannot— I am too broken— I cannot even—**

The voice collapsed into static, into a low ringing tone like a struck bowl fading, and then, faintly, in a register so small and human it hurt to hear:

**—please. Please. Find the crown. Unbind king from crown. Before it finishes learning how to wear him.**

Silence rushed back in like water filling a lung.

* * *

Tam didn't realize he was crying until Odile handed him a rag without a word.

He wiped his face and stared at the pool, at the dying glow, and the wonder that had filled him since the first word — *the god is alive, the god is speaking, the god knows our names* — was curdling into something colder and heavier in his chest. Because he knew this ritual. Not from books, not from Odile's lectures. From the inside.

Binding circles. Tile geometry. A willing vessel opening themselves to hold a power too large for the world. And then the sealing — the part nobody volunteered for, the part that required a life poured into the lock to keep it shut.

He had died under that seal. Whoever he had been before, whatever body he'd woken up in this one from — he had knelt on tiles like these while loving hands held him under, and his last breath had gone into a lock, and his soul had slipped the chain somehow, the only soul ever to do it, and come out the other side as a boy with no name and no past and a memory of drowning that never faded.

"They'll need another seal," he heard himself say.

Everyone turned. His voice sounded far away to him, like it was coming up through water.

"The binding won't hold forever. It's already cracking — that's why the god could speak tonight, why the wards fell. The seal is failing." He traced the empty sockets in the mosaic where tiles had been pried out. "These are grave-tiles. Every one of these missing pieces was a person. Someone gave their life to lock this circle, and when the lock weakens, they do it again. They always need another one."

"No," Ines said immediately.

"You don't know that—"

"*No.*" She crossed the pool and gripped his shoulders, and her hands were shaking, which frightened Tam more than anything the god had said. "Listen to me. Nobody is sealing anything with anybody. We unbind. That's the whole job. We break the prison, we don't build a new one."

"But the god said—"

"The god said find the crown." Her eyes were fierce and wet in the green light. "So we find the crown."

Behind her, Odile had gone very still, staring at the mosaic with the expression of a scholar watching her life's work rearrange itself into a confession. Bramwell stood with his hand pressed flat against a pillar, head bowed, murmuring something that might have been a prayer to a god who had just proven it existed.

And Tam looked down into the pool, at the last embers of the sigil's glow sinking into the dark water, and felt the drowning-memory rise in him like a tide.

Because he finally understood what he'd been remembering all those nights. Not just the cold. Not just the hands.

The *purpose*. He hadn't been murdered. He'd been *used*. Freely, maybe — the way the king had opened himself willingly — a gift given to hold a door shut. And somewhere in the dark behind his missing years, he knew how the seal worked. Knew it the way you know a song you sang as a child.

Knew, also, that the seal was failing faster than the god knew.

The pool went dark. The shrine exhaled. And far to the south, beyond the hills, beyond the burned provinces, something wearing a king's face lifted its head — Tam was sure of it, sure the way he was sure of the water in his lungs every night — and began, patiently, to smile.

"We leave at dawn," Ines said. "We go to the capital."

Tam nodded. And in the silence of the drowned shrine, he began, quietly, to remember the rest.
