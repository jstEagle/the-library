## 6. The Crown That Remembers

The dreams came back the way water comes back into a drowned lung — all at once, and killing.

Tam woke in the hollow beneath the shrine's broken dome with salt on his lips and the sigil humming against his chest like a second heartbeat. Above him, the others slept in a rough ring: Odile with her nose in a book even unconscious, Bramwell sitting up because deserters never truly slept, and Ines with her knife loose in its sheath, always, even here, even now.

He hadn't meant to touch it again. He'd sworn he wouldn't after the first time, when the sigil had shown him the river and he'd screamed himself hoarse at memories that weren't his — or were his, that was the horror of it, that was the whole drowning truth of him: they were his, from before, from the body he'd worn when the water took him.

But the sigil was warm tonight. Warmer than it had ever been. And it was pulling.

*Come down,* it said, in the voice of a god who had spent ten years learning to whisper. *Come down and remember with me.*

Tam pressed his palm flat against the silver. The fragments fit together wrong, edges like broken teeth, but under his hand they sang, and the song opened like a door.

* * *

He stood in a throne room made of candlelight.

Not the capital's throne room — smaller, older, hung with banners he half-recognized from Odile's histories, the old royal colors before the usurper had stitched his own sigil over them. And there, kneeling at the foot of the dais, was a young man in a plain soldier's cloak, no crown on his head, his hands open and empty on the stone.

Aldric. But not the Aldric of the broadsheets, the tyrant with the dead eyes and the burning provinces behind him. This one was twenty-five years old and terrified, and he was praying.

*I know what you are,* the young king said, to the air, to the dark, to something Tam could feel pressing close around the memory like deep water. *I know what my grandmother buried in the valley. I know what the wards cost her, and what they've kept safe. And I know the plague is at my borders and my priests have nothing and my armies are dying faster than I can bury them.*

The god did not answer in words. It answered the way it answered Tam sometimes — as pressure, as presence, as a vast slow attention turning toward him like a whale turning in the deep. Tam felt its reluctance. Its weariness. Ten thousand years of being asked for things by people who never asked what it would cost.

*Take me,* Aldric said. He lifted his head, and Tam saw his eyes, and they were ordinary eyes, brown, human, wet with fear. *Not through a priest. Not through an oracle. Through me. If your power must live in flesh to reach my people, let it be mine. I'm asking. I'm not commanding. I'm asking.*

And the god — Tam felt this too, felt it like a current dragging him under — the god hesitated, and then, gently, terribly, said yes.

The binding began.

Tam knew this ritual. That was the thing that broke him open as he watched; that was why his hands shook inside the memory, why he tasted riverwater that wasn't there. He knew every word of it. He had heard it spoken over himself, once, by men in gray robes standing waist-deep in cold water, their voices calm as accountants. *That which is given freely may be taken wholly.* He had been eight years old in another body, another life, a boy chosen because children held magic loosely, the way cupped hands hold water. They had drowned him singing. He remembered the light going green above the surface. He remembered deciding, in the last small room of his mind, that if this was what gods needed, then he would be glad to be useful.

He had died glad. That was the worst part. That was the part he'd never told anyone, not Ines, not Odile, not anyone: not terror at the end, but a child's bright certainty that he was doing something holy.

Now he watched a grown man kneel in candlelight and make the same choice, and Tam understood, finally, why the god wept.

Because it didn't want this. It had never wanted this. Every binding, every vessel, every drowned child and kneeling king — the god had said yes each time because saying no meant watching people die of things it could stop. It was not a predator. It was a well, and everyone who found it lowered their buckets, and some of them lowered themselves.

Aldric's body lit from within, silver threading up his arms like frost climbing glass, and he laughed — actually laughed, breathless, amazed — and said, *It doesn't hurt. Grandmother's records said it would hurt—*

And that was when the other one came.

* * *

Tam tried to pull out. He felt Ines's hand on his shoulder, real, solid, her voice coming from very far away — *Tam. Tam, breathe* — but the sigil held him the way the river had once held him, and the memory would not release him until it was finished, and so he stayed, and he watched, and he hated every second of it.

The man wore a chancellor's robes. Tam recognized the face from coins, from wanted posters, from the banners of the army burning its way across the free cities: Lord Veyran, the King's Voice, the regent who had never surrendered the title. But in the memory he was younger, softer, standing in the throne room doorway with a look on his face that Tam needed three heartbeats to name.

Envy. Old, patient, curdled envy.

*Your Majesty.* Veyran's voice was silk over a blade. *You're glowing.*

*Veyran.* Aldric turned, still half-lit, still laughing, drunk on power he had asked for like a beggar asks for bread. *It worked. The plague wards will hold by morning. My people—*

*Yes.* Veyran crossed the room slowly. *Your people. Always your people. Do you know what I did while you knelt here playing saint? I read your grandmother's private papers. All of them. The binding isn't anchored to your soul, Aldric. It's anchored to the crown. Whoever wears the crown holds the vessel. Whoever holds the vessel holds a god.*

Tam saw it happen and could do nothing, the way he had once done nothing in the green water. Veyran drew no weapon. He didn't need one. He simply reached up — casual, almost tender, a servant adjusting his master's collar — and set the iron-and-silver crown down on Aldric's head, and spoke six words in a language that made the candles gutter and made Tam's teeth ache and made the god scream.

The god screamed, and Tam screamed with it, because he knew that sound. He had made that sound, ten years ago, in another body, at the bottom of a river, as something poured itself into him through the drowning and the doors of his mind closed one by one.

*A gift,* Veyran whispered, bending close, his face reflected in the young king's widening eyes. *You gave yourself so freely. Let me accept on your behalf.*

The crown closed like a jaw.

What happened next, Tam's mind could only hold in pieces. Aldric, sealed behind his own eyes, hammering on the inside of his skull. The god, wrenched sideways, bound now not to the willing king but to the crown itself, to the thing Veyran wore, fed through iron and law and the machinery of kingship. Veyran standing in the candlelight flexing his fingers as borrowed divinity crawled up his arms, wearing his envy like a new skin. And underneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, Aldric — awake, aware, screaming without a mouth — screaming for ten years.

Ten years. While Veyran wore his face and burned his provinces and called it policy. While the valley's wards starved because the god's attention was chained elsewhere. While Tam drowned again in someone else's memory every night.

*He didn't betray us,* Tam heard himself say, or thought he said, somewhere far above the memory. *He's been in there the whole time. He's a prisoner. He's the first prisoner.*

* * *

He came back to himself gasping, on his hands and knees on the shrine floor, and Ines was holding his shoulders and Odile was saying his name and Bramwell stood at the entrance with his sword drawn, watching the dark, because that was what Bramwell did instead of comforting people.

"The king," Tam managed. His voice came out ruined, river-roughened. "Ines. The king. He asked for it. He *asked*, he knelt down and he asked the god to save his people, and Veyran—" His throat closed. He swallowed the water that wasn't there. "Veyran put the crown on him and locked the door. He's been screaming behind his own eyes for ten years."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down like deep water.

"That's not possible," Odile said at last, but her voice cracked in the middle of it, because she had spent her life studying the wards and she had already done the arithmetic; Tam could see it happening behind her eyes. "The tyrant. The burnings. The decrees—"

"Are Veyran's." Tam wiped his mouth. "All of them. Every year of them. Aldric watched. He's *watching*. Right now, tonight, he's watching us plan to kill him."

Ines had gone very still. Tam knew what she was thinking, because he was thinking it too, and he could see it land on her like a blow: she had spent ten years praying to a silent god and sharpening herself against a tyrant's name, and both of those things had just changed shape in her hands.

"So we don't kill him," she said slowly. Each word placed like a stone across a stream. "We get him out."

"The unbinding needs the sigil reforged," Odile said. She was already writing, quill scratching fast, her fear transmuted into the only language she trusted. "Which needs a memory freely surrendered, which we still don't fully understand, and none of that matters if we can't reach the throne room, which is currently the most guarded room on the continent—"

"It matters," Tam said quietly.

They all looked at him.

"He's been alone in there for ten years," Tam said. "Drowning. Like me." He looked down at the sigil in his hands, at the broken silver that had shown him a king's kindness and a traitor's smile and a god's grief, all in one night. "Nobody deserves that. Not even — especially not him. He said yes first. Before any of us. He said yes to save people he'd never meet."

Outside the shrine, far off across the dark hills, something rumbled — too rhythmic for thunder. Bramwell stepped out into the night and came back pale.

"Cannon," he said. "Or siege engines. A lot of them. Moving south."

Toward the capital. Toward the feast Veyran would throw when the last free city fell. Toward the throne where a man sat screaming behind his own eyes, waiting ten years for someone to come.

Ines stood, and rolled her shoulders, and Tam watched the mercy and the fury settle into her face side by side, no longer fighting each other.

"Then we'd better get there before the walls come down," she said. "Odile — I need a map of the refugee roads. Bramwell, wake everyone. We march at first light."

Tam looked at the sigil one last time before wrapping it away. Deep in the silver, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something was singing — and for the first time in ten years, it sounded like hope.
