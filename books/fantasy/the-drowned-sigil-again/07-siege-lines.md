## 7. Siege Lines

The capital announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and wet ash and the sweet rot of grain left burning in the fields, carried on a wind that had crossed forty miles of surrendered country. Odile rode in the bed of a farmer's cart between barrels of salted fish, the sigil wrapped against her chest in oilcloth, and counted the ways she could die before nightfall.

She had stopped counting at eleven. It was not that the arithmetic improved; it was that fear, given enough repetition, became a kind of notation. She could write it down now. She could work with it.

Ahead of them the refugee column stretched to the horizon — carts and handcarts and families walking with their lives bundled on their backs, funneled by the usurper's soldiers toward the Corvane Gate like water toward a drain. The soldiers did not turn anyone away. That was the horror of it. The usurper *wanted* them inside the walls: more mouths to feed meant more leverage over the free cities, more bodies for the granaries' queues, more witnesses to his victory feast.

"Odile." Ines's voice came low from under the cart's canvas. "Gate's close. Say it again."

"The plan is sound," Odile said, "in the way that all plans are sound until they meet the world."

"Say it anyway."

So she said it again, because Ines needed the words like other people needed prayer. "We enter with the column. Tam's face gets us past the muster — they're registering orphans for the usurper's foundling houses, and no one questions a child who weeps convincingly. Bramwell walks through the gate in his own armor, under his own name, and surrenders himself as a returning deserter seeking pardon. While they process him, we slip east along the tanners' row to the cistern gate Odile mapped from the old survey—" She caught herself. "That I mapped. We go down into the waterways. We come up inside the palace district after dark, during the feast."

"And if they don't let me walk out again?" Bramwell said. He was cleaning a sword he would not be allowed to keep, his hands moving with the calm of a man performing last rites.

"They will," Odile said. "They'll want to make an example of you publicly. A knight of the realm, returned and pardoned — it flatters him. You are worth more to the usurper as theater than as a corpse."

Bramwell almost smiled. "You have a gift for comfort, scholar."

"I have a gift for accuracy. Comfort is your department."

Above them, where the sky should have held stars, there was only blackness — a blackness so complete it looked painted, a ceiling rather than a sky. Ten years since the valley's wards fell, ten years since the light went out of heaven, and Odile still caught herself looking up at dusk with her heart in her throat, waiting for even one star to come back.

Tonight, though, the dark was useful. Tonight the dark was cover.

* * *

The Corvane Gate swallowed them in stages.

First the soldiers took names. Odile gave hers as Marthe Vey, a ward-scribe out of the burned province, and discovered that lying had become easier the closer they came to dying — terror sharpened into focus, and focus made her voice steady. Then the muster clerks worked down the column, and Tam did exactly what he'd promised: he stood small and hollow-eyed beside Ines and said, when asked about family, "Drowned. All of them drowned." Which was true, in a way no clerk could have survived knowing.

Then the horns began.

Not alarm horns — ceremonial ones, deep-throated, brazen, rolling out from the palace hill in long triumphant peals. The crowd around them stirred. Somewhere ahead, a crier was shouting the news: the eastern host had taken the river crossings; the free cities' alliance was broken at Vellmark; His Radiant Majesty would hold a feast of thanksgiving tonight, and the gates would stay open late so that all his people might hear the bells.

*His Radiant Majesty.* Odile felt the sigil shift against her sternum, warm as a held bird. It had been growing warmer for three days, pulling northward like a compass needle finding its pole. Whatever slept inside King Aldric — whatever wore him — was near enough now to feel them coming.

Or perhaps it wasn't the usurper she felt. Perhaps it was the other thing, the drowned god, the silent one, straining toward its own broken body the way a man strains toward a door.

They slipped away from the column at the tanners' row, and the stink covered their passage better than any ward. The cistern gate was exactly where the old survey promised: a rusted grate behind a collapsed shed, opening onto stairs slick with green stone. Odile went first with a shuttered lantern, mapping as she descended — twelve steps, landing, channel running west, junction marked with a mason's sigil from the reign of Aldric's grandfather. Her life's work had been wards, the grammar of containment, and here she was using that grammar to break into the house of the greatest containment ever built.

A crown-ward, she thought, descending into the dark. A binding woven into gold and set upon a living head. She had spent six years proving such things were theoretically possible and being laughed out of two colleges for it. Now the proof walked around wearing a king's face, and the laughter seemed very far away.

At the third junction they heard footsteps above — boots, many, crossing some grate overhead — and pressed into an alcove while the ceiling rained rust. Tam put his hand flat against the wall and closed his eyes.

"There's water above us," he whispered. "Big water. Fountains. And underneath the fountains there's..." His face went strange. "There's singing. Under everything. Like something humming through the stones."

"The god," Ines said.

"Or the thing that ate it." Tam opened his eyes. "I can't tell which. They sound the same down here. That's the worst part. They sound the same."

* * *

They surfaced at dusk through a maintenance shaft behind the palace's old aviary, into air thick with roasting meat and incense. From the great hall across the courtyard came music — strings and drums and a hundred voices raised in forced cheer — and light spilled gold across flagstones where Odile imagined blood had been spilled often enough to season the mortar.

She unrolled her maps on the aviary floor and worked by lantern-light while the others watched. This was the part she could do. This was the part where terror became useful.

"The feast runs until midnight, longer if the wine holds. The hall has four doors; three will be guarded heavily, the fourth — the servants' passage off the kitchens — lightly, because no one watches the people carrying plates. The crown-ward, if my models are right, anchors through the regalia itself but grounds through the throne dais. There will be ward-stones beneath the floor. I need to see the dais. I need maybe a quarter hour, no more, to map the grounding points and find where the sigil must be set."

"A quarter hour alone on that dais during a feast," Ines said flatly.

"Not alone. You're going to give me my quarter hour." Odile met her eyes. "You're going to draw the guards away."

Ines was quiet a moment. "How?"

"You're going to be seen. On the far side of the palace. Armed, conspicuous, unmistakably the mercenary who's been leaving dead outriders across three provinces. Every soldier in the building will want to be the one who takes you. You don't fight them. You lead them somewhere with exits and lose them, and then you come back to the aviary, and we go down together to the throne room before dawn."

"That's not a plan, that's a bonfire with legs."

"It's mathematics," Odile said. "Every guard chasing you is a guard not looking at me. I cannot map that dais with swords in the room. One of us has to be the distraction, and I am the only one who can read the ward, and Bramwell—" She stopped.

Everyone stopped.

Because Bramwell was already buckling his gorget, already settling his cloak, already becoming once more the knight he had spent this whole road trying not to be. He looked up at them and smiled the way men smile at funerals they've decided to enjoy.

"No," Ines said.

"The front gate," Bramwell said. "I walk in the front gate, loud, in colors, announcing myself. Deserter of the Third Banner, returned to beg pardon of His Radiance. They'll take me alive — Odile's right, I'm worth more as theater — and they'll parade me, and every eye in that palace will be on the prodigal traitor while a scribe and a mercenary and a drowned boy do the real work." He rolled his shoulders. "It's the same plan. I'm just better bait than Ines. I'm *known*. And I know how to stand in front of angry men and talk until talking runs out."

"Bram—"

"Ines." He said her name gently, the way you'd steady a horse. "I deserted once because I couldn't choose. This is me choosing. Let me have it done properly."

Odile wanted to argue. She ran the numbers instead, because that was her nature and her curse: probability of success with Bramwell at the gate, higher. Probability of Bramwell surviving the week afterward — she refused to finish the calculation. Some variables she declined to solve.

"We get you out," Ines said finally. "Whatever happens. We get you out."

"Of course you will," Bramwell said, and lied beautifully.

* * *

He went down through the city in his battered finery, and from the aviary roof they watched him cross the great square below — a lone figure in tarnished silver walking toward the lit gates of the palace, unhurried, upright, a man making himself into a banner. Even at this distance Odile could see the guards stiffen, could see the ripple move through the gate watch as word spread: *it's him, it's Ashgrove's deserter, it's the Knight of the Broken Oath.*

Horns sounded again — different horns this time, sharper. Doors opened. Torches converged.

"He's through," Tam breathed. "They're taking him in. He's smiling. Why is he smiling?"

"Because he wants them to look," Odile said, and hated how steady her voice was, and was grateful for it too.

Then there was nothing to do but wait, and waiting was the worst arithmetic of all. Below, the feast swelled — toasts, cheering, a wave of laughter that broke and reformed. Somewhere in that golden noise, Bramwell was standing in chains of ceremony, telling his story to a room full of enemies, buying minutes with his life.

Odile checked the sigil. It was hot now, nearly burning through the oilcloth, and when she pressed her palm to it she felt — distinctly, unmistakably — a pulse. Not the usurper's cold rhythm. Something older, slower, drowning.

*Soon,* it seemed to say. Or perhaps that was her own heart.

Below, torchlight erupted along the eastern wing — Ines, right on schedule, a flash of steel on a balcony, gone again, and the palace began to shout itself awake. Guards poured from the hall like ants from a kicked nest. The dais, Odile judged, would be thinning by the minute.

"Now," she whispered, gathering her maps. "Tam, with me. We have our quarter hour."

But as they reached the stair, Tam caught her sleeve, his face white as drowned bone.

"Odile," he said. "The singing stopped. Whatever was under the palace — it just went quiet. Like it's holding its breath."

Down in the dark beneath the feast, beneath the fountains, beneath the throne, something had noticed them coming.

And it was waiting.
