## 9. The Unbinding

The throne room smelled of smoke and spilled wine.

Ines came through the servants' corridor with the sigil wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and the noise of the feast above her had already curdled into something else — horns, screaming, the deep percussion of a ram against gates that would not hold past dawn. The usurper's armies were in the city. They were in the palace. And she was out of time.

Odile's map of the crown-ward burned in her memory: seven anchor points, six broken on the road here, one remaining — the crown itself, seated on the head of a king who might already be nothing but a hollow the thing wore.

She pushed open the doors.

The throne room was vast and gold-lit, braziers guttering, courtiers fled or dead. At the far end, beneath a canopy of black silk stitched with false stars, sat King Aldric — or the shape of him. He rose slowly, the way a puppet rises when its strings are gathered, and his face was wrong. Not cruel. *Crowded.* Like a house with too many people behind one window.

"You brought it," he said, and the voice was two voices braided together, one rich and amused, one thin as a wire and screaming underneath. "Ten years I've worn this valley's little god like a stone in my boot, and now you carry it to me. How considerate."

Ines drew no weapon. There was no weapon for this. She unwrapped the sigil instead, and the shattered silver caught the brazier light and threw it back wrong — throwing it back the way starlight used to look, before the sky went dark.

Behind the usurper's eyes, something *moved*.

*Ines.* The god's voice, faint, frayed, coming through the sigil and through the man both at once. *He is still here. He has been here every day. Do not strike.*

"I know," she said aloud, and the usurper laughed.

---

Tam could not feel his hands.

That was the first sign, Odile had told him later — that the sigil's pull was taking more than direction from him, that the drowned memories in his skull were resonating with the binding ritual like a struck bell answering a distant chime. He stood at Ines's shoulder now, in the shadow of the great doors, and the cold in his fingers was the same cold as the river, ten years ago, another body, water filling lungs that had never drawn breath before that moment.

*I can do it,* he thought at the sigil, at the god, at anything listening. *I remember drowning. It's mine. Take it.*

The god answered, and its voice was grief itself.

*No, small one. A death freely given is a door closing. The sigil must be reforged from a living memory — a home. Something warm that the giver walks away from while it still glows. That is the price. That is the only coin it accepts.*

Tam looked at Ines. Ines looked at the sigil. And he saw her understand one heartbeat before he did, and he saw her begin to step forward, and something in his chest tore open because he knew — he *knew* — what memory she carried that could count as home.

"No," Tam said.

"Ines." The god again, through the sigil, gentle and terrible. *It must be you. You are the last one who remembers the valley as it was. Bramwell has no valley. Odile's home is books. The boy's home drowned before it began. Only you walked those wards every night for ten years, speaking to a god who could not answer, and loved it anyway. Only your memory of that place is alive enough to burn.*

"Then take it," Ines said.

---

The usurper lunged.

Not at her — at the sigil, at the silver in her hands, and Ines rolled aside as a body that had been a king for twenty years moved with a speed no king should have. Bramwell was somewhere below, buying minutes with his blood; Odile was somewhere above, holding the ward-map in her mind like a lit match. There was no one left to buy them more.

So Ines planted her feet on the stones of a palace she'd entered as an intruder, held up a god's broken heart in both hands, and began to give away the only thing she had ever owned.

"The valley," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "The border stones with the moss on their north faces. The river that ran shallow over white rock so you could count the fish. The way the wards hummed under my palm at dusk — not magic, just *home*, just the sound of a place holding its people close—"

The sigil drank it. She felt it drinking. Each image pulled loose like a tooth, and where each one had been there was nothing — not pain, not absence, just a smooth blank wall where a room full of light used to be.

The usurper stopped mid-stride. Tilted his head. "What are you doing?" And for the first time, the second voice under his was louder than the first — afraid.

"She's unmaking the lock," Tam shouted, running forward, because he understood now too, because the binding needed a memory and the memory was becoming the key. "The wards were built from love of the valley — the god's prison was built from the same material — she's turning the key inside the lock—"

---

Tam reached her side as the last of the valley went.

He watched it happen. Watched Ines Sandoval, who had described that valley to him a hundred times on the road — the fish-countable river, the moss, the humming wards — watch her mouth keep moving and her eyes go slowly, terribly empty, not dead-empty but *unhomed*-empty, a woman standing in a country that had been deleted from her.

And then the sigil blazed.

Silver light poured upward through the rafters, through the black silk canopy with its false stars, and the false stars burned away and for one impossible moment the ceiling of the throne room showed the true night sky — every constellation restored, hanging indoors, wheeling slow — and the whole city outside must have seen it, must have seen the heavens relight above the burning capital.

The usurper screamed.

It was not a kingly sound. It was the sound of a thing being torn out of a house it had squatted in for ten years, root by root, and Aldric's body arched backward off the throne and the two voices split — one rising out of his open mouth like smoke given a throat, black and shrieking and *old*, and one staying, staying, collapsing inward with a sob ten years long.

The black thing hit the sigil's light and came apart. Not dramatically. Almost quietly. Like frost in sunlight. Like something that had always been temporary finally admitting it.

Aldric fell to his knees on the steps of his own dais, and he was weeping, and he was *thin*, thinned by a decade of captivity inside his own skin, and when he looked up at Ines his eyes were only a man's eyes, exhausted and ruined and free.

"It talked to me," he whispered. "All these years. Through the walls of myself. I thought I was going mad. I thought—" His hand found the floor. "Is it over?"

Above them, the true stars turned. Below them, the gates gave — a sound like the world cracking, and then the roar of the usurper's army flooding into a city whose master had just ceased to exist.

---

Tam caught Ines as she swayed.

The sigil hung in the air between them, whole now, reforged, spinning slow, and the god's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm as the river in summer.

*She gave everything,* it said, to Tam, to all of them, to the freed king kneeling in the wreckage of his reign. *Remember it for her. All of you. That is the last work of the faithful — to be the memory of the one who paid.*

"I will," Tam said, and his voice broke, and he said it again into Ines's blank, blinking confusion as she looked around the throne room like a stranger. "I'll tell you about the fish. I'll tell you about the moss on the stones. Every day if you want. I remember being drowned and I remember being loved after, and I know which one matters."

Ines stared at him. At the sigil. At the stars on the ceiling that she felt, distantly, she ought to recognize and didn't.

"Who are you people?" she asked softly. "And why does it feel like coming home?"

The doors burst open behind them — soldiers, torchlight, and at their head a mud-spattered figure limping on a borrowed spear, grinning through a split lip.

Bramwell. Alive.

"Well," he said, surveying the blazing sigil, the weeping king, the relit sky, "someone had better explain all this before the council gets here. Because they're coming. And they're going to want answers."
