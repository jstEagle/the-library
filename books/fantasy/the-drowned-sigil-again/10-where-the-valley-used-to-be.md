## 10. Where the Valley Used to Be

The stars came back all at once, like a held breath finally released.

Ines was still on her knees on the throne room floor, the reforged sigil cooling in her hands, when the light poured through the high windows — silver, then gold, then a wash of colors she had no names for. Around her, the usurper's courtiers screamed and scattered. The great doors hung splintered where Bramwell's last stand had bought them minutes he'd paid for with blood and bruises. Somewhere below, the armies that had been breaching the walls simply... stopped.

She felt it happen. Not heard — felt. A thousand thousand soldiers at the gates, mid-scream, mid-swing, going quiet as puppets whose strings had been cut. The will that drove them drained out of the world through the wound in Aldric's crown, and what remained was just men. Tired men. Cold men standing in siege mud, looking up at a sky that had been black for ten years and was suddenly, impossibly, full.

Tam made a sound beside her that was half laugh, half sob. "It's over," he said. "Ines. It's actually—"

"It's not." Her voice came out rough as gravel. She nodded toward the throne.

Aldric lay crumpled at its foot, and he was weeping.

* * *

They carried him down from the dais because he could not walk. That was the thing Ines kept returning to in the days after — the king of the realm, the tyrant whose name emptied cities, too broken to cross his own throne room without two mercenaries and a drowned boy holding him upright.

The god left him slowly. It had lived in him for a decade, pressed flat and silent beneath the usurper's weight, and now that it was free it seemed reluctant to go, threading through Aldric's veins like warmth returning to frozen hands. When they laid him in the infirmary of the palace he'd been imprisoned in, he opened his eyes and looked at Ines with an expression she'd never expected to see on that face: gratitude, and underneath it, grief so vast it had nowhere left to put itself.

"You gave it your valley," he said. His voice was ruined, scraped thin from screaming behind another man's eyes for ten years. "Odile told me. I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "I took that burden to spare my people. You've taken a greater one to spare me."

"Don't make it noble," Ines said. "I did it because Tam asked. Because Bramwell bled for it. Because Odile mapped every ward between here and the throne room and didn't sleep for four days doing it." She looked away from him, toward the window, where stars burned in patterns she suddenly couldn't read. "And because someone had to. Not because of you."

"Even so." He tried to rise; failed; settled for pressing his palm flat against his chest, where the sigil's echo still hummed. "There will be those who say the crown should return to me. That the law demands it."

"The law," Ines said, "sat in this room and watched you scream for ten years."

Aldric smiled — a terrible, cracked thing. "Then hear my last act as king." He turned his head and raised his voice, and though it was weak, the whole infirmary heard it: witnesses, guards, Odile scribbling in the corner, Bramwell propped against the doorframe with his arm in a sling. "I abdicate. Let the free cities' council hold the realm until it can choose its own shape. Let them try something new. Gods know the old way only made room for things like *him*."

He didn't say the usurper's name. Nobody did anymore. The thing had been torn out of Aldric and unmade by the sigil's light, and what remained of it was ash on marble, and ash has no name worth speaking.

Bramwell pushed off the doorframe and limped forward. "You'll need someone to carry the terms north. The council will want proof, not rumor."

"They'll want you," Aldric said. "A knight who chose his conscience over his commander is worth more than any seal."

"I'm a deserter."

"You *were*," said the former king. "Now you're an envoy. The difference is whether you accept."

Bramwell looked at Ines. She shrugged. "Don't look at me. I swore off leading anyone, remember? I'm bad at it. Ask them."

"He's lying," Tam said solemnly. "He's very good at it."

* * *

They left the capital a week later, when Aldric could stand on his own and the council's first envoys had arrived — wary, sharp-eyed merchants and magistrates who eyed Ines's company like cats eyeing a dog that had somehow gotten into the pantry. The roads were filling with people going home. Armies dissolving back into farmers. Refugees walking the other direction, toward cities that needed rebuilding, carrying hammers instead of fear.

At the city gates, Aldric waited for them, dressed plainly for the first time in his adult life. No crown. He'd had it melted down, Odile reported, and the silver given to the families of the siege dead. There was a poetry in that which Ines suspected the man had planned deliberately, and she found she didn't mind.

"I'm going north with Bramwell," he said. "To kneel, if kneeling helps. To work, if it doesn't." He hesitated, and for a moment she saw the young man Tam's memories showed her — the one who had taken a god into his body to save people who never learned his name. "If you ever need anything—"

"We won't," Ines said. Then, because Tam elbowed her hard enough to bruise: "Probably. Send word anyway."

He bowed to her. A king, bowing to a mercenary, in the mud of a gate road while carters cursed around them. She let him.

* * *

The valley was three weeks' ride south, and Ines knew it the way you know a place in a story someone else told you.

That was the strange arithmetic of it. She remembered leaving. She remembered the road, the ambushes, the shrine under the river, the burned province, the feast, the throne room. Every step since the night the wards fell sat in her mind bright and complete. But before that night — nothing. A smooth white absence shaped like a life. She knew she'd patrolled the valley border for a god that hadn't spoken in ten years, because Odile had told her so, twice, gently, both times watching her face for the crack. She knew she'd been born there. Knew there had been people. Names, even: Odile had written them down for her, in her careful scholar's hand, on a page Ines carried but hadn't yet been able to read.

What she had was a map. Odile had drawn it — the valley, the river, the shrine, the border stones Ines had walked for a decade. She held it now as their horses crested the last ridge, and looked down at the place where she had come from, and felt nothing.

No. Not nothing. That wasn't honest. She felt the way you feel looking at a portrait of a stranger who is said to resemble you. Recognition without memory. Grief without an object.

"It's beautiful," Tam said quietly beside her.

"Is it?"

"You can't see that?"

"I can see hills. Trees. A river catching the light." She folded the map along its worn crease. "I can't see home. There isn't one in me anymore. There's just a hole with everyone else's memories of it around the edges."

Tam was silent for a moment. Then: "When I drowned — the first time, in the body before this one — I lost things too. Whole years. My mother's voice. The color of our kitchen door." He swung down from his horse and stood beside her, small and grave, twelve years old and older than the hills. "But Bramwell remembers the kitchen door."

"What?"

"He listened. When I talked about it, on the road, that night after the shrine. He remembers it now. So do you — you were there when I told it. And Odile wrote everything down, because Odile writes everything down." He shrugged, matter-of-fact, devastating. "I lost my memories. I didn't lose them *alone*. That's what a family is, I think. Other people holding what you can't."

Ines looked at him for a long time. Then she looked back down the ridge, at the others dismounting in the long gold light — Odile already unpacking her instruments to survey the fallen ward-stones, muttering about a lifetime of study beginning properly at last; Bramwell building a fire with the competence of a man who had finally stopped apologizing for existing; the horses cropping grass, unconcerned with history.

Below them, the valley lay green and quiet, its people spilling out of doors to stare at the impossible stars. Somewhere down there was a house she'd lived in. A patrol route her feet had known better than her own heartbeat. A god she'd loved in silence for ten years, who now slept easy somewhere in the world's deep places, freed, whole, and — Odile was certain of it — grateful.

Ines couldn't remember loving it. But she could watch Tam watch the valley, and see the love reflected there, and know it had been hers.

"That'll have to be enough," she said.

"It's more than most people get," Tam said.

* * *

They camped that night on the ridge, above the valley, because Ines found she wasn't ready to walk into a home that wouldn't know her either. Odile didn't push. Bramwell cooked. Tam fell asleep against Ines's shoulder halfway through a story about the drowned shrine, and she let him stay there, and stared up at a sky crowded with constellations she would have to learn like a foreign alphabet.

"Odile," she said, into the firelight. "The names. On the page you wrote. Read them to me."

Odile looked up. Something moved behind her eyes — pity, maybe, or its more useful cousin, resolve. She took out the page, smoothed it on her knee, and began.

"Ines Sandoval. Born in the valley, in a house with a blue door—"

"A blue door," Ines repeated. "Like Tam's kitchen."

"Everyone's doors are blue in the valley," Odile said softly. "It's a tradition. Or it was." She cleared her throat and went on. "Your mother was a warden before you. Your father mended nets. You had a dog named Bracken who followed your patrols for eleven years and bit exactly one person, who deserved it—"

Ines laughed. It came out of her sideways, surprising them both, and once it started it wouldn't stop — laughter and tears in the same breath, grief with nowhere to land finding a landing after all. Bramwell grinned at her across the fire. Tam slept on, dreaming, she hoped, of rivers that stayed kind.

Above them the stars burned on, steady, restored, indifferent and gorgeous. Below them the valley breathed. And Ines Sandoval — mercenary, exile, the woman who had traded her whole world to give the sky back its light — sat in the circle of the only home she had left, listening to strangers describe her life to her, and found, to her astonishment, that she believed them.

Somewhere far to the north, a rider was already carrying word of a new thing: a realm without a king, a council of cities, a law that answered to people instead of crowns. Trouble would come of it. Trouble always came. But that was tomorrow's road.

Tonight there was a fire, and a page of names, and four voices taking turns holding a memory none of them would let go dark again.

"Read it again," Ines said. "From the top."

And Odile did.
