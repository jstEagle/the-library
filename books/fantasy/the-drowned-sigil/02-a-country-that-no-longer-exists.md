## 2. A Country That No Longer Exists

The lowquarters had learned to drown long before the crown taught them anything else.

Mirabel moved through streets that were half canal, half memory, the floodwater at his shins and rising, black with the ash of the district that had burned two nights ago. The Gilded Meridian's quarter sat on higher ground, all gilt and dry stone, but down here the city showed its bones—old Vessany, the part no map admitted to, where doorways opened three steps below street level and staircases descended into water that had never been drained because draining it would mean admitting it had once been something else.

He kept to the shadowed side of the aqueduct, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, bound there with a bone-carver's strap. His hands ached. They always ached now, since Odile had said his name aloud like a price being quoted.

*Two hundred crowns for the gifted one who keeps accounts,* she'd said, not even looking at him. *The hunters wanted a name. I gave them yours instead of mine. You may consider that the last kindness this guild will ever do you.*

He had run. He was still running, though he'd stopped admitting it to himself somewhere around the third bridge.

Behind him, faint across the water, came the sound he'd been dreading: boots in the flooded lanes, unhurried, methodical. Witch-hunters did not hurry. Hurrying implied doubt about whether their quarry would still be there.

Mirabel slipped beneath the arch of a drowned tannery, where the water went dark and cold and the ceiling pressed close enough to touch. He crouched on a submerged step, breathing shallow, and let the water carry away the sound of him.

In his pocket, against his hip, something knocked.

Not the hunters. Something smaller. Patient.

He drew it out: the bone compass he'd taken from the guild's reliquary shelf without thinking, without deciding—the way he took most things that whispered. It was old, older than the guild, its housing carved from the radius bone of someone long dead, the needle a sliver of petrified ivory that pointed nowhere any compass should point. Not north. Not toward anything above the water.

It pointed *down*.

And when he held it, as he held everything he carved or carried, the dead man inside it spoke.

*You're going the wrong way, ledger-keeper.*

The voice was dry and precise, a cartographer's voice, the voice of a man who had spent his life correcting other people's errors and had died mid-correction. Mirabel had heard him before, faintly, in the guild's reliquary—a muttering presence among the shelves—but here, in the drowned dark, the voice came clear as a bell rung underwater.

"Which way is right?" Mirabel whispered. Speaking to relics in public was how the gifted got burned; speaking to them alone was how they stayed sane. He'd stopped distinguishing between the two risks.

*Down,* said the cartographer. *Down and east, past the salt line, into the country that isn't. But first you'll want to know what you're carrying, and you don't. Open your ledger. The margins, boy. Everyone reads the columns. Nobody reads the margins.*

"I've read every line of that ledger for six years."

*Every line they let you see.* A dry click, like a tongue against teeth—or the needle tapping its housing. *I drew the maps they burned. My name was Anselm Vireo. Was. The crown paid my own guild to unname me, and the guild paid me in silence, and I paid the river with everything else. So believe me when I tell you what erasure looks like from the inside.*

Mirabel hesitated. Above him, through the tannery's rotted planking, the boots passed—three sets, maybe four, lantern-light sliding along the waterline like a blade laid flat. He counted his own heartbeats until they faded.

Then he unwrapped the ledger.

* * *

The Gilded Meridian's ledgers were famous for their beauty: vellum smooth as skin, ink mixed with silver so entries seemed to float. Six years Mirabel had kept them, entering impossible things in impeccable script—relics sourced from graves that hadn't been dug yet, contracts signed by the dead, debts owed to persons whose births the crown had never recorded. He had thought himself the ledger's most thorough reader.

He turned to the margins.

They were not empty. Under the angle of the lantern he'd shuttered to a knife-thin slit, he saw what he had never seen: writing in the gutters of the pages, in ink that absorbed light rather than reflecting it, visible only when wet. The flood-damp that had soaked through the oilcloth had woken it.

*There,* said Anselm Vireo, satisfaction roughening his dead voice. *Water remembers. That was always the problem with drowning a country—the water refuses to forget. Read.*

The marginalia was cramped, urgent, written by many hands across decades. Names. Always names first—names struck through, names rewritten, names replaced with the word *VACANT*. And beside each vacancy, a small notation in the same absorbed-light ink:

*Paid. Sealed. Drowned.*

"It's a record," Mirabel breathed. "Of the unnaming."

*It is the only record,* said Vireo. *The crown didn't just kill the gifted, boy. It killed the country they belonged to. Old Vessany had borders that ran under the sea and doors that opened inward. When the first Meridian king seized the throne, he couldn't govern a country whose ground remembered another allegiance—so he drowned the lowlands, bought the guilds, and had every map redrawn. Every map but mine. And every ledger but the ones too proud to burn.*

Mirabel's thumb found a page near the middle, where the marginalia thickened into a single repeated phrase, written and rewritten like a man practicing a confession:

*The Sigil stands. The Sigil stands. Let them drown around it.*

"What sigil?"

*The Drowned Sigil.* The compass needle swung, insistent, tugging at its housing like a hound at a leash. *A door. The last true door of old Vessany, set in the heart of the sunken archive beneath the first capital—which is to say, beneath the flood, beneath the lowquarters, beneath everything your crown built on top of pretending nothing is there. It was sealed when the last free cartographers were unnamed. I sealed it myself. I have regretted it ever since.*

"Why seal it?"

*Because sealing is not destroying. Because a door can wait.* The voice gentled, which was somehow worse. *Because I thought someone would come who needed out more than the country needed in. I was wrong about the order of things, perhaps. But not about the door.*

Mirabel looked up through the black water at the distant, lamplit world above—the world of bounties and witch-hunters and a guildmaster who had sold him with the same pen she used for receipts. Two hundred crowns. His whole invisible life, priced like a relic.

He could go east, the refugees said. Over the mountains, out of Vessany entirely, if a man had papers—and the gifted had no papers anymore, only pyres.

Or he could go down.

"Show me," he said.

*That,* said Anselm Vireo, *is the first sensible thing a ledger-keeper has said in six years.*

* * *

He went down through the places the city pretended it didn't have: a sluice-gate rusted open since before his grandmother, a smuggler's stair that ended in water, then a tunnel where the water ended and only the dark continued. The compass guided him, its needle steady as a pulse, and Vireo talked the whole way—not directions only, but testimony, the cartographer unable to stop cataloguing what had been erased.

*On your left, a customs house. It collected tariffs for a king the maps say never ruled here. Ahead, the Salt Exchange, where they traded in border-tolls for a border that is officially a fable. You are walking through a country, boy. You have been walking through it your whole life. They just charged you rent and called it a different name.*

Grief caught Mirabel off guard, then—grief not for his own lost life, though that was there too, sharp and specific, the little room above the chandlery, the tea bowl with the chipped rim, the clients who would never again bring him their grandmothers' knucklebones—but grief for the sheer scale of it. A whole country, drowned and renamed, and everyone living on top of it, paying taxes to the men who'd held it under.

His hearing felt strange. He noticed it in the quiet stretches of the tunnel: sounds arriving slightly wrong, slightly late, as if the world were being transcribed by a careless clerk. He told himself it was the water, the pressure, the dark.

The dead in the ledger had begun to murmur as he walked. He could feel them through the oilcloth, dozens of voices now, the unnamed pressing close to their names, wanting to be read.

*Almost there,* said Vireo. *The archive lies below the old tide-gate. And ledger-keeper—one warning, freely given, as one erased man to another. Doors that wait do not wait kindly. What's behind the Sigil has been keeping its own accounts.*

"And?"

*And it knows yours.*

The tunnel opened. Below him, vast and black, lay the sunken archive—and standing on the flooded threshold, lantern raised, her coat immaculate above the waterline, was Odile Ferrant.

"You have something of mine, Mirabel," called the guildmaster of the Gilded Meridian, and her voice carried across the water like a bill come due.
