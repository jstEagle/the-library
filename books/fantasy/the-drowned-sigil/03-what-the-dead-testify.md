## 3. What the Dead Testify

The water in the lowquarters rose to Mirabel's knees and did not stop there. It came up the stairwells of the drowned district like a slow tide with a purpose, carrying silt and lamp-oil and the pale petals of flowers no one had planted, and Mirabel waded through it with the ledger strapped against his chest beneath an oilskin coat, feeling it beat against his ribs like a second heart.

He had not slept. Sleep invited the dead woman's voice back, and she had not finished with him.

*You knew,* she had said, through the little reliquary of her finger-bone he'd carved three winters ago for a client who never collected. *You kept the books, bone-carver. You wrote down what we were worth.*

He had known the guild traded in impossible things. He had told himself the ledgers were only arithmetic—acquisitions, disbursements, amortizations of the strange. But arithmetic was a kind of consent. Every column he had balanced had been a life converted into currency, and he had made the numbers neat.

Now the flooded streets guided him by accident or design. The old capital lay beneath the new one; everyone in Vessany knew that much and knew better than to say it. When the first king raised his walls, he had not built on empty ground. He had built on a country, and then he had drowned it, and then he had burned every map that remembered its name. The cartographer's voice in the bone compass—the compass Mirabel had stolen from Odile's own cabinet on his way out—had spent two days telling him how to walk downward through a city that insisted it had no basement.

*Left at the tannery that isn't a tannery,* the drowned man murmured, his accent older than any living dialect, vowels rounded like river stones. *Down where the cistern drinks. The archive keeps what the crown could not burn, because the crown never learned to swim.*

Mirabel descended. The water went from knee to waist to chest, warm as breath, and then—he could not have said where the threshold was—it went quiet around him, holding him up, and he understood he had crossed into the part of Vessany that had never agreed to be Vessany at all.

The sunken archive opened before him like a cathedral that had learned to breathe underwater. Vaulted ceilings of black stone, uncracked after centuries submerged. Shelves rising out of the gloom, their contents not paper but bone—thousands upon thousands of carved bones, each one a record, each one still speaking in a whisper so vast and so layered that it resolved into something like silence, like the sea heard from the inside.

And they knew he had come.

The dead crowded him—not bodies, but voices, attention, weight. They pressed against his gift the way moths press against glass. *Read us. Count us. We are owed.* A child's voice asking after a mother sold north. An old man reciting the boundaries of a field that no longer existed, insisting on his fence-lines, insisting on his name. A woman singing half a lullaby over and over because the other half had been taxed away from her. They had unfinished accounts, every one of them, and Mirabel felt the ledger against his chest grow warm, grow eager, as if it recognized its siblings.

"I can't read you all," he said aloud, and his voice broke the water's surface in silver rings. "I don't even know all your names."

*You will,* said the drowned cartographer, from the compass in his pocket. *That is what the ledger is for. That is why it was written. Not to hide the debts, bone-carver. To itemize them.*

Mirabel unwrapped the oilskin. The Gilded Meridian's great ledger lay open in his hands, and here, in the true dark of the drowned country, the marginalia woke. Ink moved between the lines like fish under ice. Names surfaced in the margins—hundreds of names, thousands—and beside each name, a figure, and beside each figure, a word in the old tongue: *owed. owed. owed.*

It was not a book of secrets. It had never been a book of secrets. It was a bill.

He was still reading when the water behind him shivered.

* * *

Sergeant Cassian Roe had read forty-one confiscated ledgers in six weeks, and the forty-second was going to be the one that undid him.

He sat in the requisitioned customs house in the highquarters with a lamp turned low, because he had begun reading these things the way he once read letters from home: alone, at night, ashamed. The witch-hunters' procedure was simple. Seize the effects of the gifted. Inventory them. Burn what burned. But ledgers didn't burn well, and someone in command—Roe had stopped pretending he didn't know who—wanted them read first. *Find the networks,* the orders said. *Follow the names.*

So he followed the names. And the names led somewhere the orders had not anticipated.

The forty-first ledger had belonged to a midwife in the Tanner's Row district, the one they'd burned. She had recorded every birth she attended for thirty years, and beside each entry, in a small careful hand, a fee paid—not to her, but *by* her, to an agent of the Gilded Meridian. A tithe. A licensing fee. The guild had sold her the right to her own gift, year after year, and when the crown's bounty came, the guild had sold her too. Roe had found the receipt. Dated nine days before the burning. Her name, her district, her gift, itemized and delivered.

He had signed the order that put hunters on Tanner's Row. His seal was on the manifest.

Roe set down the forty-second ledger—a shipping manifest from the Meridian, decades old, listing cargo that made his scalp prickle: *bottled grief, 12 units; a border, folded; the name of a river, one (1), used.* Impossible things, invoiced. And stamped in the corner of the final page, in wax he now recognized because he'd seen it on crown documents his whole career, the royal sigil.

The crown hadn't hunted the guild. The crown was the guild's best customer.

He thought of the woman from Tanner's Row whose neighbors swore she'd pulled a fever out of a dying boy like drawing a splinter. He thought of the bounty notices with his signature at the bottom. He thought about what a country would be worth, what a *border* would be worth, if you could buy one.

A knock. Then his lieutenant, pale, rain-wet. "Sergeant. The Ferrant woman's people report the Villalobos man was seen entering the lowquarters. Headed down." A pause. "The old capital way."

Roe looked at the manifest, at the royal wax, at the word *owed* he now saw everywhere he looked.

"Get me six men," he said. "And Lieutenant—if anyone asks, we're pursuing a fugitive. That's all we're doing."

It was not all he was doing, and they both knew it, and neither said so.

* * *

Odile Ferrant came into the sunken archive the way she came into every room: as though the room had been waiting for her.

She stood on the water without sinking. That was the first thing Mirabel understood, watching her descend the flooded stair with lamplight bending around her—that guildmasters of the Gilded Meridian did not merely trade in impossible things. They *were* collateral. Somewhere in some ledger, Odile Ferrant's own name was posted against her power, and the debt held her up.

"You've cost me a great deal, Mirabel." Her voice carried flat across the black water. "My amnesty. My standing. Twenty years of your excellent, discreet bookkeeping."

"You sold my name." He closed the ledger and held it against his chest. Around him, the dead had gone quiet—not absent, but listening, the way a courtroom listens. "You sold all of them. Every name in this book is a sale, Odile. I finally read it properly."

"Yes." She did not deny it. That was somehow worse. "And now you'll give it back, because you are a reasonable man who understands that a ledger in the hands of the crown means nothing—they'll burn it—and in the hands of the dead means nothing—they have no hands—but in *my* hands it remains what it has always been. Leverage. Order. Civilization."

"It's a bill," Mirabel said. "And it's come due."

Something moved behind her eyes—the flicker of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. Hunters' boots sounded on the stairs above, many of them, descending. She heard them too. Her chin lifted a fraction.

"Ah," she said softly. "And here comes the crown to collect us both."

Torches bloomed at the top of the archive stair, and Sergeant Cassian Roe started down into the drowned country, and Mirabel Villalobos understood, with terrible clarity, that whatever happened next, the reading could not wait. The dead were owed. The door was close—he could feel the Drowned Sigil humming through the stone beneath his feet like a struck bell that had been ringing for three hundred years, waiting for someone to answer it.

But Odile was walking toward him across the water, and the hunters were walking down behind her, and the ledger in his arms weighed suddenly as much as a country.

"Last chance to be sensible," Odile said.

Mirabel opened the book.
