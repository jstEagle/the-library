## 4. The Reading

The water reached Mirabel's knees before he saw the door.

It was not a door in any way the guild's ledgers would have honored—no gilt, no hinges, no lock worth picking. It was a circle cut into a wall of black stone at the heart of the sunken archive, and it was full of sea. Not seawater, exactly. Older water. Water that remembered being rain on hills that no longer existed, that remembered running in rivers with names the crown had struck from every map. The Drowned Sigil turned slowly behind its curtain of water, a wheel of bone-white lines, and through it—if he tilted his head, if he let his gift lean toward it—he could see a country. Green. Unburned. Whole.

*Vessany,* said the drowned cartographer, her voice rising from the compass in his pocket like breath from a grave. *That is Vessany as it was. As it still is, underneath their lies.*

Mirabel stood in water to his knees with the ledger clutched against his chest, and he understood, finally, what the door was for.

It was not for him.

It had never been for anyone living. The dead of Vessany had built it—the drowned cartographer, the erased cartographers after her, every soul whose name had been sold or burned or blotted from the record. They had carved one door out of their own erasure, a way back into the world, and they had waited two hundred years for someone who could hear them well enough to open it.

He could feel them now, all around him in the flooded dark: the crowd of unfinished accounts pressing close, patient as sediment. He had heard them singly before—a woman's accusation in a carved knucklebone, a child's counting-song in a whale-ivory comb. But here, at the sigil, they were a chorus, and the chorus was saying one thing, over and over, in ten thousand drowned voices:

*Read us.*

The door would take him away. That was its mercy. Step through, and the gift would be someone else's burden; step through, and Vessany-the-lie could keep standing while he lived out his days in Vessany-the-truth, green and unburned. No hunters. No Odile. No ledgers.

His hands were shaking. He noticed, distantly, that they were shaking the way they shook when he began a carving—as if they already knew what they were going to do before he did.

"No," he said aloud, to the door, to the dead, to himself. "I didn't come all this way to escape you."

The water stilled. Even the drip from the archive's broken ceiling seemed to hold its breath.

"I came to read you out."

* * *

Odile Ferrant came down into the archive like a woman descending into her own tomb, which was, in a sense, exactly what she was doing.

She stood at the top of the drowned stair with lantern-light guttering behind her, and even soaked to the waist, even hunted by the very hunters she'd bought off, she looked immaculate—because Odile's kind of ruin always arrived dressed beautifully. Her eyes found the ledger against his chest first. Then the sigil. Then, last and least, Mirabel himself.

"Give it back," she said, "and I'll tell them you died in the flood. There's a version of this where we both walk out."

"There isn't," Mirabel said. His voice sounded strange to him—too loud, too alive, in a room so full of the dead. "You know there isn't. You've known since you sold my name."

Her jaw tightened. In the lantern light he watched her do the arithmetic she had done her whole life: assets, liabilities, exits. And he watched her arrive, at last, at the sum she could not escape.

"You were the best of them," she said quietly. "The only one who ever listened properly."

"That was the problem." He opened the ledger.

The sound it made was small—a spine cracking, pages parting—and it was the loudest thing Mirabel Villalobos had ever done. Because the moment the ledger opened in the presence of the sigil, the dead did not wait to be invited. Two hundred years of testimony rose out of the ink like heat off summer stone.

He began to read.

Not the guild's tidy columns—not the prices paid, the names erased, though those were there too, damning in their neatness. He read the marginalia. The hidden hand. The testimony the dead had pressed into the paper across generations of bone-carvers who half-heard them and wrote down what they could: *I owned the mill on the Aldenwater before there was an Aldenwater. My name was Sefa. I had three daughters.* *The king's charter was signed with magic bought from my grandmother, and she was never paid.* *We are the borders. We are what was drowned. Count us.*

And the sigil answered.

Light came up through the black water like dawn coming up through deep sea, and the remembered truth of old Vessany poured out of the door and into the city above—not as flood but as *memory*, rising through cellars and streets and crown halls, filling every room with the sound of names. Mirabel felt each name leave him as he spoke it, felt it pass through him like a coin through a scale, and with every name the gift grew quieter, the way a river quiets when it reaches the sea.

Above them, distantly, he heard the city begin to change. Bells ringing themselves. Doors unlocking. The witch-hunters' bounty lists curling into blank paper. A kingdom built on stolen magic discovering, all at once, that its foundation had been itemized.

Odile screamed at him to stop. Then she begged. Then, when neither worked, she waded toward him through the rising light, reaching for the ledger—and the debts reached back. He saw it happen and could not look away: every unpaid price, every sold name, every erased account she had ever balanced came due at once, called in by the sigil itself, and the water took her the way water takes a ledger dropped from a bridge—swiftly, without malice, without ceremony. She went under still reaching, and the last expression on her face was not fear. It was arithmetic, finally finished.

The light held. The reading went on. And Mirabel read until there was nothing left in him to read with.

* * *

When Cassian Roe came down the drowned stair, his lantern found a man sitting in knee-deep glowing water beside an open book, surrounded by a silence so complete it looked like frost.

"Sergeant," Mirabel said—or tried to say. His mouth moved. Nothing came out. He touched his own throat, puzzled, then laughed—a silent laugh, terrifying and free—and tapped his ear. Tapped his chest. Shook his head.

Cassian understood. He had spent three weeks reading confiscated ledgers in a guardhouse cellar, three weeks watching the neat columns of erased names add up to something no oath could survive. He had come down these stairs prepared to arrest a fugitive or kneel to a prophet, and instead he found a man who had paid everything he had and was smiling about it.

"The city's waking up," Cassian said, because someone had to say something ordinary. "They're pulling the bounty boards down in the lowquarters. Someone's chalked your ledger's first line on the customs house wall. Everyone's copying it."

Mirabel looked up at him. Around them, the sigil's light was softening into something steadier—not a door anymore, or not only a door. An archive. A country's memory, open for business.

Cassian set his lantern down on a dry ledge, shrugged out of his coat, and sat down in the water beside the man who could no longer hear the dead, because now, at last, the living would have to say it all themselves.

"Well then," he said. "Teach me to read it."
