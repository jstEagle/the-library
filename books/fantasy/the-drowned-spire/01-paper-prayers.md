## 1. Paper Prayers

The fish market of Greyharbor smelled the way it always did on a Tuesday: like money that had gone slightly off. Wren Thornbury had long ago made her peace with the stench of brine and old nets, mostly because complaining about it was free but breathing was not, and she intended to keep doing the latter.

"Charms!" she called, holding one up between two fingers so the morning light caught the cheap gilt edging. "Blessed charms against drowning, dampness, despair, and — for a copper more — your mother-in-law!"

A fishwife with forearms like dock rope snorted. "You're a fraud, Thornbury."

"I'm an *artisan*," Wren corrected, "of the paper persuasion. There's a difference. A fraud takes your money and gives you nothing. I take your money and give you *paper*. Beautifully folded paper. With a prayer inside."

"And does the prayer work?"

"The prayer," Wren said solemnly, "works exactly as well as you believe it does. Which is why I sell to optimists." She pressed a charm into the woman's hand before she could argue — protection from gulls, three coppers — and moved along before the woman could remember she hadn't agreed to buy anything.

This was the whole architecture of Wren's livelihood: speed, charm, and the careful management of other people's hope. The charms were genuine in every way that mattered, which was to say they contained prayers written out in her own small, neat hand, folded into wax-sealed packets, and absolutely no magic whatsoever. Magic was outlawed in Greyharbor. Had been for a generation. The Wardens enforced the ban with grey cloaks and greyer expressions, and they did not have a sense of humor about it.

Which was unfortunate, because Wren's actual gift was the sort of thing that would get her hanged if anyone thought about it too hard.

Her ink remembered.

Not in any way the Wardens' manuals described — she couldn't scry through it or set it crawling across parchment like a spider. It was smaller than that, quieter. Whatever her ink wrote, it *held*. Words written in her ink stayed true. A contract signed with her ink could not be forged over. A prayer written in her ink kept its shape in the mind longer than it had any right to. She'd discovered it at nine years old, copying temple records as an indentured scribe, when she noticed that pages she'd copied were the ones people came back asking about — the ones that seemed to *stick*.

She'd told no one. Not then, not since. In Greyharbor, a gift like hers wasn't a blessing; it was a confession waiting to be read aloud.

So she sold paper and prayer, and she kept her ink in a stoppered vial against her heart like the dangerous little secret it was, and she got through Tuesdays.

The market was loud in the usual ways — gulls screaming theft at each other overhead, fishmongers crying the freshness of things that had been dead considerably longer than they claimed, somewhere a child wailing about eels. Wren worked the edges of it, where the desperate people gathered. Desperate people bought charms. Comfortable people bought fish.

That was how she saw the widow.

She was standing by the old customs post, a thin woman in a coat that had been mended so many times it was less a garment than a philosophy, staring out at the grey water beyond the breakwater with the particular stillness of someone deciding something enormous. Wren knew that stillness. She'd worn it herself once, years ago, standing in this same market with four coppers and a hungry younger brother.

Wren drifted closer, professional instinct overriding the small voice that said *leave her alone, some griefs aren't inventory.*

"Widow?" she asked gently.

The woman turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, which meant she was past weeping and into the country beyond it. "Maren Salt. My husband — the sea took his boat two weeks back. They found the mast." She said it the way people recite things they've said so often the words have worn smooth. "No body. The temple won't say the rites without a body. Says he's not dead, just... unproven. And I can't bury a mast, can I? Can't mourn a *mast*."

"No," Wren said quietly. "You can't."

"They say—" Maren lowered her voice, glancing around. "They say the drowned god used to answer. Before the ban. That the old temple under the tide-line, the one they sealed — that petitions left there used to be *heard*."

Wren's stomach performed a slow, cold roll. "That's superstition. And also illegal to repeat near a Warden, so let's both be careful."

"I don't care about illegal." Maren's hands closed around Wren's wrist, surprisingly strong. "I want him home. Dead and buried and *home*, so I can put a stone down and stop listening for his boots on the stair. Do you understand? I'll pay anything. Do you have anything?"

Everything in Wren's training said: sell her the widow's-mourning charm, the standard one, folded paper and a nice verse about the sea giving back what it borrows. Two silvers. Everyone goes home.

But Maren's eyes were the eyes of a woman who had already paid everything and been given nothing, and Wren had never quite learned how to lie to those eyes.

"I have a petition charm," Wren heard herself say. "It's... stronger than my usual stock. Old rite. You write the wish yourself, in the packet, and leave it where the water can reach it. Under the old temple's threshold, if you're brave. That's all it is. Paper and prayer." She hesitated. "It doesn't work. I want to be honest with you — none of them truly work. But sometimes saying a thing properly helps a person carry it."

Maren paid her five silvers without blinking, which told Wren more about the woman's desperation than any words could. She wrote her wish right there at the customs post, shielding the paper with her body, and Wren looked away politely while the ink — *her* ink, dammit, why had she filled the packet from her own vial, habit, stupid sentimental habit — drank in the words.

When Maren walked away toward the drowned temple, shoulders straighter than they'd been in weeks, Wren stood in the fish market with five silvers in her palm and a feeling in her chest like the first cold drop before a storm.

*Paper and prayer,* she told herself. *That's all it is.*

The trouble started at dusk, which was when trouble in Greyharbor usually clocked in.

Wren was counting her day's take in her rented room above the chandler's shop — eleven silvers, a fistful of coppers, and one button someone had given her by mistake, which she decided to keep on principle — when the shouting began down by the water.

She should have stayed in. She wants it noted, later, that she *knew* she should have stayed in. But half the street was running toward the old temple district, and curiosity in Greyharbor is a tide: it doesn't ask permission.

They found Maren Salt sitting on the flooded steps of the sealed temple, perfectly calm, perfectly dry despite the knee-deep seawater lapping around her, smiling at the sunset.

"Maren?" someone called. "Maren, they found Tam! Down past the breakwater — the sea gave him back!"

And that should have been a miracle. That *was* a miracle, everyone said so, pushing forward to see the body laid out on the stones, whole and uncorrupted after two weeks in deep water, looking for all the world like a man asleep.

But Maren didn't go to him. Maren didn't weep. Maren sat on her step and smiled her empty smile and when her sister knelt beside her and shook her and shouted her name, Maren tilted her head with mild, polite interest, the way you'd look at a stranger asking directions.

"She doesn't know me," the sister whispered. "She doesn't— Maren, it's *Betta*, it's your sister—"

Wren stood at the edge of the crowd with her heart going like a drum in a storm, because she had seen that look before. Once. As a child, in the temple, on the face of an old man whose petition had been granted — back when the god still answered, back before the sealing. The scribes had a word for it. *Hollowed.* The god gave, and the god took payment, and the payment was always the same currency.

The crowd murmured — wonder, joy, fear. Someone said *the sea returned him*, someone else said *prayer works*, and a boy near Wren said, very quietly, "My gran says the old god's waking up," in the tone of someone reporting a fire.

Then Wren saw the grey cloaks pushing through the crowd, and her survival instincts finally outvoted her curiosity.

Two Wardens. The tall one was crouched by the body; the shorter one had picked up something from the water at Maren's feet — something pale and wax-sealed that caught the last light.

Wren knew that packet. She'd folded it herself that morning. Her gilt-edged paper, her seal, her prayer, written in her remembering ink, and it was sitting in a Warden's glove like a signed confession.

*Leave now,* said every sensible voice she owned. *Walk away. Don't run, running looks guilty, just walk—*

The short Warden turned the packet over, frowning at the seal, and said something to his partner. The tall one straightened and scanned the crowd — not searching for anything in particular yet, just doing the terrible patient arithmetic Wardens did, connecting a charm to a widow to a corpse to whoever had sold it.

Wren walked. Briskly. Like a woman with errands. She was three streets gone before she let herself breathe, and six before she understood the shape of what was coming for her: by tomorrow, every Warden in Greyharbor would know a charm-seller named Thornbury had sold the widow her miracle. By tomorrow, Maren's family would know it too, and grieving families are not famous for their patience.

She stopped in a dark doorway and pressed her hand flat against her chest, against the little vial of ink warming there like a second heartbeat.

Something old had answered a prayer today. Something that took memories as payment, something the city had sealed away a generation ago and told itself stories about forgetting. And it had answered through *her* charm, *her* ink, *her* careless five silvers.

"Paper and prayer," she whispered to the empty street. "Right."

Above the rooftops, far off toward the drowned temple, the water in the old flood-channels was moving against the tide — sliding uphill, slow and silver and wrong, like something beneath the city turning over in its sleep.

Wren pulled her hood up and went to find out what she'd woken.
