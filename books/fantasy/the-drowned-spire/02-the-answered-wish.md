## 2. The Answered Wish

Wren Thornbury had a rule about trouble: it always arrived before breakfast.

She was still chewing the heel of yesterday's loaf when the knock came — three sharp raps on her door above the chandler's shop, the kind of knocking that didn't want an answer so much as a door. She froze mid-chew. Through the floorboards she could hear old Master Pell grumbling at his customers below, which meant the shop was open, which meant whoever was upstairs had been *let* up, which meant they'd said something convincing or something frightening.

In Greyharbor those were usually the same thing.

"Wren." A woman's voice. Maren Quill, who sold eels two stalls down from Wren's corner in the fish market and had never once in six years climbed her stairs. "Wren, open the door. It's about the widow Sess."

The bread turned to chalk in Wren's mouth.

* * *

They found the widow Sess where she had lived for forty years, in a narrow house that leaned against its neighbor like a tired drunk. The neighbors had laid her out on her own settee because she wouldn't stay in bed — she kept getting up and wandering to the window, looking out at the harbor as though she expected it to explain itself.

She wasn't hurt. That was the terrible part. She sat with her hands folded in her lap, pink and healthy, blinking politely at everyone who came near, and when her daughter knelt in front of her and said "Mama, it's me, it's Lark," the widow smiled the way you smile at a stranger's baby.

"Hello," she said pleasantly. "Have we met?"

Her memories hadn't been stolen so much as scooped out, clean as a gull taking fish from a barrel. Her name, her daughter, her forty years of mending nets and burying friends and singing off-key at the Tidefire festival — gone. What remained was a pleasant, empty courtesy, a person-shaped room with the furniture carried off.

And on the table beside her, exactly where she'd left it, lay Wren's charm.

It looked so harmless. A twist of grey paper and waxed thread, a scrap of ink inside bearing words Wren herself had written three days ago: *For the widow Sess, that her heart's wish find its way home.* She'd charged her four coppers. Four coppers, and a prayer that was mostly packaging.

Lark held the charm up with a hand that shook. "You sold her this."

"I sell them to half the city," Wren said, and heard how it sounded even as she said it.

"You told her it would work."

"I tell everyone it will work. That's what makes them *buy* them." Wren reached for the charm, and Lark snatched it back. Behind her, two of the widow's nephews filled the doorway with the particular patience of men deciding whether today was a day for talking.

"The Wardens are coming," one of them said. "Someone sent word this morning. Magic answered, magic marked. They'll want the charm-seller."

"They'll want *you*," Lark said, quieter. "I'm telling you because my mother liked you. Because you used to slip her extra blessings and never charge. Run, Wren Thornbury. And take your paper gods with you."

Wren ran.

* * *

Here is what nobody in Greyharbor understood about charms, including most of the people who sold them: ninety-nine of every hundred did nothing at all. They were paper and prayer, as Wren liked to say, emphasis on the paper. A charm worked because the buyer believed it worked, and belief was free, and four coppers was four coppers. Wren had built a small, warm, dishonest living on that arithmetic, and she slept fine at night, mostly, except in winter.

But there was one thing she'd never told anyone, not even Maren, not even in the years when she'd been too young to know better than to have secrets: her ink remembered.

Whatever Wren wrote with her own hand, mixed with her own spit and a pinch of ash, stayed written somewhere deeper than paper. Words that should have faded clung. Once, aged nine, she'd written *I hate you* about a boy who pulled her braids, and for a week the boy had flinched whenever she passed, though she'd burned the page. She'd learned then that her gift was small and strange and absolutely, catastrophically illegal, and she had spent her whole life since making it smaller. Harmless charms. Paper prayers. Nothing that could ever, ever be traced back to the girl whose ink remembered.

Except now something had taken her harmless words and *used* them. Something had read *her heart's wish find its way home* and decided to deliver.

Wren cut through the tannery rows with her hood up and her heart going like a drum in a parade. The Wardens would come to her rooms. They'd find her stock of charms, her ledger of sales — oh, saints, her *ledger*, her neat little book of names and purchases, which suddenly looked less like bookkeeping and more like a confession. Every name in it was a petitioner. Every petitioner was a door something might walk through.

How many had she sold? Forty? Fifty? How many wishes were out there in the wet grey city, waiting to be granted?

* * *

The drowned temple stood at the edge of the Saltmarsh district where the city gave up pretending to be dry land. A generation ago it had been the pride of Greyharbor — the Temple of the Tidewife, some called her god, others the Drowned One, others nothing at all because naming him aloud had become a crime along with everything else. Then came the Edict, and the Wardens, and the elders of the temple itself bending their knees to the new order so fast you could hear them squeak. The tide had done the rest, creeping up through the foundations year by year until the lower halls were permanent black water and the upper ones were home to gulls and rats and, once, to a thin indentured girl with quick hands and remembering ink.

Wren had copied contracts there for five years. Ledgers, mostly. Debt-records. She'd been eleven and her family had been hungry, and the temple paid in bread, and children with small hands made fewer mistakes on fine script. She had left at sixteen with calloused fingers, a hatred of ledgers, and no idea what half the documents she'd copied actually *said*.

Now she waded up the flooded processional path as the light went copper behind the western hills, and the temple rose ahead of her like a ribcage the sea had swallowed and partially digested. Gulls argued on the broken dome. The great doors hung open, warped, furred with salt-moss.

Inside smelled the way it always had: brine and stone and old incense gone to dust. Wren picked her way across the cracked mosaic floor — the Tidewife's face, all but worn away, though one mosaic eye still gleamed green under the grime — and stopped at the edge of the nave, where the altar dais rose out of shallow floodwater.

She'd come here because it was the only place in Greyharbor she knew how to be invisible. She'd told herself she was hiding, full stop, and if a small voice pointed out that hiding places were usually chosen closer to exits, she told it to hush.

Then she saw the water.

The floodwater in the nave was, and had always been, dead water. Stagnant, scummed, smelling of rot. Every child in the Saltmarsh knew not to drink it, and every child drank it anyway during bad summers and regretted it.

This morning, pooled in the basin of the altar itself, the water was *clear*. Clear as glass, clear as the mountain springs in the songs, moving gently against no current Wren could feel, catching the last copper light and holding it like a coin.

Fresh water. In the drowned temple. On the altar of a god the whole city had agreed to forget.

Wren stood very still. Somewhere overhead a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"Right," she said aloud, because her voice steadied her and because there was no one left to lie to. "So you're awake. Wonderful. Excellent timing. I don't suppose you take complaints?"

The water didn't answer, but as she spoke, its surface shivered — not with her voice, she was almost sure, but with something underneath, attentive, patient, the way the sea is patient.

Because here was the shape of it, the shape she'd been refusing to look at since the widow's empty eyes: the charm had worked. Not the paper — the paper was rubbish, the paper was four coppers of comfort. But the *wish* had worked, the prayer beneath the prayer, and prayers needed a listener. Something had heard a lonely old woman wish for her heart's desire, and something had granted it in the manner of old things granting wishes: literally, terribly, taking *home* to mean the grave of memory, taking everything and leaving politeness behind.

And it had known to use *her* charm. Her words. Her remembered ink.

Which meant either the god of this temple had woken after a generation of silence and chosen Wren Thornbury — charm-seller, fraud, ex-debt-child — as its instrument, or something wearing the god's silence had.

Neither option improved her mood.

Wren crouched at the edge of the dais and looked into the clear water. Her reflection looked back, thin and sharp-faced, hood fallen back, hair escaping its braid in salt-stiff wisps. Behind her reflected shoulder, the ruined nave stretched away into shadow, and for just a moment — she would swear this later, swearing it on bread and salt — the shadows of the pillars were wrong. Too deep. Arranged, almost, like columns in a court.

Somewhere far below the waterline, faint as a pulse, something knocked. Three slow beats, like a hand on a door.

Like a petition being received.

"Oh," Wren said softly. "You're not just listening. You're *answering*. And I'm the one holding the pen."

She thought of the widow Sess smiling her pleasant stranger's smile. She thought of her ledger sitting in her rooms, waiting for the Wardens' torches, full of fifty names and fifty wishes. She thought of her mother, who had signed her over to this temple's care without ever explaining why, and who had died still not explaining, and whose face Wren found she was suddenly, desperately trying to remember clearly — the exact color of her laugh, the scar on her thumb — as though memory itself had become a thing that could be scooped out and carried off.

The water pulsed again. Three beats. Patient. Expectant.

Wren rolled up her sleeves, took out her little pot of ink, and began, with the practiced calm of a woman doing something extremely unwise, to write.

Behind her, at the far end of the nave, boot heels rang on wet stone — measured, official, and entirely too steady for a gull.

She didn't turn around. "If you're a Warden," she said to the clear water, "now would be a lovely time to prove you're listening."
