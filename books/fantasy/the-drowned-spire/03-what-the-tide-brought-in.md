## 3. What the Tide Brought In

The tide had been out for three hours when Caspian Hale found the first footprint.

It pressed into the black silt of the drowned temple's threshold, small, quick-striding, the heel dug deep as though its owner had been running and had decided, somewhere in the last hundred yards, to stop running. Caspian crouched over it for a long moment, his Warden's coat — no longer his, officially; he'd been made to hand over the badge but they'd let him keep the coat, which was either mercy or an insult, and with Captain Brannock it was usually both — hanging open in the sea wind.

"You're a fool," he told himself quietly. "You know that."

He did know that. He was a disgraced Warden tracking a fugitive charm-seller into a forbidden temple on the strength of a hunch and a prayer he wasn't supposed to believe in anymore. If Brannock caught him here, he wouldn't be demoted again. There was nothing left to demote him *to*. They'd give him a shovel and point him at the oyster beds.

But the footprint was there, and so was he.

He followed it down into the temple.

* * *

Three days earlier, they had pulled Marta Ellery out of her own kitchen, sitting upright at her table with her hands folded like a woman waiting politely for tea that would never come. Her eyes had been open. Her eyes were always the worst part, Caspian thought. Empty the way a house is empty after the furniture's been sold — not ruined, just *vacated*, swept clean down to the floorboards.

And on the table beside her, one of Wren Thornbury's paper charms, ink still faintly wet.

The Wardens had gone for Wren Thornbury by sundown. Caspian had gone home and sat in the dark and thought about his brother.

Tam had bought a prayer two years ago. Not from Thornbury — from a temple elder, back when the elders still pretended their petitions went somewhere. Tam had prayed for their mother's fever to break, and it had broken, and their mother had lived another four years, and Tam had spent those four years slowly forgetting how to laugh. Forgetting names. Forgetting, near the end, Caspian's face, looking at him across the breakfast table with polite blankness, like a stranger who'd wandered in from the rain.

Caspian had reported Tam to the Wardens himself. It was procedure. Unregistered affliction of the mind, possible magical cause. He'd given testimony — clean, thorough, professional testimony — and watched them carry his brother off to the sanatorium, where Tam still sat today, hands folded, eyes open, waiting for tea.

Caspian had told himself it was duty. He had told himself that for two years, the way you tell yourself a rope holds because you haven't yet looked down.

Then Marta Ellery died with a paper charm at her elbow, and Caspian understood, all at once and far too late, that the rope had never been tied to anything at all.

So: the hunch. The prayer-sellers' charms were paper and prayer, everyone said so. But someone was answering. And if someone was answering, then the old god under Greyharbor — the Drowned One, the Tide-Father, the name you weren't supposed to say above a whisper — was awake. And if He was awake, then maybe He kept accounts. Maybe He remembered every prayer ever fed to Him, including a fevered woman's plea and a boy's desperate coin.

Maybe He could tell Caspian what exactly his testimony had purchased.

He descended the temple steps into air that smelled of cold stone and older water.

* * *

Wren heard him coming before she saw him — boots on wet stone, the particular careful tread of a man trying not to sound like a Warden and failing, because Wardens always walked like the floor owed them money.

She pressed herself behind the fallen statue of some saint whose face had been eaten away by salt, her heart going like a fisherman's mallet, and watched a broad-shouldered man pick his way down into the flooded nave. He carried no lantern. That was strange. Stranger still, he didn't seem to need one, because the water was glowing.

That was the thing Wren hadn't been able to stop staring at since sundown. The floodwater that filled the old temple's lower reaches — stagnant, brackish, dead as ditchwater for as long as anyone could remember — was lit from beneath with a soft green-gold shimmer, and drifting through that light, unmistakable, was a sigil. A spiral coiled around a single vertical line, like a wave standing up to be counted. She'd copied that sigil a thousand times as an indentured child-scribe, grinding ink in this very building, before the ban came and the temple fell silent. The Tide-Father's mark. And it was *glowing*, and the water around it moved in slow, deliberate spirals, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Thornbury." The man's voice echoed off the vaulting. "I know you're here. Your boot prints are the only ones going in that don't come out."

Wren considered her options. They were, broadly: stay hidden until he left (he wouldn't), run (into what?), or step out and see whether a disgraced Warden was more dangerous than a hungry god.

"I didn't kill Marta Ellery," she said, stepping into the light.

The man turned. Up close he had a weathered face and tired eyes and the look of someone who had been carrying something heavy for years and had recently discovered it was heavier than he'd thought. His hand rested on his baton without gripping it.

"I know," he said.

That took the wind out of her. "...You know?"

"The charm was bait, Thornbury. Someone wanted you found at that scene. Your paper-and-prayer rubbish doesn't answer anything — I've confiscated forty of them. They're confetti with ambitions." He paused. "Something else answered her. Something that wanted a trail leading to a charm-seller."

Wren's legs felt suddenly unreliable. She sat down on the statue's plinth, mostly on purpose. "The widow came to my stall. She was crying. She asked for a prayer for her son lost at sea, and I sold her the usual nonsense, and I told her — gods forgive me — I told her it would work, because that's what you say, that's the whole trade, you sell people the *saying* of it—"

"And it worked," Caspian said quietly.

"It worked." Wren laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you understand? In twenty years nobody's prayer has worked. That's the arrangement. That's the whole *bargain* of this city — we gave up magic and the gods went quiet and everybody gets to keep their soul intact. And now some old thing downstairs is taking orders again, and it takes them *literally*, and it leaves people hollow as gull eggs, and it used *my handwriting* to do it."

The man was silent a moment. Then he said, "Hale. Caspian Hale. Formerly of the Wardens, currently of nowhere in particular." He hesitated, and she watched him decide to say the rest. "My brother prayed once. To the old temple, before the ban. His prayer was answered too."

Wren looked up sharply. "Your brother is empty-eyed? Hands folded? Sitting somewhere waiting for tea that isn't coming?"

Caspian went very still. "How could you possibly know that."

"Because I've seen it before." Her voice dropped. "When I was nine, indentured here as a scribe, there was a petition-clerk named Sorrel Fenn. Bright as a bell. He prayed for his daughter's illness to lift, and it lifted, and within a season he couldn't remember her name. The elders called it 'grace-fading.' They said it was holy. They filed him away in the undercrofts with the others." She wrapped her arms around herself. "I used to bring him bread. He'd thank me. Every day he thanked me, like it was the first time, because for him it *was* the first time."

The water glowed between them, patient as a held breath.

"So it's been happening for twenty years," Caspian said slowly. "Quietly. Small numbers. The elders knew."

"The elders *ran* it." The certainty arrived in Wren fully formed, terrible and clean, like a wave finding its shape. "They must have. Sorrel Fenn didn't pray to a wall, Hale. Somebody passed his prayer along. Somebody kept the pipe open when the rest of us thought it was sealed."

"Which means somebody's been harvesting souls for twenty years," Caspian said, "and using a god to do it."

They looked at each other across the shining water, and Wren saw her own expression mirrored on his face: the particular horror of two people realizing the ground beneath them had been hollow all along.

"Why are you really here, Hale?" she asked. "Not for me. Wardens don't track fugitives alone, at night, without a lantern."

He was quiet long enough that the tide sighed against the outer steps. "Because I want to ask it a question," he said finally. "The thing that answers. I want to know what my brother's prayer cost, and who collected, and whether there's anything left of him to get back." His jaw tightened. "And because I testified that he was afflicted. I handed him to the sanatorium myself. Procedure." He said the word like spitting out a bone. "If there's a debt anywhere in this city with my name on it, I want to find it and pay it."

Wren thought about telling him to go away. She thought about the fact that he was, technically, hunting her; that trusting a Warden was a category error she'd survived twenty-three years by never making. But he'd come without a lantern into a god's house, and he'd said *pay it*, and grief like his didn't lie.

"Here's what I know," she said. "There's fresh water on the altar upstairs. Fresh — in a temple flooded with brine for twenty years. Something's listening, and it's listening *here*, and I intend to ask it why my prayers started this." She stood. "We go together or not at all. You don't arrest me, I don't run. Truce."

"A truce," Caspian repeated, as if tasting the word for rot.

"Born of shared guilt. It's the strongest kind. Nobody in it has any illusions about anybody else."

Something that was almost a smile crossed his tired face. "Thornbury, you're going to be trouble."

"I'm already trouble, Hale. I'm the most wanted woman in Greyharbor. What I'm going to be is *thorough*."

They climbed together toward the altar, and the sigil in the water brightened as they passed, following them like a lantern being politely offered.

At the top of the steps, Wren stopped.

The altar basin was full — brimming, overflowing slightly, clear water catching the moonlight through the broken roof. And floating on its surface, ink-black and impossible, was a sheet of fine paper. Blank. Waiting.

Beside it lay a pen.

"It wants us to write," Caspian said.

"No," Wren breathed, reaching out with fingers that shook. The paper was good stock — temple stock, the kind she'd ground ink for as a child. And the pen was silver-nibbed, ceremonial, the petition-pen of the high clerk. She knew it. She had *cleaned* it.

"Hale," she whispered. "This is my old master's pen. This is the pen that wrote the contracts."

The water in the basin began, very softly, to glow.
