## 4. The Ledger Beneath

The chest had been waiting for her under the floorboards of her mother's house for eleven years, and it smelled like it.

Wren knelt in the abandoned kitchen with the boards pried up around her, dust in her hair, salt rot in her nose, and a chest of blackened oak open before her like a mouth that had finally decided to speak. Outside, Greyharbor went about its morning — gulls screaming over the fish market, the bell of the Warden station tolling the hour, someone two streets over beating a rug with the rhythm of a person who had never once been hunted. She envied them all.

Caspian Hale was out buying them bread and, more importantly, not being seen buying her anything. Ossa Vane had vanished at dawn with a promise to "ask around the mountain" that Wren suspected meant *sell information to whoever pays*, but she'd paid him half up front and hope was a currency she couldn't afford to be picky about. Which left her alone with the chest, the widow's empty eyes fresh behind her own, and the small terrible fact that she had come home.

Not home, exactly. Her mother's house. The difference mattered. Home was a word for places that wanted you back.

She lifted out the first bundle of papers and knew the hand on the outside before she read a word. She would have known it anywhere. It was her own.

*Child copies,* the temple scribes had called them. Every indentured child copied records by candlelight — contracts, tithes, petitions, the great rolling accounts of what the drowned temple owed and was owed. It taught penmanship, the elders said. It taught obedience. Mostly it taught children that their lives were entries in other people's arithmetic. Wren had copied thousands of pages between the ages of seven and twelve, her small fingers cramping around a quill, copying words she wasn't permitted to understand.

But her ink remembered. That was the gift she'd hidden even then, the one that got her sold out of the temple and into debt-servitude, or so she'd always believed: whatever her ink wrote, it kept. A charm written in her hand held its prayer a little too well. And these pages — her own childish script, faithful as a mirror — were still warm with everything they'd ever copied.

She smoothed the first page flat on the kitchen table and let her thumb rest against the ink, and the memory of the original rose up through her skin like cold water through cloth.

*Petition of Merren Salt, widow, aged 54. Requested: return of husband from the sea. Granted. Price assessed.*

Price assessed. The phrase sat there, patient as a hook. In the old records, granted wishes weren't miracles. They were transactions. Someone paid. Someone collected. And the collector —

She pulled the next bundle. Then the next. The kitchen grew dim around her; she didn't notice. Page after page rose under her thumb, and slowly, entry by entry, a shape assembled itself out of her childhood handwriting, a shape she had copied a hundred times without ever seeing:

*The Court Below the Mountain.*

It wasn't a story the dockworkers told to frighten each other. It was an institution. It had officers. It had *rates*. Souls entered as collateral against prayers granted, held at interest, redeemable at terms the court alone set. There were schedules of assessment — a memory of a wedding priced against a memory of a mother's face; a first love weighed against a name. There were forfeiture clauses. There was, God help her, a *grace period*.

And there was a ledger. One ledger, singular, capital-L, maintained in fire — because ink could be forged, stone could be chiseled, but flame, true flame, remembered what it burned and could not lie. The court's whole authority rested on a fire that never went out.

Wren set the page down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping.

*When the fire dies,* wrote some long-dead clerk in her own eleven-year-old hand, *all accounts fall forfeit to the holder.*

The holder. Not the petitioners. Not the souls. Whoever held the ledger when the fire guttered out would own every soul bound in it, free and clear, thousands of them, a city's worth of collateral falling into one pair of hands.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty kitchen, because her mother had always said fear spoken aloud shrinks to fit the room. "That's fine. That's completely fine."

Her voice did the thing voices do in empty kitchens, which is sound smaller than intended.

* * *

It took her until well past noon to work up the courage to touch the last bundle, and she knew why she was avoiding it. She'd known since she saw the wax seal on the outside — temple blue, the sigil of the Seneschal's office, pressed with a signet she'd traced a thousand times as a child because it was the prettiest thing in the copying room.

A contract. Original format, child copy. Dated nineteen years ago.

She made tea first. There was no reason to make tea. The kettle was full of rust and the leaves were older than her grief, but her hands wanted a task that wasn't trembling, so she made tea she wouldn't drink and carried the cup to the table like a woman walking to her own trial.

She put her thumb to the page.

The room went away.

She was small again, seven years old and barefoot on cold flagstone, copying by tallow-light while the original lay open before the supervising elder. But this time she was also herself, kneeling in her mother's kitchen, and both selves watched the same words surface:

*Contract of Surety, executed this day between the Temple of the Drowned God, Greyharbor, party of the first part, and Maren Thornbury, party of the second part.*

Her mother's name. Her mother's actual name, in the summary of parties, and below it, in the terms, her mother's signature — the real one, the one Wren knew better than her own heartbeat, the slightly crushed *M* because her mother always pressed too hard when she was frightened.

*In consideration of the remission of debts outstanding against the household of said Maren Thornbury, and the guarantee of continued sustenance and shelter for said household through the famine years ensuing, the party of the second part conveys unto the Court Below the Mountain, as surety and collateral—*

Wren read the next line four times. Her lips moved. Nothing came out.

*—one (1) soul, being that of Wren Thornbury, daughter of the contracting party, aged five (5) years, to be held at the court's pleasure until redeemed or forfeit.*

Five years old. She'd been five. She'd thought her childhood in the temple — the copying, the chores, the five years of it before they'd transferred her to debt-servitude with the fishmongers — had been misfortune. Bad luck. A family too poor to keep her. She'd built her whole self on top of that story the way Greyharbor built its houses on pilings: carefully, knowing the water was underneath, never looking down.

Her mother hadn't lost her. Her mother had *signed* for her. Had walked into the Seneschal's office — the same office whose seal was on this copy — and traded her daughter's soul for bread through the famine years. For the roof. For survival. And the court had held the paper for nineteen years, collecting interest, waiting, while Wren grew up selling paper prayers three streets away from the thing that owned her.

The teacup cracked in her hand. She looked down at it with mild surprise, blood welling along her palm where the glaze had bitten, and found that she felt almost nothing, which is how you know a feeling is too large to fit through the door yet.

"That's not true," she told the page.

The page, being ink, did not argue. Ink never argues. That was the whole trouble with ink; it just remembers.

But her ink remembered something else, too. Because she had copied this contract as a child, and children notice things, and her thumb slid to the margin where her seven-year-old self had — in defiance of every rule, in the tiniest possible letters — annotated the original. She'd forgotten doing it. She'd forgotten for nineteen years.

*Why does Mama cry in the Seneschal's office?*

She had seen her. She had *seen* her mother sign it, from the copying room doorway, and she'd written down the question instead of asking it, because asking questions got children reassigned, and somewhere in nineteen years the question had sunk down into the ink and waited.

*Mama cries.* Not *Mama signs gladly*. Not *Mama betrays*. Cries. Presses the M too hard because her hands are shaking. Sits across from the Seneschal with her shoulders folded in like a woman standing in weather.

Wren stood up. Sat down. Stood up again and paced the length of the ruined kitchen, holding her bleeding hand against her apron, talking to a dead woman.

"You cried." Her voice cracked in the middle. "You cried and you signed anyway, and I'm supposed to — what? Forgive that? You gave them *me*, Mum. You gave them the only part of me that — "

That was the thing, wasn't it. The part that explained everything. Why her gift had felt cursed from the beginning. Why the temple had noticed her ink so young. Why the debt-servitude after the temple had lasted exactly as long as it took for her to be no use to anyone's ledger anymore. She hadn't stumbled into any of it. She'd been *collateral* her entire life, walking around Greyharbor with a price tag sewn into her soul, and everyone who'd ever told her she was unlucky had been reading from the wrong book.

The ground of her opened. Not dramatically — nothing so kind as an earthquake. Just quietly, the way pilings rot: everything she stood on suddenly uncertain, every memory re-sorting itself. The day her mother taught her to write. Was that love, or was that training? The lullaby about the sea taking back what it lent. Was that comfort, or a warning? The last thing her mother ever said to her — *be careful what you write, little bird* — had sounded like tenderness for nineteen years, and now it sounded like a woman who knew exactly what her daughter's writing was worth and to whom.

Outside, the gulls went on screaming. The rug-beater had stopped. Somewhere a Warden's whistle blew twice, short and sharp, and Wren realized she'd been sitting in her mother's house for hours with the door unlocked, like bait in a trap she'd set for herself.

She gathered the contract copy with hands that shook and folded it into her coat, against her heart, because apparently even betrayal gets carried close to the chest.

The widow's prayer had been answered. The god was listening again. The court below the mountain held her soul at interest, and the fire that kept the ledger was dying, and when it died, everything fell forfeit to the holder.

Which raised the question she'd been circling all afternoon without letting herself land on it, the question that now landed, heavy as an anchor:

*Who holds the ledger?*

And beneath it, quieter, worse, the question shaped like her mother's crushed M:

*What else did she sign?*

The front door creaked. Wren spun, quill already in her fist, ink-black and ready — and Caspian Hale filled the doorway, pale, breathless, bread forgotten on the step behind him.

"Wren," he said. "You need to see what the tide brought in."
