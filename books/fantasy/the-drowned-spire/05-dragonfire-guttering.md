## 5. Dragonfire, Guttering

The entrance to the old Vane mine was hidden behind a fishmonger's stall, which Caspian Hale thought was either very clever or very Greyharbor.

"Mind the eels," said Ossa Vane, hauling aside a crate of brine-packed kelp. Beneath it, a rusted hatch sat flush with the cobblestones, its iron ring worn smooth by generations of hands that officially did not exist. "They get in during spring tides. Nothing dangerous. Just judgmental."

Caspian looked at the woman who was to guide them into the mountain. Ossa Vane was perhaps fifty, built like a barrel that had been in several fights and won most of them, with a coil of grey-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be an actual harpoon tip. She wore a smuggler's coat with forty pockets, and he had the distinct impression that at least ten of them contained knives and the other thirty contained things worse than knives.

"You're certain this leads to the Court Below the Mountain," he said. It wasn't quite a question. He'd spent three days verifying her credentials through channels that didn't officially exist either, and everything he'd found said the same thing: Ossa Vane moved cargo no one else would touch, and she'd never once lost a shipment or a client. The second part was the part that worried him.

"I'm certain it leads *down*," Ossa said. "Down is down is down. Eventually you hit the court, or you hit something that used to be the court, or you hit rock and we all have a good laugh about it in whatever afterlife takes smugglers." She spat expertly into the gutter. "Your coin's good either way."

Behind him, Wren Thornbury shifted her satchel higher on her shoulder. She'd barely spoken since the temple — since the fresh water on the altar, since the sigil glowing in the floodwater like an eye opening after a long sleep. She looked, Caspian thought, like someone carrying a letter she already knew was bad news.

"Three days' descent," Ossa went on, counting on thick fingers. "First day, mine-shafts, mostly safe. Second day, the warm veins — don't touch them, don't lick them, I know what you're thinking, everyone thinks about licking them—"

"I wasn't," said Wren.

"Everyone thinks about licking them." Ossa shouldered a lantern that burned with a greenish flame Caspian decided not to ask about. "Third day, we stop walking and start being quiet, because past the warm veins the stone starts listening back." She grinned, and it was not entirely a comforting sight. "Right. In you get. Mind the eels."

* * *

The first day was ladders, and Caspian hated every rung of it.

He'd been a Warden for eleven years. He knew how to march, how to stand watch, how to fill out forms in triplicate describing magical infractions he privately believed were about as dangerous as wet kindling. Nobody had told him that hunting a fugitive would eventually involve descending a ladder into the dark while a smuggler above him hummed a sea shanty with lyrics he was fairly sure were anatomically impossible.

"Tell me about your brother," Wren said somewhere below him, her voice floating up the shaft.

He nearly missed a rung. "What makes you think there's a brother?"

"Because you flinch every time someone mentions answered prayers. Because you pray — don't look shocked, I heard you at the temple, you've got a Warden's cadence but you weren't reciting the Concord litany. And because nobody tracks a charm-seller across half of Greyharbor out of duty." A pause. "So. Brother."

Caspian climbed in silence for a while. The lantern light wobbled below him, painting Ossa's shadow enormous against the shaft wall.

"Tomas," he said finally. "Younger. Better-looking. Terrible with money." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Six years ago his daughter was dying. Fever, the kind that takes children in Greyharbor every winter. The Wardens had sealed the last healers' shops. So he went to the drowned temple, back when everyone said it was dead, and he prayed."

"And it was answered."

"His daughter lived. Woke up the next morning healthy as a gull." Caspian's hands tightened on the ladder. "And Tomas came home hollowed. Didn't know my name. Didn't know his own. Sat by his daughter's bed smiling at her like she was a stranger he was fond of. They put him in the almshouse on Cordwainer Street. He's still there. Smiling."

The ladder creaked under him. Below, Wren said nothing, but he could feel her listening the way you feel weather change.

"When I found your charm at the widow's house," he went on, "I thought — here's the thread. Pull it, follow it, maybe find out what took my brother. And then I saw the god's sigil burning in the water, and I understood I hadn't been hunting a criminal. I'd been hunting a door." He laughed, short and bitter. "Eleven years enforcing a ban on magic, and the whole time there was a court under our feet trading in souls like herring."

"Careful," Ossa called up cheerfully. "Talk like that gets people promoted. Or disappeared. Depends on the magistrate."

* * *

On the second day they reached the warm veins, and Caspian understood why everyone thought about licking them.

The mine-shafts opened without warning into galleries of stone that glowed faintly from within, veins of amber light running through black rock like fire frozen mid-thought. The air grew warm — not summer-warm, but hearth-warm, the warmth of a room where someone has just left. The walls breathed heat against his palms when he touched them, and beneath the heat, so faint he might have imagined it, a rhythm. Slow. Labored.

Like breathing.

"It's the dragon," Wren whispered. Her hand lay flat against the glowing vein, and the light pulsed once, gently, under her fingers. "It's not dead. The records said the fire powered the ledger, but I thought — I thought it would be ashes by now. It's alive."

"Barely," Ossa said, and for the first time since the fish stall, there was nothing cheerful in her voice. She crouched beside the vein, holding her lantern low. "My grandmother ran this route when the fire was strong. Said you could read a broadsheet by the glow, whole galleries lit like festival night. Now?" She gestured at the dimming amber, the long stretches of cold black stone between the veins. "Look at the gaps. Every year the gaps get wider. My mother said the same. Her mother too. The fire's guttering, girl. And when it goes out—"

"The ledger closes," Wren said. Her face had gone pale in the amber light. "No. Worse. The Seneschal said — the contracts are written in fire. If the fire dies before a debt is discharged, the collateral is forfeit. All of it. Every soul in that ledger, forfeit to the court. Permanently." She wrapped her arms around herself. "Mine included."

"How many?" Caspian asked quietly.

Wren stared at the vein. "The ledgers I copied as a child — one scribe's worth, five years of entries — named four thousand souls. That was one desk. One clerk. Thirty years ago." She swallowed. "There could be tens of thousands. Everyone whose wish came true too easily. Every widow, every desperate father, every child sold by a parent who thought they were buying survival."

The warmth of the stone suddenly felt less like comfort and more like a held breath.

Caspian thought of Tomas, smiling at his daughter in the almshouse. Somewhere in that ledger was his brother's name, written in dragonfire, and the fire was going out.

"We move faster," he said.

"Faster's fine," Ossa said, standing and brushing off her knees. "Faster's also louder. Pick one."

* * *

They made camp that night in a gallery where two great veins crossed, casting enough light to see by. Ossa produced bread, hard cheese, and a bottle of something that tasted like it had been aged inside a boot, and they ate in the pulsing amber glow while the mountain breathed around them.

Caspian couldn't sleep. He sat propped against the warm stone, watching the vein nearest him pulse — slow, slow, slower — and found himself doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. The gaps between pulses were longer than they'd been that morning. He was almost sure of it.

"Can't sleep either?"

Wren settled beside him, knees drawn up, her satchel clutched in her lap like a child holds a doll. Up close, in the dragon-light, she looked very young and very tired and absolutely unwilling to admit to either.

"Tell me what you're not saying," he said.

She was quiet a moment. "I found my name today. In the copies I brought. My mother signed a contract — my soul, as collateral. That's why I was indentured to the temple. That's why any of this—" She stopped, pressed her lips together. "I keep trying to make it make sense. She was keeping us alive. There was famine. But she signed *my name*, Caspian, and she never told me, and I can't decide if that makes her a monster or just another drowning woman grabbing for a rope."

"She could be both," Caspian said. "Most people are."

"That's the worst comforting thing anyone's ever said to me."

"I'm a disgraced Warden. Comfort isn't in my training." He nudged her shoulder with his. "But listen — whatever's in that ledger, we're going to see it clearly. That's the job now. Not the Wardens' version, not the temple elders' version. The truth, and then whatever we can burn of the rest."

Something in her face eased, just slightly. "You sound like you mean it."

"I've spent six years watching my brother smile at strangers. I mean it with my whole chest."

Across the gallery, Ossa snored with the deep contentment of a woman with forty pockets and no conscience, and the vein behind Caspian's head pulsed — slow, slow, slow — like a heart deciding whether to bother beating again.

* * *

On the third day, the stone began to listen.

Ossa had warned them, and she'd been right: past the warm veins, the air changed. Sound behaved strangely — their footsteps arrived a half-beat late, as though the mountain were considering each one before permitting it. Their whispers came back to them softened, almost kindly, like a parent repeating a child's words. Once, Wren dropped her ink bottle, and the clatter echoed back to them before the bottle actually struck the ground.

Caspian chose not to think about that.

They walked in near-silence, following Ossa's lantern down a shaft so old its ladder-rungs had been replaced twice with different kinds of wood, until the smuggler abruptly raised a fist and stopped.

Ahead, the tunnel ended at a wall — except it wasn't a wall. It was a door, tall as three men, carved from a single slab of black stone, and across its surface ran lines of fire. Not the dim amber of the veins. Living fire, gold-white and restless, tracing script older than any alphabet Caspian knew. And the script was moving. Flickering. Guttering, like a candle in a draft.

"There it is," Ossa murmured. "Door to the court. Written in the big fellow's fire." She turned, and even her grin had gone solemn. "Last chance to turn around, Wardens. Past this door, prices go up."

Caspian looked at the flickering script. Each character burned a little lower than the last. He thought of four thousand names on one clerk's desk. Tens of thousands in the full ledger. Tomas among them, and Wren, and gods knew who else — every bound soul in Greyharbor riding on a fire that was visibly, measurably, dying.

"How long?" Wren asked. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "How long before it goes out?"

Ossa studied the door a long moment, reading fire the way sailors read sky.

"If I had to guess? Days." She shrugged. "Maybe hours. The mountain'll tell you when — whole place'll shudder, doors slam, magistrates scream. Very dramatic." She pulled a thin black key from pocket thirty-one. "So. Are we doing this?"

The door-script flickered. Somewhere beyond it, faint as memory, Caspian heard — or thought he heard — a voice. Low and labored and achingly familiar, speaking words in a language he didn't know, in a rhythm he did.

It sounded like his brother.

"Open it," said Wren Thornbury.
