## 6. The Court Below the Mountain

The last stretch of the mine-shaft was not a shaft at all but a throat — smooth-walled, warm, and breathing. Wren felt the breath of it against her face as they walked, a slow tide of air that smelled of hot stone and something sweeter underneath, like sugar left too long near a fire.

"That's not wind," Ossa Vane said over her shoulder. The smuggler had gone quiet for most of the descent, which Wren had privately counted among the day's small mercies. Now her voice came out flat and careful, the way people spoke in churches. "That's lungs."

"Whose?" Caspian asked.

"You'll see." Ossa's lantern swung. "Or you won't, and it won't matter much either way."

Wren had stopped being frightened somewhere around the third hour underground, and she wasn't sure what to make of that. Fear, she'd always assumed, was like ink — once it got into you, it stayed. But this was different. This was the particular calm of a person walking toward an answer she had already written herself, years ago, in a child's careful hand: *the Court Below the Mountain, where names are kept as coin.* She had copied those words fifty times as an indentured scribe, believing them fairy tale, believing them safe because they were old.

Her mother had signed her name to them. That was the part that still made her hands shake, when she let it.

The throat opened.

They stepped out onto a gallery of black stone that curved away in both directions, and below them — there was no other word for it — *below* them, though the room had no floor so much as a bowl — spread the court.

It was lit from beneath. That was the first thing Wren understood, and the second thing was that she would never stop understanding it wrong no matter how long she looked. Light came up through the stone itself, pale gold, sourceless, the color of candlelight seen through a bedsheet. And in that light moved the magistrates.

There were perhaps thirty of them. They were tall the way reeds are tall, and pale the way things are pale when they have never once needed blood to hurry anywhere. They wore robes the grey of old ledger paper, and their faces were smooth as the backs of spoons — features present, technically, but arranged with the indifference of a clerk who has written the same name ten thousand times. They did not walk so much as *file* themselves from place to place.

Between them, on long tables of black stone, lay books. Hundreds of books. Thousands. And around each book hung a faint shimmer, like heat-haze off summer cobbles, except the haze was shaped like people.

"Oh," Wren said quietly. "Oh, that's — those are—"

"Souls," Caspian said. His voice had gone very controlled, the control of a man holding a door shut against something. "Bound souls. Waiting to be... processed."

"Filed," Ossa corrected grimly. "Everything down here gets filed."

*Don't look for yours,* Wren told herself, and then looked, immediately, helplessly, scanning the haze-shapes for one that might be five years old and shaped like a girl who liked to draw fish in the margins of her copywork. She couldn't tell. They all looked like everyone. That was somehow worse.

A bell rang. It did not come from any direction; it came from the light itself, a single clear note like a fingertip run round the rim of a glass. Every magistrate stopped filing at once. Thirty spoon-smooth faces turned upward, in perfect unison, toward the gallery where three dusty intruders stood holding a smuggler's lantern.

"Visitors," said a voice, "are not customary."

He came forward through his colleagues the way a boat comes through reeds, and Wren knew him at once, though she had never seen him. She knew him from the ledgers. Every contract in the temple archives bore, above the seal, a single line of notation in a hand so fine it looked printed: *negotiated personally, S.*

The Seneschal was tall even by the standards of the tall pale things around him, and he alone had a face worth describing — not because it was kind, but because it had been *arranged* to look kind, the way a shop window is arranged. Grey eyes. A mouth built for sympathetic pauses. He regarded them with the expression of a host delighted, genuinely delighted, that you have finally come to dinner.

"Wren Thornbury," he said. Her name in his mouth sounded like a figure being read aloud from a column. "And Caspian Hale, formerly of the Wardens. And—" his gaze touched Ossa and moved on with the mild dismissal of a man noting a delivery at the servants' entrance, "—a guide. How thorough of you all to come together. It saves such a quantity of correspondence."

"We're not staying," Caspian said.

"Everyone says that." The Seneschal smiled. It was a good smile. Wren hated how good it was. "Please. Descend. You've come a very long way through very warm rock; it would be inhospitable to conduct business across a railing."

*Business.* The word landed in Wren's stomach like a swallowed stone. She descended. She told herself it was because refusing would gain nothing, which was true, and not because some traitorous part of her wanted to hear the rest, which was also true.

* * *

The floor of the court was warm underfoot, and the light came up through it so that they seemed to walk on a slow golden river frozen mid-pour. Up close, the magistrates ignored them entirely, which was its own kind of horror — Wren could have wept on one of their tables and been filed around.

The Seneschal led them to a table set slightly apart, bearing a single book, closed, with a ribbon of grey silk marking a page. He did not offer chairs. There were no chairs. She suspected furniture was for creatures with spines that tired.

"You want to know," he said to Wren, "about the bargain."

"I want to know about my name."

"The same thing, in this establishment." He laid one long finger on the book's cover without opening it. "But before I show you the entry — and I will show you, I am not in the practice of hiding accounts; hiding is for merchants, and we are archivists — I wonder if you understand what your mother purchased."

"She bought our survival." Wren's voice came out steadier than she felt. "That's what the temple records say. Famine year. The harbor failed, the granaries were sealed, and the families who signed got bread."

"The families who signed got bread," the Seneschal agreed pleasantly. "Your mother got more. Your mother came to me — do you imagine the elders of your bright little temple ever dirtied their hands? No. They sent petitions downward, and I received them, and I negotiated *personally*, as the record notes, because the item under discussion was unusual." His grey eyes rested on her with something that in a human face might have been appetite. "Most souls come to us already spent. Hollowed by use, worn thin by debt, barely worth the shelf space. Yours came to us whole. Unspent. A child's soul, entire, with the ink-gift already showing in it. Do you know what a soul like that is worth down here?"

"No," Wren said. "And I don't care."

"It is worth," the Seneschal continued, with the serenity of a man who had never once been interrupted successfully, "enough to buy a famine's end for one family. Enough to buy your mother's life, your brother's apprenticeship, your father's boat refitted and his debts struck clean. Enough, Wren Thornbury, that every meal you ate between your eighth year and your thirteenth was paid for in advance. You did not survive despite the court." He opened the book at last, gently, the way one opens a thing one loves. "You survived *on its credit*."

The page was her own. Not her name upon it — her. A shimmer rose from the paper, faint, familiar in a way that made her chest ache, the way a smell can be familiar from a dream. Somewhere in the haze-fields behind her, she understood, a shape the size of a girl was waiting to be reconciled with this page.

"There," the Seneschal said softly. "You wanted truth. Truth is our principal export."

* * *

For a while nobody said anything. The magistrates filed. The light breathed.

It was Caspian who broke the silence, low and rough: "She was eight."

"She was collateral," the Seneschal said, without cruelty, which was worse. "There is a difference, Warden, and the difference is the whole of civilization. But — I perceive you think me a monster, so let me offer you what I offered her mother, whom I assure you I did not cheat." He folded his hands. "Walk away."

Wren blinked. "What?"

"Walk away. All three of you. The stairs behind you remain open; Ossa Vane knows the way, and my colleagues will not pursue — pursuit is so vulgar. You keep your lives. Your memories. Your little charm-shop and your remembering ink and your Warden's disgrace, all of it, exactly as it was this morning. In exchange—" he tapped the page, once, lightly, "—you leave the account open. The girl-shaped sum stays where it has safely sat for fifteen years. Greyharbor goes on standing, fed on credit, its temple elders fat and its poor praying to a god who answers. Everyone remains comfortable. Everyone remains *lied to*, comfortably. That is the mercy I extend, and I extend it precisely once, because your mother bargained well and I honor good bargaining."

"And if I don't walk away?"

"Then you learn everything." His smile returned, patient as tide. "Every entry. Every elder's signature. Every family in Greyharbor that stands on borrowed soul. And truths, Miss Thornbury, cannot be unlearned — only carried. Most people find the weight changes them. Your mother found the weight changed her *into someone who could sign*. So." He gestured, gracious, terrible, at the dark stair behind them. "Comfort, or the ledger. Choose."

Wren looked at the page. At the shimmer that was hers. She thought about her mother's hands — cracked knuckles, flour in the creases, the hands that had braided her hair and signed her away in the same season. She thought about the widow Marta, hollow-eyed in her chair, smiling at nothing. She thought about how easy it would be. God, how *easy*. Walk up the stairs, go home, sell charms, sleep at night, never know.

The temptation didn't feel like temptation. It felt like rest.

"I used to write copies of contracts like this," Wren said at last. "When I was small. Fifty lines a day. And you know what I learned?" She lifted her chin. "Ink remembers what it writes. And so do I."

The Seneschal's arranged kindness did not flicker, but something behind it did — the smallest recalculation, a clerk finding an error in a column he had balanced for centuries.

"Then," he said, "we proceed to the asset question. And you should know that the asset in question is nearly spent."

Far above them — or far below; in this place the directions had begun to argue — something vast exhaled, and the golden light guttered, just once, like a candle in a doorway.

Behind Wren, she heard Caspian Hale stop breathing.
