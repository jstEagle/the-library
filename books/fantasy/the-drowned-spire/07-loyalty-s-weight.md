## 7. Loyalty's Weight

Caspian Hale had spent eleven years as a Warden learning to read people the way sailors read weather: the twitch of a lip before a lie, the stillness of guilt, the particular way fear sat in the shoulders. He had thought himself good at it. Then he had been disgraced for it — for reading too well, they said, for seeing corruption in men who had the rank to be above suspicion — and now he stood in a court beneath a mountain where everyone's face was wrong in ways no training had prepared him for.

The magistrates did not blink. That was the first thing. They blinked, technically — lids descended and rose on pale eyes like fish bellies — but it was a mechanical courtesy, performed at intervals, as if someone had told them blinking was expected of faces and they had taken notes.

"Stop staring," Wren murmured beside him.

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring like a man who's never seen a person before."

"I've never seen *these* people." He kept his voice low. Around them, the Court Below the Mountain went about its business in long, whispering processions: clerks carrying ledgers whose pages whispered when turned, petitioners kneeling at brass counters, trading something Caspian could not see but could feel, the way you feel a draft from a door left open somewhere in a large house. "I used to think the worst thing a courtroom could do was take your freedom. This one takes your *self*, apparently, and does it with excellent penmanship."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

Wren didn't laugh. She hadn't laughed since finding her mother's name on her own contract, and Caspian understood that kind of silence. It was the silence of a house after the funeral, when everyone keeps speaking normally because the alternative is screaming.

The Seneschal had given them an hour. An hour to consider his offer, he'd said, in the tone of a man offering tea. Walk away, Wren Thornbury, and keep your life. The court admired audacity; it simply also owned everything audacity touched.

So while Wren pored over the public copies of the ledger — hunting for loopholes, or allies, or anything that wasn't despair — Caspian wandered. It was habit. When a room frightened him, he read it. And the ledger hall was a room worth reading: mile upon mile of shelving carved into living stone, warm underfoot, the veins of granite glowing faint amber where the dragon's fire bled through from its cage far below.

He told himself he was looking for tactical information. Exits. Guard rotations. The number of steps between himself and daylight.

What he found was the H section.

*Hale, Tomas.* His brother's name sat on a spine of grey vellum, neat as a hymnal. Caspian's hand rose toward it and stopped, hovering, the way you hover over a door you're suddenly afraid to open.

He opened it anyway. He'd always been bad at leaving doors shut. It was half of why he'd been disgraced.

The entry was brief. Courts that trade in souls are efficient, not cruel — cruelty is overhead. *Hale, Tomas. Collateral against testimony rendered. Witness: Hale, Caspian. Status: bound, fourteen years.*

Fourteen years ago, Caspian had been twenty-two and certain of everything. A smuggler named Brannock Vey had been caught running charms through Greyharbor's north gate, and the Wardens needed testimony to make the case close cleanly, and there had been a boy present — Tomas, sixteen, holding the lantern, guilty of nothing but being his brother's brother. The captain had said, *One statement, Hale, just confirm what you saw,* and Caspian, certain of everything, had confirmed it. He had testified that both brothers were complicit. It had earned him a commendation. It had earned Tomas a cell, and then — because the temple elders had been quietly buying what the law condemned — a transfer to a place the paperwork called *custodial indenture* and Caspian had never thought about again.

Never thought about again. Fourteen years. He had written letters. He had sent money to a dead address. He had told himself his brother chose not to answer, and some nights, in the honest dark, he had almost believed it.

Tomas hadn't chosen anything. Tomas was here. Or the part of him that was Tomas was here, filed between *Halloway* and *Hammond*, bound as collateral against a testimony his own brother had given.

Caspian sat down on the warm stone floor of the ledger hall, because his legs had made an executive decision without consulting him, and he put his head in his hands, and he did not cry, mostly, though the distinction felt academic.

* * *

"There you are." Ossa Vane's voice came down the aisle like smoke finding a draft. She dropped into a crouch beside him, all elbows and easy grin, her smuggler's coat smelling of lamp oil and oranges. "You look like a man reading his own obituary. Bad news?"

"My brother's in this book."

Ossa's grin flickered — not gone, but banked, the way she banked all her expressions, keeping them cheap to spend. "Ah," she said. "That kind of bad news."

"He's been here fourteen years. Because of me." Caspian heard his own voice and hated how steady it was. Steadiness was a Warden habit; it had outlived the uniform. "I gave testimony. I was *sure*. Everyone was sure. That was the whole disease of those years — we were all so sure."

Ossa said nothing, which from Ossa was a eulogy. She let the silence sit until it stopped hurting quite so much, then bumped her shoulder against his. "For what it's worth, my family's got three names in books like these. Different court, same penmanship. So when I say I know the flavor — I know the flavor."

"How do you stand it?"

"Badly. But standing is the job." She stood, demonstrating, and offered him a hand up. "Come on. Your girl found something. She's doing the face she does when she's found something and hates it."

"She's not my girl."

"Mm," said Ossa, in the tone of a woman filing information for later use.

* * *

Wren had found something and hated it. She explained it in the antechamber, pacing, ink-stained fingers sketching shapes in the air. "The contracts are reciprocal. Every soul held is a soul owed interest — the court pays the dragon in memories to keep the fire alive, and the fire keeps the ledger binding. If the fire dies, the ledger doesn't just stop working. It *calls due*. Everything forfeit, everything consumed. Thousands of souls, gone in an hour."

"So we steal yours back before then," Caspian said. "Simple."

"Nothing about this is simple."

"Fine. Complicated, then. We steal yours back before then."

She almost smiled. It was progress; he noted it the way a sailor notes wind.

It was Ossa who cleared her throat. "And the way out? Because I've been counting guards while you two have been having feelings, and the count is unfavorable. There's one route the magistrates don't watch — the old drainage gallery, west shaft, floods twice a day. We go at slack water, we're out before the next tide."

"You've been down here before," Wren said slowly.

"I've been everywhere before. It's my defining feature." Ossa smiled her banked smile. "Get some rest. Slack water's in six hours."

Later — much later, in the ruin of hindsight — Caspian would try to remember whether he had seen anything in her face that night. He would search the memory the way he'd once searched suspects' rooms, thorough and merciless. And he would find nothing, because Ossa Vane was better at faces than he had ever been, and because the truth was worse than a tell: there had been nothing to see. She had already decided.

* * *

The Seneschal found Caspian alone an hour before slack water. Of course he did. Pale magistrates probably smelled solitude the way sharks smelled blood.

"Hale," the Seneschal said, and the name sounded different in his mouth — official, archival, a file being consulted. "Former Warden-Commander. Restored rank is available to you. Full pardon. The testimony that ended your career, expunged. Your brother—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—released. Unbound. Whole. He would wake tomorrow in Greyharbor with no memory of this place and every memory of you."

Caspian's heart did something complicated and painful. "In exchange for?"

"The Thornbury woman. Her route out. Her cooperation — or her capture, whichever proves simpler. You are a man who understands testimony, Hale. You know how much weight a single honest sentence carries."

The offer hung in the warm amber air. Somewhere below them, the dragon breathed its guttering fire into the bones of the mountain, and the mountain glowed like a hearth, and every glow was paid for with somebody's mother, somebody's childhood, somebody's name.

"You've done your research," Caspian said.

"We are a court. Research is our liturgy."

"Then research this." He stepped closer, and eleven years of Warden steadiness funneled into one sentence, quiet and level. "I gave false testimony once because I was sure, and it cost my brother fourteen years. I will not give true testimony now because I'm afraid. Rank is a coat. My brother is my brother. Find another traitor."

The Seneschal regarded him with those fish-belly eyes, and for a moment — one strange, suspended moment — something almost like respect moved behind them, or perhaps indigestion; it was hard to tell with magistrates.

"A pity," the Seneschal said. "You would have made an excellent magistrate."

"I'll take that as the insult it is."

He walked away and did not run, which cost him more than the speech had.

* * *

Slack water came, and the drainage gallery did not come with it.

They reached the west shaft to find it lit — lanterns strung along the flooded passage like festival lights, and magistrates waiting in rows, patient as tide charts. At their center stood Ossa Vane, arms crossed, wearing her banked smile at full flame for the first time since Caspian had known her.

"No," Caspian said. It came out very small.

"Payment in advance," Ossa said, and her voice was light, professional, terrible. "Three names, Hale. Mine, my sister's, my mother's. Freed, unbound, whole. All I had to do was sell you a tunnel." She shrugged. "You'd have done the arithmetic too. Don't pretend you wouldn't. You were a Warden; arithmetic was your sacrament."

"The difference," Caspian said, "is that I stopped taking communion."

Something crossed her face then — quick, quickly buried — and he would spend a long time afterward wondering if it had been guilt, or only the effort of not letting it be.

"Take them," she said to the magistrates, and stepped aside.

Chains of pale metal, cold despite the mountain's warmth. Wren fought — of course she fought, small and furious and magnificent, biting a clerk's hand hard enough to draw blood that was the wrong color — but there were twelve of them and the corridor was narrow and Caspian's hands were bound behind him, and the last thing he saw before they hooded him was Wren's eyes over the gag, saying what her mouth couldn't:

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm not done yet.*

They took her deeper. Down past the ledger halls, down past the warm veins of stone, down toward the cage where the last dragon burned what remained of itself to keep the world's cruelest book open.

And Caspian, hooded and chained in the dark, began — methodically, desperately, with eleven years of training and nothing left to lose — to count footsteps.
