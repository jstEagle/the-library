## 8. The Last Flame

They did not tie her hands.

That was the first insult. Wren had spent the entire march down from the magistrate's hall cataloguing escape routes — the loose grate at the second landing, the guard who favored his left knee, the narrow service passage that smelled of old tallow and probably went somewhere — and it turned out none of it mattered, because the magistrates simply walked her through the mountain's veins like a guest being shown to a table. No ropes. No chains. Just a pale man in grey robes walking half a step ahead of her, saying nothing, while two more followed behind with the patient air of people herding something that had nowhere else to be.

"You could at least pretend I'm dangerous," she told the one in front.

He didn't turn around. "You are not dangerous, Thornbury. You are collateral."

"Collateral can be *burned*," she said, which sounded braver than it felt, and got no answer for it.

Caspian they had taken somewhere else. She'd shouted his name once, in the hall, and heard him shouting hers from behind a door that closed too quickly, and then there had been only the sound of her own boots on stone worn smooth by centuries of feet that hadn't wanted to be going where they were going. Ossa Vane had not been taken anywhere at all. Ossa Vane had been given a chair, and wine, and — Wren had seen this with her own eyes before the guards pulled her away — a small dish of candied ginger. The traitor had waved. Cheerfully.

"I hope you choke on it," Wren had called.

"Wouldn't dream of it, love," Ossa had said. "Business is business."

*Business is business.* Wren turned the phrase over as they descended, because turning things over was what she did instead of screaming. Somewhere above her, Caspian was in a cell or worse. Somewhere below her — she could feel it now, a warmth rising through the soles of her boots like standing on a sleeping animal — was the thing the whole rotten court was built around. And somewhere in between was a ledger with her name in it, written in her mother's hand, and five years of her life that had never been an accident at all.

The tunnel opened without warning into a cavern so large the ceiling vanished into darkness, and Wren stopped cataloguing escape routes, because there were none, and stopped breathing, briefly, because there didn't seem much point.

The dragon filled the cavern the way grief fills a house: completely, and leaving room for nothing else.

It lay coiled in a cage of black iron taller than the temple spire, bars thick as masts, and even caged — especially caged — it was the most beautiful thing Wren had ever seen. Its scales were the grey of Greyharbor's winter sea, shot through with veins of ember-light that pulsed slowly, dimly, like a heart deciding whether to bother. Its fire, she understood suddenly, was almost gone. What she'd thought was light was mostly memory of light.

And it was looking at her.

"It's smaller than I expected," said a voice beside her, and Wren's whole body went cold, because she knew that voice. She knew it better than her own. She knew the slight catch in it when it was amused, the way it softened on the second syllable of her name—

"*Wren*."

Her mother stood at the edge of the cage. Not a ghost, not quite — a woman made of borrowed things, wearing Maren Thornbury's face the way a charm wore paper. The Seneschal's pale clerks had dressed her in a fishwife's shawl and put flour on her hands, and every detail was right, and every detail was wrong, because her mother had been dead four years and dead women did not stand with their weight on both feet so evenly.

"No," Wren said.

"You've gotten thin." Her mother's mouth smiled. Her mother's eyes did not. "Sit down, love. You look tired."

"They feed it memories," said the grey-robed magistrate behind her, in the tone of a man reciting a shipping manifest. "To keep the flame alive. It speaks in whatever it is fed. Yours were among the most... nourishing."

Wren looked at the thing wearing her mother and felt something crack cleanly inside her, the way ice cracks — not painfully, at first. Just a sound. A fact settling.

"You're not her."

"No," the dragon said, still in her mother's voice, and there was something underneath the borrowed words now, something vast and old and terribly weary. "But she is in me. All of her. Every morning you woke coughing from the salt-fog and she sat up with you. Every charm you sold at seven years old that she called 'rubbish' and kept anyway. The song about the herring king. The way she cried after they took your name and told you it was for the best." The great head lowered until one ember-veined eye hung level with Wren's face, close enough that she could feel heat like an open oven. "I have been carrying your mother for eleven years, little scribe. Would you like to know how heavy a woman is?"

* * *

They gave her a stool. That was the worst part, somehow. They gave her a stool and a cup of water and let her sit in the warm dark while the dragon told her about her mother, and it was all true, every word of it, and truth has never been a kindness just because it's accurate.

"She bargained well," the dragon said. "For a frightened woman with nothing but a sick daughter and a debt to the elders. Survival for the girl. A name as payment. She wept. She signed anyway. She came back every year on the anniversary and left flowers at the drowned altar, and every year the elders took the flowers and sold them back to her at market." A plume of smoke curled from its nostrils, thin and grey. "She loved you badly and completely, which is the only way anyone loves anything in this city."

"Stop," Wren whispered.

"You asked me to talk."

"I asked you to stop wearing her."

The eye regarded her. The ember-light in it guttered, steadied. "Ah," the dragon said softly. "There it is. The anger. Good. Anger burns cleaner than grief. I should know; I have been burning both for three hundred years."

Wren set down the cup. Her hands were shaking, which annoyed her, so she pressed them flat against her knees. "What do you want from me?"

"What do *you* want?" And now the mother-voice fell away entirely, and what remained was a voice like a landslide remembering itself, deep enough to feel in her teeth. "You came all this way. Through the mines, past my bones' warmth, into the court of thieves. You did not come to sit on a stool."

"I came for my soul."

"It is in the ledger. As is the Warden's brother's. As are nine thousand others." The dragon shifted, and the whole cavern groaned, iron singing against stone. "And here is the joke, little scribe, the joke the magistrates do not find funny anymore: the ledger burns. Everything burns, if the fire is hot enough. My fire. What remains of it."

Wren's breath caught. "You're offering—"

"I am dying. When I die, the ledger does not close. It *forfeits*. Nine thousand souls rendered unto the court, forever, to keep their contracts warm. Unless someone takes what fire I have left and puts it somewhere useful first." The head swung toward a brazier at the cavern's far side, where a book the size of a tomb door lay open under glass, its pages crawling with names. "Take my flame. Burn the ledger. Free them all."

"And the cost," Wren said. "There's always a cost. This city taught me that before it taught me letters."

The dragon was quiet a long moment. The ember-veins pulsed. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, Greyharbor's evening bells rang, and Wren thought absurdly of her stall, her paper charms, the widow Marta buying luck she couldn't afford.

"The flame consumes what the bearer loves most," the dragon said. "That is its nature. It cannot help it. Fire eats."

Wren laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, though she tried to put some there, because humor was also something this city had taught her. "Of course it does. Of course. Nothing in this mountain gives change."

"You would lose—" the dragon began.

"Don't." Wren held up a hand. "Don't tell me what it'll take. If you say it out loud I'll start protecting it, and if I start protecting it I'll hesitate, and if I hesitate, nine thousand people stay in that book forever." She looked at the creature in the cage — at the last dragon in the world, dying in a birdcage built by accountants — and felt the anger in her chest settle into something harder and more useful. Resolve, her mother would have called it. Stubbornness, her mother would actually have called it, fondly, over the herring-king song.

"Wren Thornbury," the dragon said, and for one moment the borrowed voice was so exactly her mother's — exasperated, proud, afraid for her — that Wren's eyes burned. "Do you understand what you're choosing?"

"I'm a charm-seller," Wren said. "I understand exactly what things cost. That's the whole trade." She stood. Her legs held. "One condition. When the ledger burns — you get out of that cage. You fly. You don't die down here in the dark for their bookkeeping."

A long silence. Then, low, a sound that might have been laughter in a younger throat. "Greedy to the end."

"It's the family failing."

The dragon opened its mouth, and light poured out — not the guttering ember-glow of its scales but true fire, banked and ancient, the first fire, maybe, the fire the world was forged in — and it gathered itself in the air between them like a living thing waiting to be asked.

Wren reached into her coat and drew out her inkpot. Her remembering-ink. The one gift the ban never managed to burn out of her, the ink that remembered everything it wrote.

If the flame was going to take what she loved most, then she would make sure the ledger's nine thousand souls were written somewhere it couldn't reach. Somewhere it would never think to look.

Somewhere it loved.

She uncorked the ink, stepped toward the fire, and heard, behind her, the soft deliberate click of the cavern door opening — and Caspian's voice, hoarse and furious, shouting her name.
