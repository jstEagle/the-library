## 9. Ash and Ink

The dragon's fire hung in the air between them like a held breath.

Wren could feel it waiting — not heat exactly, but *attention*, the way a page feels attention when a pen hovers over it. The dragon had spoken in her mother's voice, and it was still speaking with it, patient and terrible: *"Take what remains of me, little scribe. But know the price. Fire eats what its bearer loves most. It will not ask twice."*

Behind her, Caspian strained against the pale chains the magistrates had grown from his own guilt — they liked to do that, she'd learned. They liked their restraints to be personal. Ossa Vane stood apart by the ledger's pedestal, arms crossed, wearing the expression of someone calculating exits. Wren would deal with Ossa later. Or not. Priorities.

"I need ink," Wren said.

The Seneschal's laugh was dry as old vellum. "You are offered a god's dying flame, child, and you ask for *ink*?"

"You don't understand what my ink does." She turned to face him, and her hands were steady, which surprised her. She'd expected them to shake. Maybe they would later, when there was time for later. "It remembers what it writes. Every charm I ever sold in that fish market — paper and prayer, mostly nonsense — but when I wrote something true, it stayed true. When I copied a line from your temple records as a girl, the copy didn't fade like the others' copies did. You noticed. That's why you kept me five years instead of one."

The Seneschal's smile thinned. "A curiosity."

"A *loophole*." She stepped toward the ledger. It sat on its pedestal of black stone, vast as a tomb door, pages of grey skin-soft parchment covered in names — thousands upon thousands of them, each one a soul folded flat and filed away. Somewhere on those pages: *Caspian Hale's brother*. Somewhere: *Wren Thornbury*, in her mother's careful hand.

"You cannot copy it," the Seneschal said. "The ledger is not paper. It is law."

"Then it's lucky," Wren said, "that I'm very good at copying."

* * *

They let her have ink because they were proud, and pride is the slowest poison in any court. The magistrates drifted around her like moths made of contracts, murmuring about interest and forfeiture, certain that whatever the little scribe intended, it would end in their favor. Everything always did. That was the whole architecture of the place.

Wren knelt beside the ledger and uncorked the small clay pot she'd carried since she was nine years old — smuggled out of the temple scriptorium in a hollowed heel of bread, the only inheritance her mother had ever given her that wasn't a debt. The ink inside was blacker than black, the remembering kind, mixed once from temple soot and tide-water and something else she'd never been able to identify. Her mother's recipe. Her mother's hands had taught hers to stir it.

*Don't think about her,* she told herself. *Not yet. Think after.*

She bared her left arm to the shoulder, then began to unlace her collar, rolling the fabric down. Skin, she'd realized somewhere in the dark of the descent, was simply another kind of page. Better than paper. Paper burned; skin remembered.

"What is she doing?" one magistrate asked another, in the bored tone of clerks watching weather.

"Writing," said the other. "They do that."

Wren dipped her pen and touched it to the first page of the ledger, then to her own forearm, back and forth, back and forth, a shuttle building a cloth. The words came across reluctantly at first — the ledger fought her, each name clinging to its place like a barnacle to a hull — but her ink was stubborn too, and it had been waiting twenty years for a job worthy of it.

*Marta Ellery, sold for three barrels of salt-fish, term: perpetual.*

The name rose on Wren's skin in fine black script, and somewhere far above them — up through the mountain, through the mine-shafts, through the drowned temple, into the crooked streets of Greyharbor — a woman who hadn't dreamed in eleven years suddenly dreamed of her wedding day.

"Oh," whispered one of the magistrates, and stopped drifting.

Wren kept writing.

*Ossian Hale, sold against his brother's testimony, term: perpetual.* She felt Caspian go rigid behind her. She wrote his brother's name carefully, lovingly, the way you write a letter to someone you've never met but intend to.

*Tobias Marn. Ilse Varrow. The whole of the Fenwick family, collateralized against a fishing boat that never came home—*

"Stop her." The Seneschal's voice cracked like ice on a pond. "STOP HER — she's *transcribing the law onto living flesh*, do you imbeciles understand nothing? A contract on skin that remembers is a contract that can't be amended!"

The magistrates moved at last, all at once, pale robes whispering — and the dragon laughed.

It was not a kind sound. It was furnace-deep, and it used her mother's voice, and it said: *"No. Let the little scribe finish. I want to see what she buys with what she's about to pay."*

* * *

She wrote until dawn meant nothing anymore and her arms were black to the shoulders, black across her collarbones, black down her ribs where she'd stretched the skin taut over the ledger's final pages. Names. Terms. Signatures. The whole rotten architecture of the Court Below the Mountain, transcribed onto the one vault no magistrate could enter and no amendment could reach.

Her mother's signature last of all. *Given freely: one daughter's name, in exchange for the lives of two.* Wren traced it once with her fingertip, memorizing the shape of the letters even though her ink needed no such help. Because after tonight, she suspected, she would need the memorizing more than the ink did.

"It's done," she said. Her voice came out scraped raw. "Every name. Every debt. It's all right here." She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, over the heart, where she'd written the largest entry of all: *LEDGER OF THE COURT BELOW THE MOUNTAIN, COPY OF TRUE AND ORIGINAL, ATTESTED BY THE HAND THAT REMEMBERS.*

"And now," said the Seneschal softly, "you will find that a copy has no power while the original stands. That is the first law of law, child. I wrote it."

"I know," Wren said. "That's why I'm going to burn yours."

She turned to the dragon.

Up close, in the guttering light of its own dying fire, the creature was smaller than legend and sadder than song — scales gone grey as ash, wings folded like a beggar's coat, chained to the heart of the mountain by links of solidified contract-law older than Greyharbor itself. Its eyes were the only bright thing left, banked coals banked lower every year, fed on memories the court stole from the hollowed ones to keep the ledger's flame alive.

*"You understand,"* it said, in her mother's voice, gentle now, almost sorry. *"What I have left will take what you love best. There is no choosing around it. There never was."*

Wren thought of her mother's hands stirring ink. Of bread with a clay pot hidden in the heel. Of a woman who had signed her daughter's soul away to keep her daughters breathing, and had probably — *probably* — hated herself every day since. The person she loved most in all the world was already half-lost to her: sold, spent, filed. And the flame would take even the memory of having loved her.

"Yes," Wren said. "I understand. I've understood since the fish market, if I'm honest. I just wanted someone to make me say it out loud first."

*"Cruel of me,"* the dragon agreed. *"Forgive me. I have been caged a long time, and cruelty is the only coin they let me keep."*

It lowered its great grey head, and opened its mouth, and breathed.

Not fire — not at first. What passed between them was warmer than fire and quieter, a light like the last coal of a hearth on the last night of winter, and it poured into Wren through her open hands and her black-written skin and settled behind her ribs like a second heart.

And then it began to eat.

It went for her mother gently, which was somehow worse. Not a tearing — a *lifting*, the way you lift a sleeping child from a cart. Wren felt her mother's laugh go first, the low one, the private one. Then the smell of the ink. Then the kitchen. Then the hands. Then the name — *her* name, the way her mother said it, soft at the edges — and Wren gripped the pedestal and wept and did not stop writing, because there was one thing left to write, and her hand knew it even as the rest of her emptied.

With the last of the stolen fire gathered in her fist like a handful of sunset, she pressed her palm to the ledger's first page.

"Burn," she said.

The ledger caught like the world's oldest sin catching at last. Flame ran down the columns of names faster than reading, faster than grief, and every name it touched *rose* — Wren felt them go, thousands of souls unfolding from their flat grey imprisonment like letters finally opened, streaming up through the mountain's stone, homeward. Somewhere above, Ossian Hale woke in a bed he didn't remember buying with tears he couldn't explain. Somewhere above, Marta Ellery remembered her wedding, and her husband, and why she'd stopped dreaming.

"No—" The Seneschal lunged forward, hands outstretched toward his burning life's work. "The terms! The *terms*—"

But the terms were ash, and the Seneschal had never been anything except his terms. Wren watched him come apart the way a contract comes apart — clause by clause, provision by provision, his pale hands unraveling into fine grey thread, his voice fraying mid-sentence into *whereas* and *hereinafter* and then into nothing at all. The magistrates lasted a little longer, shrieking in perfect legalese, and then they too were unmade, and the Court Below the Mountain stood empty for the first time in a thousand years except for one freed dragon, one weeping scribe, one stunned Warden, and one smuggler already edging toward the exit.

"Caspian," Wren said. Her voice sounded strange to her — lighter, somehow, and emptier. "Your brother. He's free. I wrote him myself."

Caspian was staring at her arms — at the black names crawling over her skin, already beginning to fade as their owners reclaimed themselves, the ink doing its last faithful work and letting go. "Wren," he said carefully, "your face. You're crying, but you look like you're trying to remember why."

"I am," she said. And then, because some part of her understood the shape of the loss even with the center of it gone: "There was someone. Someone who saved me. I can feel the space where she was, like a tooth that's been pulled." She laughed, wet and broken. "I sold everything to free everyone, and the flame took my receipt."

The dragon rose, chains sloughing off like old snow, and its fire — what remained of it, what lived now in Wren — flickered in sympathy. *"Come, little scribe,"* it said, in a voice that was only its own now, deep and strange and new. *"You gave me your dearest thing. The least I owe you is the sky."*

Far above, faint through a thousand feet of stone, Greyharbor's bells had begun to ring — all of them, at once, unscheduled, unhallowed, ringing the way bells ring when the people pulling the ropes don't know why except that everything feels different and they must make noise about it.

Wren looked down at her fading arms, at the last name dissolving off her wrist — her own, *Wren Thornbury*, paid in full — and climbed onto the dragon's back.

Above them, the mountain was already learning how to open.
