## 10. The City Without Debts

**Wren**

The mountain let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long — slowly, and with a sound like knuckles cracking.

Wren rode between the dragon's shoulders with her hands buried in scales still warm from the last of its fire, and she did not look back at the dark. There was nothing down there to look at anymore. The court was ash. The ledger was ash. The Seneschal was ash, which was more than he deserved and less than she'd have chosen if she'd had time to be creative about it.

The dragon climbed through the mountain's heart as though the stone were water, and where it passed, old veins of heat bloomed red in the rock like flowers remembering spring. Caspian clung behind her, swearing steadily and creatively — she hadn't known Wardens knew that many words for *falling* — and somewhere below them Ossa Vane whooped like a woman who had just discovered she was, against all odds and her own best efforts, still alive.

"You could have mentioned," Caspian shouted over the wind of their ascent, "that dragons fly."

"I could have mentioned a great many things," Wren shouted back. "You didn't ask."

The mountain opened above them like a mouth, and then they were out — into grey morning light, into salt wind, into the enormous ordinary sky over Greyharbor. The dragon banked once over the drowned temple, its shadow passing across the floodwater where the god's sigil still glowed faintly, patient as ever, and then it turned toward the sea.

It did not speak to her again. It didn't need to. She had heard her mother's voice come out of it, borrowed and burning, and she had taken what it offered anyway, and both of them understood that this was the whole of their conversation now.

At the edge of the harbor cliffs it descended low enough that they could slide from its back onto the grass, which they did with considerably less grace than either would later claim. Then the last dragon of Greyharbor stretched wings that blotted out the sun for one long breath, lifted, and flew east over the water until it was a smudge, and then a rumor, and then nothing at all.

"Well," said Ossa, flat on her back in the grass. "I've been paid worse for worse work."

"You were paid *nothing*," said Caspian.

"And I've been paid nothing for worse work. There's a difference." She sat up, wincing. "So. What now? Because I sold your escape route to the magistrates, in case anyone's keeping score, and I'd like to establish before the recriminations start that I feel *terrible*."

"You don't," said Wren.

"I feel moderately bad. It's a start. I'm told growth is a journey."

Caspian looked at Wren, and Wren looked at Caspian, and some unspoken negotiation passed between them — the kind that happens between people who have seen each other at their worst and decided, provisionally, to keep seeing each other anyway.

"Now," Wren said, "we go home. And we tell everyone everything."

* * *

Greyharbor woke slowly, the way a sleeper wakes from a fever — confused, aching, unsure what was dream.

It began with the widow's family, who found old Marta Tull sitting up in bed on the third morning after her soul came home, asking crossly why her tea was cold and who had let the cat in, and weeping without knowing why when she saw the charm — Wren's charm, paper and prayer — still hanging by her door. It began with fishermen who paused mid-haul because suddenly they remembered their fathers' faces, their daughters' first words, debts of love they'd forgotten they owed. It began with three hundred people standing in the fish market at dawn, staring at each other, hollow-eyed no longer, saying *I remember* in voices that cracked.

By noon the city was weeping openly in the streets, and Greyharbor is not a city that weeps easily. By evening it was singing, badly, in taverns, songs nobody had taught anybody — songs that had been locked in souls for years and came out rough and off-key and unstoppable.

And underneath the joy, like a current under tide: questions. Whose names were taken. Who took them. Who signed.

Wren had the answers. She carried them on her own skin — every contract, every signature, every bargain struck beneath the mountain, copied line by line in ink that remembers. Her arms were black with it to the elbow. When she rolled up her sleeves in the light, people flinched, and then leaned closer, and then read.

That was the part she hadn't expected: how they read. Not like a mob. Like a congregation.

She stood in the ruined temple on the seventh day, on the altar where fresh water still seeped up through old stone, and read the records aloud to whoever came — and everyone came. Fishwives and Wardens and dockhands and, in the back, sitting very straight and very uncomfortable, six temple elders who had built the debt system brick by brick and soul by soul and had convinced themselves, the way powerful men do, that it was mercy.

"Read the names," someone called from the crowd. "Read who signed."

And there it was, inked along her left forearm in her own careful childhood hand: *Maren Thornbury, mother of the debtor, in exchange for grain, rent, and the life of her child through the winter of the gray fever.*

Her mother's name. Her mother's signature. And Wren stood there looking at it and felt — nothing. That was the price, and she had known the price, and knowing did not make the nothing smaller. She could not remember Maren Thornbury's face. She could not remember whether her laugh came easy or hard, whether she'd been tall, whether she'd smelled of salt or bread or lamp oil. There was a hole in her shaped exactly like a woman, and the whole city could see it, and the whole city could see the name that filled it.

"Your mother," said an old fishwife near the front, gently. "She saved you. That winter took half the children on my street. Took two of mine."

"She also sold me," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Both things are true. I want both things written down."

"That's not how most folk tell it."

"Most folk weren't there. Neither was I, as it happens." A ripple of laughter went through the crowd — startled, then warm, the laughter of people giving themselves permission. "But the records don't lie. That's the whole point of records. So we keep them. All of it. The saving and the selling. Anyone who tells you it was only one or the other is selling you something too."

The elders tried, that day and the days after, to frame it as necessity — hard years, hard choices, the city's survival. And it was true, mostly, which made it worse. But truth cuts both ways in Greyharbor; it always had. Within a month, four elders had resigned. One had fled. The last stood up at the autumn assembly, in front of the whole city, and read his own name from Wren's arm as she held it up like a lantern, and then sat down among the people instead of above them, and no one cheered, but no one threw anything either, which in Greyharbor is nearly the same thing.

* * *

**Caspian**

Caspian Hale got his brother back on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tam had never respected the calendar and wasn't about to start.

He came to the door of Caspian's narrow rooms above the chandlery with a bundle of eels under one arm and ten missing years in his eyes, and he looked at Caspian for a long moment and said, "You got old."

"You got *dead*, according to the harbor rolls. We're even."

They ate the eels. They argued about how to cook eels, which was how the Hale brothers said *I missed you*, and always had been. And somewhere in the middle of it Tam went quiet, and looked into his cup, and asked the question Caspian had been dreading for a month.

"The testimony. What they used to take me. That was yours, wasn't it. I can feel the shape of the hole."

Caspian set down his cup. "Yes."

Tam nodded slowly. "And the Seneschal offered you your rank back if you gave up the woman who freed me."

"Yes."

"And you said no."

"I said several words. Most of them were no."

Tam laughed — the same crooked laugh, thank every drowned god, the same one — and refilled both cups. "Then we're square, little brother. Or near enough. Near enough is the Hale family motto; I'm having it embroidered."

Caspian found, in the weeks that followed, that he did not want his rank back. He wanted something stranger. He started showing up at the temple readings, standing at the back with his arms crossed like a man attending under protest, and eventually Wren called him on it.

"You could just admit you like it here," she said.

"I'm monitoring a potential magical hazard," he said. "You. The ink. It's clearly spreading up your neck."

"It's a birthmark."

"It's a *ledger*, Wren."

"It's a very tasteful ledger." She smiled, and then, because she was Wren and honesty had become a habit she couldn't shake: "Stay, then. If you're going to monitor me, do it properly. There's a lot of record-keeping to be done, and the city needs someone who knows which laws are real and which ones the elders invented to keep themselves comfortable."

He stayed. He told himself it was civic duty. Ossa told him it was something else, at length, in rhyme, and charged him a copper per verse.

* * *

**Wren**

Winter came, and this time the granaries were full, because no one's harvest had been quietly eaten by a debt. Wren learned the shape of her new life in small ways: a stall in the market that people simply began leaving offerings at, until she gave up and started selling charms again — honest ones, paper and prayer and, occasionally, ink that remembered, priced fairly, no wishes granted, *the management accepts no liability for miracles*.

She kept the records. Every night, by lamplight, she transcribed from her own skin onto proper vellum, and the scribes' hall reopened with nine students, then twenty, then forty, children learning letters because letters were freedom now, not collateral.

On the anniversary of the burning, she walked out to the harbor cliffs alone, where the grass still grew in the shape of a dragon's landing, and she sat with the hole inside her and let it ache.

She couldn't remember her mother. But the fishwife's granddaughter brought her bread on feast days "because your ma fed us the winter of the gray fever." A retired Warden tipped his hat and said, "She argued the Wardens into the ground once, your mother, over an eviction — finest thing I ever saw." A woman at the market pressed a skein of blue wool into her hands: "Maren Thornbury's favorite color. Everyone knew it. Thought you should know it too."

The city remembered for her. Piece by piece, story by story, the woman was being rebuilt out of other people's keeping, and Wren understood, finally, that this was what inheritance actually was — not a name on a contract, not a debt, but a self held in common and handed down.

She opened her ink bottle. She wrote, on a scrap of good paper, in her careful hand:

*Maren Thornbury. Saved her daughter. Sold her daughter. Fed the hungry. Argued like a storm. Loved blue.*

She folded it small and put it next to her heart, where memories live when they have nowhere else to go.

Behind her, boots on the grass — Caspian, breathing hard from the climb, carrying two cups of terrible tavern wine and pretending he hadn't run. Ossa's voice drifted up from halfway down the cliff path, already composing verses about this exact moment, for a fee.

"Thought you might want company," Caspian said.

Wren looked out at the sea, where the light was doing something golden and improbable on the water, and at the city below, waking into another morning that belonged to no one's ledger.

"I want a great many things," she said. "Sit down. You didn't ask."
