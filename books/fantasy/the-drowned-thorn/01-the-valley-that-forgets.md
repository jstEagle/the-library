## 1. The Valley That Forgets

There were three things everyone in Thornvale agreed on, and only one of them was true.

The true thing was that the valley forgot. Not the people—people remembered fine, mostly, except for old Tam Wicker who called every child "Bram" regardless of name or gender—but the valley itself. Words spoken in Thornvale did not carry the way words carried elsewhere. Prayers said here went nowhere in particular. Blessings curdled into ordinary breath. A curse was just a rude noise, and a wish was just a want with better posture.

Theo Villalobos had lived nineteen years inside that forgetting and had learned to love it the way you love a roof: without thinking about it until it leaks.

The second thing everyone agreed on was that the thorn hedge at the valley's mouth had been there forever, which was false, though "forever" was close enough that arguing about it seemed a waste of a good afternoon.

The third thing was that Theo was a quiet young man who would amount to nothing much, and this was the lie Theo worked hardest to keep alive.

* * *

Morning came to Thornvale the way it always did: gray, unhurried, smelling of woodsmoke and wet stone. Theo was awake before it finished arriving, as they were every morning, lying in the narrow bed under the eaves of Grandmother Ilsa's house and listening to the particular silence of a place where magic could not follow.

It was not an empty silence. People imagined silence as absence, but Theo knew better. It was a held silence, a silence with its hand over its own mouth. Somewhere beyond the ridge, past the thorn hedge and the gray border-stones that marked the edge of the valley's forgetting, the world was full of humming—wards and blessings and hexes and the low constant drone of ley-lines, if half the peddlers' tales were true. In here, all of it stopped at the stones like water at a weir.

In here, Theo's blood was just blood.

They lay still a moment longer, cataloguing, the way they did every morning. Heartbeat: ordinary. Hands: steady. The word that lived behind their sternum, folded up small and patient as a moth in winter: still asleep.

Good. Still asleep. Still safe.

Then they got up, dressed in the dark, took the wooden sword from under the floorboard, and went out to practice the oath they were never supposed to know.

* * *

The practice yard was really just the flat ground behind the tannery, which meant it smelled like ambition gone wrong, but it was private, and privacy was worth more than perfume in Thornvale. The village didn't wake properly for another hour. Mist sat in the low fields like a guest who'd overstayed. Somewhere a rooster made its argument, lost, and tried again.

Theo set their feet shoulder-width on the packed earth. Drew a slow breath. And began.

No sound. That was the whole discipline of it—the whole impossible art. The forms had names; the names had weight; the weight was the danger. So Theo moved through them silently, mouthing each word against the back of their teeth, letting the wooden blade trace what the voice would do if the voice ever dared. First form: *the greeting*, blade rising slow as a door opening. Second form: *the naming*, a short sharp cut that would have been a word like a struck bell. Third through ninth, each one a sentence Theo had never once spoken aloud, learned from a book that no longer existed, taught by hands that no longer moved.

Grandmother Ilsa had burned the book when Theo was nine. She'd burned it in the kitchen hearth on an ordinary Tuesday while making bread, and she'd watched it catch with an expression Theo had spent ten years trying not to remember: not grief exactly. Arithmetic. The look of someone counting what a thing cost and deciding they could pay it.

"You'll thank me," she'd said, and turned back to her dough.

Theo had not thanked her. But they had also not forgotten a single form, because some part of them understood—even at nine, even furious—that a thing burned out of a book could still live in a body, and Ilsa must have known it too, because why else leave the floorboard loose? Why else let Theo find the wooden sword, whittled smooth by hands two generations dead, hidden under the boards like a relic under an altar?

You couldn't kill an inheritance. You could only decide what your children would inherit: the truth, or the shape of the truth with all the dangerous parts sanded off.

Ilsa had chosen the shape. Theo practiced the sanding away, every morning, alone, behind the tannery.

* * *

By the fourth form, the mist had begun to lift and Theo had begun to sweat, which was the point. Sweat was honest. Sweat said: *this is work, this is exercise, this is a country person keeping fit*, and if anyone ever saw—and no one ever saw, but *if*—that was the story. Theo Villalobos, swinging a stick around before dawn because there was nothing better to do in a valley whose chief exports were wool, cheese, and resignation.

The seventh form was the hard one. They always were, the ones near the end. Theo's arms knew it, though—knew the long diagonal sweep, the turn, the counter-turn, the moment where the blade should sing a word so old it predated the kingdom that now hunted such words. Their mouth shaped it silently. *Vael.* Just the first syllable. Even silent, even toothless, it felt like holding a coal in the cupped tongue.

Their skin prickled.

Theo stopped mid-turn.

Not cold-prickle. Not morning-mist prickle. The other kind—the kind that started in the marrow and moved outward, like something turning over in sleep. Like the moth behind their sternum shifting its wings without opening them.

"None of that," Theo whispered, which was itself a small irony, whispering to their own blood. "We talked about this. We're a quiet family. We're a *boring* family."

The prickling faded, grudgingly, the way a cat leaves a lap: slowly, with the clear implication that it could return whenever it pleased.

Theo stood in the thinning mist, heart going too fast for someone who'd supposedly just been doing calisthenics, and waited until their body agreed to be ordinary again. Then they finished the forms—all of them, because stopping mid-sequence felt like leaving a door unlatched—and sheathed the wooden sword in the loop of their coat where it looked, to any casual observer, like a walking stick. A very well-balanced walking stick. With a guard.

They were halfway home when they passed Bram Wicker—actually a girl named Odile, but the valley had given up correcting him decades ago—who was mending a fence in the gray light.

"Morning, Bram," Theo said.

"Morning, Bram," Odile replied, with the serenity of the long-acclimated. "Your grandmother sent me looking for you. Says the peddler's cart came over the pass early, and she wants you home before he starts talking."

Theo felt it then, small and cold, sliding down between the ribs like a splinter: the edge-of-fear feeling, the one that lived permanently under their contentment like a stone under a mattress. Peddlers talked. Talking was the entire profession. And Ilsa wanting Theo home *before* the talking started was Ilsa's way of saying the talking might matter.

"Anything wrong?" Odile asked, squinting at their face.

"No," Theo said, and found, as always, that the valley helped. In Thornvale, a lie came out smooth as river stone. "Just hungry."

* * *

Ilsa Villalobos was seventy-one years old and built like the answer to a question nobody wanted asked. She stood at the hearth stirring porridge with the same arm that had once, according to a story she told exactly once and never repeated, broken a tax collector's nose over an unfair assessment of the goat count. Her hair was white and pinned like a fortification. Her eyes were pale gray, and when she looked at Theo, Theo always had the sensation of being read rather than seen.

"You're late," she said.

"The mist was thick."

"Mm." She handed them a bowl without looking. "Sit. Eat. When the peddler comes, you'll be in the back room mending nets, and you won't come out, and you won't listen."

"I never listen."

"You listen beautifully. It's one of your most alarming qualities." She sat across from Theo, and for a moment the fortification flickered—just a hairline crack, quickly mortared over. "Salt on the wind this morning," she said, to the porridge more than to Theo. "Early cart. Off-season."

"So?"

"So salt means change, and change means talk, and talk—" She stopped herself, which she almost never did. Ilsa finished sentences the way she finished arguments: completely. "Eat your breakfast, little moth."

That was the old nickname. Nobody living knew why she used it. Theo had stopped asking at twelve, when the asking had made her go quiet for two full days.

They ate. Outside, the rooster finally won its argument. Down the lane, shutters opened one by one, and Thornvale began its day of forgetting: bread baked, wool carded, children scolded, all of it wrapped in that soft gray hush where nothing spoken meant quite what it would mean anywhere else in the world.

Theo washed the bowls and went to the back room and got out the nets, because obedience was cheaper than questions, and began to mend. The knots came easy. Knots always had. There was something in the hands—Ilsa said it came from her side, though she said everything came from her side except the things that worried her—and the netting needle moved through the mesh with a rhythm close enough to the sword-forms to be almost restful.

Almost.

Through the wall, they heard the cart arrive. Wheels on stone. The complaint of an axle. The peddler's voice, big and warm and carrying, greeting the village by name—which was impressive, since the peddler changed every season and the names changed with him.

And then, beneath the chatter, the sound Theo had been dreading without knowing it: the creak of the front door, Ilsa's footsteps crossing the main room, and the peddler's voice dropping—not to a whisper, peddlers didn't whisper, but to that lower register reserved for news that wasn't merchandise.

"—from the capital, mistress, straight from the capital, and I'll say it plain since you're paying in eggs: the crown's put out a decree. A hunt, they're calling it. For the gifted."

The netting needle stopped.

Theo sat very still in the back room, among the mended nets, in the magic-dead quiet of the valley that forgets, and listened to the one thing they had been practicing nineteen years never to hear.

"They're calling it a census," the peddler went on, cheerfully, horribly. "But my cousin's wife's brother drives for the surveyor's office, and he says the surveyors aren't counting sheep."

A chair scraped. Ilsa's voice, when it came, was perfectly level, perfectly pleasant, and pitched—Theo realized with a chill—to be heard through walls.

"How interesting," said Grandmother Ilsa. "Theo! Come out here. Bring the eggs."

Theo looked down at their hands. Steady. Ordinary. Liar's hands, trained liar's hands, and even so—

Even so, the moth behind their sternum stirred, once, like something hearing its name.

They went out to the main room, carrying the basket, wearing their best boring face, and found the peddler watching them with the bright, incurious eyes of a man who noticed absolutely everything and reported none of it for free.
