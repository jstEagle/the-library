## 2. Salt on the Wind

The peddler came on a Tuesday, which was itself an omen, because Tam Wicker never came to Thornvale on a Tuesday. He came on market days, or he came when his mule decided it had suffered enough of him, but Tuesdays were for the low road and the river towns, and everyone in the valley knew it.

Theo knew it best of all, because Theo kept the valley's small ledger of habits the way other people kept ledgers of coin. Tam bought honey from the Fenwick sisters on the first Thursday of every month. He complained about his knees at every hearth that would host him. He told the same story about the bear in the Kettle Mountains at least four times a year, improving it slightly each time, so that by now the bear was the size of a chapel and had opinions about cheese.

So when the mule's bell sounded at the top of the switchback road on a gray Tuesday morning, Theo set down the basket of late apples they'd been hauling up from the terraces and stood very still in the orchard grass, listening.

The bell sounded wrong. Not broken—wrong the way a voice is wrong when someone is trying to sound cheerful and failing. It rang quick and nervous, jangled by a man driving his animal too fast for the last mile of a road that punished hurry.

"Grandmother," Theo said to the empty air, out of habit, though Ilsa Villalobos was half a valley away at her loom. Then, because the air didn't answer and never did—*that's the whole point of this place*—Theo shouldered the basket and started down through the trees at a pace just short of running.

Thornvale sat in its bowl of hills like water sits in a cupped hand, held there by nothing anyone could name. The road in wound down through birch and old stone walls, and where the road crossed the boundary—the line, Ilsa called it, tapping the ground with her stick as if the ground might confirm it—the world went quiet in a way visitors always noticed and villagers always forgot. No charms worked here. No blessings took root. A priest had come once, years back, and stood at the crossing muttering his little lights into being, and watched them gutter out like wet wicks, and had left looking personally insulted.

Magic could not follow you into Thornvale. That was the promise. That was the whole architecture of Theo's life, built on that one flat fact the way a house is built on a foundation nobody looks at.

Which meant that whatever made Tam Wicker drive his mule like the hounds of the deep dark were behind him had happened *outside*, and was now standing at the edge of the valley, politely unable to come in.

* * *

Tam had stopped at the well in the lower square, which he never did either—he always went first to the inn, because Tam's priorities were famously organized around ale. The mule stood steaming gently, ears flat, radiating the opinion that all humans were a mistake. A knot of villagers had already gathered, the way knots of villagers do, growing by ones and twos with the shameless curiosity of people who live somewhere nothing happens.

"—telling you, it's a proclamation." Tam's voice carried across the square, pitched high. "Read it myself, or near enough. Crier read it in Marrowbridge and then again at the toll bridge, wax seal and everything, the big seal, not the little one."

"What kind of proclamation?" Old Marta Fenwick, arms crossed over her apron, in the tone she used for weather and other things that thought themselves important.

"The crown's declared a—" Tam lowered his voice, which had exactly no effect whatsoever, since the entire square leaned in as one organism. "A hunt. For the gifted. Anyone carrying the talent, any bloodline known or suspected. They're calling it a Survey of Loyalties, but the crier said *hunt*, plain as bread. Said there's coin in it, too. Informers get paid."

The square made a sound—not a gasp, exactly. More the collective exhale of forty people deciding how worried to be.

"Well," said Marta, after a moment. "We've got none of that here. Never had any. Valley's dead as a doornail to all that."

"Dead as a doornail," agreed the miller's son, who was nineteen and said everything twice.

Theo stood at the edge of the gathering with the apple basket against their hip and felt the words go through them like cold water through cloth. Not soaking. Passing. But passing *through*, all the same, touching everything on the way.

*A hunt.* The words arranged themselves in Theo's chest and refused to rearrange. Somewhere beyond the ridge, beyond the quiet, men with the big wax seal were making lists. Names of families. Bloodlines known or suspected. And there was a family in Thornvale whose bloodline was not merely suspected but ancient, and oath-bound, and currently disguised as a woman who wove wool and a grandchild who grew apples and talked to absolutely no one.

Tam spotted Theo then, and his face did something complicated—relief and guilt wrestling briefly before relief won. "Theo! Good, good. Your grandmother sent word down last week she wanted the Almshill papers, and I've got them, plus news from three towns, plus—" he patted the mule's flank, "—this poor creature has walked through my dreams all night, so if the inn's open—"

"I'll take the papers to her," Theo said. Their voice came out level. They were proud of that later, the levelness. "She'll want them today."

"Oh, she will," Tam said, and then, quieter, with the peddler's particular skill of saying a true thing while pretending to say nothing: "She'll want them today, right enough. There's a letter in among them, Theo. Sealed. Crown seal." His eyes flicked up, met Theo's, and slid away. "I didn't read it. Wouldn't know how, mostly. But paper travels heavy sometimes, and that one traveled heavy."

He dug in the cart and produced a packet tied in string—land records, tax receipts, the dull sediment of valley life—and Theo took it with hands that did not shake, because they had practiced not shaking for seventeen years, and practice holds even when your stomach drops through the floor of the world.

* * *

Ilsa Villalobos was at her loom when Theo came up the hill, and she did not stop weaving, and that was the first confirmation. Grandmother Ilsa noticed everything—weather, lameness in sheep, the exact moment a lie entered a room—and she noticed the shape of the packet in Theo's arms from thirty feet away, and her shuttle paused for exactly one beat too long before continuing.

"Tam came early," she said.

"He came on a Tuesday."

"Mm." The shuttle flew. "And?"

"There's a letter. Crown seal." Theo set the packet on the table by the window, where the light fell, where letters were meant to be read. "Grandmother. They're hunting the gifted. A survey, they're calling it, but Tam says the crier said *hunt*. There's coin for informers."

The loom went silent.

Ilsa sat very still, her hands resting on the warp threads, and for a long moment the only sound in the house was the fire ticking in the grate and, far off, a crow arguing with the sky. She was seventy-one that autumn and looked like what she was—a hard old woman shaped by hard work, face creased into a map of squinting against sun and wind. People in the valley called her flinty. Theo had grown up thinking it was just a way some women were.

Now, watching her, Theo understood that the flintiness had been load-bearing.

"Did you open it?" Ilsa asked.

"It's not addressed to me."

"No," Ilsa said softly. "It wouldn't be."

She rose from the loom with the unhurried care of a woman whose knees had opinions, crossed the room, and picked up the packet. She weighed it in her hand. She did not untie the string.

Instead she carried it to the hearth.

"Grandmother—"

"Hush now." Ilsa knelt at the fireplace, which she had built up hot that morning, hotter than October required, and Theo understood—with the slow, sick click of a lock turning—that the fire had been ready before the news ever arrived. That Ilsa had been burning things for days, maybe weeks, keeping the hearth fed past reason, and had simply been waiting to learn whether she could stop.

The string came off. The land records she set aside, tapping them into a neat stack. The letter she held up to the light first, turning it, reading the outside of it the way you might read a face.

"From the Office of Surveys and Records," she murmured. "Addressed to the household of record, Thornvale, regarding historical holdings." A dry, humorless breath. "Historical holdings. That's what we are now. Holdings."

"What does it say?"

"It says they're coming to count us." She fed the corner of the letter to the flames. "It says every household will be surveyed, every lineage traced back three generations, and any person found to carry the old talent will be registered for the crown's protection." The paper caught, curled, went orange at the edges. "*Protection,*" Ilsa repeated, tasting the word like milk gone off. "That's the new word for it. Used to be *cleansing*. At least that was honest."

Theo watched their family history—or whatever lived inside that envelope—burn, and felt something in their chest pull tight as wire. "You're not going to read it?"

"I don't need to read it. I wrote half of it." Ilsa glanced up, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "Thirty years ago, when the last survey lapsed, someone had to fill in the false pages, child. Who do you think kept our ledger boring? Boring is a craft. I have spent your entire life making us boring."

"You told me we were apple farmers."

"We *are* apple farmers. We are also the last of a line that bound a thing so old the mountains were young when it learned to hate, and if the crown's clerks trace three generations of Villalobos and find the gaps where names should be, they will dig, and digging is the one thing we cannot survive." She turned back to the fire and fed it the rest, piece by piece, watching each page catch. "So we burn. We burn the true ledger, the correspondence, the old letters, all of it. What's left will fit in a shoebox, and the shoebox will be duller than a sermon."

The fire roared. Shadows climbed the walls. Theo stood in the doorway with their heart going like a drum and their grandmother kneeling at the hearth like a penitent, feeding the past into the flames page by page, and the dread that had been circling all morning finally settled—came down over the valley slow and gray and total, like weather, like fog filling the bowl of the hills until nothing above knee-height could be trusted.

"How long?" Theo heard themselves ask. "How long do we have?"

"Survey riders move slow. Clerks slower." Ilsa didn't turn around. "A season, perhaps two. Long enough to seem safe. Long enough for you to convince yourself it's fine." She brushed ash from her fingers. "It will not be fine."

"They can't touch us here." Theo's voice cracked on it, young and stubborn. "The valley doesn't allow it. No magic crosses the line. You said so yourself, a hundred times. Whatever they're hunting, it can't follow us in."

Ilsa rose then, slowly, joints cracking, and came to stand in front of Theo, and took their face in both hands the way she had when Theo was small and feverish. Her palms smelled of woodsmoke and lanolin.

"No magic follows us in," she agreed quietly. "But clerks don't need magic, child. They need ink and patience, and they have oceans of both. And a hunt doesn't stay outside a border. A hunt walks in wearing a surveyor's coat and asks friendly questions about trade routes, and writes everything down, and leaves, and comes back with soldiers." Her thumbs stroked once across Theo's cheekbones. "The valley hides the gift. It has never once hidden the blood. Do you understand me? Paper remembers what the valley forgets."

Outside, the wind shifted, and Theo would swear forever after that it came off the distant sea, miles east beyond the mountains, salt and cold, though no wind had ever managed that crossing. It rattled the bare branches and pushed smoke sideways from the chimney, and somewhere down in the village Tam Wicker was buying his ale at last, telling his bear story, spreading the news like seed grain across ground that had been peaceful for a generation.

"I understand," Theo said, because their grandmother needed to hear it, and because some part of them already did—had understood since the mule's bell rang wrong on a Tuesday morning, since the word *hunt* crossed the square like a shadow of a bird of prey.

"Good." Ilsa released their face and turned back to the fire, where the last of the letter had gone to white flakes. She stood looking into the flames a long moment, and her shoulders, usually set like a wall, curved inward just slightly, just enough for Theo to see the shape of the woman underneath the flint.

"Grandmother? What was in the rest of it? The parts you burned before today?"

Ilsa was quiet for so long Theo thought she wouldn't answer.

"Names," she said finally. "Our names, child. All the ones that came before. And what they owed, and what they paid, and to whom." She picked up the poker and stirred the embers until every scrap of paper broke apart into anonymous gray. "Debts, Theo. Our family has been paying down a debt for three hundred years, and the interest has just come due."

She looked at Theo over her shoulder, firelight catching the lines of her face, and said, in a voice that was almost gentle:

"And tomorrow, I think, it's time you learned what we owe, and to whom, and why the thorn still waits. Sleep tonight. Eat breakfast first. Some truths go down easier with bread."

Down in the valley, the last light failed, and the fog came up the slopes to swallow the orchards, and Theo lay awake under the eaves listening to the wind worry the shutters, feeling—for the first time in a life built on silence—the weight of every word they had never spoken, pressing against the backs of their teeth like seeds.
