## 3. Grandmother's Hands

The morning after the peddler left, Grandmother Ilsa baked bread.

That was the first strange thing. Theo stood in the doorway of the kitchen with the cold still clinging to their coat and watched her work the dough with those broad, scarred hands of hers—hands that had gutted fish, stitched wounds, once (Theo was fairly sure) broken a tax collector's nose—and thought: *she knows something I don't, and she is making bread about it.*

"You're staring," Ilsa said without looking up. "Staring doesn't knead."

"You burned a letter last night."

"I burn lots of things. Candle stubs. Bad ideas. Your grandfather's poetry." She folded the dough over itself with a violence that seemed, in retrospect, meaningful. "Burning is a perfectly respectable activity."

"It wasn't candle stubs. It was sealed. Wax and everything. Nobody seals candle stubs."

Ilsa paused. Flour dusted her forearms to the elbow, white against the old brown of her skin, and for a moment she looked—not old, exactly. Theo had always thought of her as old the way one thinks of mountains as old: permanent, unbothered, part of the furniture of the world. But standing there in the grey kitchen light with her hands buried in dough, she looked like someone who had been carrying something heavy for a very long time and had just remembered how heavy it was.

"Sit down, Theo," she said. "This bread needs an hour, and so do we."

* * *

She sat across from Theo at the kitchen table—the same table where Theo had learned letters, lost teeth, been fed soup through three fevers—and laid her hands flat on the wood. Up close, in good light, Theo could see what they'd never really looked at before: the scars were not random. They ran in lines. Deliberate lines. Thin pale strokes crossing the backs of her fingers and up over her wrists like a script written by someone who had run out of paper.

"When did you get those?" Theo asked.

"A long time ago." Ilsa turned her hands over, palms up, as if showing they held nothing. "Before your mother was born. Before Thornvale, even."

"Thornvale has always been here."

"No." She said it simply, the way she said *the well is dry* or *winter comes early this year*. "Nothing has always been anywhere. That's the first lie I told you, and it's not even the biggest one."

Theo's stomach did something complicated. "Grandmother—"

"Ilsa." Her mouth twitched. "If we're going to do this properly, you may as well use my name. It'll be easier when you're angry with me, and you will be angry with me. You have my temper; you'll want something to aim it at."

"I'm not angry."

"You will be." She got up, went to the pantry, and moved aside a sack of barley that had sat untouched since before Theo could remember. Behind it, set into the stone, was a door no bigger than a loaf of bread. From it she drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft with age, and carried it back to the table like it might bite her.

"This belonged to your grandfather," she said. "And his mother before him. And hers before *her*, all the way back to a woman named Sera Villalobos, who was born four hundred years ago in a city that is now under a marsh."

"Vaelmora," Theo said. The name came out of them before they knew why. It tasted like salt.

Ilsa's eyebrows went up. "You've heard it?"

"No. I mean—" Theo frowned, pressing at the place in their memory where the word had come from and finding nothing but the word itself, sitting there like a stone at the bottom of clear water. "No. I don't think so. It just... arrived."

Something flickered across Ilsa's face—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. As if the word arriving unbidden in Theo's mouth confirmed something she had spent decades hoping would never need confirming.

"So it's already starting," she said quietly. "Even here. Even in the dead valley." She began unwinding the cord. "Then we haven't got the luxury of easing into it. I'm sorry, child. I'd planned to tell you gently, over years, the way you feed a fire stick by stick. But the crown has started hunting, and gentle years are a luxury we no longer own."

She opened the oilcloth.

Inside lay a book—or what had once been a book. Its cover was black leather gone greenish with age, cracked like dried mud, and its pages, when Ilsa opened it, were not paper. Theo leaned closer and felt the hair on their arms rise. The pages were thin sheets of some dark material, veined and faintly glossy, and they were covered edge to edge in writing that shifted when Theo wasn't looking directly at it.

"What is that made of?"

"Bark," said Ilsa. "From a tree you'll hear plenty about soon enough. Don't touch it yet. It remembers hands, and yours it hasn't met."

"That's not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be. Comfort is for afterward." She smoothed a page flat with one scarred finger. "Now. Questions first, or story first? You're my blood, so I know you'll say questions, and then I'll spend all morning answering the wrong ones. So I'm going to tell the story, and you're going to hold your questions until the end, and if any of them survive, I'll answer every one. Agreed?"

Theo agreed, mostly because they were too busy staring at the shifting script to argue.

* * *

"Four hundred years ago," Ilsa began, "there was a kingdom called Vaelmora. Rich, proud, built on a river delta where the water met the sea—a city of canals and bridges, towers white as bone. And beneath it, in the oldest part of the oldest quarter, grew a thorn."

"A thorn."

"A tree, if you want to be generous. Black bark, black branches, thorns as long as your forearm. It grew from a seed older than the kingdom, older than anything with a name, and it slept. Mostly. But it dreamed, and its dreams leaked." Ilsa's voice had changed—flattened into the cadence of recitation, as though she were reading from a page she'd memorized so long ago the words had worn smooth. "People near it began to speak things that came true. Small things, at first. A baker says *may your bread never burn* and it doesn't, ever again, no matter the oven. A mother says *sleep now* to a feverish child and the fever breaks. Word-magic. Gift-speech. Whatever name you like."

"And that was bad?"

"For a while, it was wonderful. That's the trap of it. The city bloomed. No drought touched Vaelmora's fields. No plague crossed its bridges. Blessings everywhere, cheap as rainwater." She looked down at her own scarred hands. "But words are like water in another way too. They find the cracks. A blessing spoken in anger works differently than one spoken in love. A curse slips out of a tired mouth on a bad night and cannot be taken back. And underneath everything, the thorn drank. Every gifted word spoken in that city fed it, and it grew, and its dreams got louder, and the gifts got stronger, and the stronger they got, the more they fed it. Do you see the shape of it?"

Theo saw it. Their mouth had gone dry. "A wheel."

"A wheel that only turns one direction. Down." Ilsa turned a page. The script rippled away from her finger like minnows scattering. "In the end, a man spoke a sentence he should not have. We don't know his name—we know almost nothing about him except that he loved someone who died, and grief made his words heavy. He stood under the thorn and said, *let the whole world drown in sleep.*"

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a crow said something rude.

"The river rose," Ilsa continued. "Not gradually. All at once, like a struck bowl. The city went under in an afternoon. Thousands of people. The thorn drank that too—all that dying, all that grief—and it woke fully, and it spread. Black brambles climbing out of the floodwater, growing over rooftops, strangling everything for miles. The marsh around Vaelmora today? That's not natural wetland, child. That's the wound, still open after four hundred years."

"And the Villalobos line?"

"Was there." Ilsa's jaw tightened. "Sera Villalobos was a speaker—one of the strongest the city produced. When the flood came, she survived, and she gathered others like her, and they went into the drowned city with iron and salt and oaths, and they bound the thorn. Chained its dreaming. Put it back to sleep so deep it couldn't leak anymore." She paused. "It cost them. Binding words are not free. Sera gave..." Her hand drifted toward her own wrists, toward the deliberate scars. "They all gave. Years. Voices. Some gave everything and didn't survive the speaking. But it held. The thorn slept, and the survivors scattered, because a gift that strong draws attention, and attention, we learned, is the thing that kills us fastest."

"Because people fear what they can't control," Theo said. "Like the peddler said."

"Because people fear what they can't control," Ilsa agreed. "The Villalobos family carried the binding down the generations. Each generation, one heir learns the oaths. One heir keeps the words alive in case the thorn ever stirs. That duty passed from mother to daughter, father to son, whichever child the words chose—and the words choose, they aren't given, they're *recognized*. Your grandmother's grandmother. Your grandmother. Me." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and terribly sad. "And you, Theo. Though I prayed every day of your life that it wouldn't be so."

The floor seemed to tilt slightly. Theo gripped the table's edge. "Me. You think—I'm—"

"I don't think. I hoped otherwise for twenty years." She reached across the table and took Theo's hand, turning it palm-up the way she used to when checking for fever. "You were born outside the valley's border—did you know that? Half a day east, at the waystation, because you came early and impatient, typical of you. For half a day, you were inside the reach of the world's magic. And in that half-day, the words found you. I've watched you ever since. The way you hum when you practice with that wooden sword. The way animals go quiet around you. The way names sit strangely in your mouth." Her thumb pressed gently into Theo's palm. "Last night, when the peddler brought news of the hunt, you flinched at the word *gifted*. Not at the soldiers. At the word. Words have weight for you already, even here, even sleeping. That's how I know."

Theo pulled their hand back slowly. The kitchen suddenly felt smaller, the walls closer, the familiar room rearranged around a fact that had apparently been sitting in it all along.

"How long," they said. Their voice came out flat and strange. "How long have you known what I am?"

"Since the day you were born."

"And you told me nothing. Twenty years. Twenty years of *chop wood, mind the goats, don't wander past the ridge*—" Theo was standing now, though they didn't remember rising. Heat crawled up their neck. "You let me practice with a wooden sword like it was a game. You let me think I was nobody. Just a farm kid in a valley where nothing happens. You lied to me every single day of my life."

"Yes," said Ilsa.

"Just—*yes?* That's it? No explanation?"

"What explanation would you have wanted at seven years old, Theo? *Good morning, you're the heir to a chain forged in a drowned kingdom, and if anyone outside this valley learns what you are, they will kill you or worse.*" Her voice rose, then caught, and she mastered it with visible effort. "I watched what knowing did to your mother. She carried it like a stone in her chest from the day I told her. She stopped singing. Did you know that? Your mother sang constantly as a girl, and after I told her, she never sang again. She said she couldn't trust her own voice anymore—that every word might be a key she didn't mean to turn. I swore I'd never do that to another child of mine. So I waited. I waited for you to be grown, and strong, and rooted enough to bear it. And then the crown started burning villages, and I ran out of waiting."

Theo stood there breathing hard, anger and grief tangled together so tightly they couldn't tell which was which. Because the worst of it—the truly unbearable part—was that they understood. They could feel, even now, the truth of it settling into their bones: that every ordinary day of their life had been a gift Ilsa had paid for with twenty years of lying awake at night.

"I'm angry with you," Theo said finally.

"I know."

"But I'd have done the same thing."

"I know that too." Ilsa's mouth quirked, tired and fond. "You have my temper and my stubbornness. Poor child. You'll suffer."

Despite everything, despite the tilting floor and the book of thorn-bark and the drowned kingdom sitting in the middle of the kitchen table like an uninvited guest, Theo laughed—a short, wet, surprised sound. Ilsa reached out and squeezed their wrist, right over the pulse, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

* * *

Later—bread eaten, tea brewed, the initial shock worn down to something manageable—Ilsa opened the book again and showed Theo a single page near the beginning. The shifting script had settled here into fixed characters, dense and angular, and beneath them, in faded ink in a much later hand, someone had written a translation.

"This is Sera's own account," Ilsa said. "The binding oath, or the start of it. Three oaths, total. The Binding, which chains the thorn. The Unbinding, which releases it. And a third." She hesitated. "The third isn't written here. There's a space for it, and then nothing. Every text agrees there *is* a third oath, and none of them record what it is."

"Why not?"

"Nobody knows. The old texts say only that the third oath 'cannot be taught, only found.' Which is either profound wisdom or the laziest footnote in history, and I've had four hundred years to form opinions." She closed the book carefully, reverently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping thing. "You won't learn them tonight. You may never learn them. If the crown's hunters pass us by—if this blows over like weather—then you live your life, and this book goes back behind the barley, and I die happy having lied to you for forty more years."

"And if it doesn't blow over?"

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. Outside, the afternoon light was going long and gold across the yard, gilding the goat pen, the woodpile, the ridge beyond—all of it so familiar, so stubbornly ordinary, that it hurt to look at.

"If it doesn't blow over," she said, "then you'll need to know which roads run east."

Theo looked at the book. At their grandmother's scarred hands resting on its cover, hands that had held this secret through fire and flood and the raising of two generations. Hands that, Theo realized with a small cold shock, bore the same marks the old texts described—binding-scars, earned not by Ilsa herself but inherited, or perhaps copied, or perhaps—

"Grandmother," Theo said slowly. "These scars on your hands. Where did you actually get them?"

Ilsa smiled, and it was not entirely a reassuring smile.

"Ah," she said. "That question survives, does it? Good. Hold onto it. But ask me tomorrow—because tonight, before I answer anything else, I have to show you what your wooden sword has been trying to tell you for years. And Theo—" her eyes went to the window, to the eastern ridge, sharp and sudden as a bird startled off a wire, "—we should hurry. I think we have less time than the peddler's news suggested."

Far off, down in the valley, a dog began to bark.
