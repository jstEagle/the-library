## 4. The First Hunter

Theo was mending the south fence when the stranger rode into Thornvale, and the first thing Theo noticed—before the gray cloak, before the crown's badge glinting at his collar like a coin someone had tried to swallow—was that the man sat his horse wrong.

Not badly. Precisely. Every line of him squared to the saddle as if he'd been measured for it, which was its own kind of strange in a valley where people slouched, leaned, draped themselves over fences and each other like laundry left out too long. Thornvale folk wore their bodies loosely, the way they wore everything. This man wore himself like armor.

"Heads up," Theo called to Bram, who was pretending to work two posts down. "We've got a visitor."

Bram squinted down the lane. "Tax man?"

"Too clean."

"Lost pilgrim?"

"Too armed."

"Then it's either a wedding or trouble," Bram said, "and nobody'd marry into this weather." He spat thoughtfully into the mud. "Trouble, then."

The rain had stopped an hour ago, leaving the whole valley rinsed and gleaming, every thorn hedge along the ridge beaded silver. That was the thing about Thornvale that visitors never believed until they saw it: nothing here grew wild. The hedges were old, older than the village, older than any record anyone bothered keeping, and they grew in lines so straight that surveyors from the lowlands had been arguing about them for a hundred years. *Natural formations,* the maps said. *Glacial debris.* Nobody in Thornvale corrected them. Correcting people meant talking about the hedges, and nobody in Thornvale talked about the hedges.

The rider reined up at the gate, and Theo walked over with the easy, unhurried gait of a person who had absolutely nothing to hide, which took practice, and which Theo had gotten very good at.

"Morning," Theo said. "You'll want to stable that animal before the afternoon rain comes back. She's blowing hard."

The man looked down. His eyes were pale gray, the color of the sky deciding what it wanted to do, and they went over Theo once—boots, hands, face—in about a second and a half. Then he smiled, and it was a good smile, warm even, the kind of smile that made you want to tell him things.

"Captain Ren Ostrev," he said, dismounting. "Crown Office of Roads and Trade. I'm mapping the eastern routes." He produced papers with a flourish. "Your headman will want to see these."

"Mm." Theo looked at the seal without touching it. "Headman's Oskar Vey. He'll be at the mill, arguing with the miller. He's always at the mill arguing with the miller. It's practically a civic institution."

"Efficient of him."

"We're an efficient valley." Theo held out a hand for the reins. "I can take your horse. You can take your questions to Oskar. Fair trade."

Ostrev laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him—and handed over the reins. "And what do you do, in this efficient valley?"

"Fences," Theo said, patting the mare's neck. "Mostly mending what other people break."

* * *

Ostrev watched the young one walk his horse toward the stable and noted three things, because noting things was the whole of his profession and he was very good at his profession.

One: the youth had looked at the crown seal for exactly long enough to identify it, no longer. Most villagers stared. Seals impressed them; seals frightened them; either way, they stared. This one had glanced, catalogued, dismissed.

Two: the hands. Calloused across the palm in a way fence-mending explained, but also calloused across the knuckles and the inside of the right forearm in a way fence-mending did not. Ostrev had spent eleven years among soldiers and four more among people who pretended not to be soldiers. He knew what a sword callus looked like on a hand that was trying to look like something else.

Three: the name, when asked, came a half-beat too late. *Theo.* Just Theo. No family name offered, though everyone else in this valley gave theirs freely, proudly, at length, sometimes including livestock.

None of it meant anything. That was the honest truth of it, and Ostrev prided himself on honesty even in the privacy of his own skull. A thousand villages held a thousand young people with sword calluses and short names and careful eyes. The war had seen to that; half the realm had learned to fight and then learned to forget it.

But orders were orders, and the order said: *survey the eastern valleys, note population, trade goods, and any irregularities.* And the word *irregularities* had a second meaning these days, written between the lines in ink that wasn't ink. Everyone knew what the crown was hunting. Nobody said it aloud. Saying it aloud was how you got noticed by the people who collected the saying-aloud.

He found Headman Vey exactly where predicted, mid-argument, red-faced, triumphant. The miller was losing with dignity.

"Captain Ostrev, Crown Office of Roads and Trade." He laid the papers on the floury table between them. "Mapping the eastern routes. I'll need lodging, provisions for six days, and answers to some tedious questions."

Vey wiped his hand on his apron before shaking, which Ostrev appreciated. Flour got everywhere. "Roads! Well. About time the crown remembered we exist. We pay our tithes same as anyone and get nothing back but tax collectors and bad weather."

"You get roads eventually," Ostrev said. "Everything is roads eventually. Now—the route over Thorn Ridge. Who uses it?"

"Nobody, in winter. Shepherds, in summer. Peddlers twice a year, if the peddler's feeling brave."

"And beyond the ridge?"

"The marsh country. Nothing there but drowned land and drowned stories." Vey shrugged. "Why? Looking to build a road through a bog? Crown money'd drown faster than the travelers."

"I'm told there are ruins east. Old city. Vael-something."

The miller, who had been ostentatiously ignoring the conversation, became very interested in his grindstone. Vey's shrug developed a slight stiffness, like a hinge needing oil.

"Vaelmora," Vey said. "Flooded a hundred years back and more. Nothing there but eels and ghosts, and the eels have better manners. Why would the crown care about a drowned city?"

"Why does the crown care about anything?" Ostrev smiled. "Because a map with a blank on it offends somebody important. I'll be asking around the village over the next few days. Names, trades, comings and goings. Standard census work."

"Census," Vey repeated. Something flickered behind his eyes—too quick to read, gone before it landed. "Well. Thornvale's an open book, Captain. Ask away."

Everyone says that, Ostrev thought. Open books. And every open book I've ever met had pages glued together somewhere.

* * *

That evening Theo sat in Grandmother Ilsa's kitchen, watching her shell beans with the focused violence of a woman who had opinions about legumes, and waited for the right moment, which did not come, because Ilsa controlled the conversational tempo of that kitchen the way a river controls a boat.

Finally Theo just said it. "There's a crown man at the inn. Surveyor."

"I heard." Shell. Flick. "Ostrev. He asked Marta for barley soup and complimented her bread, which proves he's either a liar or a spy, possibly both."

"He's doing a census."

"He's doing something." Another bean surrendered. "Did he talk to you?"

"At the gate. Asked my name."

"What did you give him?"

"Theo."

Ilsa's hands paused—one heartbeat, no longer—and resumed. If Theo hadn't spent seventeen years learning to read those hands the way other people read faces, they'd have missed it entirely. "Just Theo."

"That's my name."

"It's half your name." She dumped the beans into the pot with a rattle like distant gunfire. "Eat your supper. And tomorrow, when the nice captain asks you questions, you'll answer them slow and stupid and boring. You're a fence-mender. You love fences. Fences are your whole world. Tell him about post-holes until his eyes glaze."

"They'll glaze anyway. Post-holes are fascinating."

"Theo."

"Yes, Gran."

She softened, just slightly, the way she only ever did indoors after dark. "He's not the first one to come asking pretty questions with a seal in his pocket. He won't be the last. The trick isn't hiding. Hiding is for rabbits. The trick is being so dull that hunting you feels like work."

"And if dull doesn't work?"

Ilsa looked at the fire for a moment. In the firelight her face went strange and old—not grandmother-old, but older, some other age wearing her features like a borrowed coat.

"Then we remember what we're," she said quietly. "Eat."

* * *

Ostrev spent three days being charming, and it worked beautifully, and it told him almost nothing, which was itself information.

Thornvale was friendly the way a locked door is smooth. Everyone answered his questions. Everyone answered them *fully*, at length, with digressions about weather and livestock and the scandalous price of salt, and by the end of each conversation he realized he had learned precisely nothing he hadn't known going in. It was the most polished performance of rural openness he had ever encountered, and he could not prove it was a performance, and it was driving him quietly mad.

The census notes filled themselves easily enough. Two hundred and eleven souls. Barley, wool, timber, one mill with a feud attached. No gifted persons registered, obviously—no village ever registered gifted persons, that was rather the point of the current unpleasantness—and no irregularities.

Except.

On the third evening, walking the perimeter of the village as the light died—he told himself it was surveying; the boundary stones did need recording—he passed the field where the young one, Theo, was working late. Fence posts. At dusk. In the cold. Which was either devotion or avoidance, and Ostrev had learned to distrust both.

What caught him was the sound.

A hum. Low, brief, gone almost before he registered it—like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, except deeper, and it seemed to come from the fence line itself, or from the wooden stave in Theo's hands. One note. One syllable, almost. As if the wood had tried to say something and thought better of it.

Theo straightened, set the stave down against the fence, and stretched like a laborer ending a shift. When he turned and saw Ostrev at the field's edge, he waved, cheerful and unbothered.

"Evening, Captain! Come to admire the post-holes?"

"Just walking." Ostrev walked over, unhurried. "Working late."

"Rain's coming tomorrow. Posts go easier in dry ground." Theo picked the stave back up, spun it idly in his hand—a little flourish, purely habit, the kind of thing every farm kid did with a stick—and Ostrev watched the wood turn and caught, again, the calluses, the balance of it, the way the stave moved like it weighed nothing and like it weighed everything at once.

"That's a fine piece of wood," Ostrev said. "Oak?"

"Rowan." A beat. "Good for fences. Keeps the wolves thinking."

"Wolves." Ostrev smiled. "Any wolves around here?"

"Not for a long time." Theo said it lightly, but his eyes didn't quite match the lightness, and for one suspended moment the two of them stood in the blue dusk measuring each other across three feet of muddy ground, and Ostrev felt the peculiar vertigo of a man who has spent years looking for a hidden door and suddenly suspects he's standing on one.

"Well," Theo said pleasantly, "if you're mapping, you'll want the ford marked properly. The old maps put it half a mile wrong, and every lost traveler ends up in our turnip fields blaming the cartographers."

"I'll note it." Ostrev tipped his hat. "Good night, Theo."

"Night, Captain. Mind the mud."

Ostrev walked back toward the inn, and behind him he heard—faintly, unmistakably this time—the hum again. One note. Almost a word.

He did not look back. Looking back was for amateurs. Instead he climbed the inn stairs, lit his lamp, opened his ledger, and wrote beneath the census figures, in small careful script:

*Irregularity: acoustic anomaly near south fields, source undetermined. Recommend extended observation.*

Then he sat for a long while, lamp burning, listening to the rain start against the shutters, and thought about his brother's face the last time he'd seen it, and about what the crown paid men to find in quiet green valleys, and about whether finding it would feel like justice or like something much worse.

In the morning, he decided, he would ask the young fence-mender about his family name.

* * *

Theo stood in the dark field long after the captain's lantern had vanished, holding the rowan stave, heart hammering.

It had never done that before. Not outside the border, not inside the valley where magic couldn't follow—except apparently it could *whisper*, apparently it could seep through whatever kept Thornvale safe, thin as smoke under a door, and it had picked tonight, with a hunter's ears thirty feet away, to try.

"Stupid," Theo whispered to the wood. "Stupid, stupid."

The stave was silent. Of course it was. It was a stick. It had always been a stick.

Except it had hummed, and the captain had heard it—had to have heard it—and tomorrow he would come asking pretty questions with that warm smile, and Theo would answer slow and stupid and boring, just as Gran said, and hope to every quiet god that dull was enough.

From the house, Ilsa's silhouette appeared in the kitchen window. She had seen. She had probably seen everything, the way she always did, and the light in the window stayed lit far too long, and Theo understood, with a cold settling certainty, that tomorrow the questions would not stop at post-holes.

Somewhere east, past the ridge, past the marsh, a drowned city slept under black thorn. Theo had never thought about it once in seventeen years.

Tonight it was all Theo could think about.
