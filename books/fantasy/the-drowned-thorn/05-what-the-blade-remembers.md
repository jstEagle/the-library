## 5. What the Blade Remembers

The trouble with living in a valley that forgets, Theo had learned, was that it forgot you too—slowly, politely, the way a neighbor stops waving. Thornvale's quiet was not silence exactly. It was the sound of a held breath that never quite released, and after eleven years Theo had stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat.

Most mornings.

This morning the quiet felt thin, like ice over a well, and Theo woke before dawn with their hand already curled around nothing—the shape of a sword hilt pressed into an empty palm.

"You're doing it again," Ilsa said from the doorway, though she didn't say what *it* was. She never did. She stood there in her shawl with her gray braid coming loose and her hands smelling of woodsmoke and lye, and she looked at Theo's empty fist the way she looked at weather coming over the ridge: not with fear, exactly, but with the particular attention of someone who has seen this storm before.

"Doing what?" Theo asked, which was also a kind of ritual between them. Ask the question you know the answer to. Keep everything small and ordinary and survivable.

"Sitting up like the house is on fire." Ilsa crossed to the hearth and coaxed the coals. "There's bread. There's work. The fence by the mill stream needs mending before Ostrev's men come through again."

Theo went still. "His men are coming back?"

"The surveyor sent word. Trade routes, he says." Ilsa's voice was carefully flat, the way water goes flat just before it freezes. "He wants to walk the mill road himself this time."

*Himself.* Captain Ren Ostrev had been in the valley four days now, lodging at the Wayfarer's Rest, buying rounds for farmers and asking questions shaped like small talk. How many families wintered here. Whether anyone kept old letters. Whether the children were taught letters at all, and by whom, and in what tongue.

"He's mapping trade routes," Theo said. "Trade routes don't need to know who teaches children."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "They don't."

She said nothing else, which from Ilsa was a paragraph.

* * *

The fence by the mill stream was older than Theo's patience and twice as stubborn. Post by post, they worked down the line while the sun climbed and the mist burned off the water, and all the while the wooden sword hung across their back where it always hung—a practice blade, ash wood, worn smooth as river stone, the one inheritance Ilsa had never tried to burn or bury or explain away.

It had no edge. It had no name. It was, as far as any surveyor would ever see, a farm tool for a farm person, something to knock down apples or frighten crows.

But this morning, when Theo reached up to shift its weight on their shoulder, the wood was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not body-warm. Warm the way a stone is warm when something has been sleeping under it.

Theo stopped walking. The mill stream made its small patient noise. Somewhere upslope a cow complained about the general state of existence, and Theo stood very still with their hand resting on the hilt and listened to their own heart do something arrhythmic and stupid.

"It's nothing," they told themselves, out loud, because saying things out loud made them smaller. That was the whole trick of Thornvale. Words got heavy somewhere past the ridge; inside the valley they were just words, light as chaff, harmless as breath. Say it plain and it stays plain.

Except the sword wasn't listening to the rules of the valley.

* * *

Ostrev found them at midday.

Theo heard him before they saw him—not because he was loud but because he was *even*, his footsteps metronome-steady on the path, a man who had never once hurried or dawdled in his life. He came down the mill road with two soldiers trailing at a respectful distance and a leather ledger under one arm, and he smiled the way tax collectors smile: accurately.

"Villalobos." He consulted the ledger as if confirming, though he'd known the name since the first hour. "Still mending fences? I'd have thought the village would pool its labor."

"We pool our opinions instead. Cheaper." Theo kept both hands visible and idle, one on the fence post, one nowhere near the hilt. Near the hilt. *Nowhere near the hilt.* They moved it deliberately, and hoped the movement read as casual rather than urgent.

"May I?" Ostrev gestured at the fence line without waiting for permission, walking it slowly, reading it like text. "Good joinery. Old patterns, though. Older than the valley's charter, I'd say." He glanced up. "Your family's been here longer than the records admit."

"My family's been here long enough to get tired of being asked about it."

That earned a real laugh, brief and genuine, which was somehow worse than the fake ones. Ostrev leaned against the fence opposite, close enough that the stream noise filled the space between them, and opened his ledger to a page dense with his own cramped hand.

"I'll be honest with you, Villalobos, because I think we'd both find lying exhausting." He turned the ledger so Theo could see—not the words, which were upside down anyway, but a charcoal sketch. A sword. A practice sword, ash wood, drawn with unsettling precision, right down to the hairline crack near the guard that only someone who had studied the thing for hours could have rendered.

Theo's mouth went dry.

"A peddler came through last month," Ostrev said mildly. "Sold ribbons and salt and gossip. He mentioned—he mentions a great deal—that the Villalobos child practices forms every morning in the fog. Alone. With a wooden blade. Never sparring. Never hunting. Just... forms." He closed the ledger. "I've been a surveyor nine years. Do you know how many people practice sword forms alone, against nobody, for years?"

"People who can't afford a partner."

"Mm. People who can't afford a partner practice badly and quit." Ostrev's eyes were pale gray and entirely without cruelty, which made them so much harder to face. "People who practice alone for years are practicing for something else. Discipline. Control." He let the word sit. "Keeping something *in*."

The stream talked on. Theo discovered they had been holding a fence post hard enough to raise splinters and made themselves let go.

"It's a farm," Theo said. "We grow turnips. You're welcome to inspect the turnips."

"I may." Ostrev straightened, brushed off his coat, and for a moment his composure slipped—not into menace but into something tired and human. "My brother practiced forms too. Alone. In a barn." Something moved behind his eyes and was locked away again. "Enjoy your turnips, Villalobos."

He walked back up the road, even-footed, ledger under his arm, and Theo stood in the mud and understood with total clarity that the net had already been woven and they were standing in it, and the only question left was whether the hunters knew yet what they'd caught.

* * *

Ilsa took one look at Theo's face when they came home and put down the bread knife.

"What happened."

"He drew my sword." Theo sat heavily. "From memory. The crack in the guard. Someone's been watching me train, Gran. For months, maybe years. The peddler told him—" Their voice cracked and they hated it. "Gran, why does a crown surveyor care whether I drill forms in the fog?"

Ilsa was quiet for a long time. She wiped her hands on her apron though they were clean, then wiped them again, and Theo watched her decide, visibly, between two doors.

"Show me the sword," she said.

"It's on my back, I haven't taken it—"

"*Show me.*"

Something in her voice made Theo's hands move before their mind agreed. They slid the ash blade free and laid it on the table between them, and in the low afternoon light through the kitchen window, both of them saw it at once.

The crack near the guard had changed.

It had always been a flaw, a hairline scar in the wood, dead as anything else in Thornvale. Now it ran deeper, branching like frost on glass, and along its length the grain had darkened—and if Theo tilted their head, if they breathed just right, they could almost—

"Humming," Ilsa whispered. "It's humming."

"It's not—"

"Don't tell me what it isn't." Her hand hovered above the wood without touching, the way you hover over a snake or a stove. "Eleven years, Theo. Eleven years this valley has swallowed every spark in you, and I told myself the quiet was deep enough, I told myself the border would hold, I told myself—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Say something. Anything. Count backward from ten."

"What? Why?"

"Because I need to know if it answers *you* or if it answers *words*. Count."

"Ten," Theo said, feeling foolish. "Nine. Eight—"

On *seven*, the sword hummed.

Not a vibration. Not the table trembling. A note, low and single, rising out of the ash wood like a voice rising out of deep water, and threaded through the note—Theo felt it more than heard it, felt it behind their sternum, in their teeth, in the old hollow place where something had been scooped out of them at birth and they'd never once been told what—a *word*.

One word. Unspoken. Shaped in the dark like a key shaped to a lock they didn't know they owned.

Theo stopped counting. The hum stopped. The kitchen was brutally, ordinarily silent, bread cooling, fire ticking, cow complaining somewhere upslope, and Theo sat with their hands flat on the table because if they didn't pin them down they might fly apart.

"What was that," Theo said. It wasn't a question. Questions go up at the end. This fell straight down like a dropped stone.

Ilsa had gone the color of tallow. She was looking at the sword, but Theo understood suddenly that she wasn't seeing the sword. She was seeing something fifty years gone, some other kitchen, some other blade, some other child counting numbers while the world rearranged itself around them.

"Gran."

"Don't speak it." Her voice came out sanded rough. "Whatever it gave you—whatever shape it put in your head—you don't say it. Not here. Not anywhere. You don't whisper it in your sleep, you don't mouth it in the bath, you don't—"

"*What was that?*" Louder than intended. The fire flinched. So did Ilsa.

And Theo watched, with the strange floating clarity of terror, as their grandmother—the woman who had lied to them for eleven years with the consistency of tide—reached across the table and took the wooden sword and held it against her chest like a dying bird.

"It remembers," she said. "God help us, the valley's forgetting is wearing thin, and the blade remembers what you are."

"What *am* I?" And there it was, the question under all the other questions, eleven years of small lies and burned letters and forms drilled in fog, finally dragged up into the light, and Theo's voice shook with something that was equal parts fury and fear. "Gran. Look at me. What am I?"

Ilsa closed her eyes. When she opened them, all the deflection had drained out, and what remained was worse than any answer: it was love, vast and helpless and afraid.

"You're the last," she said. "You're the last swordspeaker, and tonight we pack, because if that blade sang once, it will sing again, and next time the wrong ears will hear it."

Outside, far down the valley, a dog began to bark—and then another, and another, a chain of voices running toward the village from the direction of the mill road, where a surveyor's camp had quietly doubled in size since morning.

Ilsa set the sword down between them like a verdict.

"Start with the letters," she said. "We'll burn what we can carry the truth of, and run from the rest."
