## 6. Ash Wednesday in Thornvale

The fire was already lit when Ilsa came down to the cellar, which was a mercy, because she had spent the whole walk down rehearsing how she would light it and her hands had not agreed to be steady.

It was a small fire, as fires went. A kettle's worth of flame in the old iron basin where Theo's grandfather had once melted solder for the beehives. It did not look like an instrument of destruction. It looked like something you'd warm your fingers over on a cold morning, and Ilsa supposed that was fitting. Most of the terrible things in her long life had arrived looking like ordinary mornings.

She set the first box down beside it and did not open it right away.

Instead she stood there in the damp dark of the cellar, listening to the house above her — the particular creak of the third floorboard that meant Theo was awake and pretending not to be, the tick of the kettle they'd left on the stove out of habit though neither of them had asked for tea, the wind worrying at the loose shingle over the kitchen that she'd been meaning to fix for six years and would now never fix, because a woman did not climb onto her roof the week before soldiers came to burn her village.

Or perhaps they wouldn't come. Perhaps the peddler's news was half rumor, the way news usually was by the time it crossed three valleys. Perhaps the crown's hunt for the gifted would pass Thornvale by like weather passing over stone.

But Ilsa had lived eighty-one years, and in that time she had learned one thing about hope: it was useful for getting out of bed, and useless for planning.

So. The fire.

* * *

The archive was not grand. That was the joke of it, if there was a joke anywhere in this. Generations of Villaloboses had died — some quietly, some not — to keep safe a collection of papers that would have embarrassed a modest parish clerk. Ledgers. Letters. A child's copybook with oath-fragments practiced in a wobbly hand. Forty years of correspondence between her husband Aldric and a man called Wren Calloway whose letters always smelled faintly of incense and always took twice as long to say what they meant as they needed to.

She had told Theo the cellar held preserves. In fairness, it had also held preserves. The jars were still there along the east wall, plum and quince, and she supposed whoever found them after all this would think the Villalobos family had been devoted above all things to fruit.

Let them. Fruit had never drowned a kingdom.

She knelt — slowly, with the complaint of her knees, which had earned the right to complain — and opened the first box.

Her mother's handwriting looked up at her. *On the discipline of silence,* the page began, and below it, in the margin, a note in a younger version of the same hand: *Mama wrote this during the fever winter. She said a word kept is a word saved.* 

Ilsa read it through once, the way you visit a grave. Then she fed it to the fire.

The paper caught reluctantly, the way old paper does, curling brown at the edges before it gave itself up. She watched her mother's careful letters blister and go black and become nothing, and she found, to her mild surprise, that she did not weep. There would be time for weeping later, or there wouldn't; either way the work came first. Grief was like threshing. You didn't stop in the middle of the field to admire the chaff.

* * *

By midmorning she had worked through four boxes and developed a system, which was the only inheritance she'd ever gotten from her father and the only one she'd ever needed: read fast, decide faster, burn without ceremony. Some pages she let herself linger over. Aldric's proposal letter, which he had written to her in the form of a formal petition to "the honorable keeper of the eastern archive" because he could not say a plain feeling plainly even on paper. Their daughter Marta's drawings of the thorn, done when Marta was seven, the great black branches rendered as scribbles with too many eyes. A lock of hair in an envelope, unmarked, that she chose not to identify because identifying it would require burning it and she was not yet strong enough, so instead she put it in her pocket like a thief.

One item stopped her entirely.

It was a page from the oldest stratum of the archive, the layer that predated Aldric, that predated her own parents, written in a hand so faded it seemed less ink than memory. The paper was heavy, ragged-edged, the kind they hadn't made in two hundred years. And the words on it were not about oaths or bindings or the thorn at all.

They were an apology.

*To whoever keeps this after us: we did not win. Understand that. Whatever the songs say, whatever we wrote in our prouder years — we did not defeat the thorn, we did not bind it out of virtue, we stole the binding and we paid for it with a debt that has not come due yet but will. If you are reading this, the debt is coming due through you, and we are sorry, and we were sorry then, and no amount of sorrow un-drowns a kingdom.*

Ilsa sat back on her heels.

She had known the shape of this truth her whole life the way you know the shape of furniture in a dark room — by bruising against it. Her grandmother had told her pieces. Aldric had told her more, near the end, in the voice of a man setting down a weight he could no longer carry. But seeing it written, in that old penitent hand, made it real in a way that talk never managed. Paper did not flinch. Paper did not soften the blow for love of you.

This was the page. This was the one she would keep.

Not because it was comfortable. Because Theo deserved, someday, the whole truth — and because Ilsa knew herself well enough to know she might never find the courage to say it aloud. Cowardice, yes. But also mercy, of a kind. Let the dead tell the child the worst of it. The dead could bear being hated. The living had to go on making breakfast.

She folded the page into quarters and put it inside her bodice, against her heart, where important documents and stolen locks of hair apparently both went now.

Then she burned everything else.

* * *

It took until late afternoon. The smoke was a problem; she'd thought of that in advance and stuffed the chimney gap with wet sacking, and still the cellar took on a haze that made her eyes stream, so that by the end she was crying whether she meant to or not, which she suspected was the design of some wiser woman somewhere. Let the body do the grieving. The mind had plans to make.

The last thing to go was the copybook. The child's hand. Oath-fragments in wobbly letters, a boy named Aldric practicing his inheritance the way other boys practiced knots or whittling.

*Word one,* he had written. *Spoke it wrong. Grandfather says the thorn laughed.*

Ilsa held it over the basin a moment longer than the system allowed.

"Goodbye, love," she said, to the boy in the book, to the man he'd become, to the whole vanished congregation of her people. "You kept your word. All of you. Every cursed word."

And she let it burn.

* * *

Theo found her at dusk, sitting on the cellar steps with a cup of tea she had no memory of making, which was either a sign of deep absorption or early senility, and she chose to believe the former.

"You've been down here all day," Theo said. "The house smells like a chimney sweep's funeral."

"That'll be the preserves," Ilsa said. "Plum. It ferments."

Theo came down the steps and stood in the ruin of it — the ash, the scorched boxes, the empty shelves where forty years of hiding had lived. Ilsa watched her grandchild take it in. Watched the moment arrive in their face, the way weather arrives, first a shadow and then the rain.

"You burned it." Not a question. Theo had grown up in this house; they knew what an empty shelf meant the way farm children know a sky about to hail.

"I burned it."

"Why?" And then, before Ilsa could answer, with the terrible precision of the young: "Because of the peddler. Because of what he said about the hunt."

"The kettle's still hot upstairs," Ilsa said, "if you'd rather have this conversation somewhere with chairs."

"We're having it here."

Ah, thought Ilsa. There it is. The Villalobos stubbornness, arriving right on schedule, generation after generation, like taxes. She patted the step beside her. Theo sat, folding down with the boneless grace of youth, and for a moment they just breathed together in the smoky dark, two people who loved each other preparing to hurt each other, which was, Ilsa reflected, most of what families were.

"The crown has declared a hunt," Ilsa said. "For anyone with the gift. Papers, rewards, informers. The peddler wasn't lying — I've seen his face go honest before, usually when he wants more money, and it went honest twice."

"So we run. Both of us. Tonight."

"No."

"Grandmother—"

"Hear me out, and then you can argue, and I will pretend to consider your arguments, and then we'll do what I say, which is the traditional order of things." She took Theo's hand. It had calluses now, from the wooden blade, from the silent morning drills they thought she didn't know about. She had known about them for eleven years. She had listened outside the barn door to her grandchild practicing words with no sound, shaping oaths into the air like a deaf woman signing to an empty room, and every morning she had stood there with her heart in her mouth and let them continue, because a gift denied rots worse than a gift hidden. She had made her choices. They had not all been good ones.

"If we run together, they follow two sets of tracks," she said. "If I stay, they follow one. An old woman with a cellar full of jam is not interesting, Theo. An old woman traveling with a stranger is."

"You're not staying to be bait."

"I'm staying because this is my house and my valley and I am eighty-one years old and I have buried everyone I ever planned to grow old with, and I will not spend my last chapter running east with my skirts up like a girl in a ballad." She squeezed the young hand. "Also, someone has to feed the cat."

"We don't have a cat."

"Then someone has to explain to the neighbors why we don't have a cat. It's a whole story. There was a fish involved."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of Theo, which had been the point. Warmth first. Then the knife, gently.

"Listen to me now," Ilsa said, and her voice changed, and Theo heard it change, and went still. "If hunters come here — truly come, with warrants and ropes and not just questions — you do not stay to fight. You do not stay to save me. You go east."

"East is marshland. East is nothing."

"East is Vaelmora."

The name dropped into the cellar like a stone into a well. Ilsa felt Theo's breath catch — felt it, because under her hand the young pulse jumped. Even here, even in the magic-dead quiet of Thornvale where the gift slept like a stone at the bottom of a lake, some part of Theo's blood knew that name. Blood remembered what families tried to make it forget.

"There's nothing in Vaelmora," Theo said. "It drowned. It's a story. Towers in the marsh that rise at low tide, wrapped in black thorn, and everyone says anyone who goes in doesn't come out."

"Everyone says," Ilsa agreed. "Everyone has said for three hundred years. And yet our family's whole history is tangled in those towers like thread around a spindle, and it is past time you knew where the spool is." She reached into her bodice and drew out the folded page, the old ragged paper, and pressed it into Theo's hands. "Don't read it tonight. Read it when you get there, or don't read it at all — it's yours, and I'm done deciding what my children can bear. But if the worst comes, you go east, you find the drowned city, and you find out what you are from people who aren't too much of a coward to tell you."

"What I am," Theo repeated slowly. "Grandmother. What am I?"

And oh, there was the question, the one she'd been dodging since the day a seven-year-old had made a wooden sword hum a single note in the barn while pretending to be a knight, and Ilsa had stood outside the door and aged ten years in ten seconds.

"You are a Villalobos," she said. "Which used to mean something proud, and actually means something complicated, and will mean, one day, something I can't predict. That's the truest answer I have, and I hate that it's the truest answer I have, and I'd give my remaining teeth to have a better one."

Theo looked down at the folded page, turning it over, not opening it. Trusting her enough not to. That trust was the heaviest thing in the room, heavier than all the ash.

"When do I go?" Theo asked quietly. Which meant: *you've already decided it's soon.*

"Not tonight. Tonight you'll help me replant the leeks, because the leeks deserve normalcy and so do we." She stood, knees complaining, and looked around the ruined cellar one last time — the empty shelves, the ash, the jars of plum preserves standing witness like little glass headstones. "Soon. Before the snowmelt, if the world gives us that long. And Theo —"

She stopped. There were a hundred things queued up behind her teeth. *I'm proud of you. I lied to protect you and it was the wrong size of lie. Your grandfather loved you before he ever met you. When the thorn speaks to you, and it will, remember that a voice offering you everything has already taken inventory of what you'll pay.*

What she said was:

"Wear the wool socks east. The marsh rot gets in through canvas, and there's no healer out there worth the name."

Theo huffed — almost a laugh, almost a sob, the place where those two things meet in the young — and hugged her, hard, sudden, smelling of woodsmoke and the cedar oil they rubbed into the practice blade. Ilsa held on and let her eyes sting and blamed the smoke, out loud, and Theo politely pretended to believe her, which was its own kind of love.

* * *

They planted leeks by lantern light, and it was nearly peaceful, and Ilsa let herself have the nearly.

It was full dark when the dog started up at the far end of the lane — not the panicked bark of a fox, but the low, sustained objection of an animal that has decided a stranger is standing where strangers should not stand.

Ilsa straightened, one hand braced on the leek hoe, and looked east down the valley road, where the lanterns of Thornvale glimmered like a handful of coins spilled on black cloth.

A rider was coming up the road. Slowly. In no hurry at all, which was worse than hurry. And behind him, faint but unmistakable on the night wind, came a smell Ilsa had hoped never to smell again in her lifetime: horses. Many horses. Farther back down the road, in the dark, waiting.

"Well," she said, to Theo, to the leeks, to the thorn sleeping somewhere far to the east beneath the water. "So much for the snowmelt."
