## 7. Ostrev's Ledger

Captain Ren Ostrev had three rules for survey work, and he wrote them at the top of every ledger he kept, in his own cramped hand, so that any clerk who inherited his papers would understand the trade: *Measure twice. Trust the map, not the memory. And never fall in love with a valley.*

He was breaking the third rule already, and it was only his ninth day in Thornvale.

The trouble was that Thornvale was *lovely*, in the way of things that don't know they're being looked at. The valley sat in its mountains like an old cat in a sunbeam—unbothered, self-contained, faintly smug. The river ran clear over pale stones. The terraced orchards climbed the slopes in neat green steps. The village itself was a huddle of slate roofs and woodsmoke, and the people—

Well. The people were the problem.

Ostrev sat at the window of the only room for rent at the Drowned Goose, which was not drowned and had never housed a goose, and made another small mark in his ledger. He had been making such marks for nine days. The innkeeper's wife thought he was tallying trade goods. Let her. A man with a ledger was boring, and boring men were invisible, and invisible men heard everything.

What he heard, these days, was silence.

Not the good kind. Not the comfortable quiet of a place with nothing to hide. This was a *practiced* silence, the silence of a room where someone has just stopped talking when you walked in. He'd heard it before. He'd heard it in Harrow Fen, eleven years ago, in the week before the uprising.

He turned back a page in the ledger and read his own notes aloud, softly, because saying things helped him think, and because no one listened to a surveyor muttering about drainage.

"Day two. No shrine to the Gifted Martyrs. Unusual—every settlement in the realm keeps one, even the ones that hate them. Asked after it; was told the valley 'keeps its own devotions.' Day four. No charms against the gifted hung over doorways. Every other village on my route hangs them like laundry. Here, nothing. Either these people have never feared a speaker in their lives—" he tapped the pen against his teeth "—or they've never needed to."

There was a third possibility, of course. He wrote it down anyway, because writing it down made it real, and real things could be managed.

*Or they are hiding one.*

* *

The trouble with suspicion, Ostrev had learned, was that it was like a splinter working toward the heart. You could ignore it for a while. You could go about your business. But you always knew which direction it was traveling.

He tested the valley gently, the way you test ice. He asked the miller about grain tariffs and watched whether the man's eyes flicked anywhere unusual. They didn't; the miller's eyes were honest and slightly stupid, and Ostrev liked him immediately. He asked the schoolmistress whether any child here showed "irregular aptitudes"—a phrase that meant nothing and made everyone flinch—and she said, "Aptitudes? Tam can count to forty if you stand behind him and threaten him with arithmetic," and laughed, and he laughed too, and believed her completely, which annoyed him.

He walked the valley's borders. That was where the strangeness lived.

At the northern ridge, where the pass came down from the high country, there was a line—he couldn't have drawn it on a map if you paid him, but he could feel it with his boots, the way you feel a threshold in an old house. On one side of it, the world hummed along as it did everywhere: prayer-charms warming faintly in his pocket, the little blessing-sigil stamped on his commission papers giving off its dull official glow. On the other side, nothing. Cold paper. Dead charms. A silence in the bones.

Magic didn't work in Thornvale. Not crown magic, not hedge magic, not anything.

And yet the people here hung no charms, kept no martyrs' shrine, spoke of the gifted with the mild indifference of men discussing weather in a country that has never known storms. As though the whole question were foreign to them. As though they had agreed, all together, without ever discussing it, to be uninterested.

That took discipline. Ostrev knew something about discipline. He knew what it cost, and he knew what it was for.

He went back to the Goose and ordered stew he didn't want, because refusing food would make the innkeeper's wife anxious, and anxious people talk to their neighbors, and their neighbors would include whoever it was he wasn't supposed to be looking for.

"You'll want more bread than that," said Marta, the innkeeper's wife, depositing a second loaf without being asked. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a doorframe, and she had decided sometime around day three that the surveyor was too thin and possibly simple. "You army men, always forgetting to eat."

"I'm not army," Ostrev said. "Crown Surveyor's Office."

"Mm." Marta said *mm* the way other people said *liar*, fondly. "My late husband was a surveyor. For the wool merchants. He measured everything in this valley twice and got it wrong both times, God rest him. You want onions in that?"

"Yes please."

She brought onions, and pickles, and a plate of little honey cakes that appeared from nowhere, and Ostrev ate all of it and hated himself a little, because the food was genuinely wonderful and the woman was genuinely kind and one of them was almost certainly lying to him by omission, and it was getting harder to remember that kindness and honesty were different animals.

In Harrow Fen, the baker had been kind too. Right up until the night the sky caught fire.

* *

He wrote the letter on the tenth day.

It took him most of an evening, because Ostrev was careful with words the way some men are careful with money—not stingy, exactly, but aware that every one spent was gone forever. He sat by the candle with the ledger open beside him and drafted, crossed out, drafted again.

*To Colonel Vess, Third Survey Command, from Capt. R. Ostrev.*

*Colonel—I write regarding the valley of Thornvale, ostensibly to report on the eastern trade route as commissioned. I will do so briefly: the route is viable, the roads adequate, the bridges sound. There. Duty discharged.*

*Now the rest of it.*

He stopped. Looked at the candle. In the next room, through the wall, he could hear the innkeeper's family at supper—the clink of bowls, a child laughing, a man's voice doing what was clearly an impression of somebody and failing, to general delight. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of people who believed tomorrow was a safe assumption.

He thought about his brother.

Everyone who wrote to Colonel Vess eventually thought about their reason for being in the service, and Ostrev's reason was buried under a cairn outside Harrow Fen with a wooden marker that said *TAMM OSTREV, BELOVED BROTHER, AGE NINETEEN*, because Tamm had been nineteen and beloved and a brother, and because the crown's stonecutters had been busy that season and the family had been poor.

Tamm had died on the third night of the uprising, in a street that Ostrev had walked down that very morning buying apples. A speaker—a girl, sixteen years old, half-starved, terrified—had torn the street open. Not out of malice. Out of panic, probably. Out of being cornered by soldiers who had orders and no imagination. The inquiry had called it an escalation. Their mother had called it murder, and had not spoken to Ren in four years, because Ren had been there, in uniform, on the wrong side of a street that used to sell apples.

Ren had spent eleven years deciding what he believed about that night, and the answer he'd arrived at was this: magic was a fire, and fires were not evil, but nobody wept for the house that burned down next to the fireplace. Control was not cruelty. Control was the only mercy left once power entered the world. Someone had to be the bucket brigade. It might as well be a man who checked his work twice.

So: the letter.

*Colonel—there is a pattern of suppression in Thornvale that I can document but not explain. No martyrs' shrine. No warding charms. A collective, disciplined incuriosity regarding the gifted that I have encountered nowhere else in nine years of survey work. The valley sits within a natural null-zone—magic does not function here—which is documented nowhere in the Office archives, which is itself remarkable. Such places are known historically to attract those who wish not to be found.*

*I request a company of regulars, held at readiness at Fort Callow, with discretion. I am not requesting a raid. If there is a speaker here, they have hidden successfully for years, perhaps generations, and a heavy hand will only teach the valley to lie better. I want enough presence to make flight look wiser than fight. In my experience, given those odds, most speakers run. Running men can be followed. Cornered men burn streets.*

*One further note, and then I will trouble you no longer. Whatever we find or do not find here, I ask that the villagers be treated as subjects, not suspects. They may be harboring a speaker knowingly. They may be harboring one unknowingly. Or they may simply be mountain folk who mind their own affairs, which is not yet a crime in this realm, whatever certain parties in the capital might wish. I have eaten their bread. I would prefer not to burn their ovens.*

He read it through twice. Then he took out the last paragraph, because Vess would strike it anyway and call it sentiment, and Ostrev had learned to keep his sentiment in ledgers instead of letters.

He sealed it, addressed it, and set it on the sill for the morning courier. Then he blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the house settle, and told himself, the way he told himself every night, that he was a careful man doing careful work, and that careful work saved lives.

Most nights, he believed it.

* *

On the eleventh day, he nearly had his proof, and it came from the least likely quarter: a wooden sword.

He'd taken to walking the orchard terraces in the late afternoon, ostensibly checking boundary stones. On this particular afternoon he came around a bend of dry-stone wall and found a young man practicing sword forms alone among the apple trees—with a stick. A carved wooden practice blade, worn smooth as driftwood, moving through drills that were, Ostrev noted with professional interest, *very* old-fashioned. Court forms. Dueling forms. The kind of thing taught by masters who charged a year's wages, in capitals far from here.

And the young man moved like water finding its level. No wasted motion. No thought. Just fluency.

Ostrev stood in the shadow of the wall and watched, and felt the splinter working deeper.

Because here was the thing: nobody in a farming valley learned court swordsmanship. Nobody learned it from books, either—you couldn't get the footwork right from a book, everyone who tried looked like a heron fighting a mop. Which meant someone local had taught him. Which meant someone local had *learned it somewhere*, long ago, well enough to pass on.

Which meant the valley had secrets older than the valley let on.

The young man finished the form, grounded the point, and bowed—to the empty air, to the trees, to some teacher who wasn't there—and Ostrev stepped out from the wall before he could decide not to.

"That third sequence," he said. "The turn at the end. Where'd you learn that?"

The young man spun, and for half a heartbeat his face did something complicated—fear, yes, but under the fear, something else, something that looked almost like recognition, like a man hearing his own name in a stranger's mouth. Then it smoothed over into the pleasant blankness of a farm boy surprised by a stranger.

"Oh—nowhere," he said. "Just messing about. My grandmother's brother was a soldier, ages ago. Family stories."

"Family stories with footwork like that." Ostrev smiled and made it easy, made it harmless, made it two men talking about swords on a hillside. "I'm Ostrev. Crown Surveyor. You must be Theo—Ilsa Villalobos's grandson. She speaks of you."

"Does she?" Something flickered. "Only good things, I hope."

"She says you're steady." He let that sit a moment. Steady. The word a handler uses. "Good traits in an orchardist."

"And in a surveyor?"

"In anyone." Ostrev looked past him, down the terraces, at the valley lying gold in the evening light, at the smoke rising straight from chimneys, at the whole soft, guarded, beautiful lie of the place. "It's a fine valley, Theo Villalobos. I hope nothing ever happens to it."

Something crossed the young man's face then—quick as a fish under ice—and Ostrev, watching carefully, saw it and could not name it, and it would keep him awake half the night, turning it over: not guilt, not fear exactly. More like grief. Like a man standing at his own future funeral, politely declining to mention it.

"Well," said Theo. "Evenings get cold fast up here. You'll want to head down before dark."

"Probably." Ostrev touched his hat brim and turned, and made himself walk away at surveyor's pace, unhurried, a boring man going to be boring elsewhere. At the bend in the path he glanced back once. The orchard was empty. The boy had simply—gone, silent as smoke, and hadn't even rustled a branch.

*They're trained,* Ostrev thought, standing in the falling dusk with his breath beginning to fog. *All of them. The whole valley. Trained to be unremarkable, and trained well, and for a long time.*

Back at the Goose, he took out the sealed letter to Colonel Vess, weighed it in his hand for a long moment, and then added a single line to the address panel in pencil, in code only Vess would know: *Confirm presence suspected. One candidate identified. Request expedited response.*

He set it on the sill for the morning courier, and this time he did not take anything back.

* *

Three days later, the reply came, and it was not the reply he wanted.

Vess's letter was brisk and pleased with itself, the way Vess's letters always were. *Reinforcements approved. Company departs Fort Callow within the fortnight.* And beneath that, in a different ink, added later by a heavier hand: *Further instructions enclosed under separate seal per directive of the Lord Regent's office. Surveyor is instructed to continue observation and to identify, locate, and hold any confirmed speaker until regulars arrive. Lethal force authorized if subject flees.*

Lethal force authorized.

Ostrev read the line four times. Outside the window, Thornvale went about its evening—Marta beating a rug off the balcony, the miller's dog chasing something that was probably holy and certainly imaginary, lamplight coming up golden in windows one by one, a valley putting itself to bed with the confidence of people who did not know what was walking toward them with his signature on the requisition.

He thought about Tamm again, the way he always did when the word *lethal* crossed his desk. Nineteen years old. Beloved brother. Apples in the morning, ash by midnight.

"No," he said quietly, to the room, to the letter, to the version of himself that had written *I would prefer not to burn their ovens* and meant it. "No cornered men. Not this time."

He took out a fresh sheet and began drafting new orders for himself—observation only, containment by patience, a plan to find the boy's grandmother and ask her, plainly, the questions he'd been circling for two weeks. Somewhere in the middle of the second page, he heard hooves on the road below, many of them, and frowned, because the courier was due tomorrow, not tonight, and couriers did not travel in companies.

He went to the window.

Down the valley road, torches were coming through the dusk like a string of orange beads—riders, a dozen at least, moving fast, with the particular forward-lean of soldiers who had not been sent for and knew it. Not his reinforcements. His were a fortnight out. These were early, and unauthorized, and flying no colors he could see.

Behind them, far back down the pass, a smudge of smoke rose against the last light.

Ostrev stood very still at the window, the unfinished orders in his hand, and understood with perfect clarity that the careful plan he had built—patience, discretion, no cornered men—had just been overtaken by men who had never heard of it.

The first screams started near the mill.
