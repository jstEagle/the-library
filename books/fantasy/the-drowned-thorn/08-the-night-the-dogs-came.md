## 8. The Night the Dogs Came

The dogs came first.

Theo heard them before anything else—a sound like the valley itself clearing its throat, low and wrong, because Thornvale had no dogs worth barking. Old Marta kept two for herding, and they were cowards; the miller's hound was deaf as a post. These were none of those. These were war dogs, deep-chested and disciplined, and their voices carried up through the fog in a way that made Theo's spine prickle before their mind caught up to what the ears already knew.

They were sitting on the roof of the woodshed, which was where Theo did most of their thinking these days—flat shingles still holding the day's warmth, a good view of the lane, and no one to ask questions about why a grown person sat on roofs. The wooden sword lay across their knees. They'd been running forms in their head again, the way you might hum a song without singing it, fingers tracing the cuts against the grain of the shingles.

The dogs stopped barking all at once.

That was worse. Dogs that stop are dogs that have been told to.

Theo slid down the back of the shed, dropped into the nettles—they'd long since stopped minding the stings; Thornvale taught you that much—and ran for the house with the particular silent urgency of someone who has spent nineteen years practicing at being nobody.

Grandmother Ilsa was already awake. Of course she was. Theo sometimes suspected the woman slept with one ear tuned to the future out of sheer spite. She stood at the hearth in her nightdress and boots, which was an outfit that meant business, feeding papers into the fire with the unhurried calm of someone baking bread.

"Theo." She didn't turn around. "Back door. The ridge path. Don't pack. Packing is how people die."

"What's happening?"

"Soldiers." A page curled and went orange. "Crown soldiers, by the sound of them. Organized. Which means someone gave them a reason to be organized."

Theo's mouth went dry. "Ostrev."

"Or his cousin, or his twin, or some other man with a ledger and a suspicion. It hardly matters now." She turned then, and her face in the firelight was the face Theo knew best in all the world—the one that had bandaged scraped knees and lied about there being no such thing as magic and burned letters at midnight—but older tonight. Sharper. "Listen to me. You go east. Over the ridge, down past the standing stone, keep the water on your left until it forgets which way it flows. Then east. To Vaelmora."

"You said never—" Theo started.

"I said a great many things," Ilsa said, "and most of them were to keep you alive long enough to ignore me properly. Go."

The front of the house lit up—not fire yet, but lantern-light, dozens of lanterns moving up the lane in formation, and beneath the dog-voices now came the creak of leather and the ring of metal and the flat cadence of men being given orders by someone who expected them followed.

Ilsa pressed something into Theo's hands. The single page she'd saved from the burning—the one she kept folded inside her bodice like a second heart. "Don't read it tonight. Read it when you're somewhere I can't take it back from you."

"Grandmother—"

"No." Her voice cracked like a whip, and then, terribly, softened. "No, love. There isn't time for the speech. The speech is: you were the best thing I ever lied for. Now run."

* * *

Theo ran.

Not out the front, where the lanterns were, and not straight out the back either—Ilsa's eyes flicked upward once, meaningfully, and Theo understood: the roof. Up the rain barrel, across the lean-to, along the ridgeline of the house where the shingles were slick with fog and every footfall had to be a promise kept. From up here Theo could see the whole lane filled with soldiers, maybe forty of them, torches and pikes and the dogs straining at leads, and at the head of them a figure Theo recognized even from above, even in the dark.

Captain Ren Ostrev. Not in his surveyor's plainness tonight. In crown gray, with a sword, looking like what he'd been all along.

He stopped at Ilsa's gate.

Theo flattened against the chimney, heart slamming, and listened.

"Mistress Villalobos," Ostrev said, and his voice carried the way voices do at night, too clearly, every word landing like a stone in still water. "We're looking for your grandchild."

"At this hour?" Ilsa's voice came out dry as autumn leaves. Theo could picture her exactly: arms crossed, hip cocked, radiating the specific disappointment of a woman who has raised children through two bad winters and does not frighten. "Captain, do you know what time the rooster thinks it is? Because he has opinions."

"Where is the child?"

"Gone to market. Gone courting. Gone fishing, knowing them—Theo can sit by dead water for six hours and call it industry." A pause, and then Ilsa's voice changed, dropped into something quieter and more dangerous. "But if you're asking whether they're in my house, Captain, then you're not asking about my grandchild at all. You're asking about the gift. And I'll tell you what I told your surveyor, who asked prettier questions: whatever you think runs in our blood died in this valley a long time ago. Magic can't live here. Everyone knows it. That's rather the point of the place."

There was a silence long enough for Theo to count their own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve.

"I have orders, mistress."

"Then show them to me in daylight, like a Christian. Or kick in an old woman's door in the dark and explain to your men why the great crown hunt of the realm begins with bread theft from a widow's pantry."

Another silence. Then, quietly, so only the fog and the chimney and Theo heard it: "Search the house."

Theo moved.

Across the roof, down the far side into the goose pen—the geese, bless their vicious little hearts, erupted in outrage, honking like the world ending, and Theo silently vowed to forgive them every egg they'd ever broken. The noise covered the sound of feet hitting mud. Behind the house, up the slope, through the bracken, toward the ridge path, and—

And then the first torch went into the thatch.

It happened fast after that. Fire catches a village like rumor catches a town; one roof, then three, then the barn, then the light of it painting the underside of the fog red so the whole sky seemed bruised. Theo made it perhaps two hundred yards up the ridge before turning around—because you always turn around, that's the cruelty of it, the body insists on witnessing—and saw the lane full of people being pulled from their homes, and saw Ilsa standing in her doorway with her arms still crossed while soldiers streamed past her into the flames she wasn't fighting.

Saw Ostrev ride up to her. Saw him lean down from the saddle. Saw her say something—couldn't hear it, would spend years inventing versions of it—and saw him straighten and shout an order, and the soldiers stopped dragging people out of houses.

Saw Ilsa raise her voice at last, loud enough to carry even up the ridge, pitched deliberately, unmistakably, for anyone within earshot who might be listening from the dark:

"It was me! Do you hear? Whatever gift you're hunting—it's mine! I'm the last of them, and I confess it, and I'll sign whatever paper you like, so leave these people be!"

A lie. A beautiful, enormous, grandmother-shaped lie, thrown out like a cloak over a fleeing child.

Theo stood in the bracken with fog soaking through their clothes and firelight on their face and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade between the ribs, exactly what was being purchased, and with whose coin.

They ran anyway.

That was the worst part, later. That they ran. That the legs did what legs do, that survival turned out to be not a choice but a reflex, something in the marrow older than love or loyalty. Theo ran over the ridge with the village burning behind them and the saved page crumpled in their fist and did not stop running until the fire was a smudge and the smudge was a memory and the memory was a weight they would carry in the chest for the rest of their life, right beside the heartbeat, taking up room.

* * *

Dawn found Theo at the standing stone, breath ragged, hands shaking, the wooden sword still somehow gripped in one fist though they had no memory of carrying it.

They sat down against the cold granite and laughed—one short, awful bark of a laugh with nothing in it—and then put their head in their hands.

Below, the fog was lifting off the valley, and for one merciful moment Thornvale looked ordinary. Green fields. Smoke rising from chimneys. Then Theo's eyes sorted the smoke from the mist and counted the columns, and the ordinary morning fell apart. Too many columns. Rising from the wrong places. The smithy. The chapel. The Rowan house, where little Pip Rowan had once paid Theo a button to teach him sword forms with a stick, and had been terrible at it, and had grinned the entire time.

"They held them off," Theo said aloud, to no one, to the stone. "She held them off. Nobody—she confessed, they'd have taken her, they wouldn't have—" But the voice ran out of arguments, because the smoke kept rising, patient and indifferent, and smoke has never once cared what a person needs to believe.

The wooden sword lay across their knees. Theo looked down at it—at the worn grip shaped by four generations of hands, at the wordless thing it had been their whole life—and for the first time felt it not as comfort but as evidence. This was why. This stick, this blood, this inheritance nobody had asked for. Ilsa burning papers at midnight. Ilsa lying for nineteen years. Ilsa standing in a doorway trading her life for a village's, and all of it, all of it, because of what slept in Theo's veins.

"Is this what you wanted?" Theo asked the sword, and hated the crack in their own voice. "Is this the price? Is it always the price?"

The sword, being wood, said nothing. But somewhere under the breastbone, in the place where the gift lay dormant as a seed in frozen ground, something stirred. Not words. Not yet. Just a warmth, faint and terrible, like a banked coal noticing the wind.

Theo wrapped the sword in their coat, shouldered it, and stood.

East. Keep the water on the left until it forgets which way it flows. Grandmother's voice in the head, steady even now, especially now. *You were the best thing I ever lied for.*

"Don't you dare die," Theo told the valley, the smoke, the woman in the doorway. "Do you hear me? You don't get to make that trade and then lose. You hold them off. You argue with them. You're insufferable. Be insufferable at them until spring and I'll come back and we'll rebuild the stupid goose pen and you can pretend you never said any of it."

The valley offered no reply. The smoke rose. Somewhere below, faint on the wind, a goose was still complaining, and Theo clung to that absurd small sound like a rope over a cliff—because if the geese were still alive and still furious, then some of Thornvale was too. Some of it. Enough of it. It had to be enough.

They turned east.

The land beyond the ridge was country Theo had never walked: the magic-dead quiet of Thornvale thinned with every step, and Theo felt the change coming the way you feel weather in old scars—a pressure behind the eyes, a tingling in the palms, the sense of approaching a door long kept locked. At the border of the valley's silence, they stopped, one foot in the quiet world and one in the other, and looked back one last time.

Thornvale smoked in the dawn. Small, from here. Almost gentle.

"I'll come back," Theo said. Not a promise to the valley this time. A vow to themselves, spoken aloud, unguarded, in a voice roughened by smoke and grief—and the moment the words left their mouth, the world beyond the ridge answered.

Every word Theo had ever learned rose up around them like floodwater. The names of things sang in their blood; the standing stone hummed with centuries; the very grass whispered its green name. The gift, waking, hit them like a wave, and Theo staggered, gasped, caught themselves against the stone—

—and began, alone, to walk east toward Vaelmora, carrying a burning village and a folded letter and a weapon that was also a wound, while behind them the dogs finally, finally went quiet.
