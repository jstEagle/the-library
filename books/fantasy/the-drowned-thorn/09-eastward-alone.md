## 9. Eastward, Alone

Theo had always believed the valley ended at the river.

That was what Ilsa said when Theo was small enough to believe anything: *the water is the edge of the world, little one. Everything past it is just a story people tell to make the water seem less lonely.* It was a pretty lie, and like all of Ilsa's prettiest lies, it had been built to keep a child alive.

The river was not the edge of the world. The river was the seam.

Theo stood on the western bank at dawn, three days after Thornvale burned, and looked at the water as if seeing it for the first time — which, in a way, was true. It was a narrow, indifferent stream this time of year, barely deep enough to wet the knees, chuckling over its stones like an old man who'd never once been asked to keep a secret. On the far bank, the land climbed into grey-green hills, and beyond the hills, eastward, everything else: the roads, the towns, the crown, the hunt.

Behind them, the smoke had finally stopped rising. Theo had watched it for two days from the high pasture, telling themselves they were counting the houses still standing and not counting the ones that weren't. The number kept changing depending on how honest they were willing to be.

*Run east,* Ilsa had said, pressing the folded page into their hand with fingers that smelled of ash and tallow. *To Vaelmora. Don't stop. Don't look back. And Theo — whatever you hear in your head once you cross, don't answer it.*

At the time, crouched in the bracken while soldiers' torches swung through the village below, Theo had filed that last instruction under *grandmother being cryptic again,* a category capacious enough to hold most of their childhood. Now, standing at the seam of the world with the page against their sternum like a second heartbeat, they understood it for what it had been: a warning label.

They waded in.

The water was colder than it had any right to be in late summer, cold in the way of things that have been waiting a long time. Halfway across, Theo's boots found the deeper channel and the current shoved at them, impatient, and they thought — absurdly, hysterically — *even the river wants me gone.* Then the far bank rose up to meet them, gravel and sedge and one crooked alder tree, and Theo Villalobos stepped out of Thornvale for the first time in nineteen years.

The world broke.

There was no gentler word for it. One moment Theo was wet, cold, and grieving; the next they were on their knees in the gravel with their hands over their ears, though no sound was making any of it better, because the sound wasn't coming through their ears at all.

It was coming through everything.

Every word in sight had weight. That was the only way Theo could ever describe it afterward, and even that description failed, the way describing drowning fails to convey the specific insult of water going down the wrong pipe. But here is the attempt: imagine that every written thing, every carved thing, every *spoken-into-the-world-and-still-humming* thing, suddenly became visible. Not readable — Theo couldn't parse most of it, not yet — but *present*, the way a bell is present after it stops ringing, the way a bruise is present under the skin.

The alder tree above them had words in it. Old ones, slow ones, grown into the grain over decades the way rings grow — someone had spoken to this tree, or over it, or perhaps simply died beneath it loudly enough that the tree remembered. The gravel held the ghost of a surveyor's marker, its letters long weathered away but the *shape* of having meant something still pressed into the stones like a footprint in wet clay. Even the air had residue, faint threads of old blessings and older curses drifting through it like pollen, and Theo's skin prickled where they touched.

And underneath all of it, distant but unmistakable, like a drum heard through a hillside:

A whisper. Rhythmic. Patient. Saying something that might have been a name.

*Don't answer it,* Ilsa had said.

"I'm not answering," Theo said out loud, which was, they realized a moment too late, technically a form of answering. The whisper paused. Considered. Resumed, slightly louder, the way a cat will repeat itself when it suspects you weren't listening.

"Oh, no," Theo told the hills, the river, the general universe. "No. We are not doing this. You don't get a voice. I don't even know what you are."

The whisper did not reply, which was somehow worse than replying, because silence from something that large felt deliberate.

Theo got up. Their legs shook. They got up anyway, because the alternative was kneeling in the gravel forever, and Ilsa hadn't raised them to kneel in gravel. They shouldered their pack — Ilsa's good pack, the one with the oiled canvas, packed in four minutes flat with everything that mattered: bread, a knife, a flint, a change of clothes, and one page of burnt-edged paper wrapped in oilcloth against their chest — and walked east.

* * *

The first mile was the worst, and then every mile was the worst, and eventually Theo made peace with the fact that "worst" was now simply the ambient condition of existence.

The gift didn't come back all at once. It came back the way feeling comes back to a limb that's been asleep — painfully, in patches, with the sense that something is deeply wrong with the plumbing. For nineteen years Theo's blood had sat quiet in the valley's hush, a banked fire under wet ash. Now the ash was blowing off and the fire remembered itself all at once, and every word Theo passed became a small event.

A milestone stone, half-sunk in the heather: the crown's mark and a distance figure, and Theo read it without meaning to, the letters leaping off the stone and into their head with a physical *thunk*, like a coin dropped into a dry well. They staggered. The stone was fifty paces off the road. Fifty paces, and it had hit them anyway.

A shepherd's hut, abandoned, its doorpost carved with a child's attempt at writing their own name — *MARA, OR MAYBE MARA, THE SECOND LETTER WENT WRONG* — and Theo stood in front of that doorpost for a full minute, eyes stinging, undone by a dead child's spelling. The name still hummed. Faintly, sweetly, the way a toy left in a corner keeps a little charge of the person who loved it. Whoever Mara had been, she'd been proud of that letter, wrong as it was. Theo could feel the pride. That was the part that undid them — not the words themselves but what clung to them. Words were never just words. They were *hands*, reaching out of the past, and now Theo could feel every one of them touching their face.

"Stop it," they muttered, and then winced, because their own muttered words came back at them doubled, echoing strangely, as if the air were repeating them in a language just adjacent to speech. Their voice had weight now too. Every syllable they spent seemed to cost something they couldn't see leaving.

By midday they had learned — clumsily, by trial and error that left their nose bleeding twice — to do what Ilsa must have done her whole life without ever mentioning it: to look *through* the words instead of at them. To let the world's writing blur the way you blur your eyes at a magic lantern show, taking in the shapes without reading the letters. It helped. Mostly. The big things still got through. A roadside shrine with a blessing carved over it struck them like a wave and knocked them into the ditch, and they lay in the nettles for a while afterward, laughing in a slightly unhinged manner, because the blessing had been so *kind*. Someone had loved travelers enough to carve *safe road, warm hearth, return whole* into stone, and centuries later it still worked, still reached out and tried to tuck a stranger in, and Theo had been tucked so hard they fell in a ditch.

"Ilsa," they said to the sky, "you absolute tyrant. You knew. You knew it was like this, and you let me think I was *nothing*."

But even as they said it, climbing back onto the road, brushing nettles from their sleeves, they understood why. In the valley, the nothing had been a mercy. Out here, the everything was a wound. There had been no version of growing up knowing this that didn't end with a child either terrified of their own skin or drunk on it. Ilsa had chosen terrified-of-everything-except-the-valley, and had nearly pulled it off, and would burn for it, and had burned for it.

Theo walked. Grief walked with them, matching stride, saying nothing, which was unlike grief, which usually had opinions.

* * *

Toward evening they reached a crossroads with a gibbet on it, long empty, its timbers silvered with age — and the gibbet was *screaming*.

Not aloud. Theo checked; the road was empty, the crows unbothered. But the post itself radiated horror the way a stove radiates heat, layer upon layer of fear soaked into the wood by everyone who had ever stood beneath it, and the last layer was freshest, sharpest, days old rather than years: a terror with a shape to it, a specific person, a specific morning, a rope.

Theo stood very still.

Someone had died here recently. Or almost died. The fear said *almost* — it had the unfinished quality of a thing interrupted — but the fear also said *crown*, because threaded through it, cool and orderly as a clerk's handwriting, ran the residue of official words. A proclamation, read aloud. Theo couldn't parse the whole of it, but fragments surfaced like drowned bodies: *...by order... the gifted... surrender or be taken...*

The hunt. Not a rumor anymore. Not a peddler's tale. Here it was, written in fear on a crossroads post, close enough to touch.

Theo touched it anyway. They couldn't help it; their new senses reached toward pain the way a tongue reaches toward a broken tooth. And in the outermost, freshest layer of that fear, they found a detail that turned their blood to well-water: the frightened one had been *young*. Small. The fear was sized like a child's fear.

They stood at the crossroads until the light went amber, doing arithmetic they didn't want to do. The proclamation was fresh. The soldiers who read it had gone — which direction? Theo cast about, reading the road itself, and caught it: hoofprints in the dried mud, many, heading south and west. Back toward the interior. Away from the marshes.

Away from Vaelmora.

East, then. East was still open. East was still possible. Theo said it out loud to make it true — "east" — and flinched at the weight of even that small word, one syllable and it rang in the air like a dropped pan.

Then they noticed the paper.

It was nailed to the gibbet's post at a child's eye height, rain-spotted but legible, and it was not a proclamation. It was a notice, hand-lettered, offering reward for information regarding "persons of unsanctioned talent," and beneath the official text someone had added, in different ink, smaller: *Pilgrims on the east road are questioned at Ferrow Bridge. Travelers without papers are detained.*

Theo read it three times. The words hummed against their mind, ordinary ink, ordinary fear, and beneath the fear — this was the strange part, the part that made them look over both shoulders — beneath the fear was a current of *care*. Whoever had added the warning had wanted travelers to see it. Had risked something to nail it here, low, where a fleeing person's eye would find it.

A friend, then. An unknown friend at an empty crossroads, and Theo could have kissed the post if the post weren't busy screaming.

"Ferrow Bridge," they murmured, memorizing it, and then, because the whisper under the hillside had been patient all day and patience was starting to feel like strategy: "And don't think I've forgotten you."

The whisper, beneath everything, pulsed once — pleased, or amused, or hungry; with something that large it was hard to tell the difference — and Theo pulled their pack tight, turned east, and walked into the dark with their grandmother's page against their heart and a stranger's warning in their head and a fire in their blood that would not, could not, ever go quietly out again.

* * *

Two days east, stiff-legged and hollow-eyed, Theo limped into the shadow of Ferrow Bridge at dusk and immediately regretted every life choice that had led there, because the bridge was crawling with soldiers.

They were checking carts. That was the first thing: carts, wagons, a line of them backed up along the near bank, and beside each one a bored-looking man in crown blue poking at sacks of onions with the enthusiasm of a man who had poked ten thousand sacks of onions and been promised glory and received onions. Papers were being examined. Voices carried across the water in the flat evening air.

Theo melted behind a drystone wall and did some rapid, desperate thinking, which was interrupted by the wall itself, which turned out to have words in it — a mason's mark, generations old, humming a low contented note of *I built this and it stands* — and Theo had to press a hand over their mouth to keep from groaning aloud. Even the hiding places were talking.

They needed a group. That much was obvious. Soldiers questioned lone travelers; soldiers waved through pilgrim bands with a grunt and a glance, because pilgrims traveled with papers and priests and the particular odor of sanctity that made even lieutenants reluctant. Theo had no papers, no priest, and an odor that was probably more *fugitive* than *sanctity*, but a group was a group, and groups could be joined. Ilsa had always said Theo's one reliable talent was looking harmless, which at the time had felt insulting and now felt like a superpower.

So when the sound of singing came up the east road — six or seven voices, rough and cheerful, accompanied by a cart with a squeaking axle — Theo stood up from behind the wall, arranged their face into the expression of a weary traveler hoping for company, and stepped out to meet them.

The song stopped. Seven faces turned. At the front of the cart, a woman with wind-chapped cheeks and ink-stained fingers lowered a rolled map she'd been studying and looked Theo up and down with the frank, measuring stare of someone who assessed terrain for a living and had just found an interesting feature on it.

"You're not from around here," she said. It wasn't a question. Her eyes flicked — briefly, professionally — to Theo's mud-caked boots, their valley-cut hair growing out unevenly, the absence of any pilgrim badge.

"No," Theo admitted. "I'm headed east."

"Aren't we all." She jerked her chin toward the bridge, the soldiers, the queue. "There's a checkpoint. There's always a checkpoint. You'll want to be walking with us when we go through, unless you enjoy explaining yourself to men whose entire personality is a helmet." She paused, and something sharp and amused moved behind her eyes. "I'm Sefa. I make maps. And you, friend, are going to tell me exactly one true thing about yourself before I decide whether you're walking with us or watching us go."

Behind her, the soldiers at the bridge laughed at something. The sun touched the hills. Somewhere far to the west, under a hillside, something patient whispered Theo's name, and somewhere far to the east, drowned and thorn-wrapped, something else waited with even more patience still.

Theo opened their mouth, chose carefully among all the words now crowding their tongue, each one heavy as a stone, and gave Sefa the one true thing they could afford.
