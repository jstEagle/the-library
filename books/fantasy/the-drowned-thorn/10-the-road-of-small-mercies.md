## 10. The Road of Small Mercies

The border crossed Theo like a fever breaking.

For three days after the ridge, they had walked east through country that meant nothing to them—pine forest giving way to wet meadow, wet meadow to a road wide enough for two carts, the road running beside a river the color of old pewter. They had spoken to no one. They had slept in ditches with their boots on and the wooden sword under their arm like a child's doll they were too old for and could not put down. And all the while, the gift had been pouring back into them the way water pours into a dry well—slowly at first, then all at once, until every word in the world had weight again.

That was the worst part. Not the pain, though there was pain—a low ringing behind the eyes, a feeling of having swallowed a struck bell. The worst part was that words had *edges* now. A farmer's curse spat at an ox hit Theo between the shoulder blades like a thrown stone. A mother's lullaby drifted across a field and settled over them warm as a blanket, and Theo had stood in the ditch for a full minute before they could make their legs move again. Blessings were worst of all. A roadside shrine, a traveler's murmured grace, a grandmother kissing her grandson's forehead with a word of protection—and each one landed on Theo's skin like rain on a burn.

So when Theo crested a hill on the fourth morning and saw, below, a knot of travelers strung out along the road in the unmistakable formation of pilgrims—carts, banners, someone singing—they did what any sensible fugitive would do.

They hid.

They crouched in the gorse at the top of the hill and told themselves they would wait for the pilgrims to pass, because pilgrims meant questions, questions meant conversation, conversation meant words, and words meant the bell-swallow feeling and the flinching and the very real possibility that some pious soul would lay a blessing on the thin stranger in the muddy coat and Theo's whole body would light up like a struck match in front of forty witnesses.

The plan was sound. The plan failed because of a goat.

It came up the hillside from the pilgrim camp, brown and disagreeable, dragging a rope, and it looked at Theo crouching in the gorse with the expression of a creature that has seen everything twice and been impressed by none of it. Then it ate Theo's sleeve.

"Stop," Theo whispered.

The goat did not stop. The goat ate the sleeve with the thoroughness of a scholar annotating a text, and then it began on the coat beneath, and Theo—exhausted, starving, and possessed of exactly one set of clothes—did the only reasonable thing. They grabbed the rope and hauled.

The goat hauled back. The goat won. Theo went down the hill in a series of undignified slides, through the gorse, past a startled sheep, and out onto the road directly in front of the lead cart, still holding the rope, still attached to the goat, wearing what remained of a sleeve.

Forty pilgrims turned to look at them.

"Well," said a voice from the cart. "Either you're robbing us or you're joining us, and I'll note the goat has already made its decision."

* * *

Her name was Sefa, and she was, by her own account, the finest cartographer in the Marchlands, a claim she delivered while sitting sideways on the cart's rail with her boots swinging, so that she could watch Theo's face while she talked. She was perhaps thirty, brown-skinned, with ink stains on three fingers and a scar through one eyebrow that she never explained and everyone eventually stopped asking about. She drew maps. She was traveling with the pilgrimage because the pilgrimage was going where she needed to go, which, she said, was the entire reason anyone traveled anywhere, and people who pretended otherwise were liars or poets, and usually both.

Theo said very little. This was partly caution and partly necessity: every time someone in the caravan spoke a blessing—and pilgrims spoke them constantly, over meals, over blisters, over the goat—the word landed on Theo like a hand pressed flat against a wound, and keeping one's face still through that took concentration that left little over for conversation.

Sefa noticed on the second day.

They had stopped at midday by the river, and old Mother Anselm, who had appointed herself the caravan's heart if not its brain, had gone down the line of travelers pressing her thumb to each forehead and murmuring the road-blessing, the one every child in the realm learned before they learned their letters. *May the road rise level, may the water run clean, may no word be laid upon you that you cannot carry.*

She reached Theo last. She pressed her thumb to their forehead, and the blessing came down like warm water, and Theo flinched—flinched hard, whole body, the way a horse flinches from a whip it remembers.

Mother Anselm clucked and moved on, assuming a headache. But Sefa, three carts back with her map spread across her knees, had gone very still, the way she went still when a coastline didn't match her charts.

That evening she sat down next to Theo at the fire without asking, which was, Theo would learn, how Sefa did most things.

"You flinch," she said.

"I have headaches."

"Mm." She produced two tin cups and poured tea into both from a pot that had seen better centuries. "You also flinched at the shrine outside Harrow Ford yesterday. And at the grace before supper. And when the ferryman blessed his boat, though I'll grant you, that blessing was so mangled even a stone might have objected." She handed Theo a cup. "Headaches don't work like that. Headaches don't care whether the words are kind."

Theo held the cup and said nothing, and the fire cracked, and somewhere down the line the goat made a sound like a door in a haunted house.

"Here's what I know about you," Sefa continued comfortably. "You joined us four days ago with no pack, no money, half a sleeve, and a wooden sword, which you sleep holding, which is either very sad or very sensible and I haven't decided. You eat like someone who's forgotten food exists but remembers suddenly and violently. You walk on the windward side of the group, always, so your smell goes away from us rather than toward—I notice things, it's my trade. And you flinch at blessings." She sipped her tea. "I'm not going to ask why. But I'm going to tell you something, and then you're going to feel less like a rabbit in a snare, and then maybe you'll talk, because honestly, four days of your silence is starting to feel personal."

Theo looked at her. "Why would you do that?"

"Because you hauled a goat down a hill for me. That animal is called Duchess, she is the property of the caravan, and if you'd lost her I'd have had to draw a map to the nearest gallows for whoever let it happen. So." She settled back against the cart wheel. "When I was nineteen, I worked a commission in the Salt Marshes, mapping channels for a peat concern. There was a woman there, a bog-witch, the real thing, old blood. Everyone knew. Nobody minded; she cured fevers and found lost cattle. Then the crown's survey came through, and the surveyor saw her pull a fever out of a child, and he wrote it down, because writing things down was his job, and six weeks later soldiers came." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb moved along the rim of her cup, around and around. "She got away. Most didn't. I've thought about that surveyor's ledger a great deal since. How much harm fits in neat handwriting."

The fire popped. Theo's throat was tight.

"So when a stranger flinches at blessings," Sefa said, "I don't write it down. I just make sure we camp downwind of the ones who might." She raised her cup slightly, a toast to nothing. "You don't have to tell me anything. But you should know the caravan turns south at Vaelmora Crossroads, and I'm told the marsh roads east are bad this season. If a person needed to disappear toward the coast, this would be the week to arrange it."

Theo stared into the tea. Something in their chest that had been clenched since the ridge loosened—not all the way, not enough to be safe, but enough to breathe around.

"The sword's not wooden," Theo heard themselves say, and immediately wished they hadn't, and then, strangely, didn't. It was the first true thing they'd said aloud in four days, and it came out small and rough. "I mean. It is. That's the point. Wood doesn't carry anything. It's quiet."

Sefa considered this with the gravity she gave to disputed borders. "A quiet sword," she said. "For a loud world."

"Something like that."

"Well." She drained her tea and stood, brushing off her skirts. "Keep it quiet and keep it close, and if anyone asks, it belonged to your grandfather and you're taking it to a shrine. Pilgrims love a relic. Half our company is hauling bones of dubious provenance and nobody's asked a single question." She paused, then added, almost as an afterthought, "There's a blessing Mother Anselm does at dawn for safe passage. I'll walk you out ahead of it. Consider it a professional courtesy."

* * *

The days found a shape after that.

Theo rose early, before the dawn blessing, and walked the first miles alone with Sefa, who talked enough for both of them and demanded nothing back. She taught Theo to read the road the way she read a chart: here a village that would sell eggs and ask no questions, there a ford where the toll-collector counted heads, so walk the shingle instead. She named hills and rivers and distances with the affection of someone introducing family, and slowly, without quite noticing it happening, Theo began to answer. A word here. A sentence there. Once, catastrophically, a joke—which Sefa received with such triumphant delight that Theo resolved never to be funny again, a resolution that lasted approximately a day and a half.

The gift stayed loud. That was the truth of it; the road did not soften it. Every hamlet had its shrines, every evening its graces, and Theo learned to stand at the edge of the firelight with their teeth set, riding out each blessing like a wave. But Sefa kept her word. At dawn she'd appear at Theo's elbow with two cups of tea and steer them up the road ahead of Mother Anselm's round, complaining cheerfully about the weather, the goat, the state of the realm's bridges, anything at all, so long as the talking covered the moment when the blessing passed over the place where Theo wasn't standing.

"Why do you know so much about avoiding blessings?" Theo asked her once, on the sixth morning, before they could stop themselves.

Sefa was quiet for a dozen paces. "My mother was from the marsh families," she said finally. "Before the crown redrew the borders, there were more of them. You learn things in a house like mine. What to burn first. Which neighbors count heads." She shrugged, briskly, closing the door on it. "And I draw maps. Maps are just ways of knowing where the dangerous things are. People are the dangerous things. So I map people too."

Ahead, the road bent around a hillside, and beyond it Theo could see the land changing—the trees thinning, the ground darkening, a gray-green flatness opening toward the horizon like a held breath. Marsh country. Even from here, even through the dull roar of the gift, Theo felt something at the edge of it. A pressure. A patience. Like a word someone was waiting, very politely, to finish saying.

"What's out there?" Theo asked.

Sefa shaded her eyes. "Vaelmora Crossroads by tomorrow night. After that—" she hesitated, and Theo watched the stillness come over her again, the chart-checking stillness, "—after that, the drowned city. Old Vaelmora. It comes up out of the marsh at low tide, towers and all, wrapped in black thorn nobody can cut. Pilgrims go to look at it. Say prayers near it. Keep a respectful distance." She glanced sideways at Theo. "You've gone pale."

"It's nothing."

"Mm," said Sefa, in the tone of a woman filing something away in a ledger of her own. "Well. Whatever it is, it's nothing you'll have to face alone, because the caravan turns south tomorrow, and I—" she stopped.

She stopped because she had reached into her satchel for her tea tin and her fingers had found paper instead, and she drew out a folded letter, heavy stock, sealed with a stamp Theo recognized from four days of watching crown riders pass on the far side of the river.

A wax seal. A crown.

Sefa looked at the letter as if it had appeared in her bag by witchcraft. "That's not mine," she said quietly. "That's not—I was paid to carry this. To deliver it at the crossroads. To a man. I never opened it, I never—" She broke off, and Theo watched her thumb start moving along the letter's edge, around and around, the way it moved when a coastline wouldn't match her charts.

"Paid by whom?" Theo asked.

Sefa didn't answer. Down the road, the pilgrims were singing the evening hymn, and the words came rolling up the hill soft and warm, and Theo flinched, and Sefa saw it, and neither of them said anything at all.
