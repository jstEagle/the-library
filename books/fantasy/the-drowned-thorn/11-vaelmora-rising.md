## 11. Vaelmora Rising

The marsh began to smell of salt three days before we saw the city.

I noticed it first as a change in the pilgrims' complaints. For a week they had grumbled about mud and midges and Brother Anselm's snoring, but on the morning the reeds turned grey and brittle, their talk shifted to older things—grandmothers' warnings, songs about towers that drank the tide, the proper way to spit when passing drowned ground.

"Vaelmora," Sefa said, falling into step beside me. She said it the way other people said *plague* or *tax collector*. "You can smell it before you see it. Like a fishmonger's stall left out in the sun for four hundred years."

"That's very specific."

"I'm a cartographer. Specificity is my whole personality." She squinted east, where the horizon had gone flat and silver. "You've never seen it?"

"No." The lie came easily now, which troubled me more than the lie itself. Two weeks on the road and I had learned to wear my ignorance like a coat. "Is it true it rises?"

"Twice a day, if you believe the tide-tables. Once a day if you believe the priests. Never, if you believe the crown, which insists the city is a ruin and always has been and anyone who says otherwise is spreading sedition." She smiled thinly. "The tide doesn't read royal decrees."

We walked on through the dying reeds. Behind us the pilgrims sang a hymn I half-recognized—one of those wandering tunes that changes words every hundred miles—and ahead of us the marsh opened like a held breath.

I should say something about what the gift felt like by then, because I have heard people describe crossing into magic-rich country as coming home, and that was not my experience at all. It was more like being handed a full cup by a stranger who then follows you everywhere, waiting for you to spill it. Every word I heard had weight now. Blessings, curses, the pilgrims' hymn, even Sefa's casual swearing—all of it pressed against my skin like rain about to fall. Words wanted things here. They leaned toward me the way iron leans toward a lodestone, and I walked hunched under them, hands jammed in my pockets, terrified of sneezing.

"You walk like an old man," Sefa observed.

"I'm tired."

"Mm." She let it go, which was unlike her, and I understood she was letting it go deliberately, the way you leave a door open so a frightened animal can decide to come through it on its own.

* * *

We made camp that night on a shelf of dry ground the locals called the Pilgrim's Tooth—a single hill in an ocean of fen, crowned with a shrine so old its saint had no name anymore, just a weathered stone figure with its hands open toward the east. The pilgrims lit candles and argued amiably about whether Vaelmora had been drowned as punishment or as mercy. Brother Anselm held for punishment. A widow named Katrin held for mercy, on the grounds that drowning was quicker than most alternatives. A boy of maybe twelve held that the city had drowned itself, on purpose, to keep something inside it.

"What sort of something?" I asked, before I could stop myself.

The boy shrugged with the magnificent indifference of youth. "Something that didn't want to be let out. Or something that did. Depends who tells it."

"Depends who survived to tell it," Sefa corrected quietly, and would not meet my eye.

I slept badly. In my dreams there was water over everything—not violent, not drowning exactly, just patient, the way water is—and beneath the water, roots. Black roots, thick as temple pillars, and they were not still. They were waiting. And somewhere in the dream a voice kept trying to pronounce something, working its way syllable by syllable toward a word it had not spoken in centuries, and I woke at dawn with my own name caught between my teeth like a fishbone.

*Vil-la-lo-bos.*

I sat up in the grey light, heart hammering, and told myself: wind in the reeds. Marsh birds. The ordinary machinery of a sleeping world.

But the wooden blade at my hip—I had carried it since Thornvale, refusing to abandon the one thing my grandmother had put in my hands before any other—it was warm. Not sun-warm. Warm from inside, the way a body is warm.

I did not take it out. I am not proud of how long I sat there not taking it out.

* * *

The tide turned at midmorning, and the whole camp went down to the waterline to watch.

I will try to describe it honestly, because everything that came after grew out of that hour, and because honest description is the only inheritance I trust anymore.

At first there was nothing. The marsh lay flat and brown and glittering, and the pilgrims stood in a loose line at the shore like mourners at a graveside, and I thought: this is a ritual, nothing more, people need rituals the way they need bread. Then the water began to move—not toward us, not away from us, but *down*, draining away in slow spirals, as though the earth itself had unstoppered a drain. Mudflats emerged, gleaming. Channels appeared where channels had no business being, running straight as roads.

And then the towers came up.

They rose the way bones rise when floodwater recedes from a battlefield—first a suggestion, then a certainty, then a horror too large to look away from. Grey-green spires furred with weed and shell. Arched bridges connecting nothing to nothing. Whole streets laid bare, cobbled and empty, water sheeting off them in silver veils. And around it all, up every tower, across every bridge, woven through the drowned city like a net thrown over a sleeping giant—

Thorns.

Black ones. Enormous. They did not look grown so much as *written*, if writing could be done in wood and pain, great looping strokes of them climbing the spires and knotting themselves across the archways. Some were thicker than the towers they gripped. Where the light touched them they shone faintly, darkly, like lacquer, like something polished by centuries of handling.

Nobody spoke. Even the pilgrims, who had sung and argued and spat their protective charms all week, stood silent. Brother Anselm crossed himself eleven times; I counted.

It was Sefa who finally broke the quiet, and her voice was strange—flat, careful, a cartographer reading coordinates off her own death warrant. "There. Do you see? The thorn isn't growing *over* the city. Look at the pattern."

I looked. And because I could not help myself—because the gift was awake in me now and looking was the same as listening—I *read* it.

The loops and crossings were not random. They were letters. Old ones, older than anything Wren Calloway had taught me, older than the oaths, worn soft by salt and time but still legible to someone whose blood had been trained for generations to read exactly this. And the word they spelled, repeated over and over across the whole drowned city, in strokes a hundred feet tall—

It was a name.

Not mine. Not yet. But close enough to mine that my knees nearly went.

"Theo?" Sefa's hand was on my arm. "Theo, you've gone white as the tide-line. What do you see?"

"Geometry," I said. "Old masonry patterns. Nothing."

She looked at me for a long moment. I watched her decide, again, to leave the door open. "Well," she said lightly, "if you're going to lie to me, at least pick something less insulting than geometry. I draw geometry for a living."

* * *

The pilgrims made their observances and turned back—that was the custom, I learned; the devout came to see the city rise, prayed at the shoreline, and went home again, because walking out onto the flats was how you became a story mothers told naughty children. But Sefa and I stayed. She claimed she needed to sketch the skyline for a commission. I claimed I wanted to keep her company. Both of us knew the claims were worth exactly what we'd paid for them.

By afternoon the tide had gone out far enough to expose the first causeway—a raised road of black stone running from the marsh edge toward the nearest gate, its surface studded with thorn-stubs sawn off long ago, leaving scars like closed mouths.

"We shouldn't," Sefa said.

"No," I agreed.

We walked out onto the causeway anyway. I want it recorded that she went first.

The city was louder up close. Not with sound—with *pressure*. Every step forward, the weight of words on my skin increased, until by the third archway I felt as though I were wading upstream through a river of language. The thorn itself was speaking. That was the truth I had been circling since dawn. Not whispering, not murmuring—*speaking*, continuously, in the old tongue, a sentence so long it had taken four hundred years to get through, and I was standing inside its mouth.

And threaded through that vast slow sentence, small as a needle in wool, was my name.

*Villalobos.*

Again. *Villalobos.* Patient. Searching. A voice calling down a corridor of centuries, checking each door.

"It knows we're here," I said. My voice came out wrong, thin.

"Who does?"

"The—" I stopped. Swallowed. Started over, because Sefa had earned better than another geometry. "Sefa. If I tell you something, will you do me the courtesy of pretending to be surprised later, when it's convenient?"

"I am a professional dissembler. It's on my map legend, under the compass rose."

So I told her. Not everything—the oath, the burning archive, the raid, Ilsa's face in the firelight saying *run east*—but enough. Enough that when I finished, she did not step away from me. She looked at the black thorn coiling up the gatehouse above our heads, and then at me, and her expression was one I would come to know well in the years after: the particular arithmetic of a woman recalculating a friendship against new figures.

"So the stories are true," she said. "The speakers. The blood-oaths. All of it."

"All of it."

"And you're—" She gestured at me, at all of me, this mud-spattered person who had spent two weeks losing at cards and complaining about blisters. "You're the last one."

"Apparently the last ones make the best pilgrims. Nobody suspects them of anything except poor hygiene."

That got a laugh out of her, brief and startled, and I clung to it like a handrail. Then her face sobered. "Theo. The thorn. You said it's saying your name. Saying it *how*?"

I listened. I had been trying not to listen for hours, but now I opened that door deliberately, and the sentence poured in, and there it was, unmistakable, no longer searching but *addressed*:

*Villalobos. Villalobos. Little jailer. Little heir. Come and count your prisoners.*

"It's inviting me home," I said.

* * *

We did not go past the second gate. I wish I could tell you it was wisdom. It was mostly that the tide had begun to turn, and the water was coming back faster than any tide has a right to, pouring up the streets in silent silver sheets, and Sefa grabbed my sleeve and said, with the calm of a woman who has read every account of every fool ever drowned in this exact spot, "*Now,*" and we ran.

Even running, I heard it behind us. The thorn's voice did not fade with distance the way voices should. It simply changed registers, the way a bell changes when you stop striking it and it goes on humming in the metal.

*Come back, little jailer. Your family left such a mess. Someone ought to tidy it.*

We gained the shore as the water swallowed the causeway. The pilgrims were long gone; only Sefa and I stood dripping on the Pilgrim's Tooth as evening came down, watching Vaelmora sink again tower by tower, the black thorn going under last, its highest loop visible for a long moment against the red sky like a signature on a letter.

Then the marsh was flat and brown and innocent, and I felt the pressure of words ease slightly, though it never fully left. It hasn't left since. I understand now that it never will.

Sefa built a fire without a word. We ate. She sketched. I pretended to sleep.

Near midnight she spoke into the dark, not unkindly. "The person who hired me. The one I'm supposed to guide to Vaelmora. They paid in crown coin, Theo. Crown coin, for a drowned city the crown pretends doesn't exist." A pause. "I've been telling myself it's a scholar. A rich eccentric. Someone harmless."

"And now?"

"Now I think whoever paid me knows exactly what lives in that city. And I think they may be expecting you." She poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "So tomorrow, you're going to tell me the rest of it. All of it. Because I've decided something, and I'd like to make it with my eyes open."

"Decided what?"

She looked at me across the flames, and for once there was no irony anywhere in her face.

"Whether I'm guiding you or selling you."

Out in the dark, beneath the water, beneath the mud, something ancient turned my name over in its mouth like a coin it had been saving for four hundred years—and waited, with terrible patience, to see which way I'd spend it.
