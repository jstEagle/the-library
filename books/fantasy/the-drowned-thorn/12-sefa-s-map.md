## 12. Sefa's Map

Sefa had learned long ago that the truth came out of people the way water came out of a cracked jug—slowly, at an angle, and usually all over something you'd rather keep dry.

She'd been carrying her crack for eleven days now, ever since the pilgrim road bent east toward the marshes, and it was getting heavier by the mile.

The trouble was Theo Villalobos—or whatever their real name was, because Sefa had stopped believing *Alder* somewhere around the second night, when "Alder" had flinched like a struck dog at old Mother Hesk's blessing over the supper bread. *The Gifted keep you. The Gifted keep you.* A perfectly ordinary blessing, the kind said in every village from here to the coast, and Alder had gone white as unbleached linen and stared at their hands until the fire burned down to coals.

Sefa knew what that meant. She'd grown up in a port town where you learned to read people the way other children learned to read weather. And she knew what *she* was carrying meant, too, which was worse, because it wasn't just knowledge—it was coin. Crown-minted, heavy-in-the-purse coin, folded into oilcloth at the bottom of her map case, next to the commission letter with its wax seal still intact.

*Guide the bearer of this token to Vaelmora at low tide. Ask nothing. Name nothing.*

She'd taken the job in a tavern in Corren's Ford with her family's debt sitting on her chest like a stone. Three hundred crowns. Her father's freedom. Her mother's freedom. Her own name scrubbed off the debtors' rolls. All she had to do was walk east with a stranger who called themselves a pilgrim and pretend not to notice anything at all.

Except the stranger had turned out to be two strangers, sort of—one sharp-eyed, soft-spoken person who carried a wooden sword wrapped in sacking and never once complained about wet boots—and Sefa had made the fatal error, the one her mother had warned her about since she was six: *Sefa, girl, don't go liking the merchandise.*

Now they were camped in the lee of a drowned orchard, half a day from the marsh edge, and the trees around them stood black and salt-killed with their roots in standing water, and Sefa sat sharpening a pencil she did not need sharpening, and watched Alder across the fire, and hated herself in a comfortable, familiar way.

"You're staring," Alder said, without looking up from the wooden blade they were rubbing with oil.

"I'm surveying."

"At me."

"You're between me and the north horizon. It's not my fault." Sefa blew graphite dust off the pencil point. "Also your hair's doing something interesting. Like a bird that gave up mid-landing."

That got the ghost of a smile. Eleven days of walking and Sefa had catalogued maybe nine of those smiles. She kept them the way she kept landmarks—carefully, against bad weather.

"Ask me whatever you're circling," Alder said. "You've been flying overhead since the ford."

"I don't know what you mean."

"You've asked me four questions about my childhood in the last two days. Nobody cares about my childhood. You care about my childhood the way Ostrev's surveyors care about bridges."

Sefa winced. That was fair, and it was also alarming, because Alder wasn't supposed to know about surveyors. "Where did you hear that name?"

"I didn't." Alder set the blade down across their knees. The firelight moved along the wood grain. "I guessed. Someone's mapping this road, someone official, and you've been checking our backtrail since the second morning. So either you're the most paranoid cartographer in the realm or you're expecting company." They looked up. Their eyes were very steady. It was unnerving, how steady. Most people's eyes wandered. Alder's eyes planted themselves like fence posts. "Which is it?"

And there it was—the crack in the jug, widening. Sefa thought about the oilcloth. About the seal. About her father in the debtors' block at Corren's Ford, coughing in the damp, waiting on her.

She thought about Mother Hesk's blessing, and the way Alder had looked at their own hands.

"Neither," Sefa said, and heard her own voice do the thing it did when she lied badly, which was go bright and brisk, a market voice, a *fresh fish, fresh fish* voice. "It's both. I'm the most paranoid cartographer in the realm *and* I'm expecting company. I contain multitudes."

"Alder" smiled again, but it didn't reach the fence posts.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, because the marsh waited for no one and the tide tables waited for even fewer people. Sefa led them off the pilgrim road onto a peat-cutters' track that wound through reeds taller than a mounted man, and for three hours the only sounds were wind, water birds arguing about territory, and Alder humming—very quietly, very badly—a tune Sefa almost recognized and then decided she was better off not recognizing.

It was during the fourth hour, when the reeds opened onto the first true sight of the marsh proper—a gray-green infinity stitched with channels—that Sefa's conscience finally won the argument it had been losing since Corren's Ford.

Not because she'd grown noble. Because she'd done the arithmetic.

If Alder was who Sefa suspected they were, and if the crown's stranger was coming to Vaelmora for the same reason the crown wanted anyone brought to Vaelmora, then Alder was walking into a trap that Sefa had helped bait. And Sefa had discovered, somewhere on the pilgrim road between the ford and the marsh, that she could live with a great many things but not with that one. Not with the memory of Alder sharing out their last dried plums without counting them first. Not with the way Alder had quietly mended the tear in Sefa's map case strap while she slept, using thread pulled from their own hem.

People didn't mend your things with their own hem-thread and then turn out to be monsters. In Sefa's considerable experience, the correlation ran entirely the other direction.

So at noon, in the shadow of a ruined tide-mill with its wheel frozen mid-turn in centuries of silt, Sefa sat down on a fallen beam, patted the space beside her, and said, "Right. Sit down. We need to talk about my terrible personality."

Alder sat. Warily. Everything Alder did was slightly wary, like a cat in a house with dogs. "Go on."

"I'm going to tell you something, and before I tell it, I want you to know that I've already decided to tell it, so there's no point doing the thing where you get quiet and dangerous. Just—listen to the whole thing. Then be quiet and dangerous if you must."

"That's a promising opening."

"It gets better." Sefa unbuckled the map case. Her hands were steadier than she'd expected, which she chose to take as a good omen. "Eleven days ago, in Corren's Ford, a man bought me dinner. Good dinner. Wine that wasn't vinegar. He said he represented parties interested in the eastern ruins, and he needed a guide to Vaelmora, and he paid me this." She drew out the oilcloth packet and unfolded it on her knee: the commission letter, seal up, and beside it a stack of crown coin that caught the gray light and threw it back cold. "Half now, half on delivery. Delivery being a person matching a description."

The silence lasted exactly as long as it took Alder to read the letter upside down, which was not long, because Alder read fast.

"And the description?" Alder asked. Their voice was level. Too level. Level the way ice is level right before it isn't.

"Young. Alone. Traveling light. Carrying—" Sefa stopped. Swallowed. "Carrying a blade wrapped in sacking. Wooden, most likely. And—" she made herself finish, because finishing was the whole point, "—and reacting poorly to blessings."

Alder looked at the coins. Looked at the seal. Looked at Sefa.

Sefa had imagined this moment roughly forty times in eleven days, and in none of the forty versions had she managed to hold the gaze. In thirty-nine of them she'd looked away first. In the fortieth she'd thrown herself into the marsh as a preemptive apology.

What actually happened was worse than any of them. Alder simply nodded, once, slowly, the way you nod at news of a death—not surprised, exactly. Confirming something they'd already grieved.

"The debtors' prison at Corren's Ford," Alder said quietly. "Your family?"

Sefa's mouth went dry. "How did you—"

"You flinched when the wine arrived. When I asked what it cost, you said 'less than some things,' and then you counted the coins in your purse twice that night, and both times you sighed like a woman doing sums she hates." Alder turned the top coin over with one finger. "Three hundred crowns says a family. Debtors' prisons don't take single people; nobody borrows that much alone."

"It's two hundred and eighty," Sefa said, absurdly, because precision was the last armor she had left. "I haggled."

Something moved at the corner of Alder's mouth. Not quite a smile. The place where a smile would go if the ground weren't so unstable. "Of course you did."

"So." Sefa made herself say it. Made herself sit in it. "Say the rest of it, then. Say what you're thinking, because I've been thinking it for eleven days and I'd rather hear it out loud than keep chewing it."

"What I'm thinking," Alder said, "is that you told me anyway."

"Don't—"

"You didn't have to. You could have delivered me at the marsh gate and collected your other half and been in Corren's Ford by the new moon with your father free and your hands clean, or clean enough, and instead you're showing me crown coin in a ruin with no witnesses." Alder picked up the commission letter, weighed it, set it down. "I'm thinking you're a very bad traitor, Sefa, and I'm glad of it."

Sefa discovered her eyes were stinging and blamed the marsh wind, which everyone knew was brutal on the eyes, and blinked hard, and said, "Well. Yes. Obviously. I'm a professional. I know when a job's gone sour."

"When did it go sour?"

"The plums," Sefa admitted. "Day six. You gave me the last plum and you didn't count them first, and I thought, oh no, oh *no*, this one's decent, and after that every mile east felt like walking downhill into something." She dragged a sleeve across her face, briskly, businesslike. "Anyway. Here's what matters. The man in the tavern—he wasn't crown army. No uniform, wrong boots, and he paid in mint-new coin, which soldiers never have. But the seal's real, and the paper's chancery stock, and whoever sent him wants a speaker at Vaelmora badly enough to spend two hundred and eighty crowns on a cartographer with debts."

"Why would the crown want a speaker *brought* to Vaelmora? Every story says the crown burns speakers."

"Every story also says Vaelmora's a myth, and we're looking at its front door." Sefa nodded east, where the reeds thinned and the marsh began its long gray breathing. "Somebody in the crown wants this done quietly. Quiet means not army. Quiet means a tavern, a purse, and a woman who asks nothing and names nothing." She laughed, short and bitter. "*Ask nothing. Name nothing.* I thought it was odd phrasing for a survey job. Now I think it was phrasing for a burial."

Alder was quiet for a while. Out in the channels, a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of furniture.

"There'll be others," Alder said at last. "Whoever paid you won't have paid only you. If the description reached one tavern keeper, it reached ten."

"Oh, absolutely. I'd bet the remaining hundred and forty crowns on it." Sefa started repacking the oilcloth, then paused, coin in hand. "Which raises the question of what I do with this."

"Keep it."

Sefa looked up sharply. "I just confessed to selling you."

"You confessed to *not* selling me. There's a difference, and it's worth two hundred and eighty crowns." Alder pushed the coins back toward her with one finger. "Buy your father out. Then burn the letter, lose the commission, and be a paranoid cartographer full-time. You're clearly gifted at it."

"That's the nicest insult anyone's ever given me." Sefa hesitated, thumb moving over the crowned face of the top coin. "There's a problem, though. A big, marsh-shaped problem."

"Go on."

"If I don't deliver, whoever hired me sends someone else. Someone who doesn't haggle. And they'll come to Vaelmora regardless—you're not the cargo, you're just the convenient route. Which means—" she tapped the commission letter, "—whoever wrote this knows the drowned city rises at low tide. Knows the channel approaches. Which means somebody with old maps, or older memories, is working with the crown. And I've seen every surviving chart of this coast, Alder. There aren't any. The charts of Vaelmora were destroyed two hundred years ago. Deliberately. By order."

Alder had gone very still. Stillness, Sefa was learning, was what Alder did instead of fear—or perhaps it was fear wearing stillness as a coat. "Then whoever hired you has a source nobody else has."

"Yes. And that source is waiting at the marsh edge, or already inside, and they're expecting a guide and a speaker to walk in together, right on schedule, because I'm a professional and professionals keep schedules." Sefa stood, brushing peat from her trousers, and slung the map case across her body where she could feel its weight like a second heartbeat. "So here's my proposal, and you're going to hate it."

"I already hate it," Alder said, standing too, "and I haven't heard it."

"We go in anyway. Off-schedule, off-channel, by the cutters' paths that aren't on any chart—including mine, because I made them up yesterday out of spite and geometry. We get to Vaelmora first, or at least not last, and we find out who's waiting before they find out we're coming." Sefa grinned, and found, to her mild surprise, that the grin was mostly real. "Because I'll tell you what eleven days of watching you has taught me, friend. Whoever this stranger is, whatever they want with the thorn and the blood and the drowned kingdom—they planned for a frightened person running alone. They did not plan for a frightened person running with a cartographer who holds grudges and draws excellent maps."

"They also didn't plan for me," Alder said quietly, and for a moment Sefa saw something pass behind those fence-post eyes—something old and heavy, a door opening a crack onto a room she couldn't see. "Nobody ever plans for me. That's rather the point of me."

"Well." Sesa shouldered her pack and turned east, toward the gray breathing of the marsh, toward the towers that would rise with the ebb like bones remembering their shape. "Let's go be unplanned."

They walked. The reeds closed behind them. And far off, at the edge of hearing—so faint Sefa could believe she'd invented it, and chose, for the moment, to believe exactly that—the marsh made a sound that was not wind and not water and not any bird she'd ever charted.

It sounded, if she were pressed, like a word.

Like a name, almost.

Almost like *Villalobos*.
