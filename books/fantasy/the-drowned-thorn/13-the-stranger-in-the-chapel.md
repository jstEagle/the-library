## 13. The Stranger in the Chapel

Wren Calloway had been waiting in the chapel for eleven days, and he had spent most of them arguing with a heron.

The bird had claimed the broken bell tower the first morning, standing on one leg above the collapsed roof like a small gray god surveying its congregation. Wren had tried shooing it. The heron had looked at him with the particular contempt of a creature that had outlived three bishops and knew it. By the fourth day they had reached an understanding: the tower was the heron's, the pews were Wren's, and the flooded nave belonged to both of them equally, which is to say neither.

"Fine company you are," he told it now, from his perch on the third pew, which was the only one still dry enough to sit on without regret. "Eleven days I've waited for this Villalobos child, and you haven't said a word of encouragement."

The heron blinked once, slowly, which Wren chose to interpret as agreement that the wait was tedious but necessary.

He was getting good at interpreting things. That was, in truth, most of what remained of his vocation. He had been a priest once—Brother Wren, of the Order of the Quiet Word, before the Quiet Word fell out of fashion and then out of law and finally out of existence. Defrocked, they called it. As if faith were a garment you could be stripped of rather than a stone you swallowed whole and carried in your belly the rest of your life. He still wore the habit's shadow: gray wool, plain-cut, mended at both elbows. Old habits. Literally.

He had come to Vaelmora because of a promise made to a dying man twenty-two years ago, and because of a letter received four months ago, and because he had nowhere else left to stand that felt honest.

The dying man had been Aldric Villalobos—grandfather to whoever now came east carrying the family name and, presumably, the family curse. Wren had served as his confessor in the last years, back when the old speaker lived half-hidden in a river town under a name that wasn't his, mending nets and saying nothing. Speakers weren't supposed to say nothing. It went against their nature like holding breath goes against drowning men. But Aldric had managed it for thirty years, and it had hollowed him into something quiet and strange and, in the end, unexpectedly tender.

*If one of mine ever comes asking,* Aldric had told him on the last night, gripping Wren's wrist with hands that trembled like candle flames, *you'll know them by the way they flinch at blessings. Teach them what I couldn't teach my son. What I was too much a coward to teach anyone.*

And then, four months ago, a letter had found him—a circuitous thing, passed hand to hand through peddlers and pilgrims, written in a spidery hand that identified itself only as *a friend of the valley*. It said: *The last speaker has fled Thornvale. Crown hunters burned it. The child runs east. If you are who Aldric believed you are, be at the drowned chapel before the autumn tides turn.*

So here he was. Eleven days. One heron. A chapel knee-deep in black water that rose and fell with the marsh tides, its saints dissolved to featureless lumps of salt-eaten stone, its altar crowned with thorn.

That was the other reason he'd come, though he kept it folded up small inside himself where he wouldn't have to look at it directly. The Amarels. The dispossessed family. The ones whose binding had been stolen—or so their story went; there were two stories about everything in this country, and Wren had learned long ago that the truth usually sat between them, bruised and unclaimed. They had found him two years ago, an old defrocked priest with nothing left to lose, and made him an offer: help us reclaim what was taken, and we will restore your name, your order, your purpose.

He had said yes. He was not proud of how quickly he had said yes.

But the child—the speaker—deserved to be taught regardless of what the Amarels intended. That was the knot he'd tied himself into, and he suspected, with the clarity available only to old men, that he would eventually have to cut it or hang from it.

Water dripped somewhere in the dark behind the altar. The heron shifted its weight. And then—

A sound at the chapel door. Not footsteps exactly. More the careful, testing silence of someone who had learned that making noise could kill you.

Wren set down the cup of rainwater he'd been nursing and did not light the lamp. Let them come to him half-blind. It was kinder than startling them.

* * *

Theo Villalobos came through the door like water finding its level—slowly, along the wall, low, watching everything.

Wren watched them back with the frank attention of a man who had spent decades reading faces across altars. Young. Exhausted in the specific way of someone who hadn't truly slept since the night their home burned. Moving with the economy of the hunted. And around them, faintly, like heat off summer stone, something that made the fine hairs on Wren's arms lift. He hadn't stood near a speaker in over twenty years. He'd forgotten how it felt—like standing near a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing.

"You're late," Wren said mildly. "By eleven days. Though I suppose the crown's soldiers don't post schedules."

Theo froze. In the dim light filtering through the broken roof, Wren saw the flinch—not at his words, at the shape of them. At speech itself, arriving unexpected in a dark place. Aldric had been right. They always flinched.

"I'm looking—" Theo stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "I was told there might be shelter here. For travelers. I don't want trouble."

"No trouble lives here," Wren said. "Only me, a heron, and several hundred years of damp." He gestured at the pew beside him. "Sit. You look like a wind-blown reed, and I've bread that's only slightly stale, and water that's only slightly full of things."

Theo didn't move. "Who are you?"

"Wren Calloway. Formerly Brother Wren of the Order of the Quiet Word, currently nobody in particular." He tore the loaf and set half on the dry end of the pew, an offering placed at a diplomatic distance. "I knew your grandfather."

Something moved behind Theo's eyes—hunger and suspicion wrestling, and suspicion winning on points. "My grandfather died before I was born."

"Yes. Twenty-two years ago, give or take a fever." Wren kept his voice easy, conversational, the tone of a man discussing weather. "In a river town called Marrow's Bend, where he was known as Aldric Weaver, net-mender, a man of no consequence whatsoever. He had a scar on his left palm shaped like a fishhook, a laugh like a door hinge, and a gift he never used after the year 1213, because using it once cost him something he never talked about and never forgave."

Silence. Long silence. The kind that either means *liar* or means *you've just described something only a dead man's ghost would know.*

Then Theo's shoulders dropped half an inch. Not trust. Just the smallest loosening of the constant vigilance, like a fist uncurling one finger.

"He taught you?" Theo asked. "The oaths?"

"He tried to teach me what he could remember of them," Wren corrected. "Which was less than either of us wanted. Your grandfather carried those words the way a man carries a wound—carefully, privately, resenting the weight. He gave me fragments. Fragments of fragments. Enough to know the shape of the whole and none of the substance." He paused. "I've spent the years since filling in the gaps. Some of what I know, I know well. Some of it, I'm guessing at, and I'll tell you honestly which is which, because a teacher who won't admit ignorance is worse than no teacher at all."

"Why?" The question came out flat, almost hostile. "Why would you do all that? For a dead man's sake? For mine?"

"Ah." Wren smiled, and it was a genuinely warm smile, the kind that arrived with crow's-feet attached. "You want the answer where I'm the hero. Selfless old priest, faithful unto death, etcetera. Very tidy. Unfortunately, the true answer is less flattering and more interesting." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Because I'm afraid of what happens if no one teaches you. Because the last time a speaker learned these words alone, from scraps and instinct, the results were—well. There's a kingdom at the bottom of this marsh that says otherwise, doesn't it. And because, if we're being entirely honest, I owe a debt to your family that isn't entirely honorable, and paying it is the closest thing to absolution I'm likely to get."

Theo picked up the bread. Didn't eat it. Held it, studying Wren the way one studies a locked box.

"What debt?"

Wren considered lying. He was, he reflected, quite good at lying—it had been a professional hazard of the priesthood, all those gentle comforting falsehoods delivered over open graves. But Aldric had extracted a promise of honesty, and besides, lies had a way of surfacing in this story like corpses in a marsh.

"Not today," he said. "Today you need food, sleep, and to know you're not alone. The debt keeps. Debts always keep; it's their one virtue." He nodded toward the far corner, where a dry loft remained above the ruined sacristy. "There's straw up there. It smells of pigeon, but pigeon-smell builds character."

Theo ate the bread. Slowly, at first, then with the sudden ferocity of the starving, and Wren winced sympathetically—he'd seen famine enough times to know you should ration a starving person's first meal, but he'd also never once had the heart to enforce it.

* * *

They fell into a rhythm over the following days, as tide-following creatures do.

Mornings, Wren taught. Afternoons, they walked the chapel's boundaries while the water receded, and Theo asked questions in a voice that grew steadily less like a whisper and more like a person's. Evenings, they sat on the highest step of the porch and watched the marsh exhale mist, and the heron stalked the shallows below them with murderous patience, and Wren narrated its hunting attempts like a sporting commentator, which Theo pretended not to find funny and then, on the fourth evening, laughed at—a real laugh, rusty and startled out of them, and Wren felt something in his chest ease that had been clenched for months.

The lessons themselves were delicate work. Like teaching someone to swim who has nearly drowned.

"The first thing," Wren said on the second morning, "is that you must stop apologizing to your own voice."

Theo blinked. "What?"

"You speak like every word might detonate. Half-volume, half-speed, trailing off. I've watched you start sentences and abandon them mid-word, as though the sentence were a fuse you lit by accident." He held up a hand before Theo could bristle. "It's sensible! Sensible kept you alive through checkpoints and patrols. But it won't serve you here. A speaker who fears their own voice can't control it. Fear makes words leak. Calm makes them hold. So: we practice speaking plainly, about nothing—bread, weather, the moral failures of herons—until your voice remembers it's allowed to exist."

Theo's jaw tightened. "You don't understand. Out there, a wrong word—"

"Out there, yes. We're not out there. We're in a drowned chapel arguing with birds." Wren tapped the salt-crusted altar. "And more importantly—you already know the danger. What you don't know yet is the discipline. Danger without discipline is just terror with better posture."

He began, cautiously, with history rather than practice. The three oaths. He named them the way Aldric had named them to him, reluctantly, each name sounding like something whispered in a cellar.

"The Binding Oath. The oldest, the one your family claims as inheritance. It chains the thorn—chains it to a place, a purpose, a pattern. Spoken fully, it holds for generations. Your ancestors spoke it, or so the story goes, and the thorn slept beneath Vaelmora for three hundred years."

"And the second?"

"The Unbinding. Its mirror. It releases what was bound—and it cannot be spoken casually, because the thorn does not wake gently. When it woke last, it drowned a kingdom. That's not metaphor. There are streets under that water," he said, pointing east through the chapel's empty doorway, "where fish swim through nursery windows."

Theo stared at the horizon. "And the third oath?"

Wren hesitated. Here was the edge of his knowledge, and he had promised to mark it honestly. "There isn't one. Or rather—there are references. Fragments. Every text that survived mentions a third oath the way people mention a room they're afraid to enter. 'Beyond the two,' one fragment says. 'The oath that is not given but made.' No text records its words. Aldric claimed not to know them. Either he was telling the truth, or he knew and judged the knowledge too dangerous to leave in a priest's keeping, and frankly, knowing Aldric, both are possible."

"So it might not exist."

"It might not. Or it might be the only one that matters." Wren shrugged, and the shrug cost him more than he showed. "Old powers love symmetry. Two oaths, bind and loose—that's clean, that's human thinking. A third that breaks the pattern? That's the sort of thing that exists precisely because someone, someday, will need it."

Theo was quiet a long moment. Then: "My grandmother said the words live in our blood. That I could destroy the thorn, or wake it, or bind myself to it forever. She made it sound like a choice between dying free and living caged."

"Your grandmother," Wren said carefully, "loved you, and lied to you, and I suspect those two facts are more entangled than grief allows us to see clearly. We'll come to what she knew and when she knew it another day. Today—" he gestured at the flooded nave, at the dissolved saints, at the thorn-wrapped bell tower where the heron stood sentinel, "—today you learn to stand in a room with power and not flinch."

* * *

It was on the sixth day that the doubt arrived, and it arrived wearing Wren's own face.

He had sent Theo wading to the far aisle to practice—nothing dangerous, only recitation of neutral forms, vowel-shapes and cadences, the scales of the speaker's art. Theo stood hip-deep in black water, repeating a phrase from the Binding's opening structure, and Wren watched from the dry pew and felt the old familiar vertigo.

Because the words were working. He could feel it. The water around Theo's legs had gone still—not calm, *attentive*, the way animals go still when a predator passes. The thorn-vines that furred the chapel walls had turned, very slightly, all together, like a field of flowers tracking the sun. Something vast and buried had lifted its head at the sound of a Villalobos voice practicing its inheritance.

And Wren thought: *the Amarels would want to know this. The Amarels would want to know exactly this, exactly now.*

His hand drifted toward the satchel where he kept the letter-code they'd given him, the means by which he was supposed to report progress. And then he looked at Theo—hip-deep in cold water, hoarse, stubborn, repeating the phrase badly and then slightly less badly, working with the desperate diligence of someone trying to earn the right to exist—and he put his hand back down.

Not today, he told himself. Which was what he'd been telling himself for six days, and would probably tell himself for sixty more, right up until the day he either burned the code or used it.

"You're making a face," Theo called across the water. "Is it that bad?"

"It's that good," Wren said, and was surprised to find he meant it. "Again. From the top. And this time, mean it."

* * *

On the ninth evening, Theo asked the question Wren had been dreading.

They were on the porch steps, sharing the last of a smoked fish Wren had traded for with a marsh eeler who accepted prayer in lieu of coin. The mist was coming in. Somewhere out in the dark, the drowned city waited under the tide, and Theo had begun, in the last two days, to hear it—not voices exactly, more a pressure, a hum beneath hearing, strongest at low tide.

"You said you'd tell me about the debt," Theo said. "When it suited you. Does it suit you now?"

Wren looked out at the dark water for a long time.

"Partly," he said at last. "Here's the part I can give you tonight. Twenty-two years ago, I did something cowardly. It concerned your grandfather, and a secret he trusted me with, and a moment when keeping that secret and acting on it were the same act, and I chose only to keep it. Someone paid for my caution. Not your grandfather. Someone else. Someone whose name I will say aloud one day soon, because you deserve it, but not tonight, because tonight I want you to sleep without hating me, and I find I've grown selfish in my old age."

Theo studied him. "You could lie to me. You're good at talking. You could make me believe anything right now—I've watched you handle the eeler."

"True. And I could also tell you a beautiful partial truth, which is the more seductive sin." Wren turned his cup of tea in his hands. "But you're going to spend the rest of your life being lied to by everyone with an interest in the thorn—the crown, the families, the priests, the historians, possibly the thorn itself. Everyone will offer you a version of events with themselves as the wounded party. Your job—your actual job, harder than any oath—is to learn to tell the sound of a true thing from the sound of a convenient thing. So I'll practice what I preach. Convenient things sound smooth. True things usually have a burr in them. Everything I've told you has burrs. Check them yourself, whenever you like."

"How?"

"Ask me the same question twice, a month apart, and see if the answers match. Ask me what I gain. Watch whether I flinch at blessings." He smiled. "Oh—and one more test. When I die, check whether I told you the debt before or after it became unavoidable. Cowards confess late. It's a reliable rule."

Theo snorted despite themself. "That's a terrible test. It requires you to die first."

"All the best tests do," Wren said serenely. "Now sleep. Tomorrow we begin the Binding Oath in earnest, and you'll want your voice rested, because the thorn will be listening, and I want very much for its first impression of you to be competence rather than terror."

Theo went up to the pigeon-scented loft. Wren sat on alone with the mist and the heron and the satchel with the letter-code in it, and took the code out, and held it over the brazier's coals, and did not drop it, and hated himself a little, and put it away.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Again.

* * *

The tenth morning broke gray and windless, the marsh lying flat as poured pewter. Wren was up early, drawing the Binding Oath's opening structure in chalk on the driest flagstone, when he heard Theo come down the stairs and stop halfway.

"Someone's coming," Theo said quietly. "Through the water. East side. Two someones. They're being careful the way soldiers are careful."

Wren's chalk snapped in his fingers.

He had chosen the chapel because it was forgotten—off every map Sefa's profession respected, reachable only at low tide, worthless except to someone who knew what it had been. He had told himself the crown couldn't find it. He had told himself a great many things.

"Back stair, through the sacristy," he said, moving toward the door to delay any visitors. "There's a causeway at low water that runs north into the reed-beds. Take the bread. Don't wait for me."

Theo's eyes went narrow. "You're staying?"

"Somebody should greet them," Wren said, "and it shouldn't be the person they're hunting." He was already composing his manner—the vague, deaf, harmless old hermit, a role he could play in his sleep. "Go. Now. If I'm not at the reed-beds by tomorrow's low tide, follow the pilgrim road south and ask for a woman named Sefa, who I suspect you've already met, and tell her the old man in the chapel said the tide turns whether we're ready or not."

Theo hesitated one heartbeat longer—long enough for Wren to see, in that hesitation, the exact shape of Aldric's face at thirty years old—and then slipped out through the dark of the sacristy, silent as spilled ink.

Outside, through the broken doorway, Wren watched two figures wade the shallows toward the chapel. One wore the gray of a crown surveyor's service. The other wore traveling clothes and moved like a blade being carried.

He straightened his back, arranged his face into senility, and prepared to lie magnificently for his life—while somewhere north, in the whispering reeds, his student ran east into the marsh with half an education and a whole inheritance, toward a city that was even now beginning, tide by tide, to rise.
