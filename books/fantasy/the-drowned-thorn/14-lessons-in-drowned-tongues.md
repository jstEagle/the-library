## 14. Lessons in Drowned Tongues

Wren Calloway taught the way water taught stone: patiently, relentlessly, and with an absolute certainty that he would win in the end.

They had made a school of the chapel's vestry, a small square room above the floodline where the tide only reached on the highest springs. A broken pew served as a desk. A shard of mirror, propped against the wall so it caught the light from the gap where a window had been, served as everything else—blackboard, candle-stand, and once, memorably, a weapon when a marsh eel got into the room and Wren defended himself with it while screaming in a register Theo hadn't known priests could reach.

"Again," Wren said now, settling onto his stool with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions. "From the beginning."

"The beginning is four hours ago," Theo said. "We've done the beginning eleven times."

"Twelve. You lost count because you were sulking."

"I was *resting my voice*."

"You were sulking with your voice." He tapped the pew. "Again."

Theo looked down at the page before them. It was one of three Wren owned—a scrap of vellum gone soft as cloth with age, its ink browned to the color of dried blood. On it, in a hand that trembled even before whatever hand had copied it began to shake, were nine words in the old drowned tongue. Three lines. Three oaths, or the skeletons of them, the way a ribcage is the skeleton of a shout.

*Learn the shape,* Ilsa had always said about swordwork. *The shape will hold you when your courage doesn't.*

Theo breathed out, and spoke the first line.

The words came out wrong. Not mispronounced—Wren had beaten the pronunciation into them over two days until Theo's mouth ached—but wrong in some deeper way, like a key cut from the right pattern for the wrong lock. The air in the vestry shivered. Somewhere below them, in the flooded nave, the water went briefly, terribly still.

And then it passed, and the eel-scented quiet came back, and Theo was sitting in a ruined chapel with a headache blooming behind their eyes and a taste in their mouth like coins.

"There it is," Wren said softly. He wasn't looking at Theo. He was looking at the water stain creeping up the far wall, as if he could see something written there. "You felt it."

"I felt like I'd licked a battery."

"A what?"

"Never mind. Something from home." Theo flexed their fingers. They still didn't feel like their own hands. Since crossing the valley border, since the gift had come flooding back into them like cold water into a drowning lung, nothing quite felt like theirs—their hearing too sharp, their skin too aware of air. And now this. Now the words had weight, and the weight had teeth. "That was the Binding Oath. That was really it. That little line of gibberish is what put the thorn to sleep for three hundred years."

"That little line of gibberish," Wren said, "is the first third of it. The whole Binding runs forty-one words. What you just did was knock politely on a door you have no business knocking on yet." He leaned forward, and the humor drained out of his face, leaving behind the man who had served Theo's grandfather and buried him, or claimed to; Theo was still deciding how much of Wren's history to believe. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? You spoke three words of a binding and the *water listened*. In Vaelmora, child, the water is not empty. There are things down there older than the thorn, and every one of them just turned over in its sleep to see who was calling."

Theo thought about that. About the black towers wrapped in thorn, visible from the marsh edge at low tide, and the whisper they'd heard on the wind that had known their name.

"Good," they said.

Wren blinked. "Good?"

"Good. Let them listen. I've spent twenty years being quiet. Being quiet got my grandmother's house burned and my village put to the torch and an old woman hanged—or worse, I don't actually know which, because I *ran*, because that's what being quiet is for." Their voice cracked on the last word, and they hated it, and kept going anyway. "If the price of learning these oaths is that something in the dark takes notice of me, then let it take notice. I'm done hiding in valleys that forget me."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. Then he smiled—not the senile mask he wore for strangers, but the real one, crooked and tired—and said, "Your grandmother used to argue exactly like that. Word for word. It was infuriating then too."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the morning became afternoon, and the afternoon became the hour when the light through the broken window went gold and the tide began its slow climb up the chapel steps.

Wren taught the way scholars teach, which is to say he taught everything except the thing itself, circling it, approaching from six directions, insisting that the oaths could not be understood as sounds but only as *arithmetic*. Words in, years out. That was the engine under all of it.

"The Villalobos speakers weren't warriors," he said, sketching in the dust with a stick. "Whatever your grandmother told you—and she told you something, don't make that face, everyone in that family tells someone something—they weren't warriors. They were accountants. Terrible, tragic accountants. Every oath is a ledger entry. You write power into the world and the world writes the cost back into you. The Binding Oath, spoken whole by a speaker in their prime, costs—" he paused, doing sums he clearly hated, "—call it thirty years. Your grandfather's mother spoke it at nineteen and died at fifty-one with white hair and hands like a crone's, and she called it a bargain."

"Thirty years." Theo heard their own voice go flat. "For the full oath."

"For the full oath, spoken alone, against a thorn at full strength. Which is why no one speaks it alone anymore. It was meant to be shared—a circle of speakers, each carrying a portion of the cost. That's how the first binding was done. Twelve of them." Wren's stick stopped moving. "Only one walked away."

"And the Unbinding?"

"Is worse, and not in the way you think." He sat back. "The Unbinding costs almost nothing. A handful of years. A few gray hairs. That's the trap of it, do you see? The crown fears the Unbinding because it thinks destruction must be expensive. But unmaking is cheap, Theo. Making is dear. Holding is dearest of all. Anyone with the blood and the hatred could loose the thorn for the price of a bad winter. It's the *keeping* that bleeds you white."

Theo looked at their hands. Twenty-two years old, or near enough. Callused from a wooden sword held in silence for two decades. Hands that had never done anything worth costing anything.

"And the third oath?" they asked, because they had asked it every day, and every day Wren had deflected, and today—for the first time—he didn't.

He was quiet long enough that the tide filled the silence, lapping at the bottom step.

"There is no text for it," he said finally. "That's not me being mysterious. I've spent forty years collecting everything written about the speakers—every fragment, every heresy, every drunk ballad. The Binding is recorded. The Unbinding is recorded. The third appears exactly once, in a marginal note in a psalter that burned with the Amarel library, and the note says—" he closed his eyes, reciting, "*'The third oath has no name because it has no author. Each speaker who needs it must write it themselves, in their own words, at the moment of greatest need, and having spoken it, it is gone.'*"

"Gone?"

"Spoken once. Never again. Not by choice—it simply *cannot be repeated*. Whatever shape it takes, it takes once, and then it belongs to the moment that demanded it." He opened his eyes. "Which means I cannot teach it to you. No one can. I can teach you the other two until you can speak them in your sleep. I can teach you the grammar of the drowned tongue, the cadences, the prices. But if the day comes when you need the third oath, you will be standing alone with it, composing it while the world burns, and no lesson of mine will help you."

Theo sat with that. Outside, the light was failing, and somewhere across the drowned city the thorn was doing whatever thorns did in the dark—growing, whispering, waiting.

"So everything you're teaching me," they said slowly, "is preparation for a test you can't describe, given by a teacher who doesn't exist."

"When you put it that way, the priesthood sounds much more honest than it was." Wren gathered his sticks and scraps, then paused. "One more thing. The most important thing, and I'll thank you to actually hear it. The oaths are not weapons. Everyone treats them like weapons—your family, the crown, the people who want them, the people who fear them. They're not. They're *promises*. A promise is just a sentence that costs you if you break it. An oath is a sentence that costs you for keeping it. The difference between a speaker and a monster is not power. It's whether you understand, going in, exactly what the sentence will cost—and choose to pay it anyway, with open eyes, for something worth the price." He pointed the stick at Theo's chest. "Say it back."

"A promise costs you if you break it. An oath costs you for keeping it."

"Again."

"Wren—"

"*Again.*"

"A promise costs you if you break it. An oath costs you for keeping it." Theo rubbed their eyes. "Happy?"

"Deliriously." He shuffled toward the door, then stopped, half in the gold light. "Rest tonight. Truly rest. Tomorrow we begin the middle third of the Binding, and it requires a clear head, because tomorrow you'll learn why your grandmother's generation called it the Weeping Verse." His smile flickered, there and gone. "And Theo—if you hear whispering in the night, from the direction of the water, don't answer it. Especially if it uses your name. Especially if it sounds kind."

Then he was gone, down the stairs and into the dusk, leaving Theo alone with a broken pew, a browning page, and the arithmetic of their own remaining years.

* * *

Sleep did not come easily. Sleep, in Vaelmora, was a negotiation.

Theo lay on a pallet in the vestry and listened to the city breathe. That was the only word for it—at high tide the drowned streets filled, and the water moved through them in long slow exhalations, and beneath the exhale there was the other sound, the one Wren had warned about. A whispering. Not words, exactly. More like the memory of words, the way a shell holds the memory of the sea.

*Theo.*

They kept their eyes closed. *Don't answer it.*

*Theo Villalobos. Little keeper. Little accountant.*

It sounded kind. That was the worst part. It sounded like a grandmother's voice heard through a wall—warm, amused, patient beyond all measure. It sounded like something that had waited three hundred years and could wait three hundred more, and knew it, and found the waiting funny.

*I knew your grandmother's grandmother,* the whisper said. *Shall I tell you what she was like? Shall I tell you what they took from me, and what they took it *from*?*

Theo pressed their palms against their ears, which was useless, because the voice wasn't coming through their ears. It was coming up through the pallet, through the stone, through the tide itself.

*Come down at low tide,* it murmured, almost drowsily now, a lullaby with hooks in it. *I'll show you the ledger. I'll show you both sides of it. Your side and theirs. Then you can decide whose accountant you'd like to be.*

And then, mercifully, the tide turned, and the whisper sank with the water, down and down, into whatever deep place it kept itself, and Theo slept without dreaming, which after that was its own small mercy.

* * *

Morning brought Sefa, banging through the chapel door with the subtlety of a landslide.

"Your priest," she announced, "owes me a new stylus, because mine snapped in half when I saw what he's been paying the ferryman with." She dropped onto the pew beside Theo, close enough that Theo could smell marsh and woodsmoke on her, and studied the oath-page with the frank greed of a cartographer confronting an unmapped coastline. "So. This is it? The famous homework?" Her finger hovered over the first line without touching it. "Does it say anything useful, or is it more of your family's specialty—important-looking nonsense?"

"It says I can trade thirty years of my life for the privilege of putting a very old plant back to sleep."

Sefa snorted. "Terrible rate. I've negotiated better tolls for mud tracks." But her eyes stayed on the page a moment longer than mockery required, and when she looked up there was something careful in her face. "There were soldiers at the marsh edge yesterday. Crown gray, surveyor's badges, asking after pilgrims. Asking after a pilgrim in particular—young, traveling alone, flinches at blessings." She said it lightly, the way she said everything, but her knee was bouncing. "I told them I hadn't seen anyone like that. Which was true, technically, since I described you terribly on purpose."

Theo's stomach dropped through the floor, through the chapel, into the black water below. "Gray uniform. Tall? A scar through his left eyebrow?"

"You know him."

"He mapped our valley for a month." Theo stood, and the wooden sword at their hip—still wooden, still silent, though it hadn't felt entirely silent since the day it hummed—seemed suddenly very light and very insufficient. "His name is Ren Ostrev. And if he's found the marsh road, then he isn't mapping trade routes anymore. He's closing a ledger of his own."

Above them, through the broken roof, the morning sky hung pale and high and empty, and somewhere out along the causeway, faint but unmistakable, the thorn whispered a single delighted syllable that might have been a name, and might have been a welcome, and might have been a warning:

*Soon.*
