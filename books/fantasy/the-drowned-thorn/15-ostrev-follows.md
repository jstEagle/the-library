## 15. Ostrev Follows

The marsh began as a rumor and ended as a fact.

Captain Ren Ostrev had learned to read rumors the way other men read weather: a shift in the wind of conversation, a cloud gathering in a merchant's evasions, the particular damp that settled on a town when someone had passed through it who did not wish to be remembered. For eleven days he had followed that damp east from Thornvale—through Harrow Fen, through the charcoal-burners' camps, through a village so small it had no name and only one opinion about crown soldiers, which they expressed by closing their shutters.

Now the ground itself turned traitor beneath his boots. Sphagnum sucked at each step. Black water stood in the hollows like spilled ink, and the reeds grew taller than a mounted man, whispering among themselves in a language of dry paper. Somewhere out in all that gray-green breathing space was a speaker. A real one. The last one, if the old records were to be believed, though Ostrev had stopped believing records sometime around the moment he'd watched a woman in Thornvale lie to his face with such gentle, grandmotherly skill that he'd almost apologized to *her*.

He thought about Ilsa Villalobos more than he liked. Not because she had deceived him—he expected deception the way he expected rain—but because she had deceived him kindly. There had been no fear in her, or rather, there had been fear, but she had carried it the way a good soldier carries a wound: quietly, without letting it slow the work. He respected that. Respect made the whole business uglier.

"Captain." Sergeant Dree came up beside him, mud to her knees, her crossbow wrapped in oilcloth against the wet. She was the only member of his survey company who still pretended this was a survey. "Scouts found tracks at the third causeway. Small boots. Recent."

"Small boots," Ostrev repeated.

"And a second set. Larger. They walk together for a while, then the small set goes on alone toward the water."

Ostrev looked out at the marsh. The sun was doing its best behind a screen of cloud, laying a dull pewter shine over everything, so that the whole world looked like the inside of a blade. "A child traveling with a pilgrim band," he said. "Then leaving it."

"That would be my reading, sir."

"Pilgrims don't usually abandon their children in a marsh, Sergeant."

"No, sir. But children sometimes leave bands that are going somewhere the child can't follow." Dree said it carefully, the way you hand back something fragile you've been asked to hold. She had a nephew, Ostrev knew. Eight years old. He tried not to know things like that; they were useless on campaign and heavy besides.

"Where is the band now?"

"Half a day south, heading for the shrine road. We could take them for questioning."

"We could," Ostrev said. "And we'd learn nothing, and we'd frighten every honest soul between here and the coast, and our speaker would be another mile gone." He turned. "We follow the small boots."

Dree hesitated. It was a very small hesitation, measured in the tightening of her jaw rather than any word, but Ostrev had commanded soldiers long enough to read hesitation the way he read rumors. "Sir," she said, "the child. If it's a child we find."

"Then we find a child."

"And if the child is gifted?"

Ostrev looked at her. Dree was thirty-one years old, competent, loyal, and had once wept over a dead mule in a way she would deny to her grave. He had chosen her for this company precisely because she was not cruel. That choice had seemed wise once.

"The writ says anyone with the gift is to be taken alive for assessment," he said. "Assessment happens in the capital. That is the law, and we are not in the business of improving upon the law. Is that understood?"

"Understood, sir." Her relief was quiet and complete, and it shamed him a little, which was interesting. He filed the feeling away with the others—the ones that had been accumulating since Thornvale like silt in a harbor—and moved out.

* * *

They found the child near dusk, at the place where the marsh finally gave up pretending to be land and became open water studded with drowned trees. The boy—it was a boy, perhaps ten, maybe eleven, all elbows and wariness—was crouched on the root-knuckle of an alder, drinking from cupped hands, and he heard them coming long before they saw him. Of course he did. Children hear everything. It is one of the great injustices of growing up, how much of that hearing gets taken away.

He bolted, naturally. He made it perhaps forty yards through the reeds before Dree's men closed the circle, and then he simply stopped, planted himself in the mud, and looked up at Ostrev with an expression that Ostrev recognized with a lurch he refused to name. It was the expression of someone deciding, very fast, whether to beg or to bite.

"Easy," Ostrev said. He raised both hands, empty, palms out—an old gesture, older than the crown, older than most laws. "No one's going to hurt you. What's your name?"

The boy said nothing. His eyes went from face to face, counting, cataloguing, finding exits. Mud to his waist. Reeds flattened behind him in a line that said he'd been walking since morning. Alone. Walking east, alone, away from the pilgrims, away from everyone, at ten years old, in a marsh full of things that ate careless travelers.

"You left your group," Ostrev said. "The pilgrims. Why?"

Still nothing. But the boy's right hand had curled closed, and Ostrev watched it curl, and felt the day tilt.

"Open your hand, son."

"I didn't do anything."

"I believe you. Open your hand anyway."

The boy opened it. In his palm lay a smooth river stone, gray-white, ordinary, except that it was glowing. Faintly—a light like the last coals of a fire, like a firefly caught under cloth, like nothing a stone had any business doing. The boy stared at it too, with an expression that was not pride and not defiance but something much worse: bewilderment. He didn't understand it either. Whatever lived in him had woken recently, and it woke things around him, and he had picked up a stone that glowed the way some children pick up a stick that turns out to be a snake.

"It started doing that yesterday," the boy said. His voice cracked in the middle of the sentence, half child, half something younger. "I didn't ask it to. I put it down and it kept doing it. I thought if I brought it far enough away—" He stopped. Swallowed. Looked up. "Are you the ones from the posters?"

Ostrev felt the marsh go very quiet around him. Even the reeds seemed to lean in.

"What posters?" Dree asked, gently, from behind him.

"The ones in Harrow Fen. With the eye on them. My gran said if anyone asks, say nothing, run east, don't stop till the water." The boy's chin came up, and now there was defiance after all, arriving late and thin, holding up a collapsing building. "I'm not telling you anything else."

There it was. Run east. The same direction, Ostrev noted with a cold settling in his stomach, that the tracks from Thornvale pointed. The same direction the drowned city lay in, according to maps so old they were more legend than cartography. Someone had told this child to run east, and someone had told—or taught, or merely failed to prevent—the speaker he was hunting to do the same. East. Toward Vaelmora. Toward whatever slept out there in the black water wearing a crown of thorn.

"Take the stone," Ostrev said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Wrap it. Don't touch it bare-handed."

Dree took it. The glow died the moment it left the boy's palm, and the boy flinched as if something had been pulled out of him—which, Ostrev supposed, in a sense it had.

"He comes with us," Ostrev said. "Alive. Assessment in the capital. That's the law."

"Yes, sir." Dree moved to take the boy's arm, and the boy shrank back against the alder root, and made a sound—not a word, just a small animal sound of pure refusal—and the stone, twenty feet away in its wrapping in Dree's pack, flickered once through the canvas like a heartbeat.

Everyone saw it. There was no unseeing it.

* * *

That night they camped on the last dry hummock before the true deepwater, and Ostrev sat apart with the writ and did not read it.

He knew what it said. He had helped draft the language, two years ago, in a committee room that smelled of ink and lilies, back when the hunt had been an abstraction with a budget. *All persons manifesting the gift shall be conveyed to the capital for assessment.* Assessment. A word chosen by lawyers because it sounded like arithmetic instead of cages. He had signed it believing—still believed—that the realm needed to know who carried the gift and how many and how strong, because the alternative was not knowing, and not knowing was how cities burned. He had seen a city burn. He had been fourteen years old, and his brother Tam had been nineteen and beautiful and certain, and Tam had stood in a crowd of the certain and cheered when the gifted rose up in Callowmere, and Tam had died in the second week when the uprising's leaders—people Tam had trusted, people who had promised protection—had used the square as a weapon. Three hundred dead in an afternoon. Half of them killed by the gifted, half by the soldiers who came after, and by the end there was no telling which half was which, and Tam had been buried in a grave so shallow the dogs—

Ostrev set the writ down.

The boy sat by the fire between two soldiers who were trying very hard not to look at him. Someone had given him stew. He was eating it in the methodical, desperate way of a child who has learned not to trust that food will keep coming. His feet, washed, were blistered from three days of walking. Three days. Ten years old. East, alone, because his grandmother had loved him enough to send him away from her.

*Run east.* To Vaelmora. Where the thorn waited. Where, if even a fraction of the old stories held, a speaker might find the oaths that could wake or bind or unmake the thing that had drowned a kingdom.

Or where a child might simply drown, alone, in the dark, following instructions given by someone who loved him and could not come along.

"Captain." Dree crouched beside him, voice low. "Dispatch rider's back. Orders from the survey office, countersigned by the Ministry." She handed over a folded paper, sealed with the eye.

Ostrev broke the seal. Read it twice, because the first time he assumed he had misread it.

*By order of the Crown: persons manifesting the gift within the eastern provinces are hereby remanded to field assessment. Field assessment may be conducted by any officer of rank captain or above. Conveyance to the capital is suspended until further notice.*

Field assessment. Four words doing the work of a gallows. Somebody in the Ministry had done the arithmetic—too many gifted, too few wagons, too far to march them—and had solved the problem with vocabulary. Any officer of rank captain or above. He was the only captain within a hundred miles of this marsh.

"They're not bringing them in anymore," Dree said. She had read it over his shoulder, or guessed it; her face was carefully blank in the firelight. "What does assessment mean, out here? Practically?"

"It means whatever the assessing officer decides it means."

"And what will you decide?"

Ostrev looked at the fire. Beyond it, the boy had fallen asleep sitting up, mid-chew almost, tipped sideways against a soldier's pack, and the soldier—a big, soft-faced man named Corvel—had adjusted the pack so the boy wouldn't slide into the mud, and was now looking anywhere except at his captain, daring him with silence to make this into something.

"In Thornvale," Ostrev said slowly, "an old woman lied to me for three days. Kindly. Skillfully. And I let myself believe her, because believing her was easier, and because part of me wanted the valley to be clean. Empty. Safe." He folded the orders along their existing creases. "I have spent two years telling myself that what happened in Callowmere was a failure of vigilance. That if we watch closely enough, count accurately enough, assess thoroughly enough, it can never happen again. That knowledge is the wall between the realm and the fire."

"And now?"

"Now I've been handed orders that replace the wall with a ditch." He stood. "Get some sleep, Sergeant. We move at first light. The speaker's trail goes into the deepwater, and I want eyes on the causeway before the tide turns."

Dree lingered. "And the boy?"

"The boy travels with us. Alive." He said it firmly enough that she nodded and went. Then, alone, quieter, to the fire: "For now."

He hated the sound of those two words in his own mouth. He examined the hatred, turned it over, and found underneath it not conscience exactly but something adjacent—fear. Not fear of the boy. Fear of the man the orders wanted him to become, and the discovery, unwelcome as a knife between ribs, that some part of him was willing. That Tam's grave was still shallow, and grief was patient, and patience curdles.

* * *

It happened on the fourth morning, at the drowned chapel.

The structure rose out of the marsh like a ribcage—roofless, its bell tower snapped off at the shoulder, the flood having claimed it a century ago and never given it back. The scouts wanted to search it; speakers, Ostrev reasoned, might shelter there. He went in with Dree and two men while the rest held the perimeter, and the boy stayed outside between guards, wrists bound with cord, because Ostrev had ordered it and had not let himself think too hard about why.

Inside, the water stood knee-deep and mirror-still. A stone font floated its own reflection. And carved into the wall above the font, worn nearly smooth, was a blessing in the old tongue—one of the handful of phrases every child in the western provinces used to learn, back before learning it became suspicious. *May the water remember you kindly.*

The boy should not have been able to see it from outside. The angle was wrong; the wall faced inward.

But when they came out, the boy was staring at the chapel wall through the broken doorway with an expression of absolute horror, and the air around him tasted—there was no other word for it—*charged*, like the moment before lightning, like the hush under a stormcloud, and the standing water inside the chapel had begun, very softly, to sing. A single sustained note, high and clear, rising.

"She read it to me," the boy said. Nobody had asked him anything. "My gran. Every night. May the water remember you kindly. She said it was just words, it was just a story, but I could always—I could always feel them, they were *heavy*, they had weight, and I thought everyone felt that, I thought—" His breath hitched. The note from the chapel climbed. The guards nearest him stepped back, and one of them, a young man named Pell, drew his blade halfway out of pure animal fear, and Ostrev saw it happen the way you see a cart start to slip its brake: slowly, and then all at once.

"Put it up," Ostrev said. "Pell. Put it *up*."

But Pell was looking at the singing water and the glowing boy and the shallow graves of Callowmere, which lived in every soldier's memory whether they'd earned them or not, and the boy saw the blade and panicked—threw up his bound hands, shouted a word that was not quite a word, more a shape of sound, and the water in the chapel leapt. All of it. A sheet of black marsh-water rising out of the doorway like a wing, hanging there for one impossible instant against the gray sky, glittering, beautiful, terrible.

Then it fell. And in the falling, in the chaos of men stumbling backward and shields coming up and Dree shouting, the boy ran.

He ran badly—wrists bound, boots sodden, ten years old—but he ran toward the deepwater, toward the alder roots and the reed banks, toward east, toward gran's instructions, and Ostrev shouted halt and the boy did not halt, because why would he, why on this drowning earth would he stop for the people with the blades, and Pell—Pell, young and terrified and thinking of fires—loosed his crossbow.

The bolt took the boy in the back, below the shoulder blade, and he pitched forward into the shallow water at the edge of the deep, and the singing stopped everywhere at once, and the marsh went silent in a way Ostrev had never heard a marsh go silent, as if the reeds themselves were holding their breath.

They reached him fast. Too fast to matter. Dree knelt in the water and gathered him up and pressed her hands over the wound and said his name—she didn't know his name, she said *easy, easy, son*—and the boy looked past all of them, past the soldiers and the sky, at something in the middle distance that only he could see, and he said, clearly, wonderingly, as if reporting a discovery:

"It doesn't hurt. Why doesn't it—"

And then the weight went out of him, all at once, the way the glow had gone out of the stone, and Dree was kneeling in the water holding a child who weighed suddenly nothing at all, and the marsh birds, after a long moment, resumed their noise, because the marsh did not care, because the marsh had drowned kingdoms and would drown this too.

* * *

They buried him on the hummock, under an alder, because the ground there was firmest. Dree dug. Ostrev dug. Nobody spoke much. Corvel laid the river stone—they had unwrapped it, and it was only a stone now, gray and dumb and ordinary—on the grave, and Pell stood at the edge of the little gathering with his crossbow slung and his face the color of ash, and when Ostrev looked at him, Pell said, "Captain, I—" and could not finish, and Ostrev found he had nothing to give him. No absolution. No condemnation. Just the same shallow grave in his own chest, and Tam's name in it.

"He was running east," Dree said at last, standing over the fresh-turned earth. Her voice was flat in the way voices get when feeling has been locked out for safekeeping. "Same as the speaker. Same direction. Someone sent them both."

"Someone did."

"And the orders say field assessment." She turned and looked at him, and behind the blankness her eyes were doing arithmetic of their own. "You're the assessing officer, Captain. Out here, you're the only law there is. So I'm asking you plainly, and I'll only ask once: what happens when we catch the speaker? The one from Thornvale. The one who can wake or bind the thorn. Do we bring them in?"

Ostrev looked east. The marsh stretched away into haze, black water and green reed and drowned trees, and somewhere out in all of it, a drowned city, and a thing with thorns, and the last person alive who could speak to it. The Ministry wanted that person assessed. The Amarel sympathizers in the archives wanted them found. The old stories wanted them bound. And a boy lay under an alder because the realm had decided that words like *assessment* could carry bolts in them.

"When we catch them," Ostrev said, "we bring them in. Alive. To the capital. Whatever the orders say." He picked up his pack. "Because if the crown wants this one dead, Sergeant, then the crown had better look me in the eye and say so with its own mouth. I won't be the vocabulary it hides behind."

Dree studied him for a long moment. "And if they refuse to come?"

"Then I'll offer terms. Everyone has terms. Even speakers." He turned east and began to walk, and the company fell in behind him, and the marsh received them the way it received everything: silently, and without forgiveness.

Behind them, on the small mound under the alder, the river stone sat in the mud. And as the last soldier passed out of sight, it glowed—once, faintly, like a candle relit by a passing breath—then darkened, and waited, and kept, in its own mute mineral way, a kind of vigil.

Far out in the deepwater, something ancient felt the small death ripple across its drowned kingdom, and turned its attention, slow as a season, toward the marsh edge.

Toward the trail. Toward the hunters.

Toward the one who was coming, whose blood it knew, whose name it had already begun to learn.
