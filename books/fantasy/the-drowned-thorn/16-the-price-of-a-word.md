## 16. The Price of a Word

The chapel flooded to the height of a man's chest in springtime, which meant that in autumn it was merely damp, and in winter it froze into something Wren Calloway called "the finest ice cathedral in Vaelmora, though admittedly the competition is limited."

Theo had come to think of it as home, which was a strange thing to feel about a building where God had drowned.

They stood now at the altar rail, morning light falling through the broken roof in slanted columns, and Wren was watching them with that particular expression he wore when he was about to say something Theo would hate.

"You've been practicing," he said.

"I've been *saying words*," Theo said. "There's a difference. One of those is practice. The other is what I do when I can't sleep and the thorn won't stop humming."

"And which is this?" He nodded at the wooden practice sword—Theo's old companion from Thornvale, carried across half a realm like a child dragging a blanket—where it lay across the rail. The blade was humming. Low, continuous, like a struck bell that hadn't decided to stop.

Theo looked at it. Looked away. "That's nothing."

"Theo."

"It hummed before. In Thornvale. Once." They picked up the sword and the hum deepened, settled into their palm like a cat finding its spot. "It's just... remembering me."

"Words remember nothing," Wren said gently. "Words are remembered *into*. You've been feeding it. Every syllable you've spoken in my lessons—the Binding fragments, the names of the old speakers—you haven't been learning them. You've been *loading* them." He came down the aisle, boots splashing shallow water, and stopped an arm's length away. "Which means we have a problem, because you're going to need to speak the full Oath eventually, and you don't yet know what it costs."

"I know what it costs. You told me. Years of life. A word, a year. You were very clear. I did the arithmetic on the walk here, actually, and I'd like to register a formal complaint about the exchange rate."

Wren didn't smile. That was the thing about Wren; he could be funny about anything except the things that mattered, and then he became so solemn he looked like a painting of himself.

"Sit down," he said.

"There's nowhere dry to sit."

"Sit down anyway."

* * *

They sat on the third pew from the back, the one that still had most of its seat, and Wren laid out his materials on the wood between them: a stub of candle, a knife, a strip of linen, and a small clay pot of ash mixed with oil that smelled like churches and funerals, which Theo supposed was accurate for both.

"What's all this?"

"A measurement," Wren said. "I want you to speak one word of the Binding. A single word. And I want to watch what happens to you while you do it."

"One word. One year." Theo turned the sword over in their lap. "Wren, I'm twenty-three years old. I've spent—" they counted, wincing, "—four words already. On the road. Accidents, mostly. The word that stopped the river. The one that opened Ostrev's lockbox by mistake, which I still maintain was worth it, because the man keeps his correspondence in there and I learned *so much*."

"Four years," Wren said quietly. "You're twenty-seven, then, in every way that matters. Your blood is twenty-seven. Your bones. The thorn doesn't take your memories or your face—not at first—but it takes the *substance*, Theo. The marrow of you. And it takes more than a year for some words. The greater oaths aren't priced per syllable. They're priced per meaning."

Theo looked at him for a long moment.

"How much is the full Binding?"

"I don't know," Wren said. "No one alive knows. The last speaker who spoke it whole was your grandfather, and he went into the binding at forty and came out—" he hesitated, choosing, and Theo watched him choose, "—old. Old old. White-haired, bent, forgetting names. He lived another six years and I buried him myself, and I was a young man, and everyone said how natural it was, how he'd simply aged. But I did sums too, even then. Even before I understood." He met Theo's eyes. "I think it took him thirty years. Maybe forty. For one oath."

The chapel dripped. Somewhere out in the marsh, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, which Sefa swore was a heron and Theo swore was a ghost, and they had agreed to disagree permanently.

"So if I speak it," Theo said slowly, "to bind the thorn during the eclipse—if I stand up in front of the Amarels and the crown and whatever else comes crawling out of the century, and I do the one thing I was born to do—I could stand up an old woman. Or not stand up at all."

"Yes."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan anyone has ever had," Wren said. "Which is why I want to measure. Because if we know the true price, we can decide whether it's payable. And if it isn't—" he picked up the knife, turned it over, set it down again, "—then we find another way. There's always another way. It just usually costs something else instead."

"That's very comforting."

"I'm a defrocked priest. Comfort is above my pay grade. Which is zero."

Despite everything, Theo laughed—a short, startled bark of a laugh—and Wren allowed himself half a smile, the way a man allows himself one biscuit on a fast day.

"All right," Theo said. "One word. Measure me."

* * *

The preparation was absurdly simple, which should have been Theo's first warning. Wren marked three lines on the inside of their forearm with the ash-oil—three thin grey strokes, like tally marks—and wrapped the linen loosely around their wrist.

"What are these for?"

"The lines will darken. Or fade. Or move. I don't know exactly—that's rather the point of measuring. The linen is to catch blood, if there's blood. There's usually blood with the old words. Your grandfather bled from the eyes, once, speaking the second verse."

"Wonderful. I'll look forward to that."

"Choose your word," Wren said. "Something small. A binding-word, but minor. The word that holds a door shut. The word that keeps a cup from spilling. Nothing grand."

Theo closed their eyes and reached inward, down past the fear and the arithmetic, to the place where the gift lived—which was, they had discovered, shaped almost exactly like the valley of Thornvale. Quiet. Green-shadowed. Full of words sleeping under moss like stones under turf.

They found the word for *hold*.

Not the great holding, the world-binding holding, the holding that had drowned a kingdom when it broke. Just the small domestic cousin of it. The word a speaker might use to keep a shutter closed in wind. It sat in their chest like a pebble warmed by sun.

"Ready?" Wren asked.

"No," Theo said honestly. "But I've been not-ready for twenty-three years and it hasn't improved anything. Go."

They spoke the word.

It left them like breath in winter—visible, somehow, a pale ribbon of sound hanging in the chapel air—and the wooden sword across their knees sang one clear note, and the ribbon of sound curled around the practice blade and *held*, and for one shining instant Theo felt the pure rightness of it, the thing they'd been made for, the purpose sewn into their blood before their grandmother ever lied to them about who they were—

And then the bill arrived.

It came up through their feet first, through the pew, through the stone, as if the chapel itself were collecting a debt on the thorn's behalf. A coldness. Then a hollowness, like a tooth pulled from the jaw of the year. Theo felt a piece of themselves go—not pain, precisely, worse than pain, a *subtraction*—a year of marrow, a year of the fat of youth, a year of the easy resilience that lets a body bounce off floors and forgive itself by morning. They felt it leave the way you feel a coin leave your hand in a crowd: instantly, irretrievably, with the sick certainty that you will never see it again.

The three ash-lines on their forearm darkened to black.

Blood welled along the middle line, slow and orderly, as if the word had signed its receipt.

Then it was over. The ribbon of light faded. The sword went quiet. Theo sat on the wet pew in the drowned chapel of Vaelmora, breathing hard, feeling simultaneously twenty-six years old and ninety.

"Well," they managed. "That was rude."

"Theo." Wren's voice had gone strange. "Look at your hands."

Theo looked at their hands.

They were still their hands. Mostly. But the knuckles had changed—the smoothness gone from them, the skin a little looser over the joints, a faint map of veins showing blue where yesterday the skin had been opaque and unbothered. And when Theo raised their arms, the motion had a faint new creak in it, a whisper of stiffness at the shoulder, the kind of thing they'd noticed in Ilsa's movements and never in their own.

Never before today.

"Oh," Theo said. Very small.

"It shows in the face fastest," Wren said quietly. He was looking at them with an expression Theo couldn't read—grief and scientific attention fighting for the same territory. "Here." He fished in his coat and produced a small hand mirror, silver-backed, tarnished at the rim. "Your grandfather kept this. He used it to watch himself spend. I never understood why until now. I think—" he paused, "—I think he wanted to see exactly what each word bought. So he could decide, every time, whether it was worth it."

Theo took the mirror.

The face that looked back was theirs. It was also, unmistakably, one year older than the face that had looked back that morning. Faint lines at the corners of the eyes that hadn't been there at breakfast. A certain settling around the mouth. Nothing dramatic. Nothing a stranger would notice. But Theo knew their own face the way they knew the shape of the valley, and the difference was there, written small and permanent, like a watermark.

One word. One year. Visible.

"Four words on the road," Theo said. Their voice sounded far away. "Did I—was I changing then, too? And I just didn't—"

"You were changing slowly enough that no one sees it day to day. That's the mercy of it. Or the cruelty. Hard to say." Wren took the mirror back gently. "Now you know. Now you can count."

"The arithmetic of power," Theo said bitterly. "Every word a withdrawal. And the account doesn't refill."

"No. It doesn't."

They sat together in the dripping silence. Out in the marsh the heron-ghost cried its rusty cry. Water ticked down through the broken roof onto the altar, patient as a clock.

"Wren," Theo said at last. "When the eclipse comes. When I speak the full Binding. How old am I going to walk out of that?"

He was quiet for a long time.

"I don't know," he said finally. "But I'll tell you what I told your grandmother, the night she asked me the same question about your grandfather." He looked at the mirror in his hands, turning it over and over. "I told her: a long life that ends in chains isn't longer than a short one that ends free. She threw a boot at me. She had excellent aim. I still think about that boot."

"Was it worth saying?"

"No," Wren admitted. "But someone had to say it, and it turned out to be me, and that's the job. Someone has to be the person who says the true thing at the wrong moment. I've built an entire vocation out of it."

Theo laughed again, weaker this time, and found that the laugh hurt somewhere under the ribs—in the hollowed place where the year had been. Everything hurt there now, a little. They suspected everything always would.

* * *

Sefa found them an hour later, sitting on the chapel steps with the practice sword across their knees, watching the tide come in over the drowned streets.

She took one look at them and stopped walking.

"What happened to your face?"

"My face?"

"You've got—" she gestured vaguely at her own sharp features, circling her eyes, her mouth, "—*history*. You look like you slept badly for a decade and enjoyed it."

"Thank you, Sefa. Truly, your tenderness sustains me."

"I'm serious." She dropped down beside them on the step, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was how Sefa said things she didn't want to say out loud. She studied their face with a cartographer's eye, the eye that measured coastlines and caught errors in old charts, and Theo watched her arrive at the answer before they could decide whether to give it.

"It's the words, isn't it," she said. "The ones you've spoken. It takes it out of you and it *shows*."

"One word, one year," Theo said. "Plus interest, apparently, in the form of creaky shoulders."

Sefa was silent. Then, unexpectedly, she reached over and took their hand—the one holding the sword—and turned it over, examining the knuckles, the veins, the faint new age of it, with the same careful attention she gave to charts of dangerous waters.

"My mother's hands look like this," she said. "She worked the dye-works in Corvess for thirty years. The chemicals got into the skin and never left." She looked up. "You've got a dyeworker's hands, Theo Villalobos. Except your chemical is *words*."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Don't make jokes. I'm being sincere and it's exhausting." But she didn't let go of their hand. "How many have you spent? Total?"

"Five, now. Four before today, one this morning. A small one. A test."

"Five years." Sefa did her own arithmetic, quick and merciless. "So somewhere out there is a version of you who gets those five years back, and she's probably having a much better time. Sleeping in. Eating breakfast without a priest hovering."

"She's welcome to them," Theo said. "I wasn't using them well anyway."

"You were using them *fine*." Sefa's grip tightened, just slightly. "That's the whole point. You were using them fine, and now you don't get to anymore, and nobody asked you whether that trade was fair. Nobody asked any of us." She stared out at the water rising green and slow through the drowned lanes, swallowing doorways one by one. "My family's in debtors' prison in Corvess. Did you know that? Three years now. My father's health is bad. Every coin I earn goes to lawyers who promise everything and deliver letters." She stopped. Closed her mouth. Opened it again. "Forget it. That's not—today's about you."

"Sefa—"

"Today's about you," she repeated firmly, in the tone of a woman nailing a door shut, and Theo let it go, because you had to let people nail doors shut sometimes, and trust that they'd open them later on their own.

They sat together as the tide climbed the steps below them, one, two, three, the water patient and green and full of drowned bells.

"Wren wants to run the full lesson tonight," Theo said at last. "The whole Binding, start to finish. Not speaking it—just mapping it. Counting the words. Working out the price before the eclipse forces us to pay it blind."

"And how many words is the full Binding?"

"Forty-one."

Sefa whistled low. "Forty-one years. You'd be sixty-four."

"If it's priced evenly. Wren says the greater verses cost more. Could be eighty. Could be—" Theo didn't finish it.

Below them, the water reached the fifth step, and somewhere beneath the surface, far down in the flooded heart of Vaelmora, the thorn pulsed once against the walls of Theo's blood—slow, attentive, listening—the way it always did when they talked about prices. As if it were checking its ledger too. As if it already knew the total better than they did.

Sefa felt it too—he saw her feel it, saw her glance sharply at his chest as though the sound had come from there, which, in fairness, it had.

"Theo," she said slowly, "what did it just do?"

And Theo, who had spent five years of a life learning when to lie and when not to, opened their mouth to reassure her—

—and heard, rising faintly through the flood-waters all around them, from every drowned street and sunken tower of Vaelmora at once, the thorn beginning to hum their name.
