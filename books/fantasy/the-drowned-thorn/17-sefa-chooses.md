## 17. Sefa Chooses

Sefa had drawn maps for three different governments, two trading houses, one smuggler who paid in cheese, and a bishop who wanted the coastline of a neighboring diocese made slightly less flattering. She knew what it was to hold a piece of paper that could ruin someone. She had simply never expected to be on the wrong end of her own cartography.

The map lay between them on the floor of the ruined watchtower, weighted at its corners with four stones and one very confused toad that Wren had shooed off twice already.

Theo hadn't moved in a long time.

They sat across from each other the way people sit when there is a fire burning quietly in the middle of the room — not close, not far, both of them aware of the heat. Outside, the marsh breathed its evening breath, the tide going out with a sound like an old man clearing his throat before telling you something you won't like.

"You went through my things," Sefa said. Not accusing. Just establishing the geography of the situation, which was, after all, her profession.

"The map fell out," Theo said. "When your pack tipped over. I was putting it back and I saw the seal."

"The crown seal."

"The crown seal."

Sefa nodded slowly. "Well," she said. "That's that, then." She reached for the wineskin, remembered it was empty, and reached for it again anyway, because some gestures are about comfort rather than results.

"Say something," Theo said.

"I'm thinking of something. Give me a moment. It's a long walk from *I took coin from the people hunting you* to anything resembling an explanation, and I'd rather not say the first thing that arrives, because the first thing that arrives is usually dressed badly."

Theo almost smiled. Almost. It was the kind of almost-smile Sefa had learned to read like contour lines: it meant the ground beneath was treacherous.

So she started talking, because if she stopped she might never start, and because the truth, like a river delta, only makes sense if you follow it all the way down.

* * *

"My father kept a ferry at Corren's Landing," she said. "Small crossing, muddy water, mostly farmers and the occasional tax collector we pretended not to see. He was good at it. Forty years on the same hundred yards of river. You'd think a man would get bored, but he used to say the river was never the same twice, so neither was the crossing." She picked at a loose thread on her sleeve. "Then the new toll roads came, and the bridge upstream, and nobody needed a ferryman who couldn't pay the harbor levy, and the levy went up every year like a fever, and one winter my mother's lungs went bad and the physic's bill went up faster than the levy, and—" She spread her hands. "Debtors' prison. Both of them. My brother too, because he was sixteen and they count sixteen as old enough to owe."

"How much?" Theo asked.

"Two hundred crowns bought out all three. Three hundred bought the debt *and* the record, so they could leave Corren's Landing afterward and not have the stain follow them to every town with a ledger-keeper and a grudge." She met Theo's eyes. "A stranger found me in a tavern in Marrow's Ford. Well-dressed. Soft hands. The kind of soft hands that have never held anything heavier than a pen. He said he needed a guide to Vaelmora. Someone who could read the marsh, time the tides, keep quiet. He paid half up front. Crown mint, fresh-stamped. I counted it twice because I didn't believe it the first time."

"And you didn't ask who he was guiding."

"I asked. He said it wasn't my business, and I decided he was right, because it wasn't, because I had two parents and a brother sitting in a stone room eating bread that was mostly sawdust, and my business was getting them out." Her voice did something complicated on the last words, and she let it. "You want to know the worst part? The truly humiliating part? When he told me where we were going, I felt *lucky*. Vaelmora. Nobody's mapped Vaelmora properly in two hundred years. There are scholars who would sell a kidney for the commission. And I stood there in that tavern thinking, *the fates have finally noticed me*, like some hero in a ballad, while all along I was just the bait on a hook, or the mule on a leash, or whatever the correct metaphor is for a person who leads someone else's wolf to someone else's door."

"You're not a metaphor," Theo said quietly. "You're a person who was scared."

"I'm a person who was *paid*," Sefa corrected. "Scared people deserve sympathy. Paid people deserve questions." She looked at the map again — her own fine lines, her own careful hand, the drowned city rendered in ink so lovingly it might as well have been a portrait of a friend. "And here's the question I've been asking myself since you looked at me like that. Since your face did the thing faces do when the floor opens. Did I know? Down deep, in the place where I keep the things I don't look at directly — did I know what I was guiding you into?"

Theo said nothing. Which was answer enough, and also no answer at all.

"Because here's what I actually knew," Sefa went on, counting on her fingers, a habit from inventory-taking that she couldn't shake. "One: he wanted Vaelmora, city of thorns and ghosts, which no honest man wants. Two: he flinched — actually flinched, I watched him do it — when a street priest blessed a fish-cart as we passed. Three: he asked me, twice, whether I'd ever traveled with anyone 'unusual.' Unusual. His word. And four—" she paused, and this finger took longer to raise than the others— "four, when I told him I'd found a companion on the pilgrim road, a quiet one, good with a wooden practice sword and flinchy around blessings, he smiled. He smiled like a man whose horse has come in. And I told myself it meant nothing. I told myself stories, Theo. I'm good at maps and I'm apparently even better at stories I tell myself."

The toad, having exhausted the entertainment value of the watchtower, hopped onto the map and sat down squarely on the Bay of Sorrows. Neither of them moved it.

"What happens now?" Theo asked. "Do you go back to him? Collect the other half?"

"Do you think I wouldn't? Do you think that thought hasn't been sitting on my chest since the night you showed me what you can do — since the sword hummed, since I realized what I'd been walking east with?" Sefa laughed, and it came out cracked. "Two hundred crowns more. That's what my family costs. That's the price tag. I could be in Marrow's Ford by week's end, and by month's end my father could be standing on his ferry again, complaining about the current, alive and free and ignorant of what his daughter sold to buy him."

"But?"

"*But*," Sefa said, and the word came out of her like something pried loose with tools. "But then I think about the child in the marsh road. The one Ostrev's soldiers—" She stopped. Started again. "I think about you, asleep by the fire, and how you sleep like a soldier, one hand open near your blade even though the blade is wood, and I think: this person trusted me. This person, who has every reason in the world to trust no one, looked at my sharp tongue and my decent maps and decided I was worth the risk. And I think about what the well-dressed man wants in Vaelmora, and I don't know exactly what it is, but I know it isn't good, because men with soft hands don't cross kingdoms for anything good."

She got up. Her knees complained; she ignored them, the way she ignored most of her body's opinions.

"So here's what happens now," she said. "I'm going to do the hardest thing I've ever done, and I've once talked my way past a customs officer using nothing but a hat and a fake limp."

"Sefa—"

"No. Let me finish, because if you interrupt me I'll lose my nerve, and I only have enough nerve for one speech per decade." She crouched, gripped the edge of the map, and looked at Theo one last time — really looked, the way she looked at terrain before committing a route to ink. "I'm sorry. Not the small sorry, the polite sorry, the sorry you put on a card. The other kind. The kind that costs. I took coin to bring you here, and I'll spend the rest of this road earning back the right to walk beside you, and if you never forgive me, that's fair, and I'll still walk beside you anyway, because two things can be true: I can owe you, and I can choose you. Now watch."

And she tore the map in half.

The sound it made was obscene — a clean rip straight through the drowned city, through towers she'd spent weeks reconstructing from fragments and rumor, through the channels she'd charted with a patience she usually reserved for grudges. She tore it again. And again. The toad fled with dignity.

"Sefa!" Theo was on their feet. "That's — you spent *months*—"

"Six months," Sefa agreed, letting the pieces fall. "And a stranger paid me to deliver you along it, which means every line I drew is a line he owns. So." She swept the pieces together, gathered them, and fed them one handful at a time into the little iron brazier Wren used for warming tea, where they curled and blackened and let go of the ink. The drowned city went up in a smell of burning linseed. "Now the only way to Vaelmora is through me. Alive, un-bought, and extremely annoyed."

Theo stared at the fire. Then at her. Their face did several things in sequence, none of which Sefa could have charted, ending somewhere in the neighborhood of undone.

"You tore up six months of work," Theo said.

"I did."

"To prove—"

"That I choose you over the coin. Yes. And before you say anything noble and forgiving, don't. Forgiveness is earned, not given, and I haven't earned it yet. What you can give me right now is a job." She jabbed a charred finger at them. "Because I may have burned the map, but I didn't burn the mapmaker, and everything I know is still in here." She tapped her temple. "Every channel, every tide-table, every sinking causeway and dry ridge in that whole drowned mess. He paid for what's in my head, and he's not going to get it. Instead, you're going to get it. Free of charge. On one condition."

"Name it."

"When this is over — however it ends, thorns and oaths and whatever Wren's cryptic little heart is hiding — you help me get my family somewhere the ledgers can't reach. A sanctuary. I hear rumors of places, up north, past the salt marches. People who've gone missing from the crown's lists. If they're real, I want to find them. For them. For everyone with a name on a list."

Theo extended a hand. Sefa took it. Her grip was a cartographer's grip — precise, strong, calibrated.

"Partners," Theo said. "Not debts. Partners."

"Partners," Sefa agreed. "And Theo? If you ever forgive me properly, I expect it to take years. Anything faster would insult us both."

* * *

Wren appeared at the doorway as the last of the map crumbled, wearing the expression of a man who had heard everything and would admit to none of it. He had a genius for arriving precisely after the dangerous part of any conversation.

"Tea?" he offered.

"We just destroyed a priceless historical document," Sefa said.

"Ah. Tea, then." He poured three cups from the dented kettle, and they drank sitting on the broken steps of the watchtower while the marsh turned silver and then dark. Somewhere out in the flats, the tide turned and began its slow crawl back toward Vaelmora, toward the black towers and the thing that slept among them whispering names.

Sefa found herself staring east. In her mind, unbidden, the map redrew itself — every line intact, every channel labeled, the whole drowned city blooming behind her eyes in perfect detail. They could burn the paper. They couldn't burn her.

Which meant, she realized, her cup halfway to her lips, that the well-dressed man with the soft hands had never needed the map at all. He'd needed her. And he would come looking for his purchase.

"Wren," she said slowly. "Your friend in Marrow's Ford. The one who hired me. Does he know my face?"

Wren set down his cup. The pause before he answered was short, but Sefa had spent a lifetime measuring distances, and she measured that one precisely.

"He knows everyone's face," Wren said. "It's rather his gift."

"Then we should assume he knows I've changed sides."

"It would be prudent."

"Prudent," Sefa repeated. She drained her tea, stood, and brushed the ash of her own handiwork from her skirts. "Wonderful. Theo — how attached are you to sleeping tonight? Because I think we should move camp, and I think we should move it now, and I think you should tell me everything Wren's been teaching you about oaths, because I have a terrible feeling we're going to need all of it before the tide comes back in."

Far to the east, faint as a heartbeat under the marsh grass, something answered her — a pulse in the dark, patient and vast, like a word waiting to be spoken.

Theo's hand went to the wooden sword at their hip. Even here, inside the border where magic could not follow, Sefa saw their knuckles whiten.

"It's awake," Theo whispered. "It knows we're coming."

"Well," said Sefa, shouldering her pack. "Let's not keep it waiting. I hate being late to anything I'm going to regret."
