## 18. The Thorn Speaks

The tide had turned an hour past dawn, and Vaelmora drank itself dry.

Theo stood on the roof of a drowned granary, watching the marsh water slide away from the city like a sheet being pulled off furniture in a house long abandoned. Tower by tower, the drowned city emerged—black stone furred with green, windows like empty eye sockets, and everywhere, everywhere, the thorn. It wrapped the bell tower in coils thick as oxen. It threaded through the arches of the aqueduct. It grew from cracks in the streets in ropes and braids and knots that might once have been beautiful, if beauty could forgive what it had done.

*Theo Villalobos.*

The voice came again, and this time Theo did not flinch. Much.

It had been whispering their name for two days now, since they'd crossed the last of the causeways at low tide with Sefa's map clutched in one fist and Wren's warnings ringing in both ears. *Don't answer it,* Wren had said. *Don't speak to it. Don't even think at it too loudly.* And then he'd stayed behind at the chapel, praying, or pretending to, while Theo walked into the thorn's throat alone because apparently that was the kind of person Theo had decided to be.

"I'm not talking to you," Theo said aloud, to the wind, to the water, to nobody. "I'm talking to myself. Loudly. In a haunted city. Which is normal."

The thorn's laughter was not a sound so much as a sensation—a prickle along the arms, a tightening behind the ribs, like the whole city was a body drawing breath.

*You came all this way to talk to yourself?*

"I came because my grandmother told me to come." Theo picked their way down the granary's collapsed stair, boots finding purchase on stone slick with weed. "She said if the hunters came, run east. She said the answers were here. She did not say the answers would be so—" they gestured at everything, the towers, the coils, the vast slow pulse beneath it all, "*chatty.*"

*Your grandmother lied to you.*

Theo stopped walking.

The word hung in the flooded street, and around them the water shivered, though there was no wind. Somewhere above, a heron lifted off a broken spire and beat away south, sensible bird, and Theo stood very still in its wake and felt something cold settle into their stomach that had nothing to do with the marsh damp.

"You don't know anything about my grandmother," they said.

*I knew her mother. I knew her mother's mother. I have known every Villalobos since the first one put their knee on my root and spoke the Binding Oath with blood in their mouth and lies in their heart.*

"That's not—Ilsa said—"

*I know what Ilsa said.* The voice was patient now, terribly patient, the patience of something that had waited three hundred years and could wait three hundred more. *Ilsa said we were heroes. That the Villalobos line was chosen. That the binding was a mercy, a gift freely given to save a drowning world. Shall I show you what actually happened?*

"I don't want anything from you."

*You want everything from me. You crossed a kingdom for it.* A pause, and when the voice came again it was almost gentle, which was somehow worse. *Come inside, little speaker. The tide will turn soon, and I would rather not have this conversation interrupted by your drowning.*

---

The thorn led them—or Theo followed it; the distinction felt important and also meaningless—through streets where the water stood waist-deep and then knee-deep and then gone entirely, leaving mud that sucked at every step. The thorn grew thicker here. It covered entire walls in woven black lattice, and where it crossed doorways it had grown *around* the frames, careful, deliberate, the way ivy grows around a grave marker.

And in the gaps between the thorns, Theo saw faces.

Not carvings. Not bones. Impressions—the way a body leaves a shape in a mattress long after it rises. Human shapes pressed into the black wood, arms outstretched or folded or raised as if in blessing, hundreds of them, thousands, the whole history of the city written in people who had been caught standing still when the thorn woke.

"They didn't all drown," Theo whispered. "Did they."

*Some ran. Some fought. Some were already dead when I reached them.* The voice had no shame in it, and no pride either. Just fact, flat as a ledger. *But some stood in doorways and held up their hands and spoke words of power at me, and I took them mid-word, and their last word is still in me. Would you like to hear it?*

"No," Theo said, and then, because lying to a plant seemed pointless: "Yes."

The thorn pressed one coil aside, and beneath it, half-buried in the wall, was the impression of a woman with her arm thrown up across her face, mouth open. And from the open mouth, faint as a memory of heat, came a single syllable—not heard so much as remembered, a shape of sound without sound:

*Ama-*

"Amarel," Theo breathed. "That's an Amarel name-shape. Wren showed me. On the old boundary stones."

*The first family of Vaelmora,* the thorn agreed. *Before the crown. Before the Villalobos. Before anyone thought to write down who owned what.* The coil slid back over the woman's face, gentle as a blanket drawn over a sleeper. *Now walk, little speaker. We are nearly there.*

---

They came at last to the great hall—or what had been the great hall, before the sea took the roof and gave it back as open sky. Pillars rose from shallow water, each one wrapped in thorn, and at the far end, where a throne might have stood, the thorn had built something else: a knot the size of a chapel, dense and dark and pulsing, very slowly, like a heart that had learned to be patient.

Theo stood at the threshold, water lapping their ankles, and made themselves look at it.

"You killed them," they said. "All these people. You drowned a kingdom."

*I did.* No denial. No excuse. *And do you know why?*

"Because you're a monster."

*Because a man named Edran Amarel asked me to.*

The hall went very quiet. Even the water seemed to stop moving.

*Sit,* the thorn said. *This is a long story, and you have been walking for days, and I am old enough to remember how hospitality works.*

There was a dry shelf of stone near the wall, and Theo sat on it because their legs wanted to anyway, and hated themselves a little for it.

*Here is the story Ilsa told you,* the thorn began. *A wild thorn, mindless and hungry, waking beneath Vaelmora. A kingdom drowning by accident. A brave young Villalobos—your many-times-great-grandmother, Sera Villalobos—who sacrificed everything to bind the monster and save the world. Yes?*

"Yes."

*Mm. Now here is the story as I remember it, and I remember everything, because I am the thing that happened.*

The pulse of the great knot slowed, deepened, and the hall filled—not with sound but with *knowing*, images pressing against the inside of Theo's skull like light through closed eyelids.

---

Vaelmora alive. White walls, gold roofs, ten thousand sails in the harbor. And beneath it, coiled in the bedrock like a sleeping serpent, the thorn—young then, small, barely awake, feeding on nothing worse than groundwater and the dreams of sleeping sailors.

And in a tower above the harbor, a man named Edran Amarel, a weather-speaker of considerable gift and considerable ambition, kneeling over a basin of salt water and speaking to the thing below.

*He did not command me,* the thorn said. *No one commands me. He bargained. His daughter was dying—wasting sickness, the lungs filling slowly, the healers out of tricks. And he had heard, as speakers hear things, that there was something old under his city, something that fed on years, and he came down into the dark and offered me a trade: her dying years, given freely, in exchange for her living.*

*And I took the trade. Because that is what I am. I take what is given. Her years passed into me and she lived, and she lived, and she lived—and Edran saw what I could do, and he wanted more. For the city. For himself. He brought me the old, the dying, the willing, and I took their years and gave them back as strength, and Vaelmora bloomed like nothing the world had seen. Three generations without famine. Ships that outran storms. A golden age bought at a fair price, freely paid.*

The images shifted. Theo watched the white city gleam, watched processions in the streets, watched an old woman walk up the thorn-wrapped tower steps on her own two feet, smiling, to give what she had left.

*And then,* the thorn said, *the crown noticed.*

---

Soldiers in the streets. Priests with censers and ledgers. A royal decree read from the harbor steps: the gift was not to be traded, not to be hoarded, not to be *owned* by any family, least of all a family whose loyalty the crown had begun to doubt.

*Edran refused them. Politely, at first. Then less politely. The crown sent soldiers; the Amarels had friends among the gifted, and the gifted of Vaelmora could drown a fleet with a word. So the crown changed tactics. They found a younger family, hungrier, more pious. A family with a daughter who had been taught all her life that her gift was a debt owed to God and king.*

*Sera Villalobos was nineteen years old.*

Theo's breath went out of them slowly.

*They told her I was a demon. They showed her the sick, the mad, the ones my feeding had damaged—yes, there were those; every bargain has its bad debts, and Edran was not always careful whom he took years from. They did not show her the smiling grandmother on the stairs. They told her that binding me would make her a saint, and that refusing would make her family traitors, and her father a head shorter.*

*And she came down into this hall with the Binding Oath in her mouth, and she knelt where you are sitting, and she spoke it. And it cost her everything they told her it would cost—her years, her voice, most of her life. She was an old woman at thirty-five and dead at forty-one, and the crown carved her name in marble and called it sacrifice.*

*But here is what the marble does not say:* the pulse quickened, and Theo felt the truth of it land in their chest like a dropped stone. *The Binding Oath does not destroy a bond. It transfers it. Sera did not free Vaelmora from me. She took Edran's place. She became my keeper—and her keepers after her, the Villalobos line, generation upon generation, holding the trade-routes of my feeding, deciding who gave years and who received them, collecting the crown's tithe from every bargain struck in this hall.*

*Your family were not my jailers, Theo Villalobos. They were my tax collectors. And when the last of them fled north rather than watch what the line had become, they did not abandon a duty. They stole the deed.*

---

The vision released them, and Theo sat on the cold stone shelf with marsh water around their ankles and three hundred years of certainty dissolving like sugar in rain.

"That's not true," they said. Their voice came out small. "My grandmother—she burned the archive rather than let the crown have it. She kept one page. She said—she said the Villalobos were sworn to protect people *from* you."

*Ask yourself,* the thorn said, *why a protector burns the proof.*

Theo opened their mouth. Closed it.

Because they had seen Ilsa burn things before. Letters, always letters, always quickly, always with her back turned. And Ilsa had never once, in all Theo's childhood, spoken the names of the dead. And the page she'd kept—the single page she'd pressed into Theo's hands with shaking fingers—had been written in a hand none of them recognized, in words Ilsa had refused to translate.

*Every Villalobos child is told the same story,* the thorn went on, softer now. *And every Villalobos, sooner or later, comes down here and hears mine. Your grandmother's mother sat where you sit. She wept for a day and then went home and told her daughter the same lie she'd been told, because the lie was lighter to carry, and because the crown was still listening, always listening, and the truth would have gotten everyone killed.*

"And you?" Theo managed. "What do you get from telling me? Why should I believe the monster's version?"

*Believe whichever version makes you kinder,* the thorn said. *I gain nothing from your belief. I only gain from your arrival, and you arrived the moment you crossed the border with my oath in your blood. But understand this, little speaker: the Unbinding is coming whether you speak it or not. There are Amarels in the world again—they survived, they remember, and they want back what was taken. When they come, someone must hold the other end of the rope. A jailer. Or a partner. Or—*

It paused. The whole hall leaned in.

*—or a third thing. Sera's oath failed within a century. Edran's bargain drowned a kingdom when the crown broke it by force. Perhaps the fault was never in the oaths, little speaker. Perhaps it was in the choosing—oaths spoken under orders, under fear, under other people's truths. Has anyone ever asked you what *you* would offer?*

"No," Theo said. "No one's ever asked me anything. People tell me what I am and then fight over it."

*Then here is my question, and take your time answering, because the tide has hours yet and I have had centuries:* the knot pulsed, slow and warm, like a banked fire. *When the time comes—and it is coming—what will YOU choose?*

Theo sat in the drowned hall of a murdered kingdom, in the house their family had built its name on, and found—to their considerable horror—that they didn't know.

Above, through the missing roof, the sky had begun to change. The light was going strange and copper-colored, and the water in the doorway was starting, very slowly, to creep back in.

And from somewhere beyond the eastern towers—distant, deliberate, unmistakable—came the sound of a horn.

Crown horns sounded twice for a sighting.

This one sounded three times.

For a speaker found.
