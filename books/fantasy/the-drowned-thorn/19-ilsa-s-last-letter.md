## 19. Ilsa's Last Letter

The courier came at dusk, the way bad news prefers to travel.

Theo heard him before they saw him—the particular rhythm of a horse that has been ridden too hard for too long, a sound like an apology made of hooves. They were sitting on Sefa's cart tail, sharing the last of a wheel of cheese that had gone slightly soft in the marsh heat, and both of them turned toward the sound at the same moment.

"That's not a merchant's horse," Sefa said.

"No," Theo said. Their mouth had gone dry around the cheese.

The rider was a boy, sixteen perhaps, mud to his knees and terror in his eyes, wearing the faded livery of a Thornvale miller's family that Theo recognized from a lifetime ago. He slid off the horse before it fully stopped, which was either great skill or great exhaustion, and stood swaying in the road with something wrapped in oilcloth clutched against his chest.

"You're—" he started, then stopped, looked around at the pilgrims' camp, at the drowned city rising black and thorned in the distance, and seemed to lose whatever speech he'd rehearsed on the ride east. "You're Villalobos."

"I am."

"She said you would be here." The boy held out the bundle with both hands, the way one hands over a sleeping infant or a live coal. "Ilsa. Before the end. She paid my father everything we had to get me out ahead of them. She said—she said *east, low tide, follow the cartographer.*" He glanced at Sefa, apologetic. "She knew about you too."

Sefa went very still. "Did she."

"I'm Tam," the boy said, as if remembering names were a thing people did. "Tam Ostrev."

The name landed between them like a dropped knife.

"Ostrev," Theo repeated carefully.

"Distant," Tam said quickly. "Cousins twice removed or some such. I've never met the captain. My father changed his own name when he left the army. Please don't—" He swallowed. "Please don't make me go back."

"Nobody's making anybody go anywhere," Theo said, and took the bundle.

It was heavier than a letter should be. Inside the oilcloth: a single page, folded three times, sealed with candle wax because proper sealing wax was a luxury Ilsa Fairweather-Villalobos had always considered a form of showing off. And beneath it, wrapped separately, something small and hard that clicked against itself when it moved.

Theo didn't open either. Not there, not with Tam watching, not with the thorn-city humming its low note on the horizon like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

"Come sit by the fire," they said instead. "When did you eat last?"

* * *

Ilsa Fairweather-Villalobos had never written a letter in her life that she hadn't first complained about writing.

This was a woman who considered correspondence a form of vanity—who said that if a thing needed saying, you should say it to a person's face, and if you couldn't say it to their face, you should probably examine why. She had once returned a neighbor's invitation unopened because, as she put it, "anyone who needs fancy paper to ask you to supper is planning to serve you something terrible." Theo had grown up thinking of her handwriting as a rare weather event, like hail in summer: memorable, unwelcome, and over quickly.

So the letter began, naturally, with an apology for existing.

*Little thorn,* it read, in handwriting gone shaky in a way that frightened Theo more than any soldier could have. *If you're reading this, then I'm dead, or worse, and you're standing somewhere east of everywhere holding the last honest thing I ever gave anyone. Sit down. No—don't sit down. You'll want to throw this in the water and I wrote it on good paper.*

*I burned the archive. You saw me do it, or you will have seen me do it by now, time being the slippery fish it is. What I told you that day was true but incomplete, which is the family disease. We are a line of incomplete truths, we Villalobos. It's how we survived. It is also, I begin to suspect at the end of things, how we damned ourselves.*

*You know the story I raised you on. That our ancestors were heroes. That they bound the Drowned Thorn to save a kingdom drowning, and paid for it with exile, and carried the binding words in their blood like a sacred debt. I told it well. I believed it myself until your grandfather made me read the actual pages, and even then I chose the pretty version for years, because the pretty version let us sleep at night.*

*Here is what the pages say.*

*We did not bind the thorn. We stole the binding.*

Theo read that sentence four times. Around them, the camp went on—Sefa arguing with a pilgrim about the correct way to stake a mule, Tam eating stew with the desperate focus of someone who had ridden two days without stopping—but the words on the page had a way of making all of it very far away, like watching rain through glass.

*There was another family,* Ilsa wrote. *Older than ours. The Amarels. They were the thorn's keepers—not its jailers, its keepers, there's a difference, though I didn't understand it for years—and the binding lived in their blood the way ours lives in yours. When the thorn drowned Vaelmora the first time, it was an Amarel who spoke the Binding Oath. An Amarel who aged thirty years in a night so that thousands could live. And when the crown came afterward, hungry and afraid, wanting the thorn's power for its wars—*

*Our ancestor was the crown's man. A court swordspeaker, favored, ambitious. He learned the Amarel oaths by treachery—I won't write how; some things should stay buried even in confession—and he did the one thing no speaker had ever done. He bound the thorn not to himself but to his whole bloodline, forever, so that no Amarel could ever speak to it again. He locked them out of their own inheritance and called it salvation.*

*The kingdom called him a hero. The Amarels called him a thief. Both were right.*

*We spent four hundred years being praised for the crime. Every Villalobos who kept the valley, who taught the silent forms, who passed the words down in whispers—we thought we were keeping a sacred trust. We were keeping stolen goods. Your grandmother's grandmother's grandmother tended a lock she had no right to, and threw away the key, and called the throwing-away virtue.*

*I found this out in my forties and I did nothing. That's my sin, little thorn, not the old theft but my silence. I told myself the Amarels were extinct, or mad, or that the past was past. I told myself you deserved a childhood without ghosts. And then the hunters came, and I understood that ghosts don't stay buried just because you've been polite to them.*

*Which brings me to the thing in the second wrapping, and I need you to hear me now, because I will only say this once and I will say it angry, because anger is easier than crying and I've never once managed to cry in front of you.*

*Do not trust the priest.*

*Wren Calloway served your grandfather, yes—that part is true, check it however you like. But he serves the Amarels first. He always has. Whatever kindness he shows you is real AND it is a leash, and those are not contradictory, that's what makes people like him dangerous. If he has told you about the third oath, the unnamed one—he will have told you it's forbidden, lost, a myth. It is none of those things. It is simply that every speaker must write their own, and no one can teach it, and that terrified the men who kept the records, because an oath no institution controls is an oath no institution can use.*

*Your grandfather tried to write one. Near the end. He said the thorn showed him the shape of it—a binding not to a bloodline, not to a crown, but to a single willing soul, freely given, freely kept. He died before he could speak it. I used to think that was tragedy. Now I think maybe the thorn was waiting. Maybe it has always been waiting. For someone who would choose it rather than inherit it.*

*That could have been you, if I'd been braver sooner. Instead I fed you the pretty story and hoped the world would leave us alone, and the world has never once left a Villalobos alone, and now you're out there carrying our theft in your veins while everyone who wants the key hunts you for it.*

*I'm sorry. I've started this apology eleven times and I keep running out of ways to say it, so I'll say it plain: I lied to you with love, which is still lying, and I'd do it again, which is still wrong, and loving me afterward is entirely your choice and I release you from it.*

*One more thing, and then I'll stop, because the dogs are barking on the ridge road and I know that sound.*

*The hard thing in the wrapping belonged to the Amarel keeper who spoke the first Binding. Our ancestor took it off her body. I don't know what it does. Your grandfather thought it might matter someday. He was usually right about things like that, and insufferable about it, and I miss him.*

*Go home eventually, little thorn. Not yet. But eventually. Valleys remember their people even when the people forget their valleys.*

*Burn this after reading. Or don't. You were always stubborn about my instructions and I loved that about you, God help me.*

*—Ilsa, who owes you better, and knows it*

*P.S. The cheese in the cellar was for your nameday. Eat cheese on your nameday wherever you are. Traditions matter more when they're inconvenient.*

*P.P.S. If a boy named Tam delivers this, feed him. He's a good egg from a bad basket, and baskets aren't eggs.*

* * *

Theo read it twice through by firelight, Sefa standing watch beside them without being asked, and then sat for a long time doing absolutely nothing, which Sefa later described as "the scariest thing I've ever watched a person do."

"The Amarels," Sefa said finally. "That's the family Wren—"

"Yes."

"And he's been teaching you the oaths. Their oaths. While working for them."

"He said he served my grandfather." Theo's voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "He didn't mention whose grandfather he helped rob."

Tam, who had been pretending very hard to be asleep by the fire, opened one eye. "The priest? Gray coat, talks like a book having a conversation with itself?"

Theo looked at him. "You've met him?"

"Saw him at the marsh edge two days back. Talking with soldiers." Tam shrugged, uncomfortable. "Not fighting them. Talking. Friendly-like. I hid, because soldiers, but—" He hesitated, and then, with the particular courage of the young and exhausted, decided to finish. "He wasn't a prisoner. He was pointing at things on a map."

The night wind came off the marsh carrying the thorn-city's hum, and Theo felt it answer in their blood, faint and patient, like a heartbeat waiting to be acknowledged.

They unwrapped the second bundle.

Inside, on a scrap of leather gone stiff with age, lay a ring. Iron, plain, sized for a woman's hand, set with a stone that wasn't a stone—it was a splinter of thorn-wood, black and glossy, and it was warm. Warm in the cool marsh night. Warm as skin.

As Theo closed their fingers around it, the hum in their blood shifted—resolved, almost, into something like recognition. Or greeting.

Or welcome home.

Behind them, at the edge of the firelight, where no one had been standing a moment before, a familiar gray figure cleared his throat.

"We need to talk," said Wren Calloway, "before you decide what that letter makes me."
