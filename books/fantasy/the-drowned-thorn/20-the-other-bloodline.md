## 20. The Other Bloodline

Wren Calloway had lied for a living once. He'd stood in pulpits and told frightened people that their dead were sleeping soundly, that the flood was a punishment, that the thorn was only a story grandmothers told to keep children from the marsh. He had been good at it. That was the shame of it — he had been *good*.

So it was a particular kind of humiliation, he reflected, standing in the doorway of the flooded chapel with rain dripping off his collar, to discover that lying to Theo Villalobos was another matter entirely. The last swordspeaker looked up from the map Sefa had left spread across the altar rail, and something in their face — not suspicion exactly, but attention, the particular attention of someone who had spent a lifetime listening for words that didn't mean what they said — made every prepared sentence in Wren's chest curdle like milk left out in the sun.

"You're staring," Theo said.

"I'm thinking."

"You're staring *and* thinking. It's very loud." They rolled the map closed with more care than the remark deserved. "Sefa went to check the tide lines. She'll be back within the hour. So if there's something you've been circling for three days, Father Calloway, you have about fifty minutes to land it."

Wren came inside properly, shaking the water from his coat like an old dog. The chapel smelled of brine and candle smoke and the green rot of things growing where they shouldn't. He had loved this place once, before the diocese stripped his orders and the marsh took the nave. He had loved it the way you love a coat that no longer fits — resentfully, and with your arms still through the sleeves.

"There's tea," he offered.

"I know. I watched you make it badly."

"It's not badly. It's penitentially."

Theo almost smiled. Almost. And Wren thought: *now*, you coward, *now* — because the smile was a door open one inch, and doors like that did not stay open, and he had watched enough of them close over sixty years to know the arithmetic.

He sat down on the front pew, the one with the back broken by a fallen rafter, and folded his hands the way he used to before sermons, which was the way liars fold their hands when they want to look honest.

"My name isn't entirely Wren Calloway," he began.

"That's a strange sentence."

"It gets stranger. It is my name. But it is not the name I was born under, and the man who gave me this one did so because the other one would have gotten us both drowned in the marsh with stones in our pockets." He breathed out. "I was born Wren Amarel."

The word dropped into the chapel like a coin into deep water. Wren watched it sink. Watched Theo go very still — not the stillness of shock, he noted, but the stillness of someone rearranging everything they knew around a new weight.

"Amarel," Theo repeated.

"You know the name."

"Ilsa never said it aloud. Not once in nineteen years. But she said it *around*, the way you talk around a hole in the floor." Theo set the map down. "The thorn said it. In Vaelmora. It showed me faces — speakers binding it, generation after generation — and then it showed me others being driven out, and it said a word I thought I'd misheard." A pause. "It didn't mishear me, though. Did it."

"No," Wren said. "It didn't."

* * *

Here is what Wren told them, and because he had decided — finally, at his age, with his knees aching and his orders long since revoked — to tell it true, he told it in order, the way his own grandmother had told it to him by a peat fire while the marsh wind worried the shutters.

The Villalobos line had not been the first family of speakers. That honor — or that curse; the Amarels had argued for four centuries about which it was — belonged to a family of marsh-folk, eel-catchers and reed-cutters, who had learned the old words the way you learn anything: slowly, badly, across generations, each one adding a syllable of understanding to the last. When the Drowned Thorn first woke beneath what would become Vaelmora and began drinking the low country dry of its people, it was an Amarel who walked into the black tangle of it and spoke the first Binding. Not a hero. Wren was careful about this. A terrified woman named Odile Amarel who went into the thorn expecting to die and instead came out bound to it, aged thirty years in an afternoon, with her hair gone white as gull-feather.

"She lived another forty years," Wren said. "Guarding it. Teaching her children the words. And her children taught theirs, and for two hundred years the Amarels kept the oath — poor as reed-cutters, because guarding a thing nobody believes in doesn't pay, and the crown certainly wasn't going to pay it."

"And then?" Theo asked quietly.

"And then the Villalobos came north." Wren's hands tightened on his knee. "Charming family. Wealthy. Well-connected at court. They arrived with documents — forged, though it took the Amarels twenty years and three ruined petitions to prove it — claiming the wardenship of the thorn by right of an older bloodline. And here is the part I need you to hear without flinching: they didn't just take the *title*. They took the words. There is a rite — there was a rite — by which a binding can be transferred between bloodlines, willingly given or otherwise. Your ancestors performed it. Whether they forced it or tricked it out of the last Amarel speaker, the records disagree. The Amarels say forced. The Villalobos records, the ones your grandmother burned—" he saw Theo flinch and pressed on gently, "—the ones that survived, say the transfer was 'necessary.' Which is the sort of word people use when the truth would cost them something."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere out in the marsh, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"So my family stole it," Theo said.

"Your family stole it," Wren agreed. "And then they guarded it. I want that said too, because I've had four hundred years of my own family's version and I no longer trust any story with only one villain in it. The Villalobos kept the thorn bound. Whatever else they were, they kept it bound, and the kingdom stayed dry, and children grew up who never had to learn what a drowning country smells like. My family lost the words and the wardenship and eventually the name itself — we were attainted, scattered, our lands given to the crown. We became a rumor. Then a grudge. Then—" he smiled, and it was not a warm smile, "—then a purpose."

"The hunters," Theo said. "Ostrev. The raids. That's not the crown's idea alone, is it?"

"No. The crown hunts the gifted because the gifted frighten it. But the pressure, the timing, the *directions* — a great deal of that has been Amarel money and Amarel counsel for years. Fear drives speakers toward Vaelmora. Toward the thorn. Toward us." He met Theo's eyes. "We needed a Villalobos heir to come home. You see? Every road that led you here was paid for by someone. Even mine."

* * *

Theo stood up. Walked to the broken window where the marsh showed gray and endless, and stood there with their back turned, and Wren let them stand, because some silences are not empty but full, like a cup filled to the brim, and jostling them spills everything.

When Theo finally spoke, their voice was level in the way that ice is level.

"How long."

"Since before Thornvale burned. Since before you crossed the border. I was sent to find you after the raid — we had word Ostrev was coming before he did."

"You knew Thornvale would burn." Theo turned. Their eyes were wet and furious and terribly calm. "You knew, and you waited at the end of the road with tea and lessons in drowned tongues, and you let me run to you like a—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "My grandmother held those soldiers off with a false confession so I could get over the ridge. She's dead now, or taken, and you knew it was coming, and you poured me tea."

"Yes," Wren said. There was no version of this conversation in which he defended himself, and he had known that since the moment he'd chosen to speak, and he chose it anyway. "Yes. All of that. I won't insult you by explaining how heavy the orders felt, or how I told myself the alternative was worse. I will tell you only this: I am defrocked, disgraced, and old, and I have spent eleven years doing the Amarels' work because they promised me access to the archive of the original oaths — the ones Odile spoke, uncorrupted by four hundred years of Villalobos editing. I wanted the truth more than I wanted a clean conscience. That is the whole of my sin, and it is not a small one, and I am not asking you to forgive it. I am asking you to believe that I am telling it to you now because the plan has changed, and I will not be part of what comes next."

"What comes next?"

Wren took a breath. This was the part he had rehearsed on the walk over, and the rehearsal had been useless, because there was no gentle way to say it.

"The eclipse. In nine days. The Amarels mean to complete the Unbinding during the coronation — publicly, irrevocably, with the whole realm watching. And they cannot do it with words alone anymore. The old rite needs blood. Living blood of the binding line." He watched Theo understand. "They don't need your cooperation, child. They need your veins."

* * *

What happened next, Wren would remember afterward in fragments, the way you remember a storm — not the whole of it but the pieces that struck you.

Theo laughing, first, a short terrible laugh with nothing in it. *"They need my blood. Of course they do. Everyone wants a piece of me — the crown wants my oath, the thorn wants my name, and now my own teacher wants my arm in a bucket."*

Then the wooden sword coming off Theo's back, and Wren's heart lurching — but Theo only drove it point-down into the chapel floorboards, hard, once, and left it standing there like a grave marker, and leaned on it with both hands.

"Why tell me?" Theo demanded. "If they need me alive and unaware, why hand me the knife? What do you get?"

And here Wren found, to his surprise, that the answer was easy — the first easy thing all evening.

"Because I served your grandfather," he said. "Not the Amarels' fiction of him. The man. He caught me forging burial records during the last purge — I was hiding gifted families in the crypt of my own church, moving them east one funeral at a time. He should have reported me. Instead he sat with me all night and taught me the Binding Oath's first line, and he said, *'A word spoken to protect is worth ten spoken to rule.'* And then he went home to Thornvale and hid his granddaughter so well that even I couldn't find her, and I have been looking, boyo, I have been looking for nineteen years." His voice cracked on the number and he let it. "Your grandfather believed the thorn should stay bound. Quietly, without glory, forever. He was the best of your line and possibly the best man I ever knew, and his family's name is built on theft, and both of those things are true, and I am tired — *tired* — of truths that come one at a time."

Theo looked at him for a long moment. Rain on the roof. The candle guttering.

"Nineteen years," they said at last. "You looked for nineteen years and found me three days ago and you've been teaching me oaths ever since. Why teach me at all, if your masters want me as a blood-bag?"

"Because the third oath exists," Wren said simply. "The one no text records. Your grandfather hinted at it. The thorn knows it. And if anyone in this drowned kingdom is going to write it, I would far rather it be you than the people who sent me." He rose, joints cracking, and gathered his coat. "They expect me to report that you're settled and unsuspecting. I will report that. It buys us nine days. After that, Theo Villalobos, you and I and that sharp-tongued cartographer of yours are going to be running again — and this time, everyone will be chasing us."

He was at the door when Theo spoke behind him, quiet as the tide coming in.

"Wren. The Amarels. Are they watching this chapel?"

He paused with his hand on the latch, and hated himself, and answered honestly, because apparently that was the discipline he'd chosen for his old age.

"They have eyes on the causeway," he said. "Two of them. They've been there since yesterday."

And through the rain-blurred window, out along the gray ribbon of the marsh road, Theo and Wren both saw them at the same moment: two figures walking the causeway through the downpour, unhurried, certain, closing the distance with the patience of people who already knew how the story ended.

"*Nine days,*" Wren murmured. "Well. Perhaps eight."
