## 21. Caught Between Oaths

They took me in the dark between the third bell and the fourth, which I later learned was deliberate. The Amarels were punctual people. Everything about them—the grey cloaks, the soft-soled boots, the way they'd drugged the wine Sefa and I had shared at supper—spoke of a household that kept its ledgers balanced to the last copper.

I woke on a cot in a stone room with no windows, my wrists bound not with rope but with river-reed woven into a braid, and I knew enough by then to understand that the reeds mattered. Wren had told me once, offhandedly, the way he told me most important things: *Reed drinks what blood carries. Old trick. Older than the crown.*

So they knew what I was. They'd known before they ever let me reach Vaelmora.

The room itself was almost kind. A cot with a wool blanket. A basin of water. A candle that someone had thoughtfully left burning, because apparently even kidnappers have manners when they're certain of their position. There was even a book on a small shelf—a psalter, its spine cracked, its pages swollen from damp. Someone had underlined passages in faded brown ink.

I lay there for what felt like an hour, taking inventory. My body ached the particular ache of a body that has been carrying something heavy for a long time and has finally been allowed to set it down. Except I hadn't set anything down. The thorn was still there, coiled behind my ribs like a second heart, patient as winter.

*Well,* it said, or seemed to say. It didn't use words anymore, exactly. Not since the crypt. Since the blood. It used something older than words, something that arrived in my chest fully formed, like weather.

*I know,* I told it. *I'm working on it.*

Somewhere beyond the door, water moved. That was the other thing about this place—I could hear it everywhere. Dripping, lapping, running through channels cut into the stone by hands long dead. We were deep. Deeper than the chapel where I'd met Wren. Deep enough that the drowned city pressed down around us like a hand cupped over a candle flame.

The door opened without a sound.

The woman who entered was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, dressed in travelling clothes of good grey wool with the hem darkened by marshwater. She carried a tray—bread, cheese, an apple, a cup of something steaming—and she set it down on the shelf beside the psalter with the care of someone arranging flowers.

"Theo Villalobos," she said. Not a question.

"That depends who's asking."

"Mereth Amarel." She said her own name the way other people said their titles. "You've met my cousins, I think. In the chapel. Though perhaps they didn't introduce themselves properly. They can be—" she considered, "—enthusiastic."

"One of them hit me with a boat hook."

"Yes, that sounds like Corvin." She sighed, and there was something so genuinely weary in it that I almost laughed. Almost. "Sit up, please. Eat if you like. I won't pretend the reeds aren't necessary, but you're not a prisoner in the way you're imagining. You're a guest whose departure has been postponed."

"Postponed until when?"

"Until you understand what we're asking. And why." She drew the room's only stool close to the cot and sat, folding her hands in her lap. Up close I could see she had ink stains on her fingers, old ones, worked deep into the skin the way stains get when they've been there for forty years. A scholar's hands. My grandmother had hands like that.

"I know what you're asking," I said. "Wren told me. The Unbinding."

"Wren Calloway tells many things," Mereth said mildly. "Some of them are even true. But no—I don't think you do know what we're asking. Because if you knew, truly knew, you would not be lying there looking at me like a cornered fox. You'd be looking at me like something worse." She leaned forward slightly. "Tell me what you know about the drowning, child. The real one. Not your grandmother's version."

"My grandmother's version is that your family tried to steal the binding and got what thieves deserve."

Something flickered across her face—not anger. Grief, maybe, worn smooth by handling. "And did she tell you how many died? When the thorn came free the first time? Before your illustrious jailers ever laid their words on it?"

"No one tells me numbers," I said. "Everyone tells me stories."

"Then let me give you the arithmetic." She spoke quietly, evenly, the way you'd read a list of debts at a funeral. "Vaelmora held two hundred thousand souls. Fishers, dyers, priests, children. When the thorn broke loose—when it was *freed*, Theo, by a Villalobos who decided his oath was more trouble than it was worth—it came up out of the marsh in a single night. The water followed it. Or the water was it; the scholars still argue. By dawn the towers were black. By noon the towers were gone. Two hundred thousand people, and the sea closed over them like a mouth."

The candle guttered. Neither of us moved.

"They found survivors," she went on, "for years afterward. Washed up on shores a hundred leagues away, alive, changed. Speaking in thorn-words. Your family caught them too, didn't they? Bound them into hedges and fence posts and church doors. Ilsa never mentioned the hedge-speakers? No. Of course not. Jailers don't count their prisoners as people."

"You want me to unbind it," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "After telling me *that*. Do you hear how that sounds?"

"I want you to understand the choice you're actually making." She stood, crossed to the psalter, and turned it toward me, open to an underlined passage. "Because here is what nobody has told you, and here is why we do what we do. The Binding your ancestors spoke—it isn't holding. It has been failing for three hundred years, slowly, the way a dam fails. Every generation of your line poured more of themselves into it, and every generation the thorn drank deeper and pushed back harder. Why do you think your family hid in a dead valley? Why do you think the gift skips and stutters and costs what it costs? You are not the heir to a victory, child. You are the heir to a debt. And the debt has come due."

"And your solution is to tear the dam down all at once."

"Our solution is to *choose* the flood rather than be chosen by it." Her eyes were steady. "There is a difference. A great one. The Unbinding spoken rightly, by willing blood, in the old forms—we believe the thorn can be released *and spent*, discharged like a storm, and then gone. Done. No more debt, no more jailers, no more children born with words in their veins waiting for men like Captain Ostrev to come burn their villages over it. Freedom, Theo. For it, and for everyone like you."

"And if you're wrong?"

She didn't flinch. I'll give her that. "Then everything drowns again, and it will have been our doing, and our names will be cursed until the last book burns. We have counted that cost too. We carry it. Every Amarel alive carries it." She touched the psalter's page gently. "Can you say the same? Can your grandmother's ghost say it? Keeping a thing caged is also a wager, child. You've simply been raised not to see the cage."

* * *

They left me alone after that. The bread was good. I ate it anyway, which felt like a betrayal of something, though I couldn't have said what. Grandmother Ilsa, probably. Ilsa would have had opinions about eating your captor's bread. She'd have had opinions delivered at volume, possibly with a wooden spoon.

*I miss you,* I thought at her, and hated myself a little for it, because the last thing she'd ever written me was a letter that turned my whole inheritance inside out, and I hadn't even finished grieving the first version of her before the second one arrived.

The thorn stirred.

Not speaking. Just... turning over, the way a sleeper turns. And in its turning I felt something I hadn't expected: fear. Actual fear, coming off it in cold waves, the way fear comes off a horse before an earthquake. It had heard Mereth. Or it had heard the word *Unbinding*, which was the same thing to it.

*You're afraid of them,* I realized. *Of us. Of being freed.*

It didn't answer. But the pressure behind my ribs shifted, and for a moment—just a moment—I felt what it felt, the way you feel a dreamer's dream when you sleep too near them. Water. Enormous, patient, green-lit water, and beneath it a hunger so old it had stopped being hunger and become something more like gravity. And threaded through all of it, wound tight as wire:

Two hundred thousand voices. Not screaming. Worse. *Waiting.* Singing, almost, in a key that made my teeth ache. The drowned, kept. Kept the way you keep preserves in a cellar. Kept the way a miser keeps coins he will never spend.

My grandmother's cage wasn't empty. It had never been empty. The binding hadn't just trapped the thorn—it had trapped everyone the thorn had taken, suspended somewhere between death and digestion, for three hundred years.

I put my head between my knees and breathed until the world stopped tilting.

*So that's the corner,* I thought. *That's the shape of it.*

Unbind it, and either the drowned go free or the kingdom above goes under—or both, and no one alive could tell me which. Keep it bound, and I became the next link in a chain of jailers, feeding my years into a failing dam, hiding my children in magic-dead valleys until the day the dam burst anyway and some Amarel grandchild wrote a bitter letter about it. Speak the Binding anew, as Ostrev wanted, and spend myself as the crown's weapon against everyone like me.

Three oaths. Three walls. And me in the middle, with a candle and half an apple and a psalter full of somebody else's underlined hopes.

"Gently humorous situation," I said aloud to the empty room, because Sefa would have said it, and saying it in her voice helped. "Truly. A+ kidnapping. Five stars. Would recommend to no one."

The door opened. Not Mereth this time—a young man, Corvin presumably, the boat-hook enthusiast, with a lantern and an apologetic set to his shoulders that didn't match the iron in his eyes.

"You're wanted upstairs," he said. "Both of you. The elder council has questions."

"For me?"

"For the thorn." He hesitated, and something human moved behind the iron. "It's been singing since midnight. Loud enough that the watch-posts on the rim heard it. Loud enough that..." He glanced at the ceiling, at whatever lay above us—marsh, tide, the drowned towers wrapped in black. "Loud enough that things are waking up down here that we sealed away a long time ago. Whatever you've been doing in your blood, Villalobos, it's answering them. Or they're answering it. The elders want to know which."

"It's not doing anything," I said. "I haven't spoken. I haven't—"

"It doesn't need you to speak," Corvin said quietly. "That's the part nobody told you, isn't it? It never needed permission. It needed proximity. And now you're here, and it's awake, and the tide comes in at dawn."

He raised the lantern. Down the corridor beyond him, water gleamed black in the torchlight, running fast now where it had dripped before, flowing *upward* along the channel-cut stones, toward the surface, toward the marsh, toward the towers.

Toward the singing.

Behind my ribs, the thorn pressed outward once, gently, almost affectionately—the way a child presses a hand against the wall of the womb—and somewhere far above us, muffled by fathoms of stone and drowned air, something vast began to answer.

Corvin held out his free hand. "Upstairs. Now. And Villalobos—if you were planning to run, I'd think about the water level first."

I looked past him, down the flooding corridor, and thought about Sefa, who was either drugged asleep in some other cell or already awake and doing arithmetic of her own, because Sefa always did arithmetic, and her sums had a way of ending with doors opening that were supposed to stay shut.

"Lead on," I said.
