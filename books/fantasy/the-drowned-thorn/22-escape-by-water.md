## 22. Escape by Water

Sefa had drawn maps for kings, smugglers, one archbishop who paid in indulgences instead of coin, and a goat farmer in the Ashen Fells who wanted to prove his property was larger than his brother-in-law's. She had charted coastlines from the deck of a leaking brigantine and mountain passes in winter with her fingers too frozen to hold a stylus properly.

She had never, until tonight, had to draw a map that would keep two people alive underwater.

"Again," she said, scratching lines into the damp clay floor of their cell with the stub of a charcoal pencil she'd hidden in her boot sole for three years. "Tell me again what you saw."

Theo sat across from her, knees drawn up, hair still dripping from the morning's "interrogation," which had been less an interrogation than a lecture delivered by a woman named Vess Amarel who spoke about bloodlines the way other people spoke about wine vintages. Theo looked wrung out. But their eyes were working, moving, remembering.

"The guard took me down the east stair," Theo said slowly. "Past the chapel level. There was water at the bottom—ankle-deep, then knee-deep. Arched ceilings. Niches in the walls with bones in them, mostly swept aside. And doors. Old doors, rotted, but the frames are stone." They closed their eyes. "I counted forty-one arches before they turned me around."

"You counted forty-one arches while being marched to a ritual that could kill you."

"I count when I'm frightened. It's a thing I do."

"It's a thing I do," Sefa said. "You're stealing my coping mechanisms."

"We can share them. We're sharing everything else. A cell, a captor, a probable doom—"

"*Probable* doom. Look at you, learning optimism." She drew another arc on the floor. "Forty-one arches going which direction?"

"East by northeast, I think. The stair spiraled left twice."

Sefa chewed the end of the pencil, then spat it out because it tasted like three years of boot. "Then here's what I know. This chapel sits on the old processional way. Every drowned city has one—it's how pilgrims moved between temples without getting their robes wet. The processional ways run *under* the water table, sealed, drained by cisterns." She tapped the clay. "And every processional way has maintenance hatches. Because stonework floods, and somebody had to go down and fix it."

Theo stared at her. "How do you know that?"

"Because I mapped the sister city, Corveth, six years ago, and nearly drowned in its processional way, and swore afterward I'd learn everything about them so it would never happen again." She smiled thinly. "Fear is an excellent tutor. Better than the cartographers' guild, honestly, and cheaper."

"And the hatch leads where?"

"Up. Eventually. Through a drainage shaft, probably narrow, probably disgusting, possibly occupied by something with more legs than any creature needs." She looked up at the high, barred window where the marsh light was already going amber. "The eclipse is in four days. Vess Amarel doesn't need us until then, which means we have maybe three days before they stop being polite about keeping us fed and start being efficient about keeping us *useful*."

"They're already using my blood."

"Yes, well. There's 'using your blood in small ceremonial quantities,' and there's 'using all of it.' I'd like to negotiate the gap between those two things by not being here."

Theo was quiet a moment. When they spoke again, their voice had gone careful. "Wren will be at the door tonight. He always takes the last watch."

Sefa kept drawing. "Mm."

"Sefa."

"I heard you."

"He let us see the archive room. He told me about the third oath. He—" Theo stopped. Started again. "He served my grandfather. Whatever else he is, whatever he owes these people, he's spent weeks teaching me how to survive them."

"Or he's spent weeks making sure you'd trust him enough to walk calmly into the ritual," Sefa said. It came out harder than she meant it to, and she saw Theo flinch, and she hated herself a little for it. She softened. "I'm not saying he's a monster. I'm saying men like Wren Calloway carry their duty in one hand and their conscience in the other, and which one drops first depends entirely on the weight of the moment. Our job is to make sure the moment weighs correctly."

"How do we do that?"

She finished the last line of her map—a rough spiral, forty-one marks, a small x where she guessed the first maintenance hatch would be—and blew the dust off it.

"We give him a reason to look away," she said. "*Then* we swim."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had exactly three parts, which Sefa considered the maximum number of parts any plan should have if it hoped to survive contact with reality.

Part one: the diversion. Sefa had spent nine days in captivity doing what she did best—being loudly, relentlessly useful. She'd corrected Vess Amarel's surveyor on the marsh tide tables (he was wrong; the man had been using a Corveth almanac, the amateur). She'd re-inked three fading charts of the catacombs for the Amarel agents, complaining the whole time about their inferior paper, their worse ink, and their criminal habit of folding maps along the same crease until the fibers died. In doing so she had learned the guards' rotation, the location of the blood stores, and—critically—that the Amarels kept their most sensitive documents in the dry gallery above the chapel, behind a lock that was older than the lock on their cell and considerably more fragile.

At the changing of the watch, she would tell the guard on duty—Doss, a young man with a head cold and a conscience like wet paper—that she'd noticed water seeping into the document gallery that afternoon, and wouldn't he want to check before it ruined the Amarel inheritance? Doss would check. Doss would find a slow drip she'd engineered that afternoon with a borrowed awl and a cistern valve, dripping directly onto a map of the northern marches. He would panic. Men with head colds panicked beautifully.

Part two: the swim. While Doss wailed over parchment, Wren would come down for the last watch as he always did. And whether Wren opened the door or merely found it open—whether he raised the alarm or stood very still in the corridor, listening to the water lap in the dark—that part, Sefa admitted, was not a plan so much as a prayer wearing a plan's clothes.

Part three: forty-one arches, one hatch, and a great deal of cold water.

"You've left out the part where we drown," Theo said, when she laid it out.

"That's not a part, that's a risk. Parts are things you *do*."

"That is a deeply stupid distinction."

"Thank you. I learned cartography from deeply stupid people."

But Theo laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, the kind of laugh that lives in prison cells—and squeezed her hand once, hard, and said, "All right. Tonight."

* * *

The changing of the watch came with the smell of rain on the marsh, and Doss, bless his congested heart, took the bait like a trout takes a fly.

"A *leak*?" he said, holding his lantern up toward the gallery stairs. "In the archive? Vess will have my—she'll—where exactly?"

"Third alcove, north wall. It's pooling under the Amarel genealogies as we speak. Centuries of lineage, dissolving." Sefa shook her head mournfully. "Of course, I'm just a prisoner. What would I know about preserving documents."

He was gone in eleven seconds. Sefa counted. Counting when frightened, counting when pleased—it was all the same discipline.

Then footsteps on the chapel stair, unhurried, familiar, and the scrape of the old key, and Wren Calloway stood in the doorway with his lamp and his tired face and his defrocked priest's hands folded in front of him like a man arriving to pray over the sleeping.

He looked at the empty cell.

He looked at Sefa, standing beside the door instead of inside it, and Theo behind her, pale and steady, and the crude map pressed flat against Theo's chest.

For a long moment nobody breathed.

"Wren," Theo said quietly. "Please."

His eyes went between them. Sefa watched the arithmetic happen behind his face—the duty in one hand, the conscience in the other, the scales trembling. She had seen that exact expression on border guards and harbor masters and one customs official whose bribe she'd been short by exactly two silver pieces. It was the face of a man calculating what a thing would cost him, and knowing he couldn't afford either answer.

"The gallery leak," he said at last. His voice was hoarse. "That was you."

"Doss is having a very emotional evening with the genealogies," Sefa said. "You have perhaps ten minutes before someone thinks to wonder why the prisoners are so quiet."

"You understand what I risk. If they learn I—"

"If they learn you *stood in a corridor*, Wren, you'll say you heard nothing, saw nothing, that the water covered our sounds. You're a priest. Lying is practically liturgy for the defrocked ones." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice, and made it gentle, because this part mattered more than the map. "You taught Theo the oaths. All three. You gave them the only weapon they have. If you stop us now, then everything you did was for them—for *Vess*. And I don't believe that. I've watched you look at Theo when you think no one's watching. That isn't a handler's face. That's a man watching someone he failed once fail better this time."

Wren closed his eyes.

"My grandfather," Theo said softly, "would have walked out that door. Wouldn't he."

A long silence. Rain ticked against the high window.

"He would have," Wren said finally, "and he would have complained about the water the entire way." He stepped back from the threshold. He turned his lamp down low, so its light fell short of the corridor. "Forty-one arches. The hatch is behind the thirty-seventh—there's a carving of a pelican above it, half worn away. The shaft climbs ninety feet. There are handholds, but they're slick. Go *slowly*. Being slow is how you stay alive down there." He hesitated, then reached into his coat and pressed something into Theo's palm: a small tin case. "Tallow and a striker. And—" his voice caught, steadied, "—there's a boat moored at the marsh gate on the city's west side. Fishermen leave it. Take it south along the channel and don't stop until the towers are out of sight."

"Why?" Theo asked. Not *why are you helping us*. Something deeper. "Why now?"

Wren smiled, and it was a terrible, beautiful, broken thing. "Because I told myself I was serving the Amarels to undo a theft. But somewhere along the way I stopped serving justice and started serving Vess's grief, and grief makes a very poor god." He looked at the dark corridor, then back at them. "Go. And Theo—if you reach the capital, if you reach anyone—remember that the thorn wants to be free. Everything it whispers will be true except the part where freedom helps anyone but it."

Then he stepped aside, held the lamp high enough to show them the way, and looked deliberately at the wall.

They went.

* * *

The water was colder than Sefa had promised, which she felt was rude of it, since she was the one who'd talked it up.

They waded the processional way in darkness, one hand on the wall, the tin case unopened because light attracted things with eyes and neither of them wanted to meet anything's eyes down here. Forty-one arches. Sefa counted aloud in a whisper, and Theo echoed each number, and somewhere around twenty-three the counting stopped being navigation and became a rosary, two voices keeping each other tethered in the black.

At thirty-seven, Theo's fingers found carved stone. "Pelican," they breathed. "Half gone. It's here."

The hatch fought them—centuries of silt welded into the frame—but the iron gave with a groan that echoed down the whole drowned hall, and both of them froze, hearts hammering, listening for boots or shouts or the particular silence of people coming to investigate a noise.

Nothing. Only water dripping somewhere, patient as a clock.

The shaft was everything Wren had promised and less: narrow, slick, smelling of bat and old stone, the handholds worn smooth by hands long dead. Sefa climbed first because she was lighter and because if she fell on Theo, at least Theo was sturdy. Ninety feet. She counted those too, though by sixty she'd lost the thread and started over twice, and by eighty her arms were shaking and her lungs felt full of wool.

Then her hand broke the surface of cool night air, and there was a rim, and there was sky—actual sky, bruise-colored, raining gently—and she hauled herself out onto the mossy stones of some forgotten ventilation court and lay there gasping like a landed fish while Theo rose out of the hole behind her, gray-faced and grinning.

"We're alive," Theo said.

"We're alive *and filthy*," Sefa said. "Prioritize correctly."

They lay side by side in the rain and laughed until it hurt, the wild helpless laughter of people who had genuinely believed, right up until this moment, that they would die in the dark below a drowned chapel. Then Theo helped her up, and they picked their way west through the ruins toward the marsh gate, toward Wren's borrowed boat, toward the long channel south.

It wasn't until they pushed off from the rotten mooring post, the drowned towers sinking behind them into the mist, that Sefa let herself think about Wren standing in that corridor, lamp lowered, choosing.

"He'll pay for it," she said quietly. "When they find the cell empty. Vess won't exile him. She'll use him."

Theo pulled the oars—badly; they were a terrible rower, and Sefa decided to allow it, tonight of all nights. "Then we make sure it was worth paying for," they said. "South, you said?"

"South along the channel. Two days to the crown road." She unfolded the scrap of map she'd redrawn from memory, holding it under the grey pre-dawn light, and frowned.

"What?"

"Smoke," she said. "On the road ahead. Campfires. A lot of them." She squinted. "And banners. Crown blue."

Theo went very still, the oars dripping.

Between them and the capital, camped across the whole width of the causeway like a hand closing, sat an army—and at its center, Sefa would learn soon enough, a captain named Ostrev who had crossed the marshes ahead of them, following a trail of rumor and burned villages straight toward the one person in the world he wanted most.

"Row faster," Sefa said.
