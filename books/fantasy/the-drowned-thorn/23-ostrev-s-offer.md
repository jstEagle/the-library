## 23. Ostrev's Offer

Ren Ostrev had learned, over thirty years of crown service, that the best place to corner a fugitive was not the place they were running *to* but the place they had to pass through to get anywhere at all.

In this case: a bridge.

The bridge in question was old and narrow and made of grey stone, spanning a river the color of weak tea, somewhere in the hill country south of Vaelmora's marshes. It had one entrance on each side, no cover within fifty paces, and — as of an hour past midnight — six of Ostrev's soldiers positioned where the road bent out of sight on the far bank. He himself sat on the near side, on a milestone, eating cold sausage and waiting for dawn the way a fisherman waits for a bite: patiently, hungrily, and with a certain professional resignation about how long it might take.

He did not have to wait long. The sun was barely clearing the hills when he saw the figure coming up the road from the marsh country — walking with the particular gait of someone who has slept badly for many nights and has stopped pretending otherwise. Young. Thin. A wooden sword hilt showing above one shoulder like a rude joke, because who carried a wooden sword? A person who could not afford to be found carrying a real one, that was who. Or a person whose weapon was not really the sword at all.

Ostrev finished his sausage, wiped his hands on his coat, and stood.

"Good morning," he called, pleasantly.

The figure stopped dead, twenty paces off. Even at that distance, in that light, Ostrev could see the moment of recognition travel through them like a shiver through a horse — the shoulders dropping half an inch, the weight shifting back onto the heels, the head tilting in that way people do when they are calculating whether the ditch is worth it.

"The river's high," Ostrev added helpfully. "You'd drown before you got your boots wet. I've watched two deer try it this week. The deer lost."

"I'm not going in the river," Theo Villalobos said. Their voice was hoarse, but steady. That was something. Most fugitives, by this point in a hunt, either begged or went silent as stones. This one sounded tired and annoyed, which Ostrev privately considered the healthiest possible condition for a human being.

"No," Ostrev agreed. "You're going to stand there, and we're going to talk, and then you're going to make a choice. That's all this is. A conversation."

"People in my experience don't bring six soldiers to conversations."

"Four are asleep behind the ridge and one is afraid of cows," Ostrev said. "But yes, fair. Six soldiers. I find it saves time. When I arrive alone, people argue with me for hours. When I arrive with soldiers, they listen." He spread his empty hands. "I'm listening now. Genuinely. Sit, if you like. There's a milestone. It's not comfortable, but it's honest company."

Theo did not sit. But they did not run, either, and after a long moment they came three steps closer, stopping where the morning light reached them fully — deliberately, Ostrev noted, putting themselves in view of the far bank too. Clever. If the soldiers moved, the speaker would see them move. The child had been learning fast.

"You burned my village," Theo said.

And there it was. Not a question. An accusation laid down on the milestone between them like a knife.

Ostrev had prepared for this. He had rehearsed answers on the ride north, in the dark, while his men snored around their fire. He had prepared justifications, explanations, the whole litany of *orders are orders* and *no one was meant to die* and *the elder woman chose her own path*. He had prepared them because they were true, or true enough, and because truth arranged correctly can look like innocence.

What came out of his mouth instead was: "Yes."

Theo blinked.

"Yes," Ostrev said again, because once apparently hadn't been enough even for him. "I burned Thornvale. My orders were to search it. The fire spread faster than it should have — the elder woman's house went up like pitch, and the wind did the rest — but I gave the order to advance with torches, so the fault is mine. Eleven houses. A granary. The mill. No deaths that I know of, though I won't insult you by claiming that as virtue. Your grandmother—" he paused, choosing, "—confessed to being a speaker. She bought the village time. She bought *you* time. I have wondered since whether she knew exactly what she was doing. I suspect she did. Elders usually do."

"She's dead," Theo said flatly.

"I know. I'm sorry." And the strange thing — the thing Ostrev would turn over in his mind for years afterward — was that he meant it. He had spent twenty years hunting the gifted and had told himself a hundred stories about why, and none of those stories had ever required him to be sorry. Sorry was new. Sorry had arrived sometime around the child in the marsh, and it had not left.

"You'll forgive me if I don't weep for your conscience," Theo said.

"I wouldn't dream of it." Ostrev gestured at the milestone again. "Sit. Please. What I have to say will take ten minutes, and you'll want your legs under you for the end of it."

To his mild surprise, Theo sat. Perched on the milestone like a bird ready to fly, wooden sword across their knees, eyes never leaving him — but they sat. Ostrev remained standing. He'd learned that standing over a seated fugitive read as threat, so he stepped to the side, leaned against the bridge rail, and put them roughly at eye level. Small courtesies. They cost nothing and bought everything.

"Here is what the crown believes," he began. "The crown believes that the Drowned Thorn is a weapon left lying in a marsh, and that you — the last of the line that bound it — are the key that fits the lock. The regent wants that key in a crown hand. Preferably on a chain. There are men at court who would prefer the key melted down entirely, and men who want to use it, and men who simply want the *problem* gone before coronation day. You've met some of these opinions already, wearing uniforms."

"The Amarels aren't wearing uniforms."

"No. They're wearing piety, which is worse. But that's not why I'm here." Ostrev took a breath. This was the part he had not rehearsed, because he had only decided to say it an hour ago, sitting in the dark, watching the river, thinking about a boy named Aldric who had died at fourteen with frost on his eyelashes, and about a different child in a marsh more recently, and about what exactly Ren Ostrev was becoming if he kept walking this road.

"Here is what *I* believe," he said. "I believe you don't want to be a weapon. I believe you ran from Thornvale to avoid being one, and everything since — the marshes, the chapel, the blood they took from you beneath the cathedral — happened *to* you rather than because of you. I believe that if you wanted the thorn awake, it would be awake already, and we would all be living in a very different and much shorter story."

Theo said nothing. But their grip on the wooden sword loosened, slightly. Ostrev counted that as a door opening a crack.

"And here is what I *know*," he continued. "I know that four days ago, my superiors intercepted word of the Amarel ritual. I know the Unbinding was begun under the cathedral and interrupted — barely. I know that whatever remains of that ritual is still coiled in your blood, waiting, and that both the Amarels and the crown would crack you open like an egg to get at it." He let that sit. "I also know where your villagers are."

The change in Theo was immediate and total. The tiredness burned off them like mist; they came up off the milestone and the wooden sword was somehow in their hand, though Ostrev noted with distant professional approval that they didn't raise it.

"If you've hurt them—"

"They're alive," Ostrev said quickly. "All forty-one of them. Held at Corven's Mill, a supply depot two days east. Fed, sheltered, unharmed — I have that on my own authority, I inspected the roster myself, because I wanted to know what my orders were building. They're being held as leverage. Against you. Which brings me," and here his voice went dry, "to the offer. Which you have already guessed, because you're not stupid, and I apologize in advance for insulting you by saying it aloud."

"Say it anyway."

"Speak the Binding Oath. Bind the thorn to the crown, willingly, in front of witnesses, at a time and place of state choosing. Serve as the realm's sworn speaker — a title, lands, protection, a life that is yours in every way that matters. In exchange: the forty-one go free. Pardons. Clean names. Thornvale rebuilt with crown stone and crown coin, if they want to go home. Every one of them walks out of Corven's Mill and no soldier ever bothers them again." He held up a finger. "And before you ask — yes, I can deliver this. It's already drafted. I wrote it myself. The regent has signed it. It is the single most generous document this hunt has produced, and the reason it exists is that the crown finally understands it cannot take what you carry. It can only ask."

The wind came down the valley and pushed at them both. Somewhere upstream a heron complained about the morning.

"And if I refuse?" Theo asked quietly.

Ostrev looked at the river for a moment. He had known this question was coming, and he had decided — in the dark, on the ride north — that he would answer it honestly, because he was done with the other kind of answer, the kind that lets a man sleep while villages burn.

"If you refuse," he said, "I arrest you. I take you to the capital. And I will not lie to you about what happens there, because you've seen the crypts under the cathedral and you know. The Amarels will come for you inside whatever cell they put you in, and the crown will pretend not to notice, and your villagers will stay at Corven's Mill until someone decides they're no longer useful, and I will spend the rest of my life knowing I offered you the one decent bargain in this whole rotten affair and you were right to spit on it." He shrugged, heavily. "That's the shape of it. Those are your choices. I didn't build this trap. I'm just the man standing in it, holding the door."

For a long time Theo said nothing. They turned the wooden sword over in their hands — and Ostrev saw, suddenly, the wear on it. Years of wear. The handle polished pale by a single pair of palms, practicing in secret, in a valley where magic couldn't follow, holding onto something they weren't allowed to use. His chest did something complicated and unwelcome.

"My grandmother used to say," Theo said at last, "that a gift freely given is worth ten taken by force. She said it about eggs, mostly. But I think she meant it about everything." They looked up. "You're asking me to give the crown the one thing everyone else has tried to steal."

"I'm asking you to choose the least bad master," Ostrev said. "I won't dress it up. The crown is a poor master. It is vain and frightened and it burns things when it's scared. But it is a *known* master, and it keeps its written promises, because its whole existence depends on paper meaning something. The Amarels keep promises too — to their own blood, and to a drowned kingdom, and God help everyone else. Choose."

"And you?" Theo's voice sharpened. "If I do this — if I kneel in front of the regent and speak your oath — what do you become? My jailer? My keeper? The man who follows me around for the rest of my life making sure the weapon stays polite?"

Ostrev opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer had arrived before the diplomatic one, and for once in his career he let the honest one win.

"I become your witness," he said. "The man who can stand up at tribunal, or at court, or in front of the mob, and say: I hunted this person for a year. I burned their village. I know exactly what they could have done to me at any point along that road — and look, I'm still alive, and so is everyone else they met. There is no better argument for what you are than me, Theo Villalobos. I am the crown's own evidence against its own fears." He smiled, thin and tired. "Assuming you leave me alive, which, given my conduct to date, would be an act of generosity I haven't earned."

"Don't flatter yourself," Theo said. "I haven't decided anything."

"No. But you're arguing with me instead of running, which from you I understand is practically a marriage proposal."

Something happened to Theo's face then — a twitch at the corner of the mouth, quickly suppressed, that Ostrev recognized with a jolt as the beginning of a laugh. *There you are*, he thought. *A person. Under all that grief, a person.* He filed it away carefully, the way he'd once filed away maps, because he suspected he would need the memory later, on harder days.

They stood together on the bridge while the sun climbed and the tea-colored river went on about its business below. And Ostrev waited, because he had learned that the most important words in any negotiation are the ones you don't say while the other person is deciding.

It was Theo who broke the silence, and when they spoke, their voice had changed — quieter, careful, the voice of someone testing ice.

"Take me to them first."

Ostrev frowned. "To—"

"Corven's Mill. Before I swear anything to anyone, I see them. All forty-one. I count them myself. I look them in the face." Theo's jaw set. "Because I've heard promises from the crown before, Captain. I heard them in Thornvale, years ago, when the surveyors came mapping trade routes and asking very precise questions. So: the village first. Then the oath. Not the other way round."

Every instinct Ostrev had honed over thirty years screamed that this was leverage walking away from him — that once the speaker stood among their own people, surrounded by forty-one pairs of pleading eyes, no bargain on paper would hold. That the smart move was to refuse, to insist on the oath first, to keep the egg intact until it was cracked on the crown's terms.

He thought about Aldric. He thought about the marsh child. He thought about a burning granary and an old woman buying time with her confession, and about what kind of man he intended to be for whatever years he had left.

"All right," he said.

Theo stared at him. "Just like that?"

"Just like that. We ride for Corven's Mill within the hour. You'll ride beside me, unbound, unchained, with my sword within your reach and my reputation within your grasp, and if you decide on the road that this was all a trick, well." He picked up his hat from the milestone and settled it on his head. "Then I'll have learned something about trust the hard way, which at my age is the only way I learn anything."

Theo rose slowly from the milestone, wooden sword still in hand, studying him as though he were a text in a language they were almost fluent in.

"Why?" they asked simply. "Why any of this? Why risk it?"

Ostrev looked east, toward the hills, toward the mill, toward forty-one people who did not yet know their fate was being decided on a bridge by a tired captain and a fugitive with a wooden sword.

"Because someone has to start keeping accounts," he said, "and I owe this ledger more than I can ever pay. Come on. The horses are behind the ridge — and fair warning, the bay one bites. It's the only honest creature in this entire army."

* * *

They rode east through the morning, and Ostrev found himself doing something he had not done in years: talking. Not interrogating — talking. About the roads, the weather, the scandalous price of oats, the cow-fearing soldier (whose name was Brannic, and who would never forgive him). Theo answered in monosyllables at first, then sentences, and somewhere past the second hill they asked a question that stopped him mid-rein:

"The oath I'd speak. The Binding. Do you know what it costs?"

And Ostrev, who had read the old reports, who knew what the Villalobos line had paid across centuries, felt the honest answer rise in him again like water finding its level.

"No," he admitted. "But I think you do. And I think that's why you're asking to see them first — because you already know the price, and you want to be sure it's worth paying." He met their eyes. "So am I, Theo. So am I."

Ahead, past the hills, the smoke of Corven's Mill was just visible against the sky — and with it, faintly, something else: riders on the far road, flying no colors he recognized, moving fast toward the same destination.

Ostrev's hand went to his sword.

"That's not my escort," he said quietly.
