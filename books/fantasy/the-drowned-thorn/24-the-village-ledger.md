## 24. The Village Ledger

They brought Theo to the prisoners at midday, which was a cruelty dressed as courtesy.

"Captain wants you to see them," said the young soldier walking beside them—Corporal Vess, who had the habit of apologizing with his eyebrows every time he gave an order he didn't like. "He thought it would help you decide."

*Help me decide.* As if Theo were a merchant weighing two bolts of cloth instead of a person deciding whether to hand their own blood over to the thing that had burned their home.

The stockade had been a wool warehouse once, back when Marrow's Crossing was a town that traded rather than a garrison that guarded. The soldiers had cleared out the bales and put up fences in three days, and now behind those fences stood forty-one people from Thornvale, and Theo counted twice because the first count made their chest hurt.

Forty-one. The valley had held better than two hundred.

They knew some of the faces. That was the worst of it—not strangers suffering, but *specific* suffering. Old Tam Wicker, who had once paid Theo three eggs to carve his name into a fence post so his wife would stop saying he couldn't spell. The Fenwick sisters, who ran the dye house and argued so loudly about mordants that you could hear them from the millpond. Little Ansel Brandt, nine years old, sitting on the packed dirt with his knees drawn up, watching the guards with the flat patience of a child who had learned too early that crying changed nothing.

Ansel's mother had died on the ridge the night of the fire. Theo had seen her fall. They had run anyway.

That was the ledger, then. That was what refusal cost, written not in ink but in faces.

"Theo!" Tam Wicker spotted them through the fence and came hobbling forward, one arm cradled against his chest in a sling made from somebody's shirt. His voice carried the way it always had, big and unbothered, a man who had spent sixty years talking over sheep. "Theo Villalobos! By the saints, they've let you in. Are you all right? You look terrible."

"I've been traveling," Theo said.

"You look like you've been *buried* and traveling." Tam gripped the fence slats with his good hand and peered at them with frank, grandfatherly appraisal. "Have you eaten? They feed us turnip soup. I don't recommend it. I don't even think it's turnip. I think it's the *idea* of turnip, boiled until it gives up."

A few of the others drifted closer. Mrs. Fenwick—Edda, the elder sister, the one who was right about mordants—pressed her face between two slats. Behind her, someone started quietly weeping, and someone else shushed them, gently, the way you'd shush wind rattling a shutter.

Theo stood there in the mud with Ostrev's offer sitting in their stomach like a swallowed stone. *Speak the Binding. Serve the crown. And these people walk free.*

They didn't know. That was the thing that kept Theo upright. These people—the ones who had shared bread with them, who had watched them practice sword forms in the mist and assumed it was just exercise, who had never once suspected that the quiet Villalobos grandchild was carrying a word in their blood old enough to drown kingdoms—they did not know what Theo was. To them, Theo was just the last survivor of the fire, another refugee, another face in the crown's net.

If they knew—if they understood that one sentence from Theo's mouth could open this fence—

"They say you're negotiating," Edda Fenwick said. Her voice was careful, stripped of its usual dye-house thunder. "With the captain. About terms."

"I'm—" Theo began.

"Take them," said a voice from the back. A man's voice, cracked and urgent. It was Joren Hale, the farrier, whose forge had been the warmest place in the valley on winter evenings. He pushed through the crowd to the fence, and his eyes were red-rimmed and desperate. "Whatever they're asking. Take it. Take whatever it is."

"Joren," Tam said, low.

"No. No, I'll say it." Joren's hands wrapped around the fence slats hard enough to whiten his knuckles. He looked at Theo with an intensity that felt like being grabbed by the collar. "My daughter is in here. Mira. She's six. She hasn't had a proper meal in eleven days. Whatever the crown wants from you—a signature, an oath, your labor, your *name*, I don't care—say yes. Say yes and get us out of here."

The stone in Theo's stomach turned over.

He didn't know. Joren didn't know that the price wasn't a signature or labor or a name. The price was becoming the weapon. The price was speaking an oath that would bind the Drowned Thorn to the crown's will—and the thorn, bound, was a leash on something that could crack mountains, and whoever held the leash would hold everything, forever, and everyone like Theo would spend the rest of their lives as instruments or corpses.

But how could Theo explain that to a father whose daughter was eating idea-of-turnip soup?

"I'm trying to fix this," Theo said. It came out smaller than they wanted.

"Fix it faster," Joren said, and there was no cruelty in it, only exhaustion, only love worn down to its bare wire. "Please. I'm begging you. Fix it faster."

*Guilt is a tool,* Ostrev had said yesterday, in that level surveyor's voice, spreading his maps across the table like a man laying out a dinner. *I don't enjoy using it. But I have learned that most people cannot be reasoned with, and nearly everyone can be shown.* He had arranged it precisely for this—had timed Theo's visit for midday, when the hunger was loudest, when the children would be visible. He hadn't lied once. That was the terrible craft of him. Every word true, every truth a hook.

Tam Wicker leaned close to the fence, lowering his big voice to something almost private. "Listen to me, child. Don't mind Joren. He's grieving and hungry and he loves that girl more than air. But also—" he sighed, and his shoulders sagged under sixty years and a bad sling, "—also don't mind me when I say the same thing, because I will. We're tired, Theo. Some of us aren't going to last a winter in here. If there's a door, walk through it. Nobody in this valley would want you to stand outside it on our account."

And that was worse. That was so much worse than Joren's desperation, because Tam meant it kindly. They all meant it kindly. Forty-one people pressing against a fence, begging Theo to save them, and not one of them knowing that the salvation they were asking for was the exact thing Grandmother Ilsa had burned her life's archive to prevent.

*Ilsa.* Theo's throat closed. Somewhere in this camp or another, or nowhere at all, was the answer to whether she still lived. She had stayed behind with her false confession, buying minutes with her own surrender, and Theo had run east as instructed, and the instruction had worked, and working instructions are cold comfort when you can't stop imagining the sound of the door closing behind her.

"How is she?" Theo asked Tam, barely above a whisper. "Do you know? Have any of you seen—"

Tam's face did something complicated. "We heard she was taken south. To the capital, maybe. Alive." He paused. "Alive as of three weeks ago, when the last cart went through. That's all anyone knows, child. I'm sorry."

Alive. Three weeks ago. Theo held onto that the way a drowning person holds a plank—gratefully, and without any illusions about how long planks float.

* * *

They let Theo stay an hour. Vess hovered at a polite distance, radiating apologetic eyebrows, while Theo walked the fence line and let people talk to them.

It was strange, being looked at like that. Like hope with legs. In Thornvale, Theo had been the quiet one, the odd one, the Villalobos who kept to themselves and practiced with a wooden sword nobody ever asked about. Useful for lifting heavy things. Fine at cards. Not anyone's savior.

Now little Ansel Brandt had come to the fence and simply stood there, close to Theo's boots, not speaking, the way children do when they've decided proximity is enough. Theo crouched down to his level.

"Hello, Ansel."

"Are you going to get us out?"

The question landed like a hammer with no handle—nothing to grip, nowhere to put it down. "I'm trying."

"Mama said you'd try." Ansel studied the mud. Then, with the devastating practicality of the very young: "Trying isn't the same as doing, though. Papa says that about the roof."

"Papa's right about the roof."

"He's right about most things except horses. He thinks horses respect him. They don't. They can tell."

Despite everything—despite the fence and the soup and the stone in their stomach—Theo felt their mouth twitch. "What do horses know that your papa doesn't?"

"That he's scared of them." Ansel shrugged. "Everybody's scared of something. Even the guards. The tall one is scared of the small one. I figured it out."

Theo glanced over at the two sentries by the gate. The tall one did keep glancing at the small one, who indeed seemed to be in a permanent state of disapproval. "You should be a surveyor," Theo said. "Or a spy."

"Don't tell them I said that."

"Your secret's safe."

Ansel nodded solemnly, satisfied, and went back to his spot in the dirt, and Theo stood up feeling like they'd been handed something fragile and told not to drop it.

That was the whole camp, really. Forty-one fragile things, and Theo holding all of them, and Ostrev's voice in their ear: *They can be shown.*

By the gate, Edda Fenwick caught Theo's sleeve as they passed. Up close, Theo could see how much weight she'd lost; her wrists looked like kindling. "There's a list," she said quietly. "The sick ones. I've been keeping it. Fever, mostly, and the coughing. Eleven names as of this morning." She pressed a scrap of paper into Theo's hand—actual paper, hoarded, precious—with names written in dye-stained letters, each one annotated in a shorthand that spoke of long nights and short supplies. "Give it to the captain. Tell him dead prisoners negotiate poorly. He strikes me as a man who understands ledgers."

"He does," Theo said. "It's practically his religion."

"Then preach to him in his own church." Edda's eyes were dry and fierce. "And Theo—" she glanced around, lowered her voice further, "—whatever it is they want from you. The soldiers talk. They talk too much, men who are bored. Something about oaths. Something about you being able to do things." Her gaze was steady and unafraid, and Theo's heart stopped and restarted. "I dyed cloth for thirty years. I know when someone's hiding the expensive color under the cheap one. I won't say anything. I just want you to know that I see you, and I'm not afraid of you, and if the choice is between you selling yourself to that man and us rotting in here comfortably ignorant—then sell yourself, and may every saint forgive the asking."

She squeezed Theo's hand once, hard, and walked away before Theo could find a single word.

* * *

Ostrev was waiting outside the stockade, arms folded, watching the gate with the patience of a man who had already read the ending of the letter.

"Well?" he said.

Theo held up Edda's list. "Eleven sick. Two might not last the week. Your prisoners make poor collateral if they die before the deal closes."

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, but Theo had gotten better at reading him, and they were fairly sure it was genuine displeasure. "I'll have the surgeon attend them."

"Your surgeon attended the Brandt boy's fever last month," Theo said. "Vess told me. He prescribed rest and thin broth. The boy is nine, Captain. He weighs less than my grandmother's dog."

"My resources are stretched across four garrisons."

"Then stretch them differently." Theo stepped closer, and kept their voice level, because shouting would lose and level might win. "You want me to believe serving the crown is the honorable road. Fine. Show me honor. Feed them. Treat the sick. Let the children have real food. Do it *now*, before I've agreed to anything, and maybe I'll believe there's a version of your offer that isn't just a prettier cage."

Ostrev regarded them for a long moment. A crow called somewhere beyond the palisade, rude and indifferent. When he finally spoke, his voice was quieter than usual, which from Ostrev was more alarming than shouting.

"You think I enjoy this."

"I think you've gotten very good at not enjoying things productively."

His jaw tightened. For a heartbeat Theo saw the other man underneath—the one whose brother had died in an uprising, the one who had knelt in ash somewhere and sworn never again—and then the captain's composure reassembled itself, brick by brick.

"The food improves today," he said. "The surgeon visits tomorrow. The children get the first shares." He unfolded his arms. "Not because you've bargained well. Because I am not the monster your grief has decided I am, and I would prefer you learn that before you make your choice."

"And if I never choose?"

"Then the ledger keeps growing." He said it without relish, without threat—just arithmetic, flat and awful. "That's all a siege is, Villalobos. A ledger with a slow hand. I'd rather you wrote the last line than I did."

He walked away toward the command tent, and Theo stood alone in the trampled mud between the stockade and the gray sky, holding a list of eleven names, hearing forty-one voices, hearing one of them above all the rest:

*Say yes. Say yes and get us out of here.*

* * *

That night, in the narrow cell they'd given Theo—better quarters than the prisoners, worse than a guest, the geometry of leverage made architectural—Theo sat on the cot with Edda's list and could not stop doing the arithmetic.

Every day of refusal was a day of thin broth. Every day of hesitation was a night of coughing in a cold warehouse. And somewhere in the capital, maybe, Grandmother Ilsa sat in a room like this one, having made her own calculation months ago, and had decided Theo was worth the spending.

*Run east,* she'd said. *Don't look back.* And Theo had run east, and found the drowned city, and found the oaths, and found out that everything Ilsa taught them was built on a theft—that the Villalobos line weren't heroes but jailers, that the binding belonged by rights to another family entirely, that the whole inheritance was a wound wearing a bandage.

So what was Theo supposed to hand the crown? A stolen thing? Their own years, word by word, aging decades to become a leash-holder for men like Ostrev—who was not a monster, that was the trap of him, who was a grieving brother with a ledger and a uniform?

Theo lay back and stared at the ceiling beams.

Through the wall, faintly, they could hear the camp settling. A guard changing shift. Wind in the palisade. And beneath it all, so faint they might have imagined it, a pulse—not in the ears, but in the blood. The thorn. Sleeping in them now, bound by the third oath, theirs alone, patient as roots.

*You carry me,* it seemed to murmur, in the wordless way it had. *And they ask you to spend yourself. And the sick are coughing. And the child counts the guards' fears.*

"Be quiet," Theo whispered to their own veins.

The pulse continued, gentle, insufferable, *right*.

Because here was the thought that had been circling all evening and finally landed: Wren had taught them the oaths. Binding, Unbinding, and the third. But Wren had also taught them, in passing, half-drunk on marsh wine, that the gift moved along other channels than oaths. Small workings. A blessing here, a mending there. Words spent freely, drawn from the speaker's own years—one word, one year, roughly. A terrible exchange rate. An obscene one.

And in the cell next to the infirmary tent, according to Vess's reluctant gossip, lay a woman named Petra Sorrel, thirty-one years old, feverish, fading, mother of none but aunt to half the valley, and the surgeon's rest-and-broth was not going to reach her in time.

One year. One word. One life.

Theo sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and looked at their hands—hands that had held a wooden sword for twenty years and never once been allowed to mean anything.

"If I start," they murmured to the listening dark, "I don't know where it stops."

The thorn pulsed, warm and terrible, like an answer, like a dare.

Outside, the sentry called the midnight hour, and Theo rose, and took the list, and went to find out exactly how much a year was worth.
