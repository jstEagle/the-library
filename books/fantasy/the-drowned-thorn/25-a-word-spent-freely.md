## 25. A Word Spent Freely

The cell smelled of wet straw and old iron, and the man in it was dying the way a candle dies—steadily, without complaint, as if he'd made his peace with the dark.

Theo stood at the bars with Ostrev's offer still ringing in their ears. *Speak the Binding and serve the crown, and the surviving villagers of Thornvale go free.* It had been three days since he'd said it, three days of Theo being fed well and left alone in a way that felt less like mercy and more like a farmer leaving grain to ripen.

The dying man coughed. A wet, rattling sound that Theo felt in their own chest.

"You shouldn't be down here," the man said. His voice was papery, amused. "Prisoners' row's no place for visitors. Bad for the constitution." He laughed, which turned into more coughing.

"I'm not a visitor," Theo said. "I'm a prisoner too. Just one with better accommodations."

"Ah." The man squinted at them through the gloom. He was perhaps fifty, though sickness had hollowed him out so thoroughly it was hard to say. "One of the important ones, then. They've got you in the cell with the window."

It was true. Theo's cell had a window—a slit of gray sky high on the wall that told them dawn from dusk and nothing else. They'd counted eleven dawns since Thornvale burned.

"What's your name?" Theo asked.

"Bram. Bram Holtz." He shifted against the wall with a wince. "Debtor's row. Owed the wool guild nine crowns. Nine! You'd think I'd robbed a church." Another cough, longer this time. "Course, now they're saying the whole block's to be cleared out for... special prisoners. Political ones. Us debtors are to be moved to the marsh camps." He said it lightly, the way people say things they've decided not to be frightened of.

Theo's stomach turned. The marsh camps. Everyone knew what happened in the marsh camps; it was simply that no one said it aloud, the way no one said aloud that the king's new coinage was clipped silver.

"How long have you been sick?" Theo asked.

Bram waved a hand. "Since before it was interesting. Lungs full of marsh rot from my younger days. The physicker looked at me last week, got that face they get—you know the face—and prescribed me kind words and a better attitude." He grinned, gap-toothed. "I told him I'd rather have wine."

*You know the face.* Yes. Theo knew it. They'd seen it on Ilsa's face the night the dogs came, when she'd kissed their forehead and pushed them toward the ridge. The face of someone measuring time and finding it short.

Something moved in Theo's chest—not the gift, not exactly, but the space where the gift lived, the dormant weight of it. Since Vaelmora, since the crypt, since the thorn had poured itself into their blood like ink into water, Theo could feel words the way a sailor feels weather. Most days they kept the feeling corked down tight. But here, alone in the dark with a stranger's rattling breath—

Here, the words were *right there*. Sitting behind their teeth like coins in a purse.

They thought about what Wren had taught them, in those few frantic lessons before everything went wrong. *Every word has weight, Theo. The old tongues have more. And the price is always paid from the same purse.*

From your years. From your life.

A year for a healing, Wren had guessed. Perhaps more for a bad one.

Theo looked at Bram Holtz, who owed nine crowns and would die in a marsh camp for it, coughing his lungs out among strangers, and felt something settle in them that had been unsettled for a long time.

For weeks now, everyone had been trying to spend Theo's words for them. Ostrev wanted the Binding Oath, spent on the crown's ledger. The Amarels had wanted the Unbinding, spent on their stolen legacy. Even Ilsa's letter—even the thorn itself—had been a kind of reaching for Theo's mouth, trying to put words in it.

Well. Here was a word no one had asked for. A word spent freely, on a debtor with marsh rot, for no reason except that it was right.

"I can help you," Theo said.

Bram blinked. "With respect, lad—or lass, or whichever—I've seen the physicker. There's naught to help *with*."

"I'm not a physicker."

"No, clearly not. Physickers don't look like they've been dragged behind a horse." Bram squinted again. "What are you, then?"

Theo hesitated. Every instinct honed over twenty-three years of hiding said *say nothing, be no one, keep the secret until the secret kills you anyway.* But the hiding was over. The hiding had been over since the night the dogs came, and all it had ever bought them was a burning valley and a grandmother's empty chair.

"I'm the last swordspeaker," Theo said. It was strange how easy it was, once said. "And I'm going to heal you, if you'll let me."

The silence that followed had texture. Somewhere down the row, another prisoner muttered in his sleep. Water dripped somewhere with the patience of stone.

"That's a fairy story," Bram said finally, but his voice had changed. Smaller. "My gran used to tell stories about speakers. Said they could talk a blade sharp and talk a fever out of a child. She said—" He stopped. Coughed. "She said they all died young."

"Some of us," Theo said quietly, "are working on it."

* * *

Getting to Bram was its own small war.

The gaoler, a broad woman named Doss with forearms like hams and a ring of keys like a bell choir, refused at first on principle. Then Theo mentioned Captain Ostrev's name, and her refusal developed a nervous quality. Then Theo said, "The captain wants me alive and cooperative. I'm asking you to let me sit with a dying man. Decide which order matters more to you," and Doss, who had clearly been doing arithmetic of her own since the special prisoner arrived, unlocked the door with the expression of a woman washing her hands of the whole affair.

"One hour," she said. "And if he dies while you're in there, that's on your head, not mine."

"He's dying either way," Theo said. "That's rather the point."

The cell was barely wide enough for two bodies. Theo sat on the straw beside Bram, who watched them with the wary hope of a man who has been promised things by institutions before.

"So how does it work?" he asked. "The speaking. Do you wave your hands? Chant? Sacrifice a goat?"

"No goats." Theo flexed their fingers, suddenly nervous. Their hands knew sword-forms; they did not know this. "The old tongue does the work. I just... pay for it."

"Pay with what?"

Theo considered lying, decided against it. This man was going to watch something impossible happen to his body; he deserved the honest ledger. "Years. Of my life. A healing costs about a year, Wren thinks. Wren's the priest who taught me. Defrocked," they added, because Bram looked impressed. "So possibly unreliable, but he's all I've got."

"A year." Bram stared at them. "You'd give a year of your life. For me. For a debtor who owes nine crowns and smells like this cell."

"When you put it that way it sounds foolish."

"It sounds *mad*." But Bram's eyes had gone bright and wet, and he turned his face to the wall for a moment, mastering himself. When he turned back, he said, "Why?"

And there it was—the question underneath everything. Why give anything? Why had Ilsa given her confession, her freedom, probably her life? Why had Sefa torn up a map worth her family's freedom? Why had Ostrev, that morning in the rain, broken his orders?

Because the world ran on ledgers, and someone had to write a different entry in one.

"Because everyone wants to spend me," Theo said. "The crown, the Amarels, the thorn itself. Everyone's been holding out their hand for my words since the day I was born, and none of them asked." They took a breath. "This one's mine. I'm choosing it. That's the whole answer. It's not a good answer, but it's mine."

Bram looked at them for a long moment. Then he nodded, slow and solemn, like a man agreeing to terms.

"All right," he said. "All right, speaker. Spend away."

* * *

The old tongue did not live in books, exactly. Books held its bones. But the living part of it—the part that mattered—lived in intent, in the shape of meaning you held in your mind while the syllables passed your lips. Wren had drilled this into them: *the word is the vessel, but you are the cargo.*

Theo closed their eyes and reached for the word.

It rose easily, eagerly, the way the thorn always rose now—*Vaelmora's black bloom, living in my blood, patient as winter.* The word for mending. The word for whole-ness. In the old tongue it sounded like water finding its level, like a bone sliding back into joint.

*Selvaren.*

They spoke it.

The world *tilted*.

Not visibly—nothing moved—but Theo felt it the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expected. A year. They felt a year leave them, a whole year, twelve months of mornings and suppers and small joys, drawn out through the roots of their teeth and down through their palms and into—

Into Bram.

Warmth flooded the cell. Not light—warmth, deep and green, like standing in a sunlit orchard. Theo heard Bram gasp, heard the rattle in his lungs smooth and soften, heard breath move through him clean for the first time in decades. The marsh rot didn't fight; it simply *left*, unwinding like thread pulled from cloth, and in its place Bram's lungs filled and filled and filled.

When Theo opened their eyes, the cell seemed brighter, though nothing had changed. Bram was sitting up straighter than he likely had in years, breathing in great astonished gulps, one hand pressed flat to his own chest.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, *oh*." He laughed—a big, unbroken sound. "It's quiet in there. Do you know how long it's been quiet in there? Forty years I've had a whistle in my chest and now it's *quiet*—" He grabbed Theo's arm, then let go as if scalded. "Your hair."

"My hair?"

"There's—" Bram gestured vaguely. "Gray. At your temples. Wasn't there before, was it?"

Theo touched their temple. Came away with two silver strands. Only two—but they both knew it was the visible tip of a much larger withdrawal. Somewhere inside, a year had been spent, and the body kept honest books even when the mind didn't want to read them.

"Worth it?" Bram asked softly.

Theo found, to their surprise, that they were smiling. Tired, older by a year, two strands grayer—and smiling. "Ask me tomorrow. Tonight it feels like the first thing I've done in months that I actually chose."

* * *

By morning, half the prison knew.

Doss came down the row at first light with her keys jingling and an expression caught between awe and terror. She didn't speak of it directly—gaolers never do—but she brought Theo bread with honey on it, which no prisoner had received in living memory, and she lingered at the bars.

"The men are saying things," she said finally. "About what you did for Holtz. Some of them are saying you could do it for others. Some are saying you should mind your own business before the captain hears." She paused. "Some are saying both."

"What are *you* saying?"

Doss looked down the row, where Bram Holtz was doing push-ups against the wall, laughing at himself, breathing like a bellows. "I'm saying I've worked this block nineteen years and I've never once seen a debtor cry from good news." She met Theo's eyes. "Whatever you're playing at, speaker—it landed soft."

But of course, it couldn't stay soft. Nothing in Theo's life stayed soft for long.

Ostrev came at midday.

He stood outside the cell in his rain-gray uniform, hands clasped behind his back, and looked at Theo for a long moment with an expression Theo couldn't read. Not anger. Something more complicated.

"Doss tells me you healed a prisoner," he said. "Unsanctioned. Unwitnessed. At unknown cost to yourself."

"Is that a crime?"

"Everything you do is a crime. That's rather the arrangement we have." He stepped closer to the bars. "Why him? He's nobody. A debtor. You gained nothing."

"That was the point."

Ostrev's jaw tightened. Theo watched him wrestle with it—the officer who believed the gifted were a fire that must be controlled, confronted with a fire that had just spent itself warming a stranger. He opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"The physicker examined Holtz this morning," he said at last. "Forty years of marsh rot, gone. Completely gone. He's requested permission to study the phenomenon." A pause. "I denied the request."

"Why?"

"Because if I let him poke at it, I'd have to report it up the chain. And if I report it up the chain..." He trailed off, and Theo understood: the crown would come for the gift itself, would try to bottle it, breed it, weaponize it. Or worse—they'd hear *speaker heals debtor for free* and start demanding miracles on schedule.

"You covered for me," Theo said slowly.

"I did *not* cover for you. I declined to escalate a matter of no strategic significance." Ostrev's voice was stiff as starched linen. "There's a difference. Legally."

Despite everything—the burning valley, the imprisoned villagers, the gilded cage closing around them—Theo felt laughter rise in their chest, tired and real. "Thank you, Captain."

"Don't thank me. And don't do it again." He turned to go, then stopped. Didn't turn back. "Holtz asked me to tell you something. He said—he said, 'Tell the speaker the year wasn't wasted. Tell them I'll make it count.'" Ostrev's shoulders shifted, almost uncomfortably. "I don't know what that means. But he was crying when he said it, and I've arrested a lot of men, and very few of them cried for good reasons."

He walked away, boots ringing on stone.

Theo sat back against the wall of their too-good cell, beneath their little window with its slice of sky, and pressed their fingers to the gray at their temples.

One year. One stranger saved. One small, stubborn proof that the gift could be something other than a weapon—that *they* could be something other than a weapon.

It wouldn't be enough. Ostrev's bargain still hung over them, the villagers still rotted in a holding camp, the Amarels still hunted them, and somewhere out there the thorn pulsed in their blood like a second heartbeat, waiting for the eclipse. One healed debtor changed nothing in the great ledger of the realm.

But it changed the only ledger Theo had ever truly kept—the one where they tallied what they'd chosen versus what had been chosen for them. And in that ledger, for the first time since Thornvale burned, the columns balanced.

They were still smiling when the guard came at dusk with supper—and with it, a folded scrap of paper, smuggled, smelling faintly of marsh water and ink.

Theo unfolded it by the light of their little window.

Three words, in a slanting hand they knew too well:

*Hold on. Coming.*

Sefa.
