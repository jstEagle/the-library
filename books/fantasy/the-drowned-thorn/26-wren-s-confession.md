## 26. Wren's Confession

Wren Calloway had told many lies in his life, and he had gotten reasonably good at it, which was the part that shamed him most. A bad liar at least suffered for his sins. A good one got comfortable.

He sat in the ruined boathouse on the edge of the marsh with a cup of chicory going cold in his hands, watching Theo sleep against the wall like someone who had learned to sleep anywhere, quickly, with one hand near the wooden sword. Sefa was out checking her snares, or pretending to. The tide breathed in and out through the broken planks below them, and somewhere under all that water, the thorn was dreaming, and Wren could feel it the way you feel weather coming in your knees.

It was time. He'd known it was time for three days. He'd spent those three days finding excellent reasons why tomorrow would be better.

*You're a coward,* said his old rector's voice, the one that had once called him "the finest student of homiletics this diocese has produced and the worst man in it."

"Yes," Wren murmured to the empty boathouse. "Thank you for that."

Theo stirred. "Talking to yourself again?"

"Practicing a confession. It's traditional to rehearse."

"Are you converting to something?"

Wren laughed despite himself — a dry, cracked sound, like a door in an abandoned house. "No, child. Quite the opposite."

* * *

He waited until Sefa came back with two marsh hens and a look of triumph, because he wanted them both present when he did this. Partly for accountability. Mostly because if they were going to hate him, he wanted it done efficiently, in one sitting, so he could stop dreading it.

"I have something to tell you," he said, "and I'd ask you to hear all of it before anyone throws anything. The hens are innocent bystanders."

Sefa set down the birds slowly. Theo didn't move at all, which was worse.

"You already know I was defrocked," Wren began. "You know I served at Vaelmora chapel before the water took it. What I haven't told you is who I serve now." He turned his cup in his hands. "The Amarels."

The name landed in the room like a dropped stone. Theo's face went very still — that particular stillness Wren had learned to read as danger, the stillness of someone counting exits.

"Before you reach for the sword," Wren said, "which is wood, and will do you no credit—"

"The Amarels tried to take my blood in a crypt under the cathedral."

"They did. And I knew they intended to take you. I led you toward them, in a manner of speaking, and I have hated myself for it every day since, which I understand is cold comfort, but it is the truth, and I am apparently constitutionally incapable of dying without telling the truth at maximum inconvenience to everyone including myself."

Sefa had gone pale. "The commission," she said quietly. "The map. You were the stranger who paid me."

"I paid you," Wren admitted. "In crown coin, which the Amarels supplied, because nothing launders money quite like a pilgrim road. I chose you specifically, Mistress Sefa, because your reputation said you'd guide anyone anywhere for the right price, and I needed someone whose motives would look mercenary rather than suspicious."

"That's flattering," Sefa said, in the voice she used for gutting fish.

"It wasn't meant to be. Very little about me is."

Theo spoke for the first time. Their voice was flat and careful. "Why are you telling us now?"

And there it was. The question he'd been circling for three days like a bird over open water.

* * *

"Because I've seen what they intend," Wren said, "and it isn't what I agreed to. Or perhaps it is what I agreed to, and I simply never looked closely enough at the words. That's the occupational hazard of a priest — we spend our lives believing language means what we want it to mean."

He stood, joints cracking, and paced to the broken window where the marsh showed silver and gray under the late light.

"When I was young, I studied the old liturgies. The real ones, from before the Concord, before the crown decided the gift was a disease. There were three oaths, as I've taught you. Binding. Unbinding. And the third, the unnamed one. But there was a fourth thing, too — not an oath. A rite. A way of forcing the Unbinding without any speaker's consent at all." He glanced back at them. "It requires blood. Speaker's blood, freely shed — that part can't be faked, the thorn knows the difference between a wound and a gift — but freely shed is not freely given. The Amarels don't need your cooperation, Theo. They only need enough of you."

"How much?" Theo asked. Their voice didn't shake. Wren wished it would; anger he could work with. This quiet arithmetic was harder.

"A bowlful would begin it. They mean to use more."

"And when?"

Wren closed his eyes. "The coronation eclipse. Nineteen days from now. The moon will pass over the sun above the capital, and the veil between the drowned places and the living ones goes thin — the old texts call it the Hour of Standing Water. Every binding ever sworn loosens for that hour. If the Unbinding rite is performed then, with your blood as the key..." He opened his eyes. "The thorn comes free. All of it. Not just here in Vaelmora. Everywhere its roots run, and they run under half the realm. The last time it woke unbound, it drowned a kingdom in a single night, and that was with a full binding waiting to catch it. This time there would be nothing waiting."

Sefa had sunk onto a crate. "The eclipse. That's why they let us go in the crypt. Why the ritual started early — they weren't failing, they were *sampling*. Testing whether your blood worked."

"They were testing whether it worked," Wren confirmed. "It does. That's my fault too. I'm the one who told them the Villalobos line carried the true key. I'm the one who identified you."

Silence. The tide sucked and gurgled beneath the floorboards. Somewhere out in the reeds, a bird made an idiotic, cheerful noise, entirely indifferent to the end of the world.

"Why?" Theo asked finally. Not *how could you*. Just *why*. Which was somehow more terrible, because it offered him a hearing, and Wren had spent forty years being given hearings by people who'd already decided.

"Because I loved your grandfather," Wren said. "There. The whole rotten root of it. Aldric Villalobos was the finest man I ever knew, and he died in chains because the crown decided his oath was treason, and the family that helped put him there — the Amarels — came to me afterward with soft voices and older grievances, and told me they only wanted justice. Restoration. Their stolen legacy returned." He laughed, without humor. "I was young and grieving and I wanted so badly to be on the side of the wronged. Do you know how easy it is to recruit a grieving man? You don't even have to lie to him. You just have to hand him a story where his grief has meaning."

"And now?" Theo asked.

"Now I've watched them for thirty years. And the story changed, slowly, the way stories do, until one day I realized I was standing in a room with men who talked about drowning a kingdom the way farmers talk about flooding a field. Necessary. Regrettable. *Productive.* They don't want their legacy back, Theo. They want the world that stole it to burn down first, and they've convinced themselves the thorn will spare the righteous." He shook his head. "The thorn spares no one. I've read the accounts. It doesn't distinguish. It never distinguished. That's precisely why your ancestors bound it — not to hoard power, whatever the Amarels tell themselves, but because someone had to hold the leash of a thing that cannot love."

"But Ilsa—" Theo stopped. Started again. "My grandmother told me our line bound it to protect people. The thorn told me we stole the binding. Both things can't be true."

"Both things are true," Wren said gently. "That's the joke of it. The Villalobos stole the binding from the Amarels, yes — stole it because the Amarels were using it as a whip, and someone had to take the whip away. Your family were thieves who became jailers who became, eventually, guards. It's not a clean story. No story worth the ink is." He smiled, tired. "Your grandmother burned everything except one page because she knew the truth would either break you or arm you, and she gambled on arming you. She was right, I think. She usually was. She once threw a hymnal at me across a chapel for singing flat."

"She never mentioned you."

"I asked her not to. I was ashamed." He spread his hands. "I've been ashamed for thirty years. It's a very familiar coat. I've grown fond of it. But coats wear out."

* * *

"So what do you want?" Sefa demanded. "Absolution? Because I keep a ledger, priest, and yours is long. You hired me. You aimed her at the crypt. You—"

"I know what I did. I have the list memorized; I recite it nightly, it's very soothing." Wren crouched down so he was level with them both, an old habit from hearing confessions, though he hadn't the right to hear anyone's in fifteen years. "What I want is to spend what's left of me buying back an hour of what I've cost. Here's what I know, and it's worth more than my apology, which you should feel free to refuse:

"The rite requires a vessel. A reliquary of old glass, consecrated before the Concord, kept in the Amarel house beneath the cathedral — the same crypt where they held you. Without it, spilled blood is just spilled blood. I know where it's kept, I know the door it sits behind, and I know the man who guards it, because I trained him, God forgive me, and he still says his prayers in the cadence I taught him, which means I know exactly when he kneels.

"Nineteen days. The eclipse won't wait, and neither will they. If we do nothing, the thorn wakes unbound and everything burns. If we destroy the vessel, the rite dies with it — and the Amarels lose their only chance for another century, because the next Hour of Standing Water is a hundred years off and none of them will live to see it.

"Or," and here his voice went quieter, "we consider that the crown also cannot be allowed to win. Ostrev's offer stands. Bind the thorn to the throne, become their weapon. I don't recommend it. But you should know all three doors are open, and I am no longer trustworthy counsel on any of them, because I have just confessed to being the sort of man who opens doors for wolves."

Theo was silent a long moment. Then: "The guard who prays in your cadence. Does he kneel at dawn or dusk?"

Wren blinked. Of all the responses he'd rehearsed for — fury, tears, the wooden sword across his ribs — he had not prepared for tactical follow-up questions.

"Dawn," he said carefully. "Matins. He's punctual about it. Guilty men usually are."

"Then we have nineteen days, a window at dawn, and one defrocked priest who knows the crypt." Theo looked at Sefa. "Well?"

Sefa stared at Wren for a long, long moment. Then she pulled out her satchel, drew forth a fresh sheet of paper, and began sharpening a pencil with a small knife, with great deliberation.

"I'm making a new map," she said. "Of the crypt. And you're going to dictate every corridor, Father, twice, and the second time I'm going to ask trick questions, and if you hesitate even once I'm leaving you tied to this post for the crabs." She looked up, and her eyes were hard and wet at the same time. "This isn't forgiveness. Forgiveness is earned. This is *inventory*."

"Inventory," Wren repeated solemnly, "is more than I deserve."

"It is," said Sefa. "Start talking."

Outside, the tide turned, and far beneath the silver water, something ancient shifted in its sleep — and Wren felt it turn over, slow and vast, like a word deciding whether to be spoken.
