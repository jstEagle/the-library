## 27. The Eclipse Road

The eclipse was in nine days, and Sefa had exactly one commission paper left that could get them through a crown checkpoint without anyone looking too closely at the tall, gray-templed traveler in the back of her cart.

She'd been rationing it like the last figs of winter.

"Third checkpoint by tomorrow night," she said, snapping the reins. The mule—Bramble, named for her temperament rather than any botanical resemblance—gave the long-suffering snort that Sefa had come to interpret as *I heard you, I simply do not care.* "If we make good time on the causeway, we can sleep at the Wayhouse of Saint Ferren instead of the ditch."

"The ditch has charm," Theo said from under the canvas.

"The ditch has water in it now. It's spring. Everything is water." Sefa glanced over her shoulder. Theo sat amid her rolled maps with their hood down for once, letting the morning light find all those new lines around their eyes—the lines they'd bought in Vaelmora, word by word, year by year. They were older than she was now, probably. She'd stopped doing the arithmetic because it made her want to throw things.

"Also," she added, "the ditch has Ostrev's soldiers in it, if he's kept pace, which he will have, because that man tracks people the way I chart coastlines. Obsessively. With notes."

Theo winced. "He let us go."

"He let us go *once*. Men like that don't repeat themselves; they revise." She flicked the reins again. "Hold on. Bramble has opinions about bridges."

* * *

The checkpoint at Millford Crossing was a sawhorse barrier, two bored soldiers, and a brazier that smoked like it had personal grievances. Sefa had been through worse. She'd been through worse last month, when the crown had doubled its patrols after the riots started in the capital and every gifted child within fifty leagues became a line item in somebody's ledger.

She drew up the cart, arranged her face into the particular expression of a woman who found checkpoints tedious but understood their necessity, and produced her commission.

"Sefa Andrade, cartographer, Royal Survey Office, seconded to the Chancery of Roads." She said it the way she'd practiced: flat, fast, faintly insulted to be asked. "I'm carrying survey instruments and an assistant. You can look, but if you touch the theodolite I'm filing a grievance, and you'll be writing it out yourself, because nobody else in this province can spell 'theodolite.'"

The younger soldier actually took a half step back. The older one squinted at the seal.

"Seconded to the Chancery," he said slowly. "That's a capital posting."

"It is."

"You're a long way from the capital."

"I'm going *to* the capital, which is the direction one travels when one is seconded there. Unless you'd prefer I report this delay. There's a column on my form for delays. It's gotten quite long since your office started stopping everyone with a face." She held out her hand for the paper, palm up, patient as a stone.

It worked. It always worked—not because the lie was perfect but because the performance was. Confidence, Sefa had learned years ago, was just a map drawn boldly enough that other people followed it.

The soldier waved them through. Sefa clicked her tongue at Bramble and rolled across the bridge, and only when the checkpoint was a smudge behind them did she let out the breath she'd been holding since Millford.

"You're enjoying that far too much," Theo said.

"I am enjoying it *exactly enough*." She grinned despite herself. "Do you know what I did before the debtors' prison? Before your friend Wren hired me? I surveyed drainage ditches. For eleven years. Do you know how many people stopped me at checkpoints to admire my drainage ditches?" She didn't wait for an answer. "None. So yes. I'm going to enjoy being important for as long as this paper keeps working, which is—" she did the math she'd been avoiding, "—four more checkpoints, maybe five, before someone in the Survey Office notices a dead woman's commission moving north."

Theo went quiet in the way they did now, the way that used to be silence and had become something heavier. "Sefa—"

"Don't." She said it lightly, which was the only way she knew how to say anything that mattered. "You'll say something noble about turning yourself in or taking another road, and I'll say something sharp about how you're the worst cargo I've ever carried, and then we'll both feel bad and the road won't change. We're going to the capital. We're going to stop whatever the Amarels are doing under that cathedral. Those are the facts on the map. Everything else is weather."

A pause. Then, from under the canvas, dry as dust: "Worst cargo. You've carried live geese."

"Geese," Sefa said, "have never once suggested sacrificing themselves for the greater good. Geese are honest about what they are."

That got a laugh out of Theo—a real one, short and startled, the kind she'd been collecting lately like a miser collects coins. She decided not to mention that. Some things you didn't put on a map; you just remembered where they were.

* * *

They made the Wayhouse of Saint Ferren by dark, which felt like a small miracle until Sefa saw the crowd.

Pilgrims, mostly—eclipse pilgrims, come early, afraid of the roads later. Refugees, too, if you knew how to read them: families with too many bundles and no luggage tags, the particular stillness of people who had left somewhere quickly. And threaded through them, in ones and twos, men and women wearing the gray sashes of the Crown Constabulary, checking papers, asking questions.

"Ah," Sefa said. "Weather."

They ate in the corner, backs to the wall, sharing a single bowl of barley stew between them because buying two would mean lingering and lingering meant talking and talking meant being remembered. Across the room, a constable was questioning a young man whose hands wouldn't stay still. Sefa watched Theo watch him, and watched something terrible and tender move across their face.

"He's not gifted," Theo murmured. "He's just scared. That's enough now."

"That's always been enough," Sefa said. "It's just official now."

She hadn't meant it to come out bitter. But some things, once spoken, refused to be unspoken, and Theo looked at her with those old-young eyes and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than an argument.

Later, up in the room they could barely afford—one bed, one chair, one window facing the stable yard—Sefa unrolled her charts on the floor and worked by candlelight while Theo pretended to sleep. Pretended badly. She'd learned their breathing patterns the way she'd learned coastlines: the false starts, the long pauses, the moment when pretending gave way to actual exhaustion and their whole body surrendered at once.

She was drawing the approach to the capital for the fourth time when Theo spoke from the dark.

"The Amarels need my blood for the Unbinding. Captured blood, Wren said. If I'm not there—"

"If you're not there, they find another way, or they fail, and either way the eclipse comes and goes and we've saved nothing and lost our only chance to be standing in the right place." Sefa didn't look up from her work. "I've run this route six ways, Theo. Every version ends with us walking into the cathedral's shadow on coronation eve. There is no clever road. There's just the road."

"You could turn back. Take the commission money, go south, start over. Nobody would—"

"Nobody would what? Know I helped a swordspeaker evade the crown?" She set down her pen. In the candlelight her hands looked steady, which pleased her, because inside they were doing something less dignified. "My family is in a debtors' prison in Corvane because I took crown coin to guide a stranger to Vaelmora. There is no version of my life anymore where I'm clean. So I've decided to be useful instead. It's more fun."

Silence. Then Theo sat up, hair flattened comically on one side, and Sefa had to bite down on a smile because the moment was serious, damn it, and they looked like a startled owl.

"You tore up the map," Theo said. "In Vaelmora. You tore it up and stayed."

"And I've regretted it every single day," Sefa said cheerfully, "usually around dawn, when Bramble steps on me. Go to sleep. We cross the Serein ford at low water tomorrow, and if you snore, I'm selling you to the constabulary at a discount."

Theo lay back down. Within minutes—actually asleep this time, no pretense about it—their breathing slowed into something peaceful.

Sefa looked at the map a while longer. The capital sat at its center like a spider in a web of roads, and somewhere beneath it, beneath the cathedral, beneath the crypts, a stolen vial of blood was waiting for the moon to go dark.

She blew out the candle and got into the narrow space beside Theo, who radiated heat like a stove and hogged the blanket like a criminal.

* * *

The Serein ford nearly killed them.

Not dramatically—no bandits, no soldiers, no thorn erupting from the riverbed. Just water, honest and stupid, running high with snowmelt so that Bramble lost her footing halfway across and the cart tilted and Sefa's entire life's work—eleven years of surveys, three years of secret maps, every chart she owned—went sliding toward the current.

She caught the case. Both arms around it, boots braced against the cart frame, water shoving at her like a drunk uncle. And then Theo was there—Theo, who paid a year of life for every word spent freely, who had aged decades in a single night and sworn off the gift entirely—Theo hauled the case back with both hands and no magic at all, just muscle and panic, and for a moment they stood chest-deep in the freezing Serein, holding her maps between them like the world's most scholarly tug-of-war.

"Let go of the cart!" Theo shouted.

"The cart is *paid for!*"

"The cart is drowning!"

The cart was, in fact, drowning. They abandoned it—or most of it—and dragged what mattered to the far bank: the map case, the food, Bramble, and one sodden blanket that Sefa refused to leave because it had been her mother's. They stood dripping on the northern bank, watching the cart's wheel spin away downstream like a coin tossed into a well.

"Well," Sefa said.

"Well," Theo agreed.

"We're forty miles from the capital with no cart, wet through, and I've lost my survey instruments, which means at the next checkpoint I'm a woman with a paper and no tools, which is like a priest with no god. Suspicious."

Theo wrung out their sleeve. Water poured out in quantities that suggested sleeves were mostly water anyway. "We could walk."

"We could walk," Sefa confirmed. "Nobly. On foot. Like pilgrims." She looked at Bramble, who stood grazing with the serene entitlement of a creature who had done nothing wrong ever. "At least the mule's honest."

They walked. And here was the thing Sefa discovered, somewhere around mile ten, when her boots had squelched past squelching into mere discomfort and the sun came out and turned the wet road to steam: she was happy. Actually, genuinely, appallingly happy. Her feet hurt, her future was a burned bridge, her family was in prison, and she was walking toward the most dangerous city in the realm beside a person who carried a drowned kingdom in their blood—and she was *happy*, because the alternative to fear, she was learning, wasn't safety. It was company.

Theo caught her smiling and frowned. "What?"

"Nothing. Your hair dried strangely."

"My hair always dries strangely."

"Yes," Sefa said warmly. "It does."

* * *

The last checkpoint before the capital was different, and Sefa knew it before they reached it, because there were four soldiers instead of two, and none of them were bored.

They'd camped the night in a copse outside Harrowgate rather than risk the town, and came in at midmorning with the crowds—pilgrims funneling toward the city for the coronation, a river of the hopeful and the frightened. Sefa had dressed Theo down: plain clothes, hood back, gray temples visible, an assistant's stoop she'd drilled into them over breakfast. *You're tired, you're underpaid, you resent me slightly. Think of me as weather.*

The sergeant at the barrier took her commission and did not give it back.

He read it twice. He looked at her. He looked at Theo. Behind him, a clerk was comparing names against a list, and Sefa's stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution.

"Royal Survey," the sergeant said. "Chancery of Roads."

"Is there an echo out here? Because I hear my words coming back to me."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite amusement, not quite suspicion. "There's a notice posted for surveyors. All commissions to be verified at the Gate of Bells. In person. By the Chancery." He handed the paper back, and Sefa's heart restarted itself with a lurch. "You can pass. But you'll present yourselves at the Gate of Bells within three days of entry, or the commission's void and a warrant issues. Understand?"

"Perfectly," Sefa said, and thought: *three days.* The eclipse was in four.

They passed through. The road opened ahead, wide and white and climbing toward the capital, which revealed itself an hour later across the plains: first a smudge, then a gleam, then the full impossible weight of it—walls and spires and the great dome of the cathedral catching the afternoon sun, gold on gold on gold.

And above it all, already visible in the bright sky if you knew where to look, a thin dark crescent at the sun's edge. The moon, arriving early. Taking its seat.

Sefa reined in—there was no cart, but habit dies hard—and stared.

"There it is," she said quietly.

Beside her, Theo had gone very still. Not afraid, exactly. Something older than fear. Sefa had seen that look once before, on the marsh road outside Vaelmora, when the drowned towers rose out of the fog and the thorn whispered a name that wasn't theirs to hear.

"What is it?" she asked.

Theo's eyes stayed fixed on the cathedral dome, on the darkness nibbling at the sun above it. When they spoke, their voice was strange—flat and far away, like sound traveling under water.

"It's counting," they said. "The thorn. It's been quiet since the oath, since it came into me—it's been *quiet*, Sefa, I could almost forget it was there." Their hand rose unconsciously to their chest. "It's counting down. And it knows this city."

The wind came off the plains, warm and smelling of cut wheat and distant smoke, and somewhere below in the capital the bells began to ring for evening, bright and oblivious, and Sefa Andrade—who had survived debtors' prisons, drowned cities, burning villages, and one catastrophic ford—took her companion's arm and started walking.

"Then we'd better get there," she said, "before it finishes."
