## 28. Coronation Eve

The capital of Aurelholm did not look like a place where people were afraid. It looked like a place where people had decided, collectively and with great expense, to be afraid *beautifully*.

Theo saw it first from the crest of the Pilgrim's Rise, where the road bent around an old watchtower and the whole city spilled out below them like jewelry dropped on dark velvet. Lanterns by the ten thousand. Banners in the royal blue-and-gold hanging from every balcony, every bridge, every spire of the cathedral whose dome caught the last of the sunset and held it, glowing, like a coal that refused to die. Music drifted up on the evening wind—fiddles and drums and something brassy from the palace district—and beneath it, fainter, the sound of a crowd already gathering for tomorrow's coronation.

"It's like a cake," Sefa said beside them. She had her map case slung across her back and her chin tipped up, appraising. "An enormous, expensive cake that someone has set on fire inside."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm a cartographer. I describe terrain." She adjusted the strap. "The terrain is on fire."

They came down into the city with the pilgrim traffic, hooded and dusty among hundreds of others who had walked days to see the young king crowned. Theo kept their head down out of habit now, though no one here was looking for them. No one here knew their face. That should have been a comfort. Instead it felt like wearing someone else's coat—warm enough, but the sleeves were wrong.

The streets swallowed them. And that was when Theo began to understand what Sefa had meant.

Because between the lanterns and the music, between the bakers selling coronation bread stamped with the royal seal and the children selling paper crowns at three coppers apiece, there were other things. A wall near the Tanner's Gate where someone had painted a figure with too-bright eyes and a mouth full of scribbled words, and beneath it, in fresh tar: **NO SPEAKERS. NO STORMS. NO MERCY.** A knot of men outside a tavern singing something with a chorus Theo couldn't quite catch until they passed close enough—and then wished they hadn't. A woman sweeping ash off her doorstep who watched them pass with the flat, exhausted stare of someone who had spent the night listening to glass break.

"They rioted two nights ago," said a voice at Theo's elbow—an old pilgrim woman with a walking stick and a face like a walnut, falling into step as though she'd known them for years. "The Gifted Quarter. Burned half of Candlemaker Street. The watch stood and watched, mostly. Some of them helped." She said it the way you'd report rain. "You're not from here."

"No," Theo said.

"Keep it that way." And she tapped her stick twice on the cobbles, which might have been a blessing or a warning, and turned down an alley.

* * *

Sefa had arranged rooms through her old commission—a surveyor's lodging house near the river, the kind of place where the beds were narrow and the stew was honest and nobody asked why a crown cartographer was traveling with a dusty pilgrim who flinched at loud voices. The landlady, a broad woman named Marta with flour to her elbows, took one look at Sefa's papers and one longer look at Theo and said only, "Back room's got a lock on the inside. Supper's at six. Don't be late; my husband gets wounded feelings."

The back room had one window overlooking a courtyard where laundry hung like flags of surrender. Theo sat on the bed while Sefa spread her maps on the floor and felt the strangeness of the city pressing against the walls—the way you feel weather coming in your teeth.

Because that was the thing about being what Theo was, now. Outside Thornvale, words had weight. All words. They pressed on the air like stones on a stretched cloth. A mother calling her child home from play left a bright ripple that faded in seconds. A priest muttering vespers left a slow golden hum. And here, in this city of a hundred thousand people, the cloth was sagging under the weight of a hundred thousand stones, and underneath all of it—far away, south, in the drowned marshes where Vaelmora slept—Theo could feel the thorn turning over in its long dream, stirring toward their blood like a dog hearing its name.

*Soon*, it didn't say, because the thorn didn't use words anymore. It used pressure. It used *leaning*.

"Stop doing that," Sefa said without looking up.

"Doing what?"

"Making the face. The one where you're listening to something I can't hear and don't want to." She weighted a corner of the map with an inkpot. "We're two streets from the cathedral. Wren says the Amarels have been moving supplies into the old crypt levels for a month—candles, rope, salt, things that don't make sense unless you're planning a ceremony. Tomorrow, during the eclipse, the whole court will be in one room looking at the sky. If they're going to take your blood, it'll be tomorrow."

"I know."

"And you keep making the face."

Theo lay back on the bed and looked at the ceiling, where water stains had bloomed into continents. "When I was small," they said, "Ilsa told me the capital was the safest place in the world. Walls forty feet thick, she said, and a king behind every wall. I used to imagine it. Marble everywhere. Everyone fed." A pause. "She never mentioned the tar paintings."

"She also told you the thorn was a monster," Sefa said. "Ilsa loved you. People we love give us maps with the dangerous parts left blank. It's how they get us out the door." She rolled up the map with a snap. "Come on. Supper's at six, and Marta's husband gets wounded feelings."

* * *

Supper was lamb stew and brown bread and Marta's husband, Oskar, telling the story of how he'd once carried a whole side of venison up four flights of stairs for a bishop who turned out to be a fraud, "and the worst part, the *worst* part, is he tipped well. What am I supposed to do with that? I can't hate him properly. He ruined my righteous anger." Sefa laughed until she had to put her cup down. Even Theo smiled, and for a few minutes the room was warm and ordinary and the weight of the city's words receded to a dull tide.

Then the shouting started.

It came up the street like floodwater—not one voice but many, braided together, and threaded through it the particular rhythm of a chant. Theo set down their spoon. Every word in the street suddenly had edges.

"—no speakers, no storms—" Oskar had gone still, his spoon halfway to his mouth. "—burned Candlemaker Street, they'll burn yours next—" Marta blew out the candle nearest the window without being asked, an old reflex, smooth as breathing.

"Stay away from the shutters," she said quietly. "Both of you."

But Theo went to the window anyway. They knew they shouldn't. They went anyway, and put their eye to the gap in the boards.

The street below filled with torchlight. Forty men, maybe sixty, some in work clothes, some in watch colors that had been very carefully made to look not-quite-official. At their center marched a man on a cart—a preacher, by his collar, or something shaped like one—holding up a book and shouting about purity, about the realm cleansed, about the abomination of tongues that unmade what God had made. Behind the cart, dragged by ropes, came a young man with his hands bound and a sack half over his head, stumbling, bleeding from the mouth.

And the crowd—the good people of Aurelholm, the ones who'd bought paper crowns for their children—lined the street and watched. Some cheered. Most just watched, which was worse, because watching was a choice made with open eyes.

"He's not even gifted," whispered a boy's voice near Theo—Marta's grandson, maybe nine years old, who had crept up beside them without anyone noticing. "That's Tam from the tannery. He's not anything. They say he is so they have somebody to carry." He said it matter-of-factly, the way children say terrible things they've grown up around. Then he added, with the same matter-of-factness: "You shouldn't watch. Watching makes them braver."

Theo looked down at him. The boy looked back, and in his eyes was the oldest expression in the world—the look of someone cataloguing danger so they can survive it later.

"What's your name?" Theo asked.

"Piet."

"Piet. Go help your grandmother blow out the candles."

He went. And Theo stayed at the gap in the boards, watching the cart pass, watching Tam stumble, watching a woman in a fine dress lean out of a balcony above the crowd and pour something onto the prisoner's head—it might have been water, might not have been—and the crowd laughed, and the sound of that laughter went through Theo like a blade finding the seam in armor.

*Ils a burned the archive to keep me from knowing. Ostrev burned the village to find me. And these people would burn me in the street for the crime of existing wrong, and tomorrow they'll all put on their best clothes and sing hymns at a coronation.*

The procession passed. The chanting faded toward the river. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, and its single word-weight hung in the air like smoke.

* * *

Sefa found them afterward sitting on the floor of the back room with their back against the bed, knees up, wooden sword across their lap. Not drawn. Just held. An old habit with new meaning.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" Sefa said, lowering herself down beside them. She smelled of woodsmoke from the kitchen. "Vaelmora. When we first saw it come up out of the marsh. You remember what you said?"

"I said a lot of things. Mostly swearing."

"You said, 'It looks like it's waiting for permission.'" She pulled her knees up to match theirs. "I've been thinking about that all day. This city's the same. All these lanterns, all this gold paint—all of it waiting for permission. To be afraid out loud. Somebody gave it to them two nights ago, and now look." She nodded at the window, where the glow of distant torches still trembled against the boards. "Fear's like fire that way. It doesn't need much. Just one person willing to strike first and a hundred willing to watch."

"When did you get wise?" Theo asked.

"I've always been wise. You were too busy being dramatic to notice." But she bumped her shoulder against theirs, gently, and let it stay there. "Wren sent word. He wants to meet before midnight. The chandler's shop on Bellows Lane—he says it's safe, or as safe as anywhere."

"Wren always says that."

"Wren is usually right, which is more than most people manage." She stood, wincing, and offered a hand. "Come on, dramatic. Midnight won't wait."

* * *

Bellows Lane was a crooked ribbon of shadow behind the chandler's row, where the smell of tallow hung so thick you could taste it. The shop's back room was low-ceilinged and lit by a single stub of candle, and Wren Calloway looked worse than the last time Theo had seen him—which was saying something, because the last time he'd looked like a man who'd been defrocked, beaten, and forgiven himself for neither. Now he looked like a man counting hours.

"You came through the Tanner's Gate district," he said. It wasn't a question.

"How could you possibly know that."

"Because you both smell like tar paint and poor decisions, and because there's tar paint on your sleeve." He pushed a stool toward them. "Sit. We don't have long. There are things you need to hear before tomorrow, and one of them I've been putting off saying since the marsh, and I'm done putting it off."

Theo sat. Sefa leaned against the door, arms crossed, positioned—Theo noticed—with one hand near the latch and her body between Wren and the exit. Old habits.

"The eclipse begins at midday tomorrow," Wren said. "Totality lasts eleven minutes. In those eleven minutes, the Amarels intend to complete the Unbinding using your blood—they already have it, Theo. From the crypt. What they took when they held you. It's enough. It was always going to be enough; blood doesn't expire, it only waits." His hands were flat on the table, steadying himself or the table, hard to tell. "But here's what I learned tonight, from a source I trust more than I trust myself, which frightens me: they don't mean to stop at Unbinding. Unbinding frees the thorn. What they intend after is *claiming*. They believe the thorn will remember its first masters. They believe it will drown the capital's enemies and crown theirs instead."

"And if it doesn't?" Sefa asked from the door. "If it drowns everyone?"

"Then they'll have died certain they were right," Wren said, "which is the most dangerous way a man can die, because he does it smiling."

The candle guttered. Theo became aware, distantly, of the thorn's pressure against the inside of their ribs—that leaning, that patient turning-toward. Eleven minutes of totality. Their blood in a stranger's hands. A kingdom holding its breath under ten thousand lanterns, waiting for permission to be afraid.

"There's another thing," Wren said quietly. "The source told me where they're keeping the blood until the ceremony. Beneath the cathedral itself. The old crypt levels—the ones that predate the building, the ones the crown pretends don't exist." He met Theo's eyes. "Tonight is the last night anyone can reach it. After dawn, the doors seal for the coronation, and nothing goes in or out except royalty and God."

Outside, far off toward the river, the rioters' chant rose again, thinner now, ragged, but persistent—a thread of fear running through the glittering night. Theo thought of Piet's flat eyes. Of Tam stumbling behind the cart. Of a whole city dressed in gold, humming with a hundred thousand weighted words, and underneath them all, in the deep places, the sound of something old beginning to wake.

"All right," Theo said, and heard their own voice come out steady, which surprised them. "Tell me how to get into the crypt."

Wren exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down something heavy. "That," he said, "is the part I've been dreading. Because the only way in runs through the consecrated ground—and the ground remembers me."
