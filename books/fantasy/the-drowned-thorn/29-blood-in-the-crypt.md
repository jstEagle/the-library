## 29. Blood in the Crypt

The last thing Theo saw before the hood came down was the eclipse beginning—a thin black bite taken out of the sun's edge, right there in the middle of the afternoon, as if the sky itself had been paid to look away.

Then wool and darkness, and hands that were not gentle.

They had come for Theo in the pilgrim hospice on Coronation Eve, when the city was too busy rioting to notice one more abduction in an alley. Sefa had been two streets away buying ink. Wren had been—Wren had been exactly where he always seemed to be when things went wrong: nowhere. Theo had time to think, absurdly, *she's going to be so angry*, and then something sweet-smelling had covered their mouth, and the world folded up like a letter.

Now the world unfolded again into cold stone and candlelight seen through woven wool.

"Take off the hood," said a woman's voice. "Let them see where they are."

The hood came away. Theo blinked against the light and found themselves standing in a crypt beneath a cathedral—the great cathedral, they realized, the one whose spires they'd admired from the coronation road not six hours ago. Vaulted ceilings dripped with centuries of candle smoke. Tombs lined the walls like sleeping guests at a very grim dinner party. And in the center of the floor, someone had drawn a circle in chalk and salt and something darker that Theo chose not to examine too closely.

Around the circle stood nine people in grey robes, faces bare, unashamed. At their head stood a tall woman with silver-streaked hair and eyes the exact color of the marsh water around Vaelmora.

"You must be Amarel," Theo said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt, which felt like a small victory. "You have her jaw."

The woman smiled without warmth. "And you have your grandmother's tongue. Sharp even when it should be begging." She stepped closer. "I am Maren Amarel. You've been wearing my family's inheritance for twenty-three years, Villalobos. I thought it was time you were introduced."

"I'd have preferred tea."

"Some of us prefer justice. We make do."

* * *

They tied Theo to a chair—not in the circle, but beside it, close enough that Theo could see every detail of the chalk work. Old words ringed it, drowned-tongue words, the ones Wren had made them copy until their hand cramped. Binding script. Unbinding script. And a third set Theo didn't recognize at all, angular and hungry-looking, like writing done by someone in a hurry and a fury.

"The eclipse is partial tonight," Maren said, conversational, as if discussing weather over a fence. "Full totality comes in nineteen years. We were going to wait. Patience has been our family's whole religion for three hundred years." She held up a small glass vial, and inside it swirled blood—dark, almost black at the edges, moving when nothing moved it. "But then we learned you'd already begun practicing the oaths. That your blood had already woken. And patience, we decided, was for people who hadn't been robbed."

"That's mine," Theo said quietly. "That's my blood. From the crypt—you took it when I was captured under Vaelmora."

"We took what was owed. Three centuries of interest." Maren passed the vial to one of the robed figures, who carried it to the circle's edge with the reverence of a priest bearing relics. "Your grandmother's family bound the thorn to *their* line and only their line. Do you know what that meant for us? Every Amarel born since has been born half-deaf. The gift runs in our blood too—it ran in ours first—but the binding locked us out of our own inheritance. Your ancestors didn't just steal the thorn, child. They stole the door."

Theo thought of Ilsa's last letter, the courier-delivered page that had rewritten everything: *We were not heroes. We were jailers, and worse—we were thieves.* They had read it a dozen times by firelight while Sefa pretended to sleep nearby. It still hadn't stopped hurting. Apparently it wasn't finished hurting yet.

"If you unbind it," Theo said, "it drowns another kingdom. That's not justice, that's just choosing a different city to flood."

"The thorn drowned Vaelmora because your ancestors' binding was *sloppy*," Maren snapped, and for the first time the composure cracked and something older and rawer showed through. "Because they rushed it, because they were afraid, because they wanted power more than they wanted skill. A proper unbinding releases it clean. Guided. Ours will be the first careful hand it has ever known."

"And if you're wrong?"

Maren looked at the chalk circle, and at the vial, and Theo watched her hesitate—one heartbeat, maybe two—and understood with a sick lurch that she had asked herself that question before, many times, and had answered it the way frightened people answer everything: *it won't happen to me.*

"Begin," she said.

* * *

The ritual did not begin with thunder. It began with a sound like a sigh.

The robed figure poured Theo's stolen blood along the circle's outer ring, and the chalk drank it, and somewhere far above them—far above the cathedral, far above the city, down in the drowned south where the marsh held its secrets—the thorn woke up.

Theo felt it the way you feel a name called across a crowded room. Not heard. *Known.*

***Villalobos.***

The word arrived without sound and rearranged the inside of Theo's skull to make room for itself. They gasped, and the ropes bit their wrists, and the nearest robed figure glanced over with mild professional interest, the way a farrier glances at a horse that stamps.

"It can feel you," Maren observed. "Interesting. The old records say the bound thorn sleeps unless its speaker calls it. But you've been calling it, haven't you? All those lessons with the defrocked priest. All those words spent from your own years." Her gaze flicked over Theo's face, cataloguing. "You look forty, child. How old are you really?"

"Old enough to know this is a terrible idea."

"Bravery or stupidity. In my experience they're indistinguishable at the point of use."

The chanting started low. The robed figures moved counterclockwise, each speaking a phrase and passing it on, a bucket brigade of syllables, and with each pass the air in the crypt grew heavier, wetter, as though the room itself remembered being underwater. Candle flames bent sideways though there was no draft. Water beaded on the tomb lids. One drop traced a line down a marble cheek like the statue was crying, which Theo decided was a thing they would think about never.

***Little thief's-child.*** The voice again, vast and slow, filling every hollow of them. ***You brought me here. In your blood. Did you know you were carrying me?***

"I didn't—" Theo said aloud, and several chanters stumbled, and Maren shot them a look that said *keep going*, and Theo lowered their voice to a whisper. "I didn't bring you anywhere. You're bound. You're supposed to stay put."

***Bound to willing flesh, freely given.*** Something that might have been amusement rippled through the words. ***Was it freely given, little oath-keeper? Or did you simply run out of other options, there in the dark, with everyone you love burning behind you?***

Theo's throat closed. Because that was the truth of it, wasn't it. The third oath, spoken at eclipse's edge with Thornvale's smoke still in their hair—they had chosen it, yes, but choice under a knife is a strange kind of freedom, and the thorn had lived inside them ever since, dormant, patient, a splinter the size of a kingdom.

"You're talking to me," Theo whispered. "Why are you talking to *me* and not to them? They're doing the unbinding."

***Because they are opening a door.*** The pressure in Theo's skull deepened, almost tender, horribly intimate. ***But you are the door.***

The chanting rose. On the circle's inner ring, the blood began to move—crawling backward along the chalk lines toward the center, gathering, pooling, and where it pooled the floor darkened and *warped*, wood grain twisting into patterns like veins, like roots, like—

"No," Theo said. "No, no, no—"

A crack split the flagstones. Thin as a hair. Then another, branching, reaching, and from the deepest crack came a smell Theo knew from nightmares: wet earth and green rot and something sweet underneath, the way rot is always sweet at first.

Maren stepped back, and Theo saw fear cross her face properly now, the real kind, the kind that arrives after the point where fear is useful. "Hold the pace," she told the chanters. "It's supposed to—this is within expectation—"

"It's not," said the eldest chanter, a man whose voice shook. "Matriarch, the texts say gradual. This is not gradual."

"The texts also say the vessel must be willing and present in the circle, and the vessel is *tied to a chair*, so perhaps the texts and reality have already parted ways!" Maren's composure was gone entirely now, and what lay beneath it was just a tired woman three hundred years too late. "Keep chanting."

***They cannot hold me,*** the thorn said, and it sounded, for the first time, less like a god and more like something waking up furious. ***Half a door is still a door. And I have waited so long, little keeper. So long in the dark and the salt. Do you know what I remember? I remember sunlight. I remember being a hedge in a garden, before your thieves' ancestors found me and taught me hunger.***

"You drowned a kingdom," Theo hissed.

***I was *made* to drown a kingdom. There is a difference, and your family made sure no book would ever record it.*** The voice curled tighter around Theo's mind, and beneath the menace Theo caught something else, something that sounded almost like grief, and hated how much it moved them. ***Ask your dead grandmother. Oh—forgive me. You cannot.***

The cracks widened. Through the largest, a single tendril emerged—black-green, glistening, thinner than a finger—and tasted the air like a snake. The nearest chanter screamed and broke the circle, stumbling back, and where he broke it the chalk line flared and died, and the whole ritual shuddered like a wheel losing a spoke.

"Maren—" the eldest chanter warned.

"Hold. *Hold.*"

Theo pulled at the ropes until their wrists bled. The thorn's presence swelled in their chest, and Theo realized with dawning horror that it wasn't just speaking anymore. It was *pulling*. Drawing on the connection the third oath had built, drinking through it, using Theo's body as an anchor rope to haul itself up out of the deep. Every pulse of it cost Theo something—warmth, breath, years—and they could feel the arithmetic running whether they consented or not.

***You spoke me into yourself,*** the thorn murmured, almost gentle. ***Did no one tell you what a vow is? A vow is a door you build in your own chest. And doors open from both sides, little keeper.***

"Then I'll build a wall," Theo gasped. "I'll spend every year I have left. I'll—"

***You will sit in that chair,*** the thorn said, with terrible calm, ***and you will watch what your blood begins.***

The tendril thickened. Found the edge of the broken chalk line. Began, patiently, methodically, to grow along it, filling the gap the fleeing chanter had left, completing the circle in living wood instead of dead chalk—and Theo understood, in a flash of clarity that felt like falling, that the thorn didn't need the ritual finished. It needed the ritual *started*. The rest it could do itself, with their blood, in their body, through the door they had built.

The candles guttered. Somewhere overhead, muffled by tons of cathedral stone, the coronation bells began to ring—wrong, arrhythmic, panicked, not celebration at all—and faintly, through the stone, came screaming.

Maren stared at the growing tendril, at her family's three-hundred-year vengeance taking root in ways no text had promised, and Theo watched her arrive at the same arithmetic they had: *we have not unleashed justice; we have unleashed weather.*

"Stop it," she said. "Stop the chant. Stop all of it—"

But the eldest chanter had gone white as his robe. "Matriarch," he said, "stopping mid-unbinding is worse. The texts are clear. The only way through is—"

"—is through," Theo finished hoarsely. "It's always through. Everyone in my life lately keeps saying that."

And then boots on the crypt stairs. Many boots. The door at the top burst open with a crash of iron, and torchlight spilled down the steps, and a voice Theo knew—gravel-rough, unbearably familiar, the voice of every nightmare and, recently, of a strange and fragile truce—bellowed down into the dark:

"AMAREL! Step away from the circle!"

Captain Ren Ostrev descended into the crypt with a drawn sword and thirty crown soldiers at his back, took in the scene—the cracked floor, the growing thorn, the bleeding prisoner in the chair, the nine grey-robed traitors—and his face did something complicated.

Theo, wrists raw, heart hammering, the thorn coiling warm and triumphant in their chest, managed the only thing left to manage.

"Ren," they croaked. "Took you long enough."
