## 31. The First Oath Fails

The blood was already moving.

Theo felt it before they saw it—warm threads crawling down their forearms where the Amarels had opened the skin, tracing lines that matched the carvings in the crypt floor. The stolen blood, drawn from them over three days of captivity, pooled in channels cut into the stone like the roots of some inverted tree. It should have been cold down here. It wasn't. The air had a fever-heat to it, thick with the smell of wet stone and old incense and something sweeter underneath, something green and growing that had no business existing beneath a cathedral.

Above them, muffled by forty feet of rock and vaulted ceiling, the coronation bells were ringing. The eclipse would begin within the hour.

"Again," said Lady Vessia Amarel.

She stood at the edge of the ritual circle with her hands folded, silver-haired and composed as a portrait, and Theo hated how much she looked like Grandmother Ilsa might have looked, standing at a hearth, about to say something true. That resemblance was the cruelest thing in this whole crypt, and there was considerable competition.

"The words," Vessia said patiently, as if instructing a slow child. "Speak the Binding. Now, while the Unbinding is half-turned. You can still close what we've opened."

"You know I can't," Theo said. Their voice came out cracked. Three days without water except what they poured into you between questions. "You've known since you took me. If I could bind it alone, you'd have no use for me at all."

"We have every use for you." Vessia's smile was gentle, which made it worse. "You are the key, child. Keys don't need to understand doors."

* * *

Let me tell you what the last three days had been, because Theo's memory of them would later arrive in fragments, like pages torn from a book and read out of order.

Day one: the cell above the crypt, a real room with a real bed, because the Amarels wanted them rested. Bread and honey. A fire. Wren Calloway sitting across from them with his defrocked priest's eyes full of something that might have been apology, saying, "They won't hurt you, Theo. They only need your blood on the stone. After the eclipse—" and then stopping, because he couldn't finish the sentence either.

Day two: the first drawing of blood, done with surgical tenderness by an old woman who hummed while she worked. Theo had asked her name and she'd told them, and asked theirs back, and it had been almost companionable, which was its own kind of horror.

Day three: down into the crypt, when the thorn's voice had grown too loud to ignore.

Because that was the thing—the voice. It had been whispering since Vaelmora, a murmur at the edge of hearing, but here, above the place where the original binding had been cut into the world, it was a choir. It knew their name. It had always known their name. And it did not sound like a monster. That was the trap of it, Theo thought. Everyone expected the Drowned Thorn to roar. Instead it spoke the way a grandmother speaks when she wakes you before dawn: softly, urgently, certain you'll thank her later.

*Theo Villalobos,* it murmured now, through the soles of their feet, through the open cuts in their arms. *Little jailer. Little thief's grandchild. Come closer.*

"I'm not doing this for you," Theo muttered.

"No," said Vessia, mishearing or pretending to. "You're doing it for the realm. As are we. Do you imagine we want the kingdom drowned? We want what was taken. The thorn is simply the instrument of return."

"And the instrument drowned a kingdom last time."

"The instrument," Vessia said, "was *bound improperly*. By thieves. By your family, who stole the oath from mine and botched it, and let a city die rather than admit their theft." Her composure slipped, just a hairline crack, and beneath it Theo saw not madness but grief—old, calcified, worn smooth by handling. "Vaelmora was my family's city too, child. We lost it as surely as anyone. More. We lost it twice—once to water, once to Villalobos lies."

The bells rang again. Closer now. Or maybe just louder because Theo's heart was.

* * *

Here is what they had planned. Here is the plan that Sefa had risked her neck to carry in, scratched on the back of her own map in charcoal, delivered during the ten minutes of chaos when a kitchen fire (Sefa's work; the girl could start a riot with a butter knife) emptied half the cathedral staff:

Wren would get them into the crypt before the eclipse peaked. Ostrev—who had broken his orders once already, who had put a sword through the lock of their cell door two nights past and then vanished back into his uniform like a man burying evidence—would hold the stairway. And Theo would speak the Binding Oath mid-ritual, while the Unbinding was only half-complete, and slam the door shut on all of it. Bind the thorn. End the threat. Save the crown from itself, save the Amarels from their own vengeance, save everyone, and pay whatever the words cost, years, decades, all of it, fine, fine—

It had been a good plan. It had been, in fact, the only plan. And it had survived exactly as long as it took for Theo to stand inside the circle and open their mouth.

* * *

The words rose in them like water rising in a throat.

That was the strange truth of the gift that no one had ever managed to explain: the oaths weren't memorized. They were *remembered*. They lived in the blood the way songs live in bones, and when Theo reached for the Binding—the great Binding, the one Ilsa's page had described in a hand that shook, the one that had cost their ancestors everything—they didn't recall it. It recalled *them*.

It began in the sternum. A pressure, a fullness, a word assembling itself from parts older than language. Theo felt their lips part. Felt the first syllable climb.

And the thorn stopped singing.

Not quieted. Not hushed. It went silent the way a room goes silent when someone says something unforgivable at dinner. And into that silence, for the first time, it did not whisper. It *spoke*, and its voice filled the crypt the way water fills a lung:

***NO.***

The word hit the blood-channels and the flames in the wall-sconces bent sideways. Vessia's hands flew apart. Somewhere above, the bells faltered—a rope caught, a ringer stumbling—as if the whole cathedral had flinched.

Theo stood in the center of the circle with half a syllable dying on their tongue and felt the most enormous attention they had ever felt turn upon them like a lighthouse beam.

***YOU SPEAK THE THIEF'S OATH,*** the thorn said. ***WITH STOLEN BLOOD, ON STOLEN STONE, TO STEAL MY SLEEP AGAIN.***

"It's not—" Theo's voice sounded absurdly small. "The binding kept you asleep for three hundred years. It kept Vaelmora's death from becoming everyone's death."

***IT KEPT ME IN A CAGE AND CALLED IT MERCY,*** the thorn said, ***AND YOU ARE OFFERING ME THE SAME CAGE WITH A NEW LOCK, AND YOU EXPECT ME TO SAY THANK YOU.***

"That's what binding *is*—"

***NO,*** said the thorn, and now the voice changed, dropped its thunder, and became terribly, horribly reasonable. ***THAT IS WHAT YOUR FAMILY TOLD THEMSELVES. I HAVE HELD EVERY ONE OF THEM IN MY MEMORY, LITTLE JAILER. I CAN SHOW YOU YOUR GREAT-GREAT-GRANDMOTHER'S FACE AS SHE SPOKE THE WORDS. SHE WEPT. DO YOU KNOW WHY SHE WEPT? NOT FROM THE COST. FROM THE SHAME.***

The blood in the channels pulsed. Theo felt it pulse—in their own arms, in time, as if the channels and their veins had become one system, one circulatory tree.

***I WILL BE BOUND BY NO OATH SPOKEN UNDER DURESS,*** the thorn said. ***NOT THE DURESS OF CHAINS—THOSE I CARE NOTHING FOR. BUT NO OATH SPOKEN IN PANIC. NO OATH SPOKEN TO END A RITUAL ALREADY TURNING. NO OATH SPOKEN BY A CHILD PROPPED UP AS A KEY BY PEOPLE WHO NEED A DOOR.***

"Then what?" Theo heard their own voice crack apart on it. "What do you want?"

And the thorn was silent for a long moment, and in that silence Theo understood, with the sick dropping certainty of a floor giving way, that they were not going to like the answer.

***WILLING FLESH,*** the thorn said. ***FREELY GIVEN. THAT IS THE ONLY BINDING THAT HOLDS. IT IS THE ONLY BINDING THERE EVER WAS. YOUR ANCESTORS KNEW IT AND COULD NOT BEAR IT, SO THEY TOOK BLOOD BY FORCE AND WORDS BY THEFT AND MADE A KNOT THAT LOOKED LIKE A BINDING AND WAS ONLY A DELAY. THREE HUNDRED YEARS OF DELAY. AND NOW THE KNOT IS UNDONE, AND YOU STAND HERE WITH HALF A SYLLABLE IN YOUR MOUTH AND CALL IT A PLAN.***

The eclipse. Theo could feel it beginning—not see it, but feel it, a wrongness in the pressure of the air, a dimming that had nothing to do with the sconces. Above them, the capital held its breath. Below them, the blood moved faster in its channels, climbing the carved roots toward the center of the circle, toward the place where the thorn's first root had once pierced the world and been cut off, leaving a scar in the stone shaped like a closed eye.

An eye that was opening.

"Theo!" Wren's voice, from the stairway—hoarse, urgent. "The moon! Whatever you're going to do, do it NOW—"

"I TRIED!" Theo screamed it, at the thorn, at the crypt, at the whole rotten architecture of inheritance. "I'm trying! Give me the words!"

***I GIVE NOTHING,*** said the thorn. ***THAT IS THE LESSON YOUR FAMILY NEVER LEARNED. NOTHING GIVEN IS NOTHING BOUND.***

"But the third oath—" Theo grasped at it, the forbidden one, the unnamed one, the shape of it they'd felt hovering at the edge of every lesson Wren had given them. "There's a third way. There has to be—"

***THERE IS,*** the thorn agreed, and its voice softened into something almost kind, which was the worst thing yet. ***BUT IT IS NOT WRITTEN, AND IT CANNOT BE TAUGHT, AND YOU HAVE MINUTES, NOT YEARS. AND YOU MUST MEAN IT. NOT WANT IT. NOT NEED IT. MEAN IT, THE WAY THE EARTH MEANS ITS TURNING.***

The blood reached the center of the circle.

The scar-eye opened.

What came through was not darkness. It was green—impossibly green, a green that had never seen sun, and through it Theo felt the thorn's attention pour into the world like a flood finding a breach, and far above, muffled through stone and bells and screaming that was just beginning, the capital learned that the eclipse had teeth.

Vessia Amarel was laughing. Quietly, tearfully, laughing, saying something in a dead language that might have been a prayer or might have been a welcome home.

Ostrev appeared at the bottom of the stairs, sword drawn, face gray, shouting words Theo couldn't parse. Sefa's charcoal map was somewhere in his fist. Everything was happening and nothing was happening and the Binding lay dead in Theo's mouth, unspoken, unspeakable, a plan collapsed to rubble—and the thorn, waking, vast, pouring upward through the opened scar toward the world it had drowned once and might drown again, turned its whole impossible attention on the last Villalobos and waited.

It did not threaten. That was the terrible thing. It simply waited, the way a door waits.

***WELL, LITTLE JAILER,*** it said. ***YOUR ANCESTORS HAD THREE HUNDRED YEARS TO WRITE THEIR OATH AND WROTE ONLY LIES. YOU HAVE UNTIL THE MOON PASSES THE SUN.***

***WHAT WILL YOU SAY?***

Theo opened their mouth, and found nothing there—no remembered words, no inherited ones, no plan, no script. Only themselves. Only the valley, and the burning, and Ilsa's hands, and Sefa's torn map, and a wooden blade that had hummed one word in a kitchen long ago.

Only minutes. Only everything.

And somewhere in the ringing silence after the failure of every word they'd ever been given, Theo began—to their own astonishment—to compose.
