## 32. The Third Oath

The blood was leaving the bowl.

Theo could see it happening—could feel it, actually, in a way that made their teeth ache and their vision swim. The Amarels had carved channels into the crypt floor, old ones, worn smooth by centuries of use, and the stolen blood ran along them in thin red threads toward the thorn's roots, which had burst up through the stone like black veins finding a heartbeat.

*Not enough,* the thorn said. Its voice was everywhere now, in the stone, in the water dripping from the vaulted ceiling, in the space between Theo's heartbeats. *But it will do. It will be enough to wake me halfway. And halfway is such an interesting place to wake.*

"Stop," Theo said. Their voice came out cracked. They were kneeling on cold stone, wrists bound—not with rope, which would have been sensible, but with iron filigree that some Amarel artisan had spent years perfecting, because rope, apparently, was beneath the dignity of a ritual murder. "You don't need my blood. You need a speaker. I'm right here."

The thorn's attention settled on them like a physical weight, like standing under a waterfall of dark water.

*A speaker,* it mused. *Yes. The last one. Do you know how many of you there once were? Whole courts of you. Chattering little creatures, spending words like coins.*

"I know what you're doing," Theo said. "You're talking so I'll listen instead of think."

Silence. Then something that might have been laughter, if laughter could grow thorns.

*Your grandmother's granddaughter,* the thorn said. *She also talked back.*

Somewhere above them, muffled through feet of cathedral stone, the bells had stopped ringing. That was how Theo knew the eclipse had begun—the whole city holding its breath at once, every bell silenced for fear of what ringing might invite.

They thought about Ilsa. They thought about her hands, knotted like tree roots, turning pages she'd sworn didn't exist. They thought about the letter that had reached them too late, carried by a courier who'd died somewhere on the road east, and how Wren had pressed it into Theo's hands with an expression like a man handing over a live coal: *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Read it anyway.*

*The Villalobos line were not heroes,* Ilsa had written, in a hand gone shaky and urgent. *We were jailers. We stole the binding from the Amarels three hundred years ago and we told ourselves it was mercy. Maybe some of us even believed it. But hear this, child, and hold it tighter than you hold anything else: the binding was never meant to be spoken alone. There were always supposed to be two oaths—one to bind, one to witness—and we took both into our own blood because sharing power felt more dangerous than hoarding it. That was our sin. Not the binding itself. The keeping of it.*

And then, lower on the page, almost as if she'd written it against her will:

*There is a third oath. No text records it because no text can. Your grandfather tried to explain it to me once, near the end, when his mind was going soft as overripe fruit. He said: the first oath binds the thorn to a bloodline. The second unbinds it. But the third—ah, the third one you cannot inherit, cannot steal, cannot teach. Each speaker must write it themselves, out of their own life, their own love, their own particular stubbornness. He said it was never written down because writing it down would be like writing down someone else's name and expecting it to answer when you called.*

*He never spoke his. He was too afraid. So am I, if I'm honest. But you—you were always too stubborn to be afraid properly.*

Theo knelt in the dark and let those words move through them like water through dry ground.

*You're thinking,* the thorn observed. *I can feel it. It tickles.*

"You're trying to distract me," Theo said. "Which means I'm on the right track."

*I am trying to keep you company while you die. It's called courtesy.*

"It's called stalling. You need the eclipse. You need the blood to finish moving. You need—" Theo's mind snagged, caught, held. "You need me to stay quiet. Because if I speak—if I speak the right thing—"

*Speak the Binding, then.* The voice went smooth and coaxing, honey poured over hooks. *Go on. Bind me again, little jailer. Put me back in your family's cage. I will wait another three hundred years. I am very patient. Ask the drowned.*

And there it was. The trap inside the trap. Because Theo had already tried the Binding—had thrown everything they had left into it, back in the first terrible minutes after the Amarels dragged them down here, and the thorn had simply... declined. Had wrapped around the words like water around a stone and let them sink, useless.

*It only takes willing flesh,* the thorn had said, savoring it. *Freely given. And you, Theo Villalobos, are not willing. You are bound hand and foot in a crypt, bleeding from cuts you did not choose, watching your blood walk away from you in little red rivers. Nothing about this is free. Nothing about this is given.*

So the Binding was closed. The Unbinding was already underway, fed by stolen blood and an eclipse and three hundred years of somebody else's stolen inheritance. And between those two doors—

Between those two doors was the room no one had ever described, because everyone who entered it built it themselves.

Theo closed their eyes.

* * *

Here is what they found there:

A wooden sword, whittled by their own hands at eleven years old, humming a single word they hadn't meant to give it.

Grandmother Ilsa burning papers in the yard, ash rising like gray birds, her face turned away so Theo wouldn't see it break.

Sefa, sharp-tongued and terrified and staying anyway, tearing a map into pieces because some things mattered more than debt. *"You absolute idiot," she'd said, and it had been the kindest sentence Theo had ever heard.*

Ostrev, breaking his own orders, unlocking iron with hands that shook. *"I spent nine years being certain," he'd said. "Certainty is just fear wearing armor."*

Wren, weeping in a flooded chapel. The pilgrims on the road, singing off-key. The valley that forgot, and the way it had forgotten gently, like a mother pretending not to notice a child hiding behind a curtain.

And underneath all of it, quiet as groundwater: the truth that had been waiting in them since Thornvale burned. Not hatred. Not duty. Not even courage, exactly.

*Care.* Plain, unreasonable, unsanctioned care—for a valley full of people who'd never known what Theo was, for a cartographer who'd betrayed them and come back, for a hunter who'd chosen wrong for nine years and chosen right in the end, for a kingdom that feared them, for a thorn that had been drowning in its own captivity for three centuries and had forgotten, maybe, that it had ever been anything but a weapon.

Everyone wanted Theo to choose a side. Bind or unbind. Cage or crown. Weapon or grave.

Nobody had ever asked: *what do you want it to be?*

*Oh,* said the thorn, very softly. And for the first time, its voice sounded young. *Oh, no. Don't.*

* * *

The iron filigree was cutting into Theo's wrists. Good. Pain was useful; pain kept a person in their body, and what Theo was about to do required a body, all of it, every scarred and stubborn inch.

They opened their eyes. Above, through a grate in the crypt ceiling meant for ventilation, they could see a sliver of sky gone strange and copper-dark, the eclipse eating the sun bite by bite. The last of their blood was reaching the roots. The thorn's presence swelled until it filled the crypt like floodwater filling a cellar.

*Don't,* the thorn said again. *Whatever you're building in there—don't. I know what speakers are. I have eaten speakers. I have been loved by speakers and bound by speakers and I am telling you, child, there is nothing in you strong enough—*

"You're right," Theo said.

The thorn paused. Even ancient things, it seemed, were not prepared for agreement.

"There's nothing in me strong enough," Theo said. "That's the whole point. Every oath before mine was strength. Strength to bind. Strength to hold. My family broke themselves carrying yours, and they called it heroism because the alternative was calling it theft. I'm not strong enough to do that. I don't want to be."

*Then what—*

"So I'm not going to bind you." Theo drew a breath, and the breath tasted of copper and candle smoke and, faintly, impossibly, of home—of Thornvale loam and woodsmoke, though Thornvale was a hundred leagues west. "And I'm not going to free you. I'm going to ask you something no one has ever asked you."

The eclipse peaked. The sliver of sky went fully dark. Somewhere far above, ten thousand people in the capital must have been looking up, and the thorn's roots shuddered through the crypt floor, and every channel of stolen blood blazed like a lit fuse.

Theo began to speak.

Not the Binding. Not the Unbinding. Something else—words that came up from somewhere below memory, below inheritance, from the place where a person keeps the things they actually believe. Some of the words were old; some were plain as bread. It didn't matter. That was the secret, the secret Ilsa's letter had circled without naming: the third oath couldn't fail, because it wasn't a formula. It was a self, spoken aloud.

*"Thorn,"* Theo said, and the word came out steady, *"you were never meant to be a weapon. You were meant to be a neighbor.*

*"I will not cage you. I will not loose you. I will carry you—as I carry my grandmother's hands, as I carry a burned valley, as I carry every kindness anyone ever did me on the road east. You will live in my blood, and my blood is not a prison. It is a garden.*

*"When I grieve, you may grieve. When I rest, you may rest. When I die, you will sleep until someone else, someday, chooses to carry you—not as a burden, not as a birthright, but as a choice, freely made, the way all real vows are made.*

*"I offer this not because I am strong. I offer it because I am willing. Take it or leave it—but know that if you leave it, you leave it freely too, and whatever wakes tonight will be neither my doing nor my name."*

The crypt went absolutely silent.

Even the water dripping from the ceiling seemed to hang mid-fall.

Then the thorn laughed—a real laugh this time, vast and cracked and astonished, shaking dust from stones that hadn't moved in three hundred years.

*A garden,* it said. *Three hundred years of kings and priests and bloodlines fighting over whether I should be a chain or a blade, and you offer me a *garden*. Who taught you this?*

"No one," Theo said. "That's rather the point."

*It will hurt,* the thorn warned, and beneath the warning Theo heard something else—hope, rusty and disused, like a gate opening for the first time in generations. *Carrying me will cost you years. Decades. You saw what single words took from you. This is every word at once.*

"I did the arithmetic a long time ago," Theo said. "On the road, the night Sefa gave back the crown coin. I decided then that some things are worth paying for. I've just been waiting for the bill to arrive."

*You're certain?*

"Freely given," Theo said. "Willing flesh. Your own rule. Are *you* certain?"

Another pause. Longer. And in it, Theo felt the thorn do something no text had ever recorded, something the Villalobos jailers in all their centuries of custody had never once thought to try.

It considered the offer.

*Yes,* it said at last. *Yes. Oh, you strange, stubborn, insufficient creature. Yes.*

The eclipse reached its deepest point, and the world outside went dark as the inside of a closed eye, and the thorn came home.

* * *

Later—though later was a strange word for what happened next, because time in the crypt had gone soft and unreliable, like a dream remembered underwater—Theo would struggle to describe the moment of taking. It was not pain, exactly. It was *accounting*: every year of their life stepping forward to be counted, weighed, and spent, and Theo standing in the ledger of their own body, signing each line.

Their hair went gray at the temples first. Then their knees, absurdly, announced themselves with a creak they'd never heard before. Their hands aged in the space of four heartbeats, smooth skin suddenly mapped with lines, and Theo watched it happen with a strange detachment, the way one watches weather.

*Forgive me,* the thorn murmured, settling into their blood like a bird folding its wings. *I will try to be light.*

"Don't be light," Theo said, and their voice came out lower, rougher, older. "Be *here*. That's the whole vow."

The iron filigree crumbled. Not broke—crumbled, rusting decades in a moment, and Theo stood up on legs that ached like they'd walked a hundred miles, which, in a sense, they had.

Above them, through the grate, the eclipse began to release the sun. A thin ring of fire bloomed around the moon's black edge, and Theo understood, with the thorn's knowledge now threaded through their own, that the partial Unbinding could not be recalled. The stolen blood had done its work. Half-woken, the thorn's wilder roots were already moving, already answering the old call of freedom with violence the new oath couldn't reach—because the new oath lived in *them*, in this crypt, in this body, and the body was down here while the city was up there.

"Theo," Wren's voice, ragged, from the stairwell—he'd fought his way back, or bribed his way, or simply talked his way past guards who no longer believed in anything. "Theo, the ritual—it's still moving, the Amarels' working, it won't stop just because you—"

"I know." Theo put one aged hand flat against the crypt wall and felt, beneath the stone, beneath the city, the half-free thorn surging upward like a tide that had been told *wait* for three hundred years and had just heard, from somewhere far away, a different word entirely. "It's waking anyway. Everything I bought, I bought for what comes *after* the waking."

They started for the stairs, gray-haired and twenty-six years old, a walking contradiction with a garden in their veins.

Above them, the sun returned in pieces, and the first screams began.
