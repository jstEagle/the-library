## 33. Drowned Kingdom, Waking Thorn

The first thing Captain Ren Ostrev noticed was the smell.

He had been standing in the coronation square for three hours, in his dress grays that itched at the collar, watching priests swing censers and courtiers fan themselves and the regent's herald bellow about the dawn of a new age. The air had smelled of incense and horse and the particular sweat of ten thousand people pretending not to be afraid of one another.

Then the smell changed. It went green.

Not the green of gardens or cut hay — the green of things that grow where they should not, in the dark, fed on old water. Riverbank rot and blooming nightshade and something sweeter underneath, like fruit gone over in a cellar.

Ostrev's hand found his sword before his mind caught up. Twenty years of soldiering had taught him this: the body knows before the head does. The body had known since Vaelmora. The body had been screaming at him since he'd unlocked that cell beneath the cathedral and put a sword in Theo Villalobos's shaking hands.

*You chose this,* he reminded himself. *Whatever comes next, you chose it.*

Across the square, the great bells of the Cathedral of the Ascension began to ring — and then stopped, mid-peal, as though a giant hand had closed around them.

The silence afterward was worse than any sound Ostrev had ever heard.

Then the cobblestones split.

* * *

Sefa had claimed a spot on the steps of the chandlers' guild hall, which was elevated, defensible, and — she had checked twice — had two exits. She had a satchel with her maps, her knife, three days of dried plums, and a flask of plum brandy she had told Theo was medicinal.

She was halfway through her first medicinal swallow when the ground moved.

Not shook. *Moved.* A slow, rolling heave, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and then the cobbles of the Processional Way buckled upward in a line that ran straight as a surveyor's cord from the cathedral doors to the river gate.

Out of the crack came thorn.

Black as wet iron, glistening, growing as she watched — a foot, then six feet, then a wall of it, barbed and flowering with pale blossoms that opened like eyes. People screamed. People ran. A flower cart overturned and the driver lay in the road staring up at a branch that had grown clean through the axle of his own cart an inch from his skull, and Sefa would remember later — because her mind did this, it always did this, it filed everything, it was why she was a mapmaker — that the man's first act after nearly dying was to grab his fallen hat.

"Right," she said aloud, to no one, to the brandy. "That's happening."

She thought of Theo. She thought of the eclipse, the black disc now sliding over the sun above the cathedral spire, the light going strange and bruised. She thought of a girl in a debtors' cell who had once told Sefa that maps were just promises you made to strangers, and how Sefa had laughed, and how she wasn't laughing now.

She slung the satchel. She started down the steps toward the screaming, because apparently that was who she was. Apparently it always had been.

"Medicinal," she muttered, and took another swallow first.

* * *

Wren Calloway had spent thirty years learning the shape of prayers, and so he recognized one when he heard it, even shouted by a thousand voices at once.

He stood in the cathedral undercroft with ash still on his sleeves from the ritual circle he'd shattered with a fire poker forty minutes ago, listening to the world come apart overhead, and he did the only useful thing left: he prayed. Not to the Ascended — he'd lost his license for that faith years ago and had no intention of renewing it — but to whatever listened in the space between words. The same place, he suspected, where oaths lived.

The Amarels were dead or fled. Lord Cassian Amarel himself lay crumpled at the foot of the stolen reliquary, his blood mixing with Theo's in a channel cut into the stone, and the Unbinding — half-finished, interrupted, *wrong* — was completing itself anyway, the way a fire completes itself once the roof is burning.

Wren had served two masters in his life: the church, which lied to him gently, and the Amarels, who lied to him honestly. He had told himself the second was better. Now, climbing the undercroft stairs toward the light and the noise, he understood they had both simply been different sizes of the same mistake.

At the top of the stairs, a door. Beyond the door, the end of the world.

He pushed it open and stepped through, an old man in a defrocked priest's coat, into a kingdom drowning in thorn.

"Well," he said. "I did warn them."

* * *

Theo ran.

They ran through streets that no longer obeyed any map — Sefa would have wept — cobbles heaving, walls sprouting, the black branches moving with a terrible patience, never quite catching anyone, never quite needing to. The panic did the killing. A stairway collapsed under fleeing crowds. A guard detail, ordered to hold the river gate, held it against their own reinforcements in the smoke and confusion until both units broke.

And through it all, inside Theo's chest, the thorn sang.

It was in their blood now — had been since the third oath, since the eclipse peak, since they had stood in the ruined ritual circle and spoken words no text recorded because each speaker must write their own. *Root to me. Bloom from me. I am your wall and you are my garden, and neither of us grows without the other.* They had meant every word. They had paid for every word; they could feel the decades stacked in their knees, in the new silver at their temples.

But the partial Unbinding had already taken root before the oath closed around it. Half-awake, half-bound, the thorn was doing what half-woken things do: thrashing.

*I'm here,* Theo said to it, silently, running. *I'm here. Slow down. Slow down.*

It slowed. A little. In the Chandler's Ward, a wall of thorn that had been reaching for a crowded tenement shuddered, hesitated, and drew back like a hand pulled from a flame. Theo staggered against a doorway, gasping, feeling the pull of it — holding the thing back was like holding a door shut against a flood with their whole body.

"You can't hold all of it," said a voice, and there was Sefa, appearing out of the smoke with her satchel and her knife and a smear of soot across one cheekbone, taking their arm as if she'd merely been delayed by traffic. "You'll burn out. Where do you need to be?"

"The cathedral. The circle. If I can close the channel—"

"Then we go to the cathedral." She was already moving, hauling them along. "Don't argue. You look ninety. Arguing is for people with time."

"I might be fifty."

"You look ninety and you argue like a teenager. Move."

* * *

Ostrev fought his way to the cathedral steps and found them impassable — a hedge of black thorn twelve feet high had grown across the entire frontage, beautiful and obscene, its pale flowers turning slowly to track the eclipse-darkened sun. His guardsmen had formed a line at the bottom and were doing nothing, because there was nothing to do. You cannot charge a hedge. He had learned this at seven, stealing apples. Some lessons keep.

"Captain!" A lieutenant, young, gray-faced. "Orders? The regent's guard has withdrawn to the palace. There's word the king's uncle is dead. There's word the *regent's* dead. There's word—"

"There's word of everything and sense of nothing." Ostrev looked at the hedge. Somewhere beyond it, in the cathedral, was the answer — or if not the answer, then the person who had become the answer, whether anyone had asked their permission or not. "Hold this line. Keep people back from the thorn. It doesn't strike unless struck — I've seen it. Get the wounded to the chandlers' hall, the steps are high ground."

"And you, Captain?"

"Me?" He drew his sword anyway, out of habit more than hope, and looked at the impossible green wall. "I'm going to knock."

He raised the flat of the blade — not the edge, never the edge — and rapped it against the nearest branch, twice, like a visitor at a gate.

For a long moment, nothing. Then the thorn rippled, parted, and left a gap exactly wide enough for one man.

Ostrev stared at it. Every instinct he owned told him it was a trap.

Every instinct he owned had been wrong about Theo Villalobos from the beginning.

"Right," he said, and walked through.

* * *

Inside, the cathedral nave was a drowned forest. Thorn had grown up through the flagstones and woven itself among the pillars, and the eclipse light came down through the broken roof in shafts of strange bronze, and in the middle of it all, at the shattered ritual circle, Theo Villalobos knelt in the channel of blood and earth with their palms pressed flat to the stone, and *sang*.

There was no better word for it. No words came out of their mouth at all, but the air hummed with them, and the thorn answered — swaying toward them, straining, a field of black wheat bending to a wind only they commanded.

Sefa stood at their shoulder with a knife, guarding them from nothing and everything. Wren stood at their other shoulder, hands folded, mouthing the old cadences under his breath — not to cast anything, just to keep time, the way he'd kept time for speakers dead fifty years.

And Ostrev walked down the nave with his sword sheathed, past blossoms the size of dinner plates, and stopped at the edge of the circle, and waited, because some part of him understood that what happened next was not a battle and could not be won with soldiers.

Theo's eyes opened. They were older eyes than the last time he'd seen them. Older than any twenty-something's had a right to be.

"You came," Theo rasped.

"You left a gap in the hedge."

"That was for Sefa."

"It was a very narrow gap."

"You're a very narrow man." Theo's mouth twitched — and even now, even here, Ostrev felt the absurd warmth of it, the human ridiculousness of these people he had hunted across a kingdom. Then the humor drained away and Theo's face went grim and young again despite everything. "I can't close it alone. The channel's cut too deep. The Unbinding finished itself — I can only choose what it wakes *into*. I need—" Their voice cracked. "I need witnesses. An oath needs witnesses or it's just a wish."

"You have witnesses," Sefa said.

"You have a witness," Wren said, "who owes the universe several apologies and intends to start paying."

Ostrev unbuckled his sword belt and set it on the stones, out of reach, deliberately, where everyone could see him do it. "You have a witness," he said, "who burned a valley and a child and a good many other things, and will stand here and watch, and carry the truth of it back out to whoever asks. That's the only penance I've got. Take it or leave it."

Theo looked at him for a long moment.

"Take it," they said. "Now hold my hands. Both of you. This is going to feel like drowning."

"What," said Sefa, gripping on, "could possibly go wrong?"

* * *

Later — much later, when the eclipse had passed and the sun came back gold and ordinary over a capital half-swallowed in sleeping thorn — people would tell the story a hundred ways. That the swordspeaker had tamed the beast. That the beast had eaten the swordspeaker. That the regent had died cursing, that the king's guard had broken and run, that a priest and a mapmaker and a disgraced captain had held hands in a burning cathedral like children playing at circles.

All of it true. None of it the truth.

The truth was that Theo spoke the third oath a second time — not new words this time, but the same ones, deepened, rooted, given freely with four hands holding theirs — and the thorn heard, and the thorn *chose*, and across the capital the eruption stopped growing and began, slowly, terribly, to sleep. Branches stilled mid-reach. Blossoms closed. The dead stayed dead — there were hundreds, and Theo felt every name land in their chest like stones dropped down a well, and knew they would carry them the rest of a life that suddenly promised to be very long.

When it was done, Theo collapsed forward into Sefa's arms, gray-faced, ancient-eyed, breathing.

"Did it work?" they whispered.

Sefa looked around at the nave — the silent forest of thorn, the bronze dust settling, the two men standing shell-shocked in the wreckage of everything they'd believed. She laughed, a small cracked sound with no humor in it and all the relief in the world.

"Oh, it worked. You're now the most feared person in the realm, you're ninety years old, and the crown owes you a debt it will spend the next decade trying not to pay." She pushed the hair out of Theo's eyes, gently. "Welcome home, gardener."

From the cathedral doors came shouting — soldiers, heralds, the regent's livery, coming to see what remained.

Ostrev picked his sword belt back up and did not put it on.

"They'll want to arrest you," he said.

"They'll want to *try*," said Theo, and stood up.
