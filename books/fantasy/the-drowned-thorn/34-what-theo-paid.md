## 34. What Theo Paid

Theo woke to the sound of birdsong and did not recognize their own hands.

They lay on a cot in the infirmary wing of the cathedral close—someone had washed the ash from their face, someone had braided their hair back with a care that could only have been Sefa's—and their hands lay on top of the blanket like two strangers' hands. The knuckles were swollen. The veins stood up beneath skin gone thin and papery at the backs of them, mapped in brown, spotted like old parchment. There was a wedding band's worth of wrinkle around each finger.

Theo held them up against the gray morning light coming through the shutters and thought, with a strange and terrible calm: *these are my grandmother's hands.*

Ilsa Fairweather had died with her hands folded just so, and Theo had sat beside her for an hour afterward because they couldn't bear to be the one who unfolded them.

"Ah," said a voice from the corner, low and careful. "You're awake."

Wren Calloway sat in a chair that had given up any pretense of comfort, his defrocked collar gone soft as cloth could go, a cup of something steaming balanced on his knee. He looked worse than Theo felt, which was a feat, because Theo felt like a coat that had been worn by four different people and never once laundered.

"How long," Theo said.

Their voice came out wrong too. Rougher. Deeper at the bottom, thinner at the top, like a bell recast by someone in a hurry.

"Two days." Wren leaned forward. "You slept through the worst of it. The fever broke last night. Sefa hasn't left the corridor except to argue with three separate captains of the guard, and she won all three arguments, which I'd have paid coin to see."

"Two days." Theo flexed the stranger's fingers. They obeyed, slowly, the way an old dog obeys—willingly, but with complaint. "And the thorn?"

Wren was quiet a moment. He had the particular stillness of a man choosing between truths, and Theo, who had spent thirty years learning to read silence in a valley that kept none, watched him choose.

"It's there," he said finally. "I can feel it when I stand near you. Like standing near a well in winter. Cold going down a long way." He hesitated. "But quiet. Dormant. Whatever you spoke into it—it took."

*It took.* As if the thorn were a fever. As if it were a seed.

As if it were exactly what it was: a kingdom-drowning thing, older than the crown, older than the drowned city, now living in Theo Villalobos's blood behind a door made of one sentence no book had ever recorded, because every speaker had to write their own.

"I dreamed about it," Theo said. "While I was under. Or I think I dreamed. It showed me—" They stopped. Their throat closed around it. "It showed me my grandmother. Young. Before Thornvale. She was arguing with someone I didn't know, and she was *laughing*, Wren. I have never once seen that woman laugh. And then it showed me the Amarel girl—the one whose family's binding was stolen—and she was teaching Ilsa how to hold the words without letting them hold you, and I understood that the thorn remembers everyone who ever touched it, and it keeps them all, somewhere, the way you keep letters you can't throw away." Theo lowered the strange hands. "It wasn't threatening me. That's the worst part. It was *introducing itself*."

Wren set down his cup. "That's more than any binding speaker ever reported. The old texts say the bound thorn is silent. A weight, nothing more."

"The old texts were written by jailers," Theo said, "about a prisoner. Of course it seemed silent. Nobody asked."

The priest flinched as if the words had edges, which they did, and Theo found they didn't much care. Two days of sleep had not softened that. Somewhere beneath the cathedral, in a crypt still scarred black where the ritual had half-completed, people had died holding knives meant for Theo's veins. Somewhere in this city, families named Amarel were being dragged out of their homes by soldiers who now called the eclipse a deliverance narrowly averted. The story was already being written, and Theo knew—with the bone-deep certainty of someone who had aged forty years in a single night—which way the ink would run.

"Sefa," Theo said. "Where is she actually? Not the corridor."

"The roof," Wren admitted. "She's been charting the damage. All of it. Every street the thorn took, every house, every—" He rubbed his eyes. "She says if she doesn't map it, someone else will map it wrong."

"That sounds like Sefa."

"She also said, and I quote, 'If Theo dies on me after all that, I will find whatever afterlife they've gone to and reorganize it badly.'"

Theo laughed, and the laugh came out cracked and rusted and cost them a coughing fit that bent them double, and even that hurt in new and interesting places—places that hadn't hurt yesterday, or rather, hadn't hurt forty years ago.

* * *

Getting down the corridor was a lesson in humility. Theo made it eight steps before their knees filed a formal objection, and Sefa appeared out of nowhere with her shoulder under their arm and her mouth already running.

"Don't," she said, before Theo could speak. "Don't apologize, don't make a joke about it, don't do the noble-suffering face. You look like my grandfather and I'm furious about it and also I've brought bread."

"You brought me bread?"

"I bribed a baker. He's hiding three gifted nieces in his cellar, so it wasn't a hard bribe." She steered them toward a bench in the cloister garden, where the morning sun came through smoke-hazed air and turned everything the color of weak tea. She pushed the bread into their hands and stood over them until they ate some.

It was good bread. That undid Theo more than anything else had. The world had ended and remade itself twice in three days, the king was dead or abdicated or something in between—the accounts disagreed—and here was bread, warm, salted, real, and Theo sat in the sun eating it with their grandmother's hands while a cartographer pretended not to watch them with wet eyes.

"You're staring," Theo said.

"I'm *memorizing*," Sefa corrected. "There's a difference. Staring is rude. Memorizing is professional." She sat down beside them, close enough that their shoulders touched, and pulled out the ever-present roll of paper. "Also I need to show you something, and you're going to hate it."

She unrolled the map across both their laps.

It was the capital—or what had been the capital. Sefa had drawn it in her precise, impatient hand, and then overlaid it in red-brown ink with the thorn's path during the eruption. Theo looked at the branching lines spreading from the cathedral crypt and felt the thing in their blood shift, faintly, like a sleeper turning over.

"It went for the granaries first," Sefa said quietly. "Did you know that? Everyone's saying it went mad, went random. It didn't. Look." Her finger traced the branches. "Granary. Cistern. The garrison armory. The records hall—it *destroyed the records hall*, Theo, specifically, and then it stopped growing the moment the eclipse ended. It took exactly what it needed to take and not one street more." She looked up. "Whatever you said to it, it heard you. It was doing something. I just can't tell what."

Theo stared at the map until the lines blurred.

A gardener's logic, whispered a voice that might have been memory and might have been the thorn itself, drowsy at the bottom of their blood. *Prune what chokes the root. Leave the rest to grow.*

"It was clearing ground," Theo said slowly. "Not conquering. Clearing." They thought of the granaries hoarded by a crown that had burned Thornvale for harboring one gifted child. The cisterns locked behind pay-gates. The records hall where every debt, every conscription, every judgment was written in the crown's favor. "Sefa, I don't think it was destroying. I think it was *requisitioning*."

"That's either very comforting or very frightening."

"Yes," Theo agreed.

* * *

Ostrev came at midday.

Theo heard him before they saw him—that particular soldier's walk, boots placed like arguments—and braced themselves, though for what, they couldn't have said. The last time they'd stood in front of Ren Ostrev he'd put his sword on the ground and cut their bonds with it, and his face had been the face of a man watching his whole cosmology burn down around his ears and deciding, barely, to let it.

He stopped at the edge of the cloister garden. Up close, the change in him was plain: he'd shaved badly, his uniform was buttoned wrong at the collar, and his eyes had the raw look of someone who had not slept since the eclipse and did not expect to again soon.

"Villalobos." Then, visibly forcing himself past decades of drill: "Theo."

"Captain."

"Not anymore. I resigned this morning. In writing. With witnesses." He said it flatly, like reporting terrain. "I'm told there will be a tribunal. About Thornvale. About—" His jaw worked. "About the child. I intend to testify to all of it. My part especially."

Theo studied him. The man who had hunted them across half a realm; the man who had offered freedom for service; the man who had, in the end, chosen the lesser evil over the righteous certainty that had carried him this far. He looked smaller than he had in Thornvale. He looked, Theo thought, like what remained when you took a conviction away from a man and left him the guilt.

"Why are you telling me?" Theo asked. "Why come here first?"

Ostrev's gaze dropped to their hands—everyone's gaze dropped to their hands now, Theo was learning; the age was unmistakable, forty years spent in a night, and people looked at those hands the way sailors look at weather—and when he raised his eyes again there was something in them that Theo recognized, unwillingly, as respect.

"Because you'll be offered things," he said. "Soon. Today, probably. The regent needs the story shaped, and a hero is cheaper than a truth. They'll offer you a title. Land. A place at court." His mouth twisted. "I know how those offers work, Theo. I carried them for the crown for twenty years. They're cages with very nice bars, and once you're inside, your testimony stops mattering."

"And yours matters?" Theo asked—not cruelly, but not softly either.

"No," Ostrev said simply. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. But it's mine, and I mean to spend it where it does the most damage." He straightened, some ghost of the captain returning to his spine for one moment. "Thornvale survivors are being moved to a camp outside the walls. Conditions are—I've seen them. Your neighbors. The miller's family. Old Petra." Something crossed his face at that name, quickly mastered. "They don't know yet what happened to you. What you did. Some of them think you're dead."

"They think I'm dead," Theo repeated.

"The village burned, Theo. From where they stood, so did you." He hesitated, and then, gruffly, almost gently: "For what it's worth—I'm glad you're not. Both of you. However many of you there are now."

And he turned and walked away across the courtyard, a man carrying his guilt like a pack he'd finally decided to stop setting down.

* * *

Evening came, and with it, the summons.

A herald in regent's colors, silk over steel, two honor guards who wouldn't meet Theo's eyes. An invitation—*the word was invitation, the tone was not*—to attend the regent at the palace tomorrow, to discuss "the realm's gratitude and its arrangements."

"They want to thank me," Theo said to the empty garden after the herald had gone, "in a room I can't leave."

The thorn stirred at the base of their blood—not speaking, not yet, but attentive, the way a dog lifts its head at a knock. *Dormant*, Wren had said. But Theo was beginning to understand that dormant was not the same as absent, and never would be again, for as long as their heart chose to beat. Every year of their life had been spent buying this moment; the arithmetic of it was written on their hands. Forty years, give or take, traded for a sentence spoken at the height of an eclipse. And the sentence had worked. The thorn lived in them now instead of loose in the world, and the world still stood, and the granaries stood, and Sefa was upstairs drawing maps of mercy, and Ostrev was writing testimony, and somewhere in a camp outside the walls, old Petra was alive and believed her neighbor's grandchild was ash.

Victory, Theo thought, looking at their grandmother's hands in the last of the light. It smelled like victory. It tasted like bread and smoke. It walked like loss and talked like duty, and it had come to stay, permanently, in the house of their body, rent-free, forever.

"Well," Theo told the darkening garden, and their ruined voice almost managed warmth. "Grandmother always said I'd amount to something. She just never said it would take this long off my life to do it."

From the doorway, Sefa's voice: "She'd also say you're talking to plants now, and that's the fever talking, and drink your broth."

Theo smiled—found, to their surprise, that the smile still fit, even on an old face—and reached for the cane Wren had left leaning against the bench.

Tomorrow, the palace. Tomorrow, the cage with very nice bars, and the regent's gratitude, and whatever shape of story the powerful wanted carved onto the ruins. But tonight there was broth, and a friend's footsteps, and a slow walk through a garden where the roses, scorched at the edges, were already putting out new buds—as if the whole city had taken the thorn's lesson to heart.

Theo gripped the cane, rose on knees that complained, and went inside toward the light.

Behind them, deep in their blood, the thorn turned over in its sleep—and for the first time, Theo could have sworn it sounded almost like a purr.
