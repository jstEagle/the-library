## 35. Judgment of the Crown

They gave me three days to recover before the regent summoned me, which I took as a compliment. A lesser catastrophe would have earned two.

I spent those days in a guest chamber of the Winter Palace that was larger than Ilsa's entire house, with a bed so wide I could have lain crosswise and still had room for a second Theo, and I did not sleep in it once. I slept instead in the chair by the window, because the bed was too soft, because the room was too quiet, and because somewhere beneath my ribs the thorn turned over in its long new sleep like a dog settling by a fire, and I wanted to be awake if it dreamed.

Sefa came on the second day with bread she had stolen from the kitchens and news she had extorted from a chambermaid.

"They're calling it the Night of Green Rain," she said, tearing the loaf in half and handing me the bigger piece, which was her way of being tender. "The thorn-blooms over the capital. Poets are already at work. There's one in the market square who's composed an epic about you. You come off very well. You have 'eyes like drowned lanterns.'"

"I don't."

"You do now. Poetry doesn't answer to fact." She settled onto the windowsill, which was where she always sat when a room offered her one, as though chairs were for people with less to say. "The regent will offer you something tomorrow. Land, probably. A title. They've already started calling you the Thornwarden in the council minutes — I read them, don't ask how."

"How did—"

"Don't ask how." She looked at me then, really looked, the way she looked at coastlines before charting them. "Theo. Whatever they offer you, it'll be a cage with good light. I've mapped enough palaces to know the architecture of captivity when I see it. The bars are just farther apart."

"I know," I said. And I did know. I had grown up in a valley that was itself a kind of cage, a gentle one, a loving one, and I knew exactly what it cost to be kept safe from the world and kept from it in the same gesture.

"Good," said Sefa. "Then eat your bread and get some sleep in the absurd bed, and tomorrow be insufferable. It's your only leverage. Nobody knows what to do with a hero who won't act like one."

* * *

The regent received me in the Hall of Mirrors, which I suspect was meant to intimidate and instead mostly showed me how tired I looked. The eclipse-night had written itself across my face in a language I was still learning to read: silver at the temples, lines around my eyes that hadn't been there a month ago. Forty years, Wren had guessed, maybe fifty, paid out of my life in a single speaking. Some mornings I caught my reflection and startled, thinking Ilsa had come back to scold me.

Lord Regent Cassian Vael stood at the far end of the hall with his hands clasped behind him, a narrow man dressed in grey so fine it made other greys apologize. He had been the king's uncle and, since the king was nine years old and hiding in a country estate while the capital counted its dead, he was currently the crown entire. He watched me walk the length of all those mirrors, and I watched him watch me, and neither of us hurried.

"Theo Villalobos," he said, when I arrived within speaking distance. "Or should I say Thornwarden?"

"You shouldn't. It isn't a title, it's a job description, and nobody asked me to fill it."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one. "You saved the capital."

"The thorn saved the capital. Badly. While trying to eat it." I kept my voice level. Under my ribs, faintly, the thorn stirred at its own name, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice. *Hush*, I thought at it, and — this was the strange grace of the third oath — it hushed. "What it actually did was stop halfway through drowning everyone because I asked it to. That's not salvation, my lord. That's a held breath. Everyone in this city is living inside a held breath, and I'd like to talk about what happens when I exhale."

Now the smile arrived properly, thin and appreciative. "You negotiate like a merchant."

"I negotiated like a granddaughter. My grandmother sold eggs at market for forty years. You learn to name your price before the buyer names theirs."

"And what is your price, Speaker?"

"Not a title."

"No?" He gestured, and a servant slid open a case beside him, and inside, on velvet, lay a signet ring and a scroll sealed with the crown's green wax. "The Barony of Thornvale. Rebuilt at the crown's expense. Your people returned to it under your protection. Your valley, your name restored, your family's service finally acknowledged after three hundred years of — " he chose the word delicately — "obscurity."

For one treacherous moment I wanted it. I want to be honest about that, because everyone always leaves out the wanting. I saw it whole: the valley mended, the burn scars greened over, Ilsa's memory given a barony instead of ash, and myself standing at the head of it, respectable, forgiven, *safe*. The cage has very good light indeed.

"And in exchange," I said, "the gifted of the realm report to their baron. For registration. For oversight." I had read the council minutes too; Sefa hadn't been the only thief in the palace. "A tidy list, kept in one place, of every person the crown might someday decide to fear."

"The crown does not fear its friends."

"The crown burned Thornvale," I said. "My lord, I watched it burn from a ridge. I could tell you what the smoke smelled like, but there's no point; you'll smell it yourself someday, everyone eventually smells something burning that they set alight themselves. The crown fears everything it cannot count. A title is a counting-house. I won't be your ledger."

The mirrors threw our reflections back and forth down the length of the hall, dozens of regents, dozens of speakers, all of us frozen mid-argument forever. Somewhere behind the walls a clock struck the hour, unhurried, as though the world had not nearly ended last month.

"Then name your terms," Vael said at last. "Since we are bargaining like egg-sellers."

* * *

We bargained for four hours. I will spare you most of it, partly because much of it was tedious and partly because some of it I am not proud of, including the moment I raised my voice and the thorn answered through the floor — a single green tendril lifting between the flagstones like a finger pressed to lips — and the regent went very still and I apologized, and he said, quite sincerely, "Don't. It is the first honest argument anyone has made in this room in twenty years."

But the shape of it came to this:

Amnesty. Full and public, for every person born with the gift of speech — not tolerance, not a registry, not a license renewed yearly at the pleasure of some frightened clerk, but amnesty, written into law, the same law that had hunted them. In exchange, the gifted would owe the crown nothing except what any subject owes: taxes, honesty, and not drowning the kingdom.

"That last clause may prove difficult to enforce," Vael observed dryly.

"It enforces itself," I said, and touched my own sternum, where the thorn slept. "Believe me. I am the enforcement."

He studied me for a long moment. "And the Amarels?"

That was the harder bargain, and the one I had expected him to refuse. The Amarel survivors — those who had not died in the crypt, those who had fled or surrendered when the ritual broke — sat in cells beneath the cathedral, waiting for trials that would be short and endings that would be shorter. Wren had sent me word of it through Sefa, and had signed the note, simply, *W.C., formerly of their household,* which was his way of asking without asking.

"They were wrong," I said. "Catastrophically, unforgivably wrong. They tried to unbind the thorn during a coronation eclipse and they killed people doing it, and I will not pretend otherwise, and they should answer for it — publicly, in courts, with sentences a person can survive. But they were also the family my grandmother's family robbed. Three hundred years ago the Villalobos stole the binding from them and called it duty and never told even their own children the truth. I found that out from a letter written by a dying woman while soldiers kicked in her door. If my line owes anything to anyone, it owes them the truth, and truth is cheaper than mercy, so I can afford both."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the thorn and I take up residence in the capital permanently," I said pleasantly. "Publicly. At court. At dinners. I'm told I have eyes like drowned lanterns; imagine them across the table from you at every state function for the next thirty years. Every treaty negotiation, every tax hearing, every time someone suggests tightening the laws against the gifted — there I'll be, my lord, aging slowly and remembering everything, with a kingdom-drowner asleep under my ribs taking notes."

The silence that followed had texture. In the mirrors, forty regents considered forty speakers, and I swear half of them were smiling.

"You would make a terrible courtier," Vael said finally, "and an excellent nuisance, and I begin to understand why the thorn chose you." He drew the scroll from its case — the barony, the gilded cage, the title I would not wear — and, to his credit, fed it to the candle himself, watching it curl. "Amnesty for the gifted. Public trial, survivable sentence, and eventual pardon for the Amarels who lay down arms. In exchange, the realm gets its Thornwarden."

"You may call me that if it helps you sleep. I won't sign anything that makes it a rank."

"What will you sign?"

"A statement," I said. "One page. *The thorn is bound, freely, to me alone. When I die, it dies with me, unless another speaks a third oath of their own making — and no oath of another's making will ever wake it.* That's the whole of it. No office. No heirs bound to my burden. No chain passed hand to hand. It ends with me, by choice, in daylight, where everyone can see the choosing."

Vael read the draft twice when I wrote it out in my own hand, which trembled slightly, because writing the words gave them weight even unspoken — everything had weight now, ink and breath alike. Then he signed beneath my name, and stamped it, and said, "Your grandmother would have made a formidable regent."

"She'd have hated every minute and been brilliant at it," I said. "That's the family gift, my lord. We do what's needed and complain about the price honestly."

* * *

I told Wren first, because he had the furthest to fall.

He received the news in the small chapel where we had first met, still half-flooded, still smelling of river and old incense, kneeling in the shallows as though the water were a congregation. When I finished, he stayed kneeling a long moment, and then he laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound, the laugh of a man who has carried a stone for years and been told he may set it down and doesn't quite trust the ground.

"Pardoned," he said. "The Amarels. Pardoned, eventually. Do you know what I planned to do with the rest of my life? Because I assure you it was shorter than that."

"You could teach," I said. "There are gifted children coming out of hiding now that the hunt is ended. Children who flinch at blessings. Someone should teach them that words are a craft and not a curse, and it shouldn't be me, because everything I know, I learned too late and paid for in decades."

"You want me to be a schoolmaster." He said it the way other men say *exile.*

"I want you to be a gardener," I said. "Of a kind. I'll explain someday. Not yet."

He looked at me across the dark water, this defrocked priest who had served two families' griefs and chosen, at the end, conscience over duty, and he bowed his head — not to me, I think, but to the idea of the work. "I was told once," he said quietly, "that the third oath was named in no text because each speaker must write it themselves. I never understood until I watched you do it. Perhaps that's the real inheritance, Theo. Not the blood. The writing."

"Tell the children that part first," I said. "Before anything else. Before the oaths, before the history, before the fear. Tell them they get to write."

* * *

Sefa found me that evening on the palace roof, because of course she had found the roof; Sefa could find high ground in a cellar. Below us the capital glowed, mended in patches, scarred in others, the thorn-blooms' green rain long dried but remembered in every song sung badly in every tavern. She handed me a cup of wine and sat close enough that our shoulders touched, which was her way of saying congratulations without having to construct a sentence about it.

"So," she said. "No barony. No title. Amnesty, pardons, a one-page statement, and a promise to go home to a burnt valley." She sipped her wine. "You are officially the worst-negotiated hero in the history of the realm. The poets will be furious. 'Eyes like drowned lanterns' and he walked away from a castle."

"I have a castle. It's a valley. It has better weather."

"It has *your* weather, which is rain with opinions." She was quiet a moment, turning her cup. Then, in a different voice, lighter and more careful at once: "I've been thinking about maps. About how every map I ever drew had edges, and past the edges were monsters, and it turns out the monsters were mostly just people nobody had bothered to chart. There are gifted families moving already — did you know? Since the amnesty was read in the squares. Walking roads at night, looking for places that won't burn them. Nobody's mapping the safe ways. Nobody's drawing *that* map."

I looked at her. Her profile against the city lights, sharp-tongued, sharp-eyed, already three journeys ahead of me as usual.

"Sefa. What are you proposing?"

"A new commission," she said. "Self-appointed. First client pending." She glanced sideways at me, and the grin that broke across her face was the old one, the road one, the one that had gotten us through checkpoints and marshes and at least one riot. "Unless the Thornwarden has other plans."

I felt the thorn turn gently beneath my ribs, patient as winter, and I thought of the ridge above Thornvale, and the green coming back through the ash come spring, and a wooden sword hanging on a wall that had earned its retirement.

"As it happens," I said, "I'm going home. And the road east is long, and I've heard it's poorly mapped."

She drained her cup and stood, pulling me up by the wrist like the tide pulling a boat.

"Then pack light, speaker," she said. "We leave at dawn, and I'm charging you double for the cartography."
