## 37. Ostrev Resigns

The commission of a crown surveyor, Ren Ostrev had learned over twenty-two years of service, was a document with three uses. It opened doors. It closed mouths. And when folded into quarters and carried against the heart, it stopped an arrow about as well as any other piece of paper.

He had never needed the third use. He suspected he was about to need none of them at all.

The tribunal chamber in Corvane had once been a granary. You could still see it in the proportions of the room—the soaring rafters, the iron hooks where sacks of barley had hung, now draped with banners that sagged in the damp like tired men pretending to stand at attention. Someone had laid rush matting over the old grain-stained floor, but the smell remained: dust and sweetness and time. Ostrev found it fitting. Justice, in his experience, was mostly agriculture. You planted testimony, you waited through seasons of argument, and what grew was rarely what you'd sown.

He stood at the witness line for the fourth morning running, his commission in his breast pocket, watching the tribunal's three magistrates arrange themselves behind the long oak bench like crows settling on a fence.

Magistrate Delvine sat center, a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and the patient expression of someone who had read every lie ever filed in triplicate. To her left, Magistrate Corren—old, gout-ridden, loyal to the regent the way a dog is loyal to a hand that feeds it, which is to say completely and without curiosity. To her right, the youngest, Magistrate Ashka, who had been appointed six months ago precisely because she had no history anyone could hold against her, and who watched everything with the hungry attention of a woman determined to have a history worth holding.

"Captain Ostrev." Delvine did not look up from her ledger. "You are present again today under your own petition. The tribunal reminds you that your testimony regarding the events at Thornvale has already been entered."

"It has, Magistrate."

"And yet you wish to amend it."

"I wish to complete it," Ostrev said. "There is a difference. Amendments suggest the first account was wrong. Completions suggest it was unfinished. Mine was unfinished because I stopped telling it at the point where I stopped being able to bear it."

A murmur moved through the gallery. There were perhaps forty people there—clerks, a few crown officers, and, seated apart under guard, a cluster of villagers from Thornvale in borrowed clothes that didn't fit them right. Ostrev had not been able to look directly at them for three days. He made himself do it now. A woman with burn scars along one forearm met his eyes and did not look away, and he held her gaze the way you hold a hot iron: deliberately, because dropping it would be worse.

"The floor is yours," Delvine said.

Ostrev took out his commission.

* * *

He had written the speech eleven times. He knew this because Sefa had counted the drafts, back when she'd agreed—grudgingly, with the air of a woman doing him an enormous favor she intended to remind him of forever—to read them.

"You bury the lede," she'd said, tapping the seventh draft with one finger. "You're a surveyor. Survey. Start with what's actually there."

"What's actually there is a burned village."

"What's actually there," she said, "is a man who requested reinforcements to arrest one gifted person and got soldiers who burned forty homes instead, and who then spent two years pretending the arithmetic worked out. Say that part out loud. The rest is decoration."

She was insufferable. She was also correct, which was the most irritating quality a person could have, and Ostrev had come to treasure it the way one treasures a whetstone: useful, abrasive, and never gentle.

So he began where the truth began.

"On the ninth of Harvestmonth, two years past, I filed a request for reinforcement to investigate Thornvale, a valley community of some three hundred souls. My stated suspicion was that the village harbored a swordspeaker—a person carrying the gift of the old oath-lines. My evidence was thin. A peddler's rumor. A child's drawing of a wooden sword that hummed. I want the tribunal to understand how thin it was, because everything that followed grew from that thinness like rot from a hairline crack."

He paused. In training they taught you to pause; it gave weight to words. He had used pauses for years as weapons. Now he used one simply to breathe.

"The reinforcements arrived under Captain Vess. Captain Vess operated under standing orders regarding gifted persons, orders which classify the gift not as a crime but as a condition—like plague—and authorize containment measures. Containment, in the field, translated to fire. I will not soften that word. Forty-one structures were burned. Eleven people died, most of them by smoke rather than blade, because the soldiers were not murderers, only men following a map drawn by frightened people. An elderly woman named Ilsa Villalobos gave herself up as the speaker they sought, knowing full well she was not, and her confession bought the village time that saved perhaps sixty lives. She died in custody before she could be tried. Her confession was false and everyone involved knew it, and we accepted it anyway, because a false answer is so much more comfortable than no answer at all."

He looked down at his hands. They were steady. He had expected them not to be, and their steadiness felt like a small betrayal—he wanted the tribunal to see him shake, wanted some outward sign that the cost of these words matched their weight. But the body, he was learning, keeps its own ledger, and his had apparently decided that trembling was a debt already paid.

"When I arrived, the village was burning and the speaker I had come for was gone. I pursued. Over the following months I pursued across half the realm. I executed—" the word stuck, and he forced it through, "—I executed a gifted child found on the marsh road east of Vaelmora. She was nine years old. Her name was Pell. She was carrying a carved bird she had made herself, and the last thing she said to me was not a curse. It was a blessing. She blessed me. I have thought about that blessing every day since, and I have concluded that either it worked or I am beyond its help, and I no longer believe I am beyond its help, because if I were, I would not be standing here feeling every word of this like a stone laid on my chest."

Silence. The granary rafters held it the way they had once held grain.

* * *

They gave him a recess after that, which he understood to mean that Magistrate Corren needed time to send a message to the regent's office, and that Magistrate Ashka needed time to stop looking delighted, and that Magistrate Delvine needed no time at all because she had known exactly what he would say, having read every lie ever filed in triplicate and, apparently, every truth as well.

In the colonnade outside, he found Sefa waiting on a barrel, sketching. She drew maps the way other people breathed—constantly, unconsciously, her charcoal moving even while she talked. What she was drawing now appeared to be the colonnade itself, with a small figure on a barrel labeled *me, suffering*.

"You went long," she said.

"I cut the part about my brother."

"Good?"

"No. Necessary-bad. There's a difference." He sat down heavily beside her. His knees had started announcing themselves lately, a development he attributed to age and she attributed to guilt, and he had stopped arguing because she was probably right about that too. "My brother died in the gifted uprising at Callow Ford. That's why I joined the surveyors. That's why I hunted speakers. I told myself I was keeping other families from what mine suffered. And then I stood in a burning village and realized I had become the thing I'd hated—men with orders, doing damage to people who had done nothing, and calling it protection."

Sefa's charcoal paused. "Do you know what Theo said about you? After Ostrev's Offer—after you let them go at the capital, after you broke your orders to open the crypt?" She didn't wait for him to guess. "They said: *he was the most dangerous kind of man, until he wasn't, and the distance between those two men was one dead child.*"

"That's generous."

"That's Theo. They're precise about people the way I'm precise about coastlines." She blew charcoal dust off the page. "The tribunal isn't going to pardon you, Ren. You know that."

"I'm not asking for pardon. I'm asking for the record to be true. Pardon is for people who want to sleep well. I've made other arrangements." He touched his breast pocket, where the commission lay folded. "I'm asking for something else entirely."

* * *

The afternoon session was Corren's counterattack, and it was competent, in the way a well-drilled soldier is competent. He produced Ostrev's service record—commendations, the Vaelmora pursuit, the offer he'd made Theo at the marsh edge, terms that had in fact saved the surviving villagers of Thornvale when honored. He produced witnesses who described a captain of rigor and principle.

"Rigor and principle," Corren said, turning to the gallery, "are precisely what the crown requires. Captain Ostrev acted within his orders. If his orders were cruel, the cruelty belongs to the order-givers, and this tribunal should note that the man before us is attempting to indict a system while wearing its uniform."

"That," Ostrev said, rising, "is exactly half right."

Delvine lifted an eyebrow. "Captain."

"The cruelty does belong to the order-givers. I agree. I am here to give it back to them." He drew the commission from his pocket, unfolded it, and held it up so the seal caught the gray light from the high windows. "This document authorized me to open doors, close mouths, and stop arrows. It did all three. It also, through the orders it served, burned a village, killed an old woman who died protecting her neighbors with a lie, and put a hole in the world where a nine-year-old girl used to be. I have carried it for twenty-two years. I am returning it."

He walked to the bench and laid it down in front of Delvine, and the sound it made on the oak was very small, smaller than any sound he had ever made in his life, and larger too.

"Ren Ostrev resigns his commission," he said formally, because formality was a kind of armor and he was allowed this last piece. "And having nothing left to protect, I am free to say what I actually know. Which is this: the gift is not a plague. I have seen it heal a dying prisoner at the cost of a year of a life—I witnessed it, I signed the report, and I lied in that report about how it was done. I have seen it bind a power that would have drowned this capital, freely given, by a person your hunt drove to the edge of the world and back. The gifted are not your enemy. Fear is your enemy, and fear wears uniforms, and I wore one for twenty-two years. I ask leave to testify—for the gifted, at the tribunals to come, wherever they are held, however I am received. Not as a captain. As a witness."

Ashka leaned forward. "You understand that the regent's office will call this treason adjacent."

"I understand that the regent's office burned Thornvale and called it containment. We are both fluent in euphemism, Magistrate. I prefer plain words now. I've heard what they cost, and I can afford them."

Corren's face had gone the color of porridge. Delvine, ink-stained fingers steepled, looked at Ostrev for a long moment with an expression he would spend the rest of his life trying to name and would eventually settle on *recognition*.

"Leave is granted," she said. "Witness Ostrev. The title costs the crown nothing and may cost you everything. I suspect you find that arrangement familiar."

* * *

That evening, in the tribunal's guest quarters—a cell, essentially, but with better light—someone knocked.

It was one of the Thornvale villagers, the woman with the burned arm. She stood in the corridor holding a covered dish, looking as though she had lost an argument with herself on the way there.

"Marta," she said. "Marta Elling. I was at Thornvale. I—" She stopped, jaw working. Then she thrust the dish at him. "Barley soup. From the granary days, we always said the building remembered how to feed people. I don't forgive you. I want that clear. But you put yourself between the soldiers and the well that night, and I saw it, and my sister lived because the bucket line kept working. So. Soup. Not forgiveness. Just... soup."

Ostrev took the dish with both hands, the way you take something holy or heavy.

"Not forgiveness," he agreed. "Soup is a start. Soup is how everything starts."

She almost smiled—it was there and gone, like a fish under ice—and left him standing in the doorway with his supper and his ruin and, for the first time in two years, something that was not quite despair sitting in his chest where the certainty used to live.

He ate the soup. It was good. He cried into it a little, which he decided did not count, there being no witnesses.

* * *

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a clerk who would not meet his eyes: the regent's office demanded Witness Ostrev appear before the Privy Council to explain his testimony, and further, that the tribunal had received a petition that same morning—one bearing a seal Ostrev recognized from his hunting days, the coiled-thorn sigil of House Amarel—requesting that certain documents in his possession be entered into evidence.

Documents. Plural.

He had only ever spoken of one report. The healing at the prison camp. He had never mentioned the second thing—the page he'd taken from Wren Calloway's effects, the one listing the names of the dispossessed family, the one that suggested the Unbinding had begun long before the eclipse, that someone inside the crown itself had fed the Amarels the schedule of the coronation.

Someone inside the crown. Which meant someone inside the council that now summoned him.

Ostrev dressed slowly, folded Marta's empty bowl into his pack, and looked at himself in the spotted mirror. A witness looked back. No uniform. No commission. Nothing left to lose, which the old Ostrev would have called destitution and which he was beginning to understand was another word for free.

"Well," he told the mirror, "let's go open some doors."

The bowl clinked softly against the page in his pack as he walked, and he found himself hoping—absurdly, stubbornly—that somewhere east, in a green valley where magic could not follow, a gardener with an old face and young eyes was tending thorn shoots and would someday hear his name without flinching.

He had a great deal of work to do before he deserved that. He intended to begin immediately.
