## 38. Return to Thornvale

The road into Thornvale had never been long. As a child, Theo had measured it in games—one hundred steps from the boundary stone to the first fence, two hundred to the well, and by the time you reached the well you were home, and home was a place where nothing ever followed you.

Now it took most of a day, because Theo's body took most of a day to do what it used to do before breakfast.

They stopped at the ridge where the valley opened below them like a cupped hand, and they let themselves look. That was the thing no one warned you about, coming home after everything: you had to earn the looking. You couldn't just arrive. You had to stand at the edge of it first and let your eyes do the grieving and the rejoicing at once, both of them shouting over each other like old friends at a wedding.

The village was rebuilt. Not as it had been—the burn scars still showed on the eastern houses, new timber pale against old stone, like bones knitting under skin—but rebuilt. Smoke rose from chimneys in honest gray threads. The mill wheel turned. Someone had replanted the orchard rows in straighter lines than the originals, which meant Sefa's maps had made it back here somehow, or someone with her particular stubbornness about right angles had taken up a spade.

And the thorn was gone.

That was the strangest part. For three hundred years the Drowned Thorn had wrapped Vaelmora like a fist around a heart, and now it lived in Theo's blood instead, dormant but present, a second pulse that beat slightly out of time with the first. Theo had spent two years learning its rhythms the way you learn a housemate's footsteps on the stairs. It stirred when they dreamed of water. It went very quiet when they were happy, which Theo had decided was either shyness or contentment, and they preferred not to examine which.

Here, at the valley border, it did something it did nowhere else in the world.

It hushed.

Not silenced—the border didn't work that way anymore, not for Theo, not since the third oath. Magic could not follow them into Thornvale; that law was older than kingdoms and had survived even the eclipse. But the thorn wasn't following them. The thorn *was* them. So it crossed the way their heartbeat crossed, the way their breath crossed, folded up inside their flesh like a letter carried through a storm in a coat pocket.

Theo stood with one foot on valley soil and felt the contradiction settle into place, familiar now, almost comfortable: the whole world said magic could not enter this place, and here was magic walking in wearing boots, humming an old song only Theo could hear.

"You're doing the face," said a voice behind them.

Sefa came up the last stretch of road with her pack riding high on her shoulders and her map case strapped across her chest like armor, which, in a sense, it was. She was browner than when they'd parted—a year charting the southern sanctuaries would do that—and there was a new scar through one eyebrow that she refused to explain, which meant it was a good story.

"What face?"

"The face where you're about to say something profound and then don't." She stopped beside them and looked down at the valley too. "Huh. They fixed the bridge."

"They fixed everything."

"Someone with taste," she said. "Those rooflines are wrong, though. See how they've pitched them? That's coastal work. Somebody hired a crew from the Saltmarsh towns."

"Sefa."

"Mm?"

"Are we going to stand here cataloguing architecture, or are you going to admit you walked four hundred miles out of your way to see me home?"

She was quiet for a moment. Below them, a dog barked at something invisible, and the sound rolled up the valley walls and came back softened.

"I had surveying to do in the north," she said with tremendous dignity.

"In the north."

"There are roads in the north."

"There is one road in the north, and it goes to a fishing village with a population of forty."

"Fifty-one now, actually, since the Amarel families—" She caught herself, and then gave up, because Sefa giving up on a deflection looked like a door being unlocked. "Fine. Yes. I wanted to see it. I spent a year drawing safe roads for people who lost everything, and half of them ended up asking me what Thornvale was like, and I realized I'd never seen it. I've been describing your home to refugees for twelve months, Theo. I described the well. I invented a tree."

"What kind of tree?"

"A willow. By the well. Very picturesque."

"There's no willow by the well."

"I know," she said. "I feel terrible about it. Come on. Let's go find out if anyone remembers you."

* * *

They remembered.

That was the other thing no one warned you about. Theo had braced for fear—they were the speaker now, publicly, undeniably, the person whose blood held a drowned kingdom—and what they got instead was Marta the baker dropping a tray of rolls to come hug them hard enough to crack ribs, and old Fenwick gripping their forearm with both hands and saying "you look terrible, lad" in a voice thick as porridge, and children.

The children were the hardest part.

They came out of doorways and around corners in a loose, curious swarm, keeping the exact distance children keep from something they've been told is important. A girl of maybe seven stared at Theo's hands as if words might be visible in the knuckles. A boy asked, with the directness of his age, "Is it true you're older than my granddad now?"

"Inside, mostly," Theo said.

"Inside what?"

"Myself."

He considered this with great seriousness. "That's where my granddad keeps his anger."

"Your granddad sounds wise."

"He shouts at geese."

"Well," Theo said gravely, "we all carry our burdens."

Sefa laughed so hard she had to sit down on the well—which had no willow beside it, and Theo made a point of noting this aloud, and Sefa said the willow was clearly seasonal—and the sound of it carried down the street, and doors opened, and more people came out, and by evening there was a feast in the square that nobody had planned and everybody contributed to, the way feasts happen in villages that have learned not to take abundance for granted.

Marta pressed a plate on Theo and watched them eat with open maternal scrutiny. "You're too thin."

"I'm seventy years old, Marta. Thin is an achievement."

"Don't joke about that." Her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she busied herself with the bread. "We heard what it cost. Ostrev wrote to us. He writes every season, did you know? Like a man paying off a debt. He told us what you did at the capital. What you gave."

Theo looked at the bread in their hands. Grain and water and salt and heat, and none of it asking anything of anyone. "It seemed like the price at the time."

"And now?"

"Now I think prices are just stories we tell about worth." They smiled, and it was mostly true. "Eat your roll, Marta. You dropped a whole tray for me. I'm not worth two trays."

"You're worth every tray in the valley," she said fiercely, and went off to be embarrassed somewhere else.

Later, near the fire, Fenwick lowered himself onto the bench beside Theo with the elaborate care of a man negotiating with his own knees. For a while he said nothing, just watched the flames, and Theo let him. Fenwick had taught them to whittle. Fenwick had taught them that a blade was a promise you made to your own hands. Fenwick had once, without comment, looked the other way during a silent practice session that had shaken the fence posts, and Theo had never forgotten the shape of that mercy.

"The wooden sword's still in my shed," he said at last.

"I know. I checked on the way in."

"Still practice?"

"Every morning. There's no gift here to speak with, but the hands remember anyway." Theo turned their palms up. Old calluses over new ones, the way sediment builds in layers. "Wren says the discipline matters more than the power. Says the oaths were never really about words. They were about knowing exactly what you mean before you say it."

"Hm." Fenwick poked the fire. "Your grandmother used to say something like that. About you."

Theo went still. "You talked to Ilsa? About me?"

"Once a year, every year, at harvest. She'd sit where you're sitting and complain that you whittled crooked, and I'd tell her crooked wood holds glue better, and we'd drink cider and neither of us would say the true thing, which was that she was terrified, and so was I, and loving something fragile makes cowards of everyone." He shrugged. "Villages run on unsaid things, lad. Always have. We just build fences around them and call them property lines."

"She never told me any of it. The lineage. The thorn. Any of it. Twenty years of lies."

"Twenty years of a roof over your head and bread in your mouth and no hunters at the door," Fenwick said. "I'm not saying it was right. I'm saying I watched her make that choice every single day, and it cost her more than the truth ever would have, and if you can't see that yet, you will, when you're the one holding something precious and deciding who to tell." He stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and patted Theo's shoulder once. "Crooked wood holds glue better. Think about it."

* * *

They walked the boundary at dawn.

This was the ritual now, and Theo suspected it always would be: each morning, a slow circuit of the valley's edge, where the world's magic pressed against the valley's silence like tide against a seawall. On the outside, Theo could feel everything—the hum of ley-lines under the earth, the weight of spoken blessings drifting along the trade roads, the thorn's deep slow attention coiled in their marrow. On the inside, all of it went quiet except the part they carried.

Two kinds of silence, and Theo was fluent in both.

At the western boundary, where the burn scars from the raid still marked the ground in pale crescents, Theo found something new.

A shoot.

It came up through the scarred earth like a question—dark, slender, thorned, unmistakable. An offshoot. A seed of the Drowned Thorn, carried out of Vaelmora in the chaos of the eruption, or perhaps carried in Theo's blood all along and planted here by some alchemy of oath and soil. It should not exist. Nothing magical should grow in Thornvale. And yet here it was, six inches tall, its thorns soft as new whiskers, and against every law of the valley, Theo could feel it.

Not hear it. Feel it. The way you feel a sleeping child breathing in the next room.

"Oh," Theo said softly. "Oh, you shouldn't be here."

The shoot did not answer. But it leaned, very slightly, toward their voice.

Theo crouched for a long time in the cold morning light, one hand hovering over the impossible small dark thing, feeling the enormous dormant thing inside their chest turn its attention toward it like a lighthouse swinging its beam. Somewhere behind them, smoke rose from chimneys. Somewhere ahead, the world was still full of frightened people and hunted families and a crown learning, slowly and badly, to keep its promises.

And here, in a valley where magic could not follow, a piece of magic had put down roots and was waiting.

Waiting for what?

Theo thought of Fenwick's crooked wood. Of Ilsa's twenty years of daily courage. Of a girl who'd invented a willow by the well because some places need shade whether or not nature provides it. Of Wren's voice: *each speaker must write the oath themselves.*

"All right," Theo told the shoot quietly. "But we do this my way. No oaths. No drowning. Just... growing. Can you manage that?"

The thorn in their blood pulsed once, gentle as a second heartbeat.

Behind them, on the road from the south, small and dusty and dragging a bundle nearly her own size, a child crested the ridge and stopped, staring down at the valley—at the stranger kneeling in the scorched grass with their hand out to a black thorned shoot that had no business existing anywhere, least of all here.

Theo did not turn around. But they smiled, because some inheritances you can feel arriving from a long way off.

"Well," they murmured to the shoot. "Looks like we're going to need a bigger garden."
