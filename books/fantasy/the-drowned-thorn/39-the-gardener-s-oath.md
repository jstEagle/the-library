## 39. The Gardener's Oath

The offshoot by the well had grown a new tendril overnight, and it was reaching for the bucket rope with the unmistakable air of something that intended to steal.

"No," Theo told it, the way one tells a cat, or a child, or a cat that is also a child.

The tendril paused. Thorn always paused when spoken to directly—it had learned, over the years, that Theo's words carried weight even here in the valley where magic was supposed to be dead. Then it curled back on itself with what Theo could only describe as wounded dignity, and went back to pretending to be an ordinary blackthorn bush, which it was not, and never had been.

"You're spoiling it," said Pip from the top of the well wall, where she sat swinging her legs and eating an apple she had absolutely not been given permission to pick. "You talk to it more than you talk to me."

"I talk to you plenty."

"You tell me things like *mind the thorns* and *don't say words you haven't weighed*." She took another bite of apple, chewed with great deliberation. "That's not talking. That's weather."

Theo leaned on the hoe and considered this. Fifteen years ago—no, sixteen now; the winters blurred together after a certain age, the way rivers blur after a certain distance—they would have bristled at the comparison. These days they mostly agreed with it. Words were weather. They came in fronts, they changed the shape of everything they touched, and if you weren't careful about them, you woke up one morning to find your whole landscape rearranged.

"Fine," Theo said. "What shall we talk about, then?"

Pip shrugged with the enormous indifference of the eleven-year-old, which is to say she cared desperately and would die before admitting it. "Why does it grow toward water? The real thorn drowned a whole kingdom, Sefa's maps say so. You'd think it would hate wells."

"Ah." Theo set down the hoe and crouched beside the offshoot, close enough that its smallest thorns caught the morning light like black glass. "That's because you've got the story backwards. It doesn't love water. It loves *weight*. Water is just the heaviest thing most places have."

"Heavier than stone?"

"Stone sits still. Water moves. Moving things carry their weight differently." Theo reached out—not touching, never quite touching—and let one finger hover over the newest tendril. Beneath the skin of their own wrist, faint as a second pulse, something answered. A slow green pressure. A wordless attention. *Good morning*, said the thorn, in the only language it had left. *Good morning*, Theo answered, without sound, without spending anything. Some conversations cost nothing. Those were the only ones worth having before breakfast.

"It listens," Pip said. Not a question. She'd known for three years, ever since the night she'd followed Theo out here against every instruction and seen the gardener and the garden speaking to each other in silence.

"It listens," Theo agreed. "And someday, when you're older, I'll teach you why that matters more than what it says."

"You always say *someday*."

"And someday you'll understand why I always say it."

Pip made a face that suggested this was the worst answer in the history of answers, hopped off the well wall, and went to bother the goats, who had the good sense to look sympathetic.

Theo stayed by the well a while longer. The morning was doing what Thornvale mornings did: arriving slowly, without ambition, gold light pooling in the rebuilt rooftops below. Sixteen years since the fire. Sixteen years since Ostrev had ridden out of the capital carrying his resignation like a wound he'd finally decided to stop hiding, and Sefa had unrolled her first sanctuary map on Theo's kitchen table and said, *Right, where do we put the next forty families?* Sixteen years since Theo had walked back through the pass with decades added to their face and a kingdom's worth of thorn sleeping in their blood, and found the village already rebuilding without them, because villages are stubborn that way, and thank every quiet god for it.

The valley had kept its promise. Magic still could not follow across the ridge—the border hummed along it still, invisible and patient, a fence made of forgetting. What Theo carried lived inside them now, past the reach of any border. A contradiction with legs. The only one of its kind.

Most mornings, that felt like peace.

Some mornings, like this one, when the offshoots grew bold and the pulse under their skin ran warm and restless, it felt like holding a door shut with your whole body while something on the other side politely asked, again, whether you might consider opening it just a crack.

Theo picked up the hoe. There were beds to turn, and the eastern offshoot needed pruning before it got ideas about the chicken coop again.

* * *

The discipline of words, as Theo taught it—and taught it they did, though never yet the oaths themselves—began with bread.

"Say what this is," Theo instructed, setting a loaf on the table between them. It was a good loaf, dark rye, Ilsa's recipe, which meant it had opinions and expressed them through crust.

"Bread," said Pip.

"Wrong."

Pip scowled. "It's *bread*. I can see it. I ate half of it."

"You named one thing. Now name what it isn't. Name what it cost. Name who ground the flour, whose hands pulled the loaves, how many mornings the oven took to heat, what the wheat drank, what the miller's daughter is called, why the crust cracks exactly there and nowhere else." Theo turned the loaf slowly. "A word is a door, Pip. Most people walk through doors without looking. A speaker has to see every hinge."

"That's not fair. Nobody knows all that about bread."

"Nobody needs to. That's the mercy of it. But you do. Because the day will come—I hope it never comes, but the day may come—when someone hands you a word the way I'm handing you this loaf, and asks you to spend it, and you will need to know what's inside it before you bite." Theo broke the bread and gave her the larger half, which was also part of the lesson, though Pip hadn't caught that yet either. "So. Start small. Tell me about this flour. Where did it come from?"

"The mill at Alderbrook."

"Whose mill?"

"Marta's. Old Marta. She has a dog that hates me."

"Why does the dog hate you?"

"Because I threw a stick once and it hit the dog instead of going over the fence, and dogs remember, Pip-the-dog remembers *everything*—"

"There. You see?" Theo smiled. "Two minutes ago this was just bread. Now it's Marta's mill and a grudge with four legs. Every word you'll ever speak is carrying that much world inside it. Your job, for the next few years, is simply to notice. Nothing more. Can you do that?"

Pip chewed her bread and thought about it with her whole face, eyebrows working. "Can I practice on the goat?"

"The goat is a poor subject. The goat only means one thing at any given time, and it's usually *food* or *revenge*."

"So it's honest."

Theo laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of them, the kind that still ached pleasantly in the chest afterward, in the place where old grief had healed into something usable. "Honest, yes. Fine. Practice on the goat. But mind—" and here Theo let the humor settle, deliberately, the way you let silt settle in a jar before pouring clean water off the top, "—when you speak near the offshoots, speak plainly. No jokes, no curses, nothing you don't mean. They're young. They take things literally."

"They're plants."

"They are," Theo said, "the children of something very old, and very lonely, and currently asleep in my blood. Humor it."

Pip looked at them for a long moment. She was sharp in the way that made teachers both proud and tired; she heard the second sentence inside every sentence. "It talks to you," she said quietly. "Not just listening. Talking."

Theo held her gaze. This, too, was part of the discipline—not lying to the child, and not telling her more than her shoulders could carry. "It dreams at me," Theo said. "Mostly about rain. When you're older, I'll tell you what else it dreams about."

"Someday."

"Someday."

She nodded, satisfied for now, and ran off to terrorize the goat, and Theo sat alone with the bread and the long afternoon light and the slow green pulse beneath their own skin, and thought: *Ilsa, you old liar, this is how you did it too, isn't it. One page at a time. One year at a time. Hoping the world stays quiet long enough.*

The world, in Theo's experience, appreciated being asked. It rarely obliged.

* * *

Sefa arrived on the autumn cart, as she did twice a year, bringing salt, news, and insults in roughly equal measure.

"You've let the east bed go to seed," she announced before she'd fully climbed down, because Sefa greeted people the way storms greeted coastlines.

"I have not. It's resting."

"It's *weeds*, Theo. I can see it from the road. In my professional opinion as a maker of maps, that patch of your garden no longer exists. It has returned to wilderness. I should chart it."

"Fifteen years of friendship and you open with my weeds."

"Someone has to. You've surrounded yourself with children and livestock and a bush with delusions, none of whom will tell you the truth." She said it grinning, though, and embraced them hard enough to creak ribs, and Theo held on a moment longer than strictly necessary, because Sefa was sixty-one now and her hands were stiff in the cold mornings, and neither of them mentioned it, and both of them noticed.

They drank tea in the kitchen while Pip hovered at the edge of the room performing the elaborate pantomime of Not Listening that meant she was listening to every word.

"Sanctuary routes are holding," Sefa reported, unrolling a fresh map across the table, weighting the corners with the salt crock, a spoon, and Theo's teacup without asking. "The northern passes are closed till spring, but the coast road's quiet. We moved eleven families through last month. Eleven." She tapped a small inked mark, one of dozens. "There's a girl among them, maybe fourteen, lights candles without touching them. Doesn't know she's doing it. Her mother thinks she's cursed." Sefa's mouth twisted. "I told the mother about a farm in the hill country where a woman teaches bread-words to an orphan. Told her the woman used to be somebody frightening."

"Did you."

"Told her the frightening part wore off. Mostly." Sefa looked up, and for a moment the grin was gone and it was just Sefa, tired and steady, the way she'd been on the night she tore up a crown-paid map and chose a side. "They'll come in spring, probably. Word travels. It always travels toward you, Theo. You know that."

"I know that."

"You could say no."

Theo turned the teacup, watching the leaves settle. "Ilsa said no for thirty years," they said. "Look how that ended."

"She'd have liked this, though. The teaching." Sefa nodded toward the window, where Pip was visible in the yard, standing very seriously in front of the goat, apparently mid-lesson. "The old woman burned everything rather than let it pass on. And here you are, planting it."

"Not planting. Gardening." Theo smiled faintly. "There's a difference. Planting is deciding what grows. Gardening is deciding what gets to live, and trimming the rest, and hoping you're wise enough to know which is which."

"Hm." Sefa rolled the map back up. "And the oaths? Still keeping those locked away?"

"Still keeping them." Theo's hand drifted, without quite meaning to, to their own forearm—the skin there mapped with fine pale lines these days, like river deltas, like roads on Sefa's charts, the marks the thorn left where it slept closest to the surface. "Wren wrote down everything he knew, before the end. Three books' worth. They sit in a box under my floor, and every year I take them out and read them and put them back, and every year I understand a little more why each speaker must write the third oath themselves." They looked out at the yard, at the girl and the goat and the black bushes standing sentinel along the fence like patient soldiers. "You can't inherit a vow, Sefa. You can only inherit the need for one. The rest a person has to make alone, in their own words, at the moment they can't turn back. If I taught Pip the Binding tonight, it would be a dead thing in her mouth. A costume. It has to grow in her, or it isn't hers."

"And if the world doesn't wait for it to grow?"

Theo was quiet a moment. Under their skin, faintly, the thorn stirred—not alarmed, not eager, just attentive, the way it always attended when the future came up in conversation. It had opinions about the future. It had mostly stopped sharing them since the oath, since it had learned that Theo would listen and then do whatever they judged right anyway, which it seemed to find either admirable or infuriating depending on the season.

"Then she'll write it faster than I did," Theo said. "And I'll stand next to her while she does, and pray my knees hold out."

* * *

Winter came early that year, the way it liked to, barging in without knocking.

Theo found Pip in the garden at dusk, standing among the offshoots with her breath steaming, her hands jammed in her pockets, staring at the eastern bed with an expression Theo recognized from the inside. It was the expression of someone who has just heard something impossible and is deciding whether to be afraid.

"What is it?" Theo asked, coming to stand beside her.

"The bush talked to me." Pip's voice was carefully flat, the voice of a child refusing to be childish. "Not— not words. But I was thinking about the candle girl Sefa told us about, the one who lights them without touching, and I wondered what that feels like, and then—" she swallowed— "then all the thorns turned toward me. All of them. At once. Like they were curious."

Theo looked at the eastern bed. The branches had indeed settled back into their ordinary angles, but there was a quality to the stillness that Theo knew well: the stillness of something listening hard and hoping not to be noticed doing it.

"They were curious," Theo said. "You wondered aloud. Wondering is a kind of speaking, Pip. The oldest kind. Before words, before oaths—wondering is just a question pointed at the world, and some things in the world answer."

"Did they answer *you*? When you were my age?"

Theo crouched down to her level, joints announcing themselves, and took her cold hands in theirs. Above the ridge, the first stars were coming out, and somewhere beyond them the border hummed its patient note, and beneath Theo's skin the old green weight lay quiet and close, closer than usual tonight, like a dog leaning against a leg during thunder.

"When I was your age," Theo said, "a wooden sword hummed a single word in my hand, and my grandmother went white as flour, and within a month our whole valley was burning. That's my answer. That's the truth of it, and you're old enough now to hear it straight." Pip's eyes were wide and wet and fierce all at once. "But hear the rest of it too. The burning wasn't the end. What came after the burning—that was the end of hiding, and the beginning of this. Of gardens. Of bread-words. Of a girl standing in the snow asking honest questions of an honest bush." Theo squeezed her hands. "Your gift woke today. Not with fire. With curiosity. That's the best beginning anyone in our line has ever had, and I intend to make sure it stays the best ending, too. Do you understand me?"

Pip nodded. Then, because she was who she was: "Does this mean I get to skip chores?"

"It means the opposite of that."

"Worth asking."

They stood together a while in the falling dusk, gardener and heir, the offshoots black and patient around them. And far off, at the edge of the fields where the valley met the ridge and the ridge met the forgetting, Theo felt—or imagined feeling, it hardly mattered anymore which—the faintest pull, like a finger hooked gently in theirs.

Not urgent. Never urgent, anymore. Just present. An old friend at the property line, waiting to be walked home before dark.

"Pip," Theo said. "Fetch the lantern. There's someone I want you to meet properly, before winter sets in. Someone at the border."

"The border's just fog and a headache."

"So it is," Theo said, and smiled, and turned toward the ridge as the last light went gold and then violet over Thornvale. "Come and see anyway. It's time you learned why I walk out there every evening—and what, exactly, I say to the dark."
