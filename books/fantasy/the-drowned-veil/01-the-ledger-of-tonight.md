## 1. The Ledger of Tonight

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in a basement that smelled of tallow, mildew, and old money. Theo Nystrom had spent three years down there, reconciling sums no one else could make balance, and in three years they had learned one truth about the impossible: it always left a paper trail.

Tonight the trail was wrong.

Theo sat beneath a single lamp with the autumn quarter's ledgers spread around them like fallen birds. The guild's business was strange by design—hauntings brokered, miracles insured, prophecies appraised and shelved—but the arithmetic never lied. Numbers were the one honest language in Saltmere, and Theo spoke it fluently enough to hear when something stuttered.

Entry forty-one. *Payment received: House Veyran, for services rendered, 400 crowns.* Dated tonight.

The trouble was that entry forty-one was written in ink that had gone brown with age. The page itself was old—older than the ledger it sat in, which was the kind of impossibility Theo was technically employed to deny existed. Someone had pasted a century-old folio into this year's book, and someone had dated it today.

They turned the page with the care owed to a sleeping animal.

It wasn't an account at all. It was a fragment of verse, copied in a clerk's cramped hand, headed with a title Theo knew only from rumor: *The Drowned Veil*. A prophecy, then—the guild bought and sold those like fishmongers bought herring. Most were frauds. This one had been paid four hundred crowns to disappear, which usually meant it wasn't.

*On the night the ledger turns,* Theo read, *the Veiled King shall open his mouth over the city, and the city shall go down into him.*

Standard apocalyptic merchandise. Theo almost smiled. Then they read the next line.

*And the storm-caller who would not call, whose name is struck from every roll but ours, shall find their name here, on this night, and know that their hiding was purchased too.*

Below it, in the same browned ink, in the same century-dead hand:

*Nystrom.*

Their own name. Spelled correctly. Even the odd archaic spelling their grandmother had used, the one Theo had abandoned with everything else when the exile-oath was sworn.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, the guild's night clerks moved through the counting rooms on soft feet, and Theo sat very still, feeling the floor tilt under a building that had not moved in two hundred years.

Invisibility was work. They had done the work. Refuse the crown's war, refuse to call the storm, take the oath of silence and erasure—and then vanish so thoroughly that even the god-king's spies lost the thread. Three years of ledgers and lamplight, of being nobody, of being safe.

Purchased. Arranged. Someone had made room for them here the way you make room for a piece on a board.

"The arithmetic bothered you."

Maren Corvane stood at the foot of the stairs. She was seventy if she was a day, the guild's First Reckoner, and she had taught Theo that numbers never lie—which meant, Theo understood now, that she had taught them to read lies fluently.

"You knew," Theo said. Their voice came out flat. "You put me in this chair."

"I put you in the chair where you'd be safest." Maren came down the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, and lowered herself onto the stool across the table. Up close her eyes were the pale gray of rain about to decide. "Sit down, child. You've gone white as a cancelled debt."

"Whose arrangement? Whose coin bought my—" Theo stopped. Started again. "The prophecy names me. Tonight. It named me a hundred years before I was born."

"Yes."

"That's not possible."

"No," Maren agreed pleasantly. "But it is true, and the guild has never confused the two. Would you like the whole of it, or will you sit there sharpening your anger first?"

Theo sat. It was that or fall over.

* * *

Maren talked for a long time, and Theo let the words come the way they let tide come over seawall—measuring, not resisting.

A hundred years ago, a king of Saltmere had been drowning—not in water, in office. The crown of this country was not a symbol; it was a vessel, and something lived in it. Something old and salt-hungry that the first kings had bound to their bloodline with oaths of loyalty, feeding it generation by generation until the thing below had grown fat enough to speak through its host. The kings called it the Veiled King. The priests called it divine right. The guild, paid handsomely by the palace, called it nothing at all.

"And we did more than stay quiet," Maren said. "When the binding began to fray—a century back, the god restless, the king of that day going strange—we were consulted. Discreetly. We sold them the mending. The rite, the wording, the architecture of the oath. We built the cage that holds him now, and we invoiced it."

Theo thought of the ledgers. Of course there was an invoice. There was always an invoice.

"So every oath since," they said slowly. "Every lord kneeling, every soldier swearing, every subject promising loyalty—it all feeds it."

"It sustains it. Feeding and binding are the same act, with that one. Loyalty given freely is a meal. Loyalty compelled is a chain. The craft was in making the people believe they chose." Maren folded her hands. "And the prophecy says the fraying has begun again. That tonight the ledger turns. That the Veiled King opens his mouth over the city."

"And my name in it."

"Your name in it." She looked at them without apology and without pity, which Theo had always privately suspected was the harder kindness. "When you refused the crown's war, the guild bought your exile. Quietly. The tribunal that struck your name, the safe passage here, the post in this basement—all arranged. Not to protect you, child. To keep you *placed*. A storm-caller who will not call is the single thread in this city the god cannot digest, and threads are either woven or cut. We chose to weave you somewhere useful."

The vertigo came then, properly, the way Maren had promised it wouldn't if Theo just breathed. Three years of safety, and the safety had a signature on it. Their refusal—the one clean thing, the one act that had been entirely theirs—had been processed, filed, and turned to account by the same institution that sold cages to gods.

"Then un-place me," Theo said. "Strike me from whatever roll I'm on. I'll walk out tonight."

"Where?" Maren asked gently. "The prophecy says tonight. The god knows it's found—or will by dawn. You are the one person in Saltmere already standing inside the story. Walking out doesn't leave the story, Theo. It just leaves you unwritten."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, a bell began to ring. Not the guild's hours-bell. The palace bell—the one that rang only for royal decrees, and rang at midnight for none in living memory.

Maren closed her eyes. "Ah," she said softly. "There's the mouth opening."

Theo was already on the stairs.
