## 2. What the Oaths Weigh

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its lies in triplicate. Theo had learned that in their first week among the ledgers: one copy for the client, one for the archive, and one for the fire, though the third was almost never burned because burning things left ash, and ash could be read.

So when the audit summons came from the palace—*the Crown requires an independent reconciliation of the Household accounts*, signed with a wax seal Theo had catalogued a hundred times—they knew it was not an invitation. It was a hook. And the guild had baited it with their name.

"You'll go as yourself," Master Corvane had said, sliding the commission across the table. "Exiled storm-caller, disgraced, harmless. Nobody fears an auditor."

"Nobody *likes* one either," Theo said, and took the papers.

Now they stood in the antechamber of the Hall of Audiences, in a borrowed coat the color of wet slate, holding a ledger that weighed more than their conscience did most days. The palace of Veymar was built to make visitors feel small—vaulted ceilings painted with the dynasty's history, each king rendered taller than the last, until the final panel where King Aldous stood alone beneath a crown so gilded it looked less like jewelry than like weather.

Theo had been counting things since childhood. It was how they survived the years before the storms answered them: count the floorboards, count the seconds between lightning and thunder, count the ways out of a room. They counted now. Four guards. Two doors. One servant girl polishing brass that was already bright, her eyes moving too much for someone doing nothing.

And one voice, coming through the audience hall's open doors, speaking with itself.

"—the granaries will be sealed at dusk." That was the king; Theo knew Aldous's public register from the broadsheets, all patience and gravitas. "And the grain within will be given to those who have sworn—" and here the voice changed, dropped half an octave, went smooth as water over a drowned thing, "—sworn, sworn, sworn. They are all sworn already. You know this. Why do you keep pretending there is anyone in this city who has not been swallowed?"

A pause. Then the king again, thinner: "There are always some. There must be some who chose freely, or none of it means—"

"*It means what I say it means.*"

The servant girl had stopped polishing. She was looking at the doors too.

Theo wrote nothing down. Some things you do not put in ledgers.

* * *

Sera had learned to read her father's decrees the way other people read weather warnings.

The latest one was nailed to the door of the chapel she wasn't allowed to pray in anymore, because the new liturgy hadn't been written yet and the old one apparently contained words the crown could no longer afford. *By order of the King: all oaths of loyalty shall be renewed monthly, in person, before a royal officer. Failure to renew shall be construed as revocation.*

Revocation. As if loyalty were a lease. As if love were rent.

She stood in the corridor outside the Hall of Audiences with the decree crumpled in her fist, and she heard the two voices.

She had been hearing them for months, telling herself it was grief—her mother dead two winters now, her father hollowed by it—and then telling herself it was illness, and then telling herself nothing at all, because telling herself things had stopped working around the time her father had ordered the harbor district emptied of everyone whose grandfather had once failed to bow low enough.

The second voice came out of her father's mouth. She had finally admitted that three nights ago, standing in his study doorway while he argued with the dark about harvest quotas, and the dark had argued back using his throat.

"Princess."

She turned. A stranger in an auditor's coat stood by the pillar, ledger under one arm, watching her with the particular stillness of someone deciding whether to run. Younger than Sera expected. Eyes the gray-green of the sea before it decides to be cruel.

"You're not supposed to be here," Sera said.

"No," the stranger agreed. "Neither are you, I'd guess. That corridor leads to the west wing, which your father's decrees closed to everyone including, I believe, you."

Sera's hand tightened on the crumpled decree. "You've read the decrees."

"I reconcile them. It's my job to find where numbers stop matching stories." The stranger hesitated, then said, quietly, "Your father is speaking in two voices in there. I heard it. Did you?"

Everything in Sera that had spent months being careful, being silent, being the dutiful daughter of a dissolving man—that everything rose up her throat like floodwater. "Every night," she said. "For a year. And every morning he doesn't remember, or pretends not to, and yesterday he told me that if I loved him I would swear the succession oath at dawn on—"

She stopped. Because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could come for you.

"On what?" the stranger asked. Not pushing. Waiting.

"There's a date," Sera said. "He keeps saying dawn, and there's a date, and it's tomorrow, and I don't know why tomorrow matters but *he* does. The other one inside him. He said—" her voice cracked and she hated it— "he said I was the last course."

The stranger had gone very pale. "The last course," they repeated. "Not the last heir."

"Does it matter?"

"It might be the only thing that matters." They glanced at the doors, then back at her, and made a decision Sera could almost see land behind their eyes. "My name is Theo Nystrom. I work for the Guild of Impossible Things, which sounds like a joke and is worse than one. May I show you something in your father's library? It involves a prophecy, a century of paid suppressions, and the reason your family has never once lost a war it deserved to lose."

* * *

The royal library at midnight smelled of dust and tallow and, faintly, of brine—as if the sea had gotten into the bindings and never left.

Theo laid the prophetic text on the reading table: the guild's suppressed copy, the one Corvane had let slip from the archive the night before, whether from guilt or strategy Theo hadn't yet decided. Beside it, Sera set the coronation rite—the actual one, not the printed pageant version, but the oath text kept under seal in the library's iron cabinet, which she had opened with her own signet because apparently nobody had imagined the princess might want to read her own future.

"Read the oath," Theo said. "Aloud. I need to hear where it sits in the room."

Sera read. "*I swear my breath to the crown, my blood to the crown, my children and my children's children to the crown, world without end, the crown above all, the crown before all—*" She stopped. "I've heard my father recite this at every ceremony. I never listened to the words."

"The guild transcribed this binding contract a hundred years ago and sold the method of it to your ancestors," Theo said. "That's the complicity part. That's the part I'm supposed to bury along with everything else. Listen to the structure. Every clause surrenders a choice. Breath, blood, lineage, end without end. It's not loyalty. Loyalty can be withdrawn. This is ownership, dressed in liturgy."

"And me?" Sera's voice was steady, which cost her. "Where do I fit?"

Theo turned to the prophecy's final page. The ink had faded to the brown of old seawater, but the line was legible, because the guild had traced over it in fresh ink sometime in the last week—Corvane's handwriting, Theo would have known it anywhere:

*The god cannot digest the unsworn heart. When the heir loves what the crown has not claimed, the feast fails.*

"When the heir loves what the crown has not claimed," Sera read slowly. "That's absurdly specific."

"Prophecies usually are. It's how you know they're accounting documents." Theo tapped the coronation rite. "You're the first heir in a century positioned to refuse. Every oath your ancestors swore fed it. Your father's oaths, your grandmother's—all of it pooled in the crown, and the thing wearing the crown grew fat on the interest. But you—" they met her eyes— "you've loved someone the crown doesn't own. Haven't you."

It wasn't a question, and Sera didn't treat it as one. "His name is Wren. He's a soldier in the harbor watch. My father doesn't know. If he knew—" She laughed, short and bitter. "If he knew, the other voice would know, and it would eat him next."

Down in the city, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. The alarm pattern—three, pause, three—which meant the harbor gates were closing.

Then footsteps in the corridor outside, unhurried, wrong somehow, like someone walking to the rhythm of a tide instead of a heartbeat. And her father's voice, alone in the empty hall, calling pleasantly:

"Sera? I know you're awake, little pearl. Come and practice your oath. Dawn comes early, and we are all so very hungry."

Theo blew out the candle. In the dark, Sera felt—not saw, *felt*—the stranger's hand find hers, cold and certain, and squeeze once: *together*.

For the first time in a year, she was not alone in a room with the thing wearing her father.
