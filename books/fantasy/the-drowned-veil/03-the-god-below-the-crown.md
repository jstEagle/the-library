## 3. The God Below the Crown

The bells stopped at midnight, and that was how Sera knew.

Veymar's bells had rung the hours since before her grandmother's grandmother—every quarter, every feast, every funeral. Tonight they simply ceased, mid-peal, like a singer struck dumb. The silence that followed had weight. It pressed against the palace windows and did not lift.

She stood in her father's audience chamber, watching him not-sleep on the throne. He hadn't left it in three days. Courtiers whispered about illness; the physicians came with their little brass instruments and left shaking their heads. But Sera had seen what they saw and named it differently: her father sat *listening*. Head tilted, eyes half-closed, as if the crown itself were murmuring into his ear.

"Theo Nystrom is in the palace," he said, without opening his eyes.

Her stomach dropped. "You know that."

"I know everything the water knows." He smiled, and it was her father's smile and it was not. It was too wide by one tooth. "Bring them to me at dawn. Bring my daughter also." His eyes opened then, and they were the grey-green of deep harbor water, of things seen from below looking up. "There is an oath to be sworn."

* * *

They came for Theo at the fourth hour, and Sera went down to meet them herself, because she could not bear to let the guards do it alone.

Theo stood between two halberds in the corridor outside the guest wing, hands bound with wet rope—the guards' ropes were always wet now, everything near the king was damp—and looked up at her with an expression she was beginning to know well: the flat calm of someone doing arithmetic about escape routes.

"Princess," Theo said.

"Auditor," she said back, because that was still their joke, their small shared thing. Three nights ago she'd caught them in the treasury with a lantern and a forged seal, and instead of calling the guard she'd asked what they were really counting. They'd told her the truth. Nobody had ever done that before—not the truth, not first, not free.

"They're going to make me swear something at dawn," she said quietly, when the guards had been dismissed to a distance. "My father. Or whatever is wearing my father. I can feel it pulling. Like a tide going out and taking my name with it."

Theo's jaw tightened. "Don't swear anything."

"I don't intend to die for my principles, Auditor."

"You won't have to. There are other options. Bad ones." A pause. "I found the ledger tonight. The old one. My guild sold the binding—a century ago. Paid to suppress the prophecy, paid again to supply the rite that anchored him to your line. Every oath since has fed him. Yours would be the last link. After yours, there's no unmaking it."

Sera leaned against the cold stone. Above them, somewhere, her father sat listening to the sea inside his own skull. "And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I was exiled for refusing to be a weapon." Theo's voice stayed level, but their hands didn't; they were trembling, fine and constant, like a struck string. "I'm apparently being offered the chance to be a different kind of weapon. And I wanted you to hear it from me before you heard it from them."

"From who—"

Doors opened at both ends of the corridor.

* * *

The guild arrived the way guilds do: politely, expensively, and all at once. Five of them in grey traveling cloaks, led by Magister Corvane, Theo's mentor, a woman whose kindness Sera had heard described as legendary and whose eyes were currently doing sums.

They met in the map room, because the throne room belonged to the king now and everyone knew it.

Corvane spread documents on the table like a merchant laying out cloth. "The exile is rescinded. Full reinstatement, senior rank, the archive access you've dreamed about since you were nine years old." She slid a sealed writ across the table toward Theo. "In exchange: you attend the dawn ceremony as our observer. You certify the rite. Nothing more is required of you. The princess swears, the binding completes, and the Veiled King settles into permanence—and Veymar gets a thousand years of stability instead of a war with a god."

"That's not stability," Theo said. "That's digestion. Slowly."

"Everything is slowly, Theodore. Empires. Marriages. Gods." Corvane's voice softened into the register she'd used on Theo since childhood, the one that meant *I am being reasonable and you are being young.* "You refused to call storms. Fine. Admirable. Here is a way to save a city without raising your hand against anyone. All it costs is one girl's freedom."

"One girl's—" Sera stepped forward before she could stop herself. "I'm standing right here."

"I know, Highness." Corvane didn't even look at her. That was the worst part. "That's rather the point of you."

The silence after that was very long. Sera watched Theo look at the writ—their name spelled correctly, their exile formally ended, everything they'd lost for seven years handed back in one page—and watched them understand exactly what accepting it would mean.

"You knew," Theo said finally. "When you trained me. When you sent me to reconcile ledgers in border towns where nothing happens. You kept me invisible so no prophecy could find me."

"Invisibility is a service we provide," Corvane said gently. "It was always someone else's arrangement, dear. It usually is."

Sera saw it happen: the moment Theo's face went from hurt to something colder and more useful. They picked up the writ. Turned it over. Set it down again without signing.

"I'll attend the ceremony," Theo said. "As observer."

Corvane smiled. "That's all we ask."

It was a lie, and everyone in the room knew it was a lie, and the lie walked out the door wearing grey cloaks.

* * *

Dawn was two hours away when Sera found Theo on the seawall, alone, watching the harbor. The water was wrong—too smooth, too high, climbing the steps of the quays like a patient animal testing a fence.

"My father used to bring me here," she said. "Before the crown got... hungry. He'd point at ships and invent histories for them. He was a good man once. I need you to remember that tomorrow, whoever's sitting on the throne."

Theo didn't turn. "I'm going to break my oath."

The words landed like ice down her spine. "Your exile-oath? If you break it—"

"—the last anchor loosens. Yes. And I lose whatever protection the oath bought me, and the guild brands me apostate, and possibly the sky falls in." Theo finally looked at her, and in the pre-dawn dark their eyes were very bright. "But oaths are what's feeding it, Sera. All of them. Loyalty curdled into possession. The only way to starve a god made of promises is to break one—loudly, publicly, at the exact moment it's most hungry."

"And if it doesn't work?"

"Then I'm an oathbreaker and you're a queen-thing, and we'll both be very disappointed."

Sera laughed despite herself—a short, cracked sound. Then, quieter: "Why? Why burn yourself down for me? We've known each other four days."

Theo was quiet long enough that the water had time to climb another step.

"Because duty put us both in cages," they said. "Yours had gilding. Mine had ledgers. And I have spent seven years telling myself that staying hidden was survival, but it wasn't. It was just a smaller cage with better accounting." Their hand found hers on the seawall—cold fingers, steady now, the trembling gone. "You're the one oath it can't digest. Did you know that? Love freely given isn't food. It's poison. Your soldier, your refusal, your *choice*—you're the flaw in the rite. I'm just the storm."

Somewhere behind them, across the sleeping city, the bells began to ring again—all of them at once, every hour collapsed into one sound, tolling the summons to dawn.

Sera closed her eyes. When she opened them, she said: "Then let's go ruin a coronation."

They walked back through streets already filling with soldiers under martial law, past criers announcing the ceremony in her father's new voice—two voices braided together, human and drowned—and up the palace steps into the light just breaking over the harbor.

On the dais, beneath the crown that gleamed like something wet, the thing that had been her father turned its head and smiled at her with too many teeth.

"Sera," it said, in the voice the water knew. "Come and be eaten."
