## 4. Storm-Called

Dawn came up gray as unwashed wool, and the whole city was made to stand in it.

They had cleared the Grand Causeway from the palace steps to the harbor mouth. Ten thousand people pressed shoulder to shoulder behind guilded barriers, and above them, on scaffolds draped in mourning-white, the banners of the crown hung motionless in air that had forgotten how to move. No gulls. That was the detail Theo kept returning to, standing in the crowd with a forged audit seal sweating against their ribs. Ten thousand people and not one gull. The birds knew. The birds had always known.

On the dais beneath the palace steps stood two thrones. One held the king.

The other held the crown.

It sat alone on a cushion of drowned silk, black iron furred with salt-bloom, and even at this distance Theo could feel it looking. Not seeing—*weighing*. The way the ledgers weighed. The way the god below the crown had weighed every oath sworn in this city for a hundred years, and found them all delicious.

Sera stood beside her father in a white gown she clearly hadn't chosen, her hands folded like a woman at a funeral. Which, Theo thought, was accurate. Her eyes swept the crowd once, twice—and stopped on Theo for half a heartbeat. No recognition. Nothing. She was better at this than Theo would ever be.

The king rose.

He moved wrong. That was the thing no decree could disguise: he moved like something wearing a king the way the crown wore iron. His mouth opened and the voice that came out was his own, cracked and old and *terrified*, saying the words of the succession rite—and underneath it, threaded through every syllable like rot through timber, the other voice. The deep voice. The one that sounded like water closing over your head.

"Let the heir approach," said the king, in both voices at once, "and be bound."

Theo's hands were shaking. Not from fear. From the ledger in their head, the column that wouldn't balance: *one oath to break, one storm to call, one chance*. They had spent the night walking the causeway's length, memorizing the placement of every guild barrier, every guard, every exit that would close the moment things went wrong. There were no exits. They had checked three times. That was how you knew you were doing the right thing—the math only worked if you didn't plan to survive it.

The Guild had offered them everything at midnight. Master Corvane, in his office that smelled of ink and old compromises, sliding a document across the desk: reinstatement, exile lifted, name restored to the rolls. All they had to do was stand in the crowd today and witness, and file the report, and let the ceremony proceed. "The binding has held a century," Corvane said. "It will hold another. Stability is also a service we provide."

"You sold the binding," Theo had said. "You wrote the instructions."

"We *archived* the instructions," Corvane said, with the patience of a man explaining rain to a fish. "There is a difference, Nystrom, and the difference is what keeps cities standing."

Theo had left the document on the desk. Un-signed. The first ledger entry they'd ever refused to make.

Now Sera climbed the dais steps, slow as a woman wading upstream, and knelt before her father, and the high priest began the words that would finish it—the oath of succession, the last thread, the one the god could not digest unless she gave it freely. Unless she *chose* it. Love against duty, made literal, laid out on a cushion of drowned silk.

Theo started walking.

Not running. Walking, the way an auditor walks toward a discrepancy. Through the crowd, past the first barrier—the guards knew the seal, and the seal was good until someone thought to check it—up along the causeway's spine where the whole city could see them, a small figure in guild-gray walking toward the throne with a ledger under one arm.

"Name yourself," the priest said, faltering.

"Theo Nystrom," they said, and their voice carried farther than it should have, because the air wanted to help now, the air had been waiting a hundred years for someone to say a true thing out loud. "Exiled storm-caller. And I'm here to conduct an audit."

"Seize them," said the king—in the king's voice, then again in the deep one, and the two voices snagged on each other like hooks.

"No," Theo said. "Listen. All of you, listen. Your king has been dead for years—not dead, *eaten slowly*. Every oath of loyalty you've ever sworn to the crown, it drank. Every 'yes, my lord,' every kneeling, every anthem—it ate them all, and it grew, and the Guild knew, and the Guild was paid to keep the recipe." They opened the ledger. Held it up. "I have the accounts. I will read them aloud. It will take four days, and I will enjoy every minute."

The crowd made a sound Theo had never heard before—not a gasp, not a roar. A *shift*. Ten thousand people reconsidering at once.

And the god turned its attention on them.

It came off the throne like cold water off a height. Theo felt it take their measure, felt it find the exile-oath coiled around their heart like anchor-chain—the oath they'd sworn at nineteen, *never to call the storm, never to wield what I was given, on pain of unmaking*—and felt it understand, with a god's slow delight, that here was the last knot. Here was the thread it had been saving.

"*Little breaker of storms,*" it said, through the king's mouth, through the priest's mouth, through ten thousand throats at once, and the crowd screamed and then went silent, holding its own breath. "*Swear to me. Kneel, and be fed, and be spared.*"

"I'd rather not," Theo said.

They took out the knife they'd stolen from the guild kitchens—unglamorous, bread-stained—and pressed it to their own palm, and spoke the words of their exile-oath backward, which is the only direction oaths can be broken, and which is why breaking them hurts.

"I swore never to call the storm," they said. "I unsay it. I swore never to wield what I was given. I unsay it. I was made small by men who profited from my smallness, and I *unsay all of it*—"

The chain didn't break. It *dissolved*, and every dissolved link went somewhere, and where it went was into the sky.

Because the sky had been waiting too.

Clouds that weren't there an hour ago were there now, stacking up over the harbor in towers, going green-black at the base, and the pressure dropped so fast that ears popped across the entire causeway. The god shrieked through ten thousand mouths. Its anchor-threads were snapping—one, two, a dozen—as the city's oaths, hearing truth spoken at volume, began to loosen on their own. But the deepest threads still ran down into the drowned dark, and the god gathered itself behind the king's eyes and *held*.

So Theo did the thing they had been exiled for refusing to do.

They called the storm.

Not with rage. With bookkeeping. They named the debt—a century of swallowed loyalty, compounded daily—and they called it due, and the sea stood up.

It came in off the harbor like a wall with opinions, and the rain hit sideways, and lightning walked the causeway on legs of white fire, and Theo stood in the middle of it with their arms open, conducting, *reconciling*, pouring everything the sky owed and everything the sea remembered down onto one black iron crown. The god fought. Of course it fought. It drowned a hundred years of the city's kneeling back up at them, showed Theo every oath it had ever eaten, offered them all of it—power, permanence, a throne of their own—

"I've seen your ledgers," Theo said. "They don't balance."

And the sea took the crown.

That was all, in the end. The wave came up the steps, precise as a signature, and closed over the iron and the silk, and when it withdrew the crown was gone—down, down, back into the deep it had crawled out of, dragging its god behind it like an anchor finally doing its job. The pressure lifted. The rain gentled to ordinary rain. Somewhere overhead, impossibly, a gull cried.

The king collapsed.

Sera caught him. Held him while he became human again—old, and light as paper, and *himself*, his eyes clearing for the first time in years, finding his daughter's face. "Sera," he said. Just that. Just her name, in one voice, his own. "You were never mine to spend."

"I know," she said. "Rest."

He died between one breath and the next, quietly, a man and not a vessel, and the crowd—ten thousand people soaked to the skin—made no sound at all, because some silences are the loudest thing a city can do.

Then Sera Vane stood up, heir to a crown that no longer existed, and turned to face her people.

She looked at the empty cushion. She looked at the sea. Then she did the thing that would be argued about in taverns for a hundred years: she picked up the drowned silk, wrung the seawater out of it, and tore it in half.

"No more crowns," she said, and her voice carried the way Theo's had, because the air was still listening. "No more oaths you can't take back. I will lead because you choose it—tomorrow, and the day after, and every day after that, and any day you stop choosing it, I step down. That is the whole ceremony. That is the entire crown." She held up the torn silk. "This is what power weighs now. Nothing you can't put down."

The silence broke—not into cheering, not at first, but into something better: ten thousand people talking to each other, arguing, laughing, crying, *deciding*. Democracy sounds, Theo thought. The sound of a ledger being rewritten from scratch.

Sera found them at the edge of the dais, soaked through, bleeding from the palm, holding a bread knife and an incriminating ledger.

"They'll want to give you something," she said. "A title. An island. People always want to give storm-callers islands."

"I'm hard to reward," Theo said. "I keep audits."

"Good." She glanced back at her city, at the barriers coming down, at the guards removing their helmets like men waking up. "Then stay hard. Because the Guild is already drafting its account of today—I guarantee it. And in their version, none of this happened, or worse, they arranged it." Her jaw set in a way Theo was beginning to recognize as the family resemblance to something better than kingship. "I'm going to need an auditor who can't be bought, can't be promoted, and has a personal grudge."

Theo looked at the torn silk, the empty throne, the gray-gold morning coming up clean over a city that belonged, for the first time in a century, to itself.

"Personal grudge," they said. "I can start immediately."

Far out past the harbor, where the water went dark, something old settled into the sediment and began, very slowly, to count what it had lost.

It was patient. It had been patient before.

But that, Theo decided, pocketing the ledger, was tomorrow's entry.
