## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The ink froze first.

Freya Sterling noticed it before anything else—her nib dragging, the black going thick and sluggish in the cold, so that the word *tithe* came out of her pen looking like something drowned. She set the pen down, flexed her fingers, and breathed warmth onto them. The scriptorium was always cold in autumn. The temple's stone held winter the way a miser holds coin: greedily, and long past reason.

Around her, forty other scribes bent over their desks in the grey hour before dawn, copying tithes by lamplight. The scratch of forty pens was the only sound permitted. High Canon Vess had said once, in one of his rare addresses to the lower clerks, that silence was the shape devotion took when it was honest. Freya had copied that saying into three separate ledgers since. She had never believed it. Silence was the shape fear took when it was practical, and she had been both silent and practical for eleven years.

She was twenty-six years old and she had made herself invisible with the patience of a mason laying stones. She arrived first. She left last. She corrected no one's errors aloud and claimed credit for nothing. Her handwriting was deliberately unremarkable—the middle hand of the middle rank, neither beautiful enough to be promoted nor sloppy enough to be punished. She had learned early that the temple noticed two kinds of people: the ambitious and the failing. Everyone else it digested slowly and without ceremony.

So when the wards failed, she did not gasp like the others.

She felt it happen. That was the strange part—that she felt it at all. A sensation like a door opening somewhere behind her ribs, a draft moving through a house she hadn't known had rooms. The lamplight didn't change. The cold didn't deepen. But every hair on her arms lifted, and the ink in her well shivered though nothing had touched it, and somewhere far above them all—

Sister Maren looked up first. Maren was seventy and half-blind and had copied tithes for longer than Freya had been alive, and when a woman like that lifts her head from her work, you look too.

"The stars," Maren said. Her voice cracked across the silence like a dropped plate. "Children. The stars are gone."

Forty scribes rose. Forty chairs scraped. Through the high windows of the scriptorium, where the mountain sky should have been wheeling with its familiar constellations—the Plough, the Sisters, the Wolf that gave the valley its oldest stories—there was nothing. Not cloud. Not fog. Clouds have edges; fog has weight. This was an absence with the texture of absence, a black so complete it looked painted on, and Freya's stomach dropped through the floor of her careful life as she understood that the sky itself had been *unmade*.

"Sit down."

High Canon Vess stood in the doorway. He had come silently, which he could do; he wore soft-soled boots beneath his vestments, a small vanity Freya had noticed years ago and filed away among the hundred other observations she kept and told no one. He was a tall man gone slightly stooped, silver at the temples, with the mild face of a man who has never once raised his voice because he has never needed to.

"Holy Father," Maren began, "the wards—"

"Are being attended to." His gaze moved across the room, unhurried, and settled nowhere in particular, which was its own kind of threat. "You will return to your desks. You will copy your tithes. Whatever you saw or did not see tonight will not be discussed in the refectory, the dormitory, the wash-house, or anywhere else the temple can hear. There are explanations, and they will come from me, and until they do, speculation is a form of theft—stealing peace from your brothers and sisters. Am I understood?"

Forty voices murmured assent. Forty scribes sat. Forty pens resumed their scratching, though Freya noted—and noted that she noted, because noticing was the one rebellion she allowed herself—that more than half of them were no longer writing words. Their pens moved; the lines came out blank, or loops, or the same letter over and over. Fear writes gibberish. She knew because hers was doing it too.

* * *

She lasted four hours before she broke her own rules.

It was not courage. She wanted to be clear about that, even alone in her own head, especially alone in her own head. Courage would have meant walking to Vess's office and asking questions to his face. What Freya did instead was what she always did: she went to the records.

The debt rolls lived in the undercroft below the tithe-vault, and Freya had spent eleven years earning the right to fetch and reshelve them unsupervised, one unremarkable competence at a time. The rolls recorded every bonded soul in the valley—every family whose name carried a debt to the temple, every obligation, every term of service, every year remaining. It was dull work, sacred only in the way a well is sacred: necessary, deep, and easy to fall into.

Her own name was in there. It had been in there since before she could read. *Sterling, Freya. Bonded. Term: life.* Orphaned at five, taken in by the temple, bound in payment of a debt no one had ever explained to her because everyone assumed she already knew. She had stopped asking at nine, when Brother Aldous had told her that some debts were inherited like eye color, and asking after them was bad manners.

Tonight she pulled the roll of the current decade and unrolled it down the S's with hands that were almost steady.

Sterling, Halden. Sterling, Ivetta. Sterling, Marta. Old names, dead names, names of farmers three generations gone. The roll was meticulous. The roll was *always* meticulous—it was the one document in the valley that could not afford errors, because souls were weighed against it.

There was no Sterling, Freya.

She read the column twice. Three times. She ran her finger down it the way she'd been taught, tracking each line with a fingertip so the eye couldn't skip. Nothing. Her name simply wasn't there, and where it should have been there was a gap—a clean break in the sequence, the parchment there paler than the parchment around it, as if something had been scraped away and the roll resealed.

Freya had repaired damaged rolls. She knew what honest damage looked like: water stains that bloomed, rodent teeth that left crescents, ink that faded unevenly because age is never tidy. This was none of those things. This was a gap with *edges*. Someone had cut her out of the record of her own life, and then someone else—or the same someone—had smoothed the parchment down so carefully that only a person who had spent eleven years learning what honest ledgers looked like would ever see the lie.

No honest ledger has a gap. That was the first law of the undercroft, taught to every novice with their first pen. A ledger may be wrong, but it is never *silent*. Silence, in a record of souls, is not emptiness. It is concealment.

She stood in the cold dark of the undercroft with the roll open in her hands and felt the carefully invisible architecture of her life shift and groan around her like a building deciding whether to stand. Eleven years. Eleven years of unremarkable handwriting and unclaimed credit and questions swallowed at nine years old, all of it built on the assumption that the ledger was true, that the temple was true, that her bond was real and her place was earned and her invisibility was safe.

If her name was not in the roll, then either she was not bonded—which meant eleven years of service had been given freely to an institution that would have taken them anyway—or her bond existed somewhere else, in some other record, written by some other hand.

Both possibilities were worse than the life she'd had this morning.

And underneath the fear, quiet as a second heartbeat, something else stirred. Curiosity. Forbidden, warm, alive. The same feeling she'd had at seven, finding a locked drawer in the tithe-vault and lying awake all night wondering—not what punishment she'd earn, but what was *inside*.

Vess had ordered silence. Vess had come to the scriptorium within minutes of the wards failing, soft-soled and calm, ordering forty witnesses back to work before anyone could think. And the roll with the gap in it was the current decade's roll—the one most recently handled, most recently sealed, most recently within reach of very few hands.

Freya looked down at the roll in her arms. The pale gap where her name should be seemed to look back.

She thought: *if I put this back, I will spend the rest of my life not knowing, and I have already spent eleven years not knowing, and the stars are gone, and the sky does not care about my manners.*

She thought: *they cannot punish what they cannot see. They never learned to look at me.*

She took the roll.

Not all of it—she was still practical, still Freya, still the woman who laid her invisibility stone by stone. She took the leaves bearing her family's name, the S-section entire, wrapped in her spare shift and bound with the plain cord she used for her hair. Then she reshelved the empty casing, closed the vault, and walked back up through the sleeping temple with the stolen record pressed against her ribs like a second heart, past the chapel where the ward-lamps burned steady and false, out into the courtyard where the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

Above the eastern wall, where the border-stones ran toward the mountains, she could just make out the old ward-markers, dark and dead as burnt matches. Somewhere along that line, she would later learn, a disgraced smith was watching the same empty sky.

But that was later. For now there was only the cold, the silence, the roll against her heart, and the first honest thought she'd had in eleven years:

*Someone wrote me out. Which means someone, somewhere, wrote me in.*

She went to find out who.
