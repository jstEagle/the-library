## 3. Where Magic Ends

The border was a scar you could walk along.

Freya had copied its dimensions a hundred times in the tithe rolls—*Wardline, northern reach, 40 leagues, maintenance due at solstice*—but ink had never prepared her for the feel of it. The wards should have hummed. Every child in the valley learned that before they learned their letters: stand near the border stones and you'll hear the valley breathing, a low sweet note like a wet finger on glass. Pilgrims made journeys just to press their palms against the stones and feel it shiver up their arms.

There was nothing. The silence was so complete it had texture, like wool packed in her ears. She walked the line with her satchel of repair tools she didn't know how to use and her exile writ folded against her heart, and the dead stones passed under her hands one after another, cold as riverbed pebbles.

She had been walking since dawn. The temple's last kindness was a mule, and the mule had abandoned her at the ford, trotting back toward the valley with what she chose to interpret as pity.

By midday she reached the shrine-stone at Wolf's Tooth Pass, the largest on the line, and sat down against it because her legs simply declined to hold her anymore. From here she could see the whole sweep of the border country—the scrub hills, the gray ribbon of the river, and beyond it the mountain where the maps stopped pretending and became guesses.

The sky above the mountain was wrong. She'd been telling herself all morning that it was weather, that clouds gathered differently over high peaks, but there were no clouds anywhere else. Just that one patch of darkness, dense as spilled ink, sitting over the summit like a lid.

The stars hadn't come back. Of course they hadn't. But Freya kept looking up anyway, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

"You're supposed to be three days ahead of me."

She came off the stone so fast she scraped her palm. A man stood ten paces down the wardline, and she knew him instantly, though she'd only seen him once across a scriptorium floor—Ash, the disgraced ward-smith, the one whose trial the canon had summarized for the rolls in four dry lines. He looked worse than his reputation. Gray stubble, a coat burned through at both cuffs, and eyes that had the particular flatness of someone who had stopped expecting anything from anyone.

"They said you were exiled past the border," Freya said. "Years ago."

"I was." He shrugged. "Exile means they stop looking. It doesn't mean I left."

"That's not how exile works."

"That's exactly how exile works. You'll learn." He nodded at the shrine-stone behind her. "You've been touching them."

"Repair duty," she said, and heard how hollow it sounded even as she said it. "The canon sentenced me to—"

"The canon sentenced you to die somewhere he wouldn't have to watch." Ash crouched, pulled a knife, and scraped at the base of the shrine-stone. Under the moss and lichen, carved into the granite, was a line of script Freya didn't recognize—not temple script, not any script. Grooves worn nearly smooth by centuries of rain. "You know what these say?"

"No."

"Neither do I. Nobody does. That's rather the point." He wiped the carving with his thumb. "I spent eleven years out here trying to read them. Eleven years, because I believed what the temple taught—that the wards were prayer made solid, that the founders sang the valley safe and the song needed tending." His mouth twisted. "It's a lovely story. It's even mostly true. The wards *are* a song. But songs need singers, scribe. And the founders didn't sing it themselves."

Freya's mouth had gone dry. "What did they use?"

Ash stood. He studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to decide something.

"Come with me," he said. "Not to the valley. Downriver. There's a place I want to show you, and I'm not showing it to a woman who's going to run back to the canon and report every word."

"I can't leave the border. My sentence—"

"Your sentence is a fiction wrapped around a forgery. You know that better than anyone alive." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "You stole the debt roll. I know you did. The whole temple knows you did; the canon let you keep your head specifically so you'd carry yourself out here where inconvenient questions starve quietly." He turned and started walking downriver, not checking whether she followed. "So. Inconvenient questions. I have several thousand. You have until the river bend to decide if you want answers or safety. You can't have both. Nobody can."

Freya stood at the shrine-stone a long moment. Behind her, north, lay the valley—cold scriptoriums, warm bread, the canon's voice saying *silence* in that gentle terrible way. Ahead, a burned-out man walked toward the dark patch in the sky.

She followed him. She told herself it was because the mule had taken the supplies.

* * *

The place he showed her was a drowned chapel, half-swallowed by the river where the river bent wrong—a channel that looped back on itself, water running sluggish and backward, as though the current couldn't remember which way was downhill. The chapel's roof broke the surface like a ribcage. Ash waded in to his waist and gestured her toward a ledge of stone above the waterline, where something was bricked into the wall behind a crust of mineral bloom.

He chipped it free with his knife.

A contract. Vellum, water-swollen, but intact—protected by some craft Freya itched to examine, some seal that had kept the river out for two hundred years. She took it with the automatic reverence of her trade and began to read, and then stopped reading, because reading it and understanding it were two different acts and the bridge between them had collapsed.

*"Be it known beneath sky and stone: the undersigned has sold one soul entire, unencumbered, unto the Court Beneath, in perpetuity, as surety for the Warding of the Valley, termless..."*

"It powers everything," Ash said quietly. He had watched her read before she'd realized he was watching. "Every ward on the line. The lights in the temple. The blessing-candles. The little charms mothers sew into hems. All of it—one engine, burning one fuel." He tapped the vellum. "Prayer doesn't power the wards. Prayer is the *invoice*. The temple collects devotion and pays it downward, and downward takes payment in the only coin that matters."

Freya's lips were numb. "Whose soul?"

"Read the signature block."

She read it. Her vision swam and steadied.

The seller's name meant nothing to her—an old name, a founder's name, worn strange by archaic spelling. But beside it, in the clause of inheritance, where the contract named the blood-line bound to the bargain, the surety of sureties, the family answerable should the soul fail—

*Sterling.*

The river ran backward around her ankles. Somewhere overhead, in the wrong-colored sky, no stars waited.

"My family," she said. "My name is on this."

"Your name is on more than this." Ash crouched on the ledge beside her, and for the first time he looked at her not as a stranger or a nuisance but as something fragile he was tired of watching break. "I told you I spent eleven years out here. I wasn't reading carvings, not really. I was listening to the wards die. They've been dying my whole life, slowly, the note going flatter each winter. Because the soul that fuels them—" he exhaled— "it isn't infinite. It's *spent*. And when it goes out entirely, the wards go with it, and the things beyond the border that the founders bargained away the soul to keep out—" he gestured at the mountain, at the lid of darkness sitting on it— "come home."

"The stars," Freya whispered. "The night the stars went out—"

"Was the night the soul started dying in earnest. Not failing—dying. There's a difference. A flame gutters; a creature bleeds." He met her eyes. "It's still alive down there, scribe. Dying, but alive. Which means it can be saved. And if it's saved, the wards live, and the stars come back, because the stars were never stars. They're sparks off the fire the soul feeds. The valley's whole sky is a hearth-light, and somebody downstairs has been letting it burn low."

Freya sat down hard on the ledge. The contract lay in her lap, her family name staring up at her like an accusation, like a summons. Two hundred years of Sterling tithes. Two hundred years of her ancestors paying the temple, and the temple paying downward, and nobody ever saying aloud what the coin was.

"Why tell me?" she asked finally. "Why me? You could have shown this to anyone."

"Because the court below doesn't deal in strangers." Ash's voice dropped. "It deals in names, in debts, in blood-lines written into contracts. Your name is already in their books, Freya Sterling. When you walk down there—and you will, because you stole a ledger-roll and got exiled for it and you're the type who follows burned men toward dark mountains—you won't arrive as a stranger. You'll arrive as *collateral*." He held out his hand for the contract, hesitated, and instead pressed it back into hers. "Keep it. You're the one it binds."

"And you?" she said. "Why are *you* here? Eleven years at a dying border, watching. What's your stake?"

Something moved behind his flat eyes—grief, maybe, or its fossil. He looked away, down the backward-running water, toward the mountain.

"I sold something once too," he said. "Long time ago. I've been trying to buy it back ever since, and I'm out of coin." He stood, water streaming off him, and offered her his hand. "So. Scribe. You've read the contract. You know what's owed and who owes it. There's a road under that mountain and it starts at the river's drowned throat, and every hour we stand here the soul bleeds a little drier."

Freya looked at his hand. Then past it, at the dark lid over the peak, where her stolen stars were being held hostage in the dark.

She took it.

His grip was cold and certain, and together they turned upriver, toward the mountain, toward the flooded throat of the world—two debtors walking into the dark to collect what had been sold in their names.

Behind them, unnoticed by either, the backward water of the drowned chapel began, very faintly, to run the right way again—as if something far below had felt them coming, and opened its ledger.
