## 4. The Debt-Collector's Ledger

Ash had been to the drowned shrine twice before. The first time, he'd come as a ward-smith with a commission and a clear head, and he'd walked away without touching the thing at the bottom of the pool, because he'd understood even then what it would cost. The second time he'd come drunk on grief and river-spirits both, and he'd gotten as far as the water's edge before his own name—spoken aloud by the shrine itself, in his sister's voice—had turned him back.

Tonight would be the third time. Third times were for finishing things. That was an old smith's superstition, and like most superstitions it was a lie that happened to be useful.

He didn't tell Freya any of this. He told her only what she needed: *There's a place two days east of the border, where the river goes underground. There's something there that can help us.* He let her fill in the rest herself. She was a scribe; her whole life was filling in the rest from fragments. It made her easier to lead than she'd ever have admitted.

She walked like someone who had already lost everything worth guarding. That was good. That was also dangerous, because people who had nothing left to lose started thinking they could afford anything.

The valley behind them was dark in a way valleys were never meant to be. No stars. No wards. Just the temple's bell tolling the hours into a blackness that swallowed sound along with light, and somewhere back in that blackness, the high canon sitting in his warm scriptorium, pretending his ledgers balanced. Ash thought about the forged bond with Freya's family name on it and felt his hands curl into fists inside his gloves. He knew that handwriting too. Everyone who'd ever worked the border wards knew it. The canon had been signing off on the tithe-rolls for thirty years, and thirty years of signatures made a man confident that no one read them anymore.

Freya had read them. That was the trouble with scribes. You never noticed them until they noticed you.

* * *

They camped the first night in the ruins of a waystation that had once served ward-pilgrims, back when the border still hummed and pilgrims still came to press their palms against the standing stones. Ash built a fire small enough to hide and watched Freya sit beside it without warming her hands, staring into the flames like they owed her an explanation.

"The shrine we're going to," she said finally. "Whose is it?"

"Was," Ash said. "It belonged to a debt-collector of the Undercourt. One of the ones who walked the surface, back when the bargains were fresh and the ink was wet."

"Collectors die?"

"Everything dies." He fed a stick to the fire. "But collectors don't get buried. They get *left*. Wherever they fall, the ground takes them, and their ledger stays with them. Ink doesn't rot. Ink just waits."

"For whom?"

"For whoever's desperate enough." He looked at her across the fire and saw her flinch, and didn't soften it. "You should know what you're walking toward. The ledger can rewrite any bargain. Any contract, any debt, any bond. But it doesn't work for free. Nothing down here works for free—it's all Undercourt logic, even the abandoned pieces of it. To claim the ledger, you pay a price. And the price isn't coin."

"What then?"

"Memory." He said it flatly, the way you'd report a weather condition. "The ink drinks memory. Whatever you feed it, you lose. Not set aside. Not stored. *Lost*, like it was never written in you at all. You'll know there was a hole, because everyone always knows, but you won't know what was in it."

Freya was quiet for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightbird called and got no answer—the birds had been singing wrong since the stars went out, confused songs at wrong hours, the whole valley's clockwork coming apart at the seams.

"My mother," she said at last.

Ash didn't ask. Asking would have been cruel, because asking would have made her say it out loud before she'd decided, and decisions made out loud were harder to take back.

"She died when I was nine," Freya said anyway, because apparently she needed to hear herself decide. "I've kept her my whole life. Every memory I have of her, I've carried like—" Her voice caught and steadied. Scribes learned to steady their voices over ledgers; grief was just another entry error. "Like a book I was never allowed to finish reading. If I give her up, I don't lose her dying. I lose her *living*. Her hands. Her voice saying my name."

"Then we find another way," Ash said, and meant it, and hated himself for meaning it, because he knew there wasn't another way. The soul in the mountain was dying. The stars were already gone. Every hour they spent being decent was an hour the Undercourt's interest compounded.

"There isn't another way," Freya said. "You know there isn't. You brought me here because there isn't."

"I brought you here because I'm a coward who wanted someone else to make the choice."

That surprised her. He saw it land, saw her recalibrate whatever she'd built of him in two days of walking—a disgraced ward-smith, an exile, a guide—and understand that he was mostly just a man carrying a debt of his own, one the ledger hadn't written yet but would, eventually, collect.

"Then watch me make it," she said.

* * *

The river went underground through a mouth of black stone, and the shrine sat in the hollow where the water pooled before it fell. It had been a chapel once, maybe, or something older than chapels—walls of fitted stone furred with white lichen, a ceiling open to a sky that held no stars, and at its center, a pool perhaps ten paces across, so still it looked solid, so dark it looked deep past any honest depth.

On a stone shelf above the water lay the ledger.

Ash had remembered it smaller. Memory did that—shrank the frightening things so you could carry them. In truth it was a slab of a book, bound in something that might have been leather and might have been skin, its cover stamped with a seal he recognized from the old ward-contracts: a circle broken by a hand reaching through. The collector's mark. The Undercourt's mark.

The air around the pool tasted of iron and cold candle-smoke. Nothing moved. Nothing needed to. The shrine had been waiting longer than either of them had been alive, and it was very good at waiting.

"How does it work?" Freya whispered. She'd been whispering since they crossed the threshold, though Ash hadn't told her to. Some places instructed you without words.

"You wade in. You put your hand on the ledger. You speak the memory you're offering—describe it, truly, hold it in your mind while you do. If the ink accepts, the memory leaves. If it refuses..." He shrugged. "No one knows what refusal looks like. No one's come back to describe it."

"And you've stood here before."

"Twice."

"Why didn't you take it?"

Ash looked at the water. In its stillness he could see the reflection of the ledger's shelf, and below that, faint as a watermark, something else—a shape beneath the pool's floor, vast and coiled, like a root system grown around the world's foundations. The Undercourt's reach went deep. Deeper than the mountain. Maybe deeper than the sky the stars had abandoned.

"Because taking it means owing it," he said. "And I already owe the Undercourt everything. My sister's soul sits in a lantern under that mountain because I signed a maintenance contract I didn't read closely enough. I stopped paying the wards' true price, and the price came due, and they took her instead of me. If I touch that ledger, I don't know what's left of me to bargain with." He turned to face her. "You still have things. Spend one. Spend it well."

Freya took off her boots. Folded her coat. Set it on the stone with the neatness of a woman who had spent her life putting things in order before disasters, as if tidiness could hold the world together at the seams.

Then she waded in.

The water was colder than cold—it bypassed temperature entirely and went straight for something older, pulling heat out of her like ink out of a blotter. Ash watched her gasp, watched her keep moving, watched the water rise past her knees, her waist, until she stood chest-deep before the shelf, hair plastered flat, breath smoking in the shrine's dead air.

Her hand came down on the ledger's cover.

The pool lit from beneath. Not light exactly—*recognition*, a slow red-gold bloom rising through the black water like dawn happening in the wrong direction, and every surface of the shrine suddenly seemed to lean inward, listening. The lichen glowed. The stones hummed a note below hearing. And the ledger opened under Freya's palm, pages turning themselves with a sound like wings.

"Speak it," Ash said. "Hold it and speak it."

Freya closed her eyes.

"She's kneeling in the garden behind the temple," Freya said, and her voice shook and held. "Late summer. She's showing me how to pinch basil so it grows back fuller, and her hands smell green, and she's laughing at something I said—I don't remember what I said anymore, I only remember that she laughed, that laugh like a door opening into a warm room. She says my name. She says it the way nobody else ever has, like it's a small good thing she made herself. Like I'm hers. Like I belong to—"

The ink drank.

Ash saw it happen and would spend years failing to describe it. The memory didn't leave Freya like water leaving a cup. It left like a page being cut cleanly out of a book—her face going slack mid-word, the sentence ending not in silence but in absence, the warm room closing, the door gone, the wall where the door had been smooth and unbroken and utterly blank.

Freya opened her eyes.

She looked at Ash across the water, and her expression was the worst thing he'd seen since the stars went out: calm. Perfect scribe's calm. A woman checking a ledger and finding it balanced, unaware of the line that had been struck from it.

"It accepted," she said. Her voice was level. Curious, almost. "I don't... there was something. Wasn't there? Something I was holding."

"Yes," Ash said.

"I can feel the shape of it. Like a word I knew this morning." She looked down at her own hand, still resting on the ledger, and the ledger looked back—pages settling, ink drying, a new entry sealing itself into the record with a soft finality that Ash felt in his teeth. "What did I pay?"

He could have lied. He considered it, briefly, the way a drowning man considers breathing water.

"A memory of your mother," he said. "You chose it yourself. You described it. The garden. Her laugh. Your name in her mouth."

Freya stared at him. Then at the ledger. And Ash watched something move behind her eyes—not grief, because the grief had been taken with the rest of it, but the *space* where grief should live, a hollow with sharp edges, and he understood in that moment that the Undercourt's cruelest trick wasn't taking what you loved. It was leaving you intelligent enough to know the exact dimensions of what was missing.

"Good," Freya said.

The word came out wrong. Flat. A scribe's word, a clerk's word, a word for entries and totals. She climbed out of the pool, took the ledger from the shelf—it came willingly now, light as a prayer book in her arms—and dressed, and shouldered her pack, and stood looking back down into the tunnel where the river plunged toward the roots of the world.

"The water goes under the mountain," she said. "Doesn't it. That's the way in."

"That's the way in."

"Then we follow it." She turned to him, and her eyes were dry and clear and terribly efficient, and Ash thought: *I did this. I led her here. Whatever she becomes on the other side of this descent, I handed her the ink myself.*

"One more thing," she added, almost as an afterthought. "When we meet whoever holds my family's contract—I want to see the original. Every clause. Every signature. I want to read all of it."

"You will," Ash said. "And Freya—when we're down there, when they offer you things, and they will—remember what you paid up here. Remember that everything costs."

She tilted her head, and for half a heartbeat something flickered across her face, quick as a fish under ice—something that might have been sorrow, surfacing from a depth she could no longer reach.

"I don't remember what there is to remember," she said. "But I wrote it down."

She tapped the ledger under her arm. And Ash, following her into the dark throat of the mountain, realized with a cold turn of his stomach that she had—that somewhere in those drinking pages, the ink had recorded the memory even as it erased it, and the woman walking ahead of him now carried her mother's laughter sealed in a book she couldn't open, owned by a court she was about to enter, in a language written for bargaining.

Which meant, he understood, three steps into the flooding dark, that her mother's memory wasn't gone at all.

It was *down there*. Already filed. Already theirs.

And if the Undercourt ever figured out what it was holding—
