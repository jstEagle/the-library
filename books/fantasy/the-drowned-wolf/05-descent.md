## 5. Descent

The mountain's mouth was not a cave. It was a throat.

Freya understood this the moment they stood before it, because the rock did not open so much as *swallow*—a descending gash in the mountainside, black water sliding down its center like a tongue, and the air that breathed out of it tasted of iron and old prayers. Somewhere far below, something dripped with the patience of centuries.

"You're hesitating," Ash said.

"I'm reading," Freya said. "There's a difference."

She had spent eleven years in the temple scriptorium learning to read everything: contracts, censuses, the marginalia of dead clerks who'd grown bored copying tithes. The mountain's entrance bore markings—not wards, not quite. Invitations. *Come down. Come down and be counted.*

"River-things live in the flooded galleries," Ash said, shouldering his pack of smithing tools. "They can't take what you don't offer. But they'll ask for your name, and they'll ask in ways that sound like nothing at all. Answer true, and you pay. Refuse to answer, and they stop you. There's no third door."

"There's always a third door," Freya said. "That's what ledgers are for."

Ash looked at her then—a long, measuring look she was beginning to recognize as the way he looked at things he intended to use. "You've never lied in your life, have you? I can see it. You have the face of someone who has never once said a thing she didn't believe."

"That's not a compliment."

"It wasn't meant as one." He stepped into the water. "It's a warning. Down there, honesty is currency, and you're carrying a fortune. Spend it carefully."

The water rose to their knees within twenty paces, then their waists. Freya held the collector's ledger above her head, wrapped in oilcloth, and felt it pulse against her palms like a second heartbeat. Since the shrine, since she had paid a memory of her mother for it—the smell of bread, the sound of humming, gone now, a hole shaped like warmth where warmth used to be—the ledger had been *awake*. It knew when bargains were near.

It knew them now. She could feel it straining toward the dark like a hound on a scent.

The first gallery opened around them without warning: a cathedral hollow carved by water, ceiling lost in blackness, and the river running through its floor so clear it seemed lit from beneath though nothing below could possibly make light. Things moved in that light. Not fish. Shapes with the suggestion of faces, faces with the suggestion of patience.

A voice came from everywhere, from the water itself, gentle as silt settling:

*What do you carry, traveler?*

"The truth," Freya started to say—

And stopped. Because the truth was *a ledger that rewrites bargains, bought with my mother's memory*, and the moment she offered that truth she felt the water lean toward her, hungry, and understood that the river-thing would accept it as payment whether she consented or not. Truth given freely here became toll.

"A book," she said instead.

The water went very still.

*A book,* the voice repeated, tasting the word. *Is that all it is?*

"It's a book," Freya said, and each word cost her something she couldn't name, some small structural piece of herself groaning like a joint bent wrong. "Paper and ink and binding. That's all any book is."

Silence stretched. Then, softly, almost fondly:

*Clever little scribe. Pass.*

The light dimmed to let them through.

* * *

They waded gallery after gallery, and the questions came with every threshold.

*What is your purpose?* — and Ash answered, "To repair what I broke," which was true enough to pass, though Freya caught the seam of it: he had broken more than he would say.

*Who sent you?* — "No one living," Freya said, and the water chuckled around her ankles.

But the third questioner was different. It surfaced directly before her, a pale shape like a drowned child grown long, and it did not ask about books or purposes. It asked:

*Little one who lies so prettily—what is your name?*

Freya's blood went cold. Because this was the question. This was the whole descent compressed into four words, and the ledger against her chest burned hot as a coal.

She thought of the debt rolls. Of her name written in the canon's forged hand, sold and resold and bound without her knowledge. Of the gap where an honest entry should have been. Her name had been stolen once already. Every instinct she owned—the eleven years of careful, invisible, honest service—screamed at her to guard it now.

But the thing waited, and behind it the passage waited, and somewhere below a dying soul kept the stars extinguished.

"My name," Freya said slowly, "is whatever the ledger says it is."

It was not a lie. It was worse than a lie—it was a truth bent into a shape that concealed everything, and she felt the bending happen inside her own chest, felt some load-bearing wall of herself crack and hold, crack and hold. The river-thing tilted its long face, considering.

*That,* it said at last, with something like respect, *is a lawyer's answer. You did not have those in you when you entered. Where did you get it?*

"From the same place I got everything else," Freya said. "I paid for it."

The thing laughed—a sound like bubbles rising through a drowned bell—and dissolved, and the passage ahead stood open, and Freya realized her hands were shaking.

Ash was watching her again. "You lied to it."

"I didn't lie."

"No." His voice was quiet. "You did something better and much worse. You made the truth lie for you." He paused. "My sister used to do that. Argue circles around our father until he forgot what he'd asked."

The mention landed between them like a dropped stone. Ash's face closed. He turned and waded on, and Freya followed, and neither spoke for a long time.

* * *

The fifth gallery was dry.

It shouldn't have been—that was the first wrongness. Water roared somewhere above them, channeled through the ceiling in a vein of thunder, but the floor here was cracked stone furred with white mold, and the air was warm. Comfortably, horribly warm, like a room someone had been sitting in.

Someone had been sitting in it. A chair of fused bone stood at the gallery's center, facing a wall covered in writing.

Not carvings. Writing—thousands of lines of cramped script covering every surface, floor to vanished ceiling, in ink that still gleamed wet. Freya approached it the way she approached all text: professionally, hungrily. And then stopped breathing.

It was a contract. One contract, continuing across the entire wall, clause after clause after clause, and the hand was beautiful, patient, meticulous—and familiar. She had seen it on the debt rolls. She had seen it on her own forged bond.

"This is his work," she whispered. "The canon's. He's been *down here*. He's been negotiating with them for years."

"Or someone taught him the hand," Ash said. "Courtiers wear stolen faces, Freya. They wear stolen hands too."

She read on, translating the old contractual liturgy as fast as her eyes could move. Payment terms. Collateral schedules. Renewal clauses. And then, near the bottom of one column, a line that made her stomach drop:

*...the undersigned offers, in perpetuity, the soul currently designated FREYA STERLING, daughter of Maren Sterling, as surety against all obligations herein...*

Her. Written here, years ago, in the warm dry dark, while she sat upstairs copying tithes and believing her life was her own.

"He didn't just forge my bond," Freya said. Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "He collateralized me. I'm not a debtor. I'm *security*."

"Freya—"

"Don't." She pressed her palm flat against the wall, against her own name, and the ledger under her arm pulsed in answer, eager, awake, *hungry*. She thought about how easy the lying had come. How natural the bending had felt by the fourth gallery. She thought: *this is what power costs, and I am already paying, and I cannot stop.* "Don't tell me we should turn back. There's no back to turn to. He sold me before I ever knew I was for sale."

Ash said nothing. In the silence, the water overhead thundered on, and somewhere far below—so far it might have been imagination—something vast exhaled.

The last line of the contract, at the bottom of the final column, was fresher than the rest. The ink gleamed. It read:

*Final delivery to be made upon the next descent of a Sterling.*

Freya took her hand off the wall very slowly.

"They were expecting me," she said. "This whole descent—I'm not breaking in, Ash. I'm *arriving*."

Ahead, the dry gallery ended at a stairway leading down into red-lit dark, and from that dark came music: distant, courtly, patient. The sound of a table being set.

The ledger beat against her ribs like a heart that wasn't hers.

*Welcome home,* it seemed to say.
