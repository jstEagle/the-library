## 6. The Court Beneath

The water ended where the court began.

One moment Freya was swimming the flooded throat of the mountain, lungs burning, Ash's hand locked around her wrist; the next, her boots found stone and she was walking out of a black lake into air that tasted of candle smoke and old iron. Her clothes were dry. That frightened her more than the drowning would have. The Undercourt did not want her wet. It wanted her presentable.

The lake lay behind them like a held breath. Ahead rose a cathedral—not built but *grown*, if stone could grow—its pillars ribbed like the inside of a throat, its vaulted ceiling lost in dark that swallowed even the ledger's faint glow. And in that dark, light moved.

Fire hung from the ceiling in chains.

Not torches. Not lamps. Dragons of flame, each no larger than a hound, bound at the neck and wing with links of black iron, circling slowly in their cages of air. Their light was wrong—not gold but deep ember-red, the color of coals an hour after the fire dies—and it turned the whole cathedral into the inside of a dying heart. As Freya watched, one of the chained flames strained against its collar and let out a sound: not a roar, but a low, grieving note, like a bell struck underwater.

"Don't look at them long," Ash murmured. "They notice being noticed."

"Are those—"

"The last dragon-fire. What's left of it." His voice had gone flat, the voice he used for facts too heavy to carry any other way. "The valley's wards were spun from its heat, once. Before my grandmother's grandmother. Someone sold the source."

Freya's hand went to the satchel where the collector's ledger rode against her ribs. It had been warm since the shrine. Now it was warm as a sleeping animal, and it pulsed—slowly, patiently—like something counting.

Courtiers lined the nave.

They stood in rows on either side of the aisle, dozens of them, still as furniture, and every one of them wore a face Freya almost knew. A miller's face from Thornbrook. A girl who'd sold apples in the temple square. An old man who'd begged outside the scriptorium for years, whose name Freya had never learned because no one ever learns the names of beggars. The faces were right down to the pores and the crooked teeth—but they were worn slightly wrong, like coats buttoned by someone else, smiles set a half-second too late.

"They're stolen," Freya whispered.

"Borrowed," said the apple-girl, without moving her stolen mouth much. "Nothing here is stolen. Everything here is owed." Her eyes were the worst part—flat and patient as coins at the bottom of a well. "The king will see the scribe now. He has been waiting since before you were born. He is very good at waiting. It is most of what he does."

*Since before you were born.* Freya looked at Ash. Ash looked at the floor. They walked.

The aisle was longer than the cathedral could hold. Freya counted her steps out of habit—scribe's habit, measure everything, trust only numbers—and stopped counting at four hundred when the pillars began repeating. The courtiers' heads turned as she passed, all together, all at the same speed, a field of borrowed faces tracking her like sunflowers track a sun that isn't there.

At the end of the nave stood a table.

It was a long, low thing of drowned wood, black and swollen, set with chairs of bone on both sides, and behind it sat the king.

He was not what she expected, which she realized was probably the point. He was small. Thin. A man-shaped absence wearing a robe of lake-water that moved when no current moved it. His crown was a ring of river-stones, and his face—

His face was unfinished. Not blank—*unfinished*, like a sketch abandoned halfway, one eye detailed and lifelike, the other a suggestion, the mouth a line someone had meant to come back to. Where a face should have been finished, there was instead a kind of attention, vast and mild, settling on her the way deep water settles on a dropped stone.

On the table before him, between two unlit candles, sat a lantern of bone.

It was made from a skull—she saw that immediately, scribe's eye for materials, sheep or calf, something gentle—and through its eye-holes and its jaw-gaps glowed a light unlike anything else in the court. Not ember-red. Not candle-yellow. It was silver-white and it flickered in rhythms, quick-quick-slow, and Freya knew that rhythm, had copied that rhythm a thousand times in tithing rolls without knowing what she was copying.

A heartbeat.

"That," Ash said, and his voice cracked on the single word.

"You may approach the table," the king said. His voice was quiet and everywhere, the way a lake's cold is everywhere. "You have carried a dead man's book a very long way, scribe. Set it down. You must be tired. Carrying debts is heavy work. I would know. I carry most of them."

Freya did not set the ledger down. She walked until the table's edge touched her stomach, and then she opened the satchel and lifted the ledger out and held it against her chest like a shield, which was foolish, because it was a book, and books do not stop kings.

But the king flinched.

It was small—a tightening around the unfinished mouth, a stillness in the water of his robe—and Freya saw it, and Ash saw it, and somewhere in the rows of courtiers a stolen face smiled with genuine delight.

"So," the king said. "You've been reading."

"A little."

"Do not lie in my court." The mildness did not change, but the temperature did. The chained fires overhead dimmed, all of them, in unison. "Lying is permitted here—it is practically the liturgy—but lying *badly* is an insult, and I am easily insulted, and my insults are expensive. You have read the ledger. You know what it can do. You came here intending to use it. Say so, and we can begin honestly, which in this place is a form of intimacy."

"I came to take back a soul," Freya said. "The one in your lantern. It was sold under false pretenses—the contract bears my family's name, and the bond on my own name was forged to hide the sale. I'm here to void it."

"Void it." The king turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected was clipped. "Scribe. Do you know how I got this court?"

"No."

"Nobody asks. Everyone assumes. They assume I conquered it, or inherited it, or crawled up from some deeper dark. The truth is duller. I *inherited the debts*. Every bargain ever struck beneath this mountain, every soul pawned, every name signed away in desperation or greed or love—they all flow downhill, and downhill is me. I did not steal your valley's fire, child. I *bought* it. Fairly. From a man who had no right to sell it, which is a flaw in the title, yes, but flaws are currency here too." He gestured, and the lantern slid an inch toward her across the black wood. "And now you arrive holding the one book in creation that can rewrite my holdings. Do you understand what you are carrying? Do you understand what *I* would pay for it?"

"What would you pay?"

"Anything," he said simply. "That is the trap of owning everything. There is nothing left to want except the things that undo ownership."

The court had gone utterly silent. Even the chained dragons had stopped their grieving notes. Freya became aware that every borrowed face in the cathedral was watching her, and that the ledger against her chest had grown hot enough to feel through her shirt, and that it was pulsing faster now, eager, like a dog smelling home.

"Here is my offer," the king said, and he spread his unfinished hands, and for a moment—just a moment—he looked tired, anciently tired, a clerk at the end of an endless audit. "Sit at my table. Take a chair. Learn the work. Someone must hold these debts; the position is eternal and the benefits are considerable. You would keep the valley safe forever—you could write its wards yourself, better than they have ever been written. No more forged bonds. No more canonists skimming souls off the tithe rolls. A just court, run by someone who actually reads the contracts. You have wanted justice your whole life, Freya Sterling. I am offering you the *infrastructure* of it."

"And the cost?"

"Ah." Something moved across the unfinished face that might have been respect. "You asked. Most don't. The cost is that you stop being a visitor. Sit at my table and you belong here, wholly, permanently, and the world above will forget you were ever in it. Your mother's grave will remember no daughter. Your ink will dry in every pot in the valley. You will be a story told wrong, and then not told at all."

Freya thought of the scriptorium. Of the cold, of the invisible life so carefully built, of the gap in the debt roll where her name should have been. She had spent years learning how to be unwritten. The king was offering to make it official.

That was the horror of it. Not that he threatened her. That he *understood* her.

"She's not taking the chair," Ash said.

The king's attention slid to him like a searchlight. "The ward-smith. The brother. You have been very quiet, Ash of the border. Shall I tell the court why you really came? Shall I tell them what's in the lantern, and whose heartbeat that is, and what you did—or rather, what you finally *stopped doing*—to—"

"Don't." Ash's voice came out ragged. "Not here. Not like this."

"Everything here is like this," the king said gently. "That is what a court *is*."

Freya looked at the lantern. At the silver-white pulse. Quick-quick-slow. She thought of chapter seven of the collector's code, the clause she'd read by drowned-candlelight while Ash slept: *a bargain binds only what it names.*

And the king, heir to every contract beneath the mountain, keeper of every debt, had offered her a seat, had told her his price, had explained his own inheritance in careful detail—

—and had never once, in all of it, said his own name.

The ledger burned against her ribs like a second heart.

"I'll consider your offer," Freya said, and smiled the way the apple-girl smiled, a half-second too late, and watched the unfinished face try to work out whether it had just been lied to.

Behind the king's chair, in the dark above the drowned altar, one of the chained dragon-fires turned its head and looked directly at her—as if it, too, had heard the flaw in his contracts, and had been waiting a very long time for someone else to hear it.
