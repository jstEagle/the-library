## 7. What the King Bought

Ash had lied to better people than Freya Sterling, and he had never once enjoyed it.

He told himself that on the third day of the descent, as the flooded throat of the mountain narrowed around them and the water turned from brown to black to a green that glowed without any light he could name. He told himself it again when she asked him, casually, over a meal of river-bread traded for a riddle, what his sister's name had been.

*Had been.* He'd said *had been*, and she hadn't caught it. Or she had, and was waiting.

That was the thing about scribes. They read everything. They just didn't always say what they'd read until it could be used against you.

The lantern came into view at dusk—or what passed for dusk down here, the water dimming like a held breath—and Ash stopped walking so fast that Freya nearly collided with his back.

"There," she said.

"I see it."

"You've seen it before."

It wasn't a question. It was worse than a question. It was the flat, patient voice of someone who had spent her life copying other people's words and knew exactly how lies were constructed—where they wobbled, where they reached for too much detail, where they went quiet.

Ash turned around. The lantern hung above a shelf of drowned stone, bone-white glass, and inside it something moved the way a candle flame moves when it is trying very hard to stay lit. A soul. Small. Stubborn. Dimming.

"That's not dragon-fire," Freya said softly. "Dragon-fire is chained in the court below. This is—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at it, watched her face do what faces did when a ledger refused to balance. "This is a person."

"Yes."

"Whose person?"

And there it was. The whole descent had been walking toward this sentence, and now it stood in front of him with its hand out, and every lie he'd ever told well deserted him at once.

"Wren," he said. "Her name was Wren. Is Wren." His voice came out like something dragged over gravel. "My sister."

* * *

She didn't shout. That was the worst part. He'd braced for shouting, for the kind of anger that burns out fast and leaves room for forgiveness. Instead Freya set down her pack with the careful precision of a woman shelving a book she intended to return to, and sat on a wet stone ledge, and looked at him.

"Tell me all of it," she said. "From the beginning. And Ash—" her voice didn't rise at all, "—if you leave anything out, I will know. I've spent eleven years reading forged ledgers without knowing I was reading them. I know what omission looks like. It looks like a gap where a number should be."

So he told her.

He told her about Wren, who was born second and laughed first, who could mend a ward-line by humming to it because she heard the frequencies the way other people heard music. He told her about the year the valley's tithe came due—the tithe nobody talked about, the one the temple collected in names instead of coin, one soul from each generation of ward-smiths' families, sold down-mountain to keep the border wards burning. The high canon called it *the Covenant*. The ward-smiths called it what it was.

"It was supposed to be my debt," Ash said. "Firstborn. That's the order of it. But I was already bound to the wards—the maintenance oaths, the smith's bond. You can't sell a soul twice; even the Undercourt honors that. So the canon came to our house and he smiled at my mother and he said, *the younger child has a purer resonance.*" Ash's hands had closed into fists somewhere during the telling; he made himself open them. "Wren was fourteen. She volunteered. She said the wards kept everyone safe and she wanted to matter that much. Fourteen-year-olds say things like that. We let her."

Freya said nothing. The lantern-light made her face unreadable, which he suspected was deliberate.

"The price of the sale wasn't just her," he went on. "The Undercourt required a witness bound to silence. A keeper of the lie. Me. My exile was written into the contract—if I ever spoke the terms aloud, if I ever stopped maintaining the wards that burned on her fuel, the bargain would begin to unravel. So I left. I walked out of the valley with everyone believing I'd been disgraced for shoddy work, and I stayed away, and once a season I came back through the dead passes and did the maintenance in secret. Fed the wards. Kept the lie fed."

"And then?" Freya said. Quiet. Terrible. "Then what changed?"

"Nothing changed." He made himself hold her eyes. "I changed. Three years ago I stood at the border with my tools in my hands and I thought: *this is theft. Every spark of this fire is stolen from my sister, and I am the one keeping the stove lit.* So I stopped. I let the maintenance lapse. I told myself the wards would fail slowly, that the temple would be forced to renegotiate the Covenant, that exposure would free her." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "I forgot that the soul itself was dying. I forgot that the stars weren't temple property—they were hers. Her light, leaking up through the wards. When the wards failed, the stars didn't go out as punishment. They went out because she's going out."

Silence filled the flooded gallery like water filling lungs.

Freya stood. She picked up her pack again, unhurried, and when she finally spoke her voice was level in a way that frightened him more than screaming would have.

"You knew. In the scriptorium, when I found the gap in the debt rolls—you knew what the gap meant. On the road, when you told me the wards ran on a sold soul—you knew whose. You let me walk into the mountain carrying your guilt like it was mine to carry too."

"Yes," Ash said. "Because if I told you, you might have done the arithmetic and decided she wasn't worth dying for. A stranger's sister. One soul among thousands the valley owes. I couldn't risk your honesty, Freya. It's the most dangerous thing about you."

"Don't," she said, "flatter me right now."

"I'm not flattering you. I'm confessing." He stepped toward her and she stepped back, and the distance between them was suddenly the width of the whole descent behind them. "Every word since the shrine has been true except the shape of why. I need you to understand—I didn't conceal her to use you. I concealed her because saying her name out loud, under the sky, would have unraveled the contract and killed her faster. Down here, past the reach of the original terms—" he gestured at the glowing dark, "—it doesn't matter anymore. The contract can't hear us. That's the only reason you're hearing it now."

"That's convenient."

"It's true. There's a difference, and you of all people can check my handwriting."

Something flickered across her face—not forgiveness, not yet, but the scribe's instinct, the itch to verify. He watched her weigh his words the way she weighed entries in a ledger, hunting for the gap, the forged stroke, the number that didn't carry.

"You should have trusted me," she said finally.

"Yes."

"You should have told me before the shrine, before I paid a memory of my own mother to fund this expedition, so I could have decided what I was paying for."

"Yes."

"You let me go into debt blind, Ash. You of all people know what that makes you." Her voice cracked, just once, on the last word. "It makes you the canon. It makes you a man who writes other people's obligations in a hand they can't read."

The comparison landed like a blade between ribs, and he took it, because he'd earned it. "I know," he said. "I've known for three years. That's why I'm here instead of running. The ledger can rewrite bargains. I want it to rewrite mine—my silence-clause, my exile, all of it. I want to stand in front of the king and say my sister's name at full volume and dare him to enforce anything."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Then she unslung the collector's ledger from her pack and held it against her chest, not offering it, just holding it, the way a person holds the last thing they own.

"When we get to the court," she said, "you tell the king yourself. Not me. Your confession, your voice, your name attached to it. If we're doing this, we do it honest from here on. Both of us. Because I've been thinking about how to beat him, Ash, and I think honesty isn't just the price of admission down here." A grim light entered her eyes that he'd last seen in the scriptorium, the night she stole the roll. "I think it's the weapon."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean I've been reading his contracts since we passed the second gate. Copies, fragments, carved into the walls like he wants them admired." She tapped the ledger's cover. "And every single one of them has the same flaw. Do you want to know what it is?"

Above them, the lantern flared—a sudden, desperate pulse, like a heart remembering fear—and far off through the flooded galleries came a sound like vast doors opening, and a voice like deep water speaking a word that was almost a summons, and almost their names.

"He never named himself," Ash said.

Freya smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "Get ready to introduce him to himself."
