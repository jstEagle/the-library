## 8. Bargaining with the Drowned

The lantern of bone sat on the table between us, and inside it the soul turned like a moth that had forgotten it could burn.

I had not touched it. I had learned, in one day beneath the mountain, that everything in the Undercourt was a bargain waiting to be made, and touch was consent. The courtiers drifted along the drowned cathedral's edges in their stolen faces—my mother's face on one of them, young and smiling, and I kept my eyes on the ledger instead because if I looked too long I would do something stupid and permanent.

Ash stood at my shoulder, saying nothing. He had said very little since I learned what he'd hidden from me, and I hadn't decided yet whether his silence was shame or strategy. Probably both. Ward-smiths were built out of both.

The drowned king poured wine into cups that had no bottoms and watched me with the patience of water.

"You keep looking at the ledger," he said. "As if ink were a weapon."

"Ink is a weapon," I said. "I've been a scribe for eleven years. Everything you own was written down by someone like me first."

Something moved behind his face—not anger. Interest. It was worse.

"Then write," he said, and gestured at the table, at the collector's ledger lying open between us, its pages drinking the light of the chained dragon-fire. "Outbid me. That is the law of my court, and I am nothing if not lawful."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about bargaining with the drowned: the table is honest.

That was the horror of it. The Undercourt's contracts were cleaner than anything the temple ever produced—no forged hands, no hidden clauses, no gaps where names should be. The king's law was absolute because it was simple: a bargain freely offered, freely accepted, paid in full. The temple hid its thefts. The mountain displayed its prices in letters of cold fire, and expected you to read them.

I read them.

For an hour I read, while Ash paced the flooded nave and the courtiers whispered in voices they'd bought from other throats. The soul in the bone lantern belonged to a girl named Wren Ashfall, sold thirty years ago for the price of her brother's exile commuted to mere disgrace. The wards above drank her slowly, decade by decade, and the stars had gone out because there was almost nothing left to burn.

The king's claim was written in the old formula:

*Let it be known that the soul called Wren is held in surety against debts outstanding, redeemable by any party offering consideration of equal or greater weight.*

Equal or greater weight. There it was. The door.

"Ash," I said. "What did your sister weigh?"

He stopped pacing. "What?"

"Not flesh. In their terms. What was she worth when she was sold? What did the contract say she cost?"

He came to the table slowly, and for the first time since the rupture between us he looked at me directly, and his eyes were wet and furious and grateful all at once, which is a terrible combination to be looked at with.

"Her name," he said. "Wren Ashfall. Full true name, spoken willing, sealed in fire. That was the whole price. My freedom was thrown in because the collector that year was generous." His mouth twisted. "Generous."

So. A true name, willingly surrendered. That was the going rate for a soul.

And the only currency I had left—the thing the ledger had been circling since the drowned shrine, patient as debt itself—was mine.

*Freya Sterling.* Not the name on the temple rolls; that was a handle, a hook to hang tithes on. The true name was the self underneath, the one that survived every rewriting, the one my mother had breathed into me before she taught me letters. I had felt it stir when I paid the memory at the shrine—one memory gone, and the name had flared like a struck match, as if to say *you still have this, this is the last of you, spend it carefully.*

I dipped the pen.

* * *

Drafting a bargain is a kind of prayer, if prayers were knives.

I wrote slowly. The ledger wanted precision; vague terms dissolved like sugar in floodwater, and the debtor woke up owing something they never agreed to. So I gave it exactness:

*Let it be known that Freya Sterling offers in trade the soul called Wren, held in surety by the king beneath the mountain. Consideration: the true name of Freya Sterling, surrendered willingly, wholly, without reservation or remainder.*

My hand shook on the last word. *Remainder.* Because that was the question, wasn't it—if anything of me would be left over after the ink finished drinking. The canon had forged my debt-bond to hide his theft, and I had spent my whole invisible life being careful, and now I was about to sign away the one thing carefulness existed to protect.

"Read it again," Ash said quietly.

"I've read it four times."

"Read it again. It's what she would have done."

So I read it again. And because I read it again, I saw it.

Not in my contract. In *his*.

The king's surety-clause, glowing in its cold-fire script above the lantern: *redeemable by any party offering consideration of equal or greater weight.* Standard formula. I'd seen it a hundred times in the tithe rolls, in the debt-bonds, in the temple's oldest charters. But formulas have bones, and I had spent eleven years learning to see bones through skin.

Every clause in the Undercourt ran on names. Names given, names held, names forfeit. The river-things demanded riddles of true names. The courtiers wore stolen faces because they had sold their own. The whole architecture of the drowned law was built on identity—*who* owed, *who* was owed.

And nowhere—in the surety-clause, in the sale-contract for Wren, in the charter of the court itself that hung behind the king's throne like a curtain of frozen rain—nowhere did the king's name appear.

He signed himself *the King Beneath the Mountain*. He signed himself *the Drowned*, *the Holder of Sureties*, *He Who Weighs*. Titles, all of them. Offices. Descriptions.

Never once, in three hundred years of contracts, had he written his name.

"Why?" I whispered.

Ash followed my finger along the charter. I watched him read it the way ward-smiths read—looking for the flaw in the pattern—and I saw the moment he found it, because he went very still, the way he'd gone still at the border when the dead wards told him the truth.

"He can't," Ash said. "Or—" His breath caught. "He won't. Freya, look at the date-marks. Every contract renewed. Every century. You don't renew a name. You renew a *title*. If he named himself, the name would be bound like everything else. Subject to the law. Redeemable. Forfeit-able."

"He wrote the law so completely that he forgot to exempt himself from only one part of it," I said. "He never named himself. Which means—"

"Which means the law doesn't know who he *is*," Ash finished. "Only what he does."

The lantern flickered. Inside, the moth-soul beat against the bone, and for a moment I would have sworn it was laughing.

I looked down at my draft. At the clause I had nearly signed. *Surrendered willingly, wholly, without reservation or remainder.* If I gave him my name, he would hold it the way he held Wren—a lamp to burn, a face to wear, another courtier drifting along the walls wearing my mother's smile with my voice.

Unless.

Unless the ledger could do what ledgers do. What I had spent my life doing, copying, correcting, redlining the temple's quiet thefts. Unless ink that remembers bargains could remember *one more*—the oldest bargain in the room, the one the king had been avoiding for three hundred years simply by refusing to say who he was.

Bind him to his own law. Make him subject to every clause he ever wrote. And a law that runs on names cannot bind a man who has none—which meant the law itself would demand payment. Would demand *his* name. Would weigh him, and find him wanting, and take.

"You're smiling," Ash said warily. "I don't like it when you smile at contracts."

"I'm a scribe," I told him. "This is the happiest day of my life."

But my hands weren't steady, and we both knew why. Because there was a flaw in my flaw, and it sat in my draft like a stone in a shoe: to invoke the law against him, I would have to bring my bargain to the court's heart—to the throne, to the charter, to the chained dragon-fire where the law lived. And to stand there and speak the invocation, I would need a name of my own to stand on.

If I surrendered it first, I would be nobody, and nobody has no standing. If I kept it, I couldn't outbid him.

There was a third way. It was written in the order of operations, in the grammar of the thing, and it was so dangerous that the ledger's pages warmed under my fingers as if warning me off. Speak the binding *first*. Bind the king to his law while my name was still mine. Then let the law itself collect my bid—let the machinery of the drowned court seize my name as consideration, automatically, in the same instant the king's emptiness came due.

One sentence. One breath. Two forfeitures at once, his and mine, weighed together on scales older than the mountain.

And if I got the wording wrong by even a syllable, I would lose my name for nothing, and Wren would burn in her bone lantern until there was nothing left, and the valley above would stay starless forever.

The king was watching me across the bottomless cups. He had watched scribes before, I realized. He had watched them falter, and sign, and drift to join his courtiers. Three hundred years of watching people read his perfect law and find it perfect.

"Take whatever time you need," he said kindly.

That kindness decided me.

I turned to a fresh page. I crossed out my draft—all of it, the surrender, the bid, the neat little suicide of a contract—and began again, slower, each word set like a stone in a wall:

*Let it be known that the law of the Undercourt binds all parties who bargain within it. Let it be known that the party styling himself the King Beneath the Mountain has bargained within it, and continues to bargain, and is therefore bound. Let his unnamed state be weighed as debt outstanding. Let consideration be collected in full.*

Then, beneath it, smaller, the part that terrified me most, because it was the part that required me to mean it:

*And let the true name of Freya Sterling stand as her stake in this weighing, tendered freely, collected by the law itself, that both accounts may close together.*

Ash read it over my shoulder. He was silent a long time.

"If this works," he said finally, "you come out of it nameless. Nothing left over. No remainder."

"I know."

"And if it doesn't work—"

"Then I'll be a courtier with my mother's smile, and you'll know exactly which face to avoid."

He laughed—a broken, startled sound—and then he pressed his forehead briefly against the back of my head, the way you greet family, and stepped back.

At the far end of the cathedral, the king rose from his table, and the chained dragon-fire lifted its head, and every contract in the drowned court began, very softly, to glow.

"It seems," the king said, "we are ready to settle accounts."

I picked up the ledger, and the pen, and my whole remaining self, and walked toward the fire.
