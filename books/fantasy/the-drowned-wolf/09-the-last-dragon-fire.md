## 9. The Last Dragon-Fire

The court's heart was a bell.

That was the first thing Freya understood when the king's courtiers parted to let them through—no throne, no dais, nothing so honest as a chair. At the center of the drowned cathedral hung a great bronze bell, green with age, its clapper wrapped in nine chains that ran up into the dark like roots in reverse. Inside it, behind iron ribs, something burned. The light came through the gaps in stuttering pulses, and each pulse made the water shiver and the courtiers flinch, all their stolen faces flickering for half a heartbeat into the faces of the people they had been.

The lantern of bone hung beside it on a hook of black coral, swaying gently though there was no current. Through its walls Freya could see the soul: a small bright knot, dimming. Dying. Every gutter of its light was another star going out somewhere above them, in a sky she would never see again if she failed.

*You feel it,* the drowned king said. He did not speak aloud; his voice arrived the way ink arrives on paper, blooming behind her eyes. *It is nearly spent. I have kept it fourteen years past its term. Sentiment, you might call it.*

"You call it collateral," Freya said.

His laughter moved through the flooded nave like a shoal of fish. He was vast without being tall—a presence folded into the shape of a crowned man, his robes trailing off into silt and shadow, his face the only part of him that stayed still. It was a handsome face. That was the worst thing about it. It was the kind of face that appeared on coins.

*And what will you give me for it, little scribe? You come to my court with a dead man's ledger and no name worth weighing. Ashren of the Ninth Ward sold his sister's debt to buy his own exile; he has nothing left but guilt, and guilt is common as gravel here.* The still face turned to her. *But you. You walked past my river-things and lied to them with your whole mouth, and something in you broke open, and now—*

"Now I have a ledger," Freya said. "And ledgers don't weigh names. They read them."

She opened the debt-collector's book.

The price had been named at the shrine, and she had paid it once already—a memory of her mother, gone as cleanly as a page cut from a binding. She knew her mother had existed. She knew the shape of a kitchen and a voice humming while ink dried. She could not find the face anymore. It was like reaching for a stair in the dark and finding the staircase removed.

The ledger did not ask for more memory. It asked for the thing beneath all memory, the thread every memory hung from.

*Her true name,* the king said, and his voice went soft and covetous, the way men's voices go over gold. *Surrendered willingly. Written in her own hand. Do you know what I could do with a name like yours, Freya Sterling? A name that survived a forged bond? A name the temple itself could not erase?*

"I know what you do with names," Freya said. "You wear them."

She uncapped the inkhorn at her belt. Ash stood at her shoulder, and she felt him trembling—not with fear, she realized, but with the effort of not speaking. Of letting her do this. His sister's soul burned in the bone lantern, guttering, and he had carried fourteen years of silence to this room, and he was keeping one more silence now because she had asked him to.

*Then write,* the king said. *Write your name into my book, and take the lantern, and go up into your valley of sheep and prayers. I am generous. Ask any soul I own.*

"Generous," Freya repeated. She dipped the pen. "Here's what I learned copying tithes for eleven years, your majesty. Nobody reads the contracts they sign. They read the first line and the last line and they trust the middle."

*The middle is mine.*

"The middle is where you never wrote yourself."

The stillness that followed was absolute. Even the bell seemed to hold its fire.

Freya turned the ledger toward him—not the blank page, but the pages before it, the centuries of bargains, and she watched the stolen faces of the courtiers turn toward it too, hungry, remembering. "Every contract in this book has two parties. Buyer and seller. Debtor and collector. Fourteen years ago, a high canon of the temple above sold a soul—you bought it. But look." She set her finger on the line. "He signed. The soul signed. The witnesses signed. And the buyer—" She looked up into the coin-bright face. "The buyer's line is blank. You never gave this book your name."

*I do not give,* the king said. *I take.*

"No," Freya said. "You trade. That's the law down here, isn't it? Your own law—the one you built this court on. Equal value, freely given, truly named. Every bargain bound by it. Including—" and here her hand shook, and she let it shake, because the ledger drank honesty the way it drank blood, "—including the bargain you're making right now. With me. You offered me the lantern for my true name. Freely offered. Which means you've entered a bargain, your majesty. And a bargain needs both parties named."

*You cannot bind me with my own rules.*

"You already did," Freya said. "Fourteen years ago. The moment you took a soul without signing for it, you became the debtor in your own court. I'm just the collector who finally found the account."

She began to write.

Not her name. Not yet. She wrote his title instead, and the ledger groaned like a ship changing course: *The buyer in the fourteenth-year contract, being unnamed, is hereby bound as debtor to every term of every bargain recorded herein, and his estate—being his self, his crown, his court, and whatever soul remains beneath the silt—is forfeit to the holder of this ledger, in settlement of all accounts outstanding.*

The water went cold enough to burn.

*Stop,* the king said, and for the first time his voice was only a voice, thin and echoing, a man's voice. *Scribe. Scribe, listen. I have worn ten thousand faces. I can wear your mother's. I can give her back to you—I keep everything, nothing is lost down here, I can—*

Freya thought of a kitchen. A hummed tune. A warmth where a face should be, and nothing inside it.

"That's how you get people," she said quietly. "That's always how you get them."

She wrote the last clause. *In exchange, the holder surrenders her true name, willingly, in her own hand, per the terms offered.*

And then, because she was a scribe, because she had spent her life learning that a document is only as strong as its weakest mercy, she added one line more: *The soul held in the lantern of bone is released entire, unharmed, and unowned, together with every name in this court that was taken rather than given.*

She signed.

She did not write *Freya Sterling*. She reached for the name the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and found the space where it had been—clean, swept, empty as a gap in a debt roll. The ledger took the emptiness itself. Ink spread from the signature line outward through the whole book, page after page after page, every contract in the Undercourt lighting up with fresh black script, rewritten, re-bound, re-owned.

The king screamed.

It was not a kingly sound. It was the sound of a ledger closing.

The chains around the bell snapped one by one—not breaking, Freya saw, but *unwinding*, each link unmaking itself as the bargains that held it were voided. The bronze swung free. The fire inside it stood up.

* * *

Dragon-fire does not roar so much as *arrive*. One moment the drowned cathedral was lit in drowning pulses; the next, every inch of water in the place turned to steam-shadow and the flame stood in the air like a pillar deciding to be a dragon—vast, coiling, old beyond the mountain, older than the court that had chained it. It turned its head, which was not a head, toward the king.

The king was coming apart. His stolen faces were leaving him—rising off his body like embers, thousands of them, streaming upward through the broken vault toward the sky. Each face went home. Freya saw a farmer's grin, a child's scowl, an old woman's laugh, peeling away into the dark, and the thing beneath them was smaller than she had imagined. Smaller and grayer. Almost pitiable.

Almost.

*This changes nothing,* the remnant said, in a voice like wet ash. *Your stars are still dead. Your wards are still—*

The pillar of fire touched the bell, and the bell rang.

Every star above the valley reignited at once. Freya knew it the way you know a door has opened in another room—not by seeing, but by the change in the air. The pressure of the dark lifted. Somewhere far above, through a mile of stone and flood, dawn-colored light began to fall.

The lantern of bone cracked down its seam.

Ash made a sound Freya had never heard from him—not words, just a single raw note, fourteen years long. The soul rose out of the broken lantern like a spark off a struck flint, and it did not gutter. It brightened. It circled the chamber once, twice, and then it came to rest in front of Ash and shaped itself, shyly, into the outline of a young woman—translucent, unfinished, but *hers*, unmistakably hers, wearing the same stubborn jaw.

"Ash?" the soul said. Her voice was small and rusted with disuse. "You look terrible."

Ash sat down in the flood-water like a puppet with cut strings, and laughed, and wept, and did not seem to know he was doing either.

Freya stood very still among the departing faces and felt the shape of herself.

It was a strange feeling. She was not frightened, exactly. She had expected terror—had braced for it, the way you brace for a blow—but what she found instead was a kind of enormous quiet, like the scriptorium at night, like fresh paper. She tried to remember her name and got the gap again, the clean swept space, and understood that it was not a wound. It was a margin. Blank. Waiting to be written in.

The dragon-fire lowered its not-head toward her. Heat that did not burn washed over her, and within it she felt attention—curious, ancient, faintly amused.

"Well, little scribe," it said, in a voice like a forge opening. "You hold the ledger now. And the ledger holds the court. And the court holds the wards, and the wards hold the valley." Smoke curled from what might have been nostrils. "They will need someone up there who knows what things cost. Someone who has read the fine print of her own life and lived."

"I don't have a name," Freya said. Her voice sounded strange to her—lighter, unburdened, unanchored. "You'd be taking on a blank page."

"Best kind," the fire said. "Names written by other people are always crooked."

Behind her, Ash was on his feet, his sister's ghost-shape holding his hand, watching her with an expression she was beginning to learn meant *your move*.

Freya looked down at the ledger in her hands. Every page glowed softly now, settled, at rest—the whole drowned economy of souls balanced at last. One page remained blank. Hers.

She thought about the girl who had copied tithes in a cold scriptorium, invisible, careful, surviving. She had liked that girl. She would not resurrect her.

Freya picked up the pen. Her hand did not shake—or it shook, but she wrote anyway, which she was beginning to understand was the same thing.

She spoke it aloud first, because some things must be witnessed to be true:

"My name is Keeper."

The ledger took the word. Ink bloomed across the blank page, and the blank page became a record, and the record became a life, and somewhere overhead a star that had no business existing flared briefly brighter than all the others, as if applauding.

The dragon-fire laughed—a sound like mountains settling—and began, slowly, to pour itself back into the bell. Not as a prisoner this time. As a garrison.

"There's a valley up there," it said, folding itself away, its voice going distant and warm. "Full of people who think wards run on prayer. They're about to meet their new keeper. Try to be gentle."

"I make no promises," said Keeper.

Above them, through the broken roof of the drowned cathedral, the first true starlight in fourteen years came falling down the mountain's flooded throat like rain.

And far above, at the border where magic ended, the ward-stones began—very softly—to sing.
