## 3. The Word That Should Not Exist

The fever broke on the third night, and Iri woke to a ceiling of dark beams and the smell of woodsmoke and dried herbs.

For a long moment they did not move. They lay very still beneath a quilt that smelled of lavender and old wool, listening to the sounds of the house — the tick and settle of cooling timber, wind pressing at the shutters, and somewhere below, the low murmur of a voice reading aloud. Not singing. Reading. The cadence was slow and deliberate, like someone sounding out words they did not quite trust.

*Run,* said the memory, unbidden. *Run now, little ember, run and do not look back.*

Iri squeezed their eyes shut against it. The woman's face was gone — burned away like everything else — but her voice remained, lodged behind their breastbone where the warm thing lived. The warm thing pulsed when she spoke in memory, a slow golden throb, as if it recognized her too.

*Do not look back.*

They had looked back. That was the last thing they remembered clearly: looking back over their shoulder at the library burning, at shelves taller than trees collapsing in fountains of sparks, at pages rising into the smoke like pale birds, thousands upon thousands of them, all the words in the world going up at once. The heat had kissed their cheeks. Someone had screamed — or maybe that had been them. And then snow, and cold, and a road with no end, and walking, always walking, until the door with the carved glyphs had opened and a tall woman with tired eyes and a sword callus on her palm had caught them before they fell.

Coral. The mercenary's name was Coral. Iri knew that much now, though not from speech — the woman barely spoke to them, and when she did it was in that clipped valley tongue Iri could not untangle. But she had said her own name once, slowly, pointing at herself, the way you offer bread to something you suspect might bite. *Co-ral.* She had given them water and broth and a place by the fire, and she had not once smiled, and she had not once turned them out either.

Iri opened their eyes again and studied the room. Small. Clean in a severe way, everything squared to the walls. A rack for the sword near the door. Shelves holding perhaps thirty books, each one spine-out and dusted, arranged by some private order Iri could not guess. On the wall above the bed, faintly glowing in the dark like banked coals, were the marks — the same marks that spiraled up the woman's forearm, that circled the doorframe, that were inked into the lintel in faded paint. Glyphs. Oath-marks, maybe. When Iri had touched them, half-delirious, they had flared like sunrise, and the woman had made a sound Iri would remember longer than any word: small, cracked, involuntary, the sound of a locked thing opening one inch.

*You are a burden,* said the cold voice, the one that walked beside them on the road. *You are a fever in a stranger's house. You eat her food. You burn her marks. She owes you nothing.*

*But she caught me,* answered the other voice, smaller, stubborn, the one that had kept them walking through the dead season. *She caught me before I fell.*

Below, the reading voice stopped. Floorboards creaked. Iri pretended to sleep.

* * *

In the morning, Coral brought porridge and set it on the bedside stool without ceremony, then stood there a moment, arms crossed, looking at Iri the way she looked at everything: as if assessing threat. Then she did something new. She crossed to the shelf, took down a slim book whose cover was stained brown with age, and laid it open on the quilt between them.

On the page was writing — but not the valley tongue. It was dense, slanted, crowded into margins, annotated and re-annotated in three different inks. And scattered through it, drawn carefully in a fourth hand, were symbols. Symbols Iri knew.

Their breath caught. Their finger moved before they could stop it, hovering over one — a spiral broken by a single vertical stroke.

"That one," Coral said, in her clipped tongue, and tapped her own forearm where the glyph sat inked. Then she pointed at the book, then at the page, then made a gesture like speaking — two fingers fluttering from her lips — and pointed at Iri.

Understanding arrived slowly, like light through deep water. *This book explains the marks. This book is about your words.* Or perhaps: *Help me read what you say.*

Iri sat up. The porridge went forgotten. They touched the symbol — the broken spiral — and felt the warm thing in their chest lean toward it like a plant toward sun, and heard themselves say, without deciding to:

"Ashvale."

The name came out whole and round and ringing, in the First Tongue, the only language Iri had left, and the moment it left their mouth the glyph on Coral's forearm blazed through her sleeve — a line of gold fire visible even through wool — and every mark on the walls lit at once, and the fire in the hearth stood up straight and tall as if called to attention.

Coral went very still. In the sudden light her face was stripped of its armor, and what lay underneath was not fear exactly. It was hunger. Twenty years of hunger, Iri thought, looking at her. Twenty years of a dog hearing nothing where the bell used to be.

Then the light died, and the stillness closed over her face like water, and she was the mercenary again. But her hand, when she reached for the book, was not steady.

* * *

They built the language together across three days, in fragments, the way you build a bridge across a gorge by throwing stones and hoping.

Coral would point at a thing — the table, the fire, the snow beyond the window — and say its name in the valley tongue. Iri would answer in the First Tongue. Coral would search her grandmother's book, running her finger down columns of cramped notation, comparing, crossing out, muttering. Sometimes she found the match: a phonetic rendering, a note in the margin in a different hand — *Grandmother's*, Iri decided; this must be Grandmother's book — sometimes an entire grammar scratched out in the last pages, abandoned mid-sentence, as if the writer had died mid-thought or mid-hope.

*Elin Vael,* said the flyleaf, in the valley script. And beneath it, in the First Tongue's alphabet, which Iri could read even if they could no longer speak much else: *For whoever comes after. The words are not dead. Only sleeping. Wake them gently.*

Iri traced those words four times. *Wake them gently.* Whoever Elin Vael had been, she had loved this language enough to spend a lifetime cataloguing it in secret, in a valley where magic died at the border and the god's name was never spoken. Had she been punished for it? Had she hidden the book? Had she died waiting for someone to come who could use it?

*I am the someone,* Iri thought, and the thought was warm and terrible at once. *I am the someone she waited for, and I cannot even tell her granddaughter thank you.*

That afternoon, working through the word for *snow*, Iri managed their first full sentence in translation. They pointed at themselves, then at the window, then made the gesture they had used since the first night — hands cupped, then spreading wide, then a shape like wings folding inward, falling.

Coral frowned. Consulted the book. Her lips moved through the notation. When she looked up, her eyes had changed.

"Burning," she said, in her tongue, and then, checking the grammar notes, laboriously, like a woman defusing something: "Library. Falling. You... fell... from a burning library?"

Iri nodded, and the memory rose so hard and fast it took their breath — the heat, the sparks, the pages like birds — and the warm thing in their chest flared in sympathy, and one of the candle flames in the room stretched tall and blue. Coral watched it happen. Watched the flame bend toward Iri like a listener leaning in.

"You're the messenger," she said quietly, in her own tongue, and Iri didn't understand the words but understood the weight of them, the way they settled on the room. She looked at Iri the way people in stories look at things sent by gods: with awe, and with the beginning of fear.

*I don't want to be a message,* Iri wanted to tell her. *I want to be a child. I want someone to ask if I'm warm enough instead of what I am.* But the sentence collapsed in their chest, untranslatable, and they ate their porridge instead, and the candle flame slowly remembered its shape.

* * *

On the fifth night, the fever tried to come back, and with it came the dream entire.

Not fragments this time. The whole of it, laid out like a table set for a meal Iri had been starving for without knowing.

The library again — but standing, whole, alive. Shelves climbing into darkness. Lamps of colored glass. The smell of vellum and lamp-oil and, beneath it, something mineral and ancient, like the inside of a mountain. Other children, laughing somewhere below. A woman with grey in her braid kneeling to Iri's height, her hands warm on Iri's shoulders, saying words Iri could suddenly, horribly, understand again because the dream gave them back their fluency:

*"Listen to me. The Court has found us. There is so little time. You carry the last of it, little ember — do you hear me? The last ember of Ashvale. They will come for you with soft voices and grey cloaks, and you must not go with them, you must run, run north, past the border, past the valley where magic cannot follow — they will never think to look where nothing lives—"*

"But I can't speak it," Iri had told her, in the dream, in perfect First Tongue. "If I speak the name, they'll hear it. If I speak at all—"

"Then be silent," she said, and pressed something into Iri's hands — a book, small, wrapped in oilcloth. "Be silent, and live, and give it to the faithful. Give it to anyone. The words are not dead. Only sleeping." Her thumb brushed Iri's cheekbone, leaving a streak of soot. "Wake them gently."

And then the doors came down — not broken, *melted*, iron running like wax — and the grey cloaks came through the smoke, and the woman turned to face them with her empty hands raised, and Iri ran, clutching the book, down stairs that shook, past windows full of orange light, out into snow—

Iri woke screaming.

They woke screaming and the room was full of light — not lamplight but *them*-light, the warm thing blazing through their skin so that their own hands glowed like paper held before a flame, and every glyph in the house was awake and singing, and downstairs something crashed — Coral, bolting upright, taking the stairs three at a time with her sword already drawn.

She burst in and stopped dead.

Iri was sitting up in a corona of gold, sobbing, and the air itself was thick with light, and Coral stood in the doorway with a naked blade and did not advance and did not retreat, and for one endless moment neither of them moved.

Then Coral did the last thing Iri expected. She sheathed the sword, crossed the room through the light as if it were rain, and sat down on the edge of the bed. She didn't touch them. She just sat, solid and present, a wall of a woman between Iri and the door.

"Bad dream," she said, in her tongue. Pointless. Useless. The kindest possible thing.

Iri's sobs slowed. The glow guttered, steadied, dimmed to embers. And in the quiet that followed, Iri did the thing they had been afraid to do since the first night: they spoke. Deliberately. In the First Tongue. Slowly, watching Coral's face, watching her follow along in her grandmother's book, which she had snatched up on her way through the door and now held open one-handed like a shield.

"*Ashvale*," Iri said. "*Ashvale ashketh. Ashvale iri-keth. Mi Ashvale.*"

Coral's finger tracked the notation. Her lips shaped the syllables. And Iri watched her arrive, word by translated word, at the meaning — watched her translate it in the margin with a shaking pencil: *Ashvale speaks. Ashvale gives. I am Ashvale's.*

Or possibly — the grammar was ambiguous, the grandmother's notes said so right there in the margin, *the first-person construction may indicate possession OR identity, context required* —

*I belong to Ashvale. Or: I am Ashvale.*

Coral looked up from the book. Looked at Iri. At the fading glow under their skin. At the marks on the walls, still faintly breathing light.

"The name," she whispered, in her own tongue, and Iri caught it now, had learned to catch it: *name*. She pointed at Iri's chest, at the warm thing. "*Ashvale*. Spoken. Aloud. Here." Her hand trembled as she wrote one more line in the margin, pressing so hard the pencil tore the paper:

*It should be impossible. No living throat can say it. The Court made sure of that. So how does a child—*

She stopped writing. Iri watched her stare at the unfinished sentence, and watched something move behind her eyes — the same look from the morning of the porridge, hunger and fear braided together, but deeper now, cracked wider.

Outside, far off across the snowfields, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then hooves — many hooves, muffled by snow, moving along the valley road toward the village.

Coral closed the book. Snuffed the candle with her fingers. And in the sudden dark, her hand found Iri's shoulder and gripped it — not gently, not cruelly, but like someone holding onto a rope over a drop.

"No more talking tonight," she breathed, in the valley tongue, and pulled Iri down beneath the quilt, and lay fully clothed on top of the blankets beside them, one hand on her sword hilt, listening to the hooves.

Iri lay in the dark with the warm thing pulsing slow and scared against their ribs, and understood, without translation, that the world outside had finally found them.

*Run,* said the woman's voice, in memory. *Run and do not look back.*

But the hooves were stopping. Right outside.
