## 4. Ashes on the Wind

The snow came down like ash that first morning of the dead season, and Coral Okonkwo stood at her window watching it bury the world she had spent her whole life keeping in order.

She had not slept. Iri had not either — the child had finally stopped shivering sometime past midnight, curled beneath every blanket Coral owned with the ember-light still faintly visible through the thin skin of their chest, a glow like a coal wrapped in cloth. Coral had sat up all night with her grandmother's notes spread across the table, cross-referencing symbols against glyphs she half-remembered from shrines, and by the time the sky went grey-white she had confirmed what she already knew in her bones: the word *Ashvale* was written there, in Iri's careful hand, transcribed phonetically from a language no living kingdom spoke.

A name spoken aloud should have been impossible. That was the first law of the valley, older than any contract: the god was silent, and silence was a kind of safety.

She heard the hoofbeats before she saw them.

* * *

There were six of them, riding out of the white blur of the north road, and they were wrong in a way Coral could not immediately name. She had spent twenty years reading riders — bandits, tax-men, rival mercenaries, desperate fathers chasing runaway daughters — and these did not move like any of them. They rode too evenly, spaced too precisely, their grey cloaks unmarked by any sigil, their horses too fine for soldiers and too calm for couriers. They came through the falling snow as if the snow were a formality they had agreed to tolerate.

Coral counted them twice, noted the way the lead rider sat — loose in the saddle, easy, the posture of someone who had never once needed to brace for an attack — and then went to wake Iri.

"Listen to me." She crouched beside the cot, and the child's eyes opened at once, dark and fever-bright and far too old for the face around them. "People are coming. Riders. If anyone asks, you are my sister's child, visiting from Harrow's Bend. Your name is Sela. You've been sick, which is true enough, so stay under the blankets and don't speak unless you must."

Iri blinked slowly. Then, in the trade-tongue they were only beginning to learn, in a voice roughened by fever: "You are lying."

"Yes."

"You do not lie." The child said it without accusation, the way one might note that water does not flow uphill. "Your hands shake when you try."

Coral looked down at her hands. They were steady. They were always steady; steadiness was the first thing the mercenary trade taught you and the last thing it let you keep. But something behind her sternum had begun to tremble, a fine vibration like a plucked string, and she wondered — not for the first time since the night of the knock — whether the oath-glyphs along her forearms could feel what she felt.

"They won't ask me," she said. "They'll ask everyone. Go back under the blankets, Sela."

The ghost of a smile crossed Iri's cracked lips before they burrowed down into the quilts, and Coral pulled the door shut between the main room and the sleeping alcove, and told herself the tightness in her chest was only the cold.

* * *

They knocked the way snow falls — softly, inevitably, everywhere at once. A single knock, courteous, and then the sound of someone waiting with infinite patience, which was worse than pounding ever would have been.

Coral opened the door to a woman in grey.

She was perhaps forty, though it was the kind of face that might have been thirty or fifty depending on the light — smooth where it mattered, lined where it chose to be, handsome in the deliberate way of a portrait rather than a person. Her cloak was undyed wool gone silver at the edges, and beneath it she wore no armor at all, which in this country, in this season, was its own kind of message: *I am not afraid of anything you could do to me.* Her hair was bound back with a plain cord. Her smile arrived a half-second before the rest of her expressions did, like a herald riding ahead of an army.

"Mistress Okonkwo," the woman said. "Or is it Captain? The contracts we've seen say both."

"The ones that matter say Captain." Coral kept her hand away from the axe by the door and her voice level. "You have the advantage of me."

"Serane Voss." A slight bow, economical, graceful. "Of the Cinder Court. We're looking for a lost child."

Behind Serane, the five other riders had dismounted and arranged themselves in the yard with a precision that made Coral's teeth ache. None of them had approached the house. None of them needed to. Two drifted toward the barn, unhurried, and one stood facing the treeline as if listening to something in it.

"A lost child." Coral leaned against the doorframe, letting her weight settle, letting herself look bored. Boredom was armor. She had worn it through a hundred negotiations. "This is a valley of two hundred souls, Mistress Voss, and most of them are related to each other. Children go missing here about as often as dragons do."

"And yet." Serane produced a folded paper from within her cloak — good paper, expensive, the kind that came from the capital's mills — and unfolded it with two fingers. On it was a sketch, done in charcoal by a competent hand: a small face, sharp-chinned, wide-eyed. It was Iri. It was unmistakably Iri, down to the particular stubborn set of the mouth, and Coral felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath her professional calm.

"We believe the child passed through the border posts some days ago," Serane continued, as if discussing weather. "Feverish. Alone. Possibly confused — the child speaks strangely, we're told. An old dialect." Her eyes came up, and they were pale grey, almost colorless, and they held Coral's with the gentle pressure of a hand on a wound. "You keep the shrine records for this district, I understand. And your grandmother kept them before you. If anything crossed your path — a stranger, a rumor, even a bad dream worth writing down — you'd know."

"I'd know," Coral agreed.

It was not yet a lie. She noticed that. She noticed it the way a swordsman notices the exact angle of a blade coming for his throat — with a clarity that seemed to slow the whole morning down. Snow ticked softly against the eaves. Somewhere in the alcove, Iri was breathing, and Coral could hear it, could hear the small wet rattle of the fever, and Serane Voss was smiling at her with the patience of a woman who had asked this question in four hundred houses and knew precisely how many kinds of silence there were.

"The Court pays for information," Serane said. "Well. The Court pays for everything. It's rather our defining feature." She laughed — a real laugh, warm, self-deprecating, the laugh of someone who found her own institution faintly ridiculous, and Coral understood with a cold drop in her stomach that this woman was dangerous in a way no bandit chief or warlord had ever been, because bandits wanted your gold and warlords wanted your land, and this woman wanted to be liked while she took whatever else she'd come for.

"There was nothing," Coral said.

And there it was. The lie. Small, three words, delivered flatly, and it went into her like a splinter of green wood — she felt it lodge beneath the breastbone, angled so it could not be pushed out the way it came in. Twenty years of contracts kept to the letter. Twenty years of oath-glyphs renewed each season at the shrine, ink and salt and the old words: *my word is my blade, my blade is my bond.* She had lied before — everyone in her trade lied, misdirection was half the work — but she had never once lied about something that mattered, never lied with her whole chest while a child breathed behind a thin wall, and the difference between those things turned out to be the difference between a scratch and a wound.

Serane studied her for a long moment. The snow fell. The string behind Coral's sternum vibrated.

"No," Serane said at last, softly, and Coral's heart stopped. "No, I don't imagine there was. Not here." The smile returned, rueful. "You know what's strange, Captain? In every village, someone lies to us. Always. Good people, honest people, people who've never told a falsehood in their lives — they see our cloaks and they lie, because everyone believes the Court takes children." She folded the sketch away with delicate fingers. "And in every village, they're wrong. We've never taken a child in our lives. We rescue them. From themselves, mostly. From what's inside them." Her gaze drifted, just slightly, past Coral's shoulder — toward the house, the walls, the alcove — and then returned, mild as milk. "But you wouldn't know anything about that."

"Nothing here but snow and sick livestock," Coral said, and the splinter twisted.

"Of course." Serane stepped back, and the courtesy of it was somehow more frightening than a threat would have been. "We'll ride on to Harrow's Bend before dark. Oh—" she paused, turning, and the movement was casual, artless, perfect. "If the child does turn up — fevered children wander, it happens — bring them to any post wearing grey. There's a reward, of course. Generous. But more than that, Captain—" and here her voice changed, dropped its charm entirely, and for one unguarded instant Coral saw the thing underneath, the thing the smile was built over, and it was tired, ancient, and afraid. "More than that, you'd be saving lives. More than you know. That's not a threat. It's the truth, and I'm sorry it has to be."

Then she was remounting, and the six riders wheeled north through the falling snow, and the yard was empty except for hoofprints already filling with white.

* * *

Coral stood in the doorway until the cold drove her inside, and then she stood inside until the shaking started.

It began in her hands, which offended her professionally, and moved outward from there — knees, jaw, the long muscles of her back. She braced both palms on the table among her grandmother's scattered notes and watched the papers tremble with her, and she thought: *that is what fear looks like when it finally gets ahold of a lie.*

The alcove curtain rustled. Iri stood there wrapped in a blanket, hair wild, fever-flushed, watching her with those too-old eyes.

"She is gone?" the child asked.

"Gone north. To Harrow's Bend." Coral straightened. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "They'll search every house on the road. They'll be back — maybe not her, maybe others, maybe worse. We can't stay."

Iri considered this with the grave slowness of the very young and the very old. Then: "You lied for me."

"Don't make it a habit of noticing things."

"It burned you." The child crossed the room — unsteady, barefoot on the cold boards, refusing to be carried — and reached up, and before Coral could stop them, pressed one small finger to the inside of her forearm, where the oath-glyphs ran in their faded lines of ink and salt-scar.

The glyphs flared.

Not the dull warmth of the shrine renewals, not the faint ember-glow of that first night — this was light, actual light, gold-white bleeding up through the skin like sunrise through a closed eyelid, and Coral gasped and snatched her arm back and the light followed her, pulsing once, twice, in rhythm with her heartbeat, in rhythm — she realized with a lurch — with the lie still lodged beneath her breastbone. The splinter and the glyph were touching. The broken oath and the burning mark were answering each other like two halves of a struck bell.

"What— " Her voice failed. She tried again. "What is it doing?"

Iri withdrew their hand slowly. The light sank back beneath the skin, leaving the old faded lines looking somehow deeper than before, truer, as if the flare had washed dust off them.

"It says yes," the child whispered. And then, in the First Tongue, a longer phrase, soft and wondering, and though Coral could not translate it she caught the shape of it, the cadence, and one word recurred three times like a bell tolling: *Ashvale. Ashvale. Ashvale.*

The fire in the hearth popped. Outside, the snow went on falling, patient as ash.

Coral looked down at her forearm, at the oath she had renewed faithfully every season of her adult life, and for the first time she asked herself the question that had been circling her like a wolf all night, all week, all twenty years: *what exactly did I swear to, and who exactly wrote the words?*

Her grandmother's notes lay on the table. Among them, dog-eared, was the copy of her own oath-contract she'd kept since her eighteenth year — the original, signed in her mother's presence, witnessed by a shrine-keeper whose face she could no longer recall. She had read it a hundred times. She had memorized it. She knew every clause the way she knew the weight of her own axe.

She picked it up anyway.

And there, in the final paragraph, in language so old-formal it slid past the eye like water off oilcloth, she saw words she had read a thousand times and never once *heard*: *...and this service shall endure unto the god's silence, and end only when the god speaks again.*

The splinter beneath her breastbone turned over in its sleep.

Outside, far to the north, grey riders were asking their gentle questions in Harrow's Bend, and the snow covered the road behind them, and Coral Okonkwo stood in her grandmother's kitchen holding the cage she had lived in all her life, reading, for the first time, the shape of its bars.
