## 5. What the Oath Actually Says

The house was too quiet after the riders left.

Coral had known silence her whole life — the valley's particular brand of it, the hush of a place where the god did not speak and never had, at least not in living memory. She had built herself inside that silence like a mason builds inside stone, fitting each day against the next until the walls were load-bearing. But this silence was different. This was the silence after a lie.

She stood in her kitchen with her back to the door for a long time, listening to the snow tick against the shutters, feeling the shape of what she'd done settle into her chest like a swallowed coal. *No child here.* Three words. Serane Voss had smiled her beautiful, patient smile, and Coral had looked her in the eye and spoken falsehood as easily as drawing breath, and the world had not ended. Her oath-glyphs had not flared to punish her. The god — silent, distant, perhaps dead, perhaps never anything but a story the valley told itself — had not struck her down.

And yet she could feel the lie. It sat behind her sternum like a splinter worked under the skin, too deep to grip, too sharp to ignore. Twenty years of keeping contracts, of honoring terms to the letter because the letter was all she had ever trusted, and now she understood what liars felt like from the inside: not powerful. Just split. Two versions of yourself walking around in one body, and no way to know which one you'd meet around the next corner.

Behind her, Iri sat at the table with a cup of barley tea gone cold between their small hands. The child watched her with those unsettling, ancient eyes — eyes that had seen something burn, Coral was sure of it now, something that had left ash in them that would never wash out. On the table beside the cup lay her grandmother's notes, open to a page of translations, and beneath the notes lay the thing Coral had avoided looking at directly since the night the child arrived.

Her oath-contract.

It hung on the wall of the front room, as it had hung there since she was seven years old, when her mother's sister had brought it down from the shrine-house and pressed it into her hands with ceremony and grief. A sheet of vellum gone the color of old honey, inscribed in ink that still held a faint metallic gleam, framed in blackwood carved with the sigil of Ashvale: a mountain with its peak broken off, snow falling into the wound. Below the sigil, in the formal script of the valley's covenant-clerks, ran the terms of her service.

*In exchange for the god's protection of this house and bloodline, Coral Okonkwo swears blade and breath to the silent god Ashvale, until—*

Until what? She had read the ending a thousand times as a girl, tracing the letters with a fingertip, memorizing them the way other children memorized prayers. She knew the words. She had always known the words.

So why, standing in her kitchen with a lying splinter in her chest and a burning child at her table, did she suddenly need to see them again?

"Coral?" Iri said. The child's Common Tongue was improving by the day, stitched together from Coral's patient corrections and the grandmother's notes, but the accent still bent every vowel toward that older language, the First Tongue, the one that should not exist. "You look like someone walked over your grave."

"Where did you learn that phrase?"

"Maren's letters. Before." Iri looked down at the tea. "Before the fire."

Coral crossed the room without deciding to. Her boots sounded loud on the floorboards. In the front room the light was grey and low, snow-light, the kind of light that made everything look like a memory of itself. The contract hung in its frame, patient as a headstone.

She lifted it from its hook. The blackwood frame was heavier than she remembered, or her hands were weaker than they should have been. She carried it to the table, set it down beside the cold tea, and worked the backing free with her belt-knife, the little brass pins popping loose one by one like knuckle-joints.

Iri leaned forward. "What is it?"

"My oath," Coral said. "The thing I've been since before I could hold a sword properly." She drew the vellum out flat. Up close, after twenty years behind glass, it smelled of dust and something faintly sweet, like incense burned down to nothing. "My mother signed me to it when I was six months old. My aunt delivered it when she died. It's why I do what I do. Escort contracts, border work, keeping the peace in a valley where nothing ever happens." She smoothed the vellum with both palms, an unconscious gesture, the way one smooths a sleeping animal. "It's the only scripture I ever needed."

"And you want to read it now? Tonight?"

"The riders asked about you," Coral said. "They used your description. Your *actual* description, Iri — the fever-burns on your hands, the way you favor your left leg. Someone talked, or someone saw, and the Cinder Court knows a child came up the pass in the dead season." She heard her own voice go flat and clipped, the mercenary's voice, the voice she used for assessing threats. "Serane Voss will be back. Women like her are always back. And before she comes, I want to know exactly what I'm sworn to. All of it. Every line."

Iri said nothing. The child's ember-lit eyes reflected the grey window-light, and for a moment Coral thought she saw fear move through them like a fish under ice.

She began to read.

*In exchange for the god's protection of this house and bloodline, Coral Okonkwo swears blade and breath to the silent god Ashvale, until the god speaks again, at which time this oath dissolves as snow dissolves in springwater, and the sworn is freed of all bonds, debts, and duties thereof.*

She stopped.

She read it again.

*Until the god speaks again.*

The words were the same words. She was certain of it — certain the way you are certain of your own face in a mirror. She had traced these letters with a child's finger. She had recited them at her aunt's funeral, at the winter covenant-renewals, at every swearing-in of the valley's oath-keepers. *Until the god speaks again.* It was the natural ending for an oath to a silent god; everyone knew the god did not speak, had not spoken in nineteen years, would likely never speak again. The clause was a formality. A courtesy. A pretty ribbon tied around a life sentence.

Except.

Except that her whole life, Coral had assumed the clause meant *never*. That was how everyone treated it. The oath-keepers of the valley spoke of their service in terms of lifetimes — *sworn till death*, *sworn to the grave* — and the clerks who renewed the contracts each midwinter never once, in all her years, had mentioned dissolution. The clause existed the way a door exists in a prison wall: technically present, functionally decorative, painted over so many times you forgot it opened at all.

But the ink was right there. *At which time this oath dissolves as snow dissolves in springwater.*

"Coral," Iri said quietly. "Your face has gone strange."

"My face has gone strange," Coral agreed. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. She was reading the clause a third time now, and this time she was reading it slowly, the way she read enemy terrain, the way she read a contract offered by a stranger with soft hands and hard eyes. Looking for the trick. Looking for the trapdoor.

There was no trick. There was no trapdoor. There was just the sentence, sitting in its honey-colored vellum, waiting for her the way it had waited for twenty years.

*Until the god speaks again.*

And three nights ago, in this very room, a feverish child had touched her oath-glyphs — the faded brands along her forearms, dull as old scars for two decades — and the glyphs had *burned bright*. Bright as forge-coals. Bright as the day they were first cut. Because the child had been speaking, half-delirious, in a language no living kingdom used, and the glyphs had answered.

Not to the touch. To the *words*.

Coral set the vellum down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Iri," she said. "When you speak that language. The First Tongue. When you spoke it the night you came here — what did you say? Do you remember?"

Iri's brow furrowed. The child's fingers went to the collar of the borrowed shirt, twisting the fabric — an old habit, Coral had learned, a self-soothing gesture from wherever Iri had come from, whatever burning place had taught a child to make themselves small. "Fragments. A woman saying *run*. A door. The word for *ash*." A pause. The furrow deepened. "And a name. I say it sometimes. In my sleep, Maren said, before — before everything. I say it and things get warm."

"What name?"

"You know what name." Iri's voice was very small now. "You wrote it in your grandmother's book. You made me point at the letters. You went white as the snow and wouldn't eat your supper."

"Ashvale," Coral said. The word came out of her like a tooth being pulled. "You said *Ashvale*. Aloud. In the First Tongue. In a valley where saying that name aloud is supposed to be impossible — where the clerks write it as a sigil because the sound won't hold, where my grandmother spent thirty years trying to reconstruct a language nobody speaks because—" She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Because the god was silenced. Nineteen years ago. And everyone in this valley treats that silencing like weather. Like something that happened to the *god*, somewhere far away, and all we got was the quiet."

"But that's not what happened," Iri said. Not a question. The child had that tone sometimes — the terrible clarity, the ember-sight, seeing the shape of a thing before the pieces were laid out. "That's not what happened at all."

"No," Coral said. "I don't think it is."

She thought about Serane Voss. The charming voice, the courteous questions, the grey riders who moved like soldiers even dressed as merchants. *We're seeking a child who may have come through the pass. There's sickness in the lowlands. We only want to help.* And underneath the courtesy, the thing Coral had felt but hadn't named: the coiled readiness, the way Serane's hand had rested near her hip the entire conversation, the way the riders had spread through the village not searching but *sweeping*, methodical as men putting out a fire that hadn't started yet.

She had thought: they hunt the child because of what the child carries. The ember. Some stolen spark of divine fire, some relic, some treasure.

She had been thinking like a mercenary. Thinking in terms of cargo, of bounty, of things that can be taken from a person and sold.

But you couldn't take a voice from a person. A voice wasn't cargo. A voice was *what the person was*.

"Iri," Coral said, and her mouth had gone dry. "The night you came. When you touched my arms. The glyphs didn't wake because you touched them."

Iri went very still. Children go still like that when they already know what's coming — when the adult's voice takes on the tone that precedes a truth too large for the room.

"They woke because you were *speaking*," Coral said. "Speaking the god's language. Speaking—" She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Speaking *for* the god. Or as the god. Or — saints and stones, Iri, I don't even have the vocabulary for this. But my oath says it ends when the god speaks again. Twenty years I've carried these glyphs, and they've been dead as riverstones, and the clerks renew my contract every midwinter and no one has ever once mentioned the dissolution clause, and now a child walks out of a snowstorm speaking the First Tongue and my skin lights up like festival-lanterns, and the Cinder Court — the people who silenced the god, if my grandmother's notes are half true — sends a woman with kind eyes to ask after that child *by description*—"

She stopped. Because saying it aloud made it real, and real was almost more than the room could hold.

"They don't want the ember," Iri whispered.

"They don't want the ember." Coral gripped the edge of the table. The honey-colored vellum lay between them, its pretty script gleaming faintly, its broken-mountain sigil watching with its snow-filled wound. "The ember isn't treasure. The ember is the *voice*. You're not carrying something that belongs to the god, Iri. You aren't a messenger. You might be — you might be the closest thing to the god's own throat that exists in this world. And the Court silenced Ashvale once, and they'll do anything — *anything* — to keep that silence, because a god who can't speak can't be worshipped, can't be obeyed, can't gather the faithful, can't *come back*." Her voice was shaking now, and she let it shake. "They don't want to capture you. They want to make sure the god never speaks again. And the simplest way to guarantee that—"

"—is to kill whoever's speaking," Iri finished.

The snow ticked against the shutters. Somewhere in the walls, the house settled and groaned, the small sounds of a home enduring winter. On the table, the oath-contract lay open in its broken frame, and Coral looked down at it and felt something shift in her chest — not the splinter of the lie, something deeper, something structural, like a keel cracking below the waterline.

Twenty years. Twenty years of discipline mistaken for devotion. Twenty years of polishing a blade for a god who could not thank her, keeping terms for a god who could not release her, kneeling at shrines whose statues had been desecrated before she was born. She had called it faith. She had called it honor. She had built an identity out of it — *the oath-keeper, the woman of her word, the one who never breaks a contract* — and worn that identity like armor, and told herself the weight was holy.

And all the while, the contract itself had held the truth in plain sight, in ink she'd traced with a child's finger: *until the god speaks again.* Her freedom had been written into her chains from the beginning. Her cage had always had a door. And no one — not the clerks, not the elders, not the covenant-renewals, not one single soul in nineteen years of pious, careful valley silence — had ever pointed at that door and said *there, Coral, there is your way out.*

Because they didn't want her out. None of them wanted anyone out. The silence suited them. The silence was comfortable. The silence meant oaths kept forever, order maintained, questions unasked, and a god safely mute while the world above the border went on burning and blooming without him.

*Cage with pretty bars,* she thought. *And I polished them.*

"Coral?" Iri's voice, hesitant. "Are you angry?"

Coral looked at the child. At the borrowed shirt, the twisted collar, the burns on the small hands, the eyes that held ash and ember in equal measure. A child who had run from a fire with a god's voice in their chest, who had walked out of a blizzard to the one door in the valley where the oath-glyphs might answer, who had eaten her barley tea and learned her Common Tongue and asked, twice now, in that small careful voice, whether they were a burden.

*They're not hunting a relic,* she thought. *They're hunting a throat. And they'll cut it.*

"No," Coral said, and found, to her surprise, that it was mostly true. "I'm not angry at you. I'm angry at—" She gestured at the contract, the valley, the snow, the nineteen years of it. "At everything I was taught to kneel to."

She stood. She went to the hearth and took down the sword — not the escort-blade she carried for contracts, but the older one, the oath-blade, the one with the faded glyph-work etched along the fuller that matched the brands on her arms. She had drawn it a thousand times and never once felt it hum. Now, holding it in the grey light, with a child who spoke the First Tongue sitting six feet away, she could have sworn she felt something in the metal. Faint. Waiting. Like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"They'll come back," she said. "Soon. Serane Voss doesn't ask questions she doesn't already know the answers to — she was confirming, not investigating. Which means we have days. Maybe less." She began checking the blade's edge, the old ritual, hands moving on their own while her mind raced ahead. "There's a road south through the high pastures that the patrols don't watch in winter. Past the border-stones. Out of the valley."

Iri's eyes widened. "Out of the valley. But the border — your grandmother's notes said magic dies at the border. You said nothing crosses it. Nothing comes in, nothing goes out—"

"I know what I said." Coral tested the edge with her thumb. The steel was keen, but that wasn't what she was checking. She was checking the glyphs, the faded etchings, and for the first time in twenty years she found herself hoping — actually hoping — that they'd burn. "I've spent my whole life inside a cage with pretty bars, Iri. I think it's time I found out what's on the other side of the wall."

She looked down at the contract one last time. Then, deliberately, she turned the honey-colored vellum face-down on the table, so the sigil of the broken mountain stared at the wood instead of at her.

Outside, far down the valley, so faint it might have been wind, a bell began to ring. The covenant-bell. The one that rang only for assemblies, for deaths, for renewals — and, in the oldest stories, for the arrival of the Court's wardens.

Coral blew out the lamp.

"We leave tonight," she said.
