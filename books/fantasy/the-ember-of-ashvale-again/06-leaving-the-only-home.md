## 6. Leaving the Only Home

Iri had learned the shape of Coral's silences the way other children learned lullabies.

There was the silence of morning, when the mercenary sat by the cold hearth and sharpened a blade that no longer held its blessing — a slow, patient sound like rain on stone. There was the silence of the grandmother's notes spread across the table, Iri's strange words translated line by line into a language that made Coral's jaw tighten. And there was this silence, tonight: the silence of packing.

Coral packed the way she did everything — without wasted motion, without ceremony. A whetstone. Dried meat wrapped in waxed cloth. The oath-contract folded into oilskin and tucked against her ribs, where Iri had noticed she kept everything that mattered. Two blankets. A small clay pot of salve for wounds that had not yet happened.

"You'll want to wear the boots," Coral said, not looking up. "The ones with the double stitching. Snow's coming in from the pass."

Iri pulled the boots on. They were too big. Everything here was too big — the boots, the house, the world outside it, the word *run* that lived now behind Iri's teeth like a seed. But the boots had been oiled and warmed by the fire first, and that was its own kind of language, one Iri was learning to read better than any tongue.

"Are we going to die?" Iri asked.

It was the kind of question Iri asked sometimes, the kind that made adults flinch and reach for comfort. Coral never flinched. She buckled her sword-belt, tested the draw, slid the blade home again.

"Not tonight," she said. "Tonight we walk."

* * *

They left through the back, past the woodpile, along the frozen creek where the ice sang its thin glass note under their feet. The valley at night in the dead season was a country of grey and silver, the mountains holding up a sky scattered hard with stars. Iri had only ever seen this valley from the window of Coral's house, and then from Maren's stories, and then from nightmares of fire. Seeing it whole — the black ranks of pines, the pale scar of the road, the border stones far off like fallen teeth — filled something in Iri's chest that had no name yet.

The ember stirred.

It always stirred near the border. Iri had felt it for days now, a warmth behind the breastbone that came and went like breath, like something turning over in its sleep and dreaming of the edge of things. Tonight it was awake and leaning, the way a dog leans toward a door.

*I know you,* the ember seemed to say. *I remember what's out there.*

"Stay close," Coral murmured. "If I say down, you go down. If I say run, you run and don't look back, and you don't come back for me. Swear it."

Iri didn't want to swear it. Swearing it meant imagining a world with no Coral in it, and Iri had already lost one world to fire; they could not bear to rehearse losing another. But they had learned that Coral's rules were not walls. They were hands. She built them around Iri the way she'd built the fire guard around the hearth — not to keep Iri in, but to keep the burning out.

"I swear," Iri whispered.

They moved through the trees. Coral walked like water finding its way downhill, silent even in snow, and Iri copied her as best they could, stepping where she stepped, breathing when she breathed. Once she stopped so suddenly that Iri walked into her back, and her hand came up flat against Iri's chest — gentle, absolute — and they stood together in the dark for the space of ten heartbeats while somewhere off to the east a wolf spoke and was answered.

Then on again.

The border stones rose out of the dark sooner than Iri expected. There were seven of them in a broken arc across the valley mouth, old ones, taller than Coral, furred with frost and lichen, each carved with a spiral worn nearly smooth. Iri had seen them in the grandmother's notes — drawings, annotations in a cramped hand: *the boundary, the stillness, the place where the god's voice cannot follow.* The notes had called the valley a sanctuary once. Now, reading between the lines, Iri understood it had also been a cell. Not for Coral.

For the thing sleeping in Iri's chest.

And for everyone who might have heard it speak.

Iri stopped three paces from the nearest stone. The ember was roaring now, silently, a furnace behind the ribs, and the cold air around them shimmered faintly, the frost on the grass softening, melting, refreezing in patterns like ferns.

"Iri." Coral's voice was low. "What's happening?"

"It wants to go home," Iri said. "Or — I want to. I can't tell which."

Coral crouched to their level. In the starlight her face was all planes and shadow, but her eyes caught the light the way a cat's do, steady and unblinking. Twenty years Iri had not known her, and already twenty days felt like twenty years' worth of knowing.

"When we cross," Coral said, "everything changes. My glyphs will wake. Whatever sleeps in your blood will wake. The Court will be able to find us easier, and the world out there—" she paused, choosing words the way she chose footing on scree, "—the world out there is louder than this one. Colder in some ways. Warmer in others. It will hurt less than you're afraid of and more than I can promise you it won't."

"That's not an answer," Iri said.

"No," Coral agreed. "It's the truth. Answers are just truths you've decided to live inside." She held out her hand. "Come on, ember-child. Let's go be loud."

Iri took her hand.

They crossed together, one stride, two — and the world broke open.

* * *

Later, Iri would try to describe it and fail, because there were no words in either language for what crossing felt like. The closest Iri ever came was this: *it was like being born twice.*

The moment their boots passed the last border stone, the ember detonated — not in fire but in *recognition*. Every hair on Iri's body lifted. The night, which had been merely dark, became layered, deep, alive: they could feel the roots of the pines drinking beneath the frost, feel the wolves as warm moving shapes in the eastern dark, feel the slow mineral patience of the mountains themselves. Magic wasn't a thing out here. It was the medium. It was the water they'd been swimming in all along without knowing, and the valley had been a room with the water drained out, and now—

Now Iri was drowning in light.

But it was Coral who fell.

She made it four steps past the stones. Then her knees hit the snow and her breath came out of her in a sound Iri had never heard a person make — not a cry, not a gasp, something older, dragged up from underneath twenty years of discipline like a root torn from soil. Her sword-hand clawed at the ground. Her oath-glyphs, the faded grey marks along her forearms that Iri had thought were scars or tattoos, ignited.

They burned blue-white, every one of them, spiraling lines of light crawling up her arms, over her shoulders, across her collarbone, and where they burned the snow around her melted in perfect circles and the frost on the grass bloomed upward into flowers of ice that had no business existing. Iri could *feel* her — feel the flood pouring into her, decades of dormant current rushing through channels carved long ago and left dry, and the channels were cracking, widening, remaking themselves around a power they'd been built to hold and never allowed to.

"Coral!" Iri dropped beside her, hands hovering, afraid to touch, afraid not to touch. "Coral, what do I do, what's happening to you—"

Her answer was a laugh.

That was the worst part. That was the part Iri would carry forever. Coral Okonkwo, kneeling in the snow with light pouring out of her skin like water through a shattered dam, was laughing — a cracked, disbelieving, half-sobbing laugh — and tears were freezing on her face, and she was saying something over and over in a voice scraped raw:

"It doesn't hurt. It doesn't hurt. Twenty years and it doesn't hurt—"

Iri touched her shoulder, and the ember reached through the touch, gentle now, the way it had been gentle the first night at the door, and steadied whatever was breaking-open inside her. The light dimmed to embers. The ice-flowers glittered. Coral knelt in the ruined circle of snow, breathing like a woman surfacing from deep water, and Iri saw — clearly, unmistakably, in the wide honest starlight — that the mercenary was crying.

Not the way Iri cried, quick and hot and over fast. This was slow. Silent. As if the tears had been waiting twenty years for permission and were now taking their time leaving.

"I'm sorry," Coral said, and dashed a hand across her eyes, embarrassed, fierce about it. "Gods. I'm sorry. You shouldn't have to see—"

"Why are you sorry?" Iri asked. "You look like the shrine pictures. Like the god waking up."

That stopped her. She looked at her own arms, at the glyph-marks glowing softly now, banked coals instead of wildfire, and something moved through her face that Iri couldn't read — grief and wonder fighting over the same ground.

"My mother used to say the faithful don't lose their blessings in the valley," Coral said quietly. "They just... hold their breath. All this time I thought she was comforting me. Making a story for a dull blade and a hollow oath." She flexed her fingers, watched the light pool in her palm. "She wasn't comforting me. She was telling me the truth, and I buried her truth under my own disappointment." A breath. "I've been holding my breath since I was nine years old, Iri. I forgot I was doing it."

Iri didn't know what to say to that, so they said nothing, which Coral, uniquely among all the adults Iri had ever known, seemed to accept as speech.

* * *

They walked until the sky went from black to iron to the bruised purple before dawn, putting miles between themselves and the border, following a track Coral knew from her escort contracts — the long way south, avoiding the King's Road, avoiding eyes. The world stayed loud. That was the thing Iri couldn't get used to: the loudness. Every bird was a struck bell. Every stream a conversation. Twice Iri felt other things moving in the wild places, old things, curious things, and both times the ember flared warning-heat and the things withdrew.

Coral felt it too. Iri could tell. She kept pausing at rises, head tilted, listening to frequencies Iri hadn't known existed until last night. Once she stopped at a stream, cupped water in her bare hand, and frowned at it.

"The water tastes different," she said. "No — it tastes the same. *I* taste different." She drank anyway, and laughed again, small and startled, like the laugh was a bird that kept escaping its cage.

By full light they found a hollow beneath a granite overhang, sheltered from the wind, and Coral built a fire with a flick of two fingers and a murmured syllable that made Iri's jaw drop. She stared at her own hand as if it belonged to someone else.

"Twenty years of flint and steel," she muttered. "Twenty years."

"Will it always be like this?" Iri asked, settling against her side, pulling the shared blanket over both of them. "The loudness?"

Coral was quiet a while. The fire crackled. Somewhere above them, snow began sifting down through the pine branches, catching the light in slow spirals.

"No," she said finally. "Loud becomes normal. That's the mercy and the tragedy of people — we get used to anything." She looked down at Iri, and her expression did the complicated thing it did sometimes, the thing where hardness and tenderness occupied the same face without contradiction. "But I hope some part of you never gets used to it. I hope you stay a little bit amazed your whole life. That's what they took from us in the valley, more than the magic. The amazement."

Iri leaned into her. The ember hummed, content, and beneath its hum Iri felt the other thing it had been carrying since the crossing — a pull, faint but constant, southeastward, toward something beyond the hills. Toward shrines, maybe. Toward the faithful. Or toward the Court. The ember couldn't tell the difference. Both roads smelled, faintly, of ash.

"Coral," Iri said, sleep dragging at the edges of the words. "When we find the others. The faithful. Will they know what I am?"

Coral's arm tightened around them.

"Yes," she said.

"And will they be happy about it?"

Another silence, longer. When Coral answered, her voice had gone flat and careful, the mercenary's voice, the voice she used when the truth had teeth.

"Iri, last night I crossed a border I was taught my whole life never to cross, and the sky didn't fall. But someone built that border, and someone guards it, and neither of them is going to be happy we're out here." She fed a stick to the fire. "So we find the faithful before the Court finds us. And we learn what you are on our own terms, not theirs."

Iri slept. And dreamed — not of the burning library this time, but of a door, standing open in a field of ash, and a voice like warm stone saying, in the First Tongue, words the grandmother's notes had translated only once, in the margin, in a shaking hand:

*The lock is a name. The name is walking toward me.*

Iri woke at dawn with the ember blazing and Coral already on her feet, sword drawn, staring south down the track — where, far off through the thinning snow, three riders in grey crested the hill like crows finding a trail.
