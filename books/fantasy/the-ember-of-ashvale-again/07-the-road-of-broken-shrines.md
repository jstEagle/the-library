## 7. The Road of Broken Shrines

The valley let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long — slowly, and with marks left behind.

Coral felt every mile of it. Three days past the border and her body was still learning itself: the old war wound in her shoulder aching with a warmth that had nothing to do with infection, her oath-glyphs humming under her skin like bees in a wall. For twenty years they had been ink. Faded brown lines she traced out of habit more than faith, the way a widow might keep setting two cups on the table. Now they burned faintly gold whenever Iri slept near her, and she had not yet decided whether that was a comfort or an accusation.

They walked the pilgrim road south, because Maren's name had been the only one Coral's grandmother's notes offered — *if you must run, run to the keeper at Hollowmere* — and because roads meant people, and people meant news of pursuit. The road itself was older than any kingdom. It had been built for feet like theirs.

"You're limping," Iri said, on the morning of the fourth day.

"I'm walking."

"You're limping *and* walking." The child fell into step beside her, breath steaming. They had taken to wearing Coral's spare cloak pinned with a thorn-twig brooch, and they wore it with the gravity of a general accepting a sword. "In my language there are eleven words for how a person walks. This one is *senneth*. It means walking while pretending not to hurt."

"Your language," Coral said, "has too many words."

"It used to have more."

That silenced her. That was the thing about Iri — the child could gut her without ever raising their voice, simply by remembering what no one else alive remembered. They walked on through frost-burned grass, and Coral thought about languages dying, and gods going quiet, and all the words for grief that no one had needed until someone made them.

* * *

The first shrine appeared just past noon, standing alone in a field of stubble like a tooth left in an empty jaw.

It was small — a waist-high cairn of grey stone, the kind farmers once raised at crossroads so travelers could pause and be seen by the god. Coral knew its shape from her grandmother's sketches. She had never seen a real one. In the valley, Ashvale had been a name in old contracts and a silence in old prayers, and Coral had assumed the rest of the world had simply forgotten him, the way the world forgot most things.

She had not assumed this.

Someone had taken a hammer to the cairn. Not weathering — weathering rounds stone, softens it. This was deliberate, methodical work: each stone split along its grain, the fragments scattered in a careful ring, as if the destroyers had wanted even the rubble arranged correctly. And carved into the largest surviving piece, where the god's mark should have been — the closed eye, the cupped hands — someone had chiseled a new sigil: a flame inside a cage of straight lines.

The Cinder Court's brand. She'd seen it stamped on the wax seals of writs and warrants, back when she took contracts that touched the law. She had never wondered what it was *for*.

"They broke all of them?" Iri asked quietly. The child stood before the ruin with their fists clenched, and Coral saw the ember-light flicker behind their breastbone, visible through the cloth like a coal breathing under ash.

"Looks like it."

"Why break stones? Stones don't pray."

"No," Coral said. "But people kneel to them."

She crouched and turned one of the split fragments over. On its underside, protected from hammer and weather both, the old carving survived: the closed eye, serene even here, even broken. Her grandmother had drawn that eye a hundred times in the margins of her notes, always with the same caption in her cramped hand: *he watches even with his eyes shut.*

Something moved in Coral's chest that she did not have a word for. She was beginning to suspect the First Tongue had eleven words for it too.

* * *

They found the second shrine two days later, and the third the day after that, and after that they stopped counting.

Every one destroyed. Every one destroyed the same way — split stones, scattered rings, the Court's caged-flame chiseled over the god's mark. Some had been shrines of real size once; they passed the foundations of what must have been a sanctuary, roofless now, its walls pulled down into the foundations of a granary built hard against it, as if the new building were sitting on the old one's chest. A waystone that had once pointed pilgrims toward the holy places had been recarved to point instead toward the nearest city, toward the Court's provincial seat, its original inscription gouged out and replaced with a legal formula: *ALL FLAME IS ACCOUNTED FOR.*

Iri read that one aloud, sounding out the trade-tongue letters, and then asked what it meant.

"It means," Coral said slowly, "that they counted everything. Every ember. Every spark. They've been doing arithmetic on a god for nineteen years."

She was beginning to understand the shape of it, and the shape frightened her. This was not vandalism. Vandalism was hot and careless. This was bookkeeping. Somewhere, in some cold archive, there was a ledger with every shrine in these lands listed in it, and clerks who checked off the destruction item by item, and perhaps — the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome — perhaps a column noting which shrines had been missed.

Which faithful had not been found.

Her grandmother's notes grew heavier in her pack with every mile. *Run to the keeper at Hollowmere.* Her grandmother had written that thirty years ago, in a hand already shaking. Which meant her grandmother had known there would be running. Had known there were keepers to run *to*. Had known — Coral stopped in the middle of the road, mid-stride, and Iri bumped into her back.

"What is it?"

"My grandmother wrote this like a woman leaving instructions for a fire she expected." Coral stared down the road, at the frost and the grey sky and the next ruined shrine waiting somewhere beyond the bend. "She wasn't a valley woman. She came from outside. She married in, or fled in, or — " She shook her head. "I never asked. You don't ask, in the valley. Everyone there is running from something, and asking is rude."

"And you never wondered?"

"Of course I wondered." She started walking again, faster. "Wondering is free. Asking costs."

* * *

Hollowmere announced itself by smell first — woodsmoke and wet peat and, underneath, something floral and strange that Coral couldn't place until they crested the last hill and saw the village: a mere as still as poured glass, ringed with reeds, and around the water's edge dozens of small gardens blooming out of season. Frost lay everywhere else, silvering the fields, but the gardens steamed gently, green and impossible, flowers open in the dead season like a held breath.

Maren's cottage sat at the far end of the mere, low-roofed, its chimney smoking a thin, disciplined line. An old woman was kneeling in one of the gardens despite the cold, bare-handed in the soil, and she looked up as they approached with eyes the color of shallow water over slate.

She looked at Coral first. Then at Iri. Then at Coral again, and whatever she saw made her go very still, the way soldiers go still.

"Well," she said at last, wiping earth from her hands. "Thirty years I've kept the lamps lit, and look what finally comes down my road. Come inside before the frost takes your toes, girl. Both of you. There's soup, and there's questions, and we'll get through the soup first because you look like you haven't eaten since the border."

Inside, the cottage was warm and close and crammed with things that made Coral's skin prickle: dried herbs hung in bundles labeled in the First Tongue, a cracked bell on the mantel that hummed faintly though nothing touched it, and above the hearth — intact, whole, lovingly maintained — a stone bearing the closed eye and the cupped hands.

"You're a shrine-keeper," Coral said. "A real one."

"Last one this side of the mountains, soon enough." Maren ladled soup into wooden bowls without ceremony. "The others are ash, or graves, or worse. Eat."

Iri ate like a child finally allowed to be hungry. Coral ate mechanically, watching the old woman, cataloguing exits out of twenty years of habit, and finding, absurdly, that she didn't want to use them.

"The shrines," Coral said, when the bowls were empty. "On the road. All of them. I thought the Court hated Ashvale."

"Hate?" Maren laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "Child, hate is honest. Hate looks you in the face. What the Court feels is *fear*, dressed up in ledgers and writs, and fear doesn't smash — fear *files*." She leaned forward. "You crossed the border to get here. Out of the valley. Tell me why."

So Coral told her. Not all of it — not yet — but the shape of it: the child at her door in the first snow, speaking a dead language, carrying light in their chest; the riders in grey; the lie she had told Serane Voss, which still sat in her stomach like a swallowed nail; the clause in her own oath-contract she had read a hundred times and never once *seen*.

When she finished, Maren was looking at her strangely. Not at Iri. At her.

"Your mother's name," the old woman said quietly. "Tell me your mother's name."

"Elira. Elira Dunmore." Coral frowned. "Why?"

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were wet, and she did not wipe them, and somehow that was worse than if she had wept openly.

"Elira Dunmore," she repeated. "Keeper of the shrine at Vale's End. Third voice of the western circle. The bravest woman I ever knelt beside, and I knelt beside women who walked into Court fires singing." She reached across the table and gripped Coral's wrist, her thumb pressing — deliberately, precisely — on one of Coral's faded oath-glyphs. "And the finest lock-smith the god ever had, though she never forged a key in her life."

The cottage seemed to tilt. Coral heard her own heartbeat, slow and enormous.

"That's not possible. My mother sold herbs. She died of fever when I was nine. She never — she didn't believe in anything, she told me — "

"She told you that so you'd never be asked the question I'm about to ask you." Maren's grip tightened. "Elira stayed behind when the purge came, girl. Someone had to hold the door shut from the inside. Nineteen years ago, when the Nine silenced our god, it wasn't chains that bound him — it was a sealing, a great working, and it needed a living anchor. A bloodline willing to stand in the doorway and *be* the lock." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Your mother volunteered. Your mother's name is the first syllable of the prison. And you — "

She stopped. Looked at Coral's wrist. At the glyph beneath her thumb, which had begun, very softly, to glow.

"You are the rest of it."

The fire popped. Outside, the impossible gardens breathed their out-of-season fragrance against the frost, and on the mantel the cracked bell gave one clear, involuntary note, like something waking.

Coral sat very still. Twenty years of discipline held her spine straight and her face smooth, and underneath it everything she had ever believed about herself — orphan, mercenary, weapon-for-hire, daughter of a herb-seller who died ordinary — was quietly coming apart at the seams, thread by thread, like a garment she had been wearing wrong-side-out her whole life.

"She lied to me," Coral said at last. Her voice sounded distant, borrowed. "My whole life. To protect me."

"Yes," Maren said. "And it worked right up until a child knocked on your door with a god's heart in their chest. Fate's got a cruel sense of humor, but it's thorough."

Iri had gone pale, the bowl forgotten. "Then the riders," the child whispered. "The ones hunting me. If Coral is part of the lock — "

"Then the Court doesn't only want the ember destroyed," Maren finished grimly. "They want the lock confirmed. Found. Accounted for." She rose and began pulling a heavy bar across the door, moving with the economy of a woman who had done this every night for thirty years. "Sleep. Tomorrow I'll teach you both what your grandmother's notes left out. But tonight, know this much —"

She turned, firelight catching the wet tracks on her cheeks she still hadn't wiped away.

"You weren't born weaponless, Coral Dunmore. You were born a *door*. And doors, child, can be opened from either side."
