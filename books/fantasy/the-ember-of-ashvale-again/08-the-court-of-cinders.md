## 8. The Court of Cinders

The road to the Court was paved with the bones of a forest.

Serane Voss had ridden it a hundred times and had never once stopped noticing — the way the trees thinned as she climbed the ash-grey ridge, how their trunks went pale and smooth like candle-wax, how no bird sang past the third milestone. The old maps called this region the Verdant Reach. The maps were two hundred years out of date. Whatever had grown here had grown before the Silencing, before the Nine had climbed to their thrones on a pyre of stolen fire, and it had not grown since.

Her horse knew better than to hurry. Horses always did. They felt what the riders pretended not to: that the air itself grew heavier here, thick with old grief, the way a house feels after a funeral that no one will speak of.

She crested the ridge at dusk and saw the Court spread below her in its bowl of dead stone — nine towers arranged in a ring around a central spire, each tower crowned with a brazier that never went out, never flickered, never warmed anything. The Cinder Court. Children in the lowlands still played at being Cinder Knights, sweeping sticks through the air and shouting *for the Nine, for the Silence*, without knowing what they were asking for.

Serane knew. She had given twenty-two years to it, and she would have given forty more, and there were nights — more of them lately, arriving like uninvited guests — when she lay awake in some roadside inn and wondered whether that made her the most faithful woman alive or the most damned.

She rode down into the bowl, and the gates opened for her without a word, because they always did, because the grey cloak and the silver cinder-pin meant *do not question me*, and Serane Voss had spent two decades learning to wear authority like armor and only recently begun to notice how much it weighed.

* * *

They received her in the Chamber of Embers, which was not a chamber of embers at all but a room of black marble so polished that the nine seated figures seemed to float above their own reflections. The Nine did not sit in chairs. Chairs implied bodies, and the Nine had spent two centuries making themselves into something other than bodies — robed, hooded, voice-modulated by the acoustics of the room until they sounded less like people than like weather.

Serane knelt on the cold stone and gave her report.

"The child reached the valley," she said. "The mercenary has taken them in. Coral Okonkwo — oath-bound to Ashvale, third generation, clean record, no family living except a grandmother who died last winter." She paused. "She lied to me."

Silence, of the particular quality the Nine cultivated. When one of them finally spoke, the voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the marble and the hood and the space between.

"Lied."

"She told me no child had passed through the valley. I had described Iri to her in detail. The fever, the age, the speech. She looked me in the eye and said no." Serane kept her gaze fixed on the floor, on her own reflection floating in the black stone, a small grey smudge among giants. "I have read Okonkwo's file. In nineteen years of service she has never filed a false report, never broken a contract, never missed an oath-payment at her village shrine. Her devotion is documented down to the copper coins."

"And yet she lied."

"Yes, First Ember."

"Why?"

It was the question Serane had turned over for six days on the ride back, turning it like a stone in the hand, looking for the crack. "I don't know," she said, and then, because lying to the Nine was a different order of sin than lying to a stranger in a snowbound valley: "I think — I think something in her recognized the child. Something older than her oaths. The grandmother's notes were seized; we're having them translated. But if I had to answer—" She stopped.

"Answer," said the Third Ember, and there was something almost gentle in the modulation, which frightened Serane more than anger would have.

"I think the god is not as silent as we believed. I think it has been speaking all along, very quietly, to the people who were listening. And I think Coral Okonkwo heard it before she ever saw the child."

The silence that followed had texture. Serane knelt in it and felt it settle over her shoulders like wet wool.

"You may rise, Knight-Captain," said the First Ember at last. "You will rest tonight. Tomorrow you will return to the valley with a full company. This time you will not ask."

* * *

They gave her a cell in the Knights' wing, which was really a fine room with a narrow bed and a window that looked out over the central spire, and Serane sat on the bed for a long time without lighting the lamp, watching the brazier-fire on the spire's crown burn its unwavering, heatless burn.

Twenty-two years. She had been nine years old when they found her — a farm girl from the Marchlands, quick with numbers, quicker with a blade, orphaned in the border raids that nobody ever claimed responsibility for. A recruiter in grey had bought her supper and asked her if she wanted her life to mean something. Every child wanted that. She had said yes the way children say yes to everything, and the Court had built itself into the marrow of her, lesson by lesson, year by year, until *Knight-Captain Serane Voss* was not something she did but something she *was*.

And the first lesson, the one they taught before swordwork, before strategy, before anything, was this:

*We are not villains.*

She could still hear old Knight-Master Corvane's voice delivering it, his ruined lung whistling under the words. *The world will call us monsters, girl, because monsters are easier to hate than fear. Let them. We did what had to be done, and we carry it so no one else has to. That is the whole of our order. We carry it.*

She had believed him. She believed him still, most days. But belief, she was learning, was like a blade — it needed maintenance, or it dulled, and a dull blade was more dangerous than none, because it made you confident while cutting you.

Tomorrow she would go back to the valley with a full company, and she would not ask. She knew what *not asking* meant. She had written the euphemisms herself often enough: *resolve the situation, contain the asset, complete the Silence.* Somewhere behind those words was a feverish child who spoke a dead language and burned with a god's stolen heart, and somewhere behind the child was a mercenary who had lied for the first time in her spotless life, and Serane sat in the dark and tried to make herself feel nothing about either of them, and failed, and hated herself for failing, and got up and lit the lamp anyway.

* * *

In the morning, before she rode out, they took her down.

They did this sometimes — took a senior knight beneath the spire, alone, unannounced. It was called *renewal*, and every knight came back from it graver and steadier, and none of them ever spoke of what they saw. Serane had gone down twice in twenty-two years. She had told herself both times that the tightness in her chest was awe.

A door of black iron, unmarked. Stairs spiraling down into the roots of the world. The air changed as she descended — warmer, then cooler, then warm again, carrying a smell she had never managed to name: struck flint, maybe, or the moment just after lightning. At the bottom, a corridor lined with glass, and behind the glass—

Behind the glass was the fire.

Not flame. Flame was a thing that happened to fuel. This was fire the way a person was a thing that happened to flesh — vast, aware, coiled in a lattice of white chains that ran floor to ceiling and vanished into walls that were not walls. It pressed against the lattice the way a sleeper presses against blankets, slow, patient, endless. As Serane walked the corridor, the fire turned. She felt it turn, the way you feel eyes on your back, and though it had no face she understood, with a certainty that lived below thought, that it was looking at her.

It had been looking at her for twenty-two years. Every renewal, every descent, every time she came down these stairs, it watched her pass, and once — only once, twelve years ago, the night after her first field execution — she had sworn she heard it make a sound. Not a roar. Not a scream. A sound like a woman trying to speak through a closed door.

"Knight-Captain." The First Ember stood at the corridor's end, hood thrown back for once, and Serane caught the glimpse she was never supposed to catch: an ordinary face, old and tired, a grandmother's face, seamed and human. "You know why we show you this."

"To remind me what we guard against."

"To remind you *why*." The old woman turned to the glass. Up close, the fire's attention was a physical weight, a hand laid flat against Serane's sternum. "Look at it. Truly look. Everyone below this level thinks the Silencing was ambition — that we climbed onto a god's corpse and took its power for ourselves. Let them think it. Fear keeps the lowland lords polite. But you are above that level now, Serane, and you deserve the truth, because the truth is worse than ambition."

Serane said nothing. The fire shifted behind the glass, and for half a heartbeat the lattice glowed where the chains crossed, as if the whole structure were one enormous sigil, one word written in restraint.

"Three hundred years ago, Ashvale woke," the First Ember said. "Fully. Briefly. You know the histories say a shrine caught fire. The histories were written by survivors, and survivors soften things." Her voice did not waver, but her hands, folded before her, had gone white at the knuckles. "It was not a shrine. It was the western continent. All of it. The god woke grieving — its faithful had been slaughtered by the empire of that age, do you understand, it woke in *mourning* — and its grief burned. Cindral, Vel Maros, the Amber Coast: gone. Not conquered. Gone. The sea boiled along a thousand miles of coast. The ash fell for a decade. Your ancestors' ancestors crossed the ocean on the cinder-winds as refugees, and the ones who founded this Court swore an oath on the charred shoreline: *never again.*"

The fire pressed against its chains. Serane realized her own hands had curled into fists at her sides.

"We did not steal its voice out of hunger," the First Ember said. "We stole it out of terror. There is a difference, and the difference is everything, and it changes nothing. Because here is what terror teaches you, Knight-Captain, if you live inside it long enough: it begins to feel like wisdom. We silenced a god to save the world, and then we needed the silencing to be righteous, so we hoarded its scattered fire — every ember, every fragment, every shard of its divided heart — so that it could never wake again, not even diminished. Especially not diminished. A wounded god, Serane. A wounded god with a grievance. Tell me you would risk it."

The fire turned its faceless attention fully upon her, and Serane — veteran of forty engagements, killer of men, keeper of the Silence — felt her knees soften.

"No," she heard herself say. "I wouldn't."

"No," the First Echo agreed softly. "No one would. Remember that tomorrow, when you stand in that valley and look at the child. Whatever mercy rises in you — and it will, you are not made of stone, I have watched you too long to believe that — remember the boiling sea. Remember that the kindest thing you can do for that child may be the thing that saves ten million lives from burning. We carry it, Serane. That is the whole of our order. We carry it."

She dismissed her, and Serane climbed the stairs with the fire's gaze between her shoulder blades the entire way, and at the top, in the grey morning light, she stood by her horse for a long moment, breathing.

*We carry it.*

Then why, she thought — and it was a treasonous thought, a splinter working deeper into the meat of her — *why does the fire look at us like prisoners look at jailers? Why does it sound like a woman trying to speak through a door?*

Grief burns. She believed that. She had seen the maps of the drowned coast; she had walked the Ashfall Marches where nothing grew. But grief also *grieves*, and there was a difference between a conflagration and a mourning, and the thing in the glass had not moved like rage in three hundred years. It had moved like sorrow. Like patience. Like something waiting — not to burn, but to be *heard*.

She mounted, and the company formed up around her, forty knights in grey, and the gates of the Cinder Court opened onto the road east, toward the valley, toward the child.

Serane Voss rode at their head with her jaw set and her hands steady, because that was what Knight-Captains did, and behind her eyes, in the place where her certainties had lived for twenty-two years, a single question burned hotter than any brazier on any tower:

If the Nine were right, why did the truth feel like a cage with pretty bars?

She did not have an answer. She had a company, a warrant, and six days of hard riding — and somewhere ahead of her, in a snowbound valley where magic could not follow, a mercenary who had lied for the first time in her life was guarding a child who might be the last ember of a grieving god.

Serane spurred her horse, and the dead forest swallowed the column whole.
