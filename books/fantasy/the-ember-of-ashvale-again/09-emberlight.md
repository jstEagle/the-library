## 9. Emberlight

The dream always began the same way: with fire that did not warm.

Iri stood in the library again — or what had been a library once, before the burning. The shelves rose higher than any tree they had ever seen, and every book on them was singing. Not words Iri could catch. More like the sound a kettle makes just before it boils, a thin silver thread of noise that wound through the smoke and made the whole vast room feel like a throat about to speak.

The woman was there too. She was always there. Her face never resolved — it was like trying to look at the moon through moving water — but her hands were clear, her hands were *always* clear, ash-smeared and steady as she pressed Iri's small shoulders and said the word she always said:

*Run.*

And Iri would try to run, because in the dream they understood that running was the only thing left to do, the last instruction of a dying world. But their feet would not move. The floor had gone soft as bread in milk. And the fire was not coming from the shelves after all; it was coming from inside Iri's own chest, blooming outward through their ribs like a flower made of knives, and the singing books were singing *to* it, calling to it, and the woman's face finally began to resolve, finally began to become someone—

Iri woke screaming.

They woke into darkness and cold and the smell of pine resin and old wool, which meant the barn loft above Maren's shrine, which meant safe, which meant *not burning*, and still their body would not stop shaking. Their nightshirt was soaked through with sweat that steamed faintly in the winter air, and when they pressed a hand to their sternum they felt it — the ember, the little captive sun beneath their skin — pulsing far too fast, far too bright, throwing a ruddy glow through the fabric like a lantern wrapped in linen.

*Iri.* Coral's voice, low and rough with sleep, from the pallet below. *Iri, you're all right. You're here.*

But Iri wasn't listening, because Iri was looking at Coral, and Coral was *glowing*.

Not glowing, exactly. Not the way the ember glowed. It was subtler than that — a warmth rising off the older woman's body like heat-shimmer over summer stone, visible only because the loft was so dark and so cold. And along Coral's left side, where Iri knew an old scar ran from hip to armpit, a wound she never spoke of and never let anyone touch, something was happening that made Iri's breath stop in their throat.

The scar was opening. No — not opening. *Unmaking.* The thick rope of damaged flesh was softening, smoothing, drawing inward like water finding its level, and beneath it new skin was surfacing, pink and unbroken, skin that had no business existing on a body twenty years past its wounding.

*Coral,* Iri whispered, and their voice came out in the First Tongue without their meaning it to, the old word for *hurt* and the old word for *healing* tangled together until they were almost the same word. *Coral, look—*

*What?* Coral sat up fast, hand going by instinct to the knife she kept even under her blankets, and then her hand found her side instead, and stopped. Iri watched her fingers press against skin that should have been ridged and knotted, watched them press again, harder, as if testing whether the smoothness was a trick of the dark. Watched her go very, very still.

*That's not possible,* she said at last, flatly, in the way she said things when she was frightened and refusing to be.

*I'm sorry.* The words tumbled out of Iri before they could weigh them. *I didn't mean to. I dreamed the burning again and the ember got loud and I couldn't hold it down, I tried, I tried to keep it quiet, and then it went out of me and into you and I couldn't call it back—*

*Hey.* Coral's voice changed. She reached up in the dark and found Iri's shoulder, gripped it, firm and real. *Breathe. Children don't apologize for nightmares. That's not how this works.*

*I'm not a child.*

*You're eleven winters if you're twelve, and you scream in your sleep, and you eat your bread crust-first to save the soft part for last because nobody ever told you it doesn't matter which end you start from.* Coral's voice was dry, but there was something underneath it that hadn't been there in the valley, something that had been growing since the border, since the night magic had flooded into her and Iri had watched her cry and pretend she hadn't. *You're a child. Mine to look after, near enough. So stop apologizing and tell me what happened.*

So Iri told her. Haltingly, in the mixture of tongues they fell into when tired — First Tongue for the deep things, the trade-speech Coral had taught them on the road for everything else. They told her about the library, the singing shelves, the woman with the steady hands. They told her how the fire lived in their chest even awake now, a small pressure behind the breastbone, like a second heartbeat that beat out of time with their own. They told her the thing they had not told anyone, the thing they had barely admitted to themselves:

*It's getting louder. Every night. Like it's trying to say something and I can't hear it yet.*

Coral was quiet for a long moment. In the dark, Iri heard her shift, and then felt the pallet dip as she climbed up to sit beside them, bringing her blanket with her, wrapping it around both their shoulders without asking. She smelled of woodsmoke and the bitter herb tea Maren brewed and, underneath it, something mineral and strange that Iri had come to understand was simply *her* — the scent of a person whose blood had been holding a door shut for nineteen years without knowing it.

*Show me,* Coral said.

*Show you what?*

*The ember. Whatever you can show.*

Iri hesitated. Then they pulled open the collar of their nightshirt.

The light spilled out. In the valley it had been a faint thing, a coal buried in ash, but since the crossing — since the border where magic died and therefore had never been able to reach them, and beyond which it rushed back in like a tide finding a breached seawall — the ember had grown bold. It sat behind Iri's sternum like a swallowed star, and its glow moved when they breathed, swelling and dimming, swelling and dimming, and Coral stared at it with an expression Iri had no name for. Not fear. Not quite wonder either. Something older than both.

*It's beautiful,* Coral said quietly, and Iri's eyes stung, because no one had ever called any part of them beautiful before. Broken, yes. Strange, yes. A burden, a duty, a vessel, a message. Never beautiful.

*It hurts sometimes,* Iri admitted. *When it gets loud. Like it's too big for me.*

*Everything true is too big for us at first.* Coral pulled the blanket tighter around them both. *My grandmother used to say that. About grief, mostly. But I think she meant it about everything.*

*Tell me about her. Your grandmother.*

Another silence. Outside, wind worried at the barn's old shingles, and somewhere below them a horse shifted in its stall, and the ordinary sounds of the world went on being ordinary, which was its own kind of mercy.

*She was the one who taught me my letters,* Coral said finally. *Before the oath-house took that over. She had notes everywhere — scraps of paper, margins of ledgers, the backs of bills of sale. Little translations. Words in a language nobody spoke anymore.* She paused. *Your language, I know that now. I didn't know it then. I thought she was just an odd old woman who liked dead words.*

*Was she faithful? To Ashvale?*

*I think...* Coral's jaw worked. Iri had learned to read the small mechanics of her face the way other children read storybooks. *I think she remembered more than she said. I think remembering was dangerous, so she hid it inside ordinary things. Inside me, maybe, without meaning to.* She looked down at her side, at the place where twenty years of scar had been unwritten in a single night. *This is going to change things, you know. What you did. When Maren sees it.*

*Maren will be angry?*

*Maren will weep,* Coral said, *and then she'll ask you a hundred questions, and then she'll feed you until you can't walk. That's how shrine-keepers grieve and celebrate at the same time. It's very efficient.*

Iri laughed — a small, cracked sound, surprising themself with it — and leaned sideways until their head found Coral's shoulder. They expected her to stiffen. She had stiffened the first dozen times, in the early weeks, when Iri's body sought hers without permission, some starved animal part of them recognizing safety before their mind caught up. But tonight she only settled, adjusting her weight so the lean was easier, and Iri felt the strangest sensation bloom in their chest alongside the ember: a warmth that had nothing to do with fire.

*Coral. Can I say something foolish?*

*All your words are allowed. That's the rule.*

*Sometimes I think I'm not a messenger at all.* The confession came slowly, each word carried up from somewhere deep. *Everyone keeps saying I carry the god's voice, the god's ember, the god's message. But messages get delivered. Messages end. And this—* they pressed their palm flat against their sternum, against the pulsing light — *this doesn't feel like it wants to end. It feels like it wants to stay. Like it's not passing through me. Like it lives here.* Their voice dropped. *I think I might not be carrying a piece of him. I think I might be what's left of him. His heart, maybe. The last piece he could save when everything else was taken.*

The wind filled the silence that followed. Iri counted the horse's movements below, one, two, three, waiting for Coral to deny it, to offer comfort, to do the adult thing and make the frightening thought smaller.

Instead Coral said: *Does that frighten you?*

*Yes.*

*Good,* Coral said, and Iri twisted to stare up at her, scandalized, and she almost smiled — that rare, crooked thing that transformed her whole hard face. *It should. If it didn't frighten you, I'd worry you'd already decided what you are, and eleven-year-olds who've decided what they are grow up into soldiers who've decided what they are, and I've killed enough of those to last me two lifetimes.* She was quiet a moment. *You get to find out slowly. That's the whole point of having someone. You get to find out slowly, and there's someone there while you do it.*

*Is that what you had? Someone?*

*No,* Coral said simply. *I had an oath. An oath is a shape someone else cut and you pour yourself into it. It holds you together, but it decides what you'll be when you set.* Her hand came up, hesitant, and rested — lightly, so lightly — on Iri's hair. *I'm learning the difference late. You shouldn't have to learn it at all.*

Iri closed their eyes. Beneath their ribs, the ember pulsed — but softer now, slower, falling into rhythm with Coral's breathing beside them, two clocks gradually agreeing on the hour. And in that half-dream state between waking and sleep, Iri felt something they had never felt before: the ember *reaching*. Gently, tentatively, the way a cat tests a new lap. Reaching toward Coral, toward the blood in her that had held the door, and Coral's body answering without her willing it, warmth meeting warmth, lock acknowledging key.

*It likes you,* Iri murmured, nearly asleep. *The ember. It knows you.*

*Hm,* said Coral, in the tone of a woman filing something away to be afraid of later. *Sleep, little ember-bearer. Tomorrow Maren feeds us until we can't walk, remember.*

But Iri was already drifting, and the last thing they felt was Coral's hand still resting on their hair, unmoving, guarding, and the ember humming its low contented note, and the dream, when it finally came, was not of fire at all. It was of a garden. A garden full of white flowers that sang instead of blooming, and a voice — not the woman's voice, a different one, deeper, older, worn smooth like river stone — saying a single word over and over, patient as rain:

*Iri. Iri. Iri.*

* * *

Morning came grey and smelling of snow. Iri woke alone in the loft, wrapped in both blankets, and descended the ladder to find the barn's main room already loud with life: Maren at the hearth, Coral at the table, and between them a third figure Iri had not met — a traveler, road-worn, mud to the knees, a courier's satchel slung across their chest. The stranger was speaking low and fast, and Coral's face, as Iri came down the stairs, was doing something terrible and still.

*—three more shrines pulled down between here and the river ford,* the courier was saying. *Grey riders at every crossroads. And they're asking questions in the villages now, proper questions, with sketches.* The courier drew a folded paper from their coat and laid it on the table. *Sketches of a child.*

Maren crossed herself in the old way, thumb to sternum, the gesture of the silenced faith. Coral picked up the paper. Turned it toward the light.

Iri did not need to see it. They knew what it showed. They knew from the way Coral's hand tightened, from the way the ember behind their ribs suddenly woke and pressed upward against their breastbone like a bird testing a cage — hot, urgent, *loud*, louder than it had ever been, as though it recognized what was drawn on that paper and wanted, desperately, to be anywhere else.

*Coral,* Iri said quietly. *We have to run again, don't we.*

Coral looked up at them across the room, and her eyes went first to their chest — to the glow bleeding through their shirt — and Iri saw her understand, in the same instant, exactly what the courier's news meant and what the ember's sudden brightness meant, and that these were the same fact wearing two faces.

*No,* Coral said, folding the sketch closed with deliberate calm. *This time, little one — this time we don't run. This time we go find the people who've been hiding all these years. Because if the Court is searching this hard—* she tapped the folded paper once, twice, *—then they're afraid of what you are. And I want to meet everyone else who's been afraid right back.*

Behind her, unnoticed, Maren touched her thumb to her own sternum and whispered a word in the First Tongue — a word Iri had never heard spoken aloud, a word that made the ember flare like a struck match.

A greeting. A very old one.

*The heart wakes,* Maren whispered. *Praise the silence, the heart wakes.*
