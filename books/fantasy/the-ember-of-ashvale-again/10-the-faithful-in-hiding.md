## 10. The Faithful in Hiding

The mountain did not want us to find it.

For three days after leaving Maren's shrine, the path climbed through pine and fog, and every mile felt like walking against a current I could not see. Iri stumbled twice on the scree and caught themselves both times with a grace that had nothing to do with their body — the ember in their chest flaring faint gold beneath their ribs, steadying them like a hand at the small of the back. My own body had no such courtesy. The magic that had flooded me at the border had settled into my bones by now, but it sat there strangely, like water in a cracked jar, always threatening to leak.

"You're limping," Iri said.

"I'm walking efficiently."

"You're limping *efficiently*."

I almost smiled. Almost. The child had learned my rhythms well enough to play them back to me, and there were moments — rare, treacherous moments — when it felt less like traveling with a burden and more like traveling with a companion. I did not let myself dwell on those moments. Companions could be lost. Burdens could be set down. I knew which grief I could survive.

Maren's directions had been a riddle wrapped in a blessing: *"Follow the cold river until it forgets its name, then climb where the cairns have no heads."* We found the river on the second day, a grey ribbon of meltwater chattering over stones, and we found the cairns on the third — stacks of river rock, each one deliberately toppled, each head-stone removed and buried somewhere out of sight. To anyone following, they would look like erosion, like accident. To someone who knew to look, they were a door marked from the inside.

"Who breaks their own trail markers?" Iri asked, breathless, hands on knees.

"People who have spent nineteen years being hunted," I said. "You don't leave a signpost for the Cinder Court. You leave a scar only family can read."

Iri was quiet for a moment. Then: "Do you think they'll read you as family?"

I didn't answer. The question sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

We climbed past the last headless cairn as the light went amber and long, and the fog thickened until the world shrank to ten paces in any direction. Then, without warning, the fog ended — cut off as cleanly as if a knife had drawn a line across the mountainside — and we stood at the mouth of a valley within the valley: a bowl of terraced gardens and slate-roofed buildings built into the rock itself, smoke rising from a dozen chimneys, laundry strung between eaves, goats bleating somewhere below. Terraces of barley and root vegetables stepped down the slope in green-gold bands. It looked, impossibly, like a village that had never known fear.

And around its whole perimeter, where the fog should have continued, stood a wall of standing stones — twelve of them, taller than a man, each carved with spirals and running script that made my teeth ache to look at. The same script Iri spoke. The same script that burned across my oath-glyphs when the child touched them.

"They hid a whole community inside an illusion," I murmured. "Nineteen years."

"It's not an illusion," Iri said softly. Their eyes had gone distant, unfocused, the way they did when the ember spoke to things I couldn't hear. "It's a *memory*. The stones remember being invisible, so they are. Ashvale taught them that, before—" They stopped. Swallowed. "Before."

Before the silencing. Before the burning library. Before a woman with my mother's face had told a child to run.

We had taken perhaps twenty steps into the open when the village noticed us.

They came out of doors and off terraces without panic, which frightened me more than shouting would have. A woman with flour to her elbows set down her basket. An old man leaned his hoe against a fence. Children peered from behind skirts. And in the center of the lane, a broad-shouldered man with a grey beard braided into two ropes — I would learn he was called Davor, and that he kept what passed for order here — looked first at Iri, then at me, and his face did something complicated and terrible.

He looked at me the way Maren had. Like a ghost walking.

"The lock," he said. Not unkindly. Not kindly either. Just the way a man names a thing he has waited nineteen years to name. "She has the lock-blood."

* * *

They fed us before they questioned us. I noted this, catalogued it, turned it over with a mercenary's suspicion even as some starved part of me drank it in like rain. Barley soup with wild thyme, dark bread still warm, goat cheese, honey. Iri ate like a small storm. I ate slowly, watching the room.

The hall they brought us to was low-beamed and firelit, and every surface carried evidence of a faith practiced in hiding. No grand shrine, no idol — those would have been burned long ago, and besides, I was learning that Ashvale's faithful had never needed idols. Instead there were small things, domestic things: a whetstone hung above the hearth with a spiral scratched into its side. A lantern in the corner that burned with a flame the color of banked coals, never needing oil. Rows of wooden bowls, each one carved with a single word in the First Tongue, stacked with the words facing inward, hidden, like prayers folded into pockets.

Davor sat across from me. Beside him, a thin woman named Sefa — the enclave's reader, I gathered, one of perhaps three people here who could still translate the old script fluently — spread a length of linen across the table. On it, in ink gone brown with age, was a genealogy.

"Say your mother's name," Sefa said.

"I never knew it," I said. "I was raised by the Order of the Oathbound. Foundling contract, standard terms. She left me at a waystation when I was three days old with nothing but—"

"With nothing but a blanket," Sefa finished quietly, "and a name stitched into the hem that wasn't yours. A name you were told never to speak aloud."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a dog barked once and fell silent.

"How do you know that?" My voice came out flat. Controlled. Twenty years of discipline holding a door shut with both hands.

"Because we wrote the instruction," Davor said. "Your mother was Veyra Okonkwo. She was our shield-sister. When the Nine came for the embers — when the purges started — she was the one who carried the prison-working. The sealing of the god's heart behind a living lock." He tapped the genealogy with one blunt finger, tracing a line down through generations of names in the First Tongue. "The lock required blood of the faithful. Blood freely given, bound by a spoken word. Your mother gave hers. And then she gave *you*, Coral, because the working demanded the lock be carried forward — hidden, unnamed, sworn to silence without ever knowing it."

"I was a mercenary," I said. "I've been a weapon since I was fourteen years old. You're telling me I was a weapon before that. From birth."

"We're telling you that you were a *door*," Sefa said gently, "and that your mother died making sure no one would find the hinges."

Iri had stopped eating. I could feel the child looking at me, and I could not look back. If I looked back, something in my face would come apart, and I had not yet decided whether I could afford that in front of strangers.

"And now the ember is here," Davor said, nodding toward Iri with a reverence that sat oddly beside the soup and the warm bread. "The last fragment of the god's heart, walking around in a child's chest, and the lock walks beside it. Nineteen years we prayed for a sign. Nineteen years we kept the stones remembering. And the answer comes up our mountain wearing a sellsword's boots." He laughed — short, disbelieving, wet-eyed. "The god has a sense of humor. It's the only thing about them the Court never managed to burn."

* * *

They gave us a room that night — one room, two pallets, a window that looked out over the terraces to where the standing stones held back the fog. Iri fell asleep mid-sentence, the way children do, one hand curled near my sleeve as though making sure I wouldn't evaporate. I sat by the window a long time, listening to the enclave breathe.

It should have felt like peace. That was the thing I couldn't stop examining, turning over like a coin I suspected was clipped. For twenty years I had served a god who never answered, kept oaths whose purpose I never understood, walked contracts alone because belonging was a debt I couldn't calculate. And now here was a valley full of people who said my mother's name like a blessing, who fed me bread and called me family, who had lit lamps for nineteen years waiting for exactly the two travelers who arrived tonight.

Belonging. It smelled like woodsmoke and barley. It sounded like children laughing behind doors. And underneath it, faint as a splinter under skin, I could feel the shape of the price tag.

In the morning, they showed me the heart of it.

* * *

Sefa led us down a stair cut into the living rock, beneath the largest of the standing stones, into a chamber that predated everything above it. Torches of emberlight — coal-red, oilless, the same impossible flame as the lantern upstairs — burned in brackets along the walls. And at the chamber's center, sunk into the floor like a keel into water, lay a slab of black stone veined with threads of dull gold.

The veins were moving. Slowly. Like something breathing under ash.

"This is a root of the prison," Sefa said. "One of seven. Your mother's working anchored the seal in living blood, and the blood drew the prison down into the world's bones. Every root answers to the same key." She turned to me, and her eyes were kind, and kindness made it worse. "The key is a name. Spoken by the lock's own blood, in consent, at the prison-heart."

"Then let's go to the prison-heart," I said. "I'll say the name. Whatever it is. I've fought worse things than a word."

The silence that followed had weight. Even Iri, beside me, went very still.

"Coral." Davor's voice was careful, the voice of a man handling something that might detonate. "The working doesn't take a word. It takes a life. The lock is living blood — the seal opens when the blood is *spent*. Freely given, fully given. Your mother knew this when she made it. It's why she hid you. It's why she stitched a false name into your blanket and begged us never to come looking." He exhaled. "She hoped you'd live and die a stranger to all of this. A whole life, unlabeled. Free."

Free. I thought of the valley border where my blade went dull. Of the oath-glyphs fading year by year like a tide going out. Of twenty years of hollow devotion to a silent god, and a clause in a contract I'd never read, and a child knocking on my door in the dead season with a language of ashes in their mouth. Some free life. Some unlabeled existence. My mother had built me a cage too — just one with softer bars than the Court's.

"No," Iri said.

It was the first word the child had spoken since we entered the chamber. Their voice cracked on it.

"No," Iri said again, stepping between me and the elders, small shoulders squared, the ember flaring visible through the fabric of their shirt — a gold glow like a coal pressed against cloth. "You're not trading her. I'm the ember. I'm the god's heart. If anyone pays, it's me, and I say *no*, and—" Their voice broke entirely. "I didn't walk into the Cinder Court's stronghold unarmed to watch them feed her to a rock."

"Iri." I put a hand on their shoulder. It trembled under my palm, or maybe my hand trembled; at this point I honestly couldn't tell. "Nobody's deciding anything tonight."

"Someone's deciding *something*," Davor said heavily. "That's the truth none of us can dodge. The roots are failing, Coral. Can't you feel it? You carry the lock-blood; the prison speaks to you even if you don't know how to listen. Each year the seal thins. Each year the Court's stolen fire burns hotter and the world gets crueler. And each year, the risk grows — that the prison cracks on its own, unconsented, unshaped, and what comes out isn't a god but a wildfire with a god's memory." He met my eyes. "We are asking you. Not commanding. Asking. That's more than the Court ever offered anyone. But we are asking."

I looked at the black stone. At the gold veins crawling beneath it, patient as a heartbeat. At the torches that burned without fuel, fed by a faith I was apparently born into and never chose.

I had spent my whole life being used by people who loved me. The Order, with its pretty clauses. My mother, with her merciful silence. Now a valley full of kind faces and warm bread, offering me the one thing I had ever wanted — a place, a people, a name that meant something — with the bill folded discreetly underneath.

And the worst part, the part I would carry into every hour that followed, was this: some traitorous corner of me wanted to say yes. Not because the world needed saving. Because if I gave my life to these people, then I would finally, finally belong to them completely, and no one could ever take the belonging back.

That was not devotion. I knew enough about devotion to recognize its counterfeit.

"I need time," I said.

"Of course," Sefa said, and the gentleness in her voice was the cruelest thing in the room.

* * *

I did not sleep. Near midnight I climbed out onto the terrace above our room and stood among the barley rows under a sky salted with stars, and I let myself feel all of it at once — the warmth of the hall, the weight of the stone, the child asleep below me who had already offered to die in my place, which was the most obscene thing I had ever heard and the purest act of love I had ever witnessed.

Below, in the dark, the standing stones hummed. Very faintly. In the First Tongue.

And I realized, with a lurch, that I could almost understand them — that the words were rising in me unbidden, syllables my mother must have sung over my cradle, syllables that were not a prayer at all but a *name*, my true name, the one stitched nowhere and spoken never, waiting all this time behind my teeth like a held breath.

The stones were singing my name back to me.

And far beneath the mountain, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep — and listened.
