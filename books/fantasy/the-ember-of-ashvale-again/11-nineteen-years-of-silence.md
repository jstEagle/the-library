## 11. Nineteen Years of Silence

The enclave kept its history in a room with no windows.

Iri noticed that first, before the shelves, before the smell of old paper and lamp oil and something older underneath — stone, maybe, or ash that had never been allowed to become anything else. Every other room in the hidden warren beneath the shrine had light somewhere: a lantern, a shaft of grey afternoon through a ventilation crack, the soft glow of Maren's hearthstones. But this room had been built to hold darkness on purpose, and the only illumination was a single candle on the historian's desk, its flame so steady it looked painted there.

"You can sit," the historian said.

Her name was Odile. She was small and folded like a letter someone had read too many times, and her hands — Iri could not stop looking at them — were stained black to the second knuckle, as though she had been reaching into inkwells for sixty years and had stopped bothering to wash. She moved among the shelves with the unhurried certainty of someone who knew where every book had decided to live.

Coral stood by the door. She had not sat. She had not taken off her sword. Iri watched her the way Iri watched everything now — hungrily, cataloguing, because Coral was the only person whose face Iri had learned to read well enough to trust it — and Coral's face right now was doing something complicated. Her jaw was set the way it set before a fight. But her eyes kept moving to the shelves, to the candle, to Odile's ink-black hands, and there was something under the soldier's stillness that looked almost like fear.

"The child should rest," Odile said. "The telling is long."

"I'm not tired." Iri's voice came out smaller than intended. They were still learning which words their mouth would carry and which ones would crumble. Common Tongue sat strangely in them, like shoes borrowed from someone with different feet. "I want to know."

Odile looked at them for a long moment. The candle flame did not flicker, but Iri felt something flicker anyway — inside their chest, where the ember lived, that warm coal of light that had burned there for as long as they could remember anything at all. It turned over in its sleep, the way a dog turns when it hears a familiar footstep.

"So do I," Odile said quietly. "I have waited nineteen years to say these words to someone who could hear them. Forgive an old woman if she takes her time."

She drew a book from the shelf — not a grand one, not a tome bound in iron or clasped with sigils, just a slim volume with a cracked spine and a cover gone soft as cloth from handling. She set it on the desk between the three of them, and did not open it.

"Before the Court," she began, "there were nine hundred shrines in this land. Perhaps more. Ashvale was not a god of thunder or harvest or war. Do you know what our god kept?"

Iri shook their head. Coral, by the door, said nothing, but Iri saw her hand move — a small motion, unconscious, toward the oath-glyphs on her forearm, the faded marks that had burned bright the night Iri first touched them.

"Silence," Odile said. "The good kind. The kind that comes after a battle ends. The kind in a house where grief has finally finished weeping. Ashvale was the god of the quiet after — the stillness where people could put down what they were carrying. Farmers came to the shrines to set down their dead. Soldiers came to set down their wars. And the god took those things, and held them, and asked nothing back except that the silence be kept holy."

"That's why the Court silenced them," Coral said. Her voice was flat, careful. "To take the quiet away."

"No." Odile's ink-stained fingers rested on the closed book. "That is the story we told ourselves for years, because it was simple and it made us angry, and anger keeps you warm. But no, mercenary. The Court did not silence Ashvale out of greed. Not at first." She opened the book at last, and Iri leaned forward despite themselves, expecting words — but the pages held only drawings, charcoal sketches, quick and fierce as if drawn by someone running. A burning city. A figure standing in the fire with arms spread wide. "They silenced Ashvale out of terror. Because the last time our god woke fully — the last time all that gathered silence was released at once —"

She turned the page. The sketch showed the same figure, but now the fire had a shape, and the shape had a hunger, and the little charcoal houses around it were not burning so much as being *unmade*.

"— a continent burned. Three kingdoms. Gone in a season. Not conquered. Erased. The histories call it the Unquieting, and the Cinder Court was founded by the survivors, and their first law was written in the ashes of their children: *the god must never speak again.*"

The ember in Iri's chest went very cold.

* * *

Iri had always known they were strange. That was not a revelation; that was Tuesday. They had woken in the ruins of a library with no name older than a year, no family older than a nightmare, and a voice in their blood that spoke a language no living kingdom used. They had walked out of the ashes of something and into the world with a coal behind their sternum that warmed them when they were frightened and flared when they dreamed.

But hearing it named — *ember* — hearing Odile say it in the same breath as *last one*, was different from knowing it alone in the dark.

"Ashvale saw what they had done," Odile continued. She had begun to pace, slowly, between the shelves, and her voice took on the rhythm of recitation, of a thing told so many times to empty air that it had worn grooves. "Or so we believe. The god woke from the Unquieting diminished and horrified, and understood that the Court was right about one thing: a god who holds every silence ever given cannot be permitted to wake whole. So Ashvale did the only thing left. They broke themselves."

"Broke themselves," Coral repeated. It was not quite a question. Her hand had not left her forearm.

"Into embers." Odile stopped pacing. She looked directly at Iri, and her eyes were wet, and she did not wipe them. "Nine hundred and twelve pieces of divine fire, scattered into mortal keeping. Each one carried to a faithful heart, hidden in flesh where the Court's hunters could not find them without killing the innocent who bore it. Nine hundred and twelve people who agreed to carry a piece of a broken god inside them for the rest of their lives, and to pass it on, or die with it, rather than let the Court gather them."

"And the Court gathered them anyway," Coral said.

"One by one. Over nineteen years." Odile's voice did not waver, but it thinned, stretched like old rope. "They called it culling. They had methods — rites that could draw an ember out without killing the bearer. I will not describe them. The bearers begged for them, in the end. Carrying a fragment of a grieving god is not gentle work. Some of our people went mad. Some burned out from the inside. And some—" she paused, and the candle flame bent, though there was no wind, "—some gave their embers up willingly, believing the Court's promise that containment was mercy. That the world was safer with each one extinguished."

"How many are left?" Iri asked. Their own voice sounded far away.

Odile looked at them. Just looked. And the answer arrived in Iri's chest before it arrived in their ears, the ember turning over and pressing outward, warm and terrible, like a hand pressed flat against the inside of their ribs.

"One," Odile said. "You."

* * *

Afterward — Iri could not have said how long afterward — they found themselves sitting on the floor with their back against a shelf, knees pulled up, breathing. Coral was crouched in front of them. When had she crossed the room? Her hands hovered near Iri's shoulders without landing, the way she always hesitated before touch, as if contact were a contract that needed signing.

"Iri. Look at me."

"I'm the last one." The words tasted like smoke. "Everyone else who was carrying him. They're — he's — most of him is *dead*, isn't it? Or in jars, or in the Court's vaults, or—"

"We don't know," Coral said. "We don't know yet."

"But I'm what's left." Iri heard their voice crack down the middle and hated it, hated being small, hated the way the ember pulsed faster with their fear as if it shared the panic, as if it were *listening*. "I'm not a messenger. I'm not carrying a message. I'm the — the last piece of a god who broke himself up so nobody could use him, and I've been walking around eating other people's food and sleeping in their barns while nine hundred people died for—"

"Hey." Coral's hands landed after all, both of them, firm on Iri's shoulders, and her grip was the grip of someone who had hauled soldiers out of mud and fire and did not know how to hold anything gently except on purpose. "Look at me. You didn't ask for this. None of it. Say it back to me."

"I didn't ask for this."

"Again."

"I didn't *ask* for this." And then, to Iri's horror and something else besides, they were crying — ugly, hiccupping, nine-year-old crying, the kind they had never once allowed in front of anyone since the burning library, since the woman with the soot on her face saying *run*. "Nobody asked me. Nobody asks anybody. Your mother didn't get asked either, did she? Did she?"

The silence that followed had weight. Iri felt it land on Coral like a dropped stone, felt the hands on their shoulders go rigid.

"What," Coral said, very evenly, "did you just say?"

* * *

It was Odile who answered, because Odile had known, because Odile had been waiting for it the way you wait beside a sickbed.

"Sit down, mercenary."

"I'm standing fine."

"Sit. Down." And something in the old historian's voice — something that had commanded novices and buried martyrs and outlived a purge — made Coral sit.

Odile drew a second book from the shelf. This one was newer, its leather still stiff, and when she opened it Iri saw that the pages were filled with a single name, written over and over in different hands, different inks, some careful and some shaking: *Sefa Okonkwo. Sefa Okonkwo. Sefa Okonkwo.*

Coral made a sound Iri had never heard a person make. Not a word. Something older than words.

"Your mother was born to the faithful," Odile said gently. "In this enclave, before the purge drove us underground. Sefa Okonkwo, daughter of the valley, carrier of the ninth ember — no. Forgive me. Carrier of the *key*." She turned the book around so Coral could see the final page, where the repetitions of the name stopped and a single paragraph stood alone, written in Odile's own cramped hand. "When the Court's culling reached the valley, nineteen years ago, the remaining faithful made a desperate choice. They could not hide the last embers forever. So they built a prison instead — a vault in the borderlands where magic dies, the one place the Court's rites could not reach — and sealed the surviving fragments of Ashvale inside it. And a prison needs a lock. And a lock needs a key. And the key they chose—"

"Was her name." Coral's voice was dead level, which Iri had learned was worse than shouting. "My name. My *name* was the lock."

"Your mother's sealing-word was your true name, spoken in the First Tongue. Yes." Odile's ink-stained hands lay flat on the desk, as if holding the book down against a wind. "Sefa died buying time for the rite to finish. The Court's riders were already in the valley. She sealed the prison with your name and died with it in her mouth, and the seal has held for nineteen years because the key has lived — unknowing — on the outside of the door."

Iri watched Coral the way they always watched her, and what they saw was worse than tears. Coral was not crying. Coral was going somewhere inward and closing doors behind her, and Iri recognized the architecture, because Iri had done the same thing in a hundred doorways of their own short life. This was the place where a person went when the ground of their life turned out to be a lid.

"She named me a lock," Coral said. "Before I could walk. Before I could talk. She decided what I was *for* before I existed enough to argue."

"Coral—"

"Don't." Coral stood. The chair scraped. "Don't tell me she had reasons. Don't tell me it was mercy, or necessity, or love. Everyone in this story had reasons. The Court had reasons. The faithful had reasons. My mother had reasons. Nineteen years of people deciding things *for* other people and calling it protection." Her hand found her sword hilt, not drawing it, just holding, the way a drowning man holds a rail. "Did she know? When she left the valley. When she raised me. Did she know what my name was doing?"

"She knew," Odile said softly. "And she hoped you would never need to find out. There is a difference, mercenary, between a cage and a shield. Your mother believed—"

"My mother *believed*." Coral laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound Iri had ever heard, worse than the burning library, worse than Serane Voss's charming voice asking after a child. "Everyone believes. That's the whole problem. That's how you get nine hundred people carrying fire in their chests until the Court digs it out of them. Belief. Reasons. Somebody always knows better than the person whose life it actually is."

The ember in Iri's chest flared — sudden, hot, answering something in the room that Iri did not understand until they felt it on their own skin: the candle flame had risen tall and thin, and the shadows of the books had stretched long across the floor, and the air itself felt *attentive*, the way a forest feels attentive when something large moves through it.

And in that attention, in that vast listening dark, Iri heard — not with their ears, but in the coal behind their sternum, in the wordless place where the god's fragments remembered being whole — a single impression, faint as a footprint in snow:

*grief.*

Not the Court's. Not Odile's. Not even Coral's, though Coral's filled the room like smoke.

Something older. Something that had been watching a girl grow up in a valley for nineteen years, unable to speak, unable to warn her, unable even to say *I am sorry* — because sorry was a word, and words were exactly what had been taken from it.

Iri stood up. Their legs shook. They crossed the floor and did the thing Coral always hesitated to do: they touched her, deliberately, both hands on the arm with the oath-glyphs, and let the ember speak through their palms the only way it could.

Coral went still. The glyphs under Iri's fingers glowed — soft gold, then deep orange, then white — and Iri felt the god's grief pour through the contact like water through a broken dam, nineteen years of it, and felt it land in Coral too, because her breath caught and her eyes went wide and wet and she whispered, in the First Tongue, a word Iri had only ever heard in nightmares:

*"Ashvale."*

The light held for three heartbeats. Then it guttered out, and the candle was just a candle again, and the room was just a room, and Coral stood trembling with a god's apology fading off her skin.

"It's sorry," Iri said. Their voice was steadier than they expected. "It's been sorry the whole time. It couldn't say it. It's been *watching* you your whole life and it couldn't say a single word."

Coral looked down at her forearm, at the glyphs dimming back to their usual faint scar-glow. When she spoke, her voice had changed — not softer, exactly. Recast. Poured into a new shape while it was still molten.

"Nineteen years," she said. "Nineteen years of silence, and everyone who could have ended it decided someone else's life for them." She turned to Odile, and her eyes were dry now, and terrible, and bright. "So here's what happens next, historian. Nobody decides mine. Not the Court. Not the enclave. Not my mother's ghost. If my name opens that prison — then *I* am the one who says it, or doesn't. Me. Understood?"

Odile inclined her head, slow and grave. "Understood."

But it was Maren in the doorway who answered — the old shrine-keeper, breathless, snow melting in her grey hair, her face the color of the candle wax.

"You'd best come quickly," Maren said. "There are riders on the ridge. Grey cloaks. And they're not searching the valley this time."

She swallowed.

"They're coming straight for the enclave."
