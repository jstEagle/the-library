## 13. The Cage of Good Reasons

They took her boots first.

Coral had expected the manacles, the blindfold, the long descent into the Cinder Court's stronghold — a fortress grown rather than built, its walls of black glassy stone that drank torchlight and gave nothing back. She had expected the cell: a hexagon of that same lightless stone, no window, a slot in the door through which meals arrived smelling of ash and rosemary, as if even her food were being softened for her.

She had not expected the boots.

"They'll be returned to you," said the grey-clad attendant, a young woman with careful hands and eyes that would not meet hers. "Serane's orders. You're a guest of the Court."

"I'm a prisoner."

"You're a guest," the girl repeated, with the flat gentleness of someone reciting doctrine, "who is not permitted to leave." And she set the boots neatly by the door, laces tucked in, soles together, like an offering at a shrine.

The door closed. The lock made no sound at all.

* * *

Coral had spent nineteen years learning to be alone without being lonely. It was a discipline, like blade-work or breath-control. You learned to fill silence with inventory: count your supplies, check your edges, rehearse tomorrow's route until sleep came as a kind of forgetting. In the valley, in her narrow house with its herb bundles drying from the rafters, she had been alone the way a drawn bow is alone — coiled, purposeful, aimed at nothing yet.

This cell gave her nothing to aim at.

The silence here was not empty. It was *full*. It pressed against her ears like deep water, and in it she could hear things she had spent two decades refusing to hear: the tick of her own pulse, the whisper of blood moving through old scars, and beneath all of it, faint as a coal buried under winter ash, the ember-song Iri carried — still there, still alive, somewhere above her in this black-glass hive. She could not feel the god. But she could feel the god's last heart, beating in a child's chest, and that was worse. That was a rope thrown into a well you'd told yourself was bottomless.

She sat on the low stone shelf they called a bed and began, because there was nothing else to do, to take inventory.

Not of supplies. Of oaths.

* * *

The first oath had been her grandmother's, though Coral hadn't understood it as an oath then. *Never cross the border, little coal. Nothing out there will love you.* She had been six. She had repeated it back, solemn as scripture, and her grandmother had kissed her hair and called her a good girl, and the words had gone into her like a hook into a fish's jaw — painless going in, impossible to work free.

*Nothing out there will love you.*

She turned the sentence over now, in the dark, the way she'd once turned a suspicious coin over in candlelight, looking for the shave, the false ring, the lie hidden in honest metal.

Her grandmother had loved her. That was true; she would stake what remained of her soul on it. Her grandmother had also lied to her every day of her life — about the border, about the fading glyphs, about why a mercenary's oath-glyphs should burn bright only inside one small valley and nowhere else in all the wide world. And the lies had been made of love the way a cage is made of iron: each bar forged sincerely, each one placed *for your own good*.

For your own good, stay in the valley.
For your own good, take contracts but never questions.
For your own good, don't ask why the god you serve never answers.

Coral pressed her palms against her eyes until sparks bloomed in the blackness, little orange ghosts, and thought: *I have been kept my whole life. Like a lamp. Like a blade. Like something too dangerous or too precious to be allowed a will of its own.*

And the terrible thing — the thing that made her stomach clench like a fist — was how *well* she had fit the keeping. How obediently she had mistaken the bars for bones. A cage you're born into doesn't feel like a cage. It feels like the shape of the world.

* * *

Meals came twice. The slot opened; the ash-and-rosemary smell drifted in; a bowl appeared. Once, a voice beyond the door asked softly, "Is the guest comfortable?" and Coral said nothing, and the voice retreated without offense, as though her silence were simply another courtesy she was entitled to.

On the second day — or what she judged was the second day; the stronghold kept no hours she could read — Serane Voss came.

Coral heard her before she saw her: the particular music of her stride, unhurried, a woman who had never once needed to hurry. Then the door swung open and torchlight poured in like something spilled, and Serane stood in it wearing grey and silver and an expression of genuine, infuriating kindness.

"You haven't eaten the evening meal," Serane said.

"Poison is traditional for guests who won't be leaving."

"We could have killed you on the road." Serane stepped inside — uninvited, unafraid — and leaned against the wall with her arms folded, close enough to talk, far enough to be safe. The door stayed open behind her. A test, or a taunt, or both. "I showed you the burning, Coral. I showed you Ashvale's last waking. A continent, reduced to glass and wind. I don't think you're a fool. So tell me why you're sitting in our cellar instead of agreeing with me."

"Because you want me to agree with you," Coral said. "That's the whole problem. Nobody in this entire wretched story wants me to *decide* anything. They want me to consent. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a person and a key."

Something moved behind Serane's composure — quick, quickly mastered. Regret, maybe. Or recognition.

"The enclave sent you to die," Serane said quietly. "You know that. They dressed it up in reverence — *the lock must be turned, the faithful must give thanks* — but they sent a woman with a name she didn't choose to a door she didn't build, and they called it destiny. We are offering you the truth instead. The vision. The evidence. The burned world, laid out plain. Choose safety. Choose the living millions over one sleeping god and one child who may not even be a child anymore under whatever wears them."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we grieve," Serane said, "and proceed." She pushed off the wall. At the threshold she paused, and did not turn around. "You think I don't understand cages? I was nine years old when the Nine found me. They fed me, trained me, told me I was chosen. It took me twenty years to see the shape of the room." Now she looked back, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "The difference between us, Coral Okonkwo, is that I decided the cage was worth it. Decide something. Anything. The waiting is the cruelest part, and I say that with love."

The door closed. The lock made no sound.

Coral sat in the returning dark and hated her, thoroughly and uselessly, because Serane was right about the waiting.

* * *

So she stopped waiting and started working, the only way she knew how: methodically, edge by edge, the way you strip and clean a weapon you intend to trust with your life.

Every rule. Every certainty. She laid them out in the dark like tools on an oilcloth and examined each one for the shave, the false ring.

*Oaths are sacred.* True — and taught to her by people who broke the largest oath in history when they helped silence a god and then built their lives on the silence. What was her word worth, sworn in a world where the most powerful institution alive had founded itself on a stolen voice?

*A mercenary keeps contract, not conscience.* She had lived by that for nineteen years. It had let her sleep after ugly jobs. But it had also been a mercy she granted herself, a small dark room she could retreat into whenever the work asked too much — and now she saw that the room had a door, and the door had been locked from the outside, and the lock was shaped like her own signature.

*The valley is home.* Home. Where her glyphs faded. Where the god she served went mute at the border like a man struck dumb at his own doorstep. She had called it home the way a bird calls a covered cage home — warm, familiar, and sized exactly wrong.

*I am not like my mother.* This one cut deepest, and she saved it for last, turning it slowly. Her mother, Veyra, who had died sealing a prison with her daughter's name as the key — who had taken Coral's identity, her future, her *self*, and folded it into a lock without asking. Coral had spent her whole life defining herself against a woman she couldn't remember: *my mother sacrificed everything; I survive everything; we are nothing alike.*

But look at her now. Sitting in a cell, doing exactly what her mother had done — treating her own life as material. As mechanism. As the thing that goes into the lock. The enclave wanted her death and the Court wanted her compliance and her mother, dead nineteen years, had wanted her name, and none of them — not one — had ever wanted her *answer*.

That was the revelation, when it finally came, and it came not like lightning but like cold water: slow, total, inescapable.

The Cinder Court and the faithful of Ashvale were the same animal wearing different pelts. Both of them loved the god. Both of them feared him. Both of them had looked at the question *what shall be done about Ashvale?* and answered it the same way: *anything but ask him.* Nineteen years of silence, and in all that time neither side had tried the simplest act of faith in existence — speaking to him. Listening. Risking the answer.

Because asking meant he might say yes. And yes might mean fire. And fire was terrifying.

But silence was also an answer, and they had chosen it for him, and then they had chosen it for her, generation after generation, cage inside cage inside cage — the god caged by the Court, the faithful caged by fear, Coral caged by everyone who had ever said *for your own good* — until the whole world was a nest of locked rooms, and everyone inside each room was absolutely certain they held the key for the people in the next one down.

Coral laughed. It came out cracked and strange in the soundless dark, and she laughed again just to hear it, rough as a whetstone.

*They want my choice made for me,* she thought. *All of them. Every faction, every faith, every frightened, loving, lying hand that has ever guided me. And the joke — the beautiful, obscene joke — is that the lock can't be forced. My mother learned that. The seal doesn't answer to sacrifice or obedience or duty. It answers to consent. Freely given. Which means every single one of them, Court and enclave alike, has spent nineteen years trying to pick a lock that only opens from the inside.*

*Which means,* she thought, and her heartbeat slowed, and steadied, and became for the first time in days entirely her own, *they need me willing. And no power in this black-glass fortress can make me willing. Not torture. Not truth. Not kindness. Not even love.*

*They can kill me. They cannot spend me.*

She lay back on the stone shelf. Somewhere above her, faint as memory, the ember pulsed — Iri, alive, and by the rhythm of it, restless. Afraid. Looking for her.

*Hold on, little coal,* she thought, using her grandmother's endearment deliberately, reclaiming it, sanding the poison off the old word. *I'm coming. Not because anyone ordered it. Not because fate demands it. Because I've decided.*

And decision, she discovered, felt nothing like duty. Duty was heavy and external, a pack strapped on by other hands. Decision was a blade coming off the whetstone — light, keen, singing.

She closed her eyes and began, silently, systematically, to memorize the stronghold. The interval of the meal-slot. The number of footsteps between her door and the stair she'd heard descending during Serane's visit. The direction of air movement — faint, but present, which meant ventilation shafts, which meant the builders of this perfect tomb had needed to breathe.

Let them wait for her answer. Let them believe the waiting was breaking her.

In the dark, smiling at nothing, Coral Okonkwo sharpened herself.

Two days later — or three; time had gone soft as wet paper — the meal-slot opened at the wrong hour, and no bowl came through. Instead, a voice, small and fierce and unmistakable, whispered into the dark:

"Coral? It's me. Maren says the east vent opens onto the cistern gallery, and I brought your boots."
