## 14. Iri Alone

The night they ran from the enclave, the snow had stopped falling and started *listening*.

Iri noticed it first as a hush too complete — the kind of silence that has weight, that presses against the ears like water. Maren moved ahead of them through the pine dark with a lantern shuttered to a coin of amber light, her breath pluming in little ghosts, and every step she took seemed to land on a world holding its breath. Behind them, the enclave's hidden fires were banked to embers. Somewhere beyond the ridge, the Cinder Court's riders were burning their way down the valley road, and the smoke of what they burned smelled wrong — not woodsmoke but something sweeter, like scorched hair, like old prayers.

"Keep to my footprints," Maren whispered without turning around. "The snow remembers who walks it."

Iri did not understand that, then. They understood many things only afterward, in the way of children and of gods.

They went down through the trees where the branches grew low and heavy, and Maren lifted them one by one so they would not spring back and betray the passage. Once a fox crossed their path, red as a struck match, and stopped to look at Iri with eyes like two small verdicts. Iri felt the ember in their chest turn over in its sleep, warm as a hand.

*It knows me,* Iri thought. *The whole night knows me.*

That should have been a comfort. It was mostly a terror.

* * *

They came out of the forest above the river road an hour before dawn, and there Maren stopped, leaning on her staff, and looked at Iri for a long time with her ruined eye and her whole one.

"The cart will meet you at the mill," she said. "Old Tamper's driving. He'll take you north, past the salt marshes, up into the hill country where the Court's writ runs thin. There are people there. Faithful people. You'll be safe."

"Safe." Iri tried the word the way Coral had taught them to try unfamiliar ones — turning it over, testing its edges. It was a word from the common tongue, round and smooth, worn down by use. It did not fit anything inside them. "Coral is not safe."

Maren's face did something complicated. "Coral is captured, child. The Court took her at the ford. By now she's behind walls no army has ever breached, in a fortress built by nine people who have had nineteen years to prepare for exactly this. You cannot help her. You can only give them the second thing they want."

"The second thing?"

"They want the mercenary because she is the lock." Maren crouched, bringing her face level with Iri's, and her breath was bitter with the tea she chewed to stay awake. "But they want *you* because you are the fire. Locks can be studied. Fire can be *taken*. Do you understand? Every hour you spend trying to be brave is an hour you spend walking toward the people who will crack open your chest to get at what's inside it."

Iri looked down at their own hands, small in the grey light, chapped from cold. Inside their chest the ember pulsed, patient, enormous, like a heart that had been waiting nineteen years to beat properly.

"When I remember," Iri said slowly, "the library — before it burned — there was a woman. She put me in a cupboard and she said *run*, and I didn't want to, and she said it again, and her hands were shaking. And I ran. And I have been running ever since. Through kingdoms I don't remember crossing. Through winters. Through years." They looked up. "Everyone tells me to run. Everyone says it is for my own good. But nobody asks what happens to the thing I'm running *from*. It just keeps burning. Everything keeps burning, and everyone keeps telling the smallest person to run away from it."

Maren said nothing. The river muttered below them, breaking its ice in long sighs.

"Coral didn't run," Iri said. "Coral walked *into* the stronghold. For me. She let them take her so I could get out." Their voice cracked on the last words, and they hated it, and they kept going anyway. "If she could do that — a person with no magic, just a sword and a stubborn heart — then how can I do less? I am not only a child. I don't know what I am. But I know I am more than someone who runs."

"You are a child," Maren said fiercely, and gripped Iri's shoulders, and her hands were strong and cracked and gentle all at once. "Whatever else burns in you, you are a child, and children are allowed to live."

"Children are allowed to live," Iri agreed. "That's why I have to go back. So that other children can."

For a moment the old woman simply held them, and Iri let themselves be held, and stored the feeling somewhere deep, the way they stored everything — every kindness, every warmth, hoarded against a hunger they couldn't name.

Then Maren sighed, long and defeated, and stood, and Iri saw that she had already known. Had perhaps known since the moment she'd said *safe* and watched Iri's face.

"Tamper's cart goes north," she said at last. "You are not getting in it, are you."

"No," Iri said.

"Maren's mercy," the old woman swore softly, which Iri had learned was the worst thing she knew how to say. She pressed something into Iri's hands: a bundle of oilcloth, and inside it, a loaf of black bread still warm, a skin of water, and a knife with a bone handle. "Then you'll do it fed, at least. The road south follows the river three days to the Cinder Court's seat at Vhal Cindris. You'll know it by the chimneys. They burn coal day and night, and the smoke never leaves the sky above it — it just hangs there, like the city is apologizing and won't stop." She paused. "There are ways in. The servants' tunnels under the ash-heaps. My sister worked those tunnels, before. I'll draw you the way."

"Why?" Iri asked. "Why help me go toward the thing you fear most?"

Maren smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of grief and iron. "Because I buried a husband and two daughters in the last burning, child, and I promised Ashvale I would never again stand aside while the faithful were taken one at a time. I've kept that promise badly for nineteen years. Let me keep it well once."

* * *

She drew the map in charcoal on the back of an old hymnal page, and she sang Iri one verse of a song in the First Tongue — a walking song, she said, for pilgrims going into danger — and then she stood at the edge of the trees and watched Iri walk south along the frozen river until the grey light swallowed them both.

Iri walked for three days.

They walked through country that grew stranger and sadder the closer they came to the Court's heart. The villages here had a hollowed look, doors nailed shut, windows stuffed with rags. Shrines stood at every crossroads — Ashvale's shrines, or what remained of them. The god's sign, the closed eye within the flame, had been chiseled off every stone, but chisels leave scars, and Iri found they could read the scars. Here a shrine where someone had scratched *WE REMEMBER* beneath the defacement. Here one where fresh bread had been left on the broken altar, secretly, recently, crumbs scattered by birds. Here one where a child's mitten hung from a nail, offered to a god who could not answer, by someone who hoped anyway.

At each shrine, the ember in Iri's chest warmed a little more.

They did not understand this at first. They thought it was only proximity, the fire drawing near to the places where it had once been worshipped. But on the second evening, sheltering in the lee of a shattered wayshrine, Iri laid their palm against the cold stone — the way Coral touched her oath-glyphs when she thought no one saw — and the stone *answered*.

Not much. Not a voice. But a faint heat rose under their hand, and a thread of gold light traced itself along the chisel-scar where the flame-and-eye had been, filling the wound in the stone the way light fills a crack in a wall. And far off — miles off, Iri was somehow sure — someone sat up suddenly in the dark and pressed a hand to their own chest and whispered a name they hadn't dared whisper in nineteen years.

Iri snatched their hand back, heart hammering.

*I woke something,* they thought. *Or someone.*

They slept badly, dreaming of the library again — but this time the dream changed. This time, when the woman shook them and said *run*, Iri turned around in the dream and looked at the fire coming through the doors, and the fire looked back, and it was not angry. It was *afraid*. It reached for them with fingers of gold and shadow, and its mouth shaped a word in the First Tongue that Iri's sleeping mind understood and waking mind lost, and Iri woke with tears frozen on their cheeks and the ember blazing so bright it lit the snow around them like a fallen star.

A fox sat at the edge of the light, watching. Red as a struck match. Waiting, perhaps, to see what they would do.

Iri got up, and ate the last of the bread, and walked on.

* * *

Vhal Cindris announced itself first by smell — coal and hot metal and that sweet wrongness underneath, the smell of the riders' torches — and then by sound, a vast industrial groaning that rolled across the plain like the breathing of some enormous sleeping animal, and finally by sight, and the sight stopped Iri dead on the last ridge for a long, cold hour.

The city sat in a bowl of hills like a fist clenched around something. Its towers were black iron and smoked glass. Its chimneys — hundreds of them, thousands — poured smoke into a sky that had forgotten how to be anything else, a permanent bruised ceiling pressing down on the streets. And at the center, rising out of the smoke like a bone out of ash, stood the Court's stronghold: a spire of white stone that did not match anything else in the city, older than everything around it, beautiful and terrible, its peak lost in the smothered clouds.

Iri knew that spire. Not from maps. From *inside*. The recognition hit them like cold water — they had seen that white stone before, in fragments, in the burning memory that was all they had of their beginning. The stronghold had been built around something. Around a prison. Around, maybe, the place where a god's voice had been cut out of the world.

And Coral was in there.

Iri stood on the ridge and did the arithmetic of fear. Three days of walking had brought them here; three days of walking had also given the Court time to learn what Maren's people already knew — that the escaped child might come back. Guards on the roads. Wardens at the gates. Nine immortal tyrants with nineteen years of stolen power and a fortress designed to hold exactly one prisoner like Iri.

Every sensible part of them — the part that was eleven years old, the part that remembered cupboards and running — screamed to turn around, to find Tamper's cart, to go north into the thin-writ hills and be safe.

But safe was a word that fit nothing inside them.

Iri thought about Coral's hands, quick and competent, teaching them to hold a knife. About Coral lying to Serane Voss with her heart hammering and her face like stone. About Coral at the border, collapsing under the flood of returning magic, crying — the first time Iri had ever seen her cry — and saying, afterwards, gruffly, wiping her face: *Don't you dare tell anyone.* About Coral letting herself be taken at the ford, turning to face the riders with her empty hands raised, buying Iri's escape with the only coin she had left.

*She walked in for me,* Iri thought. *So I walk in for her. That is the whole of the law. That is the First Tongue under all the other tongues.*

They went down toward the city as the false dusk fell, following Maren's charcoal map along the river's frozen margin to where the ash-heaps began — great grey dunes of the Court's refuse, decades deep, steaming faintly even in winter. And here Iri discovered the last thing they needed to discover, the thing that turned their fear into something else entirely.

Because the ash-heaps were full of people.

Not guards. Not wardens. *Workers* — the men and women who shoveled the Court's cinders, faces masked against the filth, moving in the perpetual twilight under the smoke-ceiling like figures in a bad dream. And as Iri passed among the dunes, keeping to the shadows, the ember in their chest reached out — unbidden, uncontrollable, *answering* — and one by one, the workers stopped.

A woman straightened over her shovel and pressed a hand flat to her breastbone, and through the ash-grime on her face her eyes went wide and wet. An old man dropped his load entirely and turned, slow, like a man hearing his name called across twenty years. A boy no older than Iri fell to his knees in the cinders, not in worship — Iri understood that, somehow, it was not worship — but in *recognition*, the way you fall when a door you'd bricked shut swings open.

Along the scarred stones of the heaps, wherever the god's defaced sign had been painted or carved, gold light was rising. Thread after thread of it, tracing wounds, filling cracks. The whole ash-plain began to glow faintly, dim as banked coals, bright enough to see by.

And Iri felt each awakening land in their chest like a step, like a heartbeat, like a bell being rung somewhere very far away by someone very close.

*Oh,* they thought, standing in the middle of the glowing field while the workers stared at them and the smoke churned overhead. *Oh. I am not carrying the fire alone. I never was. It's been asleep in all of them, all this time, waiting.*

The old man was the first to speak. His voice was rust and reverence. "The ember," he breathed. "The last ember. It's *true*. It's come."

"I need to get inside," Iri said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Under the white spire. There are tunnels —"

"The servants' ways," the woman said. "Under the third heap. We shovel past the door every day. They think we don't see it." She pulled off her mask, and her face was fierce and tear-streaked and alive. "We see everything, little lord. We see everything, and we remember."

"I'm not a lord," Iri said. "I'm — " And they stopped, because they genuinely did not know how to finish the sentence, and the woman smiled through her tears as if that were the holiest answer possible.

"You're the god's heart," she said. "Come. Before the watch changes. And walk like you belong, because little lord — tonight, you do."

They led Iri to the third heap, and shifted the ash with their shovels, unhurried, casual, hiding the work in plain sight, and there it was: an iron door set into the earth, unmarked except for a scar where a sign had been chiseled away. The old man put Iri's hand on it, and the door's lock glowed gold and opened like a flower.

Beyond lay darkness, and the smell of coal, and the distant groan of the fortress above.

Iri looked back once. The workers stood in a half-circle around the open door, ash-sifted, ordinary, extraordinary, and behind them the whole ash-plain shimmered with quiet gold, and above it all the white spire rose into the smoke like a needle threaded with storm.

"Tell the mercenary," the boy said, "that the faithful are awake."

Iri nodded. Then they stepped through the door, alone, unarmed, afraid, and ablaze — and the darkness of the Cinder Court closed around the last ember of Ashvale like a mouth, and somewhere high above, in a cell of white stone, Coral Okonkwo lifted her head as the oath-glyphs on her arms began, very softly, to shine.
