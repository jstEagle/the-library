## 16. Storming the Cinder Court

The stronghold of the Cinder Court had been a monastery once. Coral could see its bones beneath the corruption — the graceful arches of the cloister now bricked over, the bell tower crowned with an iron cage instead of a bell, the gardens where monks had grown herbs for healing torn up and replaced with fields of grey ash-wheat that fed no one and grew anyway, in defiance of every law of soil and season.

She stood on the ridge above it in the hour before dawn, and she let herself look at it the way she had once looked at contracts: coldly, professionally, cataloguing walls and gates and guard rotations. It was easier that way. Easier than feeling what she actually felt, which was a fury so large it had stopped being a feeling and become a landscape, something she lived inside, weather and all.

They had taken Iri three days ago. Three days of Maren's hand on her shoulder saying *wait, wait, we must be ready*, three days of the faithful trickling into the hidden camp from shrines and cellars and hillside hollows across half the continent, drawn like moths by the ember-light that had bloomed across the land when Iri walked unarmed through the Court's outer garrison and refused to run. Three days of Coral sharpening her blade against a whetstone until her arm screamed, because if her hands stopped moving she would think, and if she thought she would see Iri's face — small and stubborn and *brave*, gods help them all, brave in exactly the way that got children killed —

"Stop," Maren said quietly beside her. "You'll snap the edge."

Coral looked down. The whetstone was worn to a sliver. Her blade gleamed with an edge fine enough to split a falling hair, and had for two hours.

"They're hurting them," Coral said. Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. She had expected it to shake.

"Perhaps." Maren did not flinch from the word. She was old enough not to lie to comfort anyone. "But Iri is Ashvale's heart, child. And hearts are hard to hurt in any way that matters."

"Hearts stop," Coral said.

Maren said nothing to that. There was nothing to say. Below them, the stronghold's ash-fields glowed faintly in the dark, as though the ground itself remembered burning.

* * *

The assault began with silence, because they were Ashvale's faithful, and silence was their mother tongue.

No war horns. No battle cry. Two hundred people who had spent nineteen years learning to be invisible moved down from the ridge like fog rolling off a mountain, and the first the Court's sentries knew of it was when their own shadows turned against them — the faithful could not work great magic, their god being locked away, but they had spent nineteen years learning the small craft of quiet: how to move without sound, how to unbar a gate, how to put a sleeping man deeper into sleep.

Coral went through the postern gate third, behind Maren and a stonemason named Brek who had helped build half the walls he was now opening. Inside, the courtyard smelled of cinders and lye and, beneath it, something older — incense, maybe, or the ghost of incense, the monastery remembering what it had been.

Then the alarm rose, and silence ended, and the night caught fire.

It was not a battle so much as a flood meeting a wall. The Court's soldiers were well-trained and well-armored and utterly unprepared for enemies who fought like people defending their own home. Because that was what this was, Coral realized, cutting the legs out from under a pikeman and stepping over him without breaking stride. For nineteen years these grey-clad zealots had been the storm. Tonight they were the thing the storm hit.

And Coral — Coral fought like she had never fought in twenty years of mercenary work. Not because her skill was greater tonight; her skill was the same skill, honed on caravan roads and border skirmishes, and it served her as faithfully as it ever had. What was different was the reason. Every contract she had ever taken, she had fought for coin, for reputation, for the grim satisfaction of a job done properly. She had told herself that was enough. She had built a whole life on the belief that duty needed no warmth, that discipline was its own reward.

That life was ash. She could feel it going down with every stroke, the old architecture collapsing, and what burned in its place was simple and terrible and clean:

*Iri is in that tower. I am coming. Nothing between us survives.*

A sergeant in Cinder-grey got a blade against her ribs and she took the cut rather than waste a second parrying, felt it open her side hot and shallow, and drove her pommel into his throat and kept moving. A crossbowman on the wall loosed at her back and a shrine-keeper named Odile — seventy years old, hands like tree roots — stepped into the bolt's path and it shattered against a warding-sign she'd carried folded in her pocket since before Coral was born. The old faith was everywhere tonight, rising like groundwater through cracked stone.

"The tower," Maren shouted across the chaos. "The old bell stair! They'll have Iri at the top — where the cage is!"

Of course. The iron cage on the bell tower. Where a bell should hang, calling people to prayer, they had hung a prison. Coral had spent her whole life in a valley that muffled the divine, and even she understood the symbolism. The Court had never been subtle about what they feared.

She ran for the tower.

* * *

Iri had counted the hours by the light through the slot window, and by the sound of boots on stone, and by the ember in their chest, which had gone strange and quiet since they were brought here — not dimmed, never dimmed, but *muffled*, as though the whole fortress were a hand pressed gently over a mouth.

They had known that feeling before. They had been born knowing it, in whatever way a fragment of a god's heart could be said to be born. This place was built on the same principle as the valley: press down, cover over, keep the fire from breathing.

They had also counted the hours by talking. That was the part the guards hadn't expected, Coral-shaped holes in their interrogation schedule notwithstanding. Iri talked to everyone. To the woman who brought the bread-and-water meals, whose name was Sefa and who flinched the first time Iri said thank you in the First Tongue because the words glowed faintly in the air. To the captain of the tower guard, who had ordered them chained and then couldn't quite meet their eyes. To Serane Voss, twice, through the door of the cell, when she came to look at them with an expression Iri had no name for yet but recognized anyway, because they had seen it in mirrors of the soul: a person standing at the edge of a belief and looking down.

"You're not monsters," Iri had told her through the door, the second time. "I used to think you were. But you're scared. Being scared isn't evil. It's just what happens after."

"After what?" Serane's voice, muffled by ironwood.

"After you do the bad thing to make the fear go away. The fear doesn't go away. You just get used to having done it."

Silence, then. Long silence. Then boots receding, fast, like someone fleeing a room where a truth had been left burning unattended.

Now Iri sat in the dark of the cell with chains on their wrists that were lined with ash-iron — the same dull grey metal as the valley's border stones, the same hungry silence — and listened to the sounds of battle rising through the tower, and did the one thing they could do, which was hope loudly.

And then the door came off its hinges.

Not opened. *Removed*, hurled inward in two pieces, and standing in the wreckage was Coral Okonkwo, bloodied along one side, breathing like a bellows, eyes lit with a fury so pure it looked almost like joy.

"Coral!" Iri was up before the chains finished rattling, and then Coral was there, kneeling, and her hands — quick, precise, mercenary's hands that had done a thousand violent things — were suddenly gentle past all reason, checking their face, their wrists, turning them carefully in the torchlight spilling through the broken door.

"Are you hurt. Look at me. Are you hurt."

"No. They didn't — they mostly just asked questions. Coral, your side—"

"It's nothing." It was visibly not nothing; the grey uniform sleeve was dark and dripping. Coral didn't glance at it. She was already working at the chain-lock with two thin picks produced from somewhere in her boot, fingers steady despite everything. "Maren's below. Half the faithful are in the courtyard. We're leaving."

"I knew you'd come." Iri said it simply, the way they said everything, and Coral's picks slipped for the first time all night.

"Don't," she said roughly. "Don't say it like that. Like it was ever in question. I would have come through the floor of the world for you."

The lock clicked. The chains fell. And Iri, free, did something no one had instructed them to do: they pressed both small palms flat against Coral's wounded side, and closed their eyes, and the ember woke.

Warmth poured through Coral like sunrise through a window — not the searing blaze of the border-crossing, but something gentler, older, a hearth-fire kind of heat. She felt the cut in her side knitting, felt the deep ache of three sleepless nights smoothing out, felt — absurdly, unbearably — *comforted*, the way she hadn't been comforted since her grandmother died.

"You shouldn't spend yourself on me," Coral whispered. "Save it. We're not out yet."

"Ashvale's heart doesn't ration love," Iri said. "That's the whole point of it."

* * *

They made it down four flights of the bell stair before the tower guard found them.

There were eight of them, and they came around the landing in good order, shields up, blades ready, professional soldiers doing a professional job — and at their head, in grey trimmed with white, sword drawn and pointed at the stairs, stood Serane Voss.

Coral put Iri behind her and felt the world narrow to the geometry of the fight. Eight against one, on a narrow stair, with a child to protect. Bad odds. She had taken worse for money. She would take these for free.

"Stand aside," Serane said. Her voice was level. Her sword was not quite steady. "In the name of the Nine, stand aside, Coral Okonkwo."

"The Nine silenced a god," Coral said. "The Nine caged a child. The Nine can stand in line behind everyone else I'm angry at tonight." She lifted her blade. "Last chance to be somewhere else, Voss."

Something crossed Serane's face then — fast, quickly mastered, but Coral saw it, because Coral had spent twenty years reading faces for a living, and what she saw was doubt. Doubt, and beneath it, exhaustion, the bone-deep kind that comes from carrying a certainty too long.

"You don't understand what they contain," Serane said. "What the god *did*. I've seen the records. I've seen—" She stopped. Started again. "If that child's ember wakes fully, the burning begins again. Whole coastlines. I have sworn oaths too, mercenary. Mine say the world stays safe."

"And mine," Coral said, "say my service ends when the god speaks again." She took one step down the stair. "Well. Listen close, Voss. The god is speaking."

For a moment — one long breath, two — Serane Voss stood at the center of her own life and wavered. Coral watched it happen. Watched the sword-point dip a fraction, watched the soldier's eyes flick to Iri peeking around Coral's hip — small, chained-marked wrists, chin up, unafraid — and watched something in the Cinder Court's finest officer crack along a fault line that had been forming since a child spoke kindness to her through a cell door.

Serane did not lower her sword. But she did not swing it.

"Hold," she said to her guards, and her voice cracked on the word like ice on a pond. "Hold position. Do not — hold."

And in that held breath, that terrible suspended instant, the tower shuddered.

Not from the battle below. From above. From the iron cage at the belfry's crown, which had hung empty for nineteen years and was now, impossibly, beginning to glow — a dull red heat spreading through the iron like blood through water, like a brand remembering the forge. The ash-iron chains' muffling weight lifted from Iri's chest all at once, and the ember flared, and every faithful fighter in the stronghold below felt it pass through them like a bell struck once, deep, a note felt in the sternum more than heard.

Iri gasped. Clutched their own chest. And spoke — not in the First Tongue this time, but in plain trade-speech, in a voice that was theirs and yet layered, underneath, with something vast and grieving and awake:

*"The Nine lie. Come and see what they have built in the deep vaults. Come and see what they mean to feed me to."*

The glow in the belfry cage died. The voice withdrew. Iri swayed; Coral caught them.

Serane stared up the stairwell toward the glowing cage, and Coral watched the last of the officer's certainty gutter out of her face like a lamp running dry.

"What," Serane said slowly, "is in the deep vaults?"

"That," Coral said, already moving, Iri's hand locked in hers, "is exactly what we're going to find out."

Behind them, eight guards looked at their hesitating commander, and their commander looked at the empty air where a god's voice had just been, and no one followed.
