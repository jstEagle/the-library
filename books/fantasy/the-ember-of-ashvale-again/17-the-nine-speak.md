## 17. The Nine Speak

The Hall of Nine had no windows, and Serane Voss had always believed this was a kindness.

She had thought it, once, with the earnest conviction of a girl newly sworn: *no windows, so that nothing outside can distract the Nine from their vigil.* Later she had refined the thought into something more sophisticated — *no windows, because what the Nine watch is not outside but within; they keep their eyes on the ember of the world's danger, and daylight would only be noise.* She had built her faith the way masons build arches, stone by fitted stone, each belief bearing the weight of the next. *The Court silences Ashvale to protect the world. The Court watches so that no one else must suffer. The Court is the dam between humanity and the flood.*

She stood now beneath the vaulted dark of that windowless hall, and the arches were coming down one by one, and she could not stop them.

Nine thrones ringed the chamber in a crescent, carved from cinder-black basalt that drank the lamplight rather than reflecting it. Eight were occupied. The ninth — First Seat, the throne of the Voice of the Court — stood empty, as it had for nineteen years, since the night the silencing was done and the god's scream had burned out the lungs and mind of whoever had spoken the rite. Serane had been told, as a novice, that the empty throne was a memorial. A reminder of sacrifice. She had bowed her head to it every morning of her adult life.

Tonight, the eight seated figures did not look like mourners. They looked like men and women waiting at a table for a meal that had been too long in coming.

"Voss." The speaker sat at the crescent's center: Lord Aldous Crane, oldest of them, his voice like dry paper folding. "You have the child's location."

It was not a question, and she answered it anyway, because that was discipline. "The stronghold at Vaelmoor. The mercenary is taken. The child escaped with the shrine-keeper, but the ember flared during the capture — our watchers felt it from three leagues. It will not run far. It wants its other half back." She paused. "I have requested a company of ash-knives to—"

"You will not kill it."

Serane stopped. The words seemed to arrive slightly after the sound, the way thunder follows lightning. "My lord?"

Crane folded his hands atop the armrests of his throne. In the lamplight his knuckles looked like river stones, smooth and old and utterly without warmth. "You will not kill the child, Voss. You will bring it here. Alive. Intact. The ember must be delivered whole."

Something turned over in her chest — not yet alarm, only the first cold touch of it, the way ice forms at the edge of a pond before the freeze comes across. "Whole," she repeated. "Forgive me, my lord, but the doctrine is clear. The embers are fragments of divine fire. Contained, they are manageable. Destroyed, they are—" She recited it the way she had recited it in the novitiate, kneeling on cold stone: "*A mercy to the world and a grief to heaven.*"

"Yes," said Lady Imre Vail, second seat, and there was something in her voice Serane had never heard before. Something almost tender, the way a butcher might be tender with a carcass. "That was the doctrine. Do you know why we wrote it, Voss? Why we let the faithful believe their god's fragments should be snuffed like candles?"

Serane said nothing. Twenty years of training said *wait*. But some new instinct, some animal thing older than her oaths, whispered *run*.

"Because a candle that is snuffed," Vail continued, "is wasted wax. But a candle carried carefully, cupped against the wind, brought home to the hearth—" She smiled. Serane had seen Vail smile perhaps four times in fifteen years, and never once had it reached her eyes. "A candle carried home can be lit again. By other hands."

The silence that followed had texture. Serane felt it press against her skin.

"The silencing," Crane said, in the tone of a man correcting a minor error in a ledger, "was never containment. Containment implies we wished the fire gone. We wished it *ours*. Ashvale was careless with divinity — scattering it across a continent, gifting it to farmers and fishwives, letting any bleeding heart with a prayer draw from the well. Nineteen years ago we gathered what we could. We silenced the well itself. And we have spent those nineteen years doing what the god never had the patience for." His eyes, pale as frost on slate, found hers. "We have been *saving*."

*Saving.* The word landed wrong, and she heard it land wrong, and she understood in that instant that she had always heard it land wrong and had simply refused to notice. Every tithe the Court collected from terrified villages. Every shrine pulled down, every faithful family scattered or worse. She had called it triage. She had called it pruning. She had stood in burning farmyards and told herself the smoke smelled like safety.

"And the last ember," Serane said. Her voice came out level. She was distantly proud of that. "The child."

"Is the harvest," said a man Serane barely knew — Third Seat, Master of Keys, a soft-bodied man with ink-stained fingers who had spent two decades signing death warrants from behind this table. "Eight fragments we recovered in the early years. Eight we hold below this hall, in the deep vault, where they wait. They are not dead, Voss. Fire does not die. It waits to be given shape. Given wick. Given *will*."

He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught his face, and Serane saw — finally, fully, without the mercy of distance — what nineteen years of vigilance had made of these people. Not guardians. Not wardens. Something gaunt and patient and starving.

"One ember remains unclaimed," he said. "One heart still beating somewhere in the world, carrying the last true flame of a god. Bring it to us, and the Nine will do what no god ever dared: we will take all nine into ourselves. All of the divinity. None of the carelessness. No more scattered miracles, no more prayers going unanswered because the god is busy grieving over some drowned village. Order. Permanence. A heaven with *stewardship*."

"We will be gods," Vail said simply, "that actually answer."

And there it was. The whole architecture of Serane's life, laid bare in a single sentence, and it was not an arch at all. It was a gallows, and she had been building it plank by plank since she was nine years old, and they had handed her the nails and called it honor.

She thought of the vision they had shown Coral Okonkwo — the burning continent, the screaming sky, the last waking of Ashvale. She had stood beside Okonkwo while it played, had watched the mercenary's face, had used that horror as a blade: *this is what the god does. This is what we prevent.* And it had been true. It was true. The conflagration had happened; the archives did not lie about that.

But the archives also did not say what the Nine had just said. The archives did not say *we intend to eat the fire*. Somewhere in nineteen years of reports and doctrines and solemn memorials before an empty throne, the story had been rewritten, and she had never once thought to check the original hand.

"Why tell me?" she asked. "If I am to bring the child alive — if this has been the purpose all along — why tell me now, when you have kept me ignorant for twenty years?"

"Because you hesitated," Crane said.

The word fell into her like a stone into a well. She listened for it to hit bottom. It never did.

"In the stronghold," he continued. "Okonkwo in irons, the child within reach of your blade. Our watchers report you had the killing stroke available and you — delayed. Four seconds. Long enough for the shrine-keeper's diversion. Long enough for escape." He rose from his throne, and up close he was smaller than she remembered, a dried husk of a man propped upright by will and stolen years. "Hesitation is doubt, Voss, and doubt is the ember's ally. So we will burn the doubt out. You will see the truth entire. You will carry the child here yourself, and you will stand in this hall when the Nine ascend, and you will understand that everything you suffered — every farmyard, every grave, every hard mercy — was a *down payment*."

He extended his hand. In his palm lay a small object: a sliver of grey glass, veined faintly with red, warm-looking even at a distance.

"A shard of the eighth ember," he said. "Hold it. Let it show you what we were shown. Let it show you what *we* showed the mercenary — and then let it show you the rest. The part we did not show her. The part where the fire is not a disaster to be survived." His smile was gentle, and it was the cruelest thing she had ever seen. "The part where the fire is an inheritance."

Serane took the shard.

She meant, she would tell herself later, to refuse it. To set it on the floor and walk out and ride for Vaelmoor and put her sword through the soft-bodied man's ledger of a heart. But her fingers closed around the glass, because twenty years of obedience is a deeper groove than any single act of rebellion, and the moment her skin touched it, the hall went away.

* * *

She stood on a green coast under a white sun, and the world was on fire.

Not the fires of war — she knew those, had made those, tidy and terrible and bounded by rivers and treaties. This was fire with intent. This was fire as language. The sky itself had caught, great banners of aurora-flame wheeling overhead in colors that had no names, and where the light touched the sea the water did not boil but *bloomed*, coral forests erupting from the shallows, fish leaping in silver sheets through air that sang. On the headland stood a figure wreathed in quiet flame, arms open, and the figure was neither man nor woman nor anything so small, and it was speaking — not words, but weather, not sound but season — and everything it spoke came true.

This was Ashvale awake. This was the god at full strength, before the fear, before the Court, before the silencing. And it was magnificent, and it was terrifying, and Serane understood in one lurching heartbeat why frightened people with power had decided it must never happen again.

Then the vision turned, as Crane had promised it would turn.

The god diminished. The god learning restraint. The god walking among mud-farmers in a borrowed body, healing a child's crooked spine with a touch that cost the god something real — she could see the cost, a flicker of dimming in that inner flame, a candle spending itself. The god weeping over a plague village, giving and giving and giving until there was almost nothing left, and then gathering the remnant of its own fire and splitting it — nine pieces, nine embers, nine hearts entrusted to ordinary people — saying, in the wordless way of the vision: *keep this safe. Keep this kind. I am too large to be careful.*

*Keep this safe,* the god had said. *Keep this kind.*

And the Nine had heard *keep this valuable*.

The vision shifted again, and now she was in the deep vault beneath the hall, though she had never been there. Eight embers floated in a ring of iron and salt, and they were not dormant. They were *dim*. Guttering. Each one wrapped in chains of script that drank from them year by year, decade by decade, siphoning the fire upward into the hall above, into eight bodies seated on basalt thrones. She saw Lord Aldous Crane as he had been, forty years younger, and she saw the fire moving through him like light through dirty water, and she saw what it was buying: not wisdom, not protection, but years. Vigor. The slow transmutation of mortal flesh into something that did not age and did not die and did not, she realized with a nausea that came up from her knees, *care*.

They had already begun. Nineteen years of "vigilance" had been a feast. The doctrine of mercy, the memorials, the solemn bowing before the empty ninth throne — all of it a table setting. And the ninth seat, the seat of the Voice, the seat burned empty by the god's scream—

The vision showed her that too. Showed her that the Ninth had not died containing anything. The Ninth had died because the others had fed him to the ritual first, a trial run, a taste, and the fire had rejected him — had *scorned* him — and burned him hollow from the inside, and the remaining eight had declared him a martyr and used his corpse as a lesson in devotion.

The shard went cold in her hand. The vision released her.

* * *

She was on her knees on the basalt floor. She did not remember falling. The eight watched her from their thrones with expressions of mild satisfaction, the way falconers watch a hawk come back to the glove.

"Now you know," Crane said. "Now there is no doubt left to hesitate upon. Ride at dawn, Voss. Bring us the child, and take your place in the new heaven."

Serane rose. Her body obeyed the way bodies obey soldiers, automatically, while the rest of her stood very still inside and looked at the wreckage.

Twenty years. Farmyards. Graves. A widow in Drenmark whose sons she had taken, whose eyes she still saw when she slept. A shrine-keeper's cottage she had put to the torch because the doctrine required it, and the hymnbook that had lain in the ashes, half-burned, one line still legible: *the god keeps count of every small kindness*. She had told herself all of it was the price of the dam. Of the wall. Of the mercy.

There was no dam. There had never been a dam. There was only a table, and she had been both the servant laying it and — she understood now, with a clarity that felt like a blade sliding between ribs — eventually, inevitably, the meal. Hesitation was doubt, Crane had said. And doubt was punished. And what happened to instruments that dulled?

They were melted down and poured into something sharper.

"My lord," she heard herself say, and her voice was steady, and she hated how steady it was, how well-trained, how much like the girl on the cold novitiate stone. "The child trusts no one. It fled from me once already. If I ride at dawn with a company, it will run to the faithful, and the faithful will hide it as they hid Okonkwo. Blood and delay." She paused, and the lie assembled itself in her mouth from honest parts, which was the only kind of lie that had ever worked on these people. "Let me go alone. Unarmed, or seeming so. The child is a child. It is lonely, and afraid, and it healed a stranger's wound with its own fire — I have read the reports. It cannot refuse a hand held out in the snow. Give me three days. I will bring it to the gates myself, and no one dies, and the ember arrives whole."

Silence. The eight exchanged glances down the length of the crescent, a conversation conducted entirely in the muscles of faces.

"Alone," Crane said at last.

"Alone," Serane agreed. "You taught me hesitation is doubt. Very well — this is me, doubting nothing. Only choosing the surest road."

It was, she reflected, the finest performance of her life, and it worked because every word of it was true except the destination. The child *was* lonely. The child *was* afraid. The child *had* healed a stranger. She would find Iri, and she would kneel in the snow, and she would hold out her hand — and then she would spend whatever remained of her honor buying the child's life with her own, because there was nothing else left in the account. The doctrine was ash. The arches were down. All that stood was a debt she owed to every farmyard and every grave, and a child with a god's heart beating under its ribs, and three days' head start before the Nine grew suspicious.

"Three days," Crane said. "Ride alone. Fail, and we send the ash-knives, and we send them for the child *and* the instrument that dulled."

"As my lords command," Serane Voss said, and bowed — low, formal, the bow of perfect submission — and turned, and walked out of the windowless hall into the corridor beyond, where for the first time in twenty years there were windows, and beyond them the sky was going grey toward dawn, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

Behind her, faint through the basalt doors, she heard the eight begin to speak of the feast to come.

By the time the sun cleared the hills, she had a horse, a plain cloak, and a road east — riding not toward the stronghold, not toward the Court's writ, but toward the last reported flare of emberlight, toward a child in the snow, carrying nothing but a traitor's sword and a debt she intended, at last, to pay.
