## 22. Ember of Ashvale

The first thing Iri learned about being a child was that mornings could be slow.

In the burning library — in the fragments that still surfaced like drowned things rising to the light of a lake — there had been no slow mornings. There had been the woman's hands on their shoulders, the word *run* pressed into them like a seal into wax, and then years of running: through snow, through borderlands where magic thinned to nothing, through the long fever that had carried them at last to a door in a valley where a mercenary with tired eyes had opened it and changed everything.

But this morning, the first morning of the second spring, Iri woke because sunlight was crawling warm across their face through the shutters of the small stone house, and they lay there for a long time simply feeling it. Warmth without fear. Light that asked nothing of them.

Somewhere below, water was being drawn from the well. The rope creaked against the pulley in a rhythm Iri knew by heart now — Coral's rhythm, unhurried, three pulls and a pause, the way she did everything when she thought no one was counting. Down the lane, a hammer rang softly off wood: Tam and the others rebuilding the eastern wall of what was not yet a library but was already, in everyone's mouths, *the library*, as if naming it could make it true faster than mortar could.

And beyond the garden wall, faint as breath, came the sound of someone humming.

Iri knew only one person who hummed. He claimed he had learned it from the bees.

Iri dressed quickly — too quickly, one stocking inside out, and they left it, because no one here would scold them for it, and that was its own small miracle — and went out into the morning.

* * *

The garden sat behind the shrine-house, walled in old grey stone gone soft with moss, and it should not have existed. Everyone said so. The valley's soil had been thin and bitter for nineteen years, starved of anything the god's silence had touched, and even now that magic crossed the border freely again — even now that wildflowers came up in cracks where nothing had grown since Iri's grandmother's grandmother was young — the ground remembered its hunger.

But the god knelt in it anyway, sleeves rolled to the elbow, dirt to his wrists, humming his borrowed bee-song, and things grew for him the way they had never grown for anyone else. Not because he commanded them to. Iri had watched long enough to understand that. They grew because he *asked*, quietly, every day, and because asking was all he had left, and because — Iri suspected — the valley was still so starved for gentleness that even a diminished god's gentleness was enough.

He looked smaller in the spring light than he had the night the prison broke. That night he had risen out of the shattered heart of the mountain like a dawn given a body, tall and terrible and grieving, fire still clinging to him in tatters. Now he was a broad-shouldered man with ash-grey hair going silver at the temples and eyes the color of banked coals, and his hands, when he turned a clod of earth, were just hands. Scarred ones. Mortal-adjacent, Maren called it, in the careful way scholars say things they don't fully believe.

"Iri." He didn't look up, but the warmth in his voice said he'd felt them coming; he always did, some ember-echo between them that neither of them talked about much. "You're up early."

"You're up early," Iri said, dropping cross-legged onto the flat stone by the bean rows. "You're always up early. Gods are supposed to sleep until noon and demand offerings."

"Ah." He sat back on his heels and considered this with grave seriousness, wiping his forehead with the back of a wrist and leaving a smear of dirt across it. "I seem to have misplaced my thunderbolts. And my throne. And most of my divinity, if we're honest. What I have instead is beans." He gestured at the rows with genuine pride. "Forty-one of them have come up. Last year it was thirty. At this rate of divine increase, in eleven years I will be able to feed four families."

Iri laughed — really laughed, the kind that came out before permission, which was another thing they were learning — and the god smiled his slow smile, the one that always made Iri's chest-ember flicker like a candle catching a draft.

It had been strange, at first, calling him *Ashvale*. Stranger still that he'd insisted on it. *A name is a debt,* he'd told Coral once, in that first raw week after the waking, when he'd slept fourteen hours a day and wept at the smell of bread baking. *You paid mine back. Let me carry it plainly.* So he was Ashvale, and he gardened, and he went to the evening gatherings not above the faithful but among them, sitting on the same rough benches, and when people asked him questions about the old days he answered slowly and honestly and often said *I don't remember* in a voice that hurt to hear.

Once — only once — Iri had asked him what it was like, being a god who wasn't one anymore.

He'd been mending a fence. He'd thought about it for the length of three fence posts.

"It's like this," he'd finally said. "For an age, I was the fire, and everything I touched burned or bloomed according to my nature, and I never once got to choose. Now I am mostly a man with a garden." He'd set the next post. "I find I prefer choosing."

Iri understood that better every day.

* * *

Midmorning brought the lesson bell — a real bell now, salvaged from a Cinder Court watchtower and recast by Tam into something softer-voiced — and children came up the lane in a loose, noisy stream. There were more of them every season. Word had traveled, the way word does, down out of the mountains and along the river roads: *a valley where the silent god speaks again, a place where magic comes in like weather, a school where they'll teach you the First Tongue if your blood remembers it.*

Some of the children were faithful-born, grandchildren of families who had hidden their shrines under floorboards for two generations. Some were valley children who had never known anything but the dead border and were drunk on the novelty of a world that answered back. And some, like a wiry girl named Sefa who had arrived in midwinter with frostbitten ears and a chip on her shoulder the size of a hearthstone, were refugees of the Court's collapse — children of clerks and soldiers and minor cinder-priests, carrying names nobody trusted yet.

Coral taught all of them.

Iri slipped into the shrine-yard and took their usual place on the low wall, chin on knees, and watched her work. She stood in the center of the yard in her old mercenary leathers, patched now and softened, oath-glyphs still faintly gold along her forearms — she'd never let Maren's healers fade them entirely, though the oath itself was long dissolved — and twelve children arranged themselves around her in the lopsided circle that was apparently the correct shape for learning.

"Again," Coral said. "From the top. And Sefa, if you use that tone with the First Tongue again, it will bite you. It's a language, not a leash."

Sefa muttered something. Coral's mouth twitched — the almost-smile that Iri had spent a whole winter learning to read, the one that meant *I like you and will never say so* — and then she raised her hand, and the lesson began.

They were working on names today. True names, the kind that had once been seals and locks and keys — taught carefully now, with rules written on slate and drilled like sword-forms, because the world had learned the hard way what a name could do in the wrong mouth. Coral wrote each child's practice-name in the air with one finger, and the glyph hung there glowing soft gold, and the children shaped their own versions beneath it, clumsy and earnest.

Iri watched Coral move among them, correcting a grip here, a vowel there, patient in a way that would have astonished anyone who'd known her in the old days — the weapon-for-hire, the hollow-devoted, the woman who'd kept oaths like walls around a heart. Sometimes, watching her, Iri forgot to breathe. Not because she was different. Because she was the *same* — the same discipline, the same precision, the same fierce guarded care — but pointed now at building instead of enduring.

At midday break, Coral climbed the wall and sat beside Iri without ceremony, the way she always did, shoulder almost touching shoulder. She handed over half an apple without looking, also the way she always did.

"Sefa's improving," Iri said.

"Sefa's furious," Coral corrected. "Improving is what furious does when you give it somewhere to go." She bit her apple. Chewed. The valley light caught the grey at her temples, more of it every month, and the scar along her jaw that the ember had healed but hadn't erased — some scars, Iri had learned, stay because we keep them. "Your turn after noon meal. Vowel work. They listen to you better than me."

"They're scared of you."

"They should be. I'm teaching them words that can unmake prisons." But she said it lightly, and bumped Iri's shoulder with hers, and Iri leaned into the bump and stayed there, and for a while they just sat, eating one apple between them, watching the children chase each other through the shrine-yard grass that had come in green for the first time in living memory.

"Coral," Iri said, after a while.

"Mm."

"The historian's crates came. From the coast. Maren's been cataloguing since dawn." Iri picked at the apple's skin. "There might be... beginnings. First lines. Things the old library started with, before—" Before the burning. Before the run. Before the woman whose face Iri still couldn't hold onto, only her hands, only the word.

Coral was quiet a moment. Then: "You want to write yours."

"I want to know if I'm allowed."

That made her turn. She looked at Iri with those tired, level eyes that missed nothing, that had seen a child walk unarmed into a fortress of enemies, that had consented to wake a god knowing the world might burn for it.

"Iri." Her voice was the low one, the real one, the one she used maybe five times a year. "Nobody in this valley is ever going to ask your permission to exist again. Least of all me. Write whatever you want." A beat. "Write it badly, even. First drafts are allowed to be terrible. It's the second-best thing I know about freedom."

"What's the first?"

She looked out over the yard — at the children, at the open gate, at the border hills far beyond where magic now rolled in like weather, unchecked, unbarred — and something moved behind her eyes that Iri recognized as the ghost of a woman who had once believed belonging was a cage with pretty bars.

"That you get to choose what you belong to," she said. "Every single morning. Go on. I'll mind the vowels."

* * *

The library was half a shell and wholly alive.

Its eastern wall stood new-timbered, smelling of resin and sawdust; its western wall was the old shrine-wall, scorched black in places, left bare on purpose. *So we remember what it cost,* Maren had said, when Tam offered to sand it clean. Books lined two shelves only — rescued things, ransomed things, a few impossible survivors fished from the ruins of the old collection by faithful hands across nineteen silent years — but the floor space in the middle was swept clear and filled with tables, and on the tables waited paper. Stacks and stacks of it. Blank.

Maren was asleep in her chair when Iri came in, spectacles slid down her nose, a catalogue page draped over her chest like a blanket. Iri tiptoed past — though Maren, like Coral, like the god, always seemed to know — and stood in the middle of the empty tables where the afternoon light fell through the high windows in long dusty columns.

The ember stirred.

It did that sometimes now — not the terrifying flares of the early months, the nightmares that healed wounds and cracked flagstones, but a low answering warmth, like a hand resting on the inside of Iri's ribs. Ashvale had explained it once, awkwardly, scratching his ear: *You carry what's left of my heart. Hearts answer. It's what they're for.* Iri was the last ember, the final splinter of a god who had broken himself apart to keep the world safe from himself — and for a long time that truth had sat on Iri like a mountain. *Messenger or heart?* they'd confessed to Coral that night by emberlight, terrified of being either.

Now, standing in the half-built library with dust turning gold around them, Iri found the weight had changed. It wasn't a mountain anymore. It was more like — a seed. Something that wanted, very simply, to grow toward light.

Iri sat down at the center table. Took a sheet of the blank paper. Chose a pen from Maren's careful row, testing three before finding one that fit their hand.

What did a library begin with?

Not power. Not prophecy. Iri thought of the burning library, the fragments, the woman's hands — and then deliberately, gently, set those aside, the way Coral had taught them to set down a weight before lifting a heavier one. Those belonged to the old book. This was the new one.

Iri thought instead of this morning. Sunlight through shutters. A god kneeling in bean rows. An apple split two ways on a warm wall. A valley with its borders open, where magic came in like rain and nobody checked your name at the door.

The pen touched the paper.

*In the year the valley opened,* Iri wrote, in the First Tongue first and then, beneath it, in the trade-tongue, letter by careful letter, *a child woke in a house where the door was never locked, and discovered that the world was larger than fear — and that they were allowed to be in it.*

Their handwriting wobbled. The ink smudged where their sleeve dragged through it. It was, by any honest measure, a terrible first line — too long, too plain, no thunder in it at all.

Iri read it back twice. Then they laughed, quiet so as not to wake Maren, and reached for a second sheet, because Coral was right: first drafts were allowed to be terrible, and there was time — there was so much time, whole seasons of it, years of it, a life of it — to write it better.

Outside, the lesson bell rang for the afternoon session, and children came running up the lane through the green grass, and somewhere beyond the garden wall a former god hummed to his forty-one beans, and the ember in Iri's chest glowed steady and warm as a hearth that would, as far as anyone could tell, burn forever.

Iri dipped the pen, and began again.
