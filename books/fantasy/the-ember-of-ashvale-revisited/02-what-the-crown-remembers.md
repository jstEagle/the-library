## 2. What the Crown Remembers

The girl slept in the loft above the hearth, and in her sleep she spoke a language that had been dead for three hundred years.

Isandro sat below with his back to the stone, whittling a stick he had no use for, listening to Vessic fall from a child's mouth like water from a cracked jar. He knew enough of it to be dangerous. Every storm-caller did—the old rites were written in it, the binding words, the words of unmaking. His grandmother had hummed fragments of it over the wash-basin when she thought no one listened. *Ashvale veth. Ashvale korim.* The ember remembers.

The brand on his shoulder blade woke as she spoke. It did that sometimes—ached in cold weather, flared near lightning—but this was different. This was a slow cooking heat, as if someone had laid a coal against his skin and left it there. He set down the knife and pressed his palm flat against the wall until the wood groaned.

*She's one of them,* said a voice in him that sounded uncomfortably like his grandmother. *You know what she is.*

He didn't want to know. Knowing had cost him everything once already. Ten years ago he had stood in the throne room at Cael Amarth with his gift still smoking off his knuckles and told King Aldric Vane that he would not call storms down on the Vessic border villages, and Aldric—honest Aldric, laughing Aldric, who had once shared a wineskin with him on the palace roof—had looked at him with eyes gone flat as coins and said, *Then you are nothing, Isandro Oyelaran.* The exile brand had been pressed into his shoulder before sundown.

He climbed the ladder quietly. In the loft's dimness the girl lay curled under his spare blanket, frost still melting out of her hair. Her lips moved.

*"Veth-ka orun. Sefi veth-ka."*

His name. She had said his name—or the shape of it, worn down by centuries, *Oyelaran* ground smooth into something older. The brand burned so hot he smelled his own scorching hair.

He crouched beside her. "Girl," he said softly. "Sefi. Wake up."

Her eyes opened. They were gray, the gray of slate before rain, and for one unguarded moment they were not a child's eyes at all. Something looked out of them that had been waiting a very long time.

"The crown remembers you," she said—in common tongue now, but her voice had harmonics in it, two voices braided. "The first time you refused. It has been hungry ever since."

Then she blinked, and she was only a child again, frightened, eight years old maybe, pressing back against the wall. "I'm sorry," she whispered. "I don't—I say things when I sleep. I don't mean them."

Isandro sat down slowly on the edge of the loft. Below them the fire ticked and settled.

"Tell me what you remember," he said. "Before the snow. Before the knights."

She pulled the blanket to her chin. "There were seven of us," she said—and then stopped, frowning, because clearly she didn't understand what she'd just said. "No. I wasn't—there was a garden. A white garden. And men with crowns made us, seven of us, out of—" her hands turned over helplessly— "out of songs? Out of the last songs? And they put us in the world like nails in a door, to hold it shut. To hold *him* shut." Her breath went ragged. "And six doors are broken. I felt them break. Like teeth coming out."

"Sefi—"

"He wears the king," she said, and now she was crying without any sound at all, tears sliding down a perfectly still face. "He got inside the crown and he's eating him from the inside, and the king is still awake in there, he's still *screaming*, I can hear him, I've been able to hear him since the night the knights came for me. That's why they're called Hollowed. They gave themselves up to feed it. They're not people anymore, they're just—" she made a gesture at her own chest, hollowing it out— "shells it wears."

Outside, the wind came down off the Thornfell massif and rattled the shutters. Isandro thought of Aldric on the palace roof, passing the wineskin, saying *when I'm king I'll end the tithe*, and thought of those coin-flat eyes in the throne room, and understood, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that both memories were true. That the thing wearing his friend had been wearing him even then. Maybe longer.

"How do we kill it?" he asked.

"You don't." She said it simply, the way children say terrible things. "It eats killing. You have to take the king out of its mouth. There's a rite. Under the mountain. But it costs—" and here her face closed, some knowledge older than she was sealing itself away behind her eyes— "it costs more than I think you'll pay."

* * *

They walked south through the high passes for four days, following routes Isandro had used for a decade to move untaxed iron and fugitive flesh beneath the court's nose. Sefi learned to walk in snowshoes. She learned which berries lied. She asked questions constantly—about storms, about the brand, about whether the sea really had no far side—and Isandro found himself answering, and found, uneasily, that he didn't mind.

On the fifth day they reached the waystation at Cold Kettle Gap: a turf-roofed hovel where Bren the ferryman kept a stove, a stock of sour beer, and the only honest ledger in the Thornfells. Bren had smuggled with Isandro since the second winter. Bren had carried him, fevered, half a mountain once. Bren owed him nothing and had never acted like it.

Bren took one look at Sefi and his face did something complicated.

"Storms," he said instead of hello. "Coming out of the east. Court weather. You shouldn't be on the passes."

"We're crossing at the Widow's Stair," Isandro said. "Three days, then down into Vessic proper. I need provisions and silence."

Bren poured beer into wooden cups and didn't meet his eye. "Silence is expensive these days."

"Bren."

"They're offering amnesty." The word dropped between them like a stone into a well. "Full pardon. Land. For anyone who brings in the traitor Oyelaran and the child. Dead or alive, though they pay better alive." He finally looked up, and his eyes were wet and miserable. "They burned Toma's farm, Isan. With his family in it. That's what refusal buys now. I've got people."

The stove ticked. Sefi stood very still by the door, watching Bren with those slate-gray eyes.

"I would never ask you to burn," Isandro said quietly.

"No. You'd just ask me to watch you walk past the Hollowed with a walking key to the end of the world, and pretend I didn't see." Bren dragged a hand down his beard. "Go. Take the provisions. Go tonight, before I have to decide what kind of man I am."

Isandro shouldered the pack. At the door he paused. "For what it's worth—"

"Don't." Bren's voice cracked. "Just don't. If you get where you're going, send word it worked. That's all I want. Send word it worked."

They walked out into the blue dusk of the gap. Behind them, faint through the turf walls, they heard Bren begin to sob, and then stop abruptly, the way men do.

Sefi waited until the waystation was a smudge behind them before she spoke. "He's going to tell them anyway."

"I know."

"Aren't you angry?"

Isandro considered it honestly, the way he considered weather. "He loves people who are still alive. That's not betrayal. That's arithmetic." He adjusted the pack straps. "But it means we have perhaps a day before the Hollowed close the Widow's Stair. So we don't go by the Stair."

"Where, then?"

He looked north, to where the peaks of Thornfell stood black against the last light, and beneath them, buried and sealed for three centuries, the vault the first kings had built and the empire had tried to forget.

"Down," he said. "Through the old mine at Grayfall. Into the vault itself."

Sefi's hand found his. Her fingers were freezing. "The rite is there," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"And it costs more than you'll want to pay."

"Yes," Isandro said again, and began to walk, and the brand on his shoulder burned like a lit wick against the coming dark.
