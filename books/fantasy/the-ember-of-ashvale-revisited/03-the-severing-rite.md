## 3. The Severing Rite

The vault had been waiting for them.

Isandro felt it the moment they descended the last stair—a hush in the stone, a held breath. The Vessic masons had built Thornfell's roots to remember, and after a thousand years of silence, the walls remembered him too. His brand woke along his ribs, not burning now but *humming*, like a plucked string that refused to go still.

Sefi's small hand found his in the dark. She didn't speak—she hadn't spoken since the betrayal, since Torvin's face had appeared among the king's riders on the pass—but her grip said enough. *I'm here. I'm still here.*

The chamber opened around them like the inside of a bell. Seven alcoves ringed a floor of black glass, and in each alcove stood a pillar carved with figures: seven children, seven storms, seven chains. The first kings' work. Above the far arch, words crawled in the dead tongue, and Isandro read them without wanting to.

*What was bound must be fed. What is fed must be bound.*

"Cozy," he muttered, because his voice might shake otherwise.

Sefi tugged his sleeve and pointed to the center of the floor, where a shallow basin had been cut into the black glass—and where frost was growing *inward*, creeping toward the middle from every direction at once, as if the cold itself were being swallowed.

She signed quickly, hands trembling. *It knows we're here. It's happy.*

*Happy.* Isandro looked at the frost and believed her.

He knelt at the basin and spread Torvin's stolen map—the real one, the one his friend had sold for amnesty, though apparently even traitors kept copies of what they sold. The rite was laid out in Vessic script and diagrams that hurt to look at directly. He'd studied it for three days on the road, by firelight, while Sefi slept against his back. He understood it now. That was the worst part. He understood it completely.

A storm-caller's full gift, poured out through the brand. And a vessel standing in the basin—one who carried the binding in their blood—to hold the door open while the parasite was drawn out of its host and unmade.

A willing vessel.

He looked at Sefi.

She was watching him read, and she was too smart, too old in whatever ancient way she was young, not to understand what he was seeing. She stepped forward before he could stop her and stood at the basin's edge, chin lifted, and made the sign she'd invented on the road for *yes*: fist to heart, then open.

"No," Isandro said.

She signed again, faster. *I was made for this. That's what I am. That's why I exist.*

"I said no." His voice cracked against the vault walls. "You're seven years old. You're a child who likes honey on bread and cries when it thunders, and you are not going to die in a hole under a mountain so I can keep my hands clean."

Her eyes went bright and furious. She signed with terrible precision: *You refused to weaponize your storm. You chose exile over killing. But you'll kill me?*

"That's different."

*How?*

And there it was, the question he'd been running from for ten years, standing in the frost-lit dark wearing a child's face. How? Because she was offering? Because the math was cleaner? Because her life weighed less than a kingdom?

Because he was afraid.

"It's different," he said again, "because I'm asking you to trust me instead."

He pulled off his shirt.

The brand covered half his chest—a storm-sigil seared into living skin the day the court condemned him, designed to hurt, designed to remind. He pressed his palm flat against it and spoke the first word of the rite in Vessic, the language he'd sworn he'd forgotten, the language that had lived in his mouth like a splinter all these years.

The basin lit.

Frost flared white, then gold, then a color with no name, and the chains carved into the pillars began to turn—slowly, grinding, stone remembering its purpose. Isandro felt his gift answer the call, the storm that lived coiled behind his sternum rising up his throat like water finding a breach. It would take all of it. Every drop. The rite didn't ask; it took. When it finished, he would be able to call rain and nothing more. A weather-man. A damp gray shadow of what he'd been.

Half his gift for half a monster. Cheap, if it worked.

Above them, the mountain shuddered.

Dust sifted down through the gold light. Somewhere far up the stair, iron-shod boots were descending, many of them, moving with the wrong rhythm—the Hollowed didn't march, they *poured*. Sefi grabbed his arm, signing frantically: *They're coming. Stop. We have to run.*

"We can't run." He kept his hand on the brand, kept the word burning. "There's nowhere left. This is the last room in the world, little one. So we hold it."

*But you're not finished—*

"I know."

The first of them came through the archway like smoke given armor. Then more. A dozen, two dozen, filling the vault's mouth, their faces smooth as eggs where faces should have been, and at their center walked Captain Maren.

Or what wore Maren.

She had been beautiful the way a blade is beautiful, and some of that remained—the set of her shoulders, the scar through her left eyebrow he'd kissed a hundred times. But the right side of her face had gone translucent, and behind the skin something moved, patient and vast, like a fish beneath ice. Her eyes were still hers. That was the cruelest part. She saw him, and she knew him, and whatever rode her let her see.

"Isandro." Her voice was gravel and honey. "Ten years, and this is where you die. Under a mountain. With a child."

"Maren." He didn't stop the rite. Words of Vessic kept falling from his mouth, and the light kept climbing his arms. "You came yourself. I'm flattered."

"The king sends his regards." She smiled, and the smile split too wide. "He remembers you. He remembers everyone who ever refused him. You should have said yes, love. Look what saying no bought you."

"It bought me ten years of quiet," Isandro said. "It bought me her." He nodded at Sefi, who stood between him and the Hollowed with a smuggler's knife clutched in both fists, absurd and magnificent. "It bought me the chance to end you."

*I am older than your ending,* something said through Maren's mouth, three voices braided into one, and the frost on the floor screamed.

The Hollowed charged.

Isandro opened himself to the storm.

It came out of him like a dam failing—not lightning, not here in the deep stone, but pressure, raw and howling, a shockwave of everything he'd hoarded for a decade. Three knights came apart. Four more went down. The pillars blazed, the chains turned faster, and the basin drank from him greedily, pulling the gift out through the brand in long silver threads. He could feel it going—half of himself, the wild half, the half that had once split the sky over Ashvale—and he gave it willingly, furiously, *gladly*, because every thread it took was a thread the thing in the crown couldn't use.

Maren walked through his storm.

She shouldn't have been able to. No one should have. But she was half-consumed already, and what filled her ate power the way he breathed air, and she reached him just as the rite crested—just as the basin filled, just as the final word rose in his throat—

Her sword took him through the shoulder.

White pain. He fell to one knee, blood sheeting hot down his arm, and the rite *stuttered*, the light guttering, and above them the whole mountain groaned like a dying animal.

"Isandro—" Maren's voice, suddenly only her voice, breaking. Her sword arm shook. Behind her eyes, for one instant, he saw her fighting, truly fighting, the thing that wore her. "*Run.*"

"I never run," he gasped. "You remember."

"I remember everything." Tears slid down the translucent half of her face, impossibly, from an eye that wasn't entirely hers anymore. "Finish it. Whatever it costs. Finish it and don't look back."

She raised the sword to strike him down—and hesitated, one heartbeat, two, wrestling herself—

Sefi moved.

The child stepped into the basin.

Light detonated upward through her small body, and the last word tore out of Isandro's throat whether he willed it or not, and the vault became a sun, and somewhere far above them a king sat up on his throne and began, at last, to scream.

* * *

Isandro came back to himself facedown in warm water.

The basin. Except it wasn't cold anymore—it steamed, and the frost was gone, and the black glass floor was veined with cracks that glowed faint gold and were slowly, slowly dimming. The pillars stood dark and ordinary. The Hollowed lay scattered across the vault like dropped puppets, and as he watched, faces bloomed back onto them—dead men's faces, finally at rest.

His shoulder was a ruin. His chest was empty in a way that had nothing to do with wounds. He reached for the storm and found—rain. Rain and fog and the smell of wet earth. A gardener's gift. A gray little talent.

He laughed, and it hurt, and he laughed anyway.

Then he heard breathing, small and ragged, and turned over so fast his vision swam.

Sefi sat in the shallows of the basin, soaked and shaking and alive, *alive*, blinking at the world like a bird newly fledged. He crawled to her and gathered her in, heedless of the shoulder, and she clung to him, and for a long moment neither of them did anything but breathe.

When she finally pulled back, she looked up at him and opened her mouth—and what came out was not Vessic.

It was a child's voice, rusty from disuse, plain and human and shaking:

"Isandro? Why is it snowing in my head?"

He went very still. "What do you remember?"

She frowned, searching. "Warm things. Honey. Your coat smells like smoke. There was a—a door? A big door, and it was closing, and someone kind was holding it open." Her brow furrowed deeper. "Was there words? I think there was words. They're gone now. Like water."

Gone. All of it—the dead tongue, the key, the sevenfold binding—washed clean out of her, paid out like coin. She was just a girl now. Just Sefi.

"Come here," he said, his voice wrecked, and held her again.

Above them, faint through a thousand feet of stone, they heard it begin: bells. Every bell in Thornfell's citadel ringing at once, wild and unnumbered, and beneath the bells, spreading through the mountain like fire through dry grass, the sound of a kingdom waking to discover that its king was dead—and that something else had died with him, something whose name no one would ever speak again because no one would ever remember it.

Except the frost on the stairs, melting in the shape of footprints, walking up toward the light.
