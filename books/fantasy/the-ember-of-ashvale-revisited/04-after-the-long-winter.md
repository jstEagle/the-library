## 4. After the Long Winter

Sefi dreamed in a language she no longer owned.

She knew it the way you know a house you lived in before you could walk—not the words themselves, but the shape of them, the way they had fit around her like a coat. In the dream, someone was singing. The song had no meaning anymore. It was just sound, warm and low, and it made her chest ache in a place that had nothing to do with pain.

She woke in the vault's antechamber with Isandro's cloak over her and the smell of ozone still clinging to the stone. Above, through a crack in the ruined ceiling, snow was falling into the torchlight—slow, unhurried, as if the mountain had all the time in the world now.

The thing beneath the mountain was gone. She knew this the way she knew the dream-language: not with words, but with the absence of weight. For as long as she could remember—and her memory went back further than any child's should—there had been something *under* everything. Under the floorboards of the farmhouse where they'd found her. Under the roads. Under the king's voice when he spoke from his throne and the words came out wrong at the edges.

Now there was just the mountain, holding its own silence.

She sat up. Her head was strange and light, like a room after furniture has been carried out. She tried to reach for the old words—the ones that had burned in her mouth on the road, the ones Isandro had flinched from—and found only smooth blankness where they had been. It didn't frighten her. That surprised her most. It felt less like losing something and more like setting down a load she'd forgotten she was carrying.

Footsteps. Isandro came through the archway, moving carefully, favoring his left side where the brand had been. He looked older. Not worn out—*unburied*, like something that had been underground for ten years and finally stood up into daylight.

He crouched so their eyes were level. His were gray as slate and very tired.

"How do you feel?"

Sefi considered the question honestly. Then she shrugged, and pointed at her own head, and made a face that meant: *empty, but in a good way.*

He almost smiled. "Yeah," he said. "Me too."

He held out his hand, palm up, the way he'd done on the mountain path when the wind got mean. Sefi watched, holding her breath without knowing why.

Nothing happened.

Then—a shimmer, faint as breath on glass. A bead of water gathering out of dry air above his palm. It swelled, trembled, fell. One drop. He stared at it like it was a coin from a country that no longer existed.

"Rain," he said quietly. "I can call rain."

Sefi clapped. Once, twice, small hands slapping together in the cold vault air. It wasn't storms. But the man smiled anyway—a real one, crooked and unpracticed, like a door opening in a wall he'd bricked up himself.

Outside, far to the south, a kingdom was waking up to news that would take years to understand: King Aldric was dead. And the last thing he'd said, witnesses swore, with tears running down a face gone suddenly, terribly human, was *thank you.*

*

Isandro walked because sitting still felt like lying.

They'd made camp in the ruins of a shepherd's hut halfway down Thornfell's southern shoulder, and Sefi was asleep by the fire, curled against the gray mule he'd bought in a village whose name he hadn't bothered to learn. The mule tolerated her with the weary dignity of all mules. Isandro sat at the hut's broken doorway and watched the thaw happen.

It was early for it. The snow was pulling back from the rocks in ragged patches, streams waking up in every gully, the whole mountain groaning and dripping like a ship coming loose from ice. Spring, arriving weeks ahead of itself—as if the season had been waiting, all this time, for permission.

The entity had scattered into the meltwater. Maren had told him that, at the end, half her face already gone soft and hollow, her voice still hers underneath the wrongness. *It goes with the thaw. It will be everywhere and nowhere. Rain, rivers, roots.* She'd asked him—if there was anything left of her when it finished—whether he'd remember her as she was before the court got its hands on her.

He'd said yes. He intended to keep that promise the hard way, which was the only way promises like that get kept.

His hand drifted to his left forearm. The traitor's brand was still there—of course it was; fire doesn't unwrite itself—but the skin around it had changed. Smoothed. As if the storm-gift he'd poured into the rite had taken the brand's heat with it, leaving behind only the scar's history instead of its wound. Ten years the mark had burned whenever Vessic crossed his ears. Now it was just ink and memory.

He thought about Aldric. The real one. In the final moments, when the parasite had been peeled away like rot cut from fruit, the king had surfaced into his own body for perhaps thirty seconds. Thirty seconds to look at Isandro—at the exile he'd signed the order for, the storm-caller who'd refused him and been right—and say, in a voice cracked and small and entirely his own:

*I tried to fight it. I want that written down somewhere. I tried.*

Isandro had held his hand until it stopped. There was no one else to do it. The Hollowed had collapsed into ordinary corpses the moment the severing completed, and Captain Maren had been carried out of the world on the same tide. What remained of the royal guard had found them standing over the king's body, and Isandro had waited for the swords to come out.

Instead, a young lieutenant had knelt. Then another. Then all of them, because soldiers understand grief faster than they understand politics, and whatever else had died in that vault, a king had died free, and men know the difference in their bones even when their lords do not.

The courts would sort it out eventually. There would be inquiries. Someone would remember that Isandro Oyelaran was a branded traitor, and someone else would remember that he'd saved the crown along with the kingdom, and the two facts would grind against each other for years like millstones. Let them. He was done being invisible. That was the strange gift of it—invisibility had never been peace; it had only been a smaller cage. He'd built it himself, plank by careful plank, and called it survival.

The fire popped. Sefi stirred but didn't wake.

He thought about what he'd lost. Half the gift—that was the arithmetic of the rite, and the rite did not negotiate. Enough left to call rain. Enough to coax a storm's mood without ever summoning its teeth. Some men would call that mutilation. Standing there in the doorway of a ruin, watching water run silver down a mountainside that had spent a decade trying to kill him, Isandro found he couldn't.

He had spent ten years refusing to be a weapon. The price had been everything: his name, his home, Maren, the sky itself. And then a mute child had walked out of a snowstorm speaking a dead language, and he'd learned the lesson a decade late—that refusing the weapon was never the same as being free of it. You had to choose what you were. Every day. Out loud.

He was a man who called rain. He was a smuggler's guide with a scarred arm and a bad knee. He was, apparently, the guardian of a girl who had forgotten an empire's worth of secrets and kept only the important things: how to share food, how to sit with the frightened, how to laugh at a mule.

That was enough. That was, he suspected, more than most kings ever got.

Sefi woke near dawn. She padded to the doorway and stood beside him, small and sleep-warm, watching the light come up gold over the thawing valleys below. Somewhere down there, bells were ringing in a town he couldn't see—messengers, mourning, maybe both.

She tugged his sleeve. Pointed at the sunrise. Then at him. Then made a shape with her hands that he understood perfectly, though it wasn't any language at all: *we go?*

"We go," Isandro said.

He shouldered the pack. The mule complained on principle. And the two of them started down the mountain together, into a kingdom learning to grieve its king, toward a spring that had come early to wash the world clean—

—and behind them, high on the white shoulder of Thornfell, where the meltwater ran brightest, the snow shifted once, softly, like something turning over in its sleep.
