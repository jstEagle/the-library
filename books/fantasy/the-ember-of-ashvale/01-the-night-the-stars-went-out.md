## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The fire was small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and Mara Joubert had built it that way on purpose.

She crouched at the mouth of a hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite, three-quarters up the smuggler's pass called the Widow's Tooth, where the pines grew crooked with wind and the trail was nothing but a suggestion — a memory of feet worn into scree. Below her, Ashvale unrolled in the last blue light of evening: the river valleys stitched silver, the market towns like dropped coins, the border stones far off to the north and east glowing faintly amber where the wards ran under the earth like veins of buried fire.

Above her, the stars were coming out.

She had counted them since she was six years old, lying on the flat roof of her mistress's cottage while old Wren Ashfoot smoked her pipe and named them wrong on purpose. *That one's the Shepherd's Crook. That one's the Liar's Eye. And that bright one there is your mother, come back to check if you've been good.* Mara had known even then that the last one was a kindness dressed as a joke, but she had looked for it every night anyway, all these years, without ever admitting it to herself.

Tonight she did not look for it. Tonight she kept her eyes on the fire and let the stars exist at the edge of her vision, uncounted, unnamed, because naming them meant thinking about Wren, and thinking about Wren meant thinking about the cottage she had left four days ago with the smell of fever-sweat still in her nose and an oath half-spoken dying between them like a snuffed wick.

*Swear it, girl. Swear you'll never open it, and never close it.*

*I swear,* she had said, because you said that to dying people. You gave them what they needed to let go.

And then she had run.

Not walked away. Run — out the back door, down through the bean rows, over the wall where the stones knew her hands, onto the Hollow Road before her conscience could catch her hem. Four days now, and her conscience had longer legs than she'd credited. It kept pace. It sat across the fire from her wearing Wren's face, pipe smoke curling from its lips, saying nothing, which was worse than anything it could have said.

The kettle water trembled over the flame. She poured it over the last of the pine-needle tea and wrapped her hands around the tin cup, and for a little while there was only this: the wind combing the high pines, the fire ticking as it ate, the enormous patient dark of the mountains around her like a house she had grown up in.

She was halfway through the cup when the wards began to hum.

She felt it in her teeth first — a low vibration, deep and rhythmic, like a bell struck somewhere under the world. The tea shivered in rings. A pebble at the lip of the hollow chattered against its neighbor. Mara went very still, the way she'd learned to go still when a patrol's lantern swung along a road below her: *don't move, don't breathe, be a stone.* But this wasn't a patrol. This came from everywhere. From below. From the border stones forty miles away, whose amber glow she could see even now, flickering —

Flickering. Like a lamp running out of oil.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice sounded absurdly small, a mouse squeaking inside a cathedral. "No, no —"

The hum climbed. It passed through hearing into something she felt along her spine, in the roots of her hair, in the old scar on her palm that Wren had cut there when she was nine and never explained. The amber light along the borders pulsed once, twice — slow, laborious, a heart failing — and then, all along the rim of the kingdom, at every point of the compass she could see and every point she couldn't, the wards went out.

Not dimmed. *Out.* The way a candle goes out when a hand closes over it.

The silence afterward was the loudest thing Mara had ever heard. It rang. It had texture. She could feel the absence of the wards the way you feel a tooth pulled — a raw socket in the shape of the world, humming with everything that used to live there.

And then the stars went out too.

All of them. At once. Not fading, not clouding over — *blotted*, in a single instant, as though a vast black cloth had been drawn across the sky from horizon to horizon. One heartbeat there were ten thousand stars; the next, none. No moon either. No Shepherd's Crook, no Liar's Eye, no mother-bright point keeping watch over a runaway apprentice who had broken her word.

Mara stood up so fast she spilled the tea across her boot and didn't notice. She stood in the mouth of the hollow with her head tipped back and her mouth open, staring up at a sky that had become a ceiling.

Because that was the horror of it, the thing her mind kept snagging on and refusing to accept: it wasn't empty up there. An empty sky would have been terrible enough, but this was worse. This was *low*. The darkness overhead had weight and nearness, a pressure she could almost feel against her upturned face, like standing at the bottom of a well and looking up at the lid sliding shut. The stars hadn't been hidden behind clouds. They had been replaced — covered by something smooth and absolute and only just above the peaks, close enough that she had the mad, crawling certainty that if she climbed the Widow's Tooth and jumped, her fingers might brush it.

The wind stopped.

Not gentled. *Stopped*, mid-gust, the way a song stops when a door closes. The pines froze mid-shiver. In the sudden dead air, Mara heard her own breath going in and out, ragged, and beneath it — faint, so faint she might have invented it — a sound from beyond the ceiling. A shifting. A settling. Something immense adjusting itself above the roof of the world, the way a sleeper turns over.

Every story she had ever been told by firelight, every warning Wren had ever muttered over her cups and cards, every half-heard fragment of hedge-lore she'd pretended not to memorize — all of it rose in her at once, screaming the same wordless thing: *this is what they sealed away. This is why the Door has keepers.*

"Get down," she whispered to herself. "Get down, get the fire out, get —"

But her body wouldn't obey. She stood in the dead wind with her arms wrapped around herself, shaking, watching the black ceiling, and slowly, horribly, another feeling bloomed underneath the terror. A feeling so shameful she tried to swallow it back down before she even recognized it.

Relief.

It came up her throat like warm water. Because the sky was blind now. The whole kingdom was blind. Whatever had closed over Ashvale had closed over wardens and magistrates and bounty lists and the Crown's men who asked questions at bridges, and it had closed over the past, over the road behind her, over a deathbed in a cottage full of beans and pipe smoke where an old woman's last request hung unfinished in the air like a debt. No one could see her. Not the law, not her ghosts, not the stars her mistress had named wrong on purpose. For the first time since she was six years old, Mara Joubert was truly, perfectly invisible.

She hated herself for the warmth of it. She hugged her arms tighter and let herself shake, and the shaking was terror and relief braided together so tightly she couldn't tell which strand was which, and she suspected she never would.

*You swore an oath and ran from it,* said the silence, in no voice at all. *And now the sky itself has turned its face away, as if the world agrees you shouldn't be seen.*

"That's not how oaths work," she told the darkness, and her voice cracked in the middle, and the darkness didn't answer, which somehow made it worse.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She fed the fire down to embers and then, when the cold became a living thing with teeth, fed it again, sitting with her knees pulled up and her knife loose in its sheath and her eyes going from the black ceiling to the black valleys and back. Once, hours into the dead night, a light moved far below her — a lantern on the river road, hurrying north toward Lowbridge, the flame burning strangely steady, as though even fire wasn't sure the rules still held. Then another. Then a third, and after them a smudge of orange that might have been a burning barn or a signal pyre, and voices carried up the pass on air that had begun, very slowly, to move again — thin shouts, a woman's wail, a dog barking and barking without pause, as though it knew something the people didn't.

By the time the world should have dawned, it didn't.

Mara watched the east for it. She knew the exact color of false dawn over the Thornfell foothills — the grey-pearl, then the bruised rose, then the gold coming up like a tide. She had watched it a thousand mornings from rooftops and tree limbs and the high shelf of this very pass. She watched the east now with her breath held, willing it, begging it, and the east stayed black. The ceiling stayed low. Somewhere below, cocks should have been crowing; instead there was only the dog, still barking, hoarse now, and the creak of pines finding their wind again.

When true morning failed to arrive, something in Mara finished breaking and quietly reset itself. Grief could wait. Terror could wait. She was a creature of the smuggler's roads, and the roads had one law older than any kingdom: *when the world changes, move early, move quiet, and find out what everyone else knows before they know you're asking.*

She kicked dirt over the coals, packed her roll, checked the knife, checked the little pouch of hedge-charms at her belt — salt, ash, a knucklebone, a sprig of rowan gone brown and brittle — and stood at the edge of the hollow looking down into a country she no longer recognized.

Ashvale lay under a darkness deeper than night. Down in the valleys, lights were moving anyway — hundreds of them, thousands, ember-orange points scattered across the black like sparks on a hearthstone, lanterns lit in windows at what should have been dawn, torches carried along roads, bonfires kindled on hills. The whole kingdom glimmering like a banked fire, holding its own small light against the lid that had closed above it.

And on every road leading out of Ashvale — north, east, south, wherever the borders ran — other lights were turning back. She could see them from the height: little processions of lanterns reaching some invisible line and stopping, wheeling, returning the way they'd come. Refugees already, then. The wards were down, but whatever the wards had held *in* — or held *out* — the darkness itself had become the wall. A country sealed like a jar.

Mara's hand found the scar on her palm without her telling it to. She pressed it, hard, the way she had since she was nine.

"The Door," she said quietly, to no one, to the pines, to the teacher she'd abandoned. The words tasted like ash and rowan. "*Never open it, and never close it.*"

Wren had made her swear it with her dying breath, and Mara had run rather than understand it. Now the sky had fallen in, the wards were dead, and somewhere out in that ember-scattered dark, someone or something had pulled the first thread.

Below her, on the winding track that dropped toward Lowbridge, a single rider came up the pass at a pace too steady for panic and too fast for comfort, his lantern hooded, his horse's shoes muffled with sacking. A man who rode like that, at an hour like this, in a kingdom that had just ceased to exist — he wasn't fleeing the dark.

He was looking for someone in it.

Mara stepped back from the edge, into the shadow of the hollow, and became a stone.
