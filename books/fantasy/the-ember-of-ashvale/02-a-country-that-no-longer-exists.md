## 2. A Country That No Longer Exists

Mara came down off the pass the way water comes down a mountain: slowly at first, then all at once, unable to stop.

The Hollow Road unspooled beneath her feet in switchbacks she had known since childhood — the cairn shaped like a sleeping dog, the split oak where smugglers once nailed their tokens, the stream that ran cold enough to numb your ankles if you waded it instead of taking the bridge and paying the bridge-toll. She knew every stone of it. And every stone of it was wrong now, or rather, everything above the stones was wrong: there was no dawn coming from the east, no pale grey promise of morning seeping up over the Thornfell ridgeline. There was only the ceiling.

She kept glancing up as she walked, the way you keep touching a tooth that aches. The dark overhead was not cloud. Cloud moved, breathed, broke apart into shapes and let light through its seams. This was flat. This was *finished*. It hung perhaps three hundred feet above the highest trees, low enough that she could see its edge where it met the mountains to the north, a clean black hem stitched against the rock, and it did not flicker when she threw a pebble at it, though she did throw one, absurdly, just to see. The pebble rose and fell. The ceiling did not care.

*The wards are down,* her mistress had said, hours before she died, gripping Mara's wrist with fingers like dry twigs. *Not broken, girl. Down. There's a difference, and it will matter.* Then she had coughed blood into her rag and told Mara to fetch the blue bottle, and by the time Mara found it — buried under three years of clutter because Sera Vane had never met a shelf she trusted — the old woman's breathing had gone long and rattling, and Mara had done what she always did when a thing became unbearable.

She had run.

Now she was still running, two days down the mountain, and the dark had followed her, or rather she had descended into it, because the dark was not following anyone. It was simply *there*, everywhere inside the borders of Ashvale, and somewhere beyond it — past the walls of night the refugees spoke of — the rest of the world went on with its suns and stars and ordinary mornings, indifferent as weather.

Lowbridge announced itself before she saw it: woodsmoke, tallow, wet wool, and under all of it a thin sour thread of fear-smell that Mara knew from market days when the tax wagons came. The town sat in the crook of the Greywater where the river bent lazy around the mill, and by day it should have been glittering — slate roofs catching light, the mill-pond throwing coins of sun across the weir. Instead the whole town glowed a dull ember-orange from a hundred fires lit out of doors, in yards, in the square, on the bridge itself, because people had decided overnight that roofs were not to be trusted and neither was any darkness deeper than arm's reach.

Mara stopped at the rise above the town and looked at it for a long moment.

Every fire she could see was ringed by people sitting close, shoulder to shoulder, faces lit from below so they all wore the same mask — that hollowed, upward-looking look of folk watching for something to fall out of the sky. Children who should have been asleep were awake; children are always awake when the adults are afraid. And along the river road, coming in from the east in a straggling line, were carts and handcarts and families on foot, borderfolk, hillfolk, people carrying what they could grab, moving toward the firelight like moths with nowhere better to go.

She pulled her hood lower and went down among them.

* * *

Lowbridge's market square had been transformed. The stalls still stood — fishmonger, chandler, the wool-scales — but commerce had curdled into something else. A woman stood on the wool-scales platform shouting herself hoarse about the King's justice and the traitors in their midst, and half the crowd was listening to her and the other half was listening to a gray-bearded man by the well who kept saying, over and over, in a voice gone strange with disbelief:

"I walked to it. I put my hand out. My own hand, and it wouldn't go through. Like pushing into a wall of cold tar. Like the dark had a *skin*."

"Where?" someone demanded. "Where was this?"

"Past Harrow Ford. East road. I was bringing lambs to market." He laughed, a terrible small sound. "I'm still bringing them. They're penned behind my house. What do I do with lambs, in a country with no morning?"

Mara drifted along the edge of the crowd with her shoulders rounded and her face angled down, doing the oldest trick she knew: being nobody. It had never been hard. Small, wiry, hooded, dirt-brown — she had spent seventeen years perfecting the art of not being looked at, first because Sera Vane's hedge-craft made the village cross themselves, later because wardens asked questions about girls traveling alone. Invisibility was a skill like any other, and she had been its master.

It had never occurred to her until now how much it depended on the world staying predictable. When everyone is hunting someone to blame, nobody is invisible. Everyone is a suspect, and suspects get *looked at*.

"—and I say it was the smugglers," the woman on the scales was declaring, jabbing a finger at the crowd. "Who else knows the passes? Who else has been picking at the Crown's roads for a hundred years? You think the wards just *fell*? Somebody opened the door for whatever's up there, and I'll tell you who has keys—"

A murmur ran through the crowd, ugly and warm, the sound of fear finding a shape it could hold. Mara felt it pass over her skin like a change in wind direction.

Smugglers. Of course. It was always the same arithmetic: panic plus ignorance equals whoever is already hated. The Crown had spent forty years trying to strangle the free-trade roads through the Thornfell, and every honest farmer resented the men who paid no toll and no tithe, and here was the sky itself fallen in, and here was an answer ready-made, wearing a hood and knowing the mountain paths too well.

Which meant that Mara Joubert — runaway apprentice, daughter of a smuggler mother she'd never met, keeper of more illegal maps than the Crown had legal ones — was currently the most dangerous kind of person in Ashvale.

She bought nothing. She touched nothing. She turned toward the alley behind the chandler's stall, and that was when a hand closed on her sleeve.

"You. Girl." An old woman, red-eyed, smelling of woodsmoke and terror, gripping hard. "You came down off the mountain. I saw you. Which way did you come?"

"The west pass," Mara said carefully.

"Is it open? Can we get out that way?"

Mara hesitated. Every instinct said lie, disengage, vanish. But the old woman's hand was shaking, and her eyes had the wet shine of someone who had already lost something and was bracing to lose the rest.

"The pass is open," Mara said. "But the dark goes all the way to the peaks. There's no walking out of Ashvale. Not east, not west. I'm sorry."

The hand let go. The old woman's face did something complicated — hope dying without drama, folding up quietly like goods at the end of market day — and she nodded and turned back toward the fire-rings, and Mara stood in the alley feeling the cost of her answer settle into her chest. She had made a family of strangers colder and poorer by one hope. That was the price of truth these days. Everything true cost somebody something.

She needed bread. Bread required trade, and trade required talking, and talking required being someone. She worked the problem the way she worked all problems: sideways.

* * *

The baker's yard sat behind the chandler's row, walled, with a low door and a fire burning in a clay oven whose chimney poked up through the ceiling-dark like a finger pointing at nothing. Mara came over the wall rather than knocking — habit — and dropped into the yard beside a stack of cordwood, where a boy of maybe ten was feeding sticks to the oven mouth.

He didn't scream. He looked at her with the frank, unhorrified stare of children, and said, "You're a smuggler."

"I'm a traveler."

"You came over the wall. Mam says only smugglers come over walls." He held up a stick, considering. "Are the stars really gone everywhere? Or just here?"

"Just here, I think."

"My da says the whole world ended." He said it without much feeling, the way children report catastrophes. "But the hens still laid this morning, so I don't see how."

From inside the house came a man's voice, tired and sharp: "Tam! If you're letting heat out of that oven I'll—"

"It's fine, Da. There's a girl here."

There was a pause, the particular pause of a parent recalculating, and then the baker appeared in the doorway — a broad, flour-white man with the wary set of someone who had spent two days watching his neighbors turn strange. He looked at Mara. He looked at the wall she'd come over. He looked at her again.

"You'll be wanting bread," he said.

"And you'll be wanting something," Mara said. "Nobody bakes extra in a panic. Your yard smells like you've been baking all night."

His jaw tightened. "For the refugees. Whatever I can give away, I give. But my flour won't last the week, and the millers won't grind more while everyone's standing about waiting for the sky to eat us." He crossed his arms. "So either you're a thief, in which case I've a poker, or you're selling something. Which?"

"Road-knowledge," Mara said. "You want grain ground, you can't wait for the millers to grow brave. Send your carts the back way — the Hollow Road, up under Wolfjaw, down to the mill at Stonyford. No tollgate, no crowd, no warden checkpoint. The wardens have all been pulled to the eastern crossings anyway, hunting the dark-wall. Your carts would move quiet and quick."

The baker stared at her. "That road's closed. Has been for years. Landslide took it."

"Landslide took the *official* road. The smugglers' cut's been open the whole time. I walked it yesterday."

Another silence. Inside the house, a second voice — the boy's mother — murmured something urgent, and the baker's eyes flicked backward, then returned to Mara, and she watched him do the arithmetic she'd seen a hundred faces do: *this child is dangerous, but she is useful, and useful wins.*

"How many loaves is the Stonyford route worth?" he asked.

"For me? Three. For the route itself, told plain enough for your carter to follow?" She shrugged. "Six, and a wedge of cheese, and you never saw my face."

"That last part costs nothing."

"It costs the most," Mara said. "Everyone in this town is looking for someone to blame. I'd rather not be found."

Something moved behind the baker's eyes — not quite sympathy, but its cousin. "Fair enough," he said, and meant it, and that was the strangest thing she'd felt in two days: a transaction conducted honestly, in a country that had stopped existing, between two people pretending it hadn't.

She gave him the route in careful pieces — the cairn, the split oak, the ford below the scree, the place where the trail ducked through a hollow too narrow for carts until you knew to turn the axles sideways. He listened like a man memorizing scripture. When she finished, he put six loaves and a cloth-wrapped wedge in her hands and said, quietly, "There's talk in the square. About smugglers breaking the wards. If that's why you're hiding—"

"It isn't," Mara said. "I don't know why the wards fell. But I know the people being blamed didn't do it, because the people being blamed would never break the only roof over their heads. Smugglers live under this sky same as you."

"Do they?" The baker glanced up, at the flat black nothing above his chimney. "Seems to me nobody lives under this sky. Seems to me we're all living in a cellar now, and arguing about who left the door open."

Mara took her bread and went over the wall again, and the boy Tam waved at her, entirely without fear, and she thought: *that one will survive this. Children always assume the world is supposed to be strange.*

* * *

She ate in the shadow of the tithe-barn, alone, and it was there that she heard the refugees properly.

They had gathered near the eastern gate, dozens of them, arranged around a handful of fires, and their talk had the repetitive, circling quality of people processing shock aloud. Mara sat close enough to listen and far enough not to be drawn in, tearing bread into pieces smaller than hunger required, making it last.

"—wall of dark past Harrow Ford, I told you, I put my arm in up to the elbow—"

"—the birds won't fly at it. Geese came south for winter and turned at the tree line like they'd hit glass—"

"—my brother stayed. Said someone had to mind the graves. Said the dead shouldn't be the only ones keeping watch." A pause. "Said it like a joke. It wasn't a joke."

"—the King's proclamation, they read it in Duncarrie square. *By order of the Crown, all movement through the high passes is forbidden. All persons of the smuggling classes are to be detained for questioning regarding sabotage of the border wards.* Sabotage. As if we did it. As if any of us—"

"They need someone to hang," said an older man, flatly. "Sky falls in, folk want a neck. Always been so. My grandfather lived through the Fever Year. They burned three wise-women in Ashvale town before the sickness even reached the valley. Didn't cure anything. But folk felt better."

Mara's hands went still on the bread.

Wise-women. Hedge-witches. Her mistress had survived the purges by being dull, by being careful, by charging a copper for charms against warts and never once letting anyone see the real work — the ward-sense, the deep craft, the knowledge of doors and boundaries that had been passed down witch to witch since before there were kings to outlaw it. *The wards are down,* Sera Vane had said. *Not broken. Down.* And who in all Ashvale even knew there was a difference? Who could the Crown possibly find, in its hungry search for saboteurs, except the ones who understood the wards at all?

Hedge-witches. Smugglers. Runaway apprentices with illegal maps in their heads.

She wrapped the remaining bread with hands that wanted to shake and made them not. Fear was a tool like any other; you didn't let the tool use you. But underneath the discipline, something cold had begun to speak, and it sounded like her mistress's voice: *Girl, when the law starts hunting the ones who understand the door, ask yourself who taught the law to be afraid of doors.*

Across the fire-ring, a young refugee woman was rocking a baby that would not stop crying, and an old man was telling the story of the dark-wall yet again, arm outstretched, elbow-deep in nothing, and the crowd leaned in each time as if this telling might be the one where his hand came out the other side.

Mara stood, brushed crumbs from her knees, and pulled her hood down over her eyes.

She had planned, in the vague way of runaways, to go north — over the Wolfjaw, down to the coast towns, lose herself where nobody knew the name Joubert. But the north was mountain now, and the mountains were under the ceiling, and the ceiling had made every road in Ashvale a trap with teeth at both ends. And the Crown was pulling its wardens to the crossings, which meant the interior roads were thinning of patrols even as the towns thickened with fear.

There was one person in Lowbridge, she realized, who might know what was actually happening — not the rumor of it, the shape of it. A former magistrate, if the gossip in the square was to be believed, disgraced years ago for acquitting a witch, who had taken rooms above the chandler's row and was known to pay good coin for news from the passes. The smugglers called him Justice Vale, half in mockery, half in something else. He'd been asking after travelers who came down from the Thornfell.

Asking after travelers. From the Thornfell.

Someone like her.

Mara stood in the dark under the starless sky, bread warm against her ribs, and weighed it. Every instinct said walk away, take the back lanes, be nobody. But nobody was getting harder to be by the hour, and the baker's honest coin had reminded her that not every transaction in this broken country had to be a trap — and besides, beneath the fear, beneath the cold arithmetic of survival, another voice had started up, quiet and stubborn, wearing her mistress's cadence:

*The wards are down, girl. Not broken. Down. There's a difference, and it will matter. And when it matters, you'll wish you hadn't run.*

She adjusted her hood, checked her knife, and turned toward the chandler's row, toward the rooms above it, toward the disgraced justice who was looking for exactly the kind of person she had spent her whole life trying to be.

Behind her, the fires of Lowbridge burned on against a sky that gave back no light at all, and somewhere in the crowd a child asked, in the bright carrying voice children have, "Will the stars come back?" — and no one answered, and the question floated up into the dark like a spark that would never land.
