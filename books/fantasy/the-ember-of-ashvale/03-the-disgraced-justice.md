## 3. The Disgraced Justice

Corin Vale had spent thirty years learning to read a town the way other men read a ledger, and Lowbridge was telling him something ugly.

He stood in the shadow of the tithe-house archway with his hood drawn and his collar turned up, watching the market square do its slow arithmetic of fear. Three days since the stars had gone out. Three days since the black ceiling had settled over Ashvale like a lid on a pot, low enough that the watch-towers seemed to scrape it, and in that time the town had passed through grief and prayer and arrived — as towns always did — at hunger. Not for bread. The granaries were full enough for a season. They were hungry for someone to blame.

A crier stood on the fountain steps, his voice cracking against the dead air. "By order of the Crown! All persons entering or leaving the district to present themselves at the warden's post! Smugglers' roads are closed by decree! Any found upon them will be taken for saboteurs!"

*Smugglers first,* Corin thought. *It's always smugglers first.* The Crown could not admit that its wards — the great shining boast of four generations of Ashvale kings, the wards every schoolchild learned to thank at morning prayers — might have failed on their own. So the failure had to be human. And who was more human, more convenient, more already-criminal than the men and women who knew the holes in the border better than the wardens did?

The crowd shifted around him like water around a stone. He caught fragments as they passed: a woman saying her cousin had walked east toward the Thornfell passes and come back three days later from the *west*, weeping, insisting he'd never turned; a carter swearing the darkness beyond the fields wasn't night-dark but *other*-dark, a dark you could feel pressing on your eyes like deep water; two boys daring each other to throw a stone at the ceiling, though it hung far too high to hit, and flinching anyway when the stone vanished into it without ever seeming to land.

Corin pulled his hood lower and began to walk.

He had been a justice of the circuits once. Justice Corin Vale, third seat of the Eastern Assizes, a name that had meant something in courtrooms from Greywater to the coast. Now his name meant something else entirely — it meant the magistrate who had acquitted Maren Duskwater, the hedge-witch of Hollowmere, before witnesses, on the record, with reasons given. The first such acquittal in living memory. He had done it because the evidence was rotten and the accusation was land-hunger dressed in piety, and he had said so aloud, which had been the unforgivable part. Not the verdict. The *reasons*. The Crown could survive a merciful judge; it could not survive one who explained himself.

They had stripped his office with great ceremony. They had not, in their haste, remembered to strip him of everything he knew.

And what he knew — what he had spent eleven days and most of his remaining coin confirming — was this: the wards had not failed. They had been *unmade*, carefully, from the inside, by someone who understood them. Someone who had walked the old roads and touched the old stones and unpicked a weaving that took centuries to make. The pattern of the collapse, the order in which the border-stones had gone dark — he had copied the warden reports before he left the capital, bribing a clerk with his last silver signet — described a spiral. A deliberate spiral, turning inward, toward the Thornfell mountains. Toward whatever stood beneath them.

Doors did not open themselves. Someone had a key.

And somewhere in Lowbridge, according to a one-eyed ostler who owed Corin a debt older than either of their disgraces, there was a girl who knew those roads better than any warden alive. A runaway apprentice. A hedge-witch's student, if the ostler was right, which made her exactly the sort of person the Crown would hang first and question never.

Corin intended to find her before they did.

* * *

The river district smelled of tar and wet wool, and it was there, in the second hour after the false noon — the grey dimness that passed for day now, light without source, sun without warmth — that he saw her.

She was trading at the back of a chandler's stall, and she did it wrong for a thief and right for a guide. She didn't haggle over the bread itself; she offered knowledge instead of coin, leaning close, speaking fast and low, and the chandler's boy nodded along with the particular eagerness of a man being told how to move goods that officially did not exist. Corin couldn't hear the words, but he could read the shape of them: *the culvert under the fulling mill runs dry at this hour; the warden patrols change at the bell; don't take the high road, take the drainage path.* A map drawn in whispers.

She was young — twenty, perhaps twenty-two — with road-dust ground so thoroughly into her that it seemed less like dirt than like skin. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, the cut of someone who'd done it herself with a knife and no mirror. A faded green cloak, good boots gone soft at the ankles, and across her back a satchel worn at the strap-ends in the way of something carried for years. She kept her left hand closed around something in her pocket the whole time she talked, thumb moving over it in small circles, an unconscious rhythm, like a prayer bead or a talisman.

And she watched the crowd. Constantly. Not the way the frightened watched — wide-eyed, sweeping — but the way the hunted watched: specific, sequential, cataloguing. Doorways. Rooftops. The warden's post at the end of the lane. She counted the wardens twice while Corin observed her, and when a third patrol appeared earlier than it should have, something in her posture changed — a settling, a readiness, a weight shifting to the balls of her feet — and she finished her transaction in half the time and melted into the alley behind the chandler's yard like smoke finding a draft.

Corin let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

*There you are.*

He gave her a hundred count, then followed. Not quickly. She would know a quick follower; she would know a slow one too, probably, but a slow one at least had a chance of surviving the first test. He kept to the shaded side of the lane, hands visible, gait unhurried, and rehearsed what he would say, because he had learned long ago that with people like this — people the world had taught to run — the first ten words decided everything.

She led him a merry course through Lowbridge's bones. Through the tannery row where the stink covered all sound. Across the roof of a disused boathouse, balancing along the ridge-beam as if it were a garden path. Down a stair cut into the riverbank that Corin was fairly sure did not appear on any surveyor's map, emerging in a hollow beneath the bridge pilings where the mud lay thick and the river muttered against stone. By the time she stopped, Corin had lost track of the turns, and he suspected that was the point. She wanted him lost. She wanted him to understand that she could always be more lost than he could follow.

He stopped at the edge of the hollow, well within her reach and making no move to close it, and pushed back his hood.

She had a knife out before his face was fully bared. A short blade, held low and reversed, a working grip, not a duelist's flourish. Her eyes went over him the way she'd gone over the crowd — specific, sequential — and he watched her read him: the travel-worn coat that had once been fine, the ink stains still ghosting his fingers no matter how he scrubbed, the missing signet's pale band of skin on his right hand where a ring had sat for twenty years. He watched her arrive at a conclusion and brace herself.

"You're Vale," she said. "The fallen justice."

"Not fallen," Corin said. "Demoted. There's a difference. Fallen implies I hit the ground."

"The whole kingdom knows your face." The knife didn't waver. "They posted it in every market from here to the coast. *The witch-friend.* That's what they call you."

"It's not the worst thing I've been called from a bench." He kept his voice level, almost dry, and watched her eyes narrow slightly — recalculating. Good. Fear made people stupid; irritation made them think. "You have me at a disadvantage. You know my name and I don't know yours."

"Good," she said.

"Fair enough." He spread his hands slowly, showing them empty. "I'm going to say my piece, and then you can put that knife away or use it, and either way I'll consider the conversation concluded honestly. May I?"

Her jaw tightened. But she nodded once, sharp, and Corin chose his words the way he had once chosen them for juries — knowing that every sentence was a door that could only open outward.

"Three nights ago the stars went out. Four days before that, the border-stone at Cael's Crossing went dark — quietly, no storm, no surge, just dark — and the wardens logged it as a maintenance fault because logging it as anything else would have required them to think. Then the next stone. Then the next. In a spiral, turning inward." He paused. "I've seen the reports. I copied them before I left the capital, which is why I am now, technically, a fugitive as well as a disgrace. Two crimes instead of one. I find I mind less than expected."

"Get to the point."

"The wards weren't broken by accident or by force. They were *unpicked*. From inside. By someone who knew the weave." He let that sit in the cold air between them. "The Crown is hanging smugglers for it. Random ones, wherever they can catch them, to make the panic quiet down. Which means that right now, in this kingdom, the single most dangerous thing a person can be is someone who knows the hidden roads. Someone trained in the old craft. Someone, say, apprenticed to a hedge-witch."

The knife came up an inch. "You're threatening me."

"I'm describing you," Corin said. "There's a difference. I seem to be making a lot of distinctions today." He tilted his head toward the bridge above, where boots crossed and recrossed, unseen. "The wardens are looking for a runaway apprentice of Ashvale. Alive, per the order — which should frighten you more than a kill order does, because things are only wanted alive when someone wants what's in their blood or their head. I am not with the wardens. I have not been with the law since the day they took my office and thanked me for my service. What I am is a man who believes the truth about that door—" he gestured upward, at the black ceiling invisible beyond the bridge but present everywhere, a pressure at the edge of sight, "—is going to die in a courtroom or a ditch unless someone gets to the bottom of it first. And getting to the bottom of it means going where I cannot go alone. Into the Thornfell. Along roads that officially do not exist, traveled by people who officially do not exist, to a place the Crown has spent four generations pretending isn't there."

Silence, except the river. She hadn't moved, hadn't blinked much. Under the dim sourceless light her eyes were a color he couldn't fix — brown, grey, something between, changing with the angle like weather.

"And what do I get," she said finally, "for leading a disgraced magistrate into the mountains? Besides hanged. Hanged is included free."

"Freedom," Corin said. "Actual freedom. Not the kind you have now."

Something flickered across her face — quick, quickly buried, but he'd spent thirty years reading faces across a bench, faces trying not to show what they felt, and he caught it. The flicker of a person hearing a truth they'd already told themselves.

"You hide well," he continued, quieter now. "Better than anyone I've tracked. But hiding isn't freedom. It's a smaller cage with better locks. You trade knowledge for bread because coin can be traced. You sleep in a different ruin every night. You keep your hand closed around—" his eyes flicked to her pocket, and her whole body stiffened, "—whatever you carry, because it's the last thing you own that's truly yours. I know the shape of that life. I've lived a version of it for two years. And I'm offering you the one thing neither of us has had since the law finished with us: a purpose that pays, a pardon signed and sealed when this is done — I still have friends enough to get paper through, even now — and my silence about who you are, permanent and total, sworn on whatever you like. My oath, my name, my daughter's memory. Whatever carries weight for you."

At *daughter* something moved in her again — not softness, he noted, but recognition. The look of someone cataloguing another person's wound and adding it to the account.

"Why?" she asked. It came out flat, but flatness was effortful; he could hear the effort. "Why does a broken judge care what's behind the stars? You got acquitted of caring. It cost you everything. Why spend what's left?"

And there it was — the real question, the one under all the others, and Corin found he had no clever answer prepared, only the true one, which was worse and better.

"Because I sat in a courtroom for nineteen years and told myself the law was a lamp," he said. "That it lit the dark places and made them safe. And then I watched that same law burn an innocent woman on testimony bought with a land deed, and when I said so, it turned the lamp on *me*. And now the actual dark has come down over everything, and the law's answer is to hang smugglers in the square and call it justice." His voice stayed steady, but he heard the ember under it, the old heat. "I don't care about the Crown's wards. I care that something was done *deliberately*, that someone knows what and why, and that the machinery built to find out is instead hunting the easiest targets it can find. That is not justice. That is fear wearing justice's robes. And I have spent my whole life unable to tell the difference, and I am done with it."

The knife lowered. Not sheathed — lowered, the way a guard dog stops barking but keeps watching.

"You talk like a book," she said.

"I was a book for thirty years. I'm trying to learn to be a road."

That surprised a sound out of her — half a breath, half a laugh, immediately strangled, as if laughter were another thing that could be traced. She looked at him for a long moment, and Corin let himself be looked at. It was a strange sensation, being read by someone with no interest in mercy or malice, only accuracy. He felt weighed. He suspected he weighed less than he used to.

"They'll come for you too, you know," she said. "Once you're seen with me. Whatever's left of your name — it'll burn."

"It's ash already," Corin said. "Ash holds heat longer than wood."

Another flicker. This one almost a smile, killed even faster than the laugh. She turned the knife in her fingers once, absently, a habit, and he understood that the blade was about to go away.

"If I say no," she said, "you'll follow me anyway."

"Yes," Corin admitted. "But badly. You'd lose me by sundown and we'd both pretend otherwise."

"And if I say yes?"

"Then I pay for passage, provisions, and silence, and you get me to the Thornfell alive, and everything after that we invent as we go." He paused. "I should warn you — I'm stubborn, I ask questions, and I don't leave people behind. Those three traits ended my career. They may also end our lives. Consider the terms carefully."

For the first time she looked past him — up, at the underside of the bridge, at the black ceiling beyond it, at the whole sealed weight of the sky. And Corin watched her look at it and recognized the expression, because he'd seen it in his own reflection that first morning: terror braided with something shameful and unmistakable. Relief. The relief of the invisible. Of a person who had spent years wishing the world would stop looking at her, and had gotten exactly that wish in the cruelest possible form.

He didn't name it. Naming it would have cost her something, and he was asking her to pay enough already.

"Mara," she said at last.

"What?"

"My name. Since you asked." She sheathed the knife — he hadn't even seen where it lived — and pulled her cloak straight with a brisk, decisive motion, the motion of someone closing one door and stepping through another without allowing herself to look back. "Mara Joubert. Though if you repeat that anywhere east of this river, I'll deny it and then I'll rob you."

"Justice Vale," Corin said, and extended his hand. "Though you knew that."

"I knew that." She looked at his hand for a moment — weighing it, weighing him, weighing whatever arithmetic ran behind those changeable eyes — and then she took it. Her grip was calloused and strong and brief. "One condition. We leave tonight, before the bell, by the culvert under the fulling mill. And you do exactly what I say, when I say it, on the road. No questions until camp. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And Vale?" She was already turning toward the dark mouth of the passage under the bridge, pulling her satchel higher on her shoulder, and she spoke without looking back, which somehow made it heavier. "The Spiral — the way the stones went dark. How do you know it turned inward? Who told you?"

"A clerk's copies and my own eyes," Corin said. "Why?"

She was quiet for the space of three heartbeats. When she answered, her voice had changed — flattened, careful, the voice of someone standing very still on thin ice.

"Because a spiral turning inward isn't a break, Vale. It's a search pattern. Something out there is looking for the center." She stepped into the shadow of the pilings. "And I think I might know what it's looking *for*."

Then she was gone into the dark, and Corin Vale, disgraced justice of the Eastern Assizes, stood alone under the bridge with the river muttering below and the starless ceiling pressing down above, and understood that he had just hired a guide who was carrying secrets heavier than his own —

— and that whatever she knew, the dark knew it too, and was coming to find her.
