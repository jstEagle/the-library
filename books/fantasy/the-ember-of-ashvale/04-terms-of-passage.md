## 4. Terms of Passage

The room above the Drowned Bell smelled of tallow smoke and river rot, and the man who had cornered me there sat with his hands flat on the table like a magistrate taking testimony.

Which, of course, he had been.

"Say it again," I told him. "Slowly. I want to enjoy it."

Corin Vale did not rise to the bait. He had the kind of face that had been sanded down by disappointment until only the stubborn grain remained — a jaw like a fence post, eyes the gray of slate after rain. His coat had once been good; now it was good in the way ruins are good, all bones and memory. "I need a guide to Thornfell," he said. "You know the roads no warden does. I'm offering you freedom for it."

"Freedom." I turned the word over like a coin I suspected was clipped. "I'm already free. That's rather the point of me."

"You're free the way a hare is free," he said. "Until the dogs come. The Crown is naming smugglers as traitors by week's end. Every road out of Lowbridge will have a checkpoint on it. Your name is on a list — Joubert, apprentice to the hedge-witch Ilsabet of the Fenmarch, fled her oath the night before her mistress died. They want you for desertion at best. At worst—" He shrugged, a small, economical motion. "They want someone to hang for the wards, and you're convenient."

The candle between us guttered, and I let the silence stretch long enough to make him uncomfortable. It didn't. That was the first thing I learned about Corin Vale: discomfort slid off him like rain off oiled leather.

"So I'm to trade one leash for another," I said. "Yours instead of theirs."

"I'm not buying a slave. I'm hiring a guide. There's a difference, and if you can't see it, we're both wasting our evening."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being invisible: it sounds like freedom until you learn how many people are paid to look for invisible things.

I had spent three years perfecting my disappearance. I could walk through a market and leave no memory behind. I could sleep in haylofts and pay in small silver and never give a surname. But the stars had gone out four nights past, and when the sky goes dark, every shadow becomes a place where someone might be hiding, and every person becomes a shadow. Lowbridge had stopped looking at each other and started looking *for* each other. Warden patrols walked the quays in pairs. Notices went up on the tithe-board with charcoal sketches of faces, mine among them, rendered badly — they'd given me my mother's cheekbones and a stranger's mouth.

So when Vale found me, I wasn't surprised. I was furious, which is different, and more useful.

"Terms," I said finally, and sat down across from him, because bargaining standing up is for people who expect to lose.

He nodded once, as if I'd confirmed something on a form.

"One." I held up a finger. "Gold. Not promises of gold. Fifty crowns now, fifty when we reach whatever door you think is hiding under Thornfell. Paid into my hand, not an account, not a letter of credit. I've seen what happens to letters of credit in a country that doesn't exist anymore."

"Forty and forty."

"Sixty and sixty. You're a disgraced justice with a purse full of severance and no one to spend it on. I'm the only person in Ashvale who can get you up that mountain without your corpse becoming a landmark."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of appreciation, maybe, the way a dead man's estate appreciates its appraiser. "Fifty and sixty," he said. "More on completion if you earn it."

"Fifty now, sixty at the end, and you don't haggle like a fishwife in front of me again." I let that sit. "Two. A pardon. Written, sealed, witnessed. When this is done, I'm not a fugitive anymore. Whatever they think I ran from, whatever they think I am — it ends."

"That's not mine to give."

"No. But you know the Lord Warden's secretary drinks at the Gilded Hart every Thirdday, and you know which of his debts are worth calling in, because you spent fifteen years learning what powerful men owe each other. Get me the pardon. If you can't, get me the name of someone who can, and I'll decide whether you've failed or lied."

That landed. I watched it land — a small flinch around his eyes, the flicker of a man hearing his own methods quoted back at him from the wrong side of the table.

"And three," I said, and here I leaned forward, because this was the term that mattered, the one I'd have traded the gold away to keep. "Silence. You know who I am. You know whose apprentice I was. That knowledge stays in your skull. You don't speak my name to wardens, to villagers, to priests, to the wind. As far as anyone along the Hollow Road knows, I'm a hired hand with no history. If I hear my name in your mouth even once—"

"What? You'll vanish into the fells and leave me to find my own way?"

"I'll do worse. I'll stay. And you'll spend the whole journey wondering which night I'll sell you to the first patrol that looks hungry." I held his gaze. "Disgrace makes men reckless, Justice Vale. Reckless men talk. Don't talk."

For a long moment the only sound was the river slapping the pilings below the floorboards and the muffled argument of drunks downstairs arguing about whether the dark was a punishment or a war.

Then Corin Vale said, "Agreed," and produced paper, ink, and a seal ring he should no longer have had any right to carry, and wrote out our bargain in a clerk's neat hand while I watched him like a hawk watches a man handling its eggs.

* * *

We left Lowbridge before dawn, through the eel-gates, where the mud stank of low tide and the sentries were too cold to check faces properly. I'd chosen the hour and the gate; he'd accepted both without question, which was either trust or exhaustion, and I hadn't yet learned which.

The Hollow Road isn't a road, exactly. It's a habit the land fell into centuries ago — a seam running beneath and beside the king's highways, stitched together from drovers' cuts, drainage tunnels, forgotten pilgrim paths, and the kind of trails that exist only because enough desperate feet agreed on them without ever meeting. My mistress had called it the country's second spine. She'd also said, more than once, that the second spine remembers everything the first one wants forgotten.

I led. He followed. For two days, that was the entire shape of our conversation.

He was worse at walking than I expected. Not weak — there was iron in him, I could see that much — but wrong-footed. A man who has spent his life in courtrooms learns to move in rooms: measured strides, doors that open when approached correctly, floors that hold still. The Hollow Road held no such courtesies. It ducked through blackthorn hedges that took payment in blood, crossed scree that shifted like bad conscience, and twice dropped into culverts where we waded waist-deep through water cold enough to make thought difficult.

He didn't complain. I kept waiting for it, the way you wait for a dog to bark. He just put his head down and followed my heels and memorized everything — I could see him doing it, cataloguing landmarks, noting where I hesitated and why. A justice even stripped of office is still a justice. He was building a case file on me with every mile.

On the third morning, in a birch stand gone white as bone in the starless gloom, he finally spoke about something other than logistics.

"You knew that cut would flood," he said, nodding back toward the culvert. "Before we entered it."

"Tide tables and a drain that hasn't been cleared since the old king died. Yes."

"You could have told me."

"I could have. Then you'd have wanted to go around, and going around costs half a day, and half a day is the difference between a warm barn and a wet hedge." I wrung out my skirt, which had given up any pretense of dignity days ago. "Besides. You'd have managed. Men like you always manage. It's infuriating."

"Is that respect?"

"It's inventory," I said. "Don't flatter yourself."

But he smiled then — actually smiled, brief and crooked, like a crack in kiln-fired clay — and I looked away too quickly, annoyed at myself for noticing.

* * *

It was on the fifth night that I began to understand exactly what kind of man I'd bound myself to.

We'd made camp in the shell of a drover's inn, roof burned off years back, walls still sound. I built the fire small and shielded — habit; light draws eyes, and eyes draw knives — and split our bread. He ate his share without comment, though I'd seen him give half of his the night before to a family of border refugees huddled under a bridge, and lie about it afterward with a fluency that would have shamed most smugglers. *I wasn't hungry.* From a man built like a fence post who had been walking since dawn. Stubborn honor, my mistress would have called it. The kind that gets you respected and killed in roughly equal measure.

"The acquittal," I said, because the fire was low and the dark pressed close and curiosity is a hunger like any other. "The witch you freed. What was her crime?"

"Healing," he said. "Officially, unlicensed craft. Unofficially, she healed the reeve's daughter when the college physicians had already written her off. The reeve couldn't have his daughter owing her life to a hedge-witch, so he filed charges." He fed a stick to the fire. "The evidence was clear. The law was clear. I acquitted her because the law was clear."

"And it cost you everything."

"It cost me a position I'd already stopped believing in." He said it evenly, but I heard the seam in it, the place where the evenness had been sewn over something rawer. "What did it cost you? Running. Three years ago, you left a woman who was dying, and you've been paying for it ever since — I can see it in how you flinch every time someone mentions the Fenmarch."

The bread in my hand suddenly tasted like ash. "You said you'd keep silent about who I am."

"About your name. I'm asking about your scars. Different ledger."

"Is it." I stood, brushing crumbs from my knees, and let the cold come into my voice, the voice I used on marks and magistrates alike. "Let me explain commerce to you, Justice. You bought a guide. You did not buy a confession. The next time you reach into my ledger, I'll reach into yours — and I promise you, I read faster than you do."

He looked up at me across the small red circle of the fire, and he didn't apologize, and he didn't argue. He just inclined his head a fraction, a duelist acknowledging a touch, and said, "Fair terms."

I lay down with my back to him and my knife in my sleeve, as always, and stared at the black ceiling where stars should have been, and hated him a little for being right, and hated myself more for being relieved that he'd asked.

Because the truth was that no one had asked in three years. Invisibility keeps you safe, and it keeps you alone, and after a while you can't tell the difference between the two, and you stop wanting to.

* * *

On the seventh day we came to the crossing at Marrow Beck, and the Hollow Road showed us its teeth.

The ford had moved. Not flooded, not washed out — *moved*, a quarter-mile downstream, which rivers do not do, over ground that had held the same crossing since before the kingdom had a name. I stood on the bank with my childhood maps spread against my knee — real vellum, drawn in my mother's cramped hand, the only inheritance I'd ever claimed — and felt the first true cold of the journey slide down my spine.

"This is wrong," I said.

Vale crouched beside me, studying the water. "Meaning?"

"Meaning roads don't wander. Roads are agreements. Stone and use and memory. This—" I tapped the map, then the river, then the map again. "This is the third thing this week. The shepherd's path near Tarnholt that doubled back on itself. The waystation that wasn't where any record puts it. The land is rearranging itself around something, like veins around a wound."

"Or around a cure," Vale said quietly.

I shot him a look. He was gazing north, toward the Thornfell massif, a jagged black line of teeth on the horizon, and his expression was the one I'd started to recognize as his thinking face — patient, terrible, certain.

"You know what's under that mountain," I said. "And you haven't told me."

"I know what the records say is under that mountain. Records and truth are only occasionally acquainted." He rose, joints cracking, and looked at me with those slate-gray eyes. "When I know, you'll know. That's a term. Add it to the list."

I should have refused. I should have demanded the whole truth then and there, or walked away, or held his secrets hostage against my own. That's what a smart woman would have done, and I flattered myself I was smart.

Instead I folded my mother's maps along their worn creases, tucked them inside my shirt where they lay warm against my sternum like a second heartbeat, and said, "Fine. One more term. But it cuts both ways, Justice. When you tell me what you know — I'll tell you why I really ran."

His eyebrows lifted, just slightly. For the first time since the Drowned Bell, Corin Vale looked genuinely surprised.

We crossed at the new ford, the water pulling at our legs like hands, and on the far bank the road bent north toward the mountains, and somewhere behind us — though neither of us saw it, neither of us heard it, not yet — something opened a page that had been torn from a dead woman's book, and began reading our names aloud.
