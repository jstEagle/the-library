## 5. What the Hedge-Witch Left

The turnoff to Millbrook Hollow was a deer track that most travelers would have missed twice, and Mara had missed it once already before she stopped and made herself look properly.

That was the trouble with the dark. Not the fear of it — fear she could carry like a stone in her pack, had carried for days now — but the way it ate the small signs. The way the world refused to be read. On the smuggler's roads you navigated by a hundred tiny kindnesses the land did for those who knew to ask: a birch leaning east away from its own shadow in summer, a cairn of three stones where a fourth had tumbled, the particular hush of a hollow that held water. Under the black ceiling that had swallowed the stars, everything gave back only itself. No shadows. No angles. Just the road and the pressing weight of nothing above it.

She stood at the fork with Corin's lantern held high — his lantern, though he'd handed it over without comment two nights past when hers guttered out, and hadn't asked for it back — and let her memory do what her eyes could not.

*Left at the split oak. Count four hundred paces along the streambed until the water goes quiet. Then up.*

The split oak was there, or something shaped like one, its lightning-scarred trunk pale as bone in the emberlight. She counted her paces. At three hundred and eighty the stream went silent, frozen into a run of black glass, and she turned uphill with her breath smoking and her heart doing something complicated in her chest that she refused to name.

Corin followed. He had learned, in the days since Lowbridge, not to ask questions on the road. He saved them all up instead and spent them at night, sitting across the fire like a man paying out coin one piece at a time, watching her face while he did it. She hated how much he noticed. She hated more that she'd started noticing him noticing.

"You said north," he said behind her. "This is west."

"I said we go north *eventually*. There's a thing I have to get first."

"A thing."

"An inheritance." She picked her way over a fallen wall, old work, older than any kingdom, the stones furred with moss even in this dead season. "And before you ask — no, I'm not telling you what it is yet. You haven't paid enough for that."

"I paid your toll at the ford."

"You paid *a* toll." She didn't look back at him. "There are prices and there are prices, Justice Vale. Learn the difference before you spend me."

He was quiet for ten paces. Then, in that maddening level voice of his, the one that never rose and never begged: "Your mistress is dead. You told me that much. Whatever she left you, you're afraid of it."

Mara stopped so suddenly he nearly walked into her.

"I'm not afraid of it," she said. "I'm afraid of who else might want it. And if you say another word about my teacher, I'll leave you at the top of this hill with a map drawn in mud and my sincere good wishes."

But that was the lie of the day, and she knew it as she said it, and she suspected he knew it too, because he said nothing else at all, which was somehow worse.

* * *

The cottage sat in a fold of the hills like a thing the land had swallowed and half-digested. Thatch gone to rot in long gray combs. Garden beds under snow, the rosemary still stubbornly green, because rosemary doesn't know when to quit and neither had she. Wren-of-the-Hedges had lived here forty years, and the village below had needed her for thirty-nine of them and resented her for every one.

Mara stood at the gate and could not make herself open it.

Here was the door she'd slammed on her way out, six weeks ago, with her mistress's voice following her down the path: *You'll come back when the oath sits heavier than the running, child. They always do.* Here was the window through which lamplight used to spill, the light Mara had steered by from a mile off on winter nights, telling herself she wasn't looking for it. Here was the bench where she'd sat at nine years old, hands blistered from her first lesson in knot-magic, while an old woman with ink-stained fingers said: *A ward isn't a wall, girl. A wall keeps things out. A ward keeps things remembered.*

The gate swung open at her touch, unlatched, unprotesting.

That was the first wrong thing.

Wren-of-the-Hedges latched her gate against goats and children and the tax man, in that order. Mara stood looking at the hanging latch and felt the cold move from the air into her spine.

Inside, the wrongness multiplied. The door wasn't locked either — and Wren had kept a lock on that door since the incident with the peddler and the love-charms, a lock she'd bought with money meant for candles. Mara stepped into the main room and the silence had texture, a held-breath quality, the silence of a place that has been searched by someone careful.

Not wrecked. That was what raised the hair on her arms. Nothing was overturned. The crockery sat in its cupboard. The dried herbs hung in their bundles from the beams — yarrow, mugwort, vervain, all accounted for. Whoever had come had been respectful, almost tender, and had put everything back exactly where it belonged.

Almost.

Mara had spent eleven years learning this room the way other children learned prayers. She saw it the way a musician hears a false note. The mortar and pestle were turned a quarter-degree wrong, handles facing the wall instead of the fire. The third floorboard by the hearth — the loose one, the one that had hidden her teacher's savings and, once, a fugitive warden's confession — sat flush. It had always sat proud, worn at one corner, catching the toe of anyone who crossed the room carelessly. Someone had pried it up and planed it down. Or replaced it. Either way, they had known about the board, and they had cared enough to hide that they knew.

Her hands were shaking. She made them stop.

"The grimoire," Corin said quietly, from the doorway. He hadn't crossed the threshold. She realized he'd understood without being told — that some rooms you enter and some you stand witness to. "It was here?"

"It was everywhere." Her voice came out flat. "She never kept it in one place. Moved it every season. Said a book that stays still gets found, and a book that moves gets *followed*, and better to be followed than found." She turned in a slow circle, reading the room. "Under the eaves in spring. In the well-house in summer. Autumn—" Her eyes caught on the cold hearth. "Autumn it lived in the chimney flue, wrapped in oilcloth, above the smoke shelf. And winter—"

Winter it lived in the garden, beneath the rosemary, in an iron box that had been her mistress's mother's, buried the day of the first frost and dug up the day of the last.

Mara went out the back door into the gray afternoon and knelt in the frozen herb bed, and dug with her bare hands because a spade would take too long and make too much noise, and the ground came up hard as iron, and she didn't feel it, and later she would find her fingertips bleeding and be surprised.

The box was there. Of course it was there. It had been *opened*.

The lock — a puzzle-lock, three tumblers, a thing Wren had taught her to pick at age twelve as a lesson in patience — hung slack. Not broken. *Opened.* Someone had solved it, lifted the grimoire out, and closed the box again, and pressed the earth back over it, and left the rosemary standing, and gone away.

Mara sat back on her heels in the ruined bed with the empty iron box in her lap and felt grief arrive for the second time. The first time had been sharp, at the deathbed, all heat and refusal — *I won't, I'm not ready, don't you dare die with words in your mouth I can't carry.* This grief was cold. It was the grief of finding a grave already robbed.

"She's been dead five weeks," Corin said. He had come out after all; she heard his boots in the snow and didn't turn. "Whoever did this came recently. The earth was turned within the month — see how it's settled? Fresh digging settles unevenly."

"You've dug up a lot of graves, Justice?"

"Enough." He crouched beside her, and she let him, because her hands wouldn't close the box and someone had to. "They didn't take anything else?"

"How would you—"

"Your teacher hid one book in four places a year. A woman that careful hides other things. Coin. Letters. Whatever a hedge-witch accumulates in forty years of knowing everyone's secrets." He nodded at the cottage. "If they wanted her secrets, why take one book and leave the rest?"

Because they didn't want secrets, Mara thought. Because they wanted one page.

She climbed the stairs to the loft on legs that felt borrowed. The loft was where Wren had done her reading, in winter, propped in the one good chair with blankets to her chin, and where she'd kept — Mara had assumed forever — her private papers in a cedar chest at the bed's foot.

The chest was open. The papers inside were arranged with that same terrible neatness, squared edges, aligned corners. And lying on top, folded once, weighted down with a river stone taken from the windowsill, was a single sheet of heavy rag paper, blank except for four words written in a hand Mara did not know:

**SHE TOLD YOU TOO.**

Mara read it three times. The words didn't change. The room around them changed completely — tilted, receded, became a stage on which she had been walking unaware through someone else's play.

"They know me," she heard herself say. Corin had come up the stair; she could feel him at her shoulder, and for once she didn't mind, because the alternative was being alone with the sentence. "They know she taught me. They wanted me to find this. They *left* it for me."

"Or they're arrogant."

"No." She picked up the note. The paper was good stock, the kind sold in Lowbridge and Vell's Crossing and nowhere else for fifty miles. The ink was fresh enough to shine. "Arrogance leaves nothing. This is an invitation. This is someone saying: come and see."

"What did the missing page hold?"

And there it was. The question she'd been circling since the garden, since the gate, since the unlatched latch. Because she knew what the missing page held. She had copied it out herself, at fifteen, as penance for falling asleep during a lesson on sympathetic wards, copying the whole chapter in her best hand while her mistress watched from the chair and said nothing, only *again, girl, again until you understand it* —

The Door Beneath the Fell.

The oldest entry in the grimoire. Older than Wren's handwriting, older than her teacher's teacher, set down in browned ink by hands generations dead: the account of the Thornfell door, and the wards that sealed it, and the warning that ran beneath them like a seam of ore through poor country stone.

*A door is not built to keep a place in,* the old text said. *It is built to keep a hunger out. Ashvale was not walled against its neighbors. Ashvale was walled for its neighbors' sake.*

And beneath that, in Wren's own newer hand, added in the margins over years of study, small and fierce:

*The wards fail from within or not at all. Watch the ones who benefit. Watch the ones who believe.*

Mara had copied those words at fifteen and thought them an old woman's paranoia. Now she stood in the rifled loft of a dead witch, holding a stranger's invitation, and every starless night since the ceiling came down rearranged itself around the sentence.

"The page described the border wards," she said carefully. "Their making. Their purpose. Where the anchor-points sit in the Thornfell." She watched Corin's face as she said it, and saw him go very still, the stillness of a magistrate hearing testimony that changes the shape of a case. "And it described a door in the deep fells. What it was for. Why it was shut."

"And someone tore it out."

"Someone who already knew where to dig, and how to pick a three-tumbler lock, and where a hedge-witch would keep her book in the first weeks of winter." Mara folded the note into her coat, over her heart, where she kept the things that hurt. "Someone who was taught by her. Or taught by someone who was."

Corin was silent a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the dead garden, and the rosemary bent and straightened, bent and straightened, keeping its stubborn time.

"There were others," he said at last. "Apprentices. Before you."

"One." The word came out of her before she could weigh it, and once it was out she couldn't call it back, and she found she didn't want to. "Her name was Sereth. She was older than me. Brilliant — brilliant the way a fire is brilliant, everything she touched lit up and some of it burned. Mistress loved her and feared her in the same breath, and I never understood how you could do both until—" She stopped. Started again. "Sereth argued with her. About the wards. About whether a kingdom had the right to seal itself away from the world, even for a good reason. She said a cage was a cage no matter how kindly it was built. She left. Eleven years ago. Mistress burned her letters and wouldn't speak her name, and I was a child, and I learned not to ask."

The wind gusted. Somewhere below in the village, thin and far, a dog began to bark, and stopped, as if corrected.

"Mistress died calling for her," Mara said. "I thought it was guilt. Now I think it was warning."

Corin took the empty iron box from where it sat between them and weighed it in his hands, and she watched him become, briefly and completely, the justice he had been before the law discarded him — the man who acquitted a witch because the evidence demanded it, the man who believed that truth was a thing you served rather than used.

"Then we ride for Thornfell faster than whoever wrote this," he said. "Because if Sereth has the page, she has the anchor-points. And if she has the anchor-points—"

"The stars stay gone." Mara looked out the little window at the black ceiling pressing down on the world, low enough now that she could almost believe she saw it breathing. "Or worse. Mistress wrote that the wards failing was the *first* thing. The door opening was the last."

She closed the empty box. She took a handful of rosemary from the frozen bed — the one living thing her mistress had left growing — and tucked it inside the box, and buried the box again in the hole her own hands had torn open, and patted the earth flat, and stood, and did not cry, because crying was a luxury with a head start to beat.

At the gate she paused once, and looked back at the cottage in the gathering dark, at the dark windows where lamplight used to be.

"You'll come back when the oath sits heavier than the running," she said softly, to no one, to the dead, to herself. "You were right, old woman. You were right about everything, and I hate you for it."

Behind her, Corin waited on the road with the lantern raised, and its emberlight fell on the snow in a small warm circle, and beyond the circle the darkness stood waiting too — patient, vast, and, she understood now, not empty at all.

Somewhere ahead of them, in the black throat of the Thornfell passes, someone was carrying her teacher's stolen page toward a door, and moving fast.

Mara turned her back on the grave and the cottage and the life she'd fled twice now, and walked into the dark after them.
