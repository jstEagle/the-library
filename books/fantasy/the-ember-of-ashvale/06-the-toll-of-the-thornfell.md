## 6. The Toll of the Thornfell

The mountain did not want them.

Corin had climbed passes before — in his magistrate years, riding circuit through the high country with a scribe and six lancers, the road graded and graveled by the Crown's coin. The Thornfell was nothing like that. The smugglers' path Mara led him along was less a road than a rumor: a seam of bare stone threading between scree fields, sometimes wide enough for two abreast, sometimes narrowing to a ledge where the drop fell away into cloud so thick it looked solid, like walking on the rim of a bowl of milk. Except there was no light in the milk anymore. Under the starless ceiling, even the clouds had gone the color of wet slate, and the world below the ledge was simply *absence*, a gray so complete his eyes kept insisting it was a wall.

"Stop looking down," Mara said, not turning around. She moved like water finding its level, sure-footed on stone that offered no purchase to reason. "It doesn't help. The dark's the same whether you look or not."

"I'm assessing the terrain."

"You're looking down." A half-glance over her shoulder, her hood shadowing everything but the set of her mouth. "Wardens. You all do it. Like if you understand the fall you can appeal against it."

He let that go. Three days on the Hollow Road had taught him which of her barbs were invitations and which were doors closing. This one closed.

They climbed through what should have been morning, though morning had become a courtesy title rather than a fact. The cold up here was different from the lowland chill — older, drier, a cold that seemed to come out of the rock itself, as though the mountain had been keeping it since before the wards were raised. Corin pulled his coat tighter and counted his breaths, an old habit from the interrogation rooms, a way of staying level when levelness mattered. Breathe in for four. Hold for two. Out for six. Somewhere behind that discipline lived the man he used to be, and he did not open that door either.

Near midday — or what Mara called midday, tapping her wrist where a watch would be if she owned one — she stopped so abruptly he nearly walked into her.

"What is it?"

She held up a hand. Her fingers were dirty, nail-bitten, quick; he had noticed them the first day and kept noticing them, the way one notices a knife at a banquet. "Listen."

He listened. Wind. Stone settling somewhere far off, a sound like a giant shifting in sleep. And beneath it, faint but rhythmic: bells. Small ones, tin-bright, many of them, jangling in no pattern any church would bless.

"Mara." He kept his voice low. "What's up here?"

"Nothing good." She crouched, pressed her palm flat to the rock as if reading it. "I've run this pass four times. There was never anything up here but goats and one very old shrine. No tolls. Smugglers don't pay tolls; that's the whole point of smugglers."

"And now?"

"Now there are bells." She rose, and when she turned to face him fully he saw something in her expression he hadn't seen before — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman meeting a rumor she'd hoped was a lie. "Someone's organized the high country. Someone's *taxing* it."

* * *

The Lantern's people found them an hour later, or perhaps they had always been there and simply chose to become visible.

They came out of the rocks the way frost comes out of the ground — everywhere at once, silent until the moment of arrival. A dozen figures, maybe more, wrapped in gray wool dyed to match the scree, faces masked with cloth except for their eyes. Each wore a small bell at the throat, and each bell made no sound now, which somehow was worse than the jangling. They carried hooks, short staves, slings — no blades that Corin could see, and that too was wrong. Bandits without blades were not bandits. They were something with more patience.

"Well met, travelers," said the one who stepped forward. The voice was muffled, genderless, pitched carefully into neutrality. "You walk the Thornfell. The Thornfell has a keeper now."

Corin put his hand nowhere near his sword. He had learned long ago that the first thing a crowd watches for is where your hand goes, and the second thing is how long you can stand still. "We're pilgrims," he said. "Bound for the high shrines."

"No one's bound for the shrines," the masked figure said, with something almost like pity. "The shrines are empty. Everyone knows the stars went out because the gods left, and everyone knows the gods left because we deserved it. But some of us stayed to keep the doors they abandoned." The figure spread its arms. Gray wool, gray stone, gray sky. "The Thornfell is ours. Passage costs a toll."

"We have little coin," Corin said.

The masked head tilted. Behind the dozen visible figures, more shapes shifted among the rocks — twenty? thirty? Enough. "Coin," the figure said, and several of the others made a sound that might have been laughter, dry as gravel. "Coin is for the lowlands, for people who still believe in tomorrow. Up here we take payment in something surer. Memories."

The word dropped into the cold air and sat there.

"That's not possible," Corin said — and then hated himself for saying it, because it was the voice of the courtroom, the voice that named a thing impossible in order to avoid weighing it. He had acquitted a witch on evidence the court called impossible. He had lost everything to the word *impossible*. He would not use it again.

"Isn't it?" The figure drew something from its robe: a lantern, small, iron-framed, and inside it burned a flame the color of deep amber — the first warm color Corin had seen in days, and it hurt to look at, the way sunlight hurts eyes long in the dark. "The old wards ran on memory. Did your magistrates never wonder what fed them? Every oath sworn, every name remembered, every face held dear — the borders drank it for centuries. The wards are broken now, but the thirst remains. We collect. We keep. And when the world is remade, what we keep will matter more than gold ever did."

Beside him, Mara had gone very still. Not frightened-still. Calculating-still. He was learning the difference.

"The toll," she said flatly. "How much."

"That depends on what you carry." The lantern-bearer drifted closer, and Corin saw that within the amber glow, shapes moved — impressions, shadows of shadows, like fish under ice. "Memories weigh differently. A bad year is light. A beloved face is heavy. The Lantern takes according to means." The mask turned toward Corin, and he felt the regard behind it like a hand on his chest. "You, justice-man. You carry something heavy. I can see it from here. It sits behind your ribs like a stone."

*My daughter,* Corin thought, and the thought itself was a bell tolling.

Her name was Ilse. She would be eleven now, or twelve — he had lost track during the disgrace, during the trial, during the years after when he'd told himself distance was protection. She lived with his sister in the river country, east of Ashvale's heart, and he had seen her last at Midwinter two years past, when she'd stood in the doorway with flour on her cheek and laughed at something her cousin said, and the laugh had opened her whole face like a window opening onto a lit room. He had carried that face across every mile since. Through the trial. Through the stripping of his office. Through the nights when the law he'd served called him traitor and the law he'd believed in had nothing to say. It was the one possession he had never once considered losing.

And this creature could see it. Could *weigh* it.

"There are exemptions," Mara said suddenly. "For guides. For couriers. Every toll system has them — it's how the toll-keepers keep the couriers from going around. I know the roads. You need people who know the roads."

"Clever girl." The mask swung to her. "But you're not registered, are you? No mark, no token. You're just another traveler carrying less than your friend." A pause, and Corin heard the trap assembling itself. "Though you carry more than you think. Something old. Something given to you." The lantern flared slightly. "But we'll start with the justice-man. Fairness first. That's our doctrine."

Corin stepped forward before Mara could speak again. Later he would ask himself why — whether it was protectiveness, or pride, or the simple courtroom instinct to take the worst of it first and control the terms. "Name your price," he said.

The lantern-bearer raised the light. "One memory. Of your choosing — or of ours, if you refuse to choose. Refusal means we take three."

There was no choosing. They both knew it. A memory surrendered by choice might be survived; three torn out blind would leave him a husk staggering down the mountain. And yet the fiction of choice mattered — he understood that too. Even robbery wants to be called a transaction.

He thought of Ilse laughing in the doorway. Flour on her cheek. The window of her face opening onto light.

He thought: *I can keep the fact of her. The love. The duty. Only the face goes. I will remember that I loved her, and that will have to be enough.*

"Take it," he said, and his voice did not shake, and he was proud of that, and ashamed of the pride.

The lantern-bearer lifted the light to his brow. The amber glow washed over him, warm as a hearth, and for one heartbeat it felt like mercy —

— and then it *drank*.

There was no pain. That was the horror of it. There was a gentle pulling, like a thread drawn from a sleeve, and Corin felt the doorway open in his mind, felt the lit room with the flour-cheeked girl begin to recede, and he grabbed for it, he *grabbed*, the way a drowning man grabs water, and the memory slid through his fingers like water, like light, like everything—

Gone.

The lantern dimmed. Somewhere inside it, a new shape swam: a small bright fish, a laughing face, circling the amber forever.

Corin stood on the mountain with tears running freely down his face and no idea why he was crying. He knew he had a daughter. The knowledge remained, a doorframe without a room, a title page without a book. *Ilse. My daughter Ilse. I love her.* The words were intact. The face behind them was a smooth gray blank, like the ceiling where the stars had been.

"Payment received," the lantern-bearer said softly. "The Thornfell thanks you."

* * *

Mara paid next. He watched from a great distance, from somewhere outside himself, as she bargained — of course she bargained, even now, even hollow-eyed with watching him — haggling the toll down from two memories to one by offering knowledge of a warden cache on the southern slopes, a stash of salt and lamp-oil she'd hidden two summers ago. The masked figures conferred. The lantern glowed. Whatever they took from her, she gave with her chin up and her jaw set, and afterward she stood a moment swaying, one hand pressed to her temple, and then straightened and said, "Are we done?" in a voice like a blade being sheathed.

They were released. The gray figures melted back into the gray rocks, bells silent, and the pass lay open before them, and Corin walked because walking was the only instruction his body still accepted.

They made camp that night in the lee of a boulder field, a fire no larger than two fists, because Mara insisted fires be small — "the dark notices big ones," she said, and he didn't ask how she knew. He sat with his hands out to the flames and tried to find his daughter's face in the firelight and could not. He knew the shape of the absence precisely, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. He knew she had flour on her cheek once. He knew the doorway was lit. He knew he had loved the sight so much he'd built his whole ruinous integrity on the hope of deserving to see it again.

He could not remember her eyes. He could not remember her eyes.

"Corin." Mara's voice, quiet, from across the fire. "Eat something."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're shaking."

He looked at his hands. They were shaking. "It took her face," he said. The words came out stripped of rhetoric, stripped of everything, just the raw ore of it. "I can remember that I — that she —" His throat closed. Twelve years on the bench, and he had never once been speechless. "I know her name. I know I love her. And her face is — there's nothing there. It's like the sky."

Mara was silent a long time. The fire ticked. High above, the starless ceiling pressed down like a lid on the world.

"I'm sorry," she said finally, and it sounded like it cost her. "That's — I'm sorry, Corin. That's worse than anything they could have taken from me."

"How would you know what they took from you?" The cruelty surprised him as much as it did her; he saw her flinch, saw her jaw tighten, and he wanted to call the words back and found he couldn't be bothered to try. Grief made him honest, and honesty made him ugly.

"They took my mother's voice," Mara said.

The fire cracked. Somewhere downslope, loose stone clattered — wind, probably. Probably.

"Singing," she went on, staring into the flames, not looking at him. "She used to sing while she worked. I was four. Maybe five. I don't remember her face either — isn't that funny? Two of us now. But I remembered the singing. All these years, on the road, when things got bad, I'd — I'd play it in my head. The tune. I never knew the words, just the tune." She shrugged, a small hard motion. "Now when I reach for it, there's nothing. Just knowing there was something. So yes. I know."

Corin stared at her. The grief in him was a vast cold chamber, and in that chamber, for the first time since the lantern, something stirred — not comfort, nothing so kind, but *company*. The specific, unshareable company of two people standing in the same ruined room.

"Why didn't you tell me?" he asked.

"Because it's mine." She looked up, and her eyes caught the firelight, green-gold, defiant. "Because I don't hand you my wounds so you can hold them for me. Nobody holds mine. That's the arrangement."

*The arrangement.* He thought of her on the road, three days of sharp economy and sharper silences, the transaction neither of them trusted, and he understood suddenly that the arrangement was armor, and that underneath it she was as flayed as he was, and that she would die before admitting it twice.

"All right," he said quietly. "Keep your wounds. But Mara — when we find whoever built this toll, whoever's feeding that lantern — I want you to know that I intend to take it apart rivet by rivet. Not for justice." He almost smiled, and it hurt his face. "Justice is a profession. This is personal."

Something moved through her expression — quickly, gone before he could name it. She nodded once, and threw another twig on the fire, and said nothing more, and the silence between them was different afterward. Less like a wall. More like a shared roof.

What Corin did not see — what no one saw, because the dark was thorough and the hour was late — was this:

An hour after he finally slept, Mara rose silently from her bedroll and walked back up the pass alone, moving through the black with the certainty of someone who had been born to roads that hid people. She found the Lantern's camp in a fold of the mountain, fires banked, sentries drowsing at their posts, and she stood at the edge of the firelight with her hood back and her heart hammering and said to the nearest sentry, in a voice pitched low and steady:

"I have a second payment to make. Mine wasn't enough. Add it to the justice-man's account — whatever he owes beyond what's paid, I'm covering it. Don't tell him. If he learns who paid, I'll know whose bell rang, and I'll make sure the roads forget every one of your caches."

The sentry studied her a long moment. Then it fetched the lantern-bearer, and there was a quiet conference, and Mara opened her cupped hands, and the amber light leaned toward her like a hungry animal.

She paid in memories of her mistress — the hedge-witch's kitchen, the smell of bread and bitter herbs, the old woman's crooked fingers sorting dried nettle from dried comfrey. She paid in the sound of her teacher laughing, which she had thought was safe, because surely they'd already taken everything worth taking. She paid until the lantern-bearer said, "His account is clear," and meant it, and Mara walked back down the mountain in the dark with holes in her past where warmth used to be, and told herself it was a fair trade, and almost believed it.

In the morning, Corin woke feeling strangely lighter. Not healed — the gray blank where Ilse's face had been remained, a wound with clean edges. But the weight behind his ribs, the crushing arithmetic of debt he'd assumed the Lantern would return to collect, was gone. He tested it as they broke camp, probing the absence the way a tongue probes the missing tooth, and found only smoothness. Paid in full. Somehow. By some accounting he didn't understand.

"Strange," he murmured, shouldering his pack.

"What is?" Mara asked, not looking at him, already climbing.

"Nothing. Let's move."

They climbed. Above them the pass narrowed toward a saddle of black rock, and beyond the saddle, faint as breath on glass, Corin saw something that stopped him mid-stride: a smudge of amber light burning high on the ridge, unmoving, patient, like an eye.

And beside it, carved into the stone of the trailhead in letters old enough to predate the kingdom, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather — but legible, unmistakably legible, in the gray half-light:

**THE DOOR BENEATH THE FELL KEEPS ITS OWN TOLL. TURN BACK, LIVING.**

Mara had gone white. She was staring at the carving as though it had spoken her name.

"You've seen this before," Corin said quietly.

"No," she said. But her voice shook, and her hand had gone to the grimoire-bag at her hip, gripping it like a talisman, and Corin — who had spent a career learning to read the gap between what people said and what they knew — filed the contradiction away, and followed her up toward the light.
