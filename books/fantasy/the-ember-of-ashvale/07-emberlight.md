## 7. Emberlight

The storm found us an hour before the shrine did.

It came down off the Thornfell shoulders like something poured — not rain at first but a fine grey grit, ash from a sky that had no business making ash, and then the rain behind it, cold and needling, turning the smuggler's track to grease beneath our boots. I had walked the Hollow Road in every season since I was small enough to fit through the badger-gap below Widow Cren's fence, and I had never felt weather like this. It fell wrong. It fell *inward*, as if the whole black ceiling above Ashvale were slowly exhaling down upon us, pressing closer with each passing day.

"Tell me there's shelter," Corin said behind me. He had stopped trying to hide how tired he was somewhere around the second day, which I took for progress, or surrender; with him it was hard to tell the difference.

"There's shelter." I squinted through the gritting dark. "If my memory's worth anything."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we get very good at sleeping standing up."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh in a man who still remembered how. I pressed on, counting landmarks in my head — the split boulder like a broken tooth, the leaning cairn the smugglers called the Old Man's Finger, the place where the pines gave way to bare granite and the wind came through the pass with a sound like someone breathing through cloth. My mother's maps lived in my head the way other people kept prayers: wordless, constant, consulted without knowing I was consulting them. The shrine should be just past the third switchback, tucked into the fold of the mountain where the old wardens had once kept their watch-fires burning against the things the wards were built to hold back.

The wards were dead now. Everyone in Lowbridge said so, crossing themselves with salt. But the shrines remained, stone shells abandoned when the Crown decided watching was cheaper than warding, and I had slept in a dozen of them over four years of running.

There. A seam of deeper black in the black hillside — a doorway without a door, its lintel carved with letters worn to suggestions. I put my shoulder to the gap and slipped inside, Corin's breath loud behind me, and then we were out of the wind and the world went quiet all at once, the way a struck bell goes quiet.

I shook the wet from my hair and listened. Stone around us, old and dry. The smell of cold dust and long-dead fires and, faintly, underneath everything, something mineral and strange that I had smelled before in my mistress's workroom and never been able to name.

"Well?" Corin's voice came low out of the darkness. He couldn't see any better than I could. That was the cruelty of Ashvale now — even indoors, even inches apart, we were all blind together.

"Small," I said. "One room. Round. There'll be a fire-pit in the center and a bench along the wall, and probably a niche behind the eastern stone where the watch-keeper kept his tally-sticks." I paused. "And there'll be carvings on the floor you shouldn't step on until I've looked at them."

"Why?"

"Because some of them are older than the Crown, and because my teacher told me once that the watch-shrines weren't built *by* the wardens." I heard him stop moving. Good. He could follow an instruction when it mattered, whatever else he was. "They were built *for* them. By people who knew what they'd need to hold back the dark before the wards existed. Sit down. Don't wander. I'm going to see if anything in here will burn."

* * *

Nothing would burn, at first.

I went through the shrine by touch, hands out like a beggar's, and found what I expected — the fire-pit, swept clean by decades of wind through the doorway; the bench; the tally-niche, empty except for a bird's nest gone to felt. No wood. No peat. The smugglers who used this road had stripped every shrine within a day's walk years ago, and why not? Fire was life, and life was scarce under the starless ceiling.

Corin sat where I'd told him, knees drawn up, and I could feel him listening to me move the way a man listens to water in the dark — tracking me by sound alone, trusting the sound. It should have unsettled me, being tracked. Instead there was a warmth in it I didn't want to examine.

"There's nothing," I said finally. "No fuel. We're dry, and we're out of the wind, and that will have to be enough."

"We've been colder."

"We've been warmer, too. I remember it distinctly."

That earned the almost-laugh again. I lowered myself onto the bench across the pit from him, and for a while neither of us spoke, and the silence had a different texture than the silences of the last six days. Less like two strangers enduring one another. More like two people sitting in the same room on purpose.

My hands ached. Not from the cold, though the cold didn't help. They ached the way teeth ache, deep and rhythmic, and had been aching since the night the stars went out. I had told myself it was grief — I had buried my mistress three days before the sky closed, and grief does strange things to the body — but grief doesn't pulse. Grief doesn't wake you at midnight with your palms glowing faintly under the skin like coals wrapped in wool.

I hadn't told anyone about the glow. There had been no one to tell.

"Mara." Corin's voice, careful. "Your hands."

My stomach dropped. "What about them?"

"They're lit."

I looked down. He was right. In the total dark of the shrine, where I could not see my own boots or the shape of my own knees, my hands shone — a low ember-light rising through the skin of my palms and fingers, amber-gold, unsteady, breathing. Brighter when my heart beat faster. Dimmer when I forced my breath slow. Like a coal. Like a banked fire that had been waiting, all this time, for the dark to grow deep enough to matter.

"I—" Every explanation I owned scattered like startled birds. *It's nothing. It's a trick of the dark. It's hedge-craft, it's common, every apprentice can do it.* All lies, and he would know them for lies, because he had spent twenty years listening to liars for a living and I had spent four years lying to everyone I met and I still wasn't good enough to fool a man in the dark.

"Don't," he said quietly.

"Don't what?"

"Don't lie to me. I've watched you decide whether to lie maybe forty times since Lowbridge. You have a look. Your jaw does something." I couldn't see his jaw do anything, but I could hear the dry patience in his voice, and it was worse than anger. "Whatever it is, you don't have to explain it. Just don't insult both of us."

So I sat there with my burning hands held up between us like an accusation, and after a moment I did the only honest thing left. I let go.

Not of the secret — that was already gone, spilled across the dark between us. I let go of the holding-back. Four years I had clamped down on everything my mistress had poured into me, every trick and thread of the hedge-craft she'd sworn me to secrecy over, because visible magic is a hanging offense in three provinces and a drowning in the fourth. I opened the clamp the way you open a fist you've been making so long you've forgotten it's a fist.

The light rose.

It came up through my arms first, veins kindling under the skin like rivers of dawn seen from very high up, and then it lifted *off* me — that was the part that stole my breath, the part I had never managed before, never dared try — it lifted off me and spread, thin as smoke, flowing outward along the floor of the shrine until it touched the walls. And the walls answered.

The old carvings woke.

Every groove and channel in that ancient floor began to glow, amber answering amber, lines of light crawling up the curved stones and branching like frost in reverse, like lightning remembering its path, until the whole round chamber stood revealed in a web of soft gold — and I saw, for the first time with eyes instead of memory, what the watch-shrines truly were. Not rooms. *Circuits.* The carvings ran out through the doorway and up the hillside beyond, faint threads of ember-light vanishing into the storm-wet rock, hundreds of them, thousands, all converging on the pit where I sat, all of them dead for a hundred years, all of them waking now because I was sitting in the middle of them like a spark dropped into tinder.

"Oh," I said. Very quietly. To myself. Because there was nothing else to say.

Above the fire-pit, where the smoke of a hundred thousand watch-fires had once risen, the light gathered into a slow-turning sphere, and inside the sphere — I had to squint, I had to lean forward, I didn't believe it — inside the sphere hung a perfect miniature of the night sky. Stars. Dozens of them, tiny and fierce, wheeling in their old positions, the constellations my mistress had taught me to read by name. The Wagon. The Drowned Maiden. The Thorn Crown, tilted just as it should be over the Thornfell in autumn.

A map of the sky the wards used to hold up. Kept here, in every shrine, so the watchers would know what they were guarding.

"It's showing the stars," Corin breathed. He had come off the bench without my noticing, drawn forward into the light, and his face — the first time I had really seen it since Lowbridge, since the market square where he'd cornered me with my own name in his mouth — his face was open in a way I suspected it almost never was. Wonder did that to people. Stripped them. "Mara. What are you?"

And there it was. The question I had run four years to escape, asked by the one man in Ashvale stubborn enough to chase me down and ask it properly.

* * *

We sat in the emberlight for a long time before I answered, and the light made it easier, the way confession is easier in a chapel than a kitchen. I told him about my mistress, Old Sefa, whose cottage smelled of pine resin and whose hands were always cold no matter the season. About the oath she made me swear on her deathbed with her grip still strong enough to bruise: *never open it, never close it, never let them make you either thing.* I had thought she was raving. Hedge-witches rave; it's half the trade. Now I sat in a dead warden's shrine wearing my own blood as a lamp, and raving sounded different.

"I don't know what I am," I said. "That's the truth of it. She never said. She taught me the craft and she taught me the roads and she taught me how to be nobody, and she never once said *why*. And now the wards are down, and my hands glow, and the old stones wake up when I touch them, and I keep thinking—" My voice caught. I pushed through it. "I keep thinking she knew this night was coming. And she died before she could tell me what I was supposed to do about it."

Corin was quiet for a while. The little stars wheeled overhead between us. When he spoke, his voice had the flat care he used when he was choosing words like steps across ice.

"When I was a magistrate, I learned to read people the way you read those maps in your head. Most of them lie with their mouths and tell the truth everywhere else. Their hands. Their pauses." He nodded at my glowing fingers. "You're frightened. Not of me. Of yourself. Which means whatever you are, you didn't choose it."

"No."

"And your teacher tried to protect you from it, and failed, and died."

"Yes."

"Then we have that much in common, apprentice. People who loved us tried to shape our lives and got it wrong, and now we're in the mountains in the dark, and the law wants us both." He almost smiled. "I won't ask again. When you know, you'll say."

I looked at him across the fire-pit — this disgraced, stubborn, honorable ruin of a man who had traded everything he was for one acquittal he still believed in — and I understood, with a lurch, that I believed him. That I wanted to tell him. That the wanting itself was more dangerous than the secret, because secrets are armor and I was sitting here letting him watch me take mine off.

"The light holds," I said instead, because my voice needed a job. "Look — it hasn't faded. The carvings drink it. As long as I stay in the circle, the shrine stays warm." I held my palm toward the wall and the nearest threads brightened eagerly, like dogs hearing their names. "Which means the dark outside—"

"—stays outside," Corin finished. He turned toward the doorway, where the storm hissed and the true dark pressed against the threshold of gold light like floodwater against a door. "How large? Could you push it further? The circle?"

"I don't know. I've never tried. Before three days ago I couldn't do any of this on purpose at all." I flexed my fingers, watching the light ripple. "It feels like it would cost something. Everything real costs."

"Then we learn the price slowly." He settled back onto the bench, and I noticed he positioned himself exactly at the edge of the brightest ring of light — close enough to share it, far enough not to crowd it. A man accustomed to giving others room. "Sleep if you can. I'll take first watch."

"You can't see anything."

"I can see you," he said simply. "You're the best-lit thing in Ashvale."

I lay down on the cold bench with my coat for a blanket and my hands folded against my chest, dimming myself as much as I could out of habit, and the little false stars turned overhead, and exhaustion pulled me down faster than I meant to go. My last coherent thought was that my secret had a witness now, and that I had shown him willingly, and that I could not decide whether that felt like freedom or like the first link of a chain.

* * *

I woke to Corin's hand on my shoulder and his voice pitched low and wrong.

"Mara. Wake up. Look at the door."

The shrine's emberlight had thinned to embers indeed, drowsy and low — I must have sunk deep, my flame banking with sleep — and beyond the threshold the dark had changed. It wasn't the ordinary blind black of the ceiling anymore. It had *texture*, a slow shifting weight, like smoke deciding whether to pour in. Pressing at the circle of my light the way a stray dog presses at a warm window. Testing it.

And threaded through it, far down the pass, faint as a memory of a memory, moved a single point of cold white light. Not a star. Stars were gone. This light traveled, bobbing, deliberate, coming up the Hollow Road toward the shrine with the patient pace of something that knew exactly where it was going.

Corin's hand slid to his knife. His face, lit half gold and half shadow, was grim as winter.

"That's no traveler's lantern," he murmured. "Travelers don't walk in this weather. And that light—" He swallowed. "Mara, that light is the exact color of the page that was torn out of your mistress's book."

The point of white light climbed the switchbacks. Below us, in the stones, my emberlight flickered — and for one heartbeat, flared the color of fear.
