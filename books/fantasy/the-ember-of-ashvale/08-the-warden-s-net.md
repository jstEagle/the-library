## 8. The Warden's Net

Corin Vale had spent twenty-two years learning how wardens thought, and in the three years since they had stripped his robes and burned his name from the rolls, he had learned something more useful: how they feared.

They feared disorder first, and paperwork second, and the dark third — though none of them would ever say so aloud, not even now, with the stars gone and a black ceiling pressing down on the whole of Ashvale like a lid on a pot.

He smelled them before he saw them. That was the thing about Crown wardens that no training manual admitted: they wore oil of cedar in their boot-leather, a preservative mandated by the Quartermaster's Office, and after years of sharing mess halls and evidence rooms with men who reeked of it, Corin could pick a warden out of a crowd blindfolded. He caught it on the wind as he and Mara came down off the ridge above the Hollow Road, where the pines grew close and the light — what light there was — fell gray and sourceless through the canopy.

He stopped. Mara, three paces ahead, walked on two more steps before she noticed the silence behind her and turned.

"What?" she said.

"Cedar," Corin said quietly. "Downwind. Two, maybe three of them."

Mara's face did something complicated — fear and recognition braided together, and beneath both, Corin was beginning to understand, a kind of weary certainty, as if she had been waiting her whole life for someone to come collect her. "Wardens?"

"Wardens." He crouched, pressing his palm flat to the needle-strewn ground out of old habit, as if the earth itself might testify. "And not a patrol. A patrol moves loud, talks loud, trusts its numbers. These are holding still. Waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

Corin looked at her. In the dimness her eyes were the color of wet slate, and there was ash dust in the loose hair at her temple, and he found — inconveniently, at the worst possible moment — that he cared whether she ran.

"For you," he said. "I'd wager my last coin on it. The apprentice of Ashvale. Alive."

* * *

They had been three days out of the watch-shrine where Mara's ember-craft had held back the dark in a circle no wider than a hearth, three days of the Hollow Road unspooling beneath their boots like a thread pulled through the country's wound. Three days of Corin turning over in his mind what he had seen her do — the small flame rising from her cupped hands like something remembered rather than made, the darkness leaning back from her as if from an old acquaintance — and saying nothing, because some knowledge, once spoken, could not be taken back into the mouth.

Now the knowledge sat between them anyway, unspoken but present, and the cedar smell drifted closer on the wind.

"We split," Mara said. It wasn't a question; it was a plan already half-executed in her head. He could see it: her weight shifting toward the downhill slope, toward the tangle of deadfall and bramble where a body as slight as hers could vanish like smoke. "You go east along the road, make noise, be visible. They want me alive, which means they'll hesitate to put a bolt in anyone standing near me, but they won't hesitate forever. I cut south through the fern brake and—"

"No."

Her head came up sharply. "No? You don't get to say no. This is my road. My country. You're the one paying for passage, Justice, not the other way around."

"Corin," he said. "When we are about to be taken or killed, you may call me Corin."

"I'm not about to be taken. I'm about to *not* be taken. There's a difference, and the difference is that I've been dodging people who wanted me found since before you lost your fancy chair." She was already moving as she spoke, low and quick, and Corin realized with a cold drop in his stomach that she meant to simply go — that all his careful plans, all his reasons for needing her, were balanced on a woman who treated capture as a weather to be walked around.

"Mara." He said it louder than he intended, and winced, and lowered his voice to a thread. "Listen to me. If they're here for you specifically — by name, by description — then this isn't a patrol that stumbled onto us. Someone told them where we'd be. Someone told them what you look like. Which means they know the route, or enough of it, and your fern brake is already covered."

She went still. He watched her run the same arithmetic he had run, watched her arrive at the same ugly sum.

"The villager," she said softly. "The well-keeper at Harrow Ford. I paid him in silver to say nothing."

"Silver buys silence for exactly as long as it takes someone to offer more. Or a rope." Corin rose slowly, keeping his silhouette low against the bole of a pine. "Which means running is what they want. A quarry that runs can be driven. They'll have the south slope netted or manned, and every hour you spend fleeing teaches them your habits. I know their doctrine, Mara. I helped write parts of it."

"And what does the doctrine say?" Her voice had gone flat and hard, the voice of a woman deciding how much to trust a man she had known eleven days.

"It says that a warden team hunting a single fugitive sets a kill-box: fixed positions on the likely escape lines, a driver element to push the quarry into them, and a commander who holds the center and gives the orders." He paused. "It also says the commander must identify himself and state the charge before force is authorized. Procedure. The law's little vanity. And procedure—" here he allowed himself the ghost of a smile, thin and cold, "—is a door that swings both ways."

* * *

They came down to the Hollow Road not as fugitives but as travelers, because that was the first move of the game and Corin had insisted on making it. Walk openly. Look bored. A man and a woman on the road under a starless sky were a thousand other things before they were fugitives: pilgrims, peddlers, kin going to a funeral. Fear read as guilt only to those already looking for it.

The ambush sprang when they reached the bend where the road narrowed between two granite shoulders — of course it did; Corin would have chosen the same ground — and three figures stepped out of the treeline with the unhurried economy of professionals.

Two carried crossbows, held low and ready. The third wore the gray-and-silver of a warden-lieutenant, and Corin knew him.

Of course he knew him. Ashvale was not so large a country, and the law not so deep a pool, that its fish did not all swim past one another. Lieutenant Dace Halloway — young when Corin had been old, sharp when Corin had been tired, promoted partly on the strength of testimony he had given in the proceedings that stripped Corin's office. Halloway's jaw was set now in the particular way of a man performing certainty he did not feel.

"Stand where you are," Halloway said. "In the name of the Crown of Ashvale."

Corin stood. Beside him, he felt Mara gather herself like a drawn wire, and he moved — slightly, deliberately — so that his shoulder came to rest just ahead of hers. Not shielding her. Positioning. There was a difference, and the difference mattered.

"Lieutenant Halloway," Corin said pleasantly. "Still wearing your boots too tight. Some things hold even when the stars don't."

Halloway's eyes flickered — surprise, then anger, then the careful blankness of a man remembering his training. "Vale." The name came out like something spat from the teeth. "You have no standing here. No office. No name on any roll."

"True. And yet here I am, standing, on a road, being addressed by a lieutenant of the Crown who has just identified himself and his authority." Corin let the words fall into the quiet like stones into a well. "Tell me, Lieutenant — what is the charge against these travelers? Because I heard you announce the Crown's name, and I saw your crossbows, and under Section Nine of the Warden's Code, a detention without stated charge is unlawful seizure, and everything seized afterward — including testimony, including confessions — is fruit of the poisoned tree. Inadmissible. You'd know this. You sat in the gallery when they taught it to me."

"You're not a magistrate anymore."

"No," Corin agreed. "But you are still a warden, and the code binds you regardless of who stands in front of you. That was rather the point of writing it down." He took one step forward, and both crossbows came up, and he stopped with his hands open and empty at his sides, palms out, the posture of surrender performed so precisely it became accusation. "State the charge, Lieutenant. Or lower the bows and let us pass, and explain to your commander why you let the quarry walk."

Something shifted in the trees to the left — a fourth figure, maybe a fifth, the driver element revealing itself because Halloway's hesitation had cost them their concealment's purpose. Corin catalogued them without moving his eyes from the lieutenant's face. Four in the brush, probably one more on the high shoulder behind. Eight, perhaps nine. Enough to take a fugitive alive. Enough to die badly if it went wrong.

"The charge," Halloway said at last, and his voice had gone careful, "is harboring. The woman is wanted for questioning in the matter of the border wards. She is to be taken alive. Those are my orders."

"Questioning." Corin tasted the word. "Under whose warrant? Issued by whom? Because I knew the magistrates still sitting, before they unseated me, and I can tell you that Magistrate Orlenne signs nothing after sundown, Magistrate Breck is dead of the flux since midwinter, and Magistrate Serl—" he smiled again, wider this time, "—Magistrate Serl is the man who disgraced me, and he would sooner sign his own death warrant than hand a live witch-apprentice to the Crown's tender mercies. So. Whose warrant, Lieutenant? Show it to me. Read it aloud. Under Section Twelve, a detainee may demand the warrant be read in full, in the presence of witnesses, and refusal voids the detention."

"There are no witnesses," one of the crossbowmen said, and immediately regretted it, because Corin turned his head with the slow gravity of a man savoring a gift.

"On the contrary," he said. "There are four of your own colleagues in that brush who will hang beside you if this goes to inquiry, and there is the woman herself, and there is me. Three years ago I was disgraced for acquitting a hedge-witch on the letter of the law while everyone in that courtroom screamed for her blood on its spirit. Do you imagine I have forgotten a single clause since? Do you imagine the law forgot *me*?" He let his voice drop, and soften, and become almost gentle, which was worse. "You were a good warden once, Halloway. I remember your casework. Clean. Careful. You believed the code was armor. So wear it. Ask yourself who gave you these orders, and whether that person's signature will protect you when the wards fail entirely and the country starts asking why its wardens spent the dark chasing one runaway girl instead of finding the door."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, on the granite shoulder, a pebble clicked loose and skittered down — the fifth man, adjusting his position, nervous. Nervous men made mistakes. Nervous commanders made worse ones.

"They'll take your badge for this," Halloway said quietly. "Whatever you're doing. Talking. Stalling. When I bring her in, they'll take what's left of you too."

"Probably," Corin said. "But here is what you have not yet understood, Lieutenant, and I will give it to you freely, because I am generous with my ruins." He turned fully to face him, and let three years of disgrace, and thirty days of the starless dark, and one night of watching a frightened girl hold back the end of the world with a handful of ember-light, rise into his voice. "The law I served was a lantern. It worked because we all agreed to carry it, and to point it at the guilty, and never to hide it under the bed of our own convenience. These past weeks I have watched men with badges use it to hunt a child while the sky itself rots overhead. That is not the law. That is a man hiding under the bed with the lantern, calling the dark justice."

He reached up, slowly — Halloway's crossbow tracked him, trembling minutely — and drew from inside his coat the one thing of his old life he had kept: a folded writ of commission, brittle with age, bearing the seal of the Ashvale courts. He had told himself he kept it as proof of identity. He had told himself many things.

He held it out.

"This is my last standing in your law," Corin said. "I return it."

And he let it fall — no, he did not let it; he threw it, deliberately, into the mud of the Hollow Road, where it landed with a sound too soft for what it was, and a wheel-rut's worth of gray water crept toward its edges.

"Burn it or keep it," he said. "Either way, I am done asking your law's permission to do what's right. Now — Section Fifteen. A detention that cannot produce its warrant within the span of a recited creed must release the detainee or answer charges of false imprisonment before a full inquiry. Recite the creed with me, Lieutenant. All of it. Slowly. Your men will wait. Your men will *have* to wait, because if they loose a bolt while an officer of the Crown is mid-recitation, every one of you becomes a murderer under the very code you serve, and I will spend my disgraced, worthless, free life making sure the whole country hears of it."

Halloway stared at him. Behind the lieutenant's eyes Corin watched a war proceed with great speed and no mercy — duty against doubt, career against conscience, the man against the machine that had made him. Corin had fought that war himself, once, and lost it slowly over twenty years. He would not wish the long defeat on anyone. But he could offer the short victory.

"Recite," Corin said.

And Halloway — hating it, hating Corin, hating the mud and the dark and the whole crooked hour — began: "*By the light that is owed to every soul—*"

* * *

They were a mile gone before Mara spoke.

She had not run during the recitation. That was the remarkable thing, the thing Corin turned over in his mind as they climbed through the fern brake in the gray non-light: she had stood beside him, hands open, watching a warden-lieutenant recite the warden's creed at dictation speed while his men stood in the brush with loaded crossbows and no order that would survive daylight. She had trusted the procedure. Or she had trusted him. He genuinely did not know which frightened her more, and suspected she didn't either.

"That was the most obscene thing I have ever seen done to a legal code," she said at last, breathing hard from the climb, "and I once watched a smuggler argue a toll bridge into waiving his fee."

"He sounds like a man of principle."

"He's in prison." She scrambled up over a fallen birch and waited for him at the top, and for a moment the sourceless gray light caught her face and he saw that she was laughing — silently, helplessly, the laughter of the nearly-dead. "You threw your commission in the mud."

"I did."

"You can't get it back."

"No."

"They'll brand you now. Obstruction, sedition, whatever Serl invents. There's no coming back from throwing it in the mud, Corin. You understand that. Whatever you were — magistrate, justice, whatever you might have been again someday if you groveled correctly — it's finished. You burned it."

He stopped on the crest of the rise and looked back the way they had come. Far below, the Hollow Road bent away through the pines, empty now, the ambush dissolved like frost in sun. Somewhere back there, a lieutenant was fishing a ruined writ out of the mud and deciding what lie to tell his commander, and four crossbowmen were discovering that their quarry had escaped them through nothing but words, and somewhere beyond that, in Lowbridge, in the capital, in the courts that still pretended to glow, men were drafting the papers that would make Corin Vale an outlaw.

He found, examining himself honestly, that he felt lighter than he had in three years. Lighter than he had in twenty.

"My daughter used to ask me why the law needed so many books," he said. Mara went very still at the word *daughter*, and he noted it, and continued, because he had decided on the road below that he was done keeping pieces of himself in reserve. "I told her it was because justice is heavy, and it takes many hands to lift it. I was wrong. It takes few hands and clean ones. Everything else is furniture." He turned back to her. "I chose, back there. Completely. There's no version of me that goes back and grovels. So the question is no longer what the law permits. The question is what's true, and who needs it said, and whether we're brave enough to say it. That's the whole of my remaining jurisprudence."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. The wind moved through the ferns around them with a sound like whispered testimony.

"You're a fool," she said finally, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "A reckless, doomed, magnificent fool. They'll catch us eventually. You know that."

"Then we'd best find your door before they do."

Something crossed her face at the word *door* — quickly, but not quickly enough, a flinch like a shadow passing over water — and Corin filed it away with all the other small locked drawers of her, the ember-light and the torn page and the way she sometimes looked north toward Thornfell as if something there were calling her home.

They walked on. Behind them, far off, a horn sounded — one note, then another, then a third, the signal pattern for *quarry sighted, support requested* — and Corin did not break stride, but he counted the intervals, and he did not like what they told him. That was not a local signal. That pattern relayed. It would reach a garrison by nightfall, and a garrison had horses, and horses did not care about creeds or codes or commissions in the mud.

"Mara," he said quietly. "How far to the next place the roads forget to exist?"

She glanced back at the horn-notes hanging in the air, and he watched her become, in real time, the smuggler again — the girl the law had never managed to hold, the apprentice who knew the veins of the country better than its own blood did.

"Half a night," she said. "If we move. There's a place where the Hollow Road dips under an old landslide and comes up somewhere else entirely. My mother showed me. Even the wardens' maps mark it wrong." She hesitated, then, for the first time offering the thing unprompted. "Even mine does, a little. Lately."

Corin looked at her. *Lately.* As if the roads themselves were changing. As if the country were rearranging its own anatomy around whatever wound lay hidden in the Thornfell.

The horns sounded again behind them, closer now, insistent, and Mara took his sleeve and pulled him downslope into the dark, and the ferns closed behind them like water over a stone.
