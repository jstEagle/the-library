## 10. The Village Under the Ceiling

The road gave out an hour past dusk, which was no hour at all anymore, only a habit of the mind. Corin had learned to measure the dark by other means: the cold coming off the stones, the way Mara's breathing changed when she scouted ahead, the faint gray bloom of lichen that seemed to be the last thing in Ashvale still willing to catch light. They followed the lichen down into a fold of hills where, without warning, the darkness turned warm.

Not bright. Warm. A glow like banked coals seen through a shuttered window, spilling up over a ridge and touching the low clouds — no, not clouds, he corrected himself, there were no clouds now, only the Ceiling, the black lid that had replaced the sky — and beneath it, terraced down the hillside, a village.

Lanterns hung from every eave. Not oil lanterns; Corin could smell no burning wick, no tallow. These burned with a color he had no name for, a deep amber shot through with red, the color of the last moment of a fire before it dies, held forever at that moment. Emberlight, the smugglers called it on the roads. He'd thought it a figure of speech.

"Emberhold," Mara said quietly beside him. "I didn't know it still stood."

"You've been here?"

"No." Her voice did something complicated. "My mother's maps mark it. She drew a little flame beside the name."

They stood at the edge of the first terrace, two travelers crusted with road dust and the particular exhaustion of people who have been hunted, and Corin watched a child run past them laughing, chasing a wooden hoop along the lantern-lit lane, and felt something in his chest crack along an old fault line.

The child had never seen the sun. That was the thought that arrived whole and uninvited. The wards fell eight months ago. This child was perhaps six. Whatever dawn was, this child knew it only as a story adults told badly, with their voices going strange.

* * *

The village took them in the way of places that have decided generosity is a discipline rather than a mood. An old woman named Bess Arden — broad-shouldered, silver-streaked hair bound back with copper wire, hands like tree roots — met them at the well and asked no questions for exactly as long as it took to feed them.

The hall she brought them to was long and low, dug partly into the hillside, its roof beams black with age and hung with dried herbs and strings of small glass bells that chimed when the draft moved. Along one wall burned a hearth the size of a wagon bed, and above it, instead of a mantel clock or a saint's icon, hung a board of slate tablets, dozens of them, each scratched with names and numbers.

Corin read them while he ate barley soup that tasted of thyme and woodsmoke. *Days since the stars went out.* Each tablet a month. Someone updated them daily. At the end of the row, a single unmarked tablet waited, blank as an open door.

"You count," he said to Bess Arden.

"We count." She ladled him more soup without asking if he wanted it. "Counting is how you refuse to let a thing become weather. The dark is weather, if you let it be. It just *is*, and folk stop noticing, and then folk stop minding, and then folk start saying maybe it was always so." She set the ladle down with a click. "It was not always so. There were stars. I saw them. And every morning we write the number, and every morning we say: not natural, not accepted, not forever unless we make it forever by forgetting."

Mara had gone very still over her bowl. Corin glanced at her and found her watching Bess with an expression he couldn't parse — hunger of some kind, but not for soup.

* * *

He learned the shape of the village over the next two days, because Bess Arden appointed herself his instructor with the same unsentimental efficiency she applied to everything.

Emberhold had reorganized itself around the loss of the sky the way a body reorganizes around a wound. The fields still grew — barley, kale, beans, things that tolerated the dim — but the yields had fallen, and so the village had built mushroom houses into the slopes, cool and damp and lit by the same ember-lanterns, and the mushrooms fed pigs, and the pigs fed the village, and nothing was wasted because waste had become a luxury they could no longer afford.

There was a school, held in the mornings in the hall, taught by a stooped former stonemason named Aldous who taught letters and numbers and, Corin was startled to see, astronomy — from memory. He'd chalked the old constellations onto slate boards: the Antlered King, the Weaver's Loom, the Seven Sisters of the Ford.

"The children can't see them," Aldous told Corin, tapping the chalk-drawn stars. "But they can know them. Knowing is a kind of seeing. Maybe the only kind that matters, in the end." He smiled, and it was a tired smile but not a broken one. "Besides. Someday the sky comes back. And when it does, I want the first generation under it to be able to say, *ah, yes, there you are, we kept your names for you.*"

Corin had spent twenty years as a magistrate. He had administered justice across three counties. He had believed — he had *built his life* on the belief — that order was the highest good a society could achieve, that law was order's skeleton, that a kingdom was a great machine and a justice was one of its gears, and that if every gear did its precise work, the machine would carry people safely through their lives.

Standing in Aldous's schoolroom, watching children who had never seen dawn copy star-charts they might never use, Corin understood with sudden terrible clarity that he had never once thought about what the machine was *for*. He had maintained the gears. He had never looked at what rolled off the assembly, or what fell between them.

* * *

On the third evening there was a festival.

Bess announced it at breakfast the way another woman might announce rain: "Ember-Night tomorrow. You'll help, both of you. Mara, you're on lantern duty with Tam. Justice—" she had learned what he was, or had been, and used the title with a dry irony neither cruel nor kind— "you'll carry the Long Ember."

"I don't know what that is."

"No one does until they carry it."

The Long Ember turned out to be a staff of black iron, taller than Corin, crowned with a cage of filigree ironwork holding a lump of stone that glowed with the same deep amber as the village lanterns. It was heavy. It was warm. When he lifted it, the crowd in the square went quiet in a way he had never heard a crowd go quiet — not the silence of fear or ceremony, but the silence of people attending to something they loved.

Then someone began to sing, and the festival unfolded around him like water finding its level.

They sang the counting-song, the number of days set to an old harvest tune, and the children shouted the number loudest — two hundred and forty-one — with a fierce pride that undid him. They carried lanterns in procession down every terrace of the village, and at each house the household came out and touched their own lantern to the Long Ember's cage, renewing the flame, and at each house the carrier spoke the same words:

"Burn true. We remember the sky."

And the household answered: "Burn true. We are remembered."

Two hundred and forty-one houses. Corin carried the Ember to every one. His arms screamed by the fortieth. By the hundredth his shoulders had stopped being arms and become simply weight, and still he climbed the terraces, and still the words passed between him and strangers, *burn true, we remember the sky, burn true, we are remembered,* until the words stopped being words and became a pulse, became the thing itself, became the whole meaning of the village compressed into a call and response that said: *we are here, we know what was taken, we have not surrendered to it, and we will not.*

At the last house — a crooked cottage at the top terrace where an ancient man lived alone with a cat — the old man touched his lantern to the cage and then pressed his hand, briefly, over Corin's knuckles.

"You're the first outsider to carry it since the dark fell," he said. "Bess doesn't trust easy. She must see something in you."

"She sees a disgraced magistrate who needs feeding," Corin managed.

"Mm." The old man smiled. "We all see what we need to. That's why the festivals matter. Once a month, everyone sees everyone clearly. No shadows to hide in — well." He laughed, a dry rustle. "No shadows but the big one."

* * *

That night Corin sat alone in the hall, unable to sleep, turning it all over like a stone with a vein of gold in it.

Here was the thing he could not stop circling: no one in Emberhold had ever petitioned a court. No one had filed a grievance. No magistrate had ever ridden these terraces with the Crown's seal and the Crown's procedures, and yet Corin had seen more justice in three days here than in twenty years of benched rulings.

Justice, he had believed, was procedure. Evidence weighed, precedents consulted, sentences rendered according to statute. He had acquitted the hedge-witch Maren of Thornbrook because the evidence demanded it, and the law had destroyed him for it, and he had spent two years nursing his grievance against the machine, believing the machine merely needed better gears. Better men. Himself, restored.

But Emberhold needed no restoration. Emberhold had looked at the impossible — the sky stolen, the world ended, the future amputated — and had responded by teaching children the names of stars, by counting days on slate, by carrying fire door to door and saying *we remember* until remembering became a fortress. Nobody here had ever read a statute. Everybody here was doing the work.

A child appeared in the doorway. One of Aldous's students, a girl of perhaps seven with ember-light caught in her hair, clutching a slate.

"You're the star-man's friend," she said. "He told us about you. He said you knew real laws."

"I used to."

"What's a law?"

Corin opened his mouth to give the answer he'd have given at the bench — a rule binding upon the realm, enforced by rightful authority — and found it wouldn't come out. He looked at the girl waiting, patient, slate in her lap, and tried again.

"A law," he said slowly, "is a promise that everybody makes together, so that nobody gets hurt."

She considered this with great seriousness. "Like the counting?"

"Like the counting," he said, and his throat tightened. "Exactly like the counting."

"Good," she said. "I like the counting." And she went away to bed.

Corin sat in the ember-light a long while after, a disgraced justice learning jurisprudence from a seven-year-old, and understanding that everything he had lost — the office, the robes, the seal — had never been the thing itself. The thing itself was here. It had always been here, in villages like this, in the promises people made together, and the law was only ever its shadow on a wall, and he had spent his life worshipping the shadow and flogging himself when the shadow disappointed him.

* * *

He found Mara at the well near midnight, as he'd known he would. She kept watchman's hours even in safety; sleep sat poorly on her, and he had learned not to take it personally.

She was looking up. Everyone in Emberhold looked up sometimes, he'd noticed — a quick glance at the Ceiling, a flinch, a look away. But Mara stared at it the way other people stare at the sea.

"They had a festival tonight," she said, without turning. "I carried lanterns."

"So did I. Two hundred and forty-one doors' worth."

"My arms are dead."

"Mine died around house ninety." He leaned on the well beside her. For a while neither spoke. The ember-lanterns hummed very softly, a sound he'd decided was either the flames or the village dreaming.

"They're not broken," Mara said at last. "Did you notice? All of us on the roads — the refugees, the bandits, the wardens, us — we're all acting like the world ended. And it did, sort of. But it didn't *end* end. Look at them. They counted the days. They taught the kids the stars. They made a festival out of defiance." Her voice roughened. "I ran my whole life like survival meant getting small enough that nothing could find me. And these people survived by getting *bigger*. Filling the dark up with themselves."

Corin watched her profile against the lantern-glow, this woman who trusted him with routes and rations and almost nothing else, offering him this much — an admission shaped like an observation about villagers.

"That's what courage actually looks like," he said. "I spent twenty years thinking it looked like verdicts. Standing at a bench and being right. But it's this. It's carrying fire to two hundred and forty-one doors because the alternative is letting the dark decide what you're worth."

Mara finally turned and looked at him, and in the ember-light her eyes had flecks of the same deep amber, and he thought — not for the first time, but for the first time letting himself finish the thought — that whatever she was, whatever secret she carried behind those eyes, it was not a small one.

"Tomorrow we climb again," she said. "The pass through the Fell opens on the far side of the ridge. Three days, if the roads behave."

"And if they don't?"

"Then longer." She hesitated, and he watched her decide to give him one more piece. "Corin. The roads have been... moving. Rearranging. I told you the Hollow Road shifts, but it's worse than shifting now. It's like the land itself is — organizing. Around something." She looked back up at the Ceiling. "And tonight, during the festival, I felt it pull. Like a current under the floor of the world. Toward the Fell."

"Toward the Door."

"Toward whatever's at the Door." She wrapped her arms around herself. "And here's the part I didn't want to say in front of Bess. The pull isn't just in the roads. It's in *me*. Has been since the wards fell. Like something on the other side knows I'm coming, and is — calling."

The word hung between them in the warm dark. Corin thought of the stolen grimoire page, of the phrase he had copied into his own notebook and shown no one: *the Door requires two keys, raised for the purpose, breaker and keeper.* He thought of the amber flecks in her eyes. He said none of it. Some truths, he reasoned, were like the Long Ember — too heavy to hand to someone until their arms were ready.

Instead he said, "Then we'd best arrive before it gets impatient."

Mara laughed — short, surprised, real — and for one moment the well-side was warm in a way that had nothing to do with lanterns.

They left at first light, which was to say they left when Bess Arden banged the hall door and declared morning, because there was no other way to know. She packed them bread and mushrooms and a small clay pot of ember-paste that she said would hold a flame in any wind, and she pressed it into Corin's hands specifically, and she said:

"You carry fire well, Justice. Come back someday and tell us how the story ends."

At the edge of the last terrace they paused and looked back. Emberhold glowed in the blackness like a coal cupped in a dark hand, terraces of amber rising the hillside, and somewhere below a child was singing the counting-song to herself, off-key and unbothered, *two hundred and forty-two, two hundred and forty-two.*

Then the ridge swallowed the light, and they climbed into the country of the moving roads, toward the Fell, toward the pull that Mara would not speak of again but that Corin could feel radiating off her like heat off a stone — and behind them, faint but persistent, the sound of a village refusing, one day at a time, to forget the sky.
