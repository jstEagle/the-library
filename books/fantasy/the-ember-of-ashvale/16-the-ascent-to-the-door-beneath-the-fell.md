## 16. The Ascent to the Door Beneath the Fell

The mountain had begun to eat the sky.

Corin felt it before he saw it — a pressure at the back of the neck, like a hand pressing down through the crown of his skull, gentle and enormous. He stopped on the switchback above the Greywater gorge and looked up, and there it was: the ceiling of dark that had swallowed Ashvale's stars was lower now. Not metaphorically. Measurably, horribly lower. Three weeks ago it had hung at the height of heaven, a black lid over the world. Now it scraped the shoulders of the Thornfell peaks, and where it touched stone, the stone wore frost in midsummer.

Beside him, Mara stood with her head tipped back, her breath smoking in air that should have been warm.

"It's coming down," she said. "It's actually coming down."

"Then we climb faster than it does."

She gave him a look — half scorn, half the fierce bright thing he'd come to think of as hers alone — and shouldered her pack. "Faster than the sky, Justice. You always did set modest goals."

He almost smiled. It hurt his face to do it; three days of cold and fear had stiffened everything in him except the ache of watching her. Since the ford, since the warden's cellars and the escape he still dreamed about with his heart hammering, something had changed between them that neither of them named. They walked close enough that their sleeves brushed. When the trail narrowed, she took his hand without looking, the way you'd take a rope.

Behind them, forty villagers from the settlement under the ceiling came up the pass in a long, quiet line. They were farmers and smiths and grandmothers, armed with billhooks and hunting bows and one ancient pike that had last seen service in Corin's grandfather's war. Their captain was a stonemason named Aldric Vey with forearms like bridge pylons and a voice pitched low, as if the dark overhead were a sleeping thing they must not wake.

"The rear guard is set," Vey reported, falling into step beside Corin. "Bram's people are watching the lower switchbacks. If Sereth's lot come up behind us, they'll wish they hadn't."

"They'll come," Corin said. "Sereth let us leave the village too easily."

Vey spat. "Let us?"

"A cat lets a mouse reach the edge of the barn." Corin watched the dark ceiling grind against the highest crag, shedding a fine grey ash that drifted down like snow that had forgotten how to be clean. "They want us to lead them to the Door. We're not the rescue party, Captain. We're the hounds, and someone holds our leash whether we know it or not."

Mara had gone very still. "Then why climb at all?"

"Because the Door opens either way," Corin said. "With us there, or without us. And I'd rather be standing on the threshold when it happens than hiding in a valley when the sky comes down to meet the ground."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon into a country that no longer obeyed the seasons.

Ash fell in slow grey curtains, settling on their shoulders, their lashes, the muzzles of the pack mules. It did not burn. That was the worst of it — it was cold ash, star-ash, the residue of light itself being pressed out of the world above. Where it gathered thickly it deadened sound, so that forty people could move up a mountainside in near silence, their boots printing grey ghosts on the rock.

The passes were collapsing. Corin heard the first of it an hour past midday: a deep groan from the ridge above, then a crack like a giant knuckle, and a section of trail the width of a cart simply folded away into the gorge below. The mountain was shrugging off its own skin. Whether it was the weight of the descending dark or something older waking beneath the Thornfell, no one could say, but the smuggler roads — Mara's roads — were dying as they climbed them.

She navigated by memory and by touch. Twice she stopped, laid her palm flat against the rock, and closed her eyes, and Corin realized she was listening to the road the way her mother must have listened once, before anyone had spoken of mothers to him. The third time, she came away from the stone frowning.

"This way's wrong now," she murmured. "The Hollow Road used to bend east here, around the spine. It doesn't anymore. It wants to go up." She looked at him, and in the flat grey light her eyes held a color he couldn't name — ember-light, he thought later, though there was no fire anywhere. "Corin. The roads aren't just shifting around the wound. They're converging. Every path on this mountain is bending toward the Door."

"Like veins around a heart."

"Worse." She wiped ash from her mouth. "Like rivers around a drain."

They crossed a hanging shelf of scree where the footing was all loose slate, and Vey's rearguard sang — softly, under their breath, one of the ember-festival songs from the village under the ceiling, a counting song about keeping lights alive. *One for the hearth and two for the door, three for the dark that asks for more.* The song carried strangely in the deadened air, arriving at Corin's ears a half-beat late, as if even sound had to fight its way uphill now.

At the far side of the scree, a boy of perhaps fourteen — Tam, who had never seen dawn, who had been born under the starless dark and thought lanterns were a kind of weather — tugged Corin's sleeve.

"Master Justice," the boy said. "Is it true there was stars? Little ones, all over?"

"There were thousands, lad. More than you could count in a lifetime."

Tam considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "And they just went out?"

"They were taken," Corin said. "That's why we're climbing. To take them back."

The boy nodded slowly, satisfied, and ran ahead to tell the others that the little lights had been stolen, which was, Corin reflected, as accurate a summary of the whole affair as any he'd delivered from a bench of law.

* * *

The first attack came at dusk — or what would have been dusk, if dusk still meant anything beneath a ceiling that never changed.

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, fires banked to embers because smoke drew arrows, when Vey's whistle cut the dark: two notes, rising. *Contact, below.*

They came up the switchbacks fast and silent, Sereth's harriers in grey-and-black leathers, faces masked against the ash, moving with the terrible economy of people who had trained on these same broken roads. Arrows first — three of them, thudding into the watchtower's old timbers, one pinning a man's cloak to a beam. Then the rush, blades out, straight up the trail at the center of the camp.

But the villagers did not break. That was the thing Corin would remember afterward, in the years when memory was all he had of some of them: they did not break. A grandmother with a billhook stepped into the narrowest point of the trail and made herself a gate. The smith fought with a hammer in each fist. Bram, the scout, took an arrow through the shoulder and kept shooting until his bowstring snapped, and then kept shouting distances anyway, because his voice was worth more than his arm.

Corin fought the way he had learned to fight in the warden academies — economically, defensively, using the enemy's momentum. But somewhere in the second minute he stopped fighting like a justice and started fighting like a man defending a door with Mara behind it, and that was a different discipline entirely.

She was beside him, back to back, her hedge-craft crackling along her knife in thin blue threads — the old wards' language, written in light. Where she struck, the masked harriers flinched as if burned, and one of them tore off his mask and fled screaming down the trail, shouting words Corin didn't catch but understood anyway: *it's her, it's the keeper, run.*

Then the hillside answered.

From the rocks above the camp, stones began to fall — deliberate, aimed, hurled by the rest of Vey's rearguard from positions they'd taken in the last hour of light. The harriers, caught between the villagers' line below and the rain of rock above, broke. They withdrew down the mountain in good order, dragging their wounded, leaving four dead and two arrows' worth of pride.

The cost: two villagers dead, three wounded, one of them Bram, who lay propped against the watchtower wall while a grandmother stitched his shoulder by ember-light, refusing both brandy and sympathy.

"We paid," Vey said quietly, standing over the bodies. He said it the way he'd square a ledger. "Two for the passage. Fair, for a mountain like this."

Corin looked at the dead — a farmer named Ossan who had sung bass in the festival circles, and a girl of nineteen whose name Corin learned only then, Maret, who had wanted to see dawn before she died and had climbed a mountain to try. He stood over them for a long moment, and then he did what he had done at every trial where the verdict cost someone everything: he spoke their names aloud, formally, so that witnesses heard them.

"Ossan Fenner. Maret Colvane. They bought the road we walk tomorrow. Let no one forget the price."

Vey nodded once, satisfied, and Corin understood that this — not the courtroom, not the seal, not the title he'd burned — this was the justice he had left: naming what things cost, and refusing to pretend otherwise.

Mara found him afterward at the edge of the camp, where the ash fell soft as forgiveness and forgave nothing.

"You knew they'd follow," she said.

"I hoped they wouldn't."

"You're a bad liar for a lawyer."

"I was always a bad liar," Corin said. "It's why they took my office." He turned to face her. In the ember-light her face was all planes and shadows, and the blue thread of ward-craft still faintly traced her knuckles, fading. "Mara. If Sereth wants us at the Door — if we're walking into a trap with our eyes open —"

"Then we walk faster," she said, "and arrive angry." She touched his jaw, briefly, ash-grey fingers on stubble. "Get some sleep, Justice. Tomorrow the mountain gets steep."

* * *

It got steep.

Above the watchtower the trail abandoned switchbacks entirely and went at the fellside like a man going at a locked door — straight up, through fields of frost-killed pine and shelves of granite glazed with ice that had no business existing in high summer. The dark ceiling pressed lower. Corin could nearly see it now, a black underside rolling with slow motion like the belly of a storm cloud, and where it met the peaks ahead, the stone simply *ended*, sheared off smooth as glass, as if the sky had taken a bite.

The air grew thin and wrong. Fires burned sullenly, reluctant. Breath came in white rags. And the ash fell thicker, packing into the trail, into their collars, into the wounds of the wounded, so that the column moved wrapped in grey like a procession of penitents.

On the second morning they reached the place the maps called the Shepherd's Stair — a natural staircase of stone, a thousand steps worn by pilgrims in the centuries before anyone remembered what they'd been pilgrimaging toward. Halfway up, the stair was broken. A whole flight had sheared away, leaving a gap of thirty feet over a drop that ended in cloud and cold.

Mara stood at the edge for a long time, palm against the rock, listening.

"There's a way," she said finally. "Underneath. An old smugglers' gallery — a ledge cut behind the stair-face. My mother's maps show it. It'll hold maybe six at a time, and it hasn't been walked in fifty years."

"And if it's rotted through?"

"Then we've paid more than two," she said, and started down.

They crossed in sixes, roped together, in wind that moaned through the gap like a choir of the drowned. Corin went in the fourth group, with Vey and two of the wounded. The ledge was narrower than promised, slick with frost, crumbling at the edges, and halfway across a section the length of a coffin simply dropped away beneath the man in front of him — young Tam, the boy who had never seen dawn.

The boy screamed and swung, rope snapping taut, boots skating on nothing, and the whole group froze because any movement might bring the rest of the ledge down. Below him the cloud swallowed everything; below the cloud, presumably, the world.

"Hold," Corin said, and heard his own voice go flat and calm, the courtroom voice, the voice that did not shake because twelve people's futures depended on it not shaking. "Tam. Look at me. Not down. At me."

The boy's eyes found his, huge and white-rimmed.

"Now listen. There was a girl in my last case who fell off a bridge. Worse than this. She held on for an hour and we pulled her up and she's farming in the westlands now, complaining about the price of seed. You are going to complain about the price of seed someday. Do you understand me? That is your future and I am a justice and I am telling you it is *law*."

Something like a laugh tore out of the boy, half sob. His hands steadied.

They hauled him up hand over hand, Vey's great arms taking most of the weight, and when Tam lay gasping on solid stone, ash in his hair and tears freezing on his cheeks, he grabbed Corin's wrist and said, "You lied. About the girl."

"Every word," Corin agreed. "I'll answer for it at judgment."

* * *

They lost the mules on the third day, and after the mules, they lost the easy way entirely.

The final approach to the Door Beneath the Fell was a gorge — the grimoire called it the Throat — a slot of black stone winding upward into the mountain's heart, so narrow in places that the party had to turn sideways, so deep that noon was a rumor. The walls sweated frost. The dark ceiling, visible as a ragged ribbon of black directly overhead now, seemed to press down into the gorge itself, and the ash here was ankle-deep, and warm.

Warm ash. Corin tried not to think about what that meant.

Sereth's forces harried them through the Throat in relays — never a full assault, just arrows from ambush points, rockfalls triggered from above, snipers' whistling shafts that forced the column to hug the walls while the wounded stumbled. It was attrition warfare, patient and cruel, designed not to stop them but to *thin* them, to make the last mile cost so much that whoever reached the Door arrived broken.

Vey countered with the stubbornness of his trade. The stonemason read the gorge like a blueprint, predicting where the rockfalls would fall, posting archers on the high shelves, turning every ambush into a grinding, expensive stalemate. Twice Corin saw him take position personally at the point of greatest danger, arms crossed, immovable as his own masonry, daring the mountain to argue.

But the toll accumulated. Another villager dead — a bowman, killed by the last arrow of the last ambush, falling into the warm ash without a sound. Bram's wound turned feverish; he walked anyway, propped between two friends, refusing to be carried, refusing to be left. Mara's ward-craft flickered along her hands constantly now, unbidden, blue threads rising off her skin like heat-shimmer, and Corin watched her watching her own hands with an expression he couldn't read and feared to ask about.

Because he knew something she didn't. Because he'd read the torn page — the page Sereth stole from the grimoire, the page Corin had recovered from a dead harrier's coat at the ford and had not yet shown her. *Two keys,* the page said. *A breaker and a keeper, raised for the lock from childhood's first hour.* He had known since the prison cellars what Mara was. He had told himself a hundred reasons for his silence — that she'd falter, that she'd flee, that the truth would drive her away at the moment they could least afford it.

Walking in the warm ash with the sky pressing down, Corin finally admitted the plainer truth: he was afraid that if she knew she had been *made* for this — that her whole life, her running, her invisibility, her freedom, had been the long apprenticeship of a lock — she would stop being Mara in her own mind. And he could not bear to be the one who took her from her.

"Gorge bends ahead," Vey called back, low. "Open ground beyond. Big as a cathedral, by the echo."

Mara fell into step beside Corin, her shoulder against his, her voice pitched for him alone. "Whatever's at the top of this throat, Corin — whatever's waiting — I need you to promise me something."

"Anything."

"If it comes to a choice, you get the others out. You don't stay for me. You've spent your whole life paying other people's prices. Not this one."

He looked at her — at the ash in her hair, the ward-light trembling at her fingertips, the woman the law had discarded and the road had remade — and he lied to her the way he'd lied to Tam on the ledge, easily and completely and knowing he'd answer for it.

"I promise," he said.

Ahead, the Throat opened its last bend, and light that was not light spilled out of the darkness beyond — a vast ember-red glow, pulsing slow as a heartbeat, rising from the floor of a cavern the size of a buried sky. And standing in that glow, small and still and waiting, figures in grey, ranks deep, with a tall shape at their center holding a lantern that burned the color of the glow itself.

Sereth had stopped running.

They had been expected all along.
