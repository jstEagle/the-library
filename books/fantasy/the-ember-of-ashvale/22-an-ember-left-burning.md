## 22. An Ember Left Burning

The mountain had not forgiven him, and Corin Vale had never expected it to.

Sixty-one years old now, and his knees announced every switchback like a magistrate reading charges. The Thornfell path was narrower than he remembered — or perhaps he had simply been younger the last time he climbed it, younger and more afraid and carrying a lantern that did not go out. His breath came in white ribbons. The wind off the high passes smelled of stone and old snow and, beneath it all, faintly, of woodsmoke that came from no fire he could see.

He stopped at the bend where the trail turned above the Greywater gorge and let himself lean against the rock, honest about his age if nothing else. Below him the river ran bright with morning, the same water they had crossed at the ford, the same water that had run red-tinged with ward-light on the night everything ended. Ten years since. Sometimes it felt like ten days. Sometimes it felt like a life someone else had lived and told him about over wine.

The stars were back. That was the thing people forgot to marvel at anymore, the way they forgot to marvel at bread or rain or their children's voices. The stars had returned dimmed — the scholars said perhaps one in ten of them was missing still, held in trust somewhere, kept like coins in a strongbox — but they returned, and Ashvale had learned to live inside its own borders without calling it a cage. Caravans moved again through the lowland gates when the Crown finally admitted what everyone already knew: the darkness beyond the wards was not Ashvale's prison. It was Ashvale's wall. There was a difference, and the difference had cost a runaway apprentice everything, and it had been worth it.

Corin climbed on.

* * *

He had testified for the last time four years ago, in the restored courts of Lowbridge, before magistrates young enough to be his grandchildren and solemn enough to think that made them wise. He had stood at the witness bench with his back straight and told them the truth about the Cage of Order — about the plan to re-key the wards with a living girl's blood, about the interrogation rooms under the Hall of Records, about how easily justice becomes the thing it hunts when it stops asking whom it serves. He had watched the young magistrate who ordered it stripped of office, and he had felt nothing like triumph. Only the tired certainty of a man closing a ledger.

Then he had walked out of the courtroom while they were still adjourning, and he had not gone back.

They still sent him letters. Petitioners, mostly — people who had heard of the justice who acquitted a witch and lost everything for it, who wanted his name on their grievances because his name meant something now in a way it never had when he wore the chain of office. He answered some. He had learned, from a woman who gave up her name to become a road, that justice is not a building you sit inside. It is a thing you carry, like a lantern, into whatever dark will have you.

But this morning he carried nothing heavier than a pack with bread, a flask of plum brandy, and the lantern itself, wrapped in wool against the jostle of the climb.

* * *

The watch-shrine appeared around the noon bend exactly as memory insisted it must: a low stone hollow built into the mountainside by wardens three hundred years dead, its roof fallen open to the sky, its walls furred with grey lichen. Someone had repaired it — of course someone had. Pilgrims came now, quietly, the way people visit graves. Offerings cluttered the alcove where an old ward-stone still stood: dried flowers gone to dust, a child's carved wooden bird, ribbons faded colorless, a tin cup filled with rainwater and one floating rowan leaf.

And scratched into the lintel stone, in a hand Corin knew better than his own signature:

*She saw me here first.*

He set down his pack and lowered himself onto the bench of stone with a grunt, and for a long time he simply sat, letting the wind comb through his grey hair, looking out at the valley where the world had once gone black from edge to edge.

It was here. This was the place. An abandoned shrine on the fourth night of their journey, when she had cupped her hands around a dying fire and done something impossible — pulled warmth out of her own blood, kindled a circle of ember-light that held back the pressing dark like a cupped palm holds back a tide. He remembered the look on her face: terror and wonder in equal measure, a woman watching her own secret catch fire in front of the only witness she hadn't chosen. He remembered thinking, *I should pretend I didn't see.* He remembered being unable to lie to her, even then, even about that.

"You saw me," she'd said later, furious and frightened.

"I saw you," he'd agreed. "That was the whole problem."

* * *

He ate half the bread. He drank a finger of the brandy and poured a second finger onto the stones, out of habit rather than belief — though belief was a strange country for him these days, and he wandered its borders more freely than he once had. A man who had watched the love of his life dissolve into light and remain, somehow, present in every mile of road in the kingdom, could hardly be a strict materialist.

Because that was the truth of it, the truth the priests argued about and the scholars wrote dull treatises on and the villagers under the old ceiling had never needed convincing of: Mara Joubert was everywhere now. Not as a ghost. As a *ward*. Travelers spoke of warmth on cold passes where no fire burned. Lost children reported lights that walked ahead of them and waited. Smugglers — the new generation, softer than her, though none of them soft enough — swore certain roads felt *watched*, in the way a garden is watched, in the way a mother watches.

The Ember of Ashvale, they called her. They had gotten it almost right. She had never been Ashvale's ember. She had been his.

He unwrapped the lantern.

It had not dimmed in ten years. It had not consumed oil or wick or anything a lamp ought to consume; it simply burned, a small steady gold flame behind horn-thin glass, warm to the touch and utterly indifferent to wind. On the worst nights of the first year — the nights when the house in Lowbridge was too quiet and her absence had a shape, a specific weight, like a person-shaped hole cut from the air — he had carried it room to room with him like a child with a candle against nightmares. He was not ashamed of that. Grief makes honest men of us all, eventually, whether we consent or no.

"I'm getting old, Mara," he said aloud, to the empty shrine, feeling foolish and doing it anyway. "My knees file formal complaints. I've started talking to my horse. His name is Barley and he has no opinions worth hearing." The flame flickered — or seemed to. Wind, probably. "I finished the casebook. The one about the border trials. It's duller than I hoped and truer than I feared, which I'm told is the most one can ask."

Wind. Just wind, moving through the broken roof, stirring the dust of ten years of offerings.

"The village still stands. Under the ceiling, I mean — they call it Emberfall now, which would have embarrassed you so thoroughly you'd have changed your name again. The children there have seen dawn by now, real dawn, and half of them were disappointed. Too slow, they said. Too pink." He smiled despite himself, the ache of it familiar as an old scar. "I told them you'd have agreed with them."

He rose, joints protesting, and carried the lantern to the alcove beside the ward-stone. He had thought about this moment for years — rehearsed it on other climbs, on the long grey evenings, standing at windows. What do you do with a miracle you've been keeping? You cannot hold it forever. Holding is not the same as keeping, and keeping is not the same as loving, and loving — she had taught him this at the Door, in the last minutes, her hands already turning to light — loving means setting the thing down where it can do its work.

He set the lantern among the offerings, between the wooden bird and the rowan leaf, and stepped back.

The flame did not gutter. It leaned toward the ward-stone, the way a dog leans toward a known hand, and steadied, and burned.

"There," he said, and his voice cracked on the single syllable like ice on a pond. "There. That's — that's yours, then. Where we—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. Sixty-one years old, disgraced twice over, celebrated posthumously in advance by people who didn't know he was still alive, and undone entirely by a lantern on a mountain. "You saw me here first. I remember. I remember all of it. Every road. Every argument. The ford. The grimoire. The way you bargained like a fishwife and fought like a warden and forgave like neither."

The wind rose.

It came up the gorge in a long slow breath, stirring the ribbons, lifting the dust, and within it — woven into it, the way a voice is woven into an echo — something that was not wind at all.

*Corin.*

Not spoken. Not quite heard. Felt, the way you feel sunlight through a window on your closed eyelids, the way you feel a name called from another room. Warmth moved through the shrine, through his chest, through the marrow of him, and the ember-flame in the lantern flared tall and gold and glad.

"Hello," he managed. "Hello, Mara."

The warmth deepened. It was amusement, he realized. She was laughing at him — gently, the way she had laughed at his stubborn honor across a bargaining table ten years ago, two strangers trading terms neither intended to keep. And beneath the laughter, unmistakable, patient, *hers*: the sense of a presence that had never actually left him, that had been in every mile of road and every quiet evening and every letter he'd answered and every one he hadn't, holding the light in trust, waiting for him to be ready to climb.

"I know," he said. "I know you're everywhere. I know you're the roads now, and the passes, and God knows half the reason the smugglers behave themselves." He laughed, wet-eyed. "I just wanted — I wanted to say it where you first let me see you. That I never stopped. That the terms held. All of them. Even the ones we never wrote down."

The wind circled the shrine once, slowly, deliberately, touching each offering in turn — the bird, the ribbons, the cup, the lantern — the way she used to check the perimeter of a camp, thorough and unostentatious, a habit of survival transmuted into a habit of care.

And then, faint as starlight on snow, clear as a hand on his shoulder:

*Walk me home, Justice.*

He looked up at the trail, silvered with afternoon, descending through the pines toward the valley, toward Lowbridge, toward the long ordinary life he had built around an extraordinary absence that had never, in truth, been absent at all.

"All the way?" he asked.

The warmth wrapped around him like a cloak. Like an answer. Like a promise kept in both directions, across the distance between a man and the road he loved, which had never been any distance at all.

Corin Vale shouldered his empty pack, left the lantern burning among the offerings where pilgrims would find it and wonder, and started down the mountain — slower than he'd come up, unhurried, accompanied — while behind him the ember flared once, softly, like a wave goodbye, and settled into its long bright vigil over the pass.

The stars came out early that evening, dimmed but present, held in trust.

Every road home was warm.
