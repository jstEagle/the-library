## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width by the time Kestrel Nystrom reached the bottom of the second crate, and he made a note of that too — *one candle, consumed* — because the Guild of Caerlum accounted for everything, including the slow murder of its own light.

He sat alone in the counting room on the third floor of the guildhall, where the windows were tall and the glass was old enough to have gone slightly green, so that the lamplight of the harbor below came through it looking like something drowned. It was the eleventh hour of a Tuesday in late autumn, and outside, Caerlum was doing what Caerlum always did after dark: closing its shutters, banking its fires, and pretending not to hear the sea.

Kestrel dipped his pen. He wrote in a small, even hand that Master Odell once described as "the handwriting of a man who has never once been interesting," which Kestrel had taken as the compliment it was meant to be.

*Inventory audit, autumn quarter. Item: one jar, sealed, containing a wind. Provenance: the Shepherds' Reach, sixty years past. Condition: jar intact. Wind: restless.*

He turned the jar on its shelf with two fingers and watched the little grey swirl inside press against the glass like a cat wanting out. It had been restless for sixty years. It would be restless long after everyone currently breathing in Caerlum was done breathing. That was the nature of the guild's stock: it did not spoil, did not rot, did not age — which was precisely why no one wanted it anymore.

The world had stopped needing impossible things.

Once — and there were ledgers to prove it, whole rooms of them, bound in oxblood leather with brass corners gone dull as old teeth — the Guild of Caerlum had been the richest concern on the Ninefold Trade. Kings had sent factors here with chests of coin to buy bottled storms, keys to doors that no longer existed, the last breath of a saint decanted into a silver vial. The guild's motto was stitched above the great hall door in thread that still hadn't faded: *We Keep What Cannot Be Kept.*

Now the motto hung over a building with three working fireplaces and eleven members, four of whom were over seventy, and Kestrel wrote the quarterly numbers in a ledger that would, if the trend held another decade, fit entirely inside a single volume instead of forty.

He didn't mind. That was the strange truth he'd never said aloud to anyone: he didn't mind at all. A dying guild was a quiet guild, and a quiet guild was a place where a careful man could be invisible in comfort.

Kestrel had been invisible for twenty-two years, ever since Master Odell had pulled him, a seven-year-old orphan with singed eyebrows and no history, out of a charity queue and put a broom in his hand. Sweep first, Odell had said. Then count. A boy who can sweep and count will always eat. Kestrel had swept for six years and counted for sixteen, and he had learned every floorboard of the guildhall, every quirk of its locks, every debtor's name in its books, and almost no one in the city could have picked him out of a crowd of clerks. He was of average height, average coloring, with a face that invited description only in the aggregate — *youngish, dark-haired, forgettable.* He liked it that way. Invisible men paid lower bribes, told fewer lies, and slept well.

Mostly well.

He worked down the inventory shelf by shelf, because the audit had an order and order was the whole religion of the trade. Jar of wind. Box of winter, small, from the northern passes — he noted, with professional disapproval, that the seal had been re-waxed improperly sometime before his tenure and the box now sweated frost in summer. A mirror that showed the viewer at age ten; a rope that untied itself when lied to (Odell kept it coiled around nothing, and it stayed obediently loose); a seed that grew into whatever tree the planter most feared. The fearsome oak in the courtyard, the one the apprentices weren't allowed to climb, had come from that seed, planted by some guildmaster a century dead who must have feared shade, or roots, or time itself.

Item by item. Weight, condition, provenance, valuation. The valuations were the saddest column. A jar containing a wind had once been appraised at nine hundred crowns. The current book value was *sentimental only*, which was accounting language for *worthless, but we cannot bring ourselves to say so.*

Kestrel was halfway through the third crate — the one holding the guild's collection of letters that arrived before they were written, all unopened, all waiting — when he heard the step on the stair.

He knew it without looking up. Master Odell climbed stairs the way old men wade rivers: slowly, testing each footing, with pauses that were not quite sighs. The door opened on the smell of pipe smoke and camphor.

"You're still here." Odell's voice was gravel poured over paper. "I told Brannic to lock up."

"He did. I have the key." Kestrel didn't look up from the crate. "Quarterly audit doesn't do itself."

"The quarterly audit has been done by you alone for six quarters running, because you refuse help and I refuse to force you." Odell lowered himself into the second chair, the good one, the one with the cushion, which was the closest thing to a concession the old man had ever made to his own mortality. He looked at the crates, the shelves, the green-glassed windows, the drowned light. "You know what my master used to say? Audits are how a guild tells itself the truth. Most institutions can't afford that habit."

"Your master sounds like he'd have hated me."

"He'd have adored you. That's worse." Odell took out his pipe but didn't light it — the counting room was a no-flame room, one of the guild's oldest rules, though nobody remembered exactly what was in the crates that made fire a bad idea, and nobody wanted to find out experimentally. He gestured with the unlit pipe at the crate Kestrel was working. "The prophetic stock?"

"The letters that arrive early. Yes."

"Anything new?"

"A letter postmarked next spring. Brannic's niece writes that she's thinking of visiting us in March." Kestrel allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "She hasn't thought of it yet."

Odell grunted, which from him was laughter. Then he went quiet in the way that Kestrel had learned, over twenty years, to read like weather. There were silences that meant the old man was falling asleep upright, and silences that meant he was deciding whether to say something, and this was the second kind.

"Say it," Kestrel said, still writing.

"The Vane woman came by again today. Sable Vane. Ashen Court."

"I know. I signed her in." Every visitor was signed in; that was rule two, right after sweep-first. "She asked about the deep archive. I told her the deep archive is closed for cataloguing."

"It has been 'closed for cataloguing' for three years."

"Yes." Kestrel set down his pen. "It's a very thorough catalogue."

Odell studied him across the guttering candle. The old man's eyes were pale as watered milk and missed nothing, which was inconvenient, because Kestrel had built his entire life on being missed. "You lie well for a man who never practiced."

"I don't lie. I decline to confirm." He picked the pen back up. "Why does the Ashen Court care about our archives? They're ash-chanters. Fire mystics. We sell jars of wind to collectors who want conversation pieces."

"They asked for one thing." Odell said it carefully, the way a man walks on ice he doesn't trust. "They asked if we still held anything from the Emberwright estate."

Kestrel's pen paused for half a heartbeat. Only half. He'd trained the pause out of himself years ago; a ledger-keeper's face was part of the guild's security, same as the locks.

"We hold nothing from anyone's estate," he said. "We hold inventory. And the Emberwright line ended a hundred years ago. Burned out. Everyone knows."

"Everyone knows," Odell repeated softly. "That phrase has done more damage in this world than any lie, because it lets liars off the work of lying." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and stood a moment at the window looking down at the harbor lights. "Finish your audit, Kestrel. But when you get to the old codex — the founding ledger, the one chained to the desk in the archive — mind how you touch it. Old ink remembers hands."

"That's superstition, Master."

"Mm," said Odell, and went down the stairs slower than he'd come up, and Kestrel told himself the cold feeling at the base of his skull was just the draft from the window.

* * *

He finished the prophetic stock at half past midnight and should have gone home to his narrow bed over the chandlery, because a careful man keeps careful hours. Instead he found himself descending to the archive with the ring of keys he was not officially permitted to carry, telling himself it was the audit's fault. An audit that touched everything had to touch the founding codex eventually. It was procedure. He was a procedural man.

The deep archive was under the counting room, down a spiral stair worn concave by a century of feet. It smelled of stone and tallow and the particular dry sweetness of very old paper, which Kestrel privately considered the best smell in the world, better than bread, better than rain. The founding codex lay chained to its reading desk — literally chained, a length of black iron bolted to the spine, because the first guildmasters had trusted each other so little that they'd built their distrust into the furniture.

He unlocked the chain's clasp, opened the cover, and felt the draft again, or something like it, walking up his spine.

The codex was the guild's genesis: the first entries, the first oaths, the first inventory of wonders gathered when the Ninefold Trade was young and the world still had edges left to map. The earliest pages were written in a hand so fine and fierce it looked less like penmanship than engraving. In the margin of the first page, someone — the founder herself, by tradition, a woman named Maren Caerlum whose name the city had swallowed whole — had written the guild's true motto, the unofficial one, the one that wasn't stitched anywhere:

*What is kept is owed.*

Kestrel turned pages. He knew this book; he'd catalogued it twice. First oath, first seal, first debt recorded (a king of somewhere forgotten, owing the guild "one favor, unfalsifiable"). He moved through it the way he moved through all records: steadily, completely, missing nothing.

Which was why he stopped.

The page was near the middle of the codex, in a section headed *Omens Recorded for the Archive* — prophecies the guild had bought over the years, as it bought everything, because prophecy was just information arriving early, and information was stock. Most of the entries were dull. Crops. Kings. The usual riddles that resolved into nothing or into weather.

This entry was dated.

Not the date it was purchased. The date it concerned.

Kestrel read it three times, and each time the words arranged themselves identically, which was the maddening thing about writing: it refused to change just because you needed it to.

*On the eve of the first snow out of season,* the entry read, in that fine engraved hand, *the Ember wakes in the blood of the keepers. Look to the junior keeper of accounts, K. Nystrom, son of the last Emberwright. What follows is war, unless the record is set right.*

Below it, in different ink, older and browner, someone had added a single word: *Verified.*

Kestrel stood in the cold of the deep archive and did the arithmetic he was built to do, because arithmetic was safe, arithmetic was solid ground. The codex was a hundred and four years old. The parchment was right; the binding was right; the iron gall ink had aged into exactly the brown-black it should be, bloomed with the right mold patterns along the margins. This was not a forgery inserted yesterday. This page had been sitting here, chained to a desk in a locked cellar, since before his grandfather was born.

And it named him.

His full name — the name Odell had given him, the name of a seven-year-old with singed eyebrows who did not exist until twenty-two years ago. Written a century ago. Beside a date.

He checked the date against his memory of the almanac, then against the calendar hanging in the counting room upstairs, then against his own hand, which had written it that morning on the audit header without a flicker of recognition.

Tonight's date. Tonight. The eve of the first snow out of season — and the sky over Caerlum had been the color of wet slate all day, and the gulls had gone silent at noon, and old Brannic had grumbled that it smelled like snow, in autumn, weeks too early, and everyone had laughed at him.

Kestrel became aware that he was gripping the edge of the reading desk hard enough to hurt his fingers. He let go. He smoothed the page. He was a ledger-keeper; there was a procedure for anomalies. You verified. You cross-referenced. You did not stand in a cold cellar letting a sentence rearrange your life.

But his heart was going like a courier's boots on the stair, and the draft — or whatever it was — had settled at the base of his skull and would not leave, and when he finally looked down at his own hands resting on the century-old page, pale in the candlelight, steady as a counter's hands should be —

— for one instant, one, he would swear afterward to no one, because there would be no one to swear it to — they seemed to hold a faint warmth, like stones left too long in the sun.

Above him, through the stone ceiling, came a sound he had never heard in twenty-two years of nights in the guildhall.

Snow, ticking against the high windows. Out of season. Right on schedule.

Kestrel blew out the candle, and in the dark, with the codex open under his hands, he began — carefully, evenly, in the handwriting of a man who has never once been interesting — to copy the entry out.
