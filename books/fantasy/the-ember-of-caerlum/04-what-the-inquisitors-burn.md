## 4. What the Inquisitors Burn

Wren had made the run to Vellamere a hundred times, and she knew every mile of it the way other people knew their own hands: the ford at Bracken Mill that ran knee-deep in summer and chest-deep in thaw, the shepherd's cut above Hollow Tor where the wind came sideways, the long descent into the river valley where you could see the guildhouse lamps of Vellamere burning green-gold through the mist like a promise kept.

She had never once made the run in snow.

It was the wrong season for it by six weeks. The morning she'd left Caerlum, Master Odell had pressed a sealed satchel into her arms — trade contracts, nothing exciting, the usual paper shuffling between sister houses — and told her to be back inside ten days. "Before the passes close," he'd said, which was a strange thing to say in autumn, and stranger still because his eyes had done that thing they did when he was worried about something he wouldn't name.

Now, nine days later, crouched in the dead bracken on the ridge above Vellamere with the satchel's useless contracts still strapped to her back, Wren watched snow fall on the valley in fat, silent flakes, and understood that Odell had known something. He always knew something. That was the trouble with old men who'd spent their lives trading in impossible things: they stopped being surprised early, and started being prepared instead.

The guildhouse below was burning.

Not all of it. Not yet. The eastern wing — the archive wing, the wing where Vellamere kept its codices and its blood-records and its hundred years of careful entries about things that could not possibly exist but somehow did — was pouring black smoke into the falling snow, and figures moved along its galleries with lanterns that burned the wrong color. White flame. Flame without heat, or so it seemed from this distance, though Wren knew better. Everyone in the Ninefold Trade knew better. You didn't get white fire from any lamp sold in any market from here to the coast.

You got it from the Crown's Inquisitors, whose fire burned only what they judged should not exist.

Wren counted them out of habit, the way she counted everything — bridges, miles, coins, exits. Twelve riders in the yard, gray-cloaked, mounted on horses too fine for soldiers. Two more at the gate turning back travelers. A wagon being loaded with crates, and the crates were going onto the wagon and *staying* there, which meant they were taking records, not destroying all of them, which meant someone wanted to read what Vellamere had written before they erased that anyone had written it.

Her first thought — her honest, shameful first thought — was *run*. Turn around, take the southern road, be in Caerlum in four days, tell Odell the sister house had fallen, let the grown-ups sort it out. She was a courier. Couriers carried things; they did not carry causes. Her whole craft was built on the principle that the messenger survives the message. A dead courier delivered nothing.

But then she saw the archivist.

Old Mother Casselin, who had taught Wren her letters when Wren was nine and newly indentured and crying herself sick over the family she'd been sold away from. Casselin, who kept honey cakes in the third drawer of her reading desk for homesick apprentices. Casselin was being marched across the courtyard between two gray cloaks, her wrists bound, her head high in the way of a woman who has decided that dignity is the last property no one can confiscate.

They put her against the well. One of the Inquisitors said something Wren couldn't hear over the wind. Casselin said something back, and even at three hundred yards Wren could read the shape of it in her mouth: *I will not.*

The white fire went up like a struck match.

Wren did not remember deciding to move. Later, examining the memory the way she examined a ledger entry that didn't balance, she would find only a gap where the decision should have been, and on one side of the gap the woman who ran, and on the other side the woman sliding down the scree slope toward the guildhouse's northern wall, where the delivery gate stood, where the night-watch door stood, where a hundred deliveries had taught her that the bolt was loose because the Vellamere factor was a lazy man named Petch who never fixed anything until it broke twice.

The bolt was loose.

She slipped inside into smoke and screaming, and the screaming was worse than the fire, because fire at least was honest about what it intended.

* * *

Inside, the guildhall of Vellamere was dying the way an old animal dies: slowly, in pieces, with terrible dignity. The great trading floor where she'd haggled over silk weights and relic provenance was ankle-deep in scattered paper — Inquisitors worked methodically, she'd give them that, pulling ledgers off shelves and fanning pages across the stones like a farmer winnowing grain, keeping some, discarding most. The discarded pages were already ash in places, curling at their edges as if embarrassed to exist.

A gray cloak came around the corner of the counting corridor and Wren did the only thing her body knew how to do, which was to become furniture. She folded down behind a stack of shipping crates marked *GLASS — FRAGILE* and held her breath and listened to the man's boots stop three feet from her head.

"Another one here," he called. "Unopened. Sealed."

"Bring it," said a voice further off, and the voice was wrong in a way Wren couldn't name at first and then could: it was bored. Not cruel-bored, not enjoying-it-bored. The boredom of a clerk doing inventory. Of a man who had burned a dozen guildhalls this season and expected to burn a dozen more and found the work no more interesting than mucking stables. "And check the strongroom. The Preceptor wants the blood-records intact. *Intact,* I said. If there's so much as a singed margin—"

Blood-records.

Every guildhouse kept them. It wasn't secret — it was tradition, older than the Crown, older probably than the kingdoms the Crown thought it ruled. When a trader bound themselves to a guild, they gave a drop of blood to the page, and the page remembered. Lineages, adoptions, legitimizations, the quiet paperwork of families that traded in impossible things and therefore needed very precise records of who was related to whom, because certain inheritances didn't pass through law. They passed through blood.

The Inquisitors wanted Vellamere's blood-records *intact*.

Wren lay behind the crates with her heart slamming and did arithmetic, because arithmetic was how she kept from drowning. They were burning the guild. They were sparing the blood-records. Therefore the blood-records mattered more than the guild. Therefore whatever was written in them was worth killing for and worth keeping, both at once, which meant it was worth reading.

Which meant she had to steal from a building full of the Crown's own fire-priests the exact thing they had come to steal themselves.

Her grandmother — the one before the selling, the one she barely remembered — used to say that courage was just fear that had found a job. Wren got up off the floor, found her fear a job, and moved.

* * *

The archive wing was already half-lost. Heat rolled off it in waves that bent the air, and the smell was the worst part — burning vellum has a sweetness to it, like meat, like something that had been alive. Wren pulled her scarf over her mouth and went in low along the wall, because the fire was eating from the middle outward and the edges still belonged to the living.

Casselin's reading room was off the second gallery. The door was open. The desk drawers had been pulled out and dumped — the honey-cake drawer included, crumbs scattered among spilled ink — and the shelves were stripped bare except for one codex lying face-down on the floor where someone had dropped it in haste or contempt.

Wren knew the codex. Every courier in the Ninefold knew it by its binding: iron-gray leather, cornered in dull silver, the sigil of the founding houses stamped blind — no ink, no gilt, just the shape pressed into the skin of it. The *Continuance*. The master record. The book that proved the guilds had existed before the Crown decided what history was allowed to say.

Someone had torn pages from it. The stubs were still visible in the gutter of the binding, maybe twenty leaves' worth, ripped out roughly near the middle. And beside the codex, on the floor, half under the desk, was what remained of the missing section: a single leaf, scorched at one edge, evidently dropped in the tearing and missed in the gathering.

Wren picked it up because picking things up was what her hands were for.

The surviving text was fragmentary — fire had taken the left margin and the bottom third — but the hand was old, formal, the kind of script they taught you to recognize before they taught you to write it. And three phrases survived the burning whole enough to read:

*...the Emberwright line, last of the burning blood...*

*...not exterminated as the Crown's histories claim, but entered into covenant, dispersed among the founding houses...*

*...let the record show what the record must show, though the realm prefer otherwise...*

The heat was climbing. Somewhere below, boots on stairs, voices calling numbers — they were cataloguing, floor by floor, and would reach the second gallery soon. Wren folded the scorched leaf small and put it inside her glove, against her palm, because a courier's gloves were the one place on her body no one ever asked to search, and then she took the codex itself, because leaving it to burn felt like leaving Casselin against the well.

The window was the old loading access, barred, but the bars were wood-shimmed — Petch again, God rot him — and they came free with a wrench that cost her the skin off one knuckle. She dropped into the snow of the delivery yard, rolled, came up running, and did not look back at the burning wing, because looking back is how couriers die, and she had a delivery now. She'd had deliveries before that were letters, contracts, reliquaries, once a live basilisk egg packed in warm sand. This was the first delivery she'd ever carried that was also a wound.

* * *

They almost had her at the north road, and it was the snow that saved her.

The Inquisitors had posted riders on the southern approach — the sensible road, the coach road — but the northern track past the tanneries was a mud-lane in every season except this impossible one, and tonight it was a white ribbon no hoofprint yet marked. Wren ran it in the dark with the flakes thickening around her like the sky shaking out its feathers, and behind her the glow of Vellamere lit the clouds a bruised orange, and ahead of her was forty miles of empty hills and then the coast road and then, if the weather allowed, if her legs allowed, if the world allowed, Caerlum.

She stole a tanner's mule. She hated herself for it and wrote a mental note of the man's name — Aldous Ferrin, tannery row, Vellamere — intending, with a seriousness she'd never before applied to a debt, to repay him triple. The guild taught you that every borrowing was a ledger entry. She was learning, alone in the freezing dark, that some entries could only be balanced in a currency the ledger didn't have columns for.

The mule was slow and sure-footed and despised her equally, which made them honest companions. She rode through the night and the day after, off any road the Inquisitors might watch, sleeping two hours at a time in shepherd's shelters with the scorched leaf inside her glove and her hand closed around it like a fist around a coal.

Because that was the thing she couldn't stop thinking, the thing that rode with her worse than the cold:

*Dispersed among the founding houses.* Not destroyed. Absorbed. Married in, adopted in, hidden in plain sight behind guild names for a hundred years while the Crown's histories said *exterminated* and everyone nodded, because everyone prefers a finished story to an open account.

The founding houses. There were seven, once. Five had gone under or merged across the centuries.

Caerlum was one of the two that remained.

She thought about the people she'd left there nine days ago. Odell, with his worry he wouldn't name. The junior ledger-keeper, Kestrel Nystrom, precise and quiet and invisible, the boy who corrected other people's sums in pencil so gently you'd think the error was yours. The boy whose parentage was, like everything else about him, a blank column in the guild's books — entered as *foundling, ward of the house*, no line given, no lineage claimed.

No lineage *claimed*.

Wren sat in a shepherd's shelter on the second night with the mule dozing at the entrance and the storm moaning outside, and held the scorched leaf up to her little hooded lamp, and read it a fourth time, and a fifth, and understood that she was no longer carrying a document.

She was carrying a match.

* * *

Caerlum's guildhall showed its lights at dusk on the fourth day, and Wren nearly wept at the sight of them, which annoyed her, because she was tougher than that, or had believed she was.

The town looked wrong. Snow to the eaves in autumn, lanes drifted shut, the harbor iced at its mouth — the fisherfolk were out on the ice with axes, breaking a channel that should have been open water, and their curses carried across the flat white silence. The whole Ninefold Trade was locked in a winter that had no business existing, and every soul she passed wore the same expression: not fear exactly, but the wary look of people waiting to be told what they'd done wrong.

She left the mule — Aldous Ferrin's mule, she'd return it personally, she swore it on the ledger — at the livery by the gate and went up the guild steps on legs that had stopped negotiating somewhere outside Bracken Mill.

The night porter, Brammel, opened the wicket and stared at her. "Wren? You're — the roads, we heard the roads were—"

"The roads are closed," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "Vellamere's burning. Let me in, Brammel."

He let her in. He also reached past her to bar the door behind her with a speed that told her the news had traveled ahead of her in ways she'd learn about later, and that Caerlum had already begun bolting its doors.

The hall was lit but half-empty, apprentices whispering in corners, and at the long audit table under the lamp sat Master Odell and, of all people, Kestrel Nystrom, bent together over a book so old its pages had gone the color of tea. Odell looked up first, and she watched his face do three things in one second: relief, calculation, and dread — dread arriving last and staying longest, because Odell, she realized, had been dreading precisely this knock on precisely this door for longer than she'd been alive.

"You're frozen," he said. "Sit. Brammel, wine, hot—"

"Master." Her teeth were chattering too hard for ceremony. She pried off her right glove, and the scorched leaf came with it, stuck to her sweat-damp palm, and she laid it on the table between the teacolored pages and the lamp where the light could get at it. "They took the blood-records. All of them, intact, by order of somebody called the Preceptor. They burned everything else. They burned—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "They burned Mother Casselin. At the well. She wouldn't kneel."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged in the wind.

Odell looked at the leaf for a long time without touching it, the way a man looks at a snake he knows. Then, quietly, in the voice of a guildmaster closing a position that has run ruinously against the house, he said: "Kestrel. Go to your room."

But Kestrel wasn't listening. Kestrel was leaning over the scorched fragment, and Wren watched him read it — watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out — and she saw his lips move around the words *Emberwright line*, and she saw his eyes come up and fix on hers with a question in them so naked and so frightened that she knew, with the absolute certainty of a balanced column, that he had never heard the word before in his life.

And that was strange, she thought. Because the leaf was old, and the hand that wrote it was old, and the covenant it described was older still —

— and the boy staring at her across the lamplight had his mother's eyes, and nobody in this guildhall had ever been able to tell Wren whose those were.
