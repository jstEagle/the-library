## 5. Blood in the Margins

Wren slept in the infirmary with a blanket up to her chin and a knife under her pillow, because Wren was Wren even half-dead from cold. The physician had wrapped her frostbitten fingers in greasecloth and pronounced that she'd keep them all, which was more than could be said for the sister-guildhouse at Vell's Crossing.

Kestrel sat three floors below, alone in the records vault, with a scorched scrap of paper flattened between two panes of glass and a lamp turned as high as it would go without smoking.

He had not slept. He suspected he would not sleep again for some time.

The fragment was small — no bigger than his palm, its edges charred to lace. Someone had torn it from a larger document and someone else had tried to burn it, and both someones had failed to finish the job. The surviving text ran in a formal hand, the kind used for Crown writs and death sentences:

*...the line of Emberwright, declared extinct by decree of—*

The rest was ash.

*Extinct by decree.* Kestrel read the phrase until it stopped looking like words. That was the official history every schoolchild in the Ninefold Trade could recite: two hundred years ago the Emberwrights burned the world's harvests, boiled its rivers, cracked the Grey Spine itself, and the righteous armies of the Crown put an end to their madness down to the last child. It was the founding story of the Inquisition. It was why guildhalls kept fire-wards on their doors and why no ledger in Caerlum ever recorded a transaction involving flame.

And here was a fragment of a document — rescued from an Inquisitor's own bonfire by a courier with frozen lungs — that suggested somebody, somewhere, had needed to *declare* the extinction rather than simply observe it.

You did not issue decrees about things that were already true.

Kestrel pulled the guild's oldest codex toward him across the table. It was called the Root Ledger, though nobody living knew why; it predated the current guildhall, predated the current city walls, and its first hundred pages were written in a hand so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood. Master Odell let it out of the vault twice a year for inventory and otherwise pretended it didn't exist, which was how Kestrel knew it mattered.

He turned to the genealogical rolls near the back. Every guild family kept blood-records — marriages, births, adoptions, legitimizations — because the Guild of Caerlum traded in impossible things, and impossible things required provenance. You couldn't sell a vial of river-that-remembers unless you could show where the river had been. Blood was just another chain of custody.

The Emberwright entries were there. Of course they were; the guild had existed before the burning, and the Emberwrights had been patrons, clients, and — Kestrel noted with a small cold shock — *members*. Third-rank traders, listed modestly among the glassblowers and salt-merchants, as if the family that could crack mountains had once spent its afternoons haggling over freight rates like anyone else.

He began at the last full entry and worked backward. Or forward, rather — forward through time, through the decades after the decree, and this was where his hands started to shake.

Because the Emberwright line did not end.

It *branched*.

*Emberwright, Sera, m. Corvane (guild family), 3 children, name surrendered per accord.*

*Corvane-Emberwright issue: see Corvane roll, sub-entry marked †.*

*Emberwright, Ilven, adopted into House Marenne of the river trade, name surrendered per accord.*

*Per accord. Per accord. Per accord.*

The same phrase, again and again, down forty years of entries. Name surrendered per accord. As if the extinction hadn't been a slaughter but a *contract*. As if the most feared bloodline in the world had sat down across a table from the Crown and negotiated its own disappearance — not into graves, but into marriages. Into adoptions. Into the quiet, respectable, unremarkable families of the Guild of Caerlum.

Kestrel set the lamp down because his hands were no longer steady enough to hold it and read at the same time.

He thought of the guild families. All of them. The Corvanes, who dealt in memory-glass. The Marennes, who'd married into the smuggler clans of the lower river. The Thackerays—

His own name arrived in his head before he decided to think it, the way a stair always arrives one step sooner than your foot expects.

*No,* he told himself. *That's fear talking. Every guild family has an old roll. Statistically, some branch or other touches everything. That's what old means.*

But he was a ledger-keeper, and ledger-keepers do not get to stop at statistically. So he took down the Thackeray roll — his family's roll, his grandfather's careful hand in the margins, his father's additions, the blank space where his own birth should have been recorded and wasn't, because Odell had entered him separately, oddly, as a ward rather than a son — and he laid it beside the Root Ledger, and he began the work.

Cross-referencing was the one prayer he'd ever been taught. Dates against dates. Hands against hands. Names folded into names like flour into dough until you could no longer tell which was which, and that, he understood now, rising slowly from his chair with the two ledgers open before him like a man rising from a coffin lid, that was exactly the point.

The Thackeray roll went back six generations. Five of them were ordinary: grain futures, toll disputes, a marriage to a Vell cousin, a funeral. But the sixth generation — the oldest page, the brown ink — contained a single entry in a hand that matched the Root Ledger's Emberwright section stroke for stroke:

*Thackeray, Aldous, legitimized, issue of the house of Emberwright. Name taken per accord. Bond recorded. Witnessed.*

Bond recorded. There was no explanation of what bond meant. There never was, in the old entries; the ancient clerks wrote as if their readers lived inside their heads. But Kestrel had spent ten years learning to read the gaps in documents, and the gap here was shaped like something enormous. A bond recorded between the guild and the Emberwrights, witnessed, sealed, and then buried under six generations of grain futures.

They hadn't hidden the Emberwrights from the world. They'd hidden them *in plain sight* — absorbed, dispersed, renamed, married into the very institution best equipped to keep a secret forever. Because who audits a guild? Who reads a ledger no one opens? The safest place to hide a forbidden bloodline was inside an organization whose entire profession was the custody of things the world preferred forgotten.

Impossible things. The Guild of Caerlum traded in impossible things.

An extinct family was just one more item in inventory.

* * *

He did not remember climbing the stairs to the infirmary. He found himself there anyway, standing in the doorway with the Root Ledger under his arm like a shield, watching Wren sleep.

She woke the way she always woke — instantly, silently, knife already half-drawn — and then saw it was him and subsided, scowling. "You look like a man who's read something."

"I've read everything."

"That's your problem." She pushed herself up against the headboard, wincing at her bandaged hands. "Vell's Crossing is gone, Kestrel. Not damaged. *Gone.* They took the ledgers out to the yard and burned them in front of the staff, and then they arrested the guildmaster for obstruction, and then they burned the *outbuildings*, and one of the Inquisitors told the cook — she told me, before I ran — he said they were looking for a name that shouldn't exist anymore." Her eyes were steady on his face. "So whatever you found downstairs, I'd think very carefully about whether knowing it keeps you alive or gets you burned."

"It's my name."

The words came out flat and strange, like someone else's voice through a door.

Wren went very still. "What?"

"The name that shouldn't exist." He sat down on the edge of the cot because his legs had opinions of their own. "It's mine. Or it's in mine, anyway. Buried six generations back. The Emberwrights weren't exterminated, Wren. They signed an accord. They gave up their name and scattered themselves through the guild families, and the Crown agreed to call it extinction because a lie was cheaper than a war, and the guild agreed to keep the lie because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because keeping impossible things is literally our business. We're not traders. We're *evidence storage*. Two hundred years of evidence storage, and nobody remembered what we were storing."

"Or somebody remembered," Wren said quietly. "Somebody always remembers. That's why Vell's Crossing is smoke."

The silence stretched. Down in the yard, a cart horse stamped against the cold. Snow ticked faintly against the shutters — snow in autumn, snow that shouldn't be falling yet, snow that had begun the night the stranger with the dead man's token walked into the guildhall asking for the Ember.

"The fragment," Kestrel said. "The one you carried out. Where exactly did you find it?"

"Inquisitor-General's field desk. I was supposed to steal dispatch schedules, that was the whole job, and the desk was open and there was this paper on top with the seal half-broken, and I took it because—" She shrugged with one shoulder. "Because it looked important. Because I'm a thief with a courier's badge and that's the same thing."

"You stole it from the Inquisitor-General's own desk."

"He's the one who lit the fire under it. Draw your own conclusions."

Kestrel drew them. They were conclusions he'd been circling all night, refusing to land on. The Inquisitors weren't hunting the prophecy out of piety. They weren't even hunting the Emberwrights, not really — those had been safely dissolved into merchant families for two centuries, harmless as salt. What they were hunting was the *record*. The proof that the founding act of the Inquisition — the great extermination, the righteous fire, the entire architecture of their authority — was built on a signature rather than a sword. On paperwork. On an accord that the guild still held somewhere in its vaults, if only anyone knew where to look.

And the Inquisitor-General had tried to burn the fragment himself. Which meant he knew. Which meant the fraud wasn't ancient history gathering dust. It was being *maintained*.

"They're not erasing the past," Kestrel said slowly. "They're curating it. There's a difference. Erasure is panic. Curation is policy."

"You've been reading too many of Odell's sermons."

"Odell knew." The realization landed in his chest like a dropped weight. "Odell entered me as a ward. Not a son, not a nephew — a ward, with no parentage line at all, which violates every recording convention the guild has. I used to think it was sloppiness. Odell is many things but he is not sloppy. He hid my line because there was a line to hide." His voice thinned. "He looked at a baby and saw the last Emberwright and decided the kindest thing he could do was make me small."

Wren reached out with her bandaged hand and, awkwardly, because the greasecloth wouldn't bend, pressed her knuckles against his wrist. "Kestrel. Listen to me. Whatever you are on paper, you're the man who stayed up all night doing arithmetic instead of running. That's not nothing. That might be everything."

He wanted to believe her. He examined the want carefully, the way he'd examine a suspect coin, and found it mostly genuine, which frightened him more than the alternative. Because if he was honest — and honesty was the one discipline he'd never broken, not once, not until three nights ago when a stranger asked for the Ember and Kestrel lied to his face — if he was honest, then some buried part of him had felt not horror at the discovery but *recognition*. Like finding an entry in a ledger that finally explained a column of numbers that had never added up. The way he'd always felt the guildhall's fires lean toward him. The way candle flames straightened when he entered a room, subtle as a held breath. The way he'd never once, in twenty-three years, been cold.

He had catalogued all of it under *coincidence*. He was beginning to understand that he had been falsifying his own records for his entire life.

* * *

Master Odell found him at dawn, still in the vault, surrounded by open ledgers arranged in a pattern only Kestrel could read — the Root Ledger, the Thackeray roll, the Corvane roll, the Marenne roll, the fragment under glass, and the prophetic book with tonight's date and his own name in a dead woman's hand.

The old man stood in the doorway for a long moment. Lamplight caught the wet in his eyes, or maybe it was only lamplight.

"You went past the Corvane roll," Odell said at last. His voice was gravel and resignation. "I hoped you'd stop there."

"There are eleven guild families with Emberwright entries. Eleven, Master. Half the Conclave's founding houses are carrying that blood and don't know it. The extinction wasn't a lie told once. It's a lie we've all been *living in*." Kestrel stood. "Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because telling you would have killed you," Odell said simply. "Slowly, maybe. But surely. The moment you knew what you were, you stopped being a clerk and became a claim. And claims get collected." He came into the room and laid one liver-spotted hand flat on the Root Ledger, gentle, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "I made you small, boy. Small and precise and invisible. It was the only inheritance I had to give you, and I have prayed every day since that it would be enough."

"It wasn't," Kestrel said. "Someone sent a man with a dead guild's token to ask for the Ember by name. The Inquisitors burned a sister-house looking for a name that shouldn't exist. And there's a book in this vault that's known about me for a century." He closed the Thackeray roll with a soft, final sound. "Whatever you were protecting me from, Master — it's already here."

Odell looked at him for a long time, and then, heavily, he sat down in the reader's chair as if the weight of two hundred years had chosen that moment to present its invoice.

"Then fetch the brandy," the old man said, "and lock the door. Because if we're doing this, we're going to need to talk about the accord itself — and about the clause your ancestors wrote into it that nobody was ever supposed to trigger."

"What clause?"

Odell met his eyes. "The one that says the Ember can be *rekindled*."
