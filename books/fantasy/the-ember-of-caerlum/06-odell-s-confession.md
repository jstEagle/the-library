## 6. Odell's Confession

Kestrel had rehearsed the conversation eleven times on the walk up the stairs.

He knew because he counted things. It was how he kept the world from sliding. Eleven rehearsals, each one a different opening: *Master Odell, I found my name in the codex. Master Odell, who was my mother? Master Odell, why does a dead woman write like she knows me?* In every version, Odell answered plainly, the way he answered questions about interest rates and import duties, and in every version Kestrel walked away steadier than he'd come.

The stairs to the guildmaster's rooms were narrow and smelled of tallow and old paper, which was to say they smelled of Odell himself, distilled into architecture. Kestrel knocked once.

"Enter before your knuckles freeze," came the voice from within, rougher than usual. "And bring the lamp closer when you do. My eyes have decided to retire without consulting me."

Odell sat in his chair by the window, wrapped in a blanket that had been fine wool forty years ago and was now mostly memory. He was a small man made smaller by illness — the cough that had been an inconvenience at midwinter was now a tenant that paid no rent and intended to stay. His hands, which could still close a ledger with a snap that made apprentices flinch, lay folded on his knee like tools set down for good.

"You look terrible," Kestrel said, setting the lamp on the table.

"I look honest. There's a difference most people never learn." Odell studied him a moment too long. "You've found something."

Not a question. Kestrel felt his rehearsed openings crumble. "How—"

"Because you knock differently when you're carrying something heavy. You've knocked like that for two days." Odell gestured at the chair opposite. "Sit. And stop rehearsing. Whatever it is, say it plain. You learned plainness from me; give me the courtesy of it."

So Kestrel said it plain. The prophetic ledger. Tonight's date written a century ago. The codex in the deep archive, and the entry in a dead woman's hand — *the Ember comes through Nystrom* — in handwriting that matched his own the way a river matches its source.

He expected denial. He expected the old deflection, the dry joke, the redirection toward some clerical error. What he got was silence, long enough that the lamp flame leaned and righted itself twice.

Then Odell said, "Her name was Maerin Emberwright."

The word went through Kestrel like a splinter finding bone. He'd read it in the fragment Wren carried, scorched and half-legible, but hearing it aloud — hearing it in Odell's voice, in this room where he'd been taught arithmetic and honesty and how to hold a pen so the ink wouldn't blot — made it real in a way paper hadn't.

"Emberwright," Kestrel repeated.

"The last of them. Or near enough." Odell's eyes stayed on the window, where the out-of-season snow was falling past the glass in fat, silent flakes. "You want the whole of it?"

"I want the truth."

"That," said Odell, "is what I'm afraid of."

* * *

Odell had told lies before. He wanted that understood, even if only by the ceiling above his bed, because Kestrel had left — stormed out, or fled, or done whatever a young man does when the floor opens under him — and the room was quiet now except for the cough and the snow.

He had lied to Customs men about cargo weights. He had lied to the Crown's assessors about guild holdings until the guild was too poor to be worth assessing, which had been the point. He had lied, once, to a dying woman, and that lie had cost him twenty years of sleep and bought him twenty years of a boy's laughter in the halls below.

Maerin Emberwright had come to Caerlum in the autumn of a year the ledgers recorded as unremarkable, because Odell had made sure it was recorded that way. She'd arrived pregnant, hunted, and cleverer than anyone he'd ever traded with. She hadn't asked for shelter. She'd asked for *anonymity*, priced fairly, and Odell — younger then, ambitious in the small ways small men are ambitious — had named a figure that would have embarrassed him later.

She'd paid it in coin and in one other currency: the truth of who she was, given as collateral. *If you know what I am,* she'd said, *you'll keep the secret better than any oath could make you.*

She'd been right. Odell had kept it through her death — fever, not fire, though the Crown's histories would eventually claim otherwise, claiming the line ended clean — and through the birth, and through the naming. She'd wanted the boy called Kestrel, after the bird that hovers motionless over open water, watching, before it strikes. *Let him watch first,* she'd said. *Always let him watch first.*

And then she'd died, and Odell had been left holding a squalling infant and a decision that would define him more than any trade he ever closed.

He could have sent the boy to the Foundling House. Anonymous, unremarkable, gone. Safe the way stones in a riverbed are safe.

Instead he'd entered him into the guild rolls under his own surname. Nystrom. Junior ledger-keeper. Invisible by design — given work too dull to attract attention, a life too modest to invite inquiry, a future deliberately kept small the way you bank a fire to keep it alive through the night.

*I made you small,* Odell thought at the empty doorway, *because everything large in this world gets burned.*

He'd told himself it was love. Some nights, in twenty years, it had even felt like love. Other nights it had felt like what it also was: fear, wearing love's coat.

The cough took him then, long and racking, and when it released him he noticed the door was open again, and Kestrel was standing in it, coat still on, snow melting in his hair.

"You forgot," Kestrel said quietly, "that I always come back to check the figures."

* * *

"Say it again," Kestrel said. "All of it. Slowly."

Odell didn't flinch. That was somehow worse than flinching. "Your mother was Maerin Emberwright. Your father—" a pause, and here, finally, the old man's composure cracked along a seam Kestrel had never seen, "—your father I never learned. She wouldn't say, and I didn't press. One secret at a time was all I could carry."

"And me. You carried me how, exactly? Like what? An asset? A liability? A debt?"

"Like a boy," Odell said. "I carried you like a boy who needed feeding."

"Don't." Kestrel's voice came out flat and strange to his own ears. "Don't do that. Don't make it warm. You sat across a table from me for twenty years. You taught me that every entry must balance, that concealment is falsification, that a ledger that hides anything is worthless. You built my entire self out of that rule. And the whole time—" his hand found the back of a chair and gripped it, "—the whole time there was an entry missing. The largest entry. About *me*."

"Yes."

"*Yes?*"

"What would you have me say?" For the first time, heat came into the old man's voice, and illness made it ragged. "That I regret it? Some nights. That I'd do it differently? I don't know. You want to know what the world does to Emberwrights, boy? Ask the sister-house at Vaelmoor. Oh — you can't. Wren watched them burn it. Fourteen people whose only crime was sharing a roof with someone who might have had the blood. That's what 'large' buys. That's what your mother ran from while she was carrying you, and I promised her—" the cough seized him, bent him double, and Kestrel found himself moving forward without deciding to, one hand bracing the old man's shoulder, feeling the fragility of the frame beneath the blanket, "—I promised her you'd live long enough to be bored."

Kestrel withdrew his hand slowly, as if the shoulder might burn him. Everything in him was at war. The part that understood — that had spent two days reading century-old entries and knew exactly what the Crown did to inconvenient bloodlines. The part that wanted to shake the old man until his teeth rattled. The part that remembered being eight years old and feverish, and Odell sitting up all night reading customs tariffs aloud in a monotone because the boy found them soothing.

"You didn't trust me," Kestrel said finally. "That's the heart of it. Twenty years, and you judged I couldn't bear the weight."

"I judged the weight would crush you."

"You don't get to make that judgment. Not alone. Not about *me*." He heard his own voice rise and forced it down. "I'm not a holding, Master Odell. I'm not inventory you keep in a dark room for its own protection."

"No," Odell agreed softly. "You're not. That was my error, among errors. I protected the secret so long I forgot the secret wasn't the treasure. You were." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious with age. "There. Is that confession enough for your ledger? Enter it. Debit: one old man's cowardice, twenty years' standing. Credit: one boy, alive, against all odds. Let the column balance however it balances."

Kestrel turned away. He walked to the window because he couldn't look at the bed, at the blanket, at the face that had been the fixed point of his entire life and was suddenly a stranger's face wearing familiar lines. Outside, the snow kept falling — weeks early, wrong, unnatural, the prophecy's first sign arriving like a bill collector.

"Wren's fragment," he said, without turning. "It names the Emberwrights. It says absorbed, not exterminated. Guild families. Which means there may be others like me. Others Odells made small."

"There may."

"And the Ashen Court sent a man with a dead man's token asking for the Ember, and the Inquisitors burned a guildhouse, and tonight's date has been sitting in our books for a hundred years waiting for me." He turned around. "The world has already found me, Master Odell. Your smallness failed. All it did was leave me unarmed."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the night bell rang the third hour.

"Then I'll arm you," Odell said at last, "with what I have left. Which is more than you think." He pushed himself upright against the headboard, wincing, and nodded toward the writing desk in the corner. "Bottom drawer. False bottom. Bring it."

Kestrel hesitated — one last flare of the hurt, the refusal to simply obey as he always had — and then crossed the room and opened the drawer. Beneath a layer of obsolete tariff schedules, his fingers found the seam. The false bottom lifted.

Inside lay a bundle of letters tied with black cord, and beneath them, a ring. A signet, old gold, worn nearly smooth, bearing a device Kestrel recognized only from the oldest pages of the codex: a flame enclosed in a circle, neither escaping nor dying.

"Your mother's seal," Odell said. "The mark of the Emberwrights. And those are her letters — to me, and worse luck, some to people I could never find. Read them. Then decide what size you are." He sank back against the pillows, spent. "I only ask one thing, and it isn't forgiveness."

Kestrel stood holding the ring. It was warm. He told himself it was warm because his hands were cold.

"Name it."

"When they come — and they will come, both of them, the Crown and the Court — don't let either of them tell you what the fire is for." Odell's eyes held his, fierce and fading. "Whatever else you inherit, boy, refuse that inheritance. Promise me."

And Kestrel, who had lied for the first time in his life three nights ago to a stranger with a dead man's token, found that he could not lie to this dying man even now, even to comfort him.

"I promise," he said, "to decide for myself."

Odell laughed — a thin, cracked sound that became a cough that became silence — and closed his eyes. "Aye," he murmured. "That's her answer too. Exactly hers. Get out, then. Let an old man sleep, and take the light with you. I've done my reading."

Kestrel went. At the door he stopped, and almost turned, and didn't.

Behind him, in the dark, Odell lay listening to the footsteps fade down the stairs, and thought: *Now the winter comes for all of us.* Then, softer, almost hopeful: *But he'll watch first. She saw to that.*

Down in the archive, long before dawn, Kestrel untied the black cord and broke the seal on the first letter — and froze, because the handwriting inside was not his mother's.

It was the hand from the prophetic ledger. The hand that had written his name a hundred years ago.
