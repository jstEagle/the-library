## 7. The Long Winter Begins

Wren had delivered letters through every kind of weather the Ninefold Trade could produce. She'd crossed the Saltmarsh causeway in fog so thick she navigated by counting fence posts. She'd outrun a hailstorm on the Greyharrow road with her satchel held over her head like a shield. She'd once spent three days snowed into a waystation inn with a wool merchant who snored like a landslide, and she'd come out of it with a new appreciation for silence.

So when the first flakes came down over the pass at Dunmarrow, in the last week of Harvestmonth, she didn't think much of them. Early snow happened. The mountains made their own rules, and everyone who worked the trade roads knew better than to argue with them.

She was two days out of Caerlum, carrying contracts for the dyers' cooperative at Vell's Crossing and a sealed letter for the harbormaster at Port Ammen that she wasn't supposed to know contained a bribe. Routine work. The kind of run you did half-asleep, letting your feet find the ruts while your mind wandered.

The snow kept falling.

By evening it was ankle-deep and still coming down, fat slow flakes that seemed less like weather than like intention. Wren stopped at the tollhouse below the pass and found the keeper, an old woman named Bess, standing in her doorway staring up at the sky with her arms crossed tight against her chest.

"Harvestmonth," Bess said, before Wren even dismounted. "It's Harvestmonth."

"It happens."

"Not here. Not in forty years." Bess looked at her the way people look at someone they're trying to warn. "You want to turn back, girl. Whatever's in that bag can wait."

"Nothing waits," Wren said, and meant it as guild doctrine, because it was. *Nothing waits* was carved over the door of the Caerlum guildhall, right under the scales. It was the first thing they taught you: the trade must move or the trade dies. A courier who turned around because of a little early snow would be a courier looking for other work by spring.

Bess shrugged and went inside, and Wren rode on, and told herself the cold on the back of her neck was just the wind.

* * *

By the second morning, the world had gone quiet in a way Wren had never heard before.

Snow-muffle was normal — she knew that hush, the way fresh powder ate sound and made the roads feel like the inside of a held breath. But this was different. This was the silence of absence. No carts. No drovers. No other riders. The Vell's Crossing road, which in a good week saw a hundred wagons, saw none. She passed a wayside shrine where someone had left an offering of bread and salt, already buried under six inches of white, and she understood suddenly that she hadn't passed another living soul since the tollhouse.

At Vell's Crossing itself, the dyers' cooperative had shut its doors. She stood in the yard reading the notice nailed there — *closed until roads reopen, God keep all who travel* — and felt the first real crack of unease go through her. The dyers never closed. They'd worked through the fever year, through the border troubles, through the winter eleven years back that killed half the orchards. Guildhouses closed; cooperatives endured. That was the whole point of them.

An old man stacking firewood in the next yard watched her read. "First time in my life I've seen snow stick this low this early," he said. "My grandfather used to say when the sky forgets the seasons, something's been forgotten somewhere else."

"That's not guild doctrine," Wren said.

"No," the old man agreed pleasantly. "But it's older than your guild."

She took the contracts back unfulfilled and rode south toward Port Ammen, telling herself the detour was sensible route-planning and not flight.

* * *

Port Ammen was worse.

Not damaged — worse in the way that mattered more. The harbor town was functioning, but functioning wrong, the way a body functions wrong. The fish market ran at half strength because the boats were coming back early, nets light, crews quiet. The chandlers had sold out of lamp oil and candles by midday. And everywhere, everywhere, people were talking about the winter.

Not complaining about it. Talking about it. The particular low urgent murmur of a population comparing notes.

Wren delivered the sealed letter to the harbormaster's clerk, and the clerk — a young man she'd known for years, who usually flirted with her in a tired professional way — barely glanced at her. He was copying out a proclamation from the Crown magistrate: *by order, all vessels to be berthed by month's end; no sailings north of the Ammen line until season proper.*

"They're calling it an early winter," the clerk said, stamping the proclamation. "Officially."

"And unofficially?"

He hesitated. He was choosing, Wren realized, how much to tell a stranger, and the fact that he considered her one after all these years of deliveries stung more than the news itself. "Unofficially, the tide priests say the water's too warm. Warm sea, cold air, and no wind from the south for nine days. That's not a season. That's a wall."

"A wall of what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were frightened in a way he was clearly trying not to show. "There are folk saying things, courier. Out on the docks. Old things. About debts coming due. About a fire that was put out and shouldn't have been."

Wren kept her face still, because couriers learned to keep their faces still, but behind it her stomach dropped. *A fire that was put out.* She didn't know why those words landed in her like hooks. She only knew that they did, that some part of her that had carried a thousand messages without ever opening one had just opened one anyway, silently, in the space between her ribs.

"You should get back to Caerlum soon," the clerk said. "Roads north are closing one by one. The mountain passes will go first, then the river fords. After that there's the coast road, and the coast road in snow is a coffin with a view."

"I'll make my own assessment," Wren said, and hated herself a little for how much it sounded like Odell.

* * *

She stayed one night at the Anchor & Scale, and that night the superstition stopped being something other people said.

The common room was full — fuller than it had any right to be, given the season, given that half the traffic on the roads had stopped. Sailors waiting out the berth order, carters stranded mid-route, a knot of pilgrims who'd been walking to the shrine at Saint Aldery's when the snow caught them. And in the corner, an old woman with a knitted shawl and a voice like dry leaves, holding court over a table of listeners.

Wren tried not to listen. She was tired, and her feet hurt, and superstition was bad air you breathed in crowded rooms during hard times. But the old woman's voice carried the way old voices do when they've had practice.

"—my grandmother saw it, and her grandmother before her, written in a book no honest church would keep. When the Ember sleeps too long, the sky forgets its seasons. Snow in harvest. Ice in the green months. And the debt comes due on the blood that banked the fire."

"What blood?" asked a carter, leaning in.

"The blood that runs quiet," the old woman said, and smiled with no warmth at all. "In ledgers. In counting-houses. In places where nobody remembers what their great-grandfathers burned."

Wren's ale went cold in front of her. She sat very still and finished it anyway, because that was also something couriers learned.

Later, up in her room, she took out the scorched fragment.

She hadn't shown it to anyone. She'd carried it from the burning guildhouse at Marrow Fen wrapped in oilcloth inside her shirt, against her skin, for four days now, and she'd told herself she was protecting guild property, and that was true, but it was also true that she hadn't wanted it touching anyone else. It felt like carrying a coal that hadn't decided whether to go out.

She unwrapped it now, alone, with the shutter latched and the candle turned low. A triangle of vellum, edges charred soft as cloth. Most of the text was gone, eaten by fire, but along one surviving margin ran a column of names in faded brown ink, and beneath the names, in a different hand, a phrase she had read a dozen times without fully letting herself understand:

*the line endures where the ledger keeps it.*

Below that, half-burned: *...when the snow comes in harvest-month, the ember...*

And then nothing. Fire had taken the rest.

Wren sat with it for a long time. Snow in harvest-month. Outside her window, snow in harvest-month, falling on a harbor town under Crown orders to stop moving, filling streets where old women quoted books no honest church would keep, describing debts due on blood that ran quiet in counting-houses.

She thought about Kestrel Nystrom, who kept the ledgers. Who was the quietest person she knew. Whose family had been guild families for as long as guild families had existed, which was exactly the kind of thing nobody remembers about their great-grandfathers.

She thought: *that's nothing. That's pattern-matching. That's fear looking for a shape.*

Then she thought: *the fragment came out of a fire that killed three people, and the men who set it are still looking for whatever survived it, and the snow started the same week I pulled this out of the ash.*

She wrapped the fragment up again and put it back against her skin, and lay awake listening to the wind come off the water, and did not sleep.

* * *

The roads closed faster than anyone expected.

The pass at Dunmarrow went first — buried in a single night, the tollhouse keeper Bess dug out alive but done with warning travelers, her door posted with a board that said CLOSED BY WEATHER AND OTHERWISE. Then the river fords at Tarn and Lowmere froze solid in the space of two days, ice so thick and sudden that drovers swore it hadn't frozen so much as arrived. The coast road remained open, technically, but the mail riders had stopped running it after two of them went missing in a whiteout within sight of their own relay station.

Wren made it back to Caerlum on the ninth day, riding the last stretch behind a grain caravan whose master had decided that if the trade was going to die, it would die moving. She came through the guildhall doors half-frozen, crusted in ice, her eyebrows still white, and stood dripping on the flagstones while the hall's heat hit her like a wall.

Kestrel was there. Of course he was there — it was past midnight and he was always there, bent over the audit tables with his sleeves pinned back, doing the work nobody thanked anyone for. He looked up when the door opened, and whatever he saw in her face made him cross the room before she'd gotten her boots off.

"Marrow Fen," she said. Her voice came out cracked. "I got out with a fragment. It's — Kestrel, it names a line. An Emberwright line. And it says the line endures where the ledger keeps it."

Something moved behind his eyes — she saw it happen, saw the careful ledger-keeper's composure take the blow and hold, mostly. "Show me," he said quietly.

"And there's more." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger, stiff and clumsy. "The whole trade's shutting down. Passes, fords, the coast road going. And everywhere I stopped, people are saying the same things. Old words. About a fire that was put out. About debts coming due." She looked at him straight, because she'd earned the right to say the next part plainly. "About blood that runs quiet in counting-houses. Kestrel — the snow started the same week I pulled this thing out of the ashes. Everyone out there thinks it means something. And I'm starting to think they might be right."

Kestrel was silent for a moment. When he spoke, his voice was the voice he used for balancing columns — level, precise, and completely at odds with what she could see happening in his face.

"Odell needs to see this tonight," he said. "Before you sleep. Before anything else."

"He won't thank us for waking him."

"He'll thank us for not burning it." Kestrel took the oilcloth bundle from her hands like it weighed more than it did. "Come on. He's still in his study. He's always still in his study."

They went up the stairs together, courier and ledger-keeper, carrying a scrap of burnt vellum into the dying light of the master's study — and Wren, watching Kestrel's careful hands open the wrapping, watching the old man's face change as the firelight touched the scorched names, understood that the long winter had already begun.

It hadn't started with the snow.

It had started here, in this room, with what they were about to read.
