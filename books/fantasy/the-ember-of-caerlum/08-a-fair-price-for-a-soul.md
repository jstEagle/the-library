## 8. A Fair Price for a Soul

The snow came down like the sky was settling accounts.

Kestrel stood at the window of his narrow room above the guildhall and watched it bury Caerlum street by street. It was the wrong kind of snow — too early, too heavy, falling in windless silence that made it seem less like weather and more like intention. The gutters had stopped draining an hour ago. Somewhere below, a cart horse screamed once and went quiet, and no one went out to see to it, because no one went out anymore.

Three days since Odell had read the scorched fragment aloud with shaking hands. Three days since the word *Emberwright* had entered Kestrel's life and refused to leave it.

He had not slept properly since. He kept seeing the blood-records spread across the master's desk, the guild's own genealogies laid against the burnt names, the lines converging on his family like rivers finding the sea. *Absorbed, not exterminated.* His mother's mother's mother, a name written in faded iron-gall ink, marked with a small symbol he'd never understood until now: a flame drawn as a merchant would draw it, precise, denominated, owned.

He was a line item in a history someone had tried to erase.

A knock at his door. Wren's knock — two quick, one soft, the courier's signal for *trouble but not catastrophe.*

"Come in."

She came in sideways, the way she always did, as if doors were tollgates she resented paying. Snow clung to her shoulders and her short dark hair was plastered flat. She looked like she'd run the whole way from wherever she'd been, which for Wren meant she probably had.

"Maren Vale is at the Salt Door," she said.

Kestrel turned from the window. "Maren Vale doesn't come to guildhalls."

"No," Wren agreed. "She comes to dying ones. Says we're easier to negotiate with." She peeled off her wet gloves finger by finger. "She wants passage north. For us. Through roads that are closed, guarded, or both. And she wants to talk terms with you specifically. Not Odell. You."

"Why me?"

"Because," Wren said, with the particular flatness she used when she thought he was being slow on purpose, "she asked for 'the junior keeper who found the entry.' Word travels, Kest. Even through snow. Especially through snow — nothing else does."

* * *

The Salt Door was the guild's oldest entrance, a low arch of black stone down by the old canal dock, sealed now for forty years because the canal had frozen one winter and never properly thawed. Maren Vale waited inside it with two of her people and a lantern she held as if it were a pet she might have to put down.

Kestrel had heard stories about Maren Vale since he was an apprentice. The queen of the salt roads. The woman who moved cargo through borders that didn't officially exist. They said she'd been a guild courier once, before the Guild of Caerlum began its long decline, before the Crown's tariffs and the Inquisitors' inspections strangled legitimate trade until only illegitimate trade could breathe. They said she kept every promise she made and remembered every promise anyone else broke, in writing, in a ledger of her own.

Kestrel believed the ledger part. Anyone who'd spent their life among merchants knew the stories about her temper were the embellishment; the ledger was the truth of her.

She was smaller than the stories suggested. Grey-streaked hair cut practical-short, a coat lined with what was very likely stolen fox, eyes the pale grey of ice over deep water. She looked Kestrel up and down the way an assayer looks at ore.

"So you're him," she said.

"I'm the junior ledger-keeper of the Guild of Caerlum," Kestrel said. "Which is a way of saying I'm nobody, and you've wasted a trip."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. "Honest and modest. That combination gets people killed in my business." She nodded at her people, who withdrew up the frozen dock without being told twice, and then at Wren, who did not move, and whom Maren apparently decided not to test. "Walk with me, keeper. Cold conversations go faster."

They walked the length of the dead canal. The ice beneath them was black and thick and groaned occasionally, like something turning over in its sleep.

"You want to go north," Maren said. "To Greyharrow. Don't insult me by denying it — your master sent pigeons to three neutral houses last week asking after Conclave protocols, and pigeons are just slow telegraphs. The roads are closed. The Crown has garrisons on the King's Span and the Inquisitors are stopping every traveler with a guild mark and asking them questions they don't survive answering. The Ashen Court's people are moving through the high passes, and they're worse, because at least the Inquisitors want you dead instead of *used*." She said it all lightly, a recitation, a menu. "I can get you there. Four days, maybe five, through the fen roads and the drowned villages. My people, my routes, my prices."

"And your price?"

"Ah." She stopped walking. The lantern swung between them, throwing their shadows long across the ice. "There it is. You know, most people your age flinch when they ask that question. They ask around it. *What do you need? What would help?* As if the answer might be friendship." Her pale eyes settled on him. "You asked it straight. Good. Here's mine: I want a guild secret."

The cold seemed to move closer.

"The Guild of Caerlum trades in impossible things," Maren went on. "Everyone knows that. What nobody knows is *how*. How you authenticate a memory sold in a bottle. How you verify a key that opens a door in a dream. How you tell true dragonbone from carved cow rib when the forgers got good twenty years ago. Your authentication methods, keeper. Your verification protocols. The craft under the trade." She smiled, thin and unbothered. "Every smuggling operation on the Ninefold Trade is drowning in fakes since the roads started closing and desperation started outpacing caution. I buy a reliquary heart, I need to know it's real before I sell it to someone who'll kill me if it isn't. Your guild knows how to know. I want to know how you know."

"That's not a secret," Kestrel said slowly. "That's the guild."

"It's a secret," Maren said, "because your charter says so. Article Nine, isn't it? *Methods of authentication shall not be disclosed outside the Guild, upon pain of oath-breaking.* I've read your charter. I collect charters." She tilted her head. "You break that article for me, and I carry you to Greyharrow alive. You don't, and you can take your chances with the Inquisitors, who will find you eventually, because you're the man who found the entry, and everyone who matters now knows it."

* * *

He made her wait while he thought, because he'd learned that much from Odell: never let a counterparty see the scale tip.

But the scale tipped immediately, and they both knew it.

Here was the arithmetic, and he ran it the way he ran every audit, coldly, line by line, because arithmetic was the one honest language he had:

Item: one guild, dying. Forty-one members where there had once been four hundred. A hall with a leaking roof. An inventory of wonders that no longer sold because the world had grown frightened of wonder, or of Inquisitors, which amounted to the same thing. A guild that would be gone within ten years regardless — fifteen if the winters stayed kind, and the winters had stopped being kind.

Item: one secret, old, technical, protective in nature. Not a treasure. Not a person. Methods. Craft. The kind of knowledge that, in a better world, would simply be taught in schools.

Item: three lives currently standing on a frozen canal — his, Wren's, and Odell's, though Odell could barely walk to the end of the street now, and would need carrying, and had already, three days ago, gripped Kestrel's wrist with terrible strength and said, *If they come for the fragment, they come for whoever read it. Get it to Greyharrow. Get yourself to Greyharrow. Let the Conclave see it. Truth needs witnesses more than it needs heroes.*

Item: the alternative. Stay. Be found. Watch the fragment burn and the last record of the Emberwright fraud burn with it, and watch history stay forged forever because the men who forged it outlived everyone who knew.

Against that: Article Nine. One sentence in a charter written by men two centuries dead, in a world where the roads had been open and the Crown had been merely greedy rather than murderous.

It balanced. He hated how cleanly it balanced.

"There's a condition," Kestrel said.

Maren raised an eyebrow. Buyers weren't supposed to add conditions.

"You don't sell it onward whole," he said. "You use it. You teach it to your own people, inside your own operation. But if it surfaces in the open market — if I ever see our authentication marks reproduced in some Graymarket catalog with your fingerprints on the disclosure — then whatever deal we make tonight is void, and I will spend the rest of my life making sure the Guild of Caerlum's last act on this earth is ruining you. We're poor, Mistress Vale. Poor guilds are very dangerous. We have nothing left to lose and excellent records."

The silence stretched. Out on the canal, the ice boomed again, long and low, a sound like a struck bell underwater.

Then Maren Vale laughed — one short, genuine bark, quickly folded away. "Odell trained you himself, didn't he."

"Is that a yes?"

"That's a yes, and a warning you should hear for free." She stepped closer, and the lantern light caught the frost in her lashes. "Whatever you're carrying north — and I know you're carrying something, because couriers travel light and you two travel like a pair of gravediggers — understand what you're doing. Secrets are currency, keeper, and you've just made your first exchange rate. It gets easier every time. That's not corruption. That's just practice." She extended a hand, mittened in good leather. "We leave tomorrow, an hour before dawn, from the Salt Door. Bring warm boots and whatever conscience you can carry without slowing down."

Kestrel shook her hand. Her grip was dry and strong and final, like a stamp on wax.

* * *

He told Odell that night, in the study, with the fire down to coals because coal cost money now the way everything cost money.

The old man listened without interrupting, which was unlike him, and when Kestrel finished — the price, the condition, the arithmetic — Odell was quiet for so long that Kestrel began composing apologies in his head, arranging them in order of dignity.

"You weighed it," Odell said at last.

"Yes."

"You weighed it honestly. Line by line. You didn't pretend it wasn't what it was."

"No."

The guildmaster nodded slowly, and looked into the coals. His hands were laced over the head of his cane, and Kestrel noticed — not for the first time, but for the first time *seeing* — how thin they had become. Hands that had signed forty years of ledgers.

"When I was younger than you," Odell said, "I broke Article Nine myself. Did you know that?"

Kestrel stared at him. "You never—"

"During the Border Famines. To buy grain passage for a city that was eating its shoe leather. I gave a river-pirate lord our method for testing dream-keys, and I told myself the same thing you're telling yourself right now." He looked up, and his eyes were tired and clear. "Do you want to know if it was worth it?"

"Yes."

"I don't know," Odell said simply. "The city lived. The guild survived. And every audit of my soul since has come back with the same entry: *unresolved.* Some debts don't resolve, Kestrel. You just keep paying them." He tapped the cane once against the floor, softly, like a period at the end of a sentence. "Go pack. And boy — write it down. Whatever you traded, write it in the ledger yourself, in your own hand, with your own name beside it. Never let anyone else record your sins. They'll get the amounts wrong."

* * *

He wrote it that night, at his own desk, by candlelight, while the snow ticked against the window like fingers trying the latch.

*Entry 4,417. Sold: Guild authentication protocols, complete, per Article Nine breach. Purchased: safe passage for three souls to Greyharrow. Balance outstanding: unresolved.*

His hand hovered over the page. There was room beneath the entry for a justification — there was always room, ledgers were generous that way — and he almost wrote it. *Necessity. Lives against methods. The lesser loss.* He could make it look clean. He was good at making things look clean; it was half of what bookkeeping was.

Instead he wrote: *Told myself it's arithmetic.*

And beneath that, smaller, in the margin where confessions live:

*Arithmetic is what we call it when we've already decided.*

He blotted the page, closed the ledger, and sat for a while in the dark listening to the snow. Tomorrow they would ride north with a smuggler queen, carrying a burnt scrap of vellum and a broken oath, toward a Conclave where every faction wanted the truth shaped to fit its hand.

He told himself it was arithmetic.

He was almost able to believe it — right up until the candle guttered, and in the last flutter of light he saw, on the windowsill where the snow had crept through a gap in the frame, a single perfect footprint. Small. Bare. Human. Melted already at the edges, as if whatever had made it had been standing there for some time, watching him write.

And it pointed inward, toward the room.
