## 10. Ledgers and Lies

The archive beneath the guildhall of Caerlum had a smell I had loved since childhood: dust and tallow and old glue, the honest stink of paper that had worked for its living. Most people thought of the deep stacks as a tomb. I had always thought of them as a hospital — a place where broken records went to be mended.

Tonight it was also an armory.

I had carried down every ledger the guild owned that touched the year 1187, the year of the Emberwright Purge. Eleven volumes in all, plus the codex with my name in a dead woman's hand, plus Wren's scorched fragment wrapped in oilcloth like a relic. I cleared the long table by the candle cabinet, set out ink, sand, straightedges, and the guild's counting frames, and did what I had been trained to do since I was nine years old.

I audited history.

* * *

Here is what nobody outside the trade understands about ledgers: they are not records. They are arguments. Every entry is someone saying *this happened, and here is what it cost.* And because money is the one thing people never lie about casually, ledgers leave fingerprints. A forged number is easy. A forged *pattern* is nearly impossible. Numbers live in families — sums imply their parts, totals imply their sums, and a century of entries braids together so tightly that one false thread shows if you pull hard enough.

The Inquisitor-General's office had published, forty years after the Purge, the definitive account: *The Record of the Ashes*, three hundred pages of testimony, confiscation orders, and execution rolls proving that the Emberwrights were monsters who burned the Weald of Sorrel for sport, that their magic was wanton destruction, and that the Crown's response was lawful, proportionate, and mournful.

Every schoolchild in the Ninefold Trade could recite it. I could recite it. That was the problem.

Because I had spent the last six hours comparing *The Record of the Ashes* against something the Inquisitors had never been allowed to see: the Guild of Caerlum's own books. The guild had held contracts in the Weald of Sorrel. The guild had insured the harvests there. The guild had, in the years before the Purge, extended credit to Emberwright households — and a creditor keeps better records than any court.

I started with grain.

*The Record* claimed the Weald was put to the torch in the third week of Harvestmonth, 1187, as punishment for the burning of the granaries at Fordell Bridge. It was a tidy story: fire answered with fire. But Caerlum's insurance rolls told a different story. The Weald's granary bonds — policies against loss by fire — had been *cancelled* in the second week of Harvestmonth. Not claimed. Cancelled. The guild does not cancel a policy before a disaster; we cancel when we know one is coming and want no part of paying for it. Someone had known. And the cancellation was signed with a countersignature from the Crown's own excise office, dated four days *before* the granaries at Fordell Bridge allegedly burned.

You cannot insure against a fire that has already happened. You especially cannot cancel the policy four days before the fire unless you expect the fire — or plan it.

I wrote that down. My hand was steady. I remember being surprised by that.

* * *

The testimony was worse. Or better, depending on whether you were looking for truth or for weapons. I was looking for both, which is a bad combination in a man.

*The Record* contained eleven sworn depositions from witnesses of the Weald burning. I found eight of the eleven witnesses in our debt rolls. All eight had owed the Crown substantial sums at the time of their testimony. All eight had their debts marked *remitted* within a month of signing. One of them — a tanner named Hobb Vass — had his remission backdated by nine days, which is the kind of sloppiness that happens when a clerk is tired and assumes no one will ever check.

No one had checked. For a hundred and thirty years, no one had checked, because checking was heresy and heresy was ash.

But the entry that stopped me cold wasn't about the witnesses at all. It was in the guild's transfer book, autumn of 1187, two months after the Purge concluded. A single line:

*Received of the Office of the Inquisitor-General: 4,000 crowns, being payment in full for services rendered in the matter of the Weald, per contract of 12th Frostmonth, 1186.*

Services rendered. Four thousand crowns — a fortune, in those days — paid to a trading guild for *services* in the matter of a massacre. And the contract date: the twelfth of Frostmonth, 1186. Nearly a full year *before* the Weald burned.

I sat back on my stool and looked at that line for a long time. The candle guttered. Somewhere above me, the guildhall creaked the way old buildings do when they're settling into winter, and I thought: this building has been sitting on this sentence for a century. This whole dying, shabby, half-empty guildhall has been squatting on the proof, and none of us knew, because none of us read past our own losses.

What services? I dug. The answer was in the correspondence file for that year, tied with rotted ribbon. Three letters between Master Aldous Renk — guildmaster then — and the Inquisitor-General's office. The first offered "the quiet movement of certain persons and goods." The second confirmed routes. The third, the shortest, simply said: *Done as agreed. The roads were clear. No one asked questions.*

The Emberwrights hadn't just been exterminated. They had been *transported* — moved along guild roads, in guild wagons, under guild seals, to places where no one would ask questions. And the guild had been paid to keep its mouth shut, and had kept its mouth shut for five generations, right up until the day the last Emberwright bloodline got absorbed into merchant families like mine and everyone conveniently forgot why our grandmothers' marriage contracts all came from the same handful of northern houses.

My house.

I put my palms flat on the table until they stopped shaking. Then I kept working, because that was the only prayer I knew.

* * *

Wren found me near dawn. She came down the archive stairs carrying bread and a lantern and the particular expression she wore when she'd decided not to ask questions until she'd finished reading my face.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"I've slept since Frostmonth." It was true, technically. "Look at this."

She set the bread down, pulled a stool beside me, and read. I watched her the way Odell used to watch me over difficult accounts — not helping, just waiting to see where my eyes snagged. Her eyes snagged exactly where mine had.

"Four days," she said. "They cancelled the insurance four days before the fire."

"Before the fire they *used as justification*. The fire that made the purge lawful."

"And the witnesses—"

"Bought. All of them. Cheaply." I turned to the transfer book. "And this."

She read it twice. When she looked up, her face had gone very still, the way it had gone still on the road to Greyharrow when the Inquisitors' horses came through the trees. "Kestrel. This isn't evidence. This is a confession. Our confession. If you stand up in front of the Conclave and show them this page, you're not just accusing the Inquisitor-General. You're accusing the guild."

"I know."

"The guild that took you in. Fed you. Taught you your letters." Her voice stayed level, but I heard the thing underneath it. "Odell signed the annual confirmations his whole life. He renewed the seal on these archives every year. He knew."

"He didn't know everything." I said it fast, and hated how much it sounded like forgiveness. "But he knew enough. He knew the shape of it. He told me to burn the prophecy and keep my head down, and I thought he was being cautious. He was being *loyal*. To a lie older than he was."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then she did something I didn't expect. She reached across the table, turned the transfer book toward herself, and began copying the entry out in her own neat courier's hand.

"What are you doing?"

"Making a second copy. You taught me that. Never let the original travel alone." She didn't look up. "If you're going to do this, we do it properly. Two copies, separate satchels, different routes to Greyharrow. And Kestrel—" Now she looked up. "If the guild falls for this, it falls. Better a dead guild than a lying one. Isn't that what you said on the road? Justice over law?"

I had said that. I'd said it with a knife-wound in my side and her fingers keeping pressure on it, which is a time when men say brave things cheaply. She was handing the words back to me now, in daylight, with bread going stale on the table, and they weighed ten times what they had.

"Yes," I said. "That's what I said."

* * *

We worked through the morning. By the time the light through the archive grates turned from grey to gold, I had built the case the way I'd build any audit, and it was beautiful in the terrible way that only complete things are beautiful.

One: the pretext fire was foreknown and forecanceled in the Crown's own insurance countersignatures. Two: the witness testimony was purchased, provably, via debt remissions with clerical fingerprints still visible. Three: the confiscation inventories in *The Record* did not match the guild's own receiving logs — the Crown reported seizing forty-one Emberwright artifacts of power; our books showed sixty-three crossing our docks, and of those, twenty-two were entered as "miscellaneous ironware" in a hand I recognized as Aldous Renk's. The guild had helped disappear the very objects that proved the Emberwrights' magic was craft and covenant, not carnage. Four: the transport payments. Five — and this was the blade I saved for last — the chronology itself. *The Record* placed the Weald burning in response to Emberwright aggression. Our freight manifests showed Crown troops and siege-fire stores moving into the Weald *three weeks before* the first alleged Emberwright attack. You do not stage a punitive expedition before the crime it punishes. Unless the crime is yours.

I laid the five proofs out in sequence on the long table and stood over them, and I felt something kindle in my chest that had nothing to do with the fire in my blood. This was colder and steadier than that. This was arithmetic. For a hundred and thirty years the truth had been sitting in dusty rooms exactly like this one, patient as interest accruing, waiting for someone to do the sum.

"They'll say it's forgery," Wren said. She had slept two hours on a pallet of ledger-leaves and come back sharper. "That's the first thing the Inquisitor-General will say. A disgraced junior clerk, caught with forbidden documents, fabricates evidence to save his neck."

"I know. So we don't bring documents."

She frowned. "Then what do we bring?"

"Methods." I tapped the counting frame. "Any fool can forge a page. Nobody can forge a century. Five independent record series — insurance, debt, freight, receiving, transfers — kept by different clerks, in different decades, for different purposes, that happen to interlock perfectly. To fake that, the Inquisitor-General would have had to corrupt five generations of guild bookkeepers in advance, including the ones who hated each other. Renk and his successor weren't even speaking in 1203; there's a grievance file thicker than my arm. You can't run a conspiracy through men who won't share a dinner table." I looked at her. "Guild accounting will hang him. The very methods the Conclave uses to settle disputes between trades. They can call a page a lie. They can't call cross-verification a lie without calling every contract in the Ninefold Trade void."

Wren stared at me, and then she laughed — a short, startled sound, there and gone. "You're going to audit him to death."

"I'm going to audit him *in public*. There's a difference. Death is quick."

* * *

It should have felt like triumph. For about an hour, it did. I walked the rows of shelves with the five proofs copied fair into a folio, and I felt tall in a way I had never felt in this building, where I had spent my whole life making myself small.

Then I pulled the folio back and reread it, and the feeling curdled, because I am a ledger-keeper, and a ledger-keeper knows that every entry has two sides.

Here was the debit column. If I spoke at the Conclave, the guild was finished — not diminished, finished. Every charter the Crown had ever granted us would be revoked inside a season; every sister-house would disavow us; Maren's smugglers would pick our bones. The families descended from those northern marriages — my family, such as it was — would spend generations wearing the name of collaborators. Children who had done nothing would learn in school that their great-great-grandparents sold the Emberwrights to the pyres.

And here was the credit column, and it was worse: the truth might not save anyone at all. The Court wanted the prophecy fulfilled. The Crown wanted the prophecy buried. Nobody in Greyharrow — not the delegates, not the guilds, not the frozen crowds outside the walls — actually wanted to hear that the founding crime of the modern order was a fraud. Truth doesn't set people free. It hands them a bill. And most people, given a bill a century overdue, would rather burn the accountant.

I thought of Odell, dying somewhere under Inquisitor questioning, if he was still alive at all, and I understood for the first time what his warning had really been. *Burn it,* he'd said. Not because the prophecy was dangerous. Because the truth underneath it was heavier than any man should carry, and he had carried it for forty years, alone, and it had bent him small.

I set the folio down on the table between the five proofs, and I blew out all but one candle, and I made myself look at the choice plainly, the way you look at a balance sheet with no good lines in it.

Wren came back down the stairs. She must have seen it in my face, because she didn't speak. She just stood at the bottom step, waiting.

"They'll kill us," I said. "Maybe not at the Conclave — too many witnesses. But on the road home, or in a year, quietly. And the guild dies either way. That's the price of the entry."

"Then why are you smiling?"

I hadn't known I was. I touched my mouth and found it true. "Because it balances," I said. "Not fairly. Not kindly. But it balances. And I would rather be the man who tells the truth and loses everything than the man who kept perfect books for a lie."

Wren crossed the room, picked up the folio, and tucked it inside her coat, against her heart, where couriers carry the things they've decided to die for.

"Then we'd better get to Greyharrow," she said. "Before someone reads our mail."

Which is when we heard the knock at the guildhall door above — three slow strikes, official spacing, the knock of men who expected doors to open for them — and the voice of the porter calling down the archive stairs, uncertain and afraid:

"Keeper Nystrom. There are Crown riders at the gate. They say they've come for the records of 1187."
