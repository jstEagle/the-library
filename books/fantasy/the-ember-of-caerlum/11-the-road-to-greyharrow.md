## 11. The Road to Greyharrow

I learned to run before I learned to read. That's what courier work does to you — it teaches your legs to think faster than your head, and it teaches you that the world is mostly distance, and distance is mostly weather.

The weather on the road to Greyharrow was trying to kill us.

Three days out of Caerlum and the snow had stopped being snow and started being a decision the sky had made about us personally. It came sideways off the Greyharrow pass in long white slaps, erasing the road behind us as fast as we made it, which was good for pursuit and terrible for everything else. Kestrel walked like a man reading a book he hated — hunched, deliberate, one hand pressed flat against his ribs where he'd stowed the evidence copy inside his coat.

"You're favoring that side," I said.

"I'm protecting the documents."

"That's what I said."

He gave me a look over his shoulder. Even half-frozen, even hunted, Kestrel Nystrom could not stop being precise. "You said I was favoring my side. I am protecting the only copy of a century of forged Crown records. These are different activities."

"They're going to get you killed either way." I pulled my scarf up over my chin. "If an Inquisitor puts a blade in you, they'll find the papers anyway. Better to be fast than armored."

"Then we'll be fast."

We were not fast. The roads through the Ninefold Trade had closed behind us one by one — first the toll bridges, then the waystations, then whole villages shuttered with ash marks on their doors, the old sign for plague or worse. Superstition travels faster than any courier. People had heard about the winter that fell out of season, and they'd heard other things too, things with teeth in them: that the Emberwrights had come back, that fire slept in someone's blood, that the end of the world had been scheduled a hundred years ago and the date was now.

Kestrel hadn't told me everything about what happened at the waystation. I knew enough. I'd seen him afterward, sitting alone by the dead fire with his hands open in his lap like they belonged to a stranger, and I'd known better than to ask. Some questions are just knives you hand people.

So we walked. And on the fourth day, at a place called the Sorrel Ford where the river ran shallow under grey ice, the Inquisitors found us.

* * *

I smelled them before I saw them — woodsmoke and oiled leather, the particular smell of men who have been standing still in the cold for hours waiting for something to walk into them. Courier instinct again. Your nose learns ambushes before your eyes do.

I caught Kestrel's sleeve and pulled him down behind a fallen alder at the treeline. Below us, the ford. Above the ford, three figures in plain grey travel cloaks that fooled no one, because Inquisitors of the Crown always stand like they're holding a warrant against their own spines.

"Three," I breathed.

"There'll be a fourth," Kestrel whispered back. "There's always a fourth. They teach it in their doctrine — *the unseen witness*. Someone flanking."

"How do you know their doctrine?"

"I've been reading their records for two weeks. You learn how people think from what they write down." He said it flatly, but I heard the thing underneath it: he'd learned how they thought, and part of him was afraid he'd learned it too well. That was the week we were having. Everything Kestrel touched turned into a mirror.

"The river," I said. "Ice is thin at the ford this time of year. If we cross wide—"

"If we cross wide, we're slow, and slow is dead." He was already unbuttoning his coat. My stomach dropped before I understood why, and then I understood. He was taking out the copy. The ledger pages, copied fair in his own careful hand — a century of entries proving the Inquisitor-General had rewritten history, had manufactured the crime that condemned the Emberwright line, had built a lie so large the whole Ninefold Trade stood on top of it without looking down.

"No," I said.

"We split them. One of us crosses loud and draws the chase downstream. The other goes quiet through the trees with the papers."

"And which one of us is which?"

He looked at me. Snow was settling in his hair, in his lashes, and there was something terribly calm in his face, the calm of a man doing arithmetic. "You're the courier. You know this country. You take the papers."

"And you take the Inquisitors."

"I take the *attention*. There's a difference."

"It's a difference that gets people killed."

"Wren." He said my name quietly, and it landed harder than a shout would have. "Odell died keeping my name out of their mouths. Maren gave us horses and a map and asked for nothing. Everyone who helps me pays for me. I'm not going to add you to the ledger."

"That's not how ledgers work," I said. "You don't get to settle debts by dying. Dying just transfers them."

For a moment neither of us moved. The snow filled up the silence between us, and I thought — absurdly, furiously — that if we survived this I was going to yell at him properly, at length, indoors, possibly with wine.

Then the fourth Inquisitor stepped out of the trees ten feet behind us and said, "Both of you. Hands where I can see them."

* * *

Everything after that happened very fast, the way bad things do, and I remember it in pieces, the way bad things stay.

Piece one: Kestrel moving. Not running — turning, smooth and low, putting himself between me and the man with the crossbow, and shoving the document copy into my hands like it weighed nothing, like it wasn't the heaviest thing either of us would ever carry.

Piece two: the shout from the ford. The other three coming up the bank at a dead run, boots punching through crust, and the leader's voice cracking across the snow — *"Take the courier alive! The boy — the General wants the boy breathing!"*

Breathing. They wanted Kestrel breathing. I filed that away in the place couriers keep things for later, the cold drawer, and did the math instead: four of them, two of us, a river, and a forest full of places to die.

Piece three: the bolt. The fourth man fired, and he was close, and Kestrel was between us, and I watched the quarrel come out of the dark of the trees like a black stitch pulling shut.

I didn't think. Thinking is for after. I grabbed Kestrel's collar and threw us both sideways down the slope, and the bolt went through the space where his throat had been and buried itself in the alder with a sound like a knuckle cracking. We hit the snow together, rolled, came apart, and I was up and dragging him by the arm toward the treeline while the world shouted around us.

Piece four: the fire.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the whole point of everything now, and because nobody else will ever write this down but me. When the lead Inquisitor caught Kestrel's shoulder and spun him around — when the man raised his short sword with the easy, bored motion of someone executing paperwork — Kestrel's hand came up and *caught fire*.

Not like a torch. Like a word. Flame stood up along his fingers and forearm, orange-white, silent, casting our shadows huge and wrong against the snow, and the Inquisitor recoiled with a scream that wasn't pain yet, just terror, the oldest kind, the kind people screamed when the Emberwrights burned the world a hundred years ago. The man stumbled back into his fellows. The snow around Kestrel's feet slid into water. Steam rose off him in a column, and for one heartbeat he stood in the middle of the winter like a door opening onto summer.

And his face — I'll carry his face. Not rage. Horror. He looked at his own burning hand the way you look at a ledger entry that cannot exist.

Then he closed his fist, and the fire went out, and he ran.

* * *

We ran. The Inquisitors came after us, but slower now, and I understood why: they weren't chasing a fugitive anymore. They were chasing proof. The General wanted Kestrel breathing because a living Emberwright was worth more than a dead one — a weapon, a symbol, a prophecy fulfilled on schedule. Every step Kestrel took made him more valuable and less safe, and there was nothing I could do about either except run faster than him, which I did, hauling him off the game trail and into the deep trees where the snow lay soft and trackless.

We didn't stop until full dark, in the hollow under a granite shelf where the wind couldn't reach. No fire. Fire meant light; light meant death; and besides, I kept thinking, he *is* fire now, and the thought sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Kestrel sat with his back against the stone and his hands in his lap, palms up, the way he'd sat at the waystation. Studying them. Turning them over like pages.

"Say something," I said finally.

"What would you like me to say?"

"Anything true."

A long pause. Snow ticked down through the branches above us, fine as sifted flour.

"When it happened," he said, "there was a moment — half a heartbeat — where it felt *right*. Where the fire came up my arm and I thought, oh. Oh, this is what I'm for." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "That's what frightens me. Not the flame. The fit of it. It fit me like Odell's coat fits me. Like it was measured for me before I was born."

"Because it was," I said. "That's the whole point of everything we're carrying to Greyharrow. None of this was your choice. The bloodline, the prophecy, the General's forgery — all of it decided for you. But the Conclave isn't decided. Greyharrow isn't decided. What you do next is the first thing in this entire story anyone's let you choose."

He looked at me then, really looked, and the calm cracked a little, and underneath it was just Kestrel — tired, scared, twenty-some years old, a junior ledger-keeper who'd wanted an invisible life and gotten an inheritance of ash instead.

"You saved my life," he said. "At the ford. That bolt—"

"You'd have done the same."

"I genuinely don't know that I would. I've spent my whole life making sure I was never in the way of anything." He exhaled, and his breath ghosted white between us. "Why did you do it?"

It was a real question. He wasn't fishing. He honestly could not see it, this man who could read a century of lies out of marginalia, who could catch a sword on fire, who could not see the simplest entry in the whole book.

"Because you're mine," I said. "My cargo. My route. My responsibility. Guild law, chapter one."

Something moved through his face — disappointment, maybe, arriving and being politely shown out again. "Guild law."

"And," I said, because the cold makes people honest and because if we died tomorrow I refused to die with an unwritten page, "because when you stood in the snow with your hand burning and looked more frightened than the man with the sword, I thought: whatever you are, whoever wrote you into their books a hundred years ago, none of them know you. I know you. I've carried your letters for three years. You correct other people's arithmetic in your head during guild meetings, you feed the hall cat even though it's technically against inventory rules, and you apologized to a smuggler queen for tracking mud. Whatever the Emberwrights were — whatever the prophecies say — *that's* the person I threw myself in front of a bolt for. The rest is just ink."

The silence after that was a different kind of silence. Warmer. The snow kept falling, and somewhere far off a tree cracked under its weight, and neither of us moved, and something passed between us that we didn't name — both of us too well trained for that, too careful, too aware that some entries shouldn't be posted until the audit is done. But it was written. We both knew it was written. Unspoken things are still on the books; they just sit in a different column.

"Thank you," Kestrel said at last, quietly.

"Don't thank me. Thank the alder."

That got a breath of a laugh out of him, small and surprised, and I counted it as the first true thing all day.

* * *

We reached the Greyharrow road two days later, following the smoke of other travelers because smoke meant company and company meant safety in numbers, and because the Conclave city drew people the way a hearth draws moths. Refugees, traders, delegates' outriders, pilgrims of every superstition — the road thickened daily, and we folded ourselves into it, two more frozen nobodies among hundreds.

Kestrel had sewn the last of the evidence into the lining of my courier's satchel, against my body, where no search would dare go. His original had burned with the rest at the ford — deliberately, he'd told me later, dropped into the river current while they ran, so the Inquisitors would fish sodden pages out of the ice and believe they'd won something. Let them carry their prize to Greyharrow. Let the General gloat over a forgery of a forgery.

"You realize," he'd said, "this means the truth now exists in exactly one place in the world."

"Where?"

He'd tapped my shoulder, right over the satchel strap, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read at the time and have spent every day since learning to read.

Now, at dusk on the seventh day, the towers of Greyharrow rose out of the white ahead of us — black spires stitched with lights, ringed by the tents and banners of a dozen powers, the neutral city wearing its neutrality like armor. Somewhere inside those walls sat the Inquisitor-General with his stolen pages and his smiling fraud. Somewhere inside those walls waited the Ashen Court, wanting their prophecy fulfilled. Somewhere inside those walls, delegates from every guild in the Ninefold Trade were unpacking their own versions of history, each one certain, each one armed.

I touched the satchel strap. Papers rustled faintly against my ribs, warm from my own body heat, the only warm thing for fifty miles.

"Whatever happens in there," Kestrel said beside me, his eyes on the towers, "you get out. Whatever happens — you deliver it, and then you get out. Promise me."

"Couriers don't promise," I said. "Couriers deliver."

Ahead, the gates of Greyharrow opened for the evening traffic, and the light that spilled out was gold and crowded and utterly indifferent to what we carried — and on the wall above the gate, watching the road with the stillness of a held breath, stood a woman in ash-grey robes whose eyes, even at this distance, I could feel find us in the crowd and stay.
