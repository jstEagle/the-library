## 12. What I Am

The waystation at Hollowmere had been a tollhouse once, back when the Greyharrow road carried more than rumor and fear. Now it was a single stone room with a sagging roof, a hearth that smoked more than it warmed, and a stable lean-to where two horses — Wren's mare and the smuggler's nag Maren had sold them at an honest criminal's markup — stood steaming in the falling snow.

Kestrel counted what they had left. It was habit, not comfort. One copy of the Emberwright fragment, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. The master's seal, which he still caught himself touching like a wound. Fourteen silver, three coppers. Half a loaf gone hard as slate. And Wren, asleep sitting up against the wall with her cloak pulled to her chin and her knife loose in her hand even now, because she had learned on the road from Caerlum that sleep was something you did with one eye open.

He should have been asleep too. He knew the arithmetic of exhaustion as well as anyone: two days without rest made you slow, three made you dead. But every time he closed his eyes he saw Odell's face — or rather, he saw the space where Odell's face had been, because the news had come to them secondhand, through a drover who'd heard it from a carter who'd heard it from a guard drunk enough to talk. *The old guildmaster of Caerlum. Died under questioning. Never gave up a name.*

*Never gave up your name,* Kestrel corrected himself. That was the shape of it. Odell had spent twenty years keeping him small and safe, and when the bill finally came due, the old man had paid it in full and left his apprentice holding the receipt.

So Kestrel sat by the smoking fire and did what he always did when the world became unbearable. He worked.

The fragment lay open on his knee beneath the oilcloth's edge. Scorched parchment, half a page, rescued by Wren from a burning guildhouse at the cost of nearly her own life. Most of it was ash-eaten nonsense. But one line survived whole, written in the cramped, precise hand of a guild scribe three generations dead:

*...and the Ember shall pass not into ash but into account, for the blood was never ended, only entered into the ledgers of the faithful...*

Entered into the ledgers. Absorbed. Married in, adopted in, hidden in plain sight among the very families that swore the Emberwrights were extinct. His family. His blood.

He had known it intellectually for weeks. He had proven it with cross-references and marginalia, with the cold beautiful certainty of accounting. But tonight, alone with the fire and the sleeping girl and the snow piling against the door, he felt it in his hands.

They looked like ordinary hands. Ink-stained at the nails. A healing blister on the right thumb from gripping a horse's reins wrong. Hands built for pens, not —

The knock came at midnight exactly.

Three raps. Evenly spaced. Not the desperate hammering of a traveler lost in the snow; travelers knocked like people, arrhythmic and apologetic. This was the knock of someone who had decided in advance how many times to knock, and who considered the matter closed thereafter.

Wren was awake before the third rap. She didn't move otherwise — just her eyes opening, black and alert, and her hand closing silently around the knife. She looked at him. He looked back. In four days of flight they had developed a whole language of glances, and this one said: *you answer nothing, I answer everything.*

She rose without sound and put her eye to the gap beside the door.

"Two men," she breathed. "Cloaks to the ground. Snow on their shoulders — they've walked from the road." A pause. "One of them is wearing a Crown badge under his cloak. I can see the chain."

Inquisitors. Of course. The ambush on the Greyharrow road had cost them their evidence copy but not their scent; someone had tracked them east through the closed passes, someone patient enough to walk behind them for two days in weather that killed lesser men.

"They'll have seen the horses," Kestrel whispered.

"They'll have seen the horses," she agreed. "Back door?"

"There is no back door. There's a window over the stable, if the frame hasn't rotted."

"Then we go out the way we came in, fast, before they —"

The door opened.

Not broken — opened. The latch lifted with a small click, and a man stepped in out of the snow as though he owned the mortgage on the building. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the mild, reasonable face of a man who had spent his career asking questions and being believed. Behind him, filling the doorway, stood a second man built like a door itself, with a cudgel resting easy against his shoulder.

"Inquisitor Hale," the first man said pleasantly, showing a leaden badge. "Crown Office of Inquiry. You'll forgive the hour. We've been following you since the ford."

Wren moved — fast, low, toward the dark corner where the shadows were deepest, putting herself between Kestrel and the big man. Kestrel stayed seated. It wasn't courage. His legs simply declined to participate.

"You've made good time," Hale continued, warming his hands at their fire as if invited. "Better than expected, honestly. The roads are closed. Superstitious drovers, bad snow. And yet here you are, Master Nystrom, halfway to Greyharrow with a piece of paper in your shirt that has caused the Crown a great deal of embarrassment."

"I don't know what you mean," Kestrel said. "I'm a ledger-keeper. My colleague and I are traveling on guild business."

Hale smiled. It was a kind smile, which made it worse. "Yes. Guild business. Do you know what I admire about you guildsmen? The precision. Everything entered, everything balanced. It makes my work so much easier, because people who keep accounts always believe the accounts will save them." He held out a hand, palm up. "The fragment, please. And the seal. And then you and your colleague will walk back to Caerlum with me, and in the spring there will be a trial, and it will be brief."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then Brannoc here becomes involved, and I confess I find Brannoc's methods inelegant." Hale sighed, genuinely regretful. "I'd hoped to avoid it. You're not criminals, either of you. You're clerks. Clerks who've read something they shouldn't. There's no shame in handing it over."

There wasn't. That was the terrible thing. Kestrel ran the calculation the way he ran all calculations — debts, assets, likely outcomes — and the rational entry was clear. Give them the fragment. The original forgery proofs were already copied in his memory, distributed in three dead-drops along the road to Greyharrow. Losing the fragment would hurt, but it would not stop the truth reaching the Conclave. Handing it over would buy their lives, probably, maybe, if Hale was a man of his word, and Inquisitors were famously men of their word because their word was backed by the Crown's entire apparatus of violence.

*Give it to him. Live. Reach Greyharrow empty-handed and testify anyway.*

His hand went to his chest.

And stopped.

Because Wren was looking at him. Not pleading. Wren didn't plead. She was looking at him the way she'd looked at the burning guildhouse from the treeline — measuring what could be saved, deciding what was worth the cost — and what her eyes said, in their new silent language, was: *your choice. I'll follow it. But know what you're choosing.*

Odell had died without giving up a name. Had sat in a Crown cell while men like Brannoc asked their questions, and had died with the ledger of his silence perfectly balanced, and Kestrel could almost see the old man's final entry, neat and small in the great book of things: *paid in full.*

"No," Kestrel said.

It came out steadier than he felt. Hale's eyebrows rose a fraction, the first genuine expression he'd shown.

"No?"

"The fragment stays with me. So does the seal. And you can write that down in whatever ledger the Crown keeps — entry refused, balance unpaid."

Something shifted in Hale's face. The kindness drained out of it like water from a cracked basin, and what remained was older and much less tired. "Brannoc."

The big man moved.

Later — in the long hours after, in the years after — Kestrel would try to reconstruct those few seconds and fail. Memory kept presenting them to him out of order, like a ledger whose pages had been shuffled by a vandal. What he retained with terrible clarity was this:

Brannoc's cudgel coming down. Wren going under it — not struck, *dodging*, but dodging into the wall, pinned, her knife skidding across the floorboards. Hale stepping back with the patience of a man watching paperwork process itself. And Kestrel rising from his chair with his hands out — not in surrender, not in attack, just *out*, the way a man flinches, the way a body tries to protect itself —

— and the fire answering.

It didn't come from the hearth. That was the thing he would remember most, the detail that made the whole night obscene: the hearth-fire didn't leap, didn't spread, didn't do any of the natural things fire does. The heat came from *inside*. From his palms, his wrists, somewhere behind his sternum where a banked coal had waited twenty-three years for exactly this permission. Light poured out of his own skin, gold-white, edged in a color he had no name for — ember-red shot through with something colder, older, like light remembered from before the world cooled.

Brannoc turned toward him. That was all. He turned, raising the cudgel, an expression of dull surprise crossing his heavy face —

— and Kestrel's hands came up between them, and the air *ignited*.

Not a flame. A *presence*. Heat without fuel, light without source, a column of white-gold that took Brannoc the way a wave takes a swimmer. The man had time to make one sound — not a scream, worse than a scream, a short wet note of pure astonishment, as if death itself had surprised him — and then he was burning. Burning from within, the way Kestrel was burning, except Kestrel's body knew how to hold it and Brannoc's did not. Fire poured out of the man's mouth and eyes. His cloak went first, then everything else, and what dropped to the floorboards of the waystation at Hollowmere was not a man anymore. It was what remains.

The silence afterward was total. Even the storm seemed to pause outside, snow hanging in the windless dark like a held breath.

Kestrel looked at his hands.

They were unmarked. That was the horror of it — not blisters, not scorching, not so much as a smudge of soot. Clean, ink-stained at the nails, the same healing blister on the right thumb. A ledger-keeper's hands. They trembled, yes, but hands tremble after any shock, don't they, anyone's hands, ordinary hands —

Except they weren't. They had never been. Every page they'd ever touched, every careful column of figures, every debt recorded and forgiven — all of it had been done by *this*, by the thing that lived coiled behind his sternum wearing his skin like a borrowed coat, waiting a century for its entry to come due.

*The Ember shall pass not into ash but into account.*

He understood, suddenly and completely, why the Ashen Court wanted him alive. Why Sable Vane had spoken to him with such gentle sincerity about control. Because it *was* controllable — that was the trap inside the gift — it had answered him, obeyed him, risen at his flinch and struck at his terror, and a man could live a whole life telling himself he would only use it justly, only carefully, only once —

Only once was all it took. Brannoc had been a thug, a Crown instrument, a man come to beat a confession out of a clerk. And Kestrel had burned him to a shadow on the floor in less time than it took to write the sentence describing it.

"Kestrel."

Wren's voice. She had retrieved her knife; she stood between him and Hale, blade leveled at the Inquisitor's throat, though Hale hadn't moved. The Inquisitor stood pressed against the far wall, and his face — his mild, reasonable, believing face — had finally shown Kestrel something true.

Reverence. Underneath the fear, underneath the horror, unmistakable and appalling: reverence. He was looking at Kestrel the way men look at the fulfillment of prophecy.

"So it's real," Hale whispered. "The Court wasn't lying. The bloodline —" He swallowed. "Do you know what you've just done? Do you know what you've *proven*? They'll never stop now. Neither side. You've confirmed a hundred years of —"

"Get out," Kestrel said.

His voice came out wrong. Doubled, almost — his own voice with something underneath it, a resonance like a bell struck in another room. He heard it and hated it and could not stop it.

"Master Nystrom —"

"*Get out.*"

Hale went. He went fast, snatching open the door and stumbling into the snow, and Kestrel heard him running — actually running, boots slapping through drifts, a grown man fleeing into a blizzard rather than spend another minute in the room with what Kestrel had become.

Then it was quiet again, and the fire in Kestrel's chest began, slowly, to bank itself. He felt it go down reluctantly, like a creditor accepting partial payment, and the warmth retreated into that deep place behind his sternum and settled there, patient, satisfied.

He looked at the place on the floor where Brannoc had been. There was remarkably little. A scorched outline. A buckle. The cudgel, half-charred, smelling of fat and iron. Somewhere in Caerlum there was presumably a wife or a mother or a drinking companion who would wait for this man and never learn what had become of him, and Kestrel found that he could not make himself feel what he was supposed to feel, and that frightened him more than the fire had.

*You're supposed to be sick,* he told himself. *You're supposed to weep.* He was sick, a little — his stomach clenched and rolled — but beneath the nausea sat something else, something warm and calm and utterly alien, some deep-boned satisfaction that was not his, could not be his, and yet used his heartbeat to purr.

Wren touched his arm. He flinched hard enough to knock the oilcloth-wrapped fragment from his knee, and neither of them moved to pick it up.

"Look at me," she said.

He couldn't. He stared at his hands instead, turning them over, palms then backs, palms then backs, as if the evidence might change on the second audit. "I killed him."

"Yes."

"With my *hands*, Wren. I didn't stab him, I didn't trip him, I didn't — I *burned* him. From the inside. I felt it happen and part of me —" His voice broke. "Part of me *agreed* with it."

She crouched in front of him, forcing her way into his line of sight. Her face was pale and set and completely unafraid of him, and he would think about that later, how she looked at the thing he'd become and saw only Kestrel, and what that would come to mean.

"Listen to me," she said. "That man was going to beat us both and drag us south in chains, and the last person who tried to protect you from men like him died asking for water in a Crown cell. You didn't start this fight. You finished it. Those aren't the same crime."

"They are if the weapon is *me*."

"No." She said it flatly, absolutely, the way she said everything. "The weapon is a choice. You made one tonight. You'll make others. What matters is whether you keep choosing on purpose — or let the fire choose for you." She picked up the fragment from the floor, wrapped it, and pressed it back against his chest, over the place where the warmth lived. "Greyharrow is two days north. The Conclave sits in nine. Whatever you are, you get to decide what it's *for*. Nobody else. Not the Crown, not the Court, not the prophecy."

"Not even me," Kestrel said quietly. "Not after tonight."

But he took the fragment. And when they saddled the horses in the gray hour before dawn, leaving the waystation and its scorched outline behind forever, Kestrel rode with his palms flat against his thighs and noticed — with a dread that had stopped pretending to be anything else —

that the snow around them, for fifty yards in every direction,

was melting.
