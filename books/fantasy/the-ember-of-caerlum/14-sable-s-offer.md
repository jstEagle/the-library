## 14. Sable's Offer

Sable Vane had been watching Kestrel Nystrom for eleven days, and in that time she had learned three things about him.

First: he counted. Constantly, unconsciously, the way other men breathed. She had watched him tally the cost of a bowl of stew against the weight of coin in his pocket, watched his eyes flick across a market stall and price every item on it without seeming to look at any of them. It was not greed. It was grammar. Numbers were simply the language he thought in.

Second: he was afraid of himself. He hid it well — better than most, better than she'd expected from a ledger-keeper — but fear has a texture, and she had spent her life reading textures. He kept his hands still when they wanted to shake. He stood too carefully, as though his body were something he'd borrowed and didn't want to return damaged.

Third: he was kind in ways that would get him killed.

She watched him now from the shadowed gallery above Greyharrow's Pilgrim's Rest, the waystation where the Conclave's lesser delegates were being warehoused like goods awaiting inspection. The great hall below roared with argument — guild factors shouting over Crown clerks, an Ashen acolyte trying to be heard above both — but Kestrel sat apart at a corner table with Wren, the courier girl, and he was doing something no one else in the building was doing.

He was listening.

An old woman — a wool-trader by her badge, some minor house from the southern roads — had cornered their table, and instead of waving her off, Kestrel had turned his whole attention to her. Sable read lips well enough to follow it. The woman's son had been conscripted by Inquisitor patrols on the frozen passes; she wanted to know if the guild could trace him through freight records. And Kestrel — hunted man, heir to a burned bloodline, the single most valuable fugitive in nine kingdoms — pulled a scrap of paper toward him and began writing down the details of her complaint.

*He is going to die,* Sable thought, *because he cannot stop caring about small debts.*

The Court's elders would never understand that about him. They saw the Emberwright fire and imagined a weapon that needed only aiming. Sable had argued otherwise for months, and had been overruled, and had watched the hardliners begin making arrangements she could not sanction — arrangements that involved taking the boy apart to see what made him burn.

Which was why she was here, alone, against orders, committing what the Court would call treason and what she preferred to call arithmetic.

* * *

Getting him alone was the easy part. She had done harder things with less preparation.

Wren left first — sent running toward the stables by a stable-boy with a message about a lame horse, a message Sable had paid four coppers to have delivered. The old wool-trader finished and shuffled away. And when Kestrel rose to find food for the table, Sable simply walked out of the gallery's dark and fell into step beside him in the corridor toward the kitchens, close enough that he felt her before he saw her.

She knew the exact moment he recognized her, because his stride hitched — half a beat, no more — and then smoothed over, because he was too proud to show fear in public. Good instincts. Wrong reasons.

"Don't," she said quietly. "Not here. There are six of the General's people in this building wearing servants' colors, and two of them know your face better than I do."

"Then why—" he started, and she cut him off with a hand on his elbow, steering him left instead of right, away from the kitchens, into the corridor that led to the chapel annex. Nobody used the chapel annex during a Conclave; the gods had been formally declared irrelevant until spring.

The room beyond was small and cold, lit by one guttering votive lamp someone had forgotten to extinguish. Saint-glass windows threw fractured color across the floor. Sable closed the door, put her back against it, and looked at the enemy.

Kestrel Nystrom stood in the colored light with his hands loose at his sides, and she watched him do the thing she'd catalogued: count. Exits. Distances. Her size against his. The odds.

"You won't need the window," she said. "It's a forty-foot drop onto flagstones. You'd break an ankle, and then you'd be slow."

"You're Ashen Court." His voice was steady. She gave him credit for that. "You're the envoy who spoke at the last session. Sable Vane."

"And you're the last Emberwright, sitting in a room alone with me, which tells me either you're braver than you look or you've already decided I'm not here to kill you." She let that sit. "I'm not here to kill you."

"Everyone says that right before they try."

"Everyone who says it is lying." She pushed off the door and crossed to the far side of the room, putting the votive lamp between them — not for protection, but so he'd have light on her face. Trust was built out of such small architectures. "Look at me. Really look. If the Court wanted you dead, you'd have died on the road to Greyharrow, and it wouldn't have taken an ambush. It would have taken one night, one knife, and one very patient woman. Instead you're here, unharmed, having eaten three hot meals today. Ask yourself who arranged that."

Something moved behind his eyes — the ledger-keeper recalculating. "You've been following us since the waystation."

"Since Caerlum," she said. "Before that, I argued for you in a hall you've never seen, in front of people whose names you wouldn't believe. I lost, mostly. But I'm still arguing."

"Why?"

"Because you're going to burn the world or save it," Sable said, "and I have decided — after considerable study — that I would prefer the second."

* * *

He didn't believe her. Of course he didn't. She watched him not believe her with the same careful attention he gave everything, filing her words under entries pending verification, and she realized with a strange, unwelcome warmth that she liked him for it. Everyone in the Court believed instantly, or pretended to. Belief was cheap there. Doubt was the rare currency.

"The prophecy names me," Kestrel said. "I've read it. Or the parts of it your people haven't stolen. It doesn't say 'save.' It says war. Ash. The Ember rises and the Ninefold Trade burns down to its roots. Your Court has spent a century praying for exactly that."

"A century ago, yes." She let herself lean against the wall, arms folded, and made her voice flat and honest, the voice she used for numbers. "You want the truth? Here is the truth, all of it, and you can check my figures later — I know you will. The Court was founded by survivors of the Emberwright purges. People who watched the Crown burn their neighbors and call it justice. They took the prophecy and they made it a promise: *never again will we be the ones burning.* One day the fire returns, and this time it will be ours, and this time we will be holding the torch when the Inquisitors' towers come down. That was the founding faith. Revenge, dressed up as destiny."

"And you don't share it?"

"I did. For twenty years." The votive flame shivered between them. "And then I read the original text. Not the Court's copies — the Court's copies have been edited, the same as the Crown's, everyone edits — the original, in the vault under the Hall of Cinders. Do you know what the oldest line actually says?"

He shook his head slowly.

"It says: *the Ember rises, and what it burns is chosen.*" She held his gaze. "Chosen. Not fated. Not decreed. Chosen. Every copyist for a hundred years — Crown and Court alike — smoothed that word out, because a chosen fire is terrifying. A chosen fire means responsibility. A fated fire means nobody has to decide anything. Fated fires are comfortable. That's why both sides want you, Kestrel. Not because you're dangerous. Because you're *convenient.*"

His hands had gone still at his sides — that deliberate stillness again, holding down the tremor. "The winter," he said. "The snow out of season. Your elders say it's the prophecy waking."

"My elders say whatever keeps them in power." Bitterness crept into her voice before she could stop it, and she let it stay; honesty was the whole point of tonight. "The winter is real. I won't lie to you about that — you've seen more of the records than anyone alive, you'd catch me in a lie inside a minute, and I need you to trust the things I say that sound impossible. So: the winter is real, and it is tied to the Ember line, and whether it breaks or deepens depends on what the last Emberwright does next. That part of the prophecy is true. My elders intend to make sure what you do next is *war*. They have a plan for you, Kestrel. It involves taking the choice away. There are rites in the vault under the Hall of Cinders that can force the fire to answer whether you consent or not. They've begun preparing them."

The color left his face in a way no threat could have managed. She watched him arrive at it — not the danger to himself, she'd bet anything, but the danger to everyone else, to the guildhall full of clerks, to the courier girl, to the wool-trader's missing son.

"When?" he asked. Just that. One syllable, and his voice cracked on it.

"After the Conclave fails. Which it will — the General has forged evidence ready to produce, your own guild's seal on documents your guild never signed. When the hall turns to knives, the Court moves. Days, Kestrel. Maybe hours."

* * *

Here was the part she had rehearsed forty times on the road, and it came out nothing like the rehearsal, because rehearsal was for lies and this was the truest thing she'd ever said to a stranger:

"Come with me. Not as a prisoner — as a member. Take your seat in the Court. You'd have standing no one else has ever had; the founders wrote the succession clause for exactly this, though none of them dreamed the Ember would walk in the door voluntarily. You could control the fire. Learn it, shape it, choose what it burns — or choose that it burns nothing. You could end the winter deliberately instead of letting it end in slaughter. And—" she made herself say it, made herself mean it— "you could spare them. The guild. The girl. Odell's memory, if he's dead, his life if he isn't. Because understand what happens otherwise. When the Court takes you by force, they will not leave witnesses who matter to you. The guildhall of Caerlum is a known address. The courier is a known face. The elders call them *leverage* and they will use the word without flinching."

"You're threatening them."

"No." She stepped around the votive lamp, close enough now that she could see the fine tremor he'd been hiding in his hands, close enough to see how young he was underneath all that careful composure. Twenty-three, maybe twenty-four. They were going to put the fate of the world on shoulders that had never carried anything heavier than a ledger. "I am telling you the threat exists whether I speak it or not, and I am offering you the only door out of this room that doesn't lead through their ashes. Join us. Control the fire. Spare the guild. Three sentences, one offer, no tricks. Check my figures, Kestrel. It's the only honest deal anyone is going to make you at this Conclave."

For a long moment he said nothing. The saint-glass threw red and blue across his face, and Sable understood suddenly, viscerally, why the elders feared him and why she had crossed a line no Court member crossed. It wasn't the fire sleeping in him. It was that he was *listening* — really listening, weighing her words the way he weighed everything, giving even his enemy the courtesy of his whole attention. The Court had no one who listened. The Crown had no one who listened. The world was run by people who only waited for their turn to talk.

"If I refuse?" he said finally.

"Then I go back to a hall where I am now a traitor, and I keep arguing anyway, and I buy you what time I can. Probably not enough." She smiled, and it hurt. "I told you. I've done the arithmetic. You're worth more to the world alive and free than the Court is worth intact."

"Why?" The question again, but different now — quieter, almost bewildered. "Why would you burn your whole life for someone you met eleven days ago?"

Sable looked at the votive lamp between them, at the small stubborn flame that had outlasted the priests who lit it.

"Because I was eight years old," she said, "when the Crown burned my village looking for Emberwright blood. We didn't have any. It didn't matter. The Inquisitors had quotas, and history had taught them that fire must always be answered with fire, and my mother died on a pyre built out of our own fence rails because a clerk in a distant city needed his numbers to balance." She met his eyes. "Someone has to stop balancing the books with people, Kestrel. I thought it would take a war. Then I read the original text, and I thought — maybe it takes a keeper of records instead. Someone who counts the way you count. Someone who writes down the wool-trader's missing son."

He stared at her. And she saw it land — not agreement, not yet, maybe not ever, but the recognition of a person behind the enemy, and that was more than she'd hoped for and more than she deserved.

"I need time," he said.

"Days," she said. "Maybe hours. I'll steal you what I can." She moved to the door, then stopped, hand on the latch, and gave him the last piece — the one that unsettled her to give, because it was the one that made her human too. "There's a phrase. If the worst happens and the Court comes for you, say *the ash remembers mercy*, loud, to whoever leads them. Some of us will hear it. Not enough of us. But some."

She opened the door onto the cold corridor and the noise of the failing Conclave, and behind her, quiet as a confession, she heard him say:

"Sable. Why tell me about the rites? Telling me makes them harder to use. You've weakened your own side."

She didn't turn around.

"Yes," said Sable Vane, and went to commit the rest of her treason.

Behind her, in the chapel annex, the votive lamp finally guttered out — and in the dark, unseen by anyone, the wick smoked faintly gold, as though something in the room had answered her honesty the way it answered nothing else.

By morning, three of the General's servants would report an Ashen envoy meeting the fugitive, and the Conclave's fragile peace would begin to crack along lines no ledger could mend.
