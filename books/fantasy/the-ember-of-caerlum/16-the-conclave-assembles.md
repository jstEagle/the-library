## 16. The Conclave Assembles

The city of Greyharrow had been built to be forgotten.

It sat in a fold of the Grey Mountains where no road was obliged to pass, a sprawl of slate roofs and pale stone that belonged to no crown and no guild and no god. Its charter was older than any of the three. Its one law was hospitality: within the walls, no hand raised against another, no blade drawn, no debt collected by force. For nine hundred years the Conclave had met there when the Ninefold Trade needed to argue itself whole again.

Kestrel Nystrom came through the south gate at dusk with snow in his collar and Wren's hand gripping his sleeve, and he thought: *they built it here because even liars need somewhere neutral to stand.*

He was wrong about that, he would learn. But he did not know it yet.

* * *

**Wren**

The gate guards didn't ask for papers. They asked for a truth.

"Name your grievance," said the woman at the table inside the gatehouse, a broad-shouldered soul with a clerk's ink-stained fingers and a soldier's bored eyes. "One sentence. The Conclave hears all grievances equally."

Wren looked back at Kestrel, who gave her the smallest nod — *you first, you're better at this* — and she turned to the guard.

"The Crown burned my friends' guildhouse and called it accounting."

A murmur ran down the queue behind them. The guard wrote it on a slate without expression. "Next."

Kestrel stepped up. He hesitated, and Wren watched him weigh it — she could see the weighing happen, the ledger-keeper's habit of measuring every word before it left him. Then he said, quietly enough that only the guard heard:

"I am the last Emberwright, and I've come to prove a forgery."

The guard's pen stopped.

For three full seconds the gatehouse went silent except for the wind off the mountains, which had teeth in it now, real winter or prophecy-winter, nobody could tell anymore. Then the guard wrote something — Wren craned and couldn't read it — and stamped their tokens, and waved them through, and did not look at either of them again.

"You absolute fool," Wren breathed as they passed under the arch.

"It's the one place I can say it out loud." Kestrel's voice was steady but his knuckles weren't. "Neutral ground. It's the whole point of the city."

"Neutral ground is just a bigger room full of people who want things from you."

He glanced at her, and there was something tired and grateful in it. "When did you get cynical?"

"When I started carrying things worth killing for." She shouldered her pack. "Come on. We find the Caerlum delegation before someone else finds us."

* * *

**Sable Vane**

Sable Vane arrived two days early, alone, wearing grey, because the Ashen Court believed that power announced itself best by refusing to announce itself at all.

The Court's delegation proper would come at the appointed hour: twelve robed figures, the Choir of Cinders, with their censers and their cold incense and their terrible patience. Sable had argued against the procession. She had been overruled, as she was increasingly overruled these days, and so she had come ahead to do what she actually did best, which was count.

Greyharrow counted easily. She walked its streets with her hood down and her hands in her sleeves and tallied: the Crown's banners already flying over the eastern quarter, too many of them, an honor guard dressed as a diplomatic mission. The guild delegations trickling in through the gates, poorer than any Conclave in living memory — half the Ninefold Trade's houses were dark this winter, their roads closed, their ledgers bleeding. And everywhere, in every face, the same arithmetic of fear: *the snow is wrong, the year is wrong, something old has woken, and we have come to a mountain city to decide whose fault it is.*

She found the hall where the sessions would be held and stood in it a long while. Round chamber, no throne, no dais. Nine hundred years of delegates had refused to build themselves a stage here, and the architecture still held them to it. Everyone would speak from level ground or not at all.

*Good,* she thought. *Because what I have to say will only work if no one can look down on me.*

Her superiors wanted the prophecy fulfilled violently — a burning, a scapegoat, an heir put to the torch so the world could watch the winter end and call it justice. Sable had read the old texts more carefully than they had. Fire spent in anger bought a season. Fire offered freely might buy a century. The difference between those two outcomes was everything, and no one on her side of the argument seemed willing to do the reading.

So she had come early. To count. To find the boy before the Choir did. To make him an offer with no blades in it, if she could get him alone.

She touched the wall of the great hall, feeling the cold stone, and murmured to no one: "Let me be the reasonable one. Just once. Let me be the reasonable one, and maybe everyone lives."

* * *

**Inquisitor-General Corvane**

From the window of the Crown's quarters, Aldous Corvane watched the guild delegations arrive and felt the particular satisfaction of a man watching debts come due.

They were calling it the Winter Conclave in the taverns, with the hushed relish people reserved for disasters they hoped to witness. Let them. Corvane had attended four Conclaves in his career and understood what none of the starry-eyed clerks ever grasped: the neutrality of Greyharrow was a fiction maintained by mutual exhaustion. When the parties were evenly matched, the hall held. When they were not, the hall was simply a room with excellent acoustics for screaming.

This time they were not evenly matched. This time the Crown had prepared.

His agents had burned three guildhouses in as many months. The records seized in those raids — genuine records, damning ones, carefully curated — were already copied and bound and waiting on his table downstairs, ready to be entered into evidence the moment the session opened. The narrative was set: the Emberwright heresy had survived underground, hidden in the guilds like rot in a beam, and the unnatural winter was proof of its awakening. The Crown had not persecuted innocent traders; the Crown had performed surgery.

All that remained was the boy.

Corvane had lost him twice — once at the waystation, where the reports spoke of fire that no report could adequately describe, and once on the road, where his men had returned ash-stained and empty-handed and lying badly about why. The boy was dangerous. That was inconvenient. But danger, properly framed, was also useful. A frightened Conclave would grant the Crown almost anything to be protected from an Emberwright walking free.

There was only one complication, and her name was Sable Vane.

His watchers reported the Court's envoy moving through the city alone, unrobed, asking questions in low places. Off-script. The Choir would rein her in or they wouldn't, and either way she was a variable, and Corvane had built forty years of certainty on the principle that variables must be removed before they remove you.

He turned from the window. On his desk lay the forged codex page — his masterpiece, aged in smoke and handled into softness, the record that had condemned the Emberwrights a century ago and would condemn their last heir tonight.

"One more day," he told it. "Then history stays as I wrote it."

* * *

**Maren**

Maren came to Greyharrow the way she came anywhere: with forty smugglers dressed as teamsters, six wagons of contraband grain disguised as ballast, and a smile that made customs officials feel flattered rather than searched.

Officially, she had no invitation. Unofficially, half the delegations had paid her to be here — for road intelligence, for quiet rooms, for the kind of insurance that never appeared in any ledger. The Conclave needed the lawless this winter more than it cared to admit. Every closed road meant every open secret got carried by someone like her.

She'd promised Kestrel an army once, back at her hearth, when he'd been half-dead and glowing like a banked coal. He'd said no. She respected that. She also hadn't dismissed it, because Maren had lived long enough to know that *no* was often just *not yet* wearing a coat.

Now she stood in a tavern near the west gate, counting arrivals through the frost-furred glass, and saw the thing she'd been dreading: the Choir of Cinders coming up the mountain road in their grey-and-white, twelve abreast, censers swinging, and the crowds along the street going quiet the way animals go quiet.

And threading through that crowd, hood up, moving fast, two figures she knew. The courier girl and the ledger-boy, cutting across the square toward the guild quarter with their heads down.

Between them and safety stood the Choir, and between the Choir and the boy stood, apparently, nothing at all.

"Well," Maren said, draining her cup. "Damn it."

* * *

**Kestrel**

The Choir reached the square before Kestrel and Wren did.

He saw them from across the frozen fountain — twelve figures in ash-grey, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who had never once been told no — and he stopped walking, because stopping was the only sensible response to seeing your death arrive on schedule.

"They're not looking for us," Wren said, low. "Look. They're walking to the hall. Ceremony. They'll register at the gatehouse like everyone else."

"They registered me at the gatehouse." His mouth was dry. "I told the guard what I am. Out loud. In a city where the Court has eyes."

"Then we assume they know, and we stop pretending otherwise." She took his arm — not gently, Wren was never gentle when it mattered — and steered him forward. "You came here to read a forgery aloud to the most powerful people in the Ninefold Trade. One more pair of grey robes isn't going to change the math."

But the math changed anyway, because as the Choir passed the fountain, the twelfth figure broke formation.

She was younger than the others, unhooded, with dark hair going white at one temple as though frost had decided to keep her. She crossed the frozen square directly toward them, ignoring the stares, and stopped an arm's length away, and Kestrel saw Wren's hand slide toward her knife and saw the woman notice and deliberately not care.

"Kestrel Nystrom," she said. Not a question.

"You have the advantage."

"Sable Vane. Envoy of the Ashen Court." She glanced at Wren. "Your knife won't matter, but I admire the instinct." Back to him. "I have perhaps fifty words before my colleagues miss me, so listen. Tomorrow the Inquisitor-General enters a forged record into evidence. I know it's forged. You know it's forged. What I know that you don't is *how* he'll use it — not to condemn you, but to provoke you. He wants you to burn. Publicly. So the winter can be blamed on you and ended on your ashes."

"And the Court?" Kestrel managed. "Doesn't your side want exactly that?"

"The Court wants it done violently. I want it done rightly. There's a difference, and tomorrow it may be the only difference that matters." Her eyes held his, and there was no cruelty in them, which was somehow worse. "Find me before the session. Or don't, and spend the rest of your very short life wondering."

She turned and walked back to the Choir, who had not stopped, who had not looked, who folded her in among them like a drop of ash returning to the fire.

Wren exhaled slowly. "Tell me you don't trust her."

"I trust that she believes herself." Kestrel watched the grey robes recede up the steps of the great hall. "That's either the most dangerous thing in this city or the safest."

"Can't it be both?"

"It's always both." He turned away. "Come on. The Caerlum delegation has a room in the guild quarter, and tomorrow morning I'm going to stand on level ground and tell nine hundred years of neutrality exactly how rotten the record is."

* * *

**Odell's Seal**

The Caerlum delegation's lodgings were two rooms above a chandler's shop, and they were full.

Not with people. With grief, in the form of a small wooden box that had traveled from Caerlum by trusted hands, arriving the same evening they did: Master Odell's effects, released by the Crown after interrogation, sent on with a note that said only *he gave them nothing.*

Kestrel sat on the narrow bed with the box open on his knees. Inside: a spare robe, a pipe, a worn copy of the guild's founding charter annotated in Odell's cramped hand. And the master's seal — brass, heavy, older than the guildhall it had stamped for forty years.

Wren stood in the doorway and let him be silent, which was its own kind of kindness.

"He knew," Kestrel finally said. "At the end. He knew they'd take him, and he went to the archive first and moved the codex pages I'd need, and then he let himself be caught so the search would stop with him." His thumb moved over the seal's worn face. "He spent his life keeping me small. And the last thing he did was make me big enough to finish this."

"That's not contradiction," Wren said. "That's just love doing its job badly and well at the same time."

Kestrel pressed the seal into his palm until the edges bit. Tomorrow he would walk into the great hall of Greyharrow carrying a dead man's authority, a forged century, and a fire he did not fully control, and every faction in the world would be waiting to see which of the three he used first.

Outside, the snow kept falling — steady, silent, wrong — and somewhere below, Maren's teamsters were quietly buying up every candle in the chandler's shop, because Maren had learned long ago that when the powerful gather in a mountain city, the smart money is on darkness.

Tomorrow, the Conclave would assemble.

Tonight, everyone sharpened their version of history.
