## 17. Reading Aloud

They gave me a lectern because I asked for one.

That was the whole trick of it, in the end. Not fire, not blood, not the name I'd spent twenty years not knowing. A lectern. A place to set a book. When Wren heard my plan she laughed once — a short, cold sound with no humor in it — and said, "You're going to defeat the Inquisitor-General of the Ninefold Crown with *bookkeeping*."

"Yes," I said.

"Good," she said. "It's what we're for."

* * *

The Conclave of Winter met in the great hall of Greyharrow, a city that had belonged to no crown and no court for six hundred years precisely because it had nothing anyone wanted. It sat on a plateau of grey stone above a frozen river, ringed by walls too old to be impressive and too thick to be breached, and its great hall had been built by guilds who understood that arguments are settled faster when everyone can see everyone's hands.

The hall was a horseshoe of tiered benches around a floor of pale flagstone. Delegates of the Ninefold Trade filled the eastern curve: guildmasters in their chains of office, ledger-keepers like me in plain wool, factor-merchants whose furs cost more than my guildhall. The Crown's delegation held the western seats under banners of white and gold, Inquisitors in ash-grey robes seated among them like knives laid on silk. And in the northern gallery, behind a screen of black iron that they had demanded and been grudgingly granted, sat the Ashen Court's observers — Sable Vane among them, visible only as a silhouette and the occasional gleam of her eyes when the torchlight caught her at the right angle.

Above us all, through the high clerestory windows, the snow came down. It had been coming down since the Long Winter began, out of season, without mercy, and it did not stop for the Conclave. It fell past the windows in long grey curtains, and every delegate glanced up at it at least once, and no one said aloud what all of us were thinking: that the prophecy's first sign had arrived on its own, unbidden, and that whatever happened in this hall would either answer it or be drowned by it.

I stood at the center of the floor with a lectern, a book, and a folder of loose pages tied with guild cord, and I waited for the Marshal of Greyharrow to call the session to order.

My hands were steady. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here: they were steady. Odell used to say that fear is just interest accruing on a debt you haven't paid yet, and the only cure is to pay early. I had spent three days paying. Every page of evidence copied twice, one set sewn into Wren's coat lining, one set in my hands. Every citation checked against the guild's oldest codex. Every argument rehearsed aloud in a freezing room while Wren played the Inquisitor-General and tried, with genuine relish, to destroy me.

She hadn't managed it. I clung to that.

"Delegates," the Marshal called. She was an old woman with a voice like a door hinge, and she did not raise it so much as let it fall on the hall like a weight. "The Conclave of Winter is assembled. The matter before us is the winter itself, and the prophecy said to foretell it, and the question of what is to be done about both." Her eyes found mine across the floor. "The Guild of Caerlum has petitioned the floor. Guildmaster Nystrom."

Not *junior ledger-keeper*. Not *Kestrel*. Guildmaster. Odell's seal hung at my throat on its cord, warm against my sternum, and every time I felt it I thought of him dying under interrogation with my name locked behind his teeth, and I stopped being afraid and started being angry, which is a far more useful fuel.

I stepped to the lectern.

"Delegates," I said. My voice carried better than I expected; the hall was built for it. "I am Kestrel Nystrom, of the Guild of Caerlum, traders in impossible things. I come before you not to argue about the prophecy. I come before you to prove that the history you have all been taught is a forgery — and I will prove it the way guilds have proven things for nine hundred years. With arithmetic."

A murmur ran the benches. On the western curve, the Inquisitor-General's delegation went very still. He himself sat at the center of their row, a man named Aldous Verrick, silver-haired, handsome in the way that well-maintained things are handsome, his face arranged in an expression of patient, faintly amused tolerance — the look of a man waiting for a child to finish reciting.

He had burned my guildhouse's sister at Harrowgate. He had interrogated Odell Thackeray to death. He had spent a century's worth of his office's labor making sure that one family's name meant only one thing: *the Emberwrights burned the world, and the Crown put out the fire.*

I opened the book.

"This," I said, "is the Codex of Caerlum, oldest continuous ledger in the Ninefold Trade. Nine hundred years of entries in nine hundred years of hands. Guilds keep records the way rivers keep water — everything flows through us, and everything leaves a mark. So before I show you what the Crown's histories say, I want to establish what a real record looks like."

I turned pages as I spoke, holding each open toward the delegates' side of the hall, letting the scribes see.

"A true ledger has a hand that changes. Ink that changes. Paper that changes. Mistakes that are crossed out rather than erased, because erasure is theft from whoever comes after you. Look—" I turned the codex toward them again. "Here, an entry from four hundred years ago where the keeper miscounted a shipment of glass and corrected himself in the margin, and his correction is in different ink because he came back to it the next day. Here, a water stain. Here, a page torn and noted as torn, with the reason given. This is what honesty looks like on paper. It is messy. It contradicts itself. It *ages*."

Verrick's smile did not move. "Is there a point approaching, Master Nystrom?" he asked, mild as milk. "We have all read ledgers."

"You have read one ledger," I said. "The Crown's. Let's compare it to the others."

I lifted the second book from the lectern — a copy, made under Crown authority two hundred years ago, of the official history of the Emberwright Purge. *The Record of Ash*, it was called. Every delegate knew it. Children learned it.

"The Record of Ash tells us this story," I said. "One hundred and twelve years ago, the Emberwright line — last of the fire-mages — turned their power on the world. They burned the granaries of Vael. They fired the forests of the Northmarch. They were destroyed to a man, woman, and child by the Crown's Inquisition, and their name was struck from every honest record, and the world was saved. Yes?"

Murmurs of assent. Somewhere in the eastern benches, someone said, "Everyone knows this."

"Everyone knows this," I agreed. "Now. I am a ledger-keeper. When I audit a account, I do not ask whether the story is familiar. I ask whether the numbers reconcile. So let us reconcile."

I untied the folder of loose pages and began laying them on the lectern, one by one, naming each as I set it down.

"Exhibit one. The granary records of the city of Vael, year of the Purge. These are the Crown's own customs tallies — grain in, grain out, losses recorded. If the Emberwrights burned Vael's granaries, we should find the loss here." I set the page flat. "We find instead a full harvest, sold at ordinary prices, with a note in the margin — in the Crown's own hand — that the season was *unremarkable*. No fire. No loss. The burning of Vael appears in the histories but not in the arithmetic of Vael."

"Forged documents," Verrick said pleasantly. "Produced by a guild with every reason—"

"Exhibit two," I said, over him, louder. "The timber tallies of the Northmarch. Same year. The histories say the Emberwrights fired the forests. The tallies say the Northmarch shipped more timber that year than any year in the preceding decade — because a fire, gentlemen, *creates salvage*, and salvage creates records, and there are none. No salvage sales. No burn-clearance contracts. Nothing. The forests of the Northmarch were never burned, because if they had been, someone would have been paid to clean them up, and payment leaves a trail wider than any fire."

I could feel the hall changing temperature. Not the way it had changed when my fire answered in the waystation — nothing supernatural in it. Just the particular chill of several hundred powerful people realizing, slowly and unwillingly, that they might be about to learn something they would then be responsible for knowing.

"Exhibit three," I said. "And this is the one that matters. The Record of Ash states that the Emberwrights were exterminated. Root and branch. To the last child. The Crown has repeated this for a century, and on the strength of it, the Crown has claimed the right to burn, arrest, and disappear anyone whose blood shows the old inheritance — as it did at Harrowgate eleven days ago, when your Inquisitors burned a guildhouse and killed fourteen people over a scrap of parchment."

Wren stood in the eastern benches, and I did not look at her, because looking at her would have broken something open in my voice.

"But guilds do not only keep our own accounts," I said. "We keep each other's. For nine hundred years, the Ninefold Trade has maintained cross-guild sureties — marriage registers, apprenticeship bonds, blood-surety rolls kept so that debts survive the debtor. And the blood-surety rolls tell a different story than the Record of Ash." I held up a page. "Here is the roll of the Guild of Caerlum, year of the Purge. An entry recording the bond of a woman named Lysa Emberwright — married under guild law, name entered honestly, because guilds record what *is*, not what the Crown prefers — to a guildsman of Caerlum. And here—" another page, "—and here, and here — the same entry in the rolls of the Guilds of Vael, of Dunmark, of Saltmere. Four guilds. Four marriages. Children registered. Grandchildren registered. The Emberwrights were not exterminated, delegates. They were *absorbed*. Married into guild families across the Ninefold Trade, quietly, within months of the supposed purge — which means the purge never happened as described, which means the Crown has spent a hundred years hunting and burning the descendants of a crime that its own founding document invented."

The hall broke.

Not all at once — first a sound like wind through rigging, delegates turning to each other, voices rising on the eastern benches. A guildmaster of Dunmark was on his feet demanding to see the roll. The Vael delegation erupted into argument with itself. And on the western curve, the Inquisitors rose in a body, ash-grey robes moving like smoke, and Verrick finally stopped smiling.

"This is sedition," he said, and his voice was not mild anymore. It filled the hall the way cold fills a room. "This is a guild clerk waving forgeries at a Conclave while the world freezes, and I will tell you why he does it. Because he is one of *them*. Because the Emberwright blood runs in him, and the fire with it, and everything he has shown you is a condemned man's plea dressed up as scholarship."

"There it is," I said. "Thank you, Inquisitor-General. You've just confirmed exhibit four."

I held up the last page, and my hand was still steady, and I was grateful to Odell for that, wherever he was.

"The guild's own registry of my apprenticeship, witnessed and sealed twenty years ago, listing my mother's name — a name the Crown's own blood-tithes should have flagged, and didn't, because the man responsible for flagging it was the same man who forged the Record of Ash." I turned to face Verrick directly. "You knew the line survived. You've always known. Because you didn't fail to exterminate the Emberwrights, Inquisitor-General. You *invented* them. Your office needed an enemy worth a century of power, so you wrote one, and you've been collecting rent on the fear ever since. And when a real winter came — a winter no prophecy needed — you reached for the same old story, because it's the only tool your office has ever had."

"Seize him," Verrick said.

Four Inquisitors moved. And the Marshal of Greyharrow's voice came down like a portcullis:

"*No.*"

She rose from her chair, ancient and small and absolutely immovable. "Greyharrow is neutral ground, General, by treaty older than your office. Blades drawn in this hall void the treaty, and every guild here will remember which delegation drew first. Sit. Down."

They did not sit. But they stopped.

And the hall split — visibly, physically split — as the eastern benches closed ranks around the idea of the evidence, scribes already copying, guildmasters demanding the rolls be examined under seal, while the western delegation drew inward around its General like a fist closing. Between them, the undecided delegates looked from one side to the other, and I watched men and women make, in real time, the choice between a comfortable lie and an expensive truth.

Sable Vane's silhouette shifted behind the black iron screen. She leaned forward, and though I couldn't see her face, I felt her attention land on me like a hand on the back of my neck. *This,* her posture said, *this is exactly what I told them would happen.* The Court wanted the prophecy fulfilled. A Conclave tearing itself apart over the Emberwright legacy was, for them, a kind of progress.

"Master Nystrom." The Marshal again. "Your evidence will be examined under seal by a commission of this Conclave. That is procedure."

"Procedure takes weeks," Verrick said softly. "The winter is here now. And while you deliberate, the boy stands there carrying fire in his blood and a dead traitor's seal around his neck." His eyes found mine, and for the first time all day there was nothing performed in his face at all — just cold, patient certainty. "Give me the books, boy. Give me the seal. And perhaps the Crown remembers that Caerlum was always the smallest of the nine."

I looked at him across thirty feet of pale flagstone, and I understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that no commission would ever convene. That he had not come to Greyharrow to debate. That the Inquisitors' stillness was not restraint but readiness, and that somewhere in his robes or his retinue was the thing he had actually brought to the Conclave — the thing he intended to use the moment the hall's attention fractured completely.

The snow pressed against the clerestory windows. The torches guttered, all together, as if something had exhaled through the hall.

And Verrick smiled at me — genuinely, this time, with his whole face — and began to speak words I recognized. Words from a book a century old, with my name written beside an omen of war.

He wasn't going to seize the prophecy.

He was going to *read* it.
