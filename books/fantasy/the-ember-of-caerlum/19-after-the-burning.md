## 19. After the Burning

The winter broke the way a fever breaks — not all at once, but in shivers.

Wren felt it first in her feet. She had been standing in the snow outside Greyharrow's east gate for what felt like half her life, and suddenly the packed ice under her boots went soft. Then it went to slush. Then it went to water running in brown threads down the hill toward the city, and she stood there watching it like a fool while around her grown men and women — guild factors and Crown clerks and ash-marked Court loyalists who an hour ago had been trying to kill each other — did exactly the same thing. A whole hillside of people watching water run downhill as if it were a miracle.

Maybe it was. It had been falling out of season since before the roads closed. It had been falling when Odell died in an Inquisitor cell with Kestrel's name locked behind his teeth. It had been falling the whole way to Greyharrow, through the ambush on the causeway, through the waystation where Wren had dragged Kestrel out by the collar while his hands still glowed like banked coals. Snow that fell for months without stopping was not weather anymore. It was a sentence being pronounced over the world, one flake at a time.

And now it had stopped. Because Kestrel had burned the book.

She didn't know all of it yet. Nobody knew all of it. What the hall full of delegates had seen, from the gallery where Wren had wedged herself between a Greyharrow steward and a very frightened wool-trader, was this: the Inquisitor-General Veyra Corvane had drawn steel and spoken words that were older than any guild charter, words meant to force the prophecy into motion whether or not anyone consented to it. The hall had gone white-hot at the edges. The banners had smoked. And then Kestrel Nystrom — junior ledger-keeper, boy who apologized to furniture, boy who once spent an entire evening reconciling a two-copper discrepancy because *the record deserved better* — had stood up in the middle of a burning room and burned something else instead.

Not the hall. Not the people. Not even Corvane.

A book. One hundred and six years old, written in a dead woman's hand, carrying his own name beside an omen of war. He had held it up in both hands and set it alight, and the fire that came off it hadn't been red or gold but the pale grey-white of embers after the flames go down, and it had climbed the pages and eaten every word and left nothing — no ash, no scrap, no prophecy left in the world for anyone to weaponize. Then he'd folded up like a puppet with its strings cut, and Wren had screamed his name into a hall full of armed men, and that was the last thing she clearly remembered until now.

Now: water running downhill. Her hands shaking so badly she had to press them against her thighs to make them stop. And somewhere below, in the smoking shell of the Conclave hall, either the man she loved was dead or he wasn't, and nobody would let her through to find out.

* * *

The guilds counted their dead the way guilds do everything: methodically, in columns.

Wren ended up helping, because her hands needed work and her head needed silence, and counting bodies gave her both. She went door to door through the delegates' quarter with a slate borrowed from the Greyharrow stewards, marking names. The Guild of Salt and Glass had lost four. The River Compact, seven. The Crown's delegation had barricaded itself in the customs house and refused to give numbers, which told Wren everything about how the numbers were going.

Corvane was alive. That was the news that ran through the corridors like grease fire — alive, disarmed, taken into custody by three guildmasters and a Greyharrow magistrate who had, between them, roughly one working sword. She heard it secondhand from a steward, thirdhand from a courier, and each telling made the Inquisitor-General smaller and stranger: the woman who had forged a century of history, who had built an extermination out of ink, sitting in a storeroom under guard while clerks argued about whose jurisdiction she fell under. There wasn't a law for what she'd done. There wasn't a law for most of what had happened here. The world had run off the edge of its own ledger, and everyone was standing at the bottom looking up, waiting for someone to invent new arithmetic.

The Ashen Court didn't wait. By the second morning they were gone — not routed, not arrested, simply *dissolved*, the way ice goes out of a river. Sable Vane had walked out of the hall under her own power the night of the burning, and half the Court's delegates had followed her, and the other half had scattered into the city like sparks off a struck flint. Wren saw two of them at the gate the next day, ash-marks scrubbed from their wrists, arguing quietly about whether to go home or keep walking. One of them caught Wren looking and said, without heat, "It was never supposed to be this." As if that excused anything. As if it didn't, somehow, make it worse.

The winter broke unevenly, the way Maren had said it would — the smuggler queen had opinions about everything, delivered from the doorway of whatever safehouse she'd installed them in, arms crossed, watching the thaw come down the mountains "like a tide with a grudge." South of Greyharrow the roads opened within days. North, in the high passes, the snow stayed deep and mean, and the first rumor came back that villages up there still hadn't seen green. The Ninefold Trade would take a season to knit itself back together, maybe two. Caravans wouldn't move until someone decided it was safe to be the first caravan, and no one ever wanted to be the first caravan.

But water was running downhill. Wren kept coming back to that. Whatever else was broken, the oldest machinery in the world had started turning again, and it hadn't asked anyone's permission, and it hadn't needed a prophecy to tell it how.

* * *

They wouldn't let her see him for almost two days.

The Greyharrow healers had their reasons — burn-sickness, they called it, though the burns weren't on his skin. Whatever he'd done in that hall had gone through him like a draught through an open window, and what was left lying on the cot in the infirmary annex was a man emptied out. Wren stood in the corridor outside with Maren's lieutenant, a broad woman named Ashka who had carried Wren bodily away from the hall doors on the night itself, and listened to a healer explain things in the gentle voice people use for bad news.

"His fire is spent," the healer said. "I don't know another word for it. There are stories about the Emberwrights, and in the stories they burn cities. No story tells you what it costs."

"Is he dying?" Wren asked. Her voice came out flat and practical, courier's voice, ask-the-question voice. Inside she was anything but.

"No." The healer hesitated. "He's cold. That's the strange part. A man who burned a book with fire hot enough to crack stone, and now he can't get warm. We've tried everything short of putting him in the oven."

"I'll sit with him," Wren said. "I'm warm enough for both of us. I always have been."

The healer looked at her a long moment, then stepped aside.

He looked smaller than she remembered, and she remembered him small — that was the whole point of him, the careful invisible life, the boy who'd made himself a footnote in his own story so no one would ever look twice. Now he lay under three blankets with his hands above the coverlet, palms up, and even from the doorway she could see it: a faint warmth in them, a glow like the last coal in a grate at the end of a long night. Not fire. Ember. Something that had been enormous and was now very nearly nothing, and was choosing, apparently, to stay lit anyway.

His eyes opened when the door clicked shut.

"You're supposed to be resting," Wren said, because it was that or cry, and she had decided somewhere on the causeway outside Greyharrow that she was not going to cry in front of him again if she could possibly help it.

"So are you." His voice was sandpaper. He tried to smile and got about halfway. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy." She crossed the room and sat on the edge of the cot, careful of the blankets, careful of him, careful the way you're careful with something you thought you'd already lost once. "The other guy is currently a jurisdictional dispute in a storeroom, so. Easy win."

That got a real breath of laughter out of him, thin but real, and she felt it land in her chest like a coin dropped into a dry well.

"They told me you'd be cold," she said.

"I am cold." He flexed his fingers, slowly, like a man relearning the alphabet. "It's the strangest thing, Wren. I keep reaching for it — the fire — and there's just... nothing. Like a room after the furniture's been sold. I can feel the shape of where it used to be." He turned his hands over, studied the faint glow in the palms, dimmer than it had been even yesterday, she thought. Dimmer every time she looked. "Odell used to say the Emberwrights didn't have magic. They had a debt. I think I finally paid mine."

"You don't owe anyone anything," Wren said, sharper than she meant. "You paid enough. You paid more than—" She stopped. Breathed. Started over, quieter. "You burned the book. You could have taken Corvane apart. Everyone in that hall would have cheered. Half of them wanted you to. And you burned a *book* instead."

"It was the only thing in the room that was actually killing anybody." He said it simply, the way he said true things, which was always too plainly, as if plainness might hide how much they cost. "As long as it existed, someone was going to use it. Corvane. The Court. Some guildmaster in thirty years with a grievance and good handwriting. Prophecies don't die of neglect, Wren. They die of arson." A pause. "I'm a ledger-keeper. I know how to destroy a record properly."

"You're not a ledger-keeper anymore. You're the man who broke the winter."

"I'm the man who's very cold and would like his partner in trade to stop talking about him like he's inventory." But his eyes were wet when he said it, and so were hers, and neither of them pretended otherwise, and that was its own kind of settlement — debts acknowledged, balances shown, no forgery anywhere in the account.

She took his hands. They were cool, not cold, and the ember-glow pressed faintly against her palms like a cat leaning into a scratch. Outside the narrow window, Greyharrow's eaves were dripping in a hundred small rhythms, the whole city ticking like a clock winding back up.

"The guild survived," she said. "Caerlum. Word came this morning. The sisterhouses took losses — Marrowgate is gone, burned by Inquisitors before the raid order was even countermanded — but the motherhouse held. Odell's seal is intact. The ledgers are intact." She watched his face do several things at once. "There's no master to hold the seal, Kestrel. There hasn't been since the night he died."

"Don't," he said softly.

"Somebody has to sign the spring audit."

"*Don't.*"

"Fine," she said. "Then I'll do it. I'll be the worst guildmaster in the history of impossible things. First entry: one (1) guildhall, haunted. One (1) courier, overworked, demanding a partnership stake. One (1) prophecy, destroyed, no replacement scheduled—"

"Wren." He squeezed her hands, and even spent, even hollowed out, there was a warmth in the grip that hadn't been there a week ago, and wouldn't have been there a year ago, and belonged to no bloodline and no book. "Ask me in spring. When I can feel my toes. Ask me properly, with witnesses, so I can't weasel out of it the way I weaseled out of everything else."

"You've never weaseled out of anything in your life. It's your single most annoying quality."

"Ask me anyway."

She leaned down and put her forehead against his, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the drips counted the seconds, and the ember in his palms breathed against her skin like something sleeping — not dead, not awake, just *kept*, the way a guild keeps a thing nobody else knows how to hold.

When she straightened, his eyes had drifted half-shut, exhaustion pulling him under like a tide. But at the door she paused, because he'd murmured something, barely voiced.

She went back. "What?"

His eyes opened a slit. "The ledger," he whispered. "The prophetic one. The original. Check the guild vault when we get home." A breath. "If I burned the only copy... then why can I still feel it?"

The drip of the eaves filled the silence, steady as a pulse.

Outside, far north in the high passes where the thaw hadn't reached, the snow was still falling.
