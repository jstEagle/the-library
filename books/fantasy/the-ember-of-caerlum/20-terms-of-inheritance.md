## 20. Terms of Inheritance

The offer came on the ninth day after Greyharrow, delivered by a woman who had crossed half the Ninefold Trade to make it and clearly resented every mile.

She arrived at the guildhall of Caerlum in a coach too fine for our street, with outriders in Crown livery that had been quietly changed to something more neutral once they saw what was left of the roads. Her name was Dame Ilsette Varrow, Envoy Extraordinary, and she looked around my counting room — the water-stained ceiling, the ledgers stacked three deep, Wren's damp cloak dripping by the door — with the expression of someone who had been promised a palace and shown a pantry.

"Master Nystrom," she said. Not Kestrel. Not *Emberwright*. She'd been coached, and she stuck to her coaching like mortar. "The Crown wishes to express its gratitude for your service at the Conclave."

"My service," I said, "consisted of embarrassing the Inquisitor-General in front of four hundred delegates."

"Which is why he is currently enjoying retirement in a fortress with very thick walls." She smiled without warmth. "The Crown finds that arrangement preferable to a trial. Trials raise questions. Questions raise *other* questions." She let that sit, then produced a document from a lacquered case, unrolled it across my counting table, and weighted its corners with two inkwells and a candlestick, because even envoys extraordinary must work with what a poor guildhouse can offer.

I read it. I read it twice, because the first time I assumed I'd misread.

"The Crown," Dame Ilsette said, when I looked up, "restores to you the name Emberwright. All lands, titles, and holdings attainted a century past are returned — the estate at Vaelmoor, the foundry rights, the tolls on the Ashwater crossing. The archives will be amended. The histories rewritten." A pause, delicately handled. "*Corrected.*"

"You mean forged again," Wren said from the doorway. "Just in the other direction this time."

Dame Ilsette did not look at her. That took discipline; most people looked at Wren. "The Crown means to set right an old wrong."

"The Crown means to own me," I said. "That's all. It's a purchase. You've simply priced it in ancestors instead of coin."

Something flickered behind her composure — not anger. Recognition, maybe. She'd have made the same observation herself, in a quieter room. "Master Nystrom. May I speak plainly?"

"I'd be disappointed otherwise. Plain speaking is the only thing we trade in here."

"A century ago, your family's name was struck from every record in the realm. Do you know how difficult that is? To erase a name? It requires thousands of decisions, thousands of small corruptions, sustained across generations. The Inquisitor-General did not invent that machine. He merely operated it." She tapped the parchment once, lightly. "What you exposed at Greyharrow was one operator. The machine remains. And machines like that do not tolerate loose ends. They *absorb* them. That is what this offer is. Absorption. The Crown would rather make you a lord than make you a martyr, but understand — both are ways of filing you away."

I stared at her. "You're arguing against your own embassy."

"I am arguing *for* you, which happens to be against my instructions." She gathered her composure again, smooth as a ledger line. "I'll leave the document. Refusal carries no penalty. The Crown has learned, recently and expensively, what penalties cost." At the door she stopped, hand on the frame, and spoke without turning. "My grandfather kept accounts for a wool merchant in Duncarrie. Forty years. He used to say a man's real inheritance isn't what's left to him. It's what he refuses to spend." Then she was gone, down our crooked street, back toward the world of thick walls and corrected histories.

Wren waited until the coach wheels faded before she said anything. "Vaelmoor," she said. "That's a whole valley, isn't it? Foundries. River tolls. You could rebuild the guild ten times over."

"I know."

"And you're going to say no."

I looked at the parchment lying on my counting table, all that restored glory rendered in careful clerk's hand, and felt nothing in my palms. No warmth. No pull. Just paper.

"I'm going to say no," I agreed. "But not today. Today I want to do the audit."

* * *

We held the audit in the guildhall's great room, which had never in living memory held a full assembly of the Guild of Caerlum, because there had never been enough members left to fill it. There were now. Twenty-three survivors of the winter, plus eleven who had walked in off the roads in the weeks since Greyharrow — traders whose guildhouses had burned or folded or simply stopped answering their doors, carrying whatever records they'd saved and asking, hesitantly, if Caerlum was taking members.

It was. That was the first entry.

Odell's chair sat empty at the head of the table. We hadn't filled it. Nobody had suggested filling it. But everyone's eyes went to it anyway, the way eyes go to a missing tooth, and I understood, sitting there with my tally-book closed in front of me, that the audit could not proceed until somebody said the thing out loud.

So I stood up.

"The Crown offered me Vaelmoor yesterday," I said. "Name, lands, foundries, tolls. Everything my family lost. Everything restored, with apologies and amended archives." I let that land. Around the table, faces I'd known my whole life — Marda with her ink-stained fingers, old Tamms who'd kept the cellar keys through three winters of scarcity, young Pell who'd joined the year before and still flinched at raised voices — watched me the way people watch a scale settle. "I'm going to refuse it."

Marda exhaled. Tamms nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something he'd already entered in his own private ledger. Pell just looked confused, so I gave him the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the one language every person in that room shared.

"Here's why. A name is a debt instrument. That's all it's ever been — a promise that whoever carries it will honor what came before. The Emberwright name carries a debt of fire. Whole cities' worth. If I take it back, I take the debt back with it, and every creditor of the old bloodline comes collecting — the Crown wanting a weapon, the Court wanting a fulfillment, every survivor of the burning years wanting justice or revenge, depending on the hour. I'd spend the rest of my life paying other people's interest." I turned the tally-book over in my hands. "But *Nystrom* — that name carries a different debt. Small ones. Honest ones. Debts I can actually pay. My mother's name. Odell's teaching. This hall's roof, which leaks, and which we will fix come spring because we are guildsmen and that is what we do."

"And the money?" Pell asked. Someone elbowed him. I waved it off.

"It's a fair question. Here's the answer: the guild nearly died because it traded in impossible things and kept honest books about none of them. We are not going to be rebuilt rich. We are going to be rebuilt *true*, and true takes longer. If we need capital, we'll borrow it at fair rates and record every copper. If we need favor, we'll earn it. If the Crown wants to help, it can pay its tariffs like everyone else." I sat down. "Now. First item on the agenda. Does anyone object to Kestrel Nystrom assuming the mastership of this guild?"

Silence — the good kind, the kind with weight behind it. Then Tamms said, "About time," and Marda said, "Someone has to fix the roof," and Wren, beside me, said nothing at all, but under the table her hand found mine and stayed there, and that was the only vote I needed.

* * *

They installed me formally three days later, in a ceremony that lasted eleven minutes because we wrote it that way — brevity being, we agreed, a form of honesty. Odell's seal passed to me warm from the brazier where Wren had cleaned the wax from its teeth. It was heavier than I expected. Most inheritances are.

I spent the evening of that day alone in the master's study, which had been Odell's study, doing the thing he'd taught me first and best: reading the old books. The prophetic ledger lay open on the desk — what remained of it. The pages Kestrel Nystrom had not burned at Greyharrow were blank now, every prophecy gone out of them the moment the book itself died in the fire. Only the mundane entries survived, the ones written before the madness started: grain prices, weather notes, a complaint about a leaking roof from a hundred and six years ago.

I read that complaint twice and laughed until I had to sit down. Some things, apparently, are eternal.

At the bottom of the desk drawer, beneath Odell's spare spectacles and a tin of pens gone brittle with age, I found his last personal ledger — the small one, the private one, the one he'd kept the way careful men keep track of their souls. Most pages were ordinary. But near the end, in handwriting gone shaky, was a single entry dated the night before the Inquisitors took him:

*Kestrel will refuse the Crown's offer. He will refuse it because I taught him that a man's worth is what he won't sell. If I'm wrong about this, everything else I did was wasted. Note for whoever reads this: I was not wrong.*

Below it, smaller:

*Told him once that some debts are inherited. Should have told him the better truth — that some are chosen. Chosen debts bind tighter. Chosen names fit better. Whatever he calls himself, he already earned it. — O.*

I closed the book and sat for a long time in the lamplight, listening to the building settle around me the way old buildings do, creaking through their joints like old men. Somewhere below, Wren was arguing with Pell about freight rates, and losing, which meant Pell was learning. Somewhere outside, the snow was finally melting properly, running off the roofs in long bright ropes, and the gutters sounded almost like applause.

On the desk in front of me lay two documents. The Crown's offer, all gilt edges and restored glory. And a plain sheet of guild paper, headed with the words *Guildmaster's First Entry*.

I dipped my pen.

I wrote: *Let the record show that on this date, the Guild of Caerlum declines the restoration of the Emberwright name and all attendant holdings, freely and without regret. Let the record further show that the undersigned assumes the mastership of this guild not by blood, nor by grant of any crown, but by the consent of twenty-three members present and voting, and by the debt he owes to the dead man who kept this seat warm for him.*

Then, after a moment, in the space where a proud man would have signed his full style and title:

*Kestrel Nystrom. Ledger-keeper. Guildmaster. Keeper of the Ember — held, not hidden.*

I blotted it, set the Crown's letter aside to be returned unopened tomorrow, and reached for the next page, because a guildmaster's work, unlike a lord's, is never finished — and downstairs, faintly, I heard Wren calling my name, saying there was someone at the door asking, of all things, for an apprenticeship.
