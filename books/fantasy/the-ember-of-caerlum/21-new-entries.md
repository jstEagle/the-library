## 21. New Entries

The thaw came to Caerlum the way debts come to honest men: slowly, unevenly, and with a great deal of dripping.

I stood at the guildhall's east window on the first morning of true spring and watched meltwater run off the roof in bright ropes, pooling in the courtyard where the flagstones had cracked under three months of unnatural frost. The masons would want paying for that. The glaziers too — half the south windows had gone cloudy when the cold bit deep into the glass itself, which was the sort of thing that happened to glass in Caerlum and nowhere else, because everything we traded passed through this building eventually and left something of itself behind.

Impossible things leave residue. That was the first lesson Odell ever taught me, back when I was eleven years old and still believed the guild was merely strange rather than genuinely impossible. *Everything leaves a ledger line, boy,* he'd said, tapping my chest with one dry finger. *Even you.*

I had spent the winter learning exactly how large his ledger line for me had been.

But that was finished now. The Inquisitor-General was in irons awaiting a tribunal that would take years and satisfy no one, which I had come to understand was what justice looked like up close: not a clean blade-stroke but a long administrative bruise. The Ashen Court had splintered into three factions, two of which were negotiating with the Crown and one of which had simply vanished, which worried me more than either of the others. And the winter — the long, out-of-season winter that had fallen across the Ninefold Trade without anyone casting it — had broken the week after Greyharrow, retreating north like a tide going out, leaving mud and ruin and, here and there, small green shoots of something that might have been hope if you squinted.

The guild survived. Diminished, as I'd known it would be. We had lost eleven members to the raids and the road and the interrogation cells, and their names were written in the memorial codex in ink mixed with ash from the burned sister-guildhouse at Vellmark. But we survived, and more than that — we survived *honest*. For the first time in a hundred years, the Guild of Caerlum's books told the truth.

That was my doing. Mine and Wren's. And it was time, past time, to make it official.

* * *

The spring audit took four days, and I did every page myself.

This was not strictly necessary. We had six junior keepers now, three of them competent, and the audit could have been delegated the way audits always had been — passed down the hierarchy until some tired junior marked the columns by candlelight and signed without reading. That was how the old guild had worked. That was how the old guild had hidden a century of forged history inside routine arithmetic, because nobody reads what they assume is boring.

So I read all of it. Every column. Every margin note. Every entry that made sense and every entry that didn't, because in Caerlum the entries that don't make sense are usually the important ones.

The first day was ordinary grief-work: valuing what we'd lost. The Vellmark stock, scattered or seized. The Greyharrow collateral, forfeited when the Conclave city sealed its gates during the worst of the winter and our debtors inside simply stopped existing as far as repayment went. I wrote those losses down plainly, without the softening language the old ledgers used — no *assets rendered dormant*, no *holdings in abeyance*. Lost. Gone. Mourned. A ledger should say what happened, even when what happened hurts.

The second day was harder, and stranger.

I came to the debt rolls — the great bound volumes where the guild recorded what the world owed us — and I sat for a long while looking at the entries from the winter months. Debts of passage. Debts of shelter. Debts of silence, which was a real category in our books, though outsiders never believed it. And threaded through them, in my own handwriting from the desperate weeks after Greyharrow, entries like these:

*To the widow Anselm of Lowmarsh: one barrel of salt pork, given against the famine roads. No repayment expected. Entered as charity, not credit.*

*To the ferryman Doss: two silver for carrying strangers through the ice-fog without asking their names. Repayment waived. He asked why. I told him the guild was trying an experiment called honesty.*

*To Wren Calloway, courier: one life, owed by Kestrel Nystrom, keeper. Terms: ongoing.*

I had written that last one at three in the morning, half-frozen, the night after she pulled me out of the flooded culvert on the Greyharrow road with an Inquisitor's crossbow bolt buried in the doorframe an inch from where my head had been. I hadn't meant it as a formal entry. I'd meant it as a joke, the way exhausted people make jokes, and then I'd signed it properly because I was too tired to remember that jokes shouldn't be signed.

Now, in daylight, with the audit pen in my hand, I read it again and decided I wasn't striking it out.

Some debts you don't discharge. You just keep paying them forward, in installments, forever, and call it love instead of interest.

* * *

On the third day I catalogued the impossible things.

This was the part of the audit I loved most, even before everything changed, and I loved it differently now. Under Odell's mastership, the inventory of impossibles had been a locked book, seen by no one below master rank, its contents described in euphemism so dense that half our own members didn't know what we actually held. A jar of last year's echo. A key that opened whatever door you were ashamed of. The preserved breath of a woman who died forgiving someone. All of it filed under codes and ciphers, hidden even from ourselves.

I unlocked the cabinet, carried the whole inventory into the main hall, and catalogued it in plain language on open shelves where any member could read it.

There was argument about this. There is always argument about anything worth doing. Old Master Corvane — the only senior member who had sided with the Inquisitors early and then, to his credit, switched sides the moment he saw the forgery proven — insisted that openly listing impossible items would make us targets. He wasn't wrong. It would. It already had; two separate buyers had sent inquiries within a month of the Conclave, one offering sums that could have rebuilt the guildhall in marble.

"We were targets anyway," I told him. "We were just targets who lied to ourselves about it. At least now, when they come, we'll know what they're coming for."

He grumbled, but he stayed. That was the thing about Corvane — he was a coward with a conscience, and the conscience kept winning by narrower and narrower margins. I'd take it. Half of honesty is just cowardice that keeps losing arguments with itself.

So I wrote the entries plainly:

*Item 44: One jar, sealed wax, containing the echo of a bell that has not yet been cast. Provenance unknown. Do not shake. (See appendix: incident of 12th Frostmonth, previous year. See also: apology to the kitchen staff.)*

*Item 51: One key, iron, warm to the touch. Opens the door you are ashamed of. Note: we no longer sell this item. Note appended by K. Nystrom: we never should have sold it. Three prior sales located in historical ledgers; efforts underway to buy them back. This is what honest accounting is for.*

*Item 58: One feather, grey, from a bird no naturalist can name. Burns without heat. Light without flame. Kept because some things should be kept.*

And at the bottom of the list, in the space where the old inventories had always left a blank line — a tradition nobody could explain, a gap in every catalogue going back a century, as if the guild had always been waiting for something to fill it —

I paused.

Then I wrote: *Item 59: One ember, living, held in trust. Not for sale. Not for use. Keeper: K. Nystrom. Condition: banked.*

It was the closest I would ever come, in an official document, to writing down what lived in my palms. The fire that had answered at the waystation. The fire that had burned a man alive. The fire I had spent nearly all of at Greyharrow, burning the prophecy itself out of the world — and what remained, what had cooled and settled and refused entirely to die, sitting low and patient in my hands like a coal wrapped in ash.

Held, not hidden. Banked, not spent. Some inheritances you carry.

I closed the inventory and found my hands were warm. They usually were, now, when I thought about it. I was learning not to think about it, the way you learn not to tongue a loose tooth. Mostly.

* * *

The fourth day of the audit, Wren came home.

She'd been north for six weeks, running the trade routes we were rebuilding one frozen pass at a time, and she arrived in the courtyard muddy to the knees and sunburned across the cheekbones and leading a string of three pack-mules loaded with the first honest cargo Caerlum had moved since autumn: wool, iron nails, dried fish, and one crate whose manifest read, in her own blunt hand, *contents unknown, seller swears it hums, buyer beware, price fair.*

I met her in the yard. I tried to look like a guildmaster meeting a returning factor. I lasted approximately four seconds.

"You're thinner," she said, before I got a word out.

"You're taller."

"I'm not. You're just been hunching over ledgers so long your spine's given up." She dropped the mules' leads into a stablehand's hands and looked up at the guildhall — at the windows, newly reglazed, and the sign above the door, which I had repainted myself. The old sign had borne the guild's crest and motto: *All Things May Be Traded.* I'd had it redone. The crest remained. The motto now read: *All Things May Be Told.*

"People hate it," she said.

"I know."

"Three different factors complained to me on the road. Said it's bad for business, saying what you mean."

"I know."

"Good." She grinned, and it was the same grin that had gotten us both out of Greyharrow alive — reckless and certain and aimed directly at whatever needed defying next. "Keep it."

Inside, over the terrible barley tea that was the guildhall's one unchanging constant across regimes and catastrophes, I gave her the audit summary. She listened the way she always listened — completely, without blinking, like a kestrel herself, like the bird I'd been named for and had never once lived up to until she started making me.

"Debts forgiven," she said, scanning the page. "How much?"

"Enough that Corvane fainted. He's fine. He fainted dramatically, with warning."

"And the impossible inventory's public?"

"Open shelving. Plain language. Item fifty-nine included."

Her eyes flicked to my hands, resting on the table. She knew. She'd known since the waystation, since the smell of burnt cloth and worse, since the night I couldn't hold a cup because my fingers wouldn't stop trembling with something that wasn't fear. She had never once asked me to explain it, and I had never once managed to lie to her about it, and between those two facts we had built something sturdier than confession.

"It's a good audit," she said finally. "First honest one in a hundred years. Your name should be on it."

"My name is on it. Keeper's signature, bottom right."

"Not that." She reached across the table and turned the ledger toward her, and took up a pen, and in the space beside my signature — the co-signature line that had sat empty on every guild document for a century, because the old masters had trusted no one enough to share it — she wrote her name. *Wren Calloway.* Firm strokes, no flourish, ink dark on the page.

"Partner," she said. "In trade. In the books. In—" She stopped, and for the first time since I'd known her, Wren Calloway, who had outrun Inquisitors and out-bluffed smuggler queens and once talked her way past an Ashen Court sentinel using nothing but a borrowed hat and tremendous nerve, looked briefly uncertain. "In all of it. If you want. If—"

"Yes," I said.

"—because if you're going to keep being stubbornly honest for the rest of your life, someone has to be here to make sure you eat, and—"

"Wren. Yes."

"Oh." She blinked. "Well. Good. That's settled, then." And she signed the audit formally, and blotted it, and pushed it back across the table as if she hadn't just redrawn the entire architecture of my life in four seconds flat, and said, "Your turn. There's paper in the drawer. I checked weeks ago. I've been waiting for you to notice it yourself, but honestly, Kestrel, you can find a forged clause in a hundred-year-old codex but you can't notice a drawer."

* * *

The new ledger was waiting where she said it would be: a fresh-bound volume, good vellum, blank as snowfall. On its cover, in gold leaf that Corvane had donated without being asked, because even cowards with consciences sometimes want to be part of something: the guild crest, and beneath it the number *One*. Volume one. As if nothing before it counted. As if the counting had only now begun.

I sat alone in the master's study — my study, though I still knocked before entering, a habit I suspected I'd keep for the rest of my life out of respect for the man who'd died in that chair keeping my secret. Evening light came through the reglazed windows, honest and ordinary, the light of a world with weather in it again.

I dipped the pen.

The first page of a ledger matters. Odell taught me that too, though he taught it by example rather than instruction: the opening entries set the terms for everything that follows. What you write first is what you agree to be bound by.

I thought about what the old ledgers' first pages had said. *Let all things be weighed. Let nothing be spoken of outside these walls. Let the guild endure.* Terms of secrecy. Terms of survival. Terms of fear, dressed up in ceremony.

I wrote:

*Volume One of the New Count of the Guild of Caerlum, begun in the spring following the Burning at Greyharrow.*

*Terms of this ledger, set down by Kestrel Nystrom, keeper, and Wren Calloway, partner:*

*First: all things may be told. Nothing entered here shall be false, and nothing true shall be withheld from these pages out of convenience or fear.*

*Second: debts are real, and so is mercy. Both shall be recorded plainly, and neither shall be weaponized.*

*Third: the impossible shall be catalogued, not concealed. We are not afraid of what we keep.*

*Fourth: this guild exists to hold truth in trust for people who cannot hold it themselves. When law and truth diverge, we follow truth, and accept the cost, and write the cost down.*

*Fifth: some inheritances are carried, not spent. Entry reserved.*

I sat back and read it over. It was too solemn, probably. Wren would tease me about the word "weaponized." Somewhere, Odell was laughing at "Entry reserved," or possibly weeping, or possibly both, which knowing Odell would have required no special magic at all.

But it was true. Every line of it. And a ledger that tells the truth, even clumsily, is worth ten that sing.

I signed it. Then, because the co-signature line was mine to offer now, I turned the book around and left it on the desk for her, and went to the window, and watched the last light go gold and then grey over the rooftops of Caerlum — over the market stalls reopening, over the masons' scaffolding, over the ordinary miraculous world getting on with its business.

Behind me, I heard the study door open, and her footsteps cross the floor, and the small scratch of a pen.

Then the scratch stopped.

"Kestrel," Wren said, and her voice had changed — gone quiet and careful, the voice she used for wounded animals and dangerous news. "Come look at this."

I crossed the room. She was standing over the new ledger, and beneath my fifth term — beneath *entry reserved*, in the blank space I had left — there was fresh ink that neither of us had written.

Small, precise letters, in a hand I almost recognized, a hand I had last seen in a dead woman's prophecy a hundred years old:

*The ember keeps. But embers wake. Ask the child who knocks at midsummer.*

Outside, far off in the evening streets, someone was knocking on the guildhall's front door. Patiently. Once, twice, again.

Wren looked at me. My palms, without my permission, had gone warm.
