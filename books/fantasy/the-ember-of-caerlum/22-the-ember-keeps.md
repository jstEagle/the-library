## 22. The Ember Keeps

The apprentice arrived on a Tuesday, in the rain, carrying a ledger she had stolen.

Kestrel watched her from the counting-room window as she stood in the courtyard of the guildhall of Caerlum, dripping, defiant, clutching the book to her chest like a shield. She was perhaps fifteen, all elbows and stubbornness, with mud to her knees and an expression that said she had walked a long way to be here and would walk a long way back before admitting she'd made a mistake.

Wren found him at the window. "That's the third one this season."

"The third what?"

"Third one who shows up wanting to learn the trade from *the Kestrel Nystrom*." She said it dryly, but there was no mockery in it anymore. There hadn't been for years. "You're becoming a legend, love. It's very inconvenient for both of us."

"I'm a ledger-keeper."

"You're a ledger-keeper who broke a prophecy in front of two hundred delegates and lived." Wren leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, gray threading her dark hair now in a way she pretended not to care about and secretly did. "People remember things like that. Especially people with nowhere else to go."

Below, the girl had not moved. The rain came down harder, and still she stood there, book held high, water streaming off her chin, glaring up at the window as if she knew exactly where he was standing.

"She stole something," Kestrel said.

"Of course she did. She's a trader." Wren smiled. "Let's see if she stole it well."

* * *

Her name was Isbet Corrin, and she had stolen the ledger from a dead man.

Not a murdered man — a drowned one, pulled from the reed-beds of the Mirefen with his pockets full of river stones, a suicide by every sign except the ledger itself, which was dry. Which was impossible. Which was why she'd taken it.

"It wasn't right," she said, sitting in the counting-room with her boots off and her feet wrapped in one of Wren's old scarves, the stolen book open between them on the table. "A thing like that, just lying in the mud. Things that can't exist shouldn't be left lying around. Someone should keep them properly."

Kestrel turned a page. The dead man's hand was neat, disciplined, the hand of someone trained in double-entry. And the entries were wrong in exactly the way entries should never be wrong: dates that ran backward, sums that balanced against nothing, a column of debts owed to recipients who could not be found in any register of the living.

"And you brought it here," he said. "To Caerlum. Why?"

"Because you're the ones who trade in impossible things." She said it simply, without awe, which he appreciated more than she would ever know. "And because everyone says you're honest now. Honest and poor." A pause. "I don't mind poor. I've been poor my whole life. It's the honest part I want to learn."

Wren, from the doorway, snorted. "She'll do."

"I haven't agreed to take an apprentice."

"You've been staring at that ledger for ten minutes like it owes you money. You've agreed. You just haven't told yourself yet."

Kestrel looked down at the book. The dead man's backward dates. The debts to no one. Somewhere in the margins, faint as breath on glass, a warmth stirred under his palms where they rested on the paper — the old, familiar heat, the one that had never left him, that he had carried through seven years of audits and inventories and quiet mornings like a coal banked in ash.

He closed the ledger gently.

"First lesson," he said to Isbet Corrin. "Everything has a cost. Including apprenticeships. Yours is this: you tell me everything about how you found this book, and you leave nothing out, especially the parts that frighten you. Can you do that?"

She thought about it with her whole face, the way young people do before they've learned to hide.

"Yes," she said.

"Then welcome to the Guild of Caerlum. Second lesson starts tomorrow. It's called *counting*."

* * *

Teaching, it turned out, was its own kind of accounting.

Every lesson was a debit against his patience and a credit toward something he couldn't name. Every question Isbet asked forced him to know the answer more truly than he had before — because you cannot explain a thing you only half understand, and Kestrel had spent years half-understanding things on purpose.

"Why does the guild keep the impossible inventory separate?" she asked him, three months in, dusting the long shelves of the vault below the guildhall where the strange stock slept: mirrors that showed tomorrow's weather, a compass that pointed at regrets, seeds from a tree that had burned before the world learned fire.

"Because impossible things are dangerous mostly when they're treated as ordinary," Kestrel said. "An ordinary thing you can price. An impossible thing prices *you*. So we keep them apart, we name them honestly, and we never pretend they're just stock."

"That's not what the other guilds do."

"No. But the other guilds also aren't us."

She shelved the seed-jar and looked at him sideways, in the way she had when she was circling a question she'd been carrying for days. He had learned to wait her out. Patience was another thing the guild had taught him, though it had taken a dying master and a burning city to do it.

"You were there," she said finally. "At Greyharrow. When the winter broke."

"I was there."

"They say you burned the prophecy out of the air. That the words caught fire while the Inquisitor-General was reading them, and the fire went *up*, into the snow, and the snow melted, and—" She stopped. "Is it true?"

"Mostly," Kestrel said. "The version they tell leaves out that I was terrified, that I nearly killed everyone in the hall, and that I cried afterward in a stable. Those parts matter too. History that only keeps the shining bits isn't history. It's advertising."

Isbet considered this. "Master Odell used to say something like that. My grandmother heard him speak once, before the war scare. She said he was the only guildmaster who ever apologized for an error in a public audit."

"He did apologize." Kestrel ran his thumb along the edge of the shelf, feeling the grain of wood Odell himself had planed forty years ago. "It got him killed eventually. Telling the truth always costs. The trick is making sure it costs less than the lie would have."

"And did it? Cost less?"

Kestrel thought of Odell's last entry, written in a shaking hand under Inquisitor interrogation, giving up nothing. Thought of Sable Vane, who had survived the splintering of her Court and now traded honestly somewhere in the southern ports, sending him a card every midwinter that said only *Still keeping.* Thought of the Inquisitor-General's name, struck from every record Kestrel had touched, not erased — corrected, with a marginal note explaining the amendment, because erasure was just forgery pointed backward.

"Yes," he said. "Barely. Ask me again in twenty years."

* * *

The warmth in his palms never died.

Seven years, and it had faded no further than the day after Greyharrow — a faint heat sleeping in the meat of his hands, waking sometimes when he touched old paper, or open flame, or certain kinds of silence. He had tested it, early on, with Wren watching from the doorway with her hand on her knife. Held a candle flame until it bent toward his fingers like a curious bird. Never let it past that. Never wanted to.

Some inheritances, he had decided, are carried, not spent. The Emberwrights had spent theirs — had poured their fire into the world until the world burned and their name became a curse forged in someone else's ink. Kestrel kept his the way the guild kept its impossible inventory: named honestly, stored carefully, never treated as ordinary.

He told no one. Not because it was shameful — he had made his peace with shame years ago, buried it beside Odell's grave with full honors — but because it was *his*. In a life spent opening every record, correcting every entry, telling every hard truth in front of assembled powers, one small warm secret belonged to him alone. He found he treasured it precisely because he refused to weaponize it. It was the one asset on his books that appreciated through disuse.

Wren knew, of course. She always had. She had watched him stare at his own hands in the waystation after the first time the fire answered, and she had never asked him to explain, and he had loved her for it with the specific, durable love of two people who have each seen the other at their worst and stayed anyway. They married in spring, in the guildhall courtyard, with the whole diminished company of Caerlum present and Maren arriving late and drunk and bearing gifts that were technically smuggling. The vows were short. The feast lasted three days. The ledgers fell behind, and neither of them cared.

Now, some evenings, Wren would find him at the window after dark, turning his palms up to the lamplight, watching the faint gold shimmer under the skin like coins at the bottom of clear water.

"Cold hands?" she'd say.

"Warm, actually."

"Mm." She'd take one of them anyway, press it between her own, ordinary and calloused and perfect. "Well. Keep it."

He intended to.

* * *

In the fourth year of Isbet's apprenticeship, she found the prophetic ledger.

Kestrel had known she would, eventually. He had never hidden it — hiding it would have been a lie, and the guild of Caerlum did not lie anymore, not even about its ghosts. The book sat in the deepest vault, catalogued under *INSTRUMENTS, UNRESOLVED*, its pages long since rendered harmless: every prophecy line struck through in his own careful hand, each strike annotated with the date and method of nullification. *Claim severed by destruction of text. Claim severed by refusal of parties named. Claim severed by death of claimant.* Accounting, applied to fate.

Isbet read it for a whole afternoon. Then she climbed the stairs to his office, set it on his desk, and stood there with her arms crossed, seventeen now and taller than she liked to admit.

"This is you," she said. "Your name. Your date. Written a hundred years before you were born."

"Yes."

"And you crossed it out."

"Every line."

"Why?"

Kestrel took off his spectacles — he needed them now, another small tax on the years — and rubbed the bridge of his nose. Outside the window, Caerlum was doing its evening business: carts in the lane, gulls arguing over the fish-market leavings, the new guildhouse bell ringing the hour, cast from the metal of the old one that the Inquisitors' fire had cracked. The guild was smaller than it had been in his youth. It was also, by every measure that mattered, richer.

"Because a prophecy is just a debt someone claims the future owes," he said. "And I spent my whole life learning that most debts people claim are not real. They're forged. Or inflated. Or inherited by people who never signed for them." He tapped the struck-through page. "This one said the world owed a war, and that I was the collateral. I audited it. The claim didn't hold. So I wrote it off."

"Just like that?"

"Not just like anything. It cost." He smiled, thin and true. "But that's the whole trade, Isbet. Everything has a cost. The art is choosing which costs you can live inside."

She looked at the ledger a long moment. Then, quietly, in the voice she saved for the questions that actually frightened her:

"Do you think there are others? Other books. Other names. Other people walking around with a line written against them, a hundred years old, waiting."

Kestrel was silent.

Under the desk, out of her sight, his palms warmed — that faint, faithful heat, stirring the way it always did when the future leaned close and asked its old question. *What are you going to do about it?*

He thought of the dead man's ledger, four years ago, with its backward dates and its debts to no one. He thought of the impossible inventory, and how every year it grew a little longer, as if the world were quietly returning things it had once borrowed. He thought of a girl in the rain, holding a book that should have been soaked, saying *someone should keep them properly.*

"I think," he said, "that there will always be books like this. And names in them. And people who have to choose." He closed the prophetic ledger and slid it back across the desk to her. "Which is why the guild trains ledger-keepers. Take this down to the vault, Isbet. Catalogue it. And then come back up — because tomorrow, I'm going to teach you the last lesson."

She frowned. "There's a last lesson?"

"There's always a last lesson." He picked up his pen and opened the current ledger to a fresh page, and began, in a steady hand, to write. "It's the same one every generation learns, and no one believes until it's their turn: *the record outlives the recorder.* Write accordingly."

Isbet carried the book downstairs. Kestrel wrote.

Outside, the light went gold, then gray, then blue. The lamp in his palm-warm hands threw steady light across the columns. Somewhere below, Wren laughed at something the night clerk said, and the sound came up through the floorboards like a dividend paid on a very long investment.

On the fresh page, under today's date — a date no one had written a century ago, a date that belonged to no prophecy and no war, a date that was simply, freely, his — Kestrel Nystrom, guildmaster of Caerlum, keeper of records rather than maker of wars, entered the day's first line:

*Received into keeping: one future, unencumbered. Condition: good. Provenance: chosen.*

He blotted the ink, warmed his hands briefly over the drying page — a habit no one had ever remarked upon, and he hoped none ever would — and reached for the next entry.

There was always a next entry. That, too, was an inheritance. And this one, at last, he was glad to keep.
