## 1. The Cartographer's Lie

The bell over the shop door had a crack in it, so that every customer who entered announced themselves with a sound like a swallowed word. Zephyrine Whitlock had chosen the bell deliberately, fifteen years ago, when they first hung out their shingle in Candlewick Lane: *Whitlock & Sons, Purveyors of Curiosities and Fine Maps*. The *and Sons* was a fiction—there were no sons, and never had been—but fictions were the mortar of Highmoor, and Zephyrine had become, over the long grey years, something of a master mason.

They were grinding pigment when the bell gave its broken cry that morning: ochre and ash and a pinch of powdered slate, worked slow with a stone pestle until the mixture took on the color of storm-light over water. It was good pigment. It was, in fact, better than any pigment had a right to be, because Zephyrine whispered to it as they ground—a small, wordless thing, less a spell than a suggestion, the way one might warm cold hands at a hearth without thinking. The ink made this way held its lines longer. It seemed almost to *want* to be true.

That was the whole of the gift, or so Zephyrine told themselves on the days they could bear to look at it squarely. Not fire from the fingertips. Not prophecy, not healing, not the raising of storms. Just this: that maps drawn by their hand did not merely record the world. They *reached* it.

The customer was a wool merchant with damp shoulders and the harried look of a man whose wife had sent him out into the rain for something he didn't understand.

"A map," he said, shaking off his hat. "Of the Amber Coast. My wife's mother came from there before the border closed, and she's dying, and Marta wants—" He stopped, embarrassed by his own tenderness. "She wants to see where her mother was born."

Zephyrine set down the pestle. "I have coastal charts," they said, and pulled three from the drawer behind the counter, unrolling them across the scarred oak. These were honest maps, mostly. Surveyed coastlines, honest depths, the work of dead cartographers copied and recopied until the paper went soft as cloth. The merchant bent over them, and Zephyrine watched his face and saw what they always saw: not satisfaction, but hunger. A chart of the Amber Coast would show him where the town stood. It would not show him the town.

"The third one," the merchant said at last, doubtfully. "It's very fine. But it's just—it's lines, isn't it? I wanted her to be able to *see* it."

And there it was, laid open on the counter between them like a wound: the gap between what maps showed and what people needed them to hold.

"I can do better," Zephyrine heard themselves say, which was foolish, which was dangerous, which was the same sentence they had been saying for fifteen years in one form or another. "Give me two days."

* * *

The back room of Whitlock & Sons had no windows facing the street, and the one window it did have looked out onto a lightwell where nothing grew but moss and, improbably, a single stubborn fern that Zephyrine had coaxed along for years with warmth that had no business existing in a city where enchantment carried the penalty of the pyre. They kept the fern small. That was the discipline of it—the whole art of concealment distilled into a single green thing that must never grow too lush, never flower, never give itself away by thriving.

Zephyrine sat at the drawing table and pinned fresh paper to the board, and for a long moment simply breathed.

This was the part no one knew about. This was the hour that belonged to them alone, when the shop was shuttered and the city's noise dimmed to a murmur through the walls, and Zephyrine could let the gift rise in them like breath held too long finally released. It moved through their hands first, always—a warmth in the palms, an itching at the base of the fingers, as though the skin remembered being something else. Then the ink began to move.

Not visibly. Never visibly; that was the first law of survival, older even than the crown's edicts. But under the surface of things, in the way wet pigment settles and dries, the map *became*. The Amber Coast assembled itself out of Zephyrine's memory—not their memory, exactly, for they had never seen it, but the memory the world kept of itself, the deep registry of every place that had ever been loved or feared or named. Their pen traced the hook of the headland and the headland *told* them it curved a half-mile further east than any chart recorded. They drew the harbor and felt the pull of its tide. When they reached the town itself—the terracotta roofs, the shrine to some drowned god, the crooked street where a woman named Marta's mother had once been a girl—they drew it the way memory draws, soft at the edges, bright at the center, and the paper drank the image down and held it glowing beneath the surface like a lamp behind silk.

Anyone looking at the finished map would see a lovely piece of work. Skilled, certainly. Unusual, perhaps. But only someone who *looked*—truly looked, with longing, with grief, with love—would find the image rising up to meet them, would smell the salt air of a coast they'd never walked, would hear gulls crying over a harbor forty years gone.

Only someone who believed would see.

That was the mercy and the trap of it. Magic in Highmoor had not been outlawed because it was powerful. It had been outlawed because it was *contagious*—because wonder, the crown's philosophers had written in their tidy damning treatises, is the seedbed of sedition. A people who could see beyond their borders might begin to imagine beyond their borders. And so the gifted were burned, and the maps were straightened, and Highmoor's children grew up learning geography the way they learned scripture: by rote, without once being asked to close their eyes and *go there*.

Zephyrine blew across the drying ink and felt the familiar exhaustion settle into their bones, heavier than sleep. Fifteen years. Fifteen years of selling wonders disguised as trinkets, of whispering strength into copper charms shaped like luck-knots and painted beads, of watching customers walk out of the shop with miracles in their pockets and never knowing. There was a woman on Tanner's Row whose asthmatic son slept soundly because of a brass whistle Zephyrine had sung warmth into; she thought it was the dry weather. There was a ferryman whose rope never frayed, though he praised the hemp. Small mercies, scattered like seed into soil that would never know the sower's name.

It should have been enough. Some nights Zephyrine told themselves it was.

But loneliness in permanent concealment has a particular texture, a grain all its own. It is not the loneliness of absence—there are always people, always customers, always the bell's cracked voice announcing another stranger. It is the loneliness of *presence unreceived*, of standing in a crowded room holding a lantern no one else can see lit. Every kindness Zephyrine performed vanished into the world without leaving a ripple of recognition. Every year, the person they had been before Highmoor—the child who had drawn maps in the dust of a courtyard while their grandmother laughed and said, *careful, little spark, you'll draw yourself right out of the world*—grew fainter, until some mornings Zephyrine woke and had to lie still for a moment, searching for themselves the way one searches for a word on the tip of the tongue.

They rolled the Amber Coast map, tied it with plain twine, and set it beneath the counter for the merchant. Then they banked the fire, touched two fingers briefly to the fern's frond—an old habit, a secret tenderness—and climbed the narrow stair to bed above the shop.

Outside, over the rooftops of Highmoor, the moon hung thin as a paring of bone.

Thirteen turns, their grandmother had said once, in the last year before the purges, when Zephyrine was small enough to sit inside the circle of her arms. *Count them, little spark. Thirteen turns, and the Ember wakes.* She had said it like a lullaby. Zephyrine had never understood why, remembering it now, their chest went tight as a fist.

* * *

Three days later, near dusk, with rain coming down in grey ropes and the gutters running loud, a child came into the shop.

She was perhaps seven, soaked to the knees, with the ferocious dignity of a child on an errand she considers vital. She planted herself before the counter, dripping, and produced from her pocket a single copper coin, worn nearly smooth.

"Gran says you sell wishes," she announced. "But she said I wasn't to tell anyone she said it, on account of it being treason." She delivered this with the crisp precision of a memorized instruction. "So don't tell."

Zephyrine's hands went still on the counter.

This was the peril no edict could guard against, the flaw in fifteen years of careful masonry: children. Children repeated what they heard. Children said *wishes* aloud in shops, and if a warden's ear happened to be near—if the wrong word traveled up Candlewick Lane to the garrison—

"Your grandmother," Zephyrine said carefully, "sends you to buy trinkets."

"She says your trinkets *work*." The girl's chin lifted, defiant, as though daring Zephyrine to deny it. "My brother's got the fever. The fever that took Da. Gran's tried everything the physickers have and they just shake their heads and take her silver, and she heard from Widow Marsh that the little blue bead from Whitlock's cured her cough of nine years, and Widow Marsh swears it on her mother's grave."

The blue bead. Zephyrine remembered it—a glass bead cooled in moonwater and murmured over, sold for two coppers to a coughing woman eighteen months ago. They had not known it had worked. They had not dared ask.

The child stood there, water pooling around her boots, holding out her single copper, and Zephyrine felt the whole architecture of their concealment tremble like a web in wind. The safe answer rose readily: *I sell curiosities, little one. Beads and bells and pretty stones. Take your coin home to your gran.*

The fever that took Da. The physickers shaking their heads. Somewhere across the rain-dark city a boy lay burning, and his grandmother had spent her pride to send his sister out with a copper and a treasonous hope.

Zephyrine had built their life on the principle that small kindnesses were survivable so long as they stayed invisible. But invisibility, they had learned, was not silence. It was a language spoken fluently and answered by no one, and there were evenings—there were more and more evenings—when the effort of speaking it left them hollow as a struck bell.

"Wait here," Zephyrine said.

In the back room, they chose a bead of river-glass, pale as morning, and held it cupped in both hands. What they poured into it was not a cure—cures were beyond them, had always been beyond them; the gift moved through sympathy, not sovereignty. What they poured in was *ease*: coolness for a burning brow, rest for a racked body, the sense of a hand smoothing hair back in the dark. Strength for the fever to spend itself against. It took more out of them than they expected. It always did. By the time they returned to the shopfront, sweat stood cold at their temples despite the chill.

"For your brother," Zephyrine said, pressing the bead into the child's palm and closing her fingers over it. "He's to hold it when the fever's worst. Tell your gran—" They hesitated. Every instinct screamed caution. *Tell her nothing. Tell her it's only glass.* "Tell her to hang it at the window where the moon can find it."

"That's not a real instruction," the girl said shrewdly.

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "But do it anyway."

She dropped her copper on the counter with tremendous ceremony and marched out into the rain. Through the fogged glass Zephyrine watched her go, a small determined shape dissolving into the grey, carrying a stolen miracle down a public street in her bare hand.

*Foolish,* Zephyrine told themselves, and meant it, and found—to their weary, unsurprised horror—that they would do it again tomorrow. That was the truth beneath the truth of their exile: they had never once managed to stop being what they were. They had only gotten very good at hiding it, and hiding, practiced daily for fifteen years, had become its own kind of magic, the darkest kind, the kind that worked on the magician alone.

They were blowing out the lamps when the bell cracked its broken warning again.

Not a customer. Zephyrine knew it before they turned—knew it by the weight of the silence after the bell, by the way the rain suddenly sounded very loud. Two men stood in the doorway, streaming wet, wearing the slate-grey cloaks of the Crown Wardens. The taller of them held a folded paper, and though the ink on it had run with damp, Zephyrine could read the shape of the seal at its foot through the wet paper: the crowned tower of House Aldermoor.

"Whitlock," the tall warden said. His voice was mild, which was worse than shouting. Mildness meant procedure. Procedure meant this had been coming a long while. "You'll want to hear the proclamation. All of Highmoor will hear it by morning, but the guild streets get it first."

He unfolded the paper. Rain drummed on the roof. In the corner, the fern in the lightwell stood perfectly still, as if it too were listening.

"By order of the Regent's Council," the warden read, "a general inquiry into the crime of enchantment shall commence upon the next full moon. All citizens are enjoined to report signs of sorcery. Informants will be rewarded in silver."

He lowered the paper. His eyes, pale and patient, moved slowly around the shop—over the trays of beads, the stacked charts, the little brass charms turning gently on their strings—and came to rest, at last, on Zephyrine's face.

"Lovely trinkets you have here," he said.
