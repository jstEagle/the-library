## 2. Proclamation at the Gate

The bell in the Gildertower rang seven times before Zephyrine heard the hooves.

They were at their worktable when it came—that particular clatter of shod iron on the cobbles that no one in Highmoor mistook for ordinary traffic. Ordinary traffic did not travel in formation. Ordinary traffic did not come down Candlewick Lane at dawn with the deliberate rhythm of men who had somewhere official to be and no interest in being gentle about it. Zephyrine set down the fine brush they had been using to ink a river's name—a river that existed nowhere but in memory and magic—and went still, the way a hare goes still, the way they had taught themselves to go still fifteen years ago.

Through the shutters' slats they watched six riders in the crown's livery pass beneath the window. Grey cloaks, grey horses, faces like coins worn smooth by handling. Wardens. Not the street patrol that shook down fishwives and broke up tavern brawls—these were the crown's own, the ones from the Citadel on the hill, the ones whose boots Highmoor's children were taught to fear before they were taught their letters.

They drew rein at the gate square, where the old proclamation post stood—a blackened oak pillar older than the city walls, studded with the rusted nails of a hundred announcements: tax levies, curfews, executions. One of the wardens dismounted and nailed something to it. The hammer blows rang out over the rooftops, each one landing in Zephyrine's chest like a knock on a door they had spent half a lifetime pretending was not theirs.

*Not yet,* they told themselves. *It could be anything. A levy. A curfew. It is always something.*

But their hands, hanging at their sides, had already begun to tremble, and Zephyrine had learned long ago never to lie to their own hands.

* * *

By midmorning the whole lane had gathered at the gate square, pressed together in the thin autumn light like moths around a cold flame. Zephyrine went with them, hood up, satchel slung across their body the way they always wore it—with the strap crossed so no pickpurse could cut it away, though the only thing inside worth stealing was a folded map of a country that did not officially exist.

The crowd parted just enough for them to see.

The proclamation was written in the high, formal hand of the royal chancery, each letter drawn with the kind of care usually reserved for scripture:

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN OF HIGHMOOR**

Let it be known throughout the city and its holdings that the practice of enchantment remains forbidden by ancient law, and that the tolerance heretofore extended to those who conceal such practices is hereby withdrawn.

A great hunt shall commence upon the next full moon. All persons harboring knowledge of the gifted, or sheltering them, or trafficking in charms or enchanted goods, are commanded to come forward and declare such knowledge to the wardens.

Those who inform shall be rewarded in silver. Those who conceal shall share the guilty's fate.

The gifted have thirty days to surrender themselves and be judged. After the moon turns full, none shall be spared the fire.

Zephyrine read it three times. The words did not change. That was the terrible thing about words—they stayed exactly as cruel as you first found them.

*Rewarded in silver.* The chancery knew its audience. Highmoor was a city of the near-poor and the nearly-honest, where a winter's coal cost more than most folk made in a month, where children went barefoot into the ninth month of the year. Silver would buy informants the way honey bought flies. It would buy neighbors against neighbors. It would buy sisters against brothers, if the silver was bright enough and the winter cold enough.

And *the fire*. They had not spoken openly of the pyres in fifteen years—not since the last purge, the one the histories now called the Reconciliation, as though it had been a meeting of merchants rather than an ash-strewn season of screaming. Zephyrine had been a child then, small enough to be hidden in a flour barrel while the smoke came over the hills from the burning grounds. They remembered the taste of raw flour and terror, and how the two had mingled in their mouth until they could no longer tell them apart.

Around them, the crowd murmured. Zephyrine listened the way they always listened—half to the words, half to what moved underneath them.

"Thirty days," said the cooper's wife, wringing her apron. "My sister's boy went off to study in the lowlands. What if someone says he—"

"Silver," said a tanner's man, and spat. "They'll pay us to eat each other. Mark me. By the full moon there won't be a secret left in this city."

"Good," said another voice, harder, younger. "Let them burn. My father died in the marsh fever the year the charm-sellers took every coin he had. Let them all burn."

Zephyrine kept their head bowed and their face arranged into the mild, forgettable blankness they had practiced until it fit like a second skin. *Forgettable.* That was the whole art of survival in Highmoor. Be the face a neighbor cannot describe. Be the voice a warden cannot place. Be the person who sells trinkets—just trinkets, little brass suns and painted stones, nothing more—at the Saltmarket stall, year after year, unremarkable as rain.

Fifteen years of it. Fifteen years of folding their gift smaller and smaller, like a map creased so many times it threatens to tear along the folds. Fifteen years of feeling the magic move under their skin like a second heartbeat and pretending it was indigestion, pretending it was nothing, pretending so hard and so long that some mornings they woke genuinely unsure whether they had imagined the whole of it—the shimmering roads, the realms that bloomed behind the world like flowers behind a wall, the maps that drew themselves through their fingers because the truth wanted, always wanted, to be charted.

A woman beside them began to weep quietly. No one comforted her. Comfort required closeness, and closeness required risk, and the proclamation had already redrawn the map of the city: every neighbor now stood at a border, and every kindness was a crossing that might be reported.

Zephyrine looked up once, briefly, at the grey riders still mounted by the post, and caught one of them looking back—not at them, not specifically, but at the crowd, with the patient, cataloguing gaze of a man counting sheep. He held a slate. As Zephyrine watched, he wrote something on it.

They turned and walked home without hurrying. Hurrying was memorable.

* * *

That evening, they heard the dragging.

Candlewick Lane was quiet in the way lanes are quiet after a proclamation—everyone indoors, everyone listening through their own walls. So the sound carried: boots, many boots, and the wet scrape of something heavy hauled across cobblestones, and a voice Zephyrine knew.

Old Maren. The widow two doors down who grew nightshade in a clay pot on her sill and called it "decoration." Who had once, three winters past, found Zephyrine collapsed in the street with a fever and had dragged *them* inside instead, fed them willow-bark tea, and asked no questions—which in Highmoor was the deepest kindness anyone could offer. Whose nightshade, Zephyrine happened to know, was not decoration at all, because Zephyrine had seen the faint green shimmer of working clinging to it like pollen.

Maren was a hedge-witch of the smallest sort. She eased rheumatism with whispered charms. She told fortunes with knucklebones for a copper, mostly wrong. She was seventy years old and walked with a stick, and she had never harmed a living soul except perhaps with her opinions, which were freely given and freely earned.

Zephyrine was at the window before they had decided to be there.

Four wardens came down the lane with Maren between them. She was not walking so much as being moved, her stick gone, her feet catching and stumbling, and there was a rope at her wrists. Her white hair had come loose from its bun and hung around her face like frost. And she was not silent. That was the thing that would stay with Zephyrine afterward, the thing that would wake them at night for weeks: Maren was *talking*, reasonably, desperately, the way the innocent talk—as though the whole catastrophe were a misunderstanding that could be cleared up if only someone would listen.

"I grow herbs. I grow *herbs*, young man, ask anyone, ask the apothecary, I've bought seed from the apothecary these twenty years—who told you? Who told you I—" Her voice cracked and steadied and cracked again. "I eased your grandmother's hip, Warden Corl. Your own grandmother. You used to run errands for me. You carried my water. You were a *good boy*."

The youngest of the four wardens flinched. Zephyrine saw it even at that distance—a small, involuntary movement of the shoulders, the flinch of a person hearing something true. But he said nothing, and he kept walking, and his boots kept time with the others, and that was the whole tragedy of the world in a single image: good boys who keep walking.

Doors stayed shut along the lane. Zephyrine watched the shuttered windows the way a drowning person watches the shore—every one of them closed, every one of them hiding eyes, perhaps, but closed. Behind one of them, Zephyrine knew, was the tanner's man who had spat about silver. Behind another was the cooper's wife. Behind another, maybe, someone who owed Maren money, or envied her pot garden, or simply wanted the silver and needed no better reason than that.

*This is what it buys,* Zephyrine thought. *Not safety. Just the choice of who burns first.*

Maren passed directly beneath Zephyrine's window. And because the world is sometimes cruel in the specific way that breaks you open, she lifted her head—lifted it as though she felt eyes on her, as though the old hedge-craft in her bones still answered to the old craft in Zephyrine's—and for one instant her gaze found the gap in the shutters and held it.

She did not call out. She did not say *help me*. She looked at Zephyrine with eyes gone milky-bright and terrified, and then—gods, and *then*—she shook her head. Once. Small. Almost gentle.

*Don't,* the shake of the head said. *Don't you dare.*

Then the wardens pulled her around the corner by the well, toward the gatehouse and the wagons, and her voice went on a little longer—*ask the apothecary, ask anyone*—and then it didn't, and the lane was quiet again, and the quiet was the loudest thing Zephyrine had ever heard.

They stood at the window a long time. The candle burned down. Somewhere beyond the rooftops, the first stars came out over the Citadel, indifferent and clean.

Fifteen years, Zephyrine thought. Fifteen years of being careful, of being small, of being *forgettable*—and it had bought Maren nothing. Their silence had not saved her. Their caution had not slowed the wardens' boots by a single step. All those years of folding themselves away, and the crown had simply waited, patient as rot, until the political winds turned and the hunting could begin again—and when it began, it did not begin with the powerful, the hidden masters of craft the proclamations implied. It began with a seventy-year-old woman and her pot of nightshade.

The arithmetic was simple, and Zephyrine had always been good with maps, which is to say with seeing what is actually where.

If they stayed, they would be taken—not because they were careless, but because silver loosens tongues, and tongues do not need truth to wag. Someone had already talked about Maren; someone would talk about the trinket-seller from the Saltmarket, the one whose little brass suns seemed to warm the fingers longer than brass should. Zephyrine's stall had served half the city. Half the city knew their face. In thirty days, their face would be worth five pieces of silver, and Highmoor had never in its history produced thirty people willing to go hungry rather than earn five pieces of silver.

And worse—if they stayed and were taken, they would not merely burn. Zephyrine's blood was not Maren's blood. What slept in Zephyrine was not hedge-craft, not knucklebone fortunes, not whispered charms for aching hips. They had spent fifteen years refusing to name it even to themselves, but refusal had never once diminished it, and lately—these last months, as the seasons turned—it had been stirring like something rolling over in sleep, warming, waking, growing heavier in their veins with each passing week. If the wardens ever laid hands on them and felt what was actually there—

They could not finish the thought. They did not need to. Some doors you do not have to open to know the room behind them is on fire.

Zephyrine turned from the window and looked at their life laid out in the candlelight: the worktable with its inks and brushes, the drawer of finished maps—realms with names like Vessarine and the Glasswater Reach, places no crown could tax and no warden could ride to—the little brass suns waiting to be sold as trinkets, the single chair, the single cup, the single everything. Fifteen years of a life built like a hiding place. And it had all been, they understood now, not a refuge but a delay. A long breath held. The proclamation had only told them what they had always known in the marrow: that concealment is not safety. It is just a slower way of dying.

Their hands had stopped trembling. That was how they knew.

Dread was still there—it would always be there, a cold stone lodged beneath the ribs—but dread, given enough weight, does something strange. It settles. It compacts. Under pressure, terror becomes something denser and more useful, the way soft carbon becomes diamond, the way grief becomes purpose. Zephyrine sat down at the worktable and, with hands that no longer shook, drew from the drawer the oldest map they owned: a chart of the riverways south of the city, inked years ago in a different ink, showing channels that appeared on no crown survey because no crown surveyor could see them. The Wraithmarsh spread across its lower half in careful hatching, and threading through the hatching, visible only to someone who knew how to look, a safe road.

Somewhere out there was a crew that ran that road. Smugglers, the market called them—river-rats, salt-thieves. Zephyrine had drawn a map for them once, years back, in a moment of foolish kindness, and they had paid their debt with a nod across the Saltmarket ever since, and that nod had been the closest thing to friendship Zephyrine had permitted themselves in fifteen years.

Thirty days. The moon would turn full in thirty days, and the hunt would begin, and the fires would be lit, and Maren's name would be the first of many.

Zephyrine rolled the map, bound it with cord, and slid it inside their coat, against their heart, where the other secret lived.

Tomorrow they would sell their last trinkets. Tomorrow they would wind their way to the Saltmarket and find the crew with the black-sailed barge. Tomorrow they would gamble fifteen years of silence on the mercy of strangers.

Tonight, they opened the shutters wide—for the first time in living memory, wide—and let the night air move through the rooms of their small, suffocating, carefully invisible life. Far above the Citadel, the moon hung thin as a paring, a pale crescent with twenty-nine nights of fattening ahead of it.

"Count them if you like," Zephyrine said to it quietly. "I intend to be gone before you're round."

Below in the lane, a dog barked at nothing. And far off toward the river, faint as a rumor, came the creak of rigging and the slap of dark water against a hull—the sound, though Zephyrine could not yet have known it, of the Saltmarket smugglers loading cargo by lantern-light, and of the road out of Highmoor preparing, unknowingly, to receive them.
