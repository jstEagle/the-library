## 4. What the Maps Remember

The river called the Liss ran brown and slow out of Highmoor, carrying with it the smell of the city—coal smoke and tanneries and the sweet rot of the Saltmarket—and Zephyrine stood at the stern rail until the last spire of the Gate district sank below the tree line, and even then they kept standing there, as if the city might rise up behind them like a drowned thing surfacing for air.

It did not. The river bent, the banks closed in with alder and willow, and Highmoor was gone.

"You'll want to come away from that rail," said a voice at their shoulder. Sef, the healer—small, sharp-eyed, her grey hair bound back with a strip of red cloth. She pressed a tin cup into Zephyrine's hands. "River water in this stretch will put worms in your belly before you've finished swallowing. Drink that. It's willowbark and something else."

"What is the something else?"

"Trade secret." Her mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "You'll find I keep a great many of those."

Zephyrine drank. It was bitter and then, strangely, warm, spreading down through their chest like a coal set under cold hands. They had not realized how cold they were until the warmth came.

Behind them, the barge went about its business. It was a long, low vessel, patched and repatched, its hull painted once some cheerful green now faded to the color of old pond water, and it moved through the current with the unhurried confidence of something that had been doing this work since before anyone aboard was born. Crates of salted fish. Bolts of cloth of no particular provenance. Jars of honey whose labels had been soaked off. Brannoc moved among the cargo like a bear among beehives—careful, enormous, humming to himself in a low register that Zephyrine felt more than heard—and Odile Vane stood at the tiller with one boot hooked on the rail, steering by some instinct older than charts.

*Captain Odile Vane,* Zephyrine thought, watching her. *Who owes me nothing and saved me anyway.*

Except she did owe them something. That was the arrangement. That was the only reason Zephyrine Whitlock, cartographer, fugitive, liar of fifteen years' standing, was standing on this deck drinking willowbark instead of hanging from the Gate in a cage built for people exactly their size.

They would have to tell her soon what they were offering. And they would have to decide how much of it was true.

* * *

Odile called a halt at dusk, in a bend of the river where the willows grew thick enough to hide the barge's lantern-glow. Supper was barley and river eel, caught by Brannoc with his bare hands and an expression of profound contentment, cooked over a small fire on a flat stone that had clearly served this purpose for years—it was worn smooth in the middle, blackened at the edges, a stone with a history.

There was a fourth member of the crew whom Zephyrine had met only briefly: Tam, a wiry young man with quick eyes and quicker fingers, who ate fast and watched everyone eat faster. He'd shaken Zephyrine's hand at the dock with a grip like a fish hook and said, "So you're the mapmaker Odile's been mooning over for three years," and received a look from the captain that could have curdled milk.

Now he sat across the fire, turning his eel on its skewer, and asked the question Zephyrine had been dreading since the Saltmarket.

"So what did you do?" he said. "To bring the wardens down on you. Nobody runs a man through half the Saltmarket for jaywalking."

"Tam." Brannoc's voice was gentle but it filled the space between the trees like low thunder.

"No, I want to know. We're all on this boat together now. If there's a price on their head, I'd like to know how big."

"There isn't a price," Zephyrine said. "Not yet."

"Not *yet* being the important words." Tam pointed his skewer at them. "See, that's what I mean. You talk like someone who knows how these things go. Wardens don't chase a person through the Saltmarket unless somebody's paid them to care."

"They chased me because I ran," Zephyrine said. Which was true. It was also, they were becoming aware, a skill they had developed—the truth shaped like an answer, hollow inside. Fifteen years of practice. A lie made entirely of true things was still a lie; it just took longer to catch.

"And why did you run?"

"Because they were going to search my shop." Zephyrine looked into the fire. "And there are things in my shop that the crown has outlawed."

The silence stretched. Somewhere downriver, a night bird called, three notes, like a question asked twice and answered never.

"Charms," Sef said quietly. Not a question either.

Zephyrine looked up. The healer was watching them with those sharp grey eyes, and there was something in her face—not surprise. Recognition, maybe. Or the beginning of it.

"I sell trinkets," Zephyrine said. "Souvenirs. Little brass compasses that always point home. Knotted cords for safe travel. That sort of thing."

"That sort of thing," Sef repeated, and let it lie there between them, unexamined, like a card left face-down on the table.

Odile had said nothing through all of this. She sat slightly apart from the fire, sharpening a knife against a whetstone with slow, even strokes, *shk, shk, shk*, and when the conversation died she did not revive it. But when Zephyrine glanced over, they found her already looking at them, and her gaze had the particular weight of a woman doing arithmetic.

*She knows,* Zephyrine thought. *Or she suspects. And suspicion is a debt too.*

* * *

They cast off again before dawn, because Odile did not believe in sleeping where wardens might think to look, and the second day on the Liss was grey and drizzling, mist lying on the water in long white ribbons. Zephyrine spent it below, in the narrow cabin that had been given over to them—a bunk, a shelf, a lamp that swung gently with the river's motion—and unrolled their life.

Their maps, that is. The real ones. Not the trinket-maps of Highmoor sold to tourists, not the honest-and-therefore-false surveys commissioned by merchants who wanted to know where the roads went. These were the others. The ones drawn in ink mixed with a drop of their own blood, because blood remembered what the hand alone forgot. Maps of realms that existed only through magic: the Glasswood, where the trees rang like bells when the wind moved; the Sunken Choir, a cathedral drowned beneath a lake that appeared on no survey and sang on certain nights; the Amber Road, which walked itself a few miles further west every year, so that any map of it more than a season old was a map of somewhere else.

Fifteen years of drawing these in secret, hiding each finished sheet beneath the false bottom of a merchant's chest, telling themselves they drew them only to stay sane, only to keep the gift alive the way one keeps a fire alive in a banked hearth—small, contained, harmless. Never selling them. Never showing them. Because a map of an enchanted realm, in a city where enchantment was outlawed, was not paper. It was evidence. It was a confession written in your own hand.

And now they were going to hand one to a smuggler captain in exchange for their life.

Zephyrine sat with the Wraithmarsh chart across their knees for a long time before they could make themselves roll it up and carry it above.

* * *

Odile was at the tiller, as she seemed to be at every hour Zephyrine looked for her. Rain beaded on her oilskin hood. Up close she was younger than her voice suggested—mid-thirties perhaps, with a sun-weathered face and a scar running through one eyebrow, and eyes the flat grey-green of the river itself.

"The marsh," Zephyrine said, without preamble, because preamble had never once made a hard conversation easier. "We can't take the river road south. The wardens will have posts at Fennbridge by tomorrow, and the ferry crossings after that. If we're going out of crown territory, it has to be the Wraithmarsh."

Odile's knife-hand paused. "The Wraithmarsh is a graveyard with water in it."

"It's passable. There's a way through."

"People have been saying that for two hundred years. The marsh eats them, and the saying continues." She turned back to the tiller, then stopped herself, and looked at Zephyrine properly. "You're telling me you know a way."

"I'm telling you I can draw you one."

For a long moment the only sound was the river and the rain. Then Odile said, "Show me."

Zephyrine led her below, spread the chart on the little table, weighted its corners with two cups and a coil of rope. Odile leaned over it, and Zephyrine watched her face as she read it—the way a smuggler reads, not the pretty parts first but the dangerous ones. The channels marked in blue. The solid ground marked in a color that didn't exist anywhere in nature, a faint luminous gold that seemed to shift when you weren't looking directly at it.

"This ink," Odile said slowly. "It moves."

"Tides," Zephyrine said. "The marsh breathes. What's dry at dawn is flooded by dusk. Most maps show you where the land was. This one shows you where it will be."

"How?"

"It's a trade secret," Zephyrine said, and heard Sef's voice in their own mouth, and almost smiled.

Odile did not smile. She traced the route with one finger—down the Liss to the black delta, into the marsh along a channel marked *the Eel's Throat*, past islands named in Zephyrine's small precise hand: *the Drowned Orchard. The Bell-Cairn. Saint Nobody's Rest.* Three days' travel, if the weather held and the marsh held and the crew held.

"And what do you want for it?"

"Passage. Out of crown lands. Hollow Court, or beyond if Hollow Court doesn't suit."

"That's all? A man runs from wardens, risks his neck crossing half of Highmoor, and all he wants is a boat ride?" Odile straightened. Her eyes were very steady. "I've been traded with before, cartographer. I know when the other side of the scale is holding something heavy. You're giving us a map worth more than this whole barge, and asking for passage. Either you're a fool—which the Saltmarket says you're not—or the map costs you something to give. Or—" and here her voice dropped, quiet and level, the way water drops before it freezes— "you're not giving us everything. You're giving us the part that keeps you useful, and keeping the part that keeps you *safe*. And I'd take no offense at that, mind. It's good smuggling practice. But I'd like to know which it is, because I've got four people on this boat, and I've known three of them long enough to bet their lives on. You I've known two days."

The lamp swung. Outside, the rain thickened against the deck above.

"If I told you everything," Zephyrine said, "you'd put me off at the next bank."

"Probably," Odile agreed, without a flicker of shame. "Depends what it was."

"And if I told you nothing, you'd put me off now."

"Also probably."

"Then we understand each other." Zephyrine held her gaze. "The map is true. Every line of it. It will get us through the marsh alive—that much I promise you, and I've kept promises for fifteen years without breaking one. The rest—" They touched the edge of the chart, the luminous gold line of dry ground, and felt, as they always felt, the faint answering warmth under their skin, the gift stirring like a cat waking. "The rest is mine. For now."

"*For now*," Odile repeated. She looked at the map a moment longer—at the little gold islands, at the channel called the Eel's Throat—and Zephyrine saw something move behind her eyes, some recalculation, some ledger being balanced in a way she didn't yet like.

Then she rolled up the chart and handed it back.

"Keep it. You navigate; I steer. If we live through the marsh, maybe you'll decide I'm worth the rest of the story." She climbed the ladder to the deck, then paused, half in the rain-grey light. "One thing, cartographer."

"Yes?"

"That ink. The gold. My brother used to draw like that."

Zephyrine went still. "Your brother?"

"Aldric." She said the name the way one sets down something heavy. "Before the crown got him. He'd sit for hours making maps of places that couldn't be, and our father beat him for wasting paper, and he kept doing it anyway." She pulled her hood up. "He draws different now. Straighter lines. Colder ones. Anyway." She climbed out into the rain.

Zephyrine sat alone in the swaying lamplight, heart hammering, and did not move for a long time.

*Aldric.* Aldric Vane. The name surfaced from the gossip of the Saltmarket like a body from deep water: Lord Aldric Vane, the crown's new inquisitor, the man appointed to lead the hunt for the gifted. The man whose proclamations were nailed to every gate in Highmoor.

The captain's brother. The hunter's sister. And somewhere ahead, in the wet dark of the Wraithmarsh, a route that only magic could chart, carrying a cargo that only silence could protect.

Zephyrine looked down at their own hands, at the faint gold stain still ghosting the creases of their fingers where the ink had soaked in, and thought: *the maps remember everything. That's the trouble with them. So do I.*

Above, through the deck boards, they heard Odile call the course change—south, toward the delta, toward the marsh—and heard Brannoc answer, and heard the rain come harder, and beneath all of it, so faint they might have imagined it, a sound like a bell ringing far away under the water.

The marsh, singing welcome.

Or warning.
