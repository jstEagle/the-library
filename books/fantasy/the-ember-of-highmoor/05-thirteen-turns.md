## 5. Thirteen Turns

The barge moved through the river country like a slow thought, and Zephyrine could not stop thinking.

Three days out of Highmoor, and the city had not yet loosened its grip on them. It clung in smaller ways now—the smell of coal smoke that seemed to live in their hair, the habit of flinching at hoofbeats, the way their eyes still swept every stranger's face for the particular hunger of an informant. The river had no such hungers. It only carried them, brown and patient, past willows that trailed their fingers in the current and herons that stood like sentries who had forgotten what they were guarding.

Zephyrine sat at the stern with a board across their knees and drew. It was what they did instead of sleeping, instead of praying, instead of screaming. The map taking shape beneath their charcoal was the Wraithmarsh—not the version sold in shops, all vague hatching and hopeful guesses, but the true one: the drowned causeways, the channels that breathed open and shut with the tide, the standing stones where the mist gathered thickest and oldest. They drew it from memory, and memory was a kind of magic too, though no warden had ever thought to outlaw it.

"You draw like you're apologizing to the paper."

Sef dropped onto the cargo crate beside them, a tin cup in each hand. She pressed one into Zephyrine's grip without asking—bitter tea, over-steeped, the way everything on this barge was. Sharp edges softened by use.

"I'm not," Zephyrine said.

"No?" Sef nodded at the map. "You've gone over that channel line four times. Either you're apologizing or you're stalling."

Zephyrine looked down. Sef was right; the line had been finished three passes ago. They lifted the charcoal away and left the mark as it was.

"Odile wants it by tomorrow," they said.

"Odile wants everything by yesterday." Sef blew on her tea. "She'll get it when she gets it, and she'll pretend she never doubted you, and you'll both pretend this is a business arrangement and not whatever it actually is."

"And what is it?"

"Ask me again when we're past the marsh." She drank, grimaced, drank again. Her eyes went downriver, toward the gray smudge where the Wraithmarsh waited like a held breath. "Brannoc says the mist there sings sometimes. His grandmother used to say the gifted made it, back before the purges. A whole village of them, drowned by the crown's order, and their songs soaked into the fog and never came back out."

Zephyrine's hand tightened around the cup. "Do you believe that?"

"I believe Brannoc believes it, and I believe his grandmother wasn't the sort to invent things for comfort." Sef stood, brushing off her trousers. "Get some sleep tonight, cartographer. Marshes don't forgive the tired."

She went forward, and Zephyrine stayed, watching the water unspool behind them, carrying Highmoor away one mile at a time—and carrying them toward something they could not yet name.

* * *

The prophecy came to them the way truth always came: sideways, disguised as something else.

It was evening, the second night on the river, and Odile had broken out the trade goods to take inventory—a smuggler's ritual, half accounting and half superstition, because Tam swore the stock counted itself wrong if you didn't look at it under lamplight. Crates opened along the deck: salted fish, dyed wool, cheap tin mirrors, and a small chest of books salvaged from a burned manor upriver, which Odile intended to sell by weight to a bookbinder in Hollow Court who didn't ask where his leather came from.

Zephyrine had offered to help, and Odile had grunted assent, and so they found themselves elbow-deep in other people's ruined libraries while the crew worked around them in the amber circle of the lanterns. Most of the books were water-swollen beyond saving. Some were ledgers. One was a psalter with the prayers scraped out and someone else's handwriting crammed into the margins.

And one was thin, bound in cracked green leather, its title worn to ghosts: *A Chronicle of the Turning Moons.*

Zephyrine should have put it in the salvage pile. Instead they opened it.

The chronicle was old—older than the crown, older than the war, written by some monkish hand that had believed the world would keep its records forever. It tracked the moons of the year the way farmers tracked seasons, each turn annotated with omens and harvests and small human griefs. *In the fourth turn, the river froze late. In the seventh, a child born with silver eyes in Alderford, blessed or cursed according to the fear of the beholder.* Page after page of a world that had once been allowed to be strange.

Then, near the end, the handwriting changed. Newer. Hurried. As if the writer knew the ink might be confiscated before it dried.

*They say the moon must make thirteen turns before the Ember wakes. Twelve are the moon's own, given at its making. The thirteenth is owed—a debt of light the sky has carried since the Burning. When it comes round full, the Ember of the old bloodline will wake in the last vessel of that line, and what sleeps shall stand up, and what was stolen shall cry out to be returned.*

*The king's men burn the genealogies first. Remember that. A throne built on theft fears nothing so much as a family tree.*

Zephyrine read it twice. Three times. The lantern seemed to dim around the words, or perhaps the words simply took all the light there was.

Thirteen turns. But the moon made twelve turns. Everyone knew that. Twelve full moons between midwinter and midwinter, and then the year began again. There was no thirteenth—

Except.

Except that the old calendars—the ones outlawed along with everything else—had counted differently. Zephyrine had seen one once, in a collection they'd been hired to appraise, a wheel of thirteen months with the last one named in letters almost rubbed away. *Embermonth,* it had been called. The month that belonged to no season. The month the new calendar erased, folding its days quietly into the twelfth moon as if a month could be murdered by arithmetic.

This year, the almanacs whispered—if you knew how to listen—held an extra fullness. A thirteenth turning, falling at winter's edge. The scholars called it a calendrical correction. The priests called it nothing at all, because the priests had been forbidden to speak of it since the founding.

And Zephyrine had known, in the wordless way the body knows a storm is coming, since the day the proclamation went up on the Gate. Since the neighbor was dragged away. Something in them had been counting down ever since, ticking like a clock hidden in the walls.

"Find anything worth selling?"

Odile's voice came from close behind, and Zephyrine's whole body went rigid—they felt the green book seem to heat against their palms, felt their face arrange itself into blankness with the practiced speed of fifteen years.

"Ledgers, mostly," they said. "Water damage. Your bookbinder won't pay much."

Odile looked at them a moment too long. She had a way of looking that felt like being weighed at a toll station—she was deciding what you were carrying and whether it was worth the fee. Then she shrugged and moved on down the crates, and Zephyrine slid the green chronicle inside their coat, against their ribs, where their heart was doing something arrhythmic and afraid.

That night they did not sleep. They lay in the narrow bunk beneath the aft deck with the book open on their chest, reading by the sliver of moonlight through the hatch, and the prophecy sat in them like swallowed glass.

*The Ember of the old bloodline will wake in the last vessel of that line.*

There was only one bloodline. Everyone who remembered anything remembered that much, even if they remembered it wrong—remembered it the way the crown taught it, the gifted rebels and their terrible Ember, the war, the righteous purge. Fifteen years Zephyrine had lived inside the lie, wearing it like a coat, and they had told themselves the Ember was a story's ending. That it had guttered out with the last of the old line, however long ago that was. That history was a door already closed.

But doors closed from the outside can be opened from within.

Zephyrine set the book aside and held their hands up in the moonlight.

They had always known there was something more in them than charm-work. The little magics—the warming charms sewn into trinkets, the mending spells, the maps that reached realms no ship could sail—those were candle flames. But underneath, banked and patient, there was something that was not a candle at all. Something they had spent fifteen years not looking at directly, the way you do not look at the sun, the way you do not say the name of the thing that would destroy you if you named it.

Now, lying in the dark with a dead man's prophecy cooling beside them, they made themselves look.

They let the wall down—one brick, just one, the innermost brick, the one they had mortared in place at fourteen years old and never touched since.

The Ember turned over in its sleep.

It was not fire, exactly. It was the memory of fire—the warmth of a hearth in a house that no longer existed, the glow of a forge where ancestors had hammered light into shape, the slow red patience of coals that outlast the blaze. It stretched, the way a sleeper stretches, and for one endless heartbeat Zephyrine felt it touch the edges of them, feel the shape of its prison, and know—*know*, with a recognition older than language—that the walls were thinner than they had been.

Zephyrine slammed the brick back into place. Their hands were shaking. Sweat stood cold on their spine despite the river chill.

Below decks, Brannoc turned in his sleep, the timbers creaking under his great weight, and somewhere forward Tam coughed, and the river went on talking to itself in the dark, indifferent.

*The last vessel of that line.*

They were the last. They had known that too, always, the way you know you're the last leaf on a branch without counting the others. Grandmother Ilsa, who had hummed songs in a language she claimed was "just old-fashioned." Mother, who had died insisting the family had come from nowhere, from nothing, from a long line of honest nobodies. All of them gone now, all of them silent, leaving Zephyrine alone with a spark that had been handed down like a curse through generations of people who had chosen silence over stakes and hiding over history.

Fifteen years of concealment, and it had never once occurred to Zephyrine that they were concealing the very thing the whole hunt was shaped around—or that the hunt had been shaped around it all along, longer than any proclamation, longer than their lifetime. The crown had been searching for them since before they were born. Every purge, every burning, every law—it was all one long net, cast generation after generation, waiting for the fish that glowed.

And in one moon's turning, the fish would glow whether it wished to or not.

Zephyrine pressed their palms against their eyes until sparks bloomed in the darkness—ordinary sparks, mere optics, mere biology—and tried to breathe.

They thought of Odile above them, sleeping with a knife under her pillow even among friends. Of Sef's sharp eyes missing nothing. Of Brannoc, who had lost a sister to the last purge and carried the grief like ballast. Of Tam, whose debts were a smell the others pretended not to notice. Four people who had pulled them out of a Saltmarket alley on the strength of an old debt, who were about to risk the Wraithmarsh on the strength of a map, who had fed them and laughed near them and made something in Zephyrine's chest go soft for the first time in fifteen years.

What would that softness be worth, when they learned what slept in the cartographer's blood? What price would Odile weigh them at then?

Every instinct Zephyrine owned—the instincts that had kept them alive through fifteen years of small lies and averted eyes—said the same thing: tell no one. Burn the page. Bury the knowledge deeper than the brick. Ride out the thirteenth moon somewhere far from everyone, alone, the way their whole line had survived: by being no one, by being nothing, by being a secret kept so well even the keeper forgot.

But another voice, quieter, newer—born perhaps three days ago in a Saltmarket alley, in the grip of a giant's hand hauling them out of sight—asked the question Zephyrine had been dodging since the Gate:

*Alone where? The crown is hunting the whole river. And the thing inside you doesn't care how far you run. It's waking anyway.*

Outside, the moon cleared the treeline, white and complete, beginning its long climb toward the thirteenth turning. Its light fell through the hatch and lay across the bunk like a hand.

Zephyrine watched it and understood, finally, the true shape of their terror. It was not death. They had made peace with the possibility of death years ago; every gifted soul in Highmoor carried that peace folded small against the heart. No—the terror was this: that they had finally, accidentally, against every rule they lived by, begun to belong to someone. And the thing they carried would take even that. Would make them not a person but a prize, not a friend but a fuse, not Zephyrine but *the Ember*—a weapon with a heartbeat, hunted by a king and coveted by an inquisitor, and loved by no one who knew the whole truth.

The moon climbed. The river ran. Toward morning, Zephyrine rose, took the green chronicle up on deck, and stood a long time at the rail with the book in their hands, the water black below, the mist of the Wraithmarsh already visible ahead—a pale country waiting, where songs were said to soak into the fog and never come out.

It would be so easy. One motion, and the prophecy would be pulp in the current, and the secret would be theirs alone to carry, as it had always been.

Instead, Zephyrine folded the book shut, tucked it back inside their coat, and went below to finish the map.

Because Odile Vane was going to steer her barge and her crew into the most haunted wetland in the realm on the strength of Zephyrine's word, and Zephyrine could not—could not—keep drawing lines on paper for people who trusted them, while the largest line of all stayed hidden.

They would not tell them everything. Not yet. Not the Ember, not the bloodline, not the countdown ticking in their veins. But standing in the grey dawn with the charcoal in their hand, Zephyrine made themselves a promise with teeth in it: before the thirteenth moon came full, the crew would know *something*. Whatever fragment of truth they could afford. Because a lie kept whole will kill you, but a truth shared in pieces might, just possibly, keep you alive.

They bent over the map of the Wraithmarsh and began to draw—and paused, charcoal hovering, as a sound drifted across the water from the mist ahead.

Faint. Distant. Almost lost under the lap of the river.

Singing.
