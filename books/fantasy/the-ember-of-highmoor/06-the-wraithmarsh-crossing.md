## 6. The Wraithmarsh Crossing

Brannoc had crossed the Wraithmarsh eleven times in his life, and every time it felt like walking through the memory of a drowned world.

The barge slid along a channel no wider than a cart road, hemmed in by black reeds that grew taller than a man and whispered against each other even when there was no wind. There was always wind in the marsh. It came up out of the peat itself, cold and smelling of iron and old rot, and it moved the reeds in long slow waves so that from the bow it looked as though the whole world were breathing.

He stood at the stern with one hand on the sweep oar, though Odile had told him twice now to sit down and rest his shoulder. The wound from the Saltmarket — a warden's blade had opened him from elbow to armpit before Sef dragged them both behind a fish stall — pulled tight with every stroke, but he found he preferred the ache. Pain kept a man honest. Pain reminded him that he was still here, still moving, while other people were not.

Zephyrine sat near the bow on a coil of rope, knees drawn up, the map spread across their lap despite the damp. They'd been studying it since dawn without seeming to read it at all. Their eyes kept going past the inked lines to the reeds, to the water, to the grey smear of sky where the sun should have been and wasn't. Watching. Listening. Brannoc had spent enough years around frightened people to know the posture of someone listening for hounds.

"You'll wear a groove in that paper," he said, keeping his voice low. Sound carried strangely over the marsh — sometimes not at all, sometimes too far.

Zephyrine flinched. Then smoothed the map with both palms, an oddly formal gesture, like a scholar calming a restless page. "I'm checking our position."

"We're in the middle of a swamp." Brannoc nodded at the endless sameness of it. "There is no position."

"There's always a position." A ghost of a smile touched their mouth and vanished. "The channel bends east in half a mile. After that we leave the warden roads entirely."

*Warden roads.* Brannoc turned the phrase over. He'd grown up calling them deer paths, or nothing at all. But Zephyrine spoke of the marsh like a city — streets and districts and thoroughfares, a place with architecture only they could see. Fifteen years drawing maps of realms that didn't exist, Sef said. Brannoc wondered sometimes if that was true, or if the realms existed fine and it was only Highmoor that refused to believe in them.

He shipped the oar and let the current take them. The barge drifted, hull whispering against the reed stems, and the silence settled in around all four of them like a fifth passenger.

Odile was asleep below decks, or pretending to be. She hadn't slept properly since the proclamation went up at the Gate — Brannoc had heard her pacing the hold at midnight, boot heels on planking, counting her silver like a woman doing sums she already knew would come out wrong. Tam was at the mast, whittling something small and useless, his knife moving in quick guilty strokes. And Sef crouched amidships grinding herbs in a mortar, the smell of yarrow and comfrey sharp over the marsh-rot, dressing Brannoc's arm without asking permission.

"Hold still," she muttered, which was what she always said, and what he never did.

He held still anyway. His eyes stayed on Zephyrine.

* * *

His sister had loved water like this.

Not this water — Maren had been afraid of deep water, actually, ever since she fell through the ice on the Fenmere when she was six — but she'd loved the *idea* of it. Marshes, fens, the drowned forests north of Highmoor where the trees stood knee-deep in black mirror and the herons stalked like patient ghosts. She used to say the marsh was the one place the crown couldn't follow you, because the crown couldn't bear to get its boots muddy.

She'd been right about that, in the end. They hadn't followed her into the marsh. They'd waited until she came home.

Brannoc closed his eyes and let the memory come, because he'd learned that shutting it out only made it louder later. Maren at fourteen, sitting cross-legged on the dock behind their father's tannery, holding a river-smoothed stone in her cupped hands and making it glow. Not firelight-glow. A light with no heat in it, pale gold, like the last hour of a summer evening caught inside a rock. She could make it pulse. She could make it sing, very faintly, a note like a wet finger on a glass rim, and the fish under the dock would rise toward the sound.

"Show me again," he'd beg, every time, and she'd laugh at him — big brother, twelve years old and already bigger than their father, begging his little sister for magic tricks — and she'd show him again.

That was before the last purge. Before the wardens came down Tanners' Row with their grey cloaks and their iron collars and their list of names. Before the neighbors who'd bought bread from their mother for twenty years stood in doorways and watched, and said nothing, and afterward said *we had no choice*, which was the same thing as saying nothing.

Maren hadn't run. That was the part that lived in him like a splinter. She could have run — she was fast, she knew the fen paths better than any warden, and their father had the boat ready and the tarps loose. But there'd been a younger girl on the row, the cooper's daughter, who'd been named on the same list, and Maren wouldn't leave without her, and the cooper's daughter was slow, and by the time they reached the boat—

Brannoc opened his eyes.

The marsh breathed around him. Reeds whispering. Water lapping the hull. Somewhere far off, a bittern booming its low wooden note across the flats.

Fifteen years. He'd carried her fifteen years the way a man carries a stone in his chest — not proudly, not deliberately, just constantly, the way the body carries the heart. He'd joined Odile's crew two years after, because a smuggler's life meant never standing in a doorway watching, never being one of the silent ones. He'd told himself that was enough. That hauling contraband past crown tolls and lying to tax men was a kind of penance, a kind of war.

It wasn't. He'd known it wasn't. Penance that costs you nothing is just habit.

But this morning, watching Zephyrine sit at the bow of the barge with their shoulders curled inward like a person trying to take up less space than a person occupies, Brannoc had felt the stone in his chest shift for the first time in fifteen years. Not lighten. Shift. Turn over, the way soil turns before planting.

Because he knew what he was looking at. He was looking at Maren. Not her face, not her gift — Zephyrine's magic, whatever it was, stayed hidden so deep even Sef's sharp eyes hadn't found it yet. But the *posture*. The careful hands. The way Zephyrine watched every stranger on the towpath and catalogued every exit. The exhausting, permanent work of being prey in a country that called itself civilized.

Someone had to stand between that and the wardens. Someone had to be the doorway that didn't stay shut.

* * *

The channel bent east exactly where Zephyrine said it would.

Brannoc noticed, and filed the noticing away, and noticed himself filing it away, and almost laughed. This was what trust looked like when it started: not a grand decision but a series of small confirmations, each one a single plank laid across dark water. *They said the channel bends. The channel bent.* Plank. *They said the reeds would thin past the dead alder.* The reeds thinned. Plank.

By midday they were running with the sweep raised and the sail down, drifting on the current through a drowned forest where the alders stood like skeletons in black water and the light came down green and dim as light through sea. Zephyrine navigated without instruments. They'd close their eyes sometimes, head tilted, lips moving slightly — counting, maybe, or reciting — and then open them and say, "Bear left at the split ahead," and the split would be there, hidden behind a curtain of willow, and the left branch would run true.

"Who taught you the marsh?" Sef asked at one point, not looking up from her mortar. Her voice was casual in the way Brannoc had learned meant anything but.

"No one," Zephyrine said. Too quickly. Then, softer: "I've walked it. Years ago. On foot."

"On foot," Sef repeated. "Through the Wraithmarsh. Where the ground eats people."

"I'm careful."

"Mm." Sef ground the pestle harder than the herbs required. Brannoc saw her glance at Zephyrine sidelong — the look of a woman assembling pieces — and he saw Zephyrine feel the look land and pretend it hadn't. Two people circling something neither would name yet. He decided it wasn't his business. Yet.

Tam broke the quiet instead, which was Tam's way. "Anyone else hear that?"

They all stilled. The barge drifted. Reeds whispered. Bittern boomed, distant.

"Hear what?" Odile said from the hatchway. She must have given up on sleep; there were shadows under her eyes like bruises.

"Sounded like—" Tam cocked his head. "Oars. Lots of them. Behind us."

Brannoc listened with his whole body, the way his father had taught him to listen for cattle-thieves on the fen farms: not with your ears first but with your skin, feeling for the wrongness in the air. For a long moment there was nothing. Then, underneath the whisper of the reeds, so faint it might have been imagination —

Rhythm. Regular. The dip-and-draw of many oars, muffled by distance and water, coming up the channel behind them.

"How far?" Odile asked. All the sleepiness gone from her voice, replaced by something bright and hard.

"Half a league," Brannoc said. "Maybe less. The sound carries strange over open water — could be closer."

"Wardens don't row barges," Sef said flatly. "They ride horses along the towpath and complain about it."

"Then it's not wardens." Odile was already moving, kicking open the cargo hatch, dragging out the oilskin bundles that held everything worth saving. "Which means it's toll-runners, or press-gangs, or worse. Either way I want us off this channel. Cartographer!" The word cracked across the water like a whip, and Brannoc saw Zephyrine go rigid — saw the flinch, the old flinch, the one that said *that word has been shouted at me by people who wanted me dead* — and then saw them master it, unfold the map, bend over it.

"There's a side channel." Zephyrine's finger traced a line westward, into the deepest heart of the drowned forest, where the map's ink faded into blank paper marked only with three small careful words: *here be silence*. "Two hundred yards past the leaning willow. It's narrow. The barge will scrape."

"Scrape I can live with," Odile said. "Chains I can't. Take us in."

* * *

The leaning willow announced itself a quarter mile early — a vast pale shape rising out of the green gloom, fallen halfway across the channel, its living branches still trailing in the water like the hair of a drowned woman. Past it, the side channel opened: barely wider than the barge, roofed with interlaced branches, so low in places that Brannoc had to flatten himself against the deck.

The scraping was terrible. The hull groaned against submerged roots; something below shrieked like a nail across slate. Twice they stopped entirely, wedged, and Brannoc put his shoulder — the bad one — against the overhanging bank and pushed, muscles burning, until the barge lurched free and slid onward into the dark.

And behind them, fainter and fainter, the rhythm of the oars went by. They heard it pass the mouth of the side channel — a moment when everyone froze, when Tam's knife stopped moving, when even the marsh seemed to hold its breath — and continue on up the main channel, chasing a barge that was no longer there.

Nobody spoke until the sound was gone entirely. Then Odile exhaled, long and slow, and said, "Cartographer. You're worth your weight in silver after all."

"It's nothing," Zephyrine said, but Brannoc saw their hands shaking as they rolled the map, saw them hide the tremor by pressing the roll flat against their chest. Adrenaline draining. Or something more than adrenaline. Fear, maybe, of a different kind — the fear of a person who knows that every rescue makes them more valuable, and more visible, and more dangerous to keep.

Brannoc climbed down into the hold, found the clay jug of small beer, and poured a cup. He carried it forward and held it out.

Zephyrine stared at it as if unsure of the protocol.

"You're shaking," Brannoc said. It wasn't an accusation. It was just a fact, stated plainly, the way he stated most things.

"I'm fine."

"You're not. Drink. Sugar in it helps." He'd learned that from Sef, who'd learned it from treating soldiers. "After the shaking stops, the sick feeling comes. The beer blunts it."

Zephyrine took the cup. Their fingers brushed his — cool, smaller than he'd expected — and for a moment they just held the cup without drinking, looking at him with an expression Brannoc couldn't fully read. Wariness, still. But underneath it, something startled. Something that looked, painfully, like a person who had forgotten what it felt like to be handed things without a price attached.

"Why are you kind to me?" they asked quietly.

Brannoc considered the question honestly, because it deserved honesty. Because of Maren, some voice in him said. Because of the stone in your chest. Because fifteen years ago you stood in a doorway and did nothing and you have been trying to walk out of that doorway ever since.

"Someone was kind to my sister once," he said. "When it mattered. I never got the chance to pay it back." He shrugged, and the shrug pulled his wound, and he ignored it. "So I pay it forward. That's all."

Zephyrine drank the beer. Said nothing. But when they handed the empty cup back, their eyes were wet, and they turned their face away quickly, toward the reeds, toward the green dark, and pretended to study the route ahead.

Brannoc went back to the sweep oar. And there, in the dripping tunnel of the side channel, with the wardens' oar-beats fading somewhere behind them and the thirteenth moon swelling unseen beyond the canopy, he made his decision the way he made all his decisions — quietly, completely, and without announcement.

Whatever Zephyrine was hiding — and they were hiding something; Brannoc had carried enough contraband to know a smuggled secret by its shape — whatever it was, it was heavier than a map. He'd seen the way Odile watched them, hungry with suspicion. He'd seen Sef's questions gathering like stormclouds. He'd seen the fear in Zephyrine that wasn't the ordinary fear of a hunted person but the specific, bottomless fear of a person carrying something that could burn down kingdoms.

He didn't care. Maren had glowed like a summer evening and died in an iron collar, and the world had called it justice, and Brannoc had spent fifteen years learning exactly how much he trusted the world's judgment.

If the truth came out, it would come out. Until then, he would stand in the doorway. He would be the one who didn't stay silent.

He was still turning that vow over in his chest like a warm stone when the barge cleared the drowned forest at dusk and Sef's voice came down from the bow, sharp with something that wasn't alarm exactly, but wasn't anything comfortable either:

"Zephyrine. Come here a moment. I want to ask you about these charms you sold my cousin in the Saltmarket — the ones that healed a broken wrist in a night." A pause, the length of a blade being drawn. "I want to know exactly what you're hiding aboard my ship."
