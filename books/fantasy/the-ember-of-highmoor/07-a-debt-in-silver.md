## 7. A Debt in Silver

Sef had counted the crew's wounds the way other people counted coins—by habit, by necessity, and with a private suspicion that the ledger never balanced in her favor.

Three days out of Highmoor, and the barge's sick-list read thus: Brannoc, rope burn across both palms from hauling them through the reed-choked narrows of the Wraithmarsh's outer fringe; Odile, a bruise blooming violet along her ribs where a warden's cudgel had found her before the fog took them; Tam, a fever that came and went like a tide, muttering about debts and silver; and Zephyrine Whitlock, who claimed nothing at all, which was itself a kind of symptom.

Sef noticed things. It was her trade and her curse. She noticed that their new passenger ate little and slept less, sitting upright against the barge's rail through the small hours as though sleep were a country they'd been exiled from. She noticed how Zephyrine flinched—not visibly, not rudely, but in some deep register—whenever anyone on the crew said the word *magic*, even as a joke, even as an obscenity. And she noticed, most damningly, that Brannoc's hands were healing too fast.

She'd dressed those burns herself on the first night, with comfrey and clean linen and the grudging tenderness she showed all wounds. By the second dawn, the angry red had faded to pink. By the third, when she peeled back the bandage to change it, the skin beneath was smooth as a child's.

"Brannoc," she said, holding his palm up to the grey morning light. "What have you been putting on this?"

He looked at his own hand with mild surprise, the way a man looks at weather. "Nothing. Feels fine."

"Skin doesn't knit like this. Not in three days. Not unless—" She stopped herself. The word sat unspoken between them like a coin no one wanted to touch first.

*Magic.* Outlawed. Burned-at-the-stake magic, dragged-from-your-bed-in-the-night magic, silver-for-your-neighbor's-name magic. Sef had spent thirty years in the city's underbelly stitching smugglers and dock-fighters, and in all that time she had learned one law deeper than the crown's: you did not say that word aloud, even in whispers, even alone, because the walls of Highmoor had ears and the ears had teeth.

But they weren't in Highmoor anymore. They were three days into the Wraithmarsh, wrapped in fog so thick the river seemed less a river than a rumor, and Brannoc's hand was healing like something out of the old stories, and there was only one person aboard who could have done it.

Sef folded the bandage away and said nothing. Yet.

* * *

She waited until evening, until Odile had taken the tiller and Tam had finally sweated himself into exhausted sleep below, until Brannoc stood at the prow humming his low, tuneless marsh-song and the fog had thinned enough to show the first stars, pale and uncertain, like informants deciding whether to come forward.

Zephyrine sat amidships, mending a tear in their coat by lamplight—or pretending to. The needle hadn't moved in a quarter hour. Sef had been watching.

She crossed the deck and set down her kit beside them, deliberately, the way she did before any examination. The clatter of iron instruments on wood made Zephyrine look up.

"Show me your hands," Sef said.

Zephyrine blinked. "My hands?"

"You've been rubbing your left thumb for two days. Same spot, same motion, every time you think no one's looking. That's either a nervous habit or a burn, and I've watched you long enough tonight to know it isn't nerves." She held out her palm. "Hands."

For a long moment Zephyrine didn't move. The lamplight caught their face—the sharp, careful face of someone who had practiced being unremarkable until the practice had eaten the person underneath—and Sef saw the calculation running behind their eyes, fast and frightened, weighing lies the way a gambler weighs cards.

Then, slowly, Zephyrine set down the mending and extended their left hand.

The thumb bore a mark Sef knew intimately: the silvery scald of a charm worked past its strength. She'd seen it on the rare few in the city's hidden corners who dabbled in what the crown called witchery—a cook who kept her stews from spoiling, a midwife whose hands ran warm, a lamplighter whose wicks never guttered. Small magics, survival magics, each one leaving its faint signature on the flesh like a debt recorded in a ledger.

Except Zephyrine's mark wasn't small. It spread from the thumb across the heel of the palm in a tracery of pale lines, old and layered, scar over scar over scar—fifteen years of small magics written on the skin like a confession nobody had ever been allowed to read.

"Well," Sef said quietly. "That answers one question."

"Does it?" Zephyrine's voice was level. Too level. A wall built in real time.

"It answers whether you're gifted. It doesn't answer why you healed Brannoc without asking, or why you're still hiding it from people who pulled you out of a warden's net at their own risk." Sef sat down across from them, knees nearly touching, and fixed them with the look she usually reserved for patients claiming they'd only had two drinks. "I stitched Odile's ribs last night. This morning she was breathing easy. I put a poultice on Tam's fever and it broke within the hour, and I'll thank you to know my poultices are good but they aren't *that* good. You've been working charms on this whole crew since we left the city, haven't you? Quietly. Without telling us."

The fog drifted past. Somewhere off the port side, a marsh-bird cried once and fell silent, as if ashamed of the noise.

"I paid a debt," Zephyrine said at last. "Your crew owed me for a map. Now I owe you for the rescue. I don't like owing."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you get."

Sef laughed—a short, hard sound with no humor in it. "You know what I did before I fell in with Odile Vane? Ten years in the Lantern District, patching the poor, because the guild healers wouldn't take clients who couldn't pay and the crown's physicians wouldn't touch anyone with soot under their nails. And do you know what I learned?" She leaned forward. "I learned that half my patients were hiding something. A charm sewn into a hem. A blessing whispered over a cradle. Little illegal mercies, everywhere, in every tenement, in every alley—and everyone pretending, all together, all at once, that none of it existed. Fifteen years of watching people lie with their whole lives, Whitlock. So don't tell me it's the only answer I get. I've heard every version of silence there is, and yours is just the newest dialect."

Something shifted in Zephyrine's face. Not softening exactly—more like a crack in ice, hairline and dangerous, the kind that means the thaw has started whether the frozen thing consents or not.

"You want honesty," Zephyrine said.

"I want the truth. Honesty's just truth with manners."

A ghost of a smile touched Zephyrine's mouth and vanished. They looked out at the dark water, at the fog unraveling around the lantern, and when they spoke again their voice had changed—lower, rougher, the voice of someone setting down a weight they'd carried so long they'd forgotten it was heavy.

"Yes. I'm gifted. I've been gifted since I was six years old, when I drew a map of my mother's garden on the back of a flour sack and the map showed a stream that wasn't there yet—and three days later, after the spring rains, the stream was. Exactly where I'd drawn it." Their thumb moved over the scarred palm, unconscious, tracing the ledger of years. "I thought every child could do it. Draw a place truly enough and the world would listen. My mother burned the sack and told me never to do it again, and I didn't understand why until the purges started, until I watched a boy from my street taken away in irons for lighting candles with a snap of his fingers. He was nine."

Sef said nothing. The river murmured against the hull.

"So I learned to be small," Zephyrine went on. "I learned to map the way ordinary cartographers map—badly, honestly, uselessly. And at night, when the shutters were closed, I drew the other maps. The ones that reach places that only exist through magic. The Glasswood. The Sunken Choir. Realms the crown insists are fables, because admitting they're real would mean admitting the gift is real, and the whole architecture of the throne depends on the gifted being liars and freaks." A breath. "And I sold charms. Trinkets, mostly—fever-knots, calm-stones, a thread of finding for a mother whose son wandered. Nothing that would light a warden's lamp. Just enough to keep the neighborhood alive. Just enough to feel like I wasn't entirely useless."

"The healing," Sef said. "Brannoc's hands. Odile's ribs."

"Small work. Mending-work. The body wants to heal; I just remind it of the way." Zephyrine's jaw tightened. "It leaves marks. As you see."

"Fifteen years of marks."

"Fifteen years of marks."

Sef studied them—the careful hands, the hollowed eyes, the posture of a person perpetually braced for a door to break open. She had seen that posture before, on fugitives and deserters and abused wives, on everyone who had learned that safety was a loan that could be called in at any hour. But she had never seen it worn so thoroughly, so *habitually*, that it stopped being armor and became skin.

"Do the others know?" she asked.

"Odile suspects I'm more than a mapmaker. Brannoc—" Zephyrine hesitated. "Brannoc looks at me sometimes like he's deciding something. I don't know what."

"He decided days ago," Sef said. "He told me you reminded him of his sister. He doesn't talk about her. When he does, it's in the past tense, and then he stops talking altogether." She let that sit. "So it's just me, then. The only one you've told."

"You asked."

"No, Whitlock. I cornered you. There's a difference, and the fact that you can't tell the difference is the saddest thing about you." Sef reached into her kit and drew out a small clay pot of salve, setting it in Zephyrine's scarred palm. "Marigold and tallow. For the burn-marks. Won't erase them—nothing erases them—but it eases the ache when the weather turns. I make it for my own hands. Healer's hands crack too."

Zephyrine stared at the pot as if it were an artifact from a lost civilization. "Why are you being kind to me?"

"Because you've spent fifteen years being kind to an entire city and nobody's returned the favor, apparently." Sef stood, brushing off her skirts. "One more question, and then I'll leave you to your mending. The mark on your palm—it's charm-scar, like I said. I've seen it. But there's something else in it. Underneath. A warmth, when I'm close to you, like standing near a banked fire. I felt it the night I stitched your shoulder in the Saltmarket and assumed it was fever. It's not fever, is it?"

The silence stretched so long that Sef thought Zephyrine simply wouldn't answer. The lantern hissed. The fog breathed. And then Zephyrine said, very quietly:

"There's something in my blood. Old blood. I don't—I can't explain it fully, because I don't understand it fully myself. But it's why I hide. Not the gift. Anyone can hide a gift. What I carry—" They shook their head. "If the crown ever learned what I am, truly am, it wouldn't be wardens they'd send. It would be armies. So: yes, I have the gift. Yes, I used it on your crew, and I'd do it again. But whatever else you think I might be—whatever the stories say about the old bloodlines—believe me when I tell you I am not that. I'm just a mapmaker who got lucky, or unlucky, depending on the day."

It was a good speech. Practiced, even. Sef had heard a thousand practiced speeches in the Lantern District, and she recognized the craft in this one—the partial truths arranged like furniture, everything visible except the room behind the wall.

But she also recognized exhaustion. And she knew that a person who lies well is lying for a reason, and a reason is a door, and doors open if you're patient.

"All right," Sef said. "Gift, not whatever else. I'll hold you to that."

Zephyrine's shoulders dropped half an inch. Relief, or the shape of it.

"But hear me," Sef went on, and her voice went hard as riverstone. "You've been alone so long that lying is just breathing to you now. You don't even notice you're doing it. Well, you're among a crew now, such as we are—thieves and mules and a healer with bad knees—and we survive by knowing what's true. Odile will forgive a lot of things, but she will not forgive being managed. So whatever's behind that wall of yours, Whitlock—whatever's making you sweat through the nights and stare at the moon like it owes you money—you bring it to us before it brings itself to us. Because out here, in the marsh, with wardens on the banks and winter coming, secrets don't stay buried. They surface. Everything surfaces in the marsh. Ask the dead."

Zephyrine looked up at her, and for one unguarded moment Sef saw all the way down: the terror, the loneliness, the terrible arithmetic of a life spent calculating the cost of every word. Then the shutters came down again, gentle and total, and Zephyrine nodded.

"Thank you," they said. "For the salve."

"Mm." Sef gathered her kit and turned to go.

"Sef." She paused. Zephyrine was turning the clay pot over in their scarred hands, and their voice, when it came, was almost too quiet for the fog. "Fifteen years. You said you've watched people lie for fifteen years. Did any of them ever stop? Did any of them ever get to tell the truth and live?"

Sef considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, because it deserved everything, because it was the whole question, the only one, the one under all the others.

"One," she said. "An old woman in the Lantern District. Poisoner, actually—killed her husband decades back, confessed on her deathbed, expected the gallows. The magistrate just shrugged. Said the statute of mercy had lapsed, said the crime was older than the law that punished it." She smiled thinly. "Truth's safest when it's too late to matter. That's the trick nobody tells you."

"And if it's not too late? If the truth has to come out while it can still cost everything?"

Sef looked at the fog, at the black water sliding past, at the far shore where somewhere crown wardens were burning their cookfires and sharpening their questions. She thought of Brannoc's healed hands. Of Odile's easy breathing. Of the fever broken in Tam's body by a stranger's quiet mercy.

"Then you'd better make sure," she said, "that the people standing next to you when it comes out are worth the price."

She left Zephyrine with the salve and the lantern and the mending that would never get finished, and climbed to the stern where Odile held the tiller, eyes on the dark.

"Well?" Odile said, not looking around. Sef's wife-of-the-road, her captain, her plague and her anchor. "Our passenger. What's the diagnosis?"

"Gifted," Sef said. "Charm-work, small stuff. Been healing us in our sleep like a guilty saint."

Odile was silent a moment. The tiller creaked. "And the rest? The part they didn't say?"

Sef glanced back amidships, where the lamplight made a small gold island around a figure hunched over their scars, staring east—at the horizon, or at something beyond it. The moon was rising now, three nights from full, hanging low and swollen and strange over the marsh, and Zephyrine was watching it the way sailors watch a storm cloud.

"They're counting," Sef said quietly. "Every night since we left the city, they've been counting the turns of the moon. And captain—they're terrified of what happens when the counting ends."
