## 8. The Hollow Court

The river gave them up at dusk, the way it gave up everything it carried eventually—driftwood, drowned things, and smugglers. Odile Vane stood at the prow of the *Magpie's Mercy* as the barge slid through the last bend of the Graywash, and watched Hollow Court assemble itself out of the evening haze: a scatter of lamplight clinging to the banks like moths that had decided to stay, the black skeleton of the old gallows tower rising above the rooftops, and beyond it all, the pale smear of the free-town's famous bridge, arched over the water like a spine that refused to break.

Three hundred years ago, some king or other had granted Hollow Court its charter of neutrality in exchange for safe passage through the marshlands, and every crown since had regretted it. No warden held authority here. No proclamation could be nailed to these doors. It was a town built on the principle that everything could be bought, sold, or looked away from, and Odile had always found a strange comfort in its honesty. Highmoor lied about what it was. Hollow Court simply was.

Tonight, though, she felt no comfort. She felt the weight of eyes.

"Captain." Brannoc's voice came low behind her, pitched under the creak of rigging. He filled the stern like a piece of the barge itself, arms crossed over his chest, his beard silvered at the edges by river spray. "Lamplighters on the quay are counting boats."

"They count every boat. That's their job."

"They're counting ours twice."

Odile didn't turn. She kept her gaze on the town, on the slow procession of lights along the waterfront, and let herself admit what she'd been refusing to admit since the Wraithmarsh: they were not merely traveling anymore. They were fleeing. The word sat wrong in her mouth. Vanes did not flee. Vanes endured, or they died standing, or—in her family's particular tradition—they betrayed each other with impeccable manners.

"Then we'll give them something worth counting," she said. "Sef! Get our passenger below and keep her there."

From amidships came Sef's dry voice: "Our passenger is standing right here, Captain, and can hear you."

Zephyrine Whitlock emerged from the shadow of the cargo netting, hood up, hands tucked into sleeves, looking for all the world like exactly what they appeared to be—a tired mapmaker with nothing to recommend them but ink-stained fingers and a face built for forgetting. Fifteen years of practice, Odile thought. Fifteen years of making themselves small. She'd known people who hid from the law before, but she'd never known anyone who hid so thoroughly from themselves.

"The quay first," Zephyrine said quietly. "There's a factor named Corvess who handles forged papers out of the chandler's loft above the salt shed. I mapped this town once—for real, before the ban tightened. The chandlery has a back stair that doesn't appear on any crown survey."

"You mapped a free-town for the crown?" Sef asked, one eyebrow climbing.

"I mapped it for anyone who paid. That's rather the point of maps." A pause, and then, softer: "I never drew what I saw. Only what was safe to see. There's a difference, and I've been living in it for fifteen years."

Odile studied them for a moment longer than necessary. There it was again—that flicker beneath the meekness, the sense of a door held shut by someone leaning all their weight against it. She'd hauled enough contraband in her life to know sealed cargo when she saw it. You didn't ask what was inside. You asked only whether it would burn.

"Brannoc, you're with me. Sef, keep the *Mercy* ready to move and our passenger out of sight. If we're not back by the second bell—"

"We'll be back," Brannoc said.

"If we're not back by the second bell," Odile repeated, "cast off and take the eastern channel. Don't wait. Don't be clever about it. Just go."

Sef's expression did something complicated—the healer's version of an argument swallowed whole. "And leave you two to the wardens? Captain, if the hunt's reached even here—"

"It hasn't reached here. Not yet. Hollow Court sells neutrality to both sides; it always has." But even as she said it, Odile remembered the lamplighters counting twice, and the memory sat in her chest like a stone swallowed wrong. "Go on. All of you. Move."

* * *

The chandlery smelled of tallow and brine, and the back stair Zephyrine had described was exactly where they'd said it would be—narrow, unlit, breathing the cold breath of the river through gaps in the stones. Odile took it two steps at a time with Brannoc's bulk filling the space behind her, and emerged into a loft that was less a room than an argument between hoarding and commerce: coils of rope, barrels of pitch, and everywhere, everywhere, paper. Bundles of it, stacked to the rafters. Blank deeds. Half-finished licenses. Bills of lading for ships that had never sailed, carrying cargo that had never existed.

Corvess was waiting for them, because Corvess always seemed to be waiting, a narrow man assembled entirely of angles and ink stains, perched on a stool behind a desk buried under ledgers. His eyes were quick and colorless, like a fish seen through ice.

"Captain Vane." He didn't rise. "The river told me you were coming. The river tells me everything, though it's been telling me stranger things lately." He plucked a rag and began wiping his fingers, though they were already clean. "Wardens in the town. Crown coin moving through three different houses. And a notice posted at the bridge gate offering more silver than I've seen offered for anything short of a king's ransom."

Odile's stomach went cold and quiet. "For what?"

"For a person." Corvess leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the ink on his fingers, made it look like bruising. "Not a name. A description. Youngish. Ink-stained hands. Travels with a river crew." His fish-pale eyes lifted to hers. "Sound like anyone you know?"

Behind her, Brannoc shifted, the floorboards groaning under him.

"How much silver?" Odile asked.

"Enough that half the town will sell their grandmothers by morning. Enough that I'm reconsidering my own principles, and I have excellent principles." Corvess smiled without warmth. "But you didn't come here for gossip. You came for papers. So let's talk papers."

They talked papers. Four sets, clean work, names that would pass a casual inspection and a careless one—nothing would pass a careful one, not anymore, not with the crown paying informants in sums that turned neighbors into knives. Odile counted out the payment in crown-minted silver, which tasted like irony going across the table, and Corvess counted it twice, which tasted like insult, and then he paused with his hand flat on the coins.

"One more thing, Captain. Free of charge, because I knew your father."

Odile went very still. "Did you."

"He ran the same route you run now. Good man. Careful man. Died careful." Corvess slid a folded scrap of paper across the desk—not a document, just a torn corner of a broadsheet, cheap ink already smudging. "This went up at the bridge gate an hour past. Thought you'd want to see it before the whole town does."

She unfolded it.

The paper was crowned with the royal seal, and beneath the seal, in letters rendered with bureaucratic precision:

*By order of the Lord Inquisitor Aldric Vane, High Warden of the Gifted Purge, all persons harboring or concealing the gifted shall be judged complicit—*

She stopped reading. Not because the words were difficult. Because the name was.

*Aldric.*

The letters swam, doubled, reassembled themselves into a boy's face—thin and serious, always serious, a boy who read law codes for pleasure and corrected her grammar at supper and once, when she was nine and he was thirteen, had spent an entire winter teaching her the constellations so she'd never be lost on the river at night. *So you can find your way home,* he'd said. *Even when I'm not there to steer you.*

He'd grown into a man who steered the crown's darkest ship instead. Lord Inquisitor. High Warden of the Purge. The brother who had taught her the stars now hung his name over a hunt for people like the one sleeping below her deck, and Odile stood in a loft full of contraband paper and felt fifteen years collapse into the space of a single breath.

"Captain?" Brannoc's hand settled on her shoulder, gentle as falling snow. "You've gone white as sailcloth."

"My brother," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded like it belonged to someone else, someone very far away. "The inquisitor leading the hunt. It's my brother."

* * *

They said little on the walk back down to the quay. Brannoc, to his credit, said nothing at all—he simply walked beside her, a wall of quiet company, and when a pair of crown wardens passed them at the corner of Rope Walk, his hand hovered near her elbow, steering her into a doorway's shadow until they'd gone. The wardens' boots rang on the cobbles like coins dropped in a temple. Everything in Hollow Court tonight sounded like money changing hands.

The *Mercy* rode low and patient at her moorings. Sef met them at the gangplank, took one look at Odile's face, and said, "Below decks. Now. Both of you."

In the cramped cabin that served as galley, chartroom, and confessional, Odile sat on a crate that smelled of pickled herring and told them. All of it. The boy who taught her stars. The young man who'd chosen the law over the family trade, who'd stood in their mother's kitchen at nineteen and announced that the Vane name would be redeemed, that smuggling was rot, that he would make the crown see the Vanes as loyal. The silence that followed him out the door. The years of nothing—no letters, no word, until his name began appearing on proclamations, rising through the ranks of the wardens like smoke finding a chimney.

"He thinks he's saving the family name," she finished. "That's the worst part. He's not cruel for cruelty's sake. He believes. He believes every word of it—that the gifted are a sickness, that the purge is medicine, that he's the honest Vane and I'm the stain." She laughed, and it came out broken. "And now he's hunting my crew. Hunting—" She glanced at Zephyrine, hood still up, face unreadable. "Hunting us."

Silence held the cabin. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above, footsteps crossed the deck—crew, checking lines—and the hull breathed against the pilings.

It was Zephyrine who spoke first, and their voice was careful, the way a person walks on river ice. "Your brother. Does he know you?"

"He knew me. People change."

"Does he know this boat? This route? Your habits?"

"Why?" Odile narrowed her eyes. "What are you asking, mapmaker?"

"I'm asking whether running is a plan or just a longer way of being caught." Zephyrine pushed back their hood, and in the lamplight their face was pale and resolute, the face of someone who had done arithmetic no one should have to do. "If Aldric Vane leads this hunt, he won't think like a warden. He'll think like family. He'll know where you'd run, who you'd trust, what waters you'd take. Every place you feel safe is a place he's already drawn on his own map." A pause. "I'm sorry. I know what it costs to hear that."

"And what would you have us do?" Sef snapped. She was coiling a length of bandage with unnecessary force, snap after snap after snap. "Hand ourselves in? Trust the mercy of a man whose job title includes the word *purge*?"

"No. I'm saying we stop pretending distance will save us." Zephyrine's hands were steady on the table, but Odile saw the fine tremor in the fingers, the exhaustion underneath. "I've spent fifteen years learning how hunters think. They don't chase. They wait. They close the map around you, ring by ring, until the only direction left is the one they want." Those dark eyes lifted to Odile's. "Your brother is closing the ring. We can either be inside it when it closes, or we can walk out through the gap he hasn't thought to guard yet."

"Which is?"

"I need time to find it. And I need to know everything you remember about him—his habits, his thinking, the routes he trusts. Family knowledge." Zephyrine hesitated, and then, quieter: "I know it hurts to use. But it may be the only advantage we have left."

Odile stared at them across the lamplit table—at this person she'd pulled out of a Saltmarket crowd on a debt she'd never fully understood, this stranger who drew maps of places that shouldn't exist, who flinched from kindness and spoke in careful fractions of truth. She should have been angry. Some part of her was angry, a hot coal lodged under the ribs, because the universe had apparently decided that the one hunt in the realm that could destroy her crew would be led by the one person whose approval she'd spent twenty years trying not to want.

But underneath the anger, something else stirred. Something that sounded like her brother's voice on a winter night, pointing at the sky: *so you can find your way home.*

"Aldric always plans three moves ahead," she said at last, slowly. "Always. He used to play strategy games against himself because no one in the house could challenge him. He leaves himself one escape and never uses it—he keeps it as a test of discipline. He distrusts rivers; he thinks they're too easy to lie about. He'll expect us to run east, toward the border towns." She rubbed her eyes. "Gods help me, I can do this all night. Twenty years of watching him from a distance, and I still know the shape of his mind better than my own."

"Then we use it," Zephyrine said. "Every piece of it."

Sef threw the bandage down. "You're both mad. You understand that? Completely, beautifully mad." But she was already reaching for the lamp to trim the wick, already settling in, because Sef's fury had always been a form of care wearing armor, and they all knew it.

Brannoc spoke for the first time since the loft, his voice a low rumble that seemed to come up through the deck itself. "My sister ran from the last purge. Three days head start. They still found her." He looked at Odile, and his eyes were old grief worn smooth as river stone. "Running isn't living, Captain. If there's a gap in the ring—I say we find it."

Odile looked around the lamplit cabin: the healer with her armored fury, the giant with his smoothed grief, the mapmaker with their sealed doors and steady, frightened resolve. Her crew. Her impossible, borrowed family, gathered around a table in a town that sold neutrality by the pound, while her own blood closed a ring around them all.

Family as wound. Family as warning. And yet here she sat, choosing another kind of family anyway, knowing exactly what families cost.

"Second bell's long past," she said, pushing herself up from the crate. "We stay moored till dawn—moving at night marks us worse than staying. Sef, double the watch. Brannoc, wake me if a single rowboat so much as looks at us sideways." She paused at the cabin door, hand on the frame, and looked back at Zephyrine. "Mapmaker. First light, you and I start drawing. My brother's mind, this time. Every road he'll watch, every trap he'll set, every gap he'll leave—because he always leaves one. He can't help it. He thinks leaving a gap is generosity."

A ghost of something—almost a smile—crossed Zephyrine's tired face. "Then let's hope he's still generous."

"Oh, he's generous," Odile said, and heard the bitterness in her own voice, sharp as river ice. "He's the most generous man in Highmoor. He'll leave us a gap wide enough to drive a barge through." She stepped out into the cold night air, where the river breathed and the lamplighters of Hollow Court counted boats that mattered, and the gallows tower stood black against the stars her brother had once taught her by name.

"And then," she said softly, to no one, to the water, to the boy he'd been, "he'll hang us all from it."

Above the town, thin clouds were sliding across the moon. One turn closer, though none of them yet knew to count.
