## 9. Charms and Confessions

The festival came to Hollow Court the way weather comes to a valley—suddenly, from all directions at once, and impossible to argue with.

Zephyrine heard it first as a low thrum beneath the floorboards of the *Wren's Mercy*, moored at the free-town's crooked dock: drums, dozens of them, carried up through the river water and into the timbers and into their bones. Then the smell arrived—roasting chestnuts, spiced wine, woodsmoke sweetened with honey—and then the light, lantern after lantern kindling along the boardwalk until the whole town glowed like an ember cupped in dark hands.

They stood at the rail and watched it happen, and something in their chest ached with a feeling they could not immediately name. It took them a long moment to recognize it, because they had not felt it in fifteen years.

It was the ache of wanting.

*You could go down there,* said the wanting. *You could be one of them.*

"Stop looming," Sef said, appearing at their elbow with the particular silence she reserved for people she liked. She pressed a cup of mulled wine into Zephyrine's hands without asking whether they wanted it. "Looming is bad luck on a festival night. Also you look like a heron deciding whether to eat a frog."

"I don't loom."

"You absolutely loom. You have a whole posture for it." She mimed it, shoulders hunched, chin tucked, eyes flicking everywhere at once—the posture of a person perpetually measuring exits. It was, Zephyrine admitted privately, devastatingly accurate. "Down there," Sef went on, nodding at the lights, "nobody knows your face. Nobody knows any of ours. Hollow Court doesn't ask questions on festival nights; asking questions is bad for trade. So drink your wine, put down that terrible watchful expression, and come be a person for one evening."

"I'm always a person."

"You're a map with legs," Sef said fondly, and hauled them toward the gangplank before they could argue.

* * *

Hollow Court by daylight was a hard place—mud streets, sharp bargains, every third doorway hiding something smuggled or forged. Hollow Court by festival night was another country entirely. The square at its heart had been strung with ribbons dyed river-green and lamp-oil gold, and musicians had claimed the fountain steps: a fiddler, two pipers, a drummer with forearms like ship's cable, playing something old and circular that seemed less composed than remembered. People danced in the mud itself, boots stamping, skirts flying, children weaving between legs like minnows.

Odile Vane had changed clothes, which Zephyrine had never seen her do. She wore a coat of deep red instead of salt-stained black, and her hair was down, and she was laughing—actually laughing—at something Brannoc had said, her head tipped back, the sound carrying over the music. Brannoc himself had flowers tucked behind one ear, enormous and absurd against his grey-streaked beard, and he moved among the crowd like a lighthouse among rowboats, gentle and immovable, dispensing cups of cider to children who stared up at him in open worship.

"There you are!" Odile spotted them and crossed the square in six strides, catching Zephyrine's wrist and pulling them bodily into the dance before protest was possible. "No, none of that—you're dancing. That's an order from your captain."

"I'm not crew."

"You're cargo with opinions, which is close enough." She spun them; the world smeared into ribbons of lantern-light; and Zephyrine, who had spent fifteen years making themselves small and forgettable and still, found themselves laughing out loud in the middle of a crowd, hands clasped with a smuggler captain, feet finding a rhythm older than any law.

It was, they thought wildly, the most dangerous thing they had ever done. Not the marsh, not the charms sold under the counter, not the maps of realms that officially did not exist. This. Being seen. Being *present*. Every year of concealment had taught them that joy was a door left unlatched, that happiness drew the eye the way lamplight draws moths, and here they were burning like a beacon in an open square.

And yet.

Sef grabbed their other hand. Brannoc swept past with two children perched on his shoulders. Odile shouted the words of some bawdy festival song and got half of them wrong, and nobody corrected her, because in Hollow Court on this night it did not matter what was true—it only mattered that everyone sang together.

Zephyrine almost believed in it. That was the treachery of festivals: for whole minutes at a time, they actually believed. They believed in a life where they were simply a traveler passing through, where their hands were only hands, where the warmth in their chest was wine and music and nothing else. They believed so thoroughly that when a little girl stumbled into them—a child of perhaps six, brown-haired, gap-toothed, wearing a paper crown—and looked up and said, "Your fingers are sparkly," Zephyrine laughed and said, "So are yours," and meant nothing by it, and felt nothing stir, and was for one perfect moment just a stranger being kind to a child.

Then the wardens came.

* * *

Zephyrine heard them before they saw them, because Zephyrine had spent fifteen years learning the sound. Not boots—festival crowds wore boots too—but the *quality* of the footsteps: four sets, moving in unison, unhurried, cutting across the grain of the celebration like a blade through silk. The music faltered. The dancers nearest the fountain steps stopped first, then those behind them, a silence spreading outward in a ring.

Four men in crown-grey pushed into the square. They were not from Hollow Court; the free-town had no wardens of its own, which was rather the point of it. These had ridden in from somewhere loyal to the throne, and they moved through the crowd with the flat confidence of men whose authority traveled farther than their swords.

The little girl with the paper crown was standing near the fountain. She had been dancing; now she stood very still, the way gifted children learned to stand still, and Zephyrine saw it happen—saw the oldest warden's gaze snag on her the way a fishhook snags on a submerged branch. Saw the man tilt his head. Saw him say something to his fellows, low, and saw all four of them begin, casually, terribly, to drift toward the fountain steps.

"No," Zephyrine breathed.

She was doing nothing wrong. She was doing *nothing*. But she was six years old and frightened, and frightened gifted children leaked—they couldn't help it, the magic rose in them like blood to a bruise, shimmering faintly around their fingers, their hair, the air at their shoulders. Zephyrine could see it from twenty feet away: a faint gold tremor, candleflame-colored, clinging to the girl like static. To anyone ungifted it would read as trickery of the light. To these men, sent out in the season of the hunt, it would read as exactly what it was.

The crowd, sensing nothing specific but smelling trouble, began to eddy away from the fountain, leaving the child in a widening island of empty cobbles.

"Zeph." Sef's hand closed on their arm. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"She's a baby."

"Whatever you're *thinking*, there are four of them and one of us and we have forged papers that will survive exactly zero scrutiny—"

The oldest warden reached the fountain. He crouched, put his face level with the child's, and smiled the smile of a man who has done this before and been paid well for it. Zephyrine could not hear his words over the nervous murmur of the crowd, but they knew their shape. *Show me your hands, little one. Nothing to be afraid of. Just show me.*

The girl held out her hands. The gold tremor clung to her small fingers like fireflies.

The warden's smile widened, and he reached for the coil of iron chain at his belt—the short length, the child-sized length, the kind used for wrists—and the sound that went through the watching crowd was not a gasp but a *withdrawal*, a hundred people simultaneously deciding not to see.

Later, Zephyrine would try to reconstruct the next thirty seconds and fail. Memory kept it as sensation rather than sequence: the cold shock of their own heartbeat; Odile's voice saying their name in a tone of warning; the warden's hand closing around the girl's thin wrist; the child's cry, high and brief; and underneath everything, rising like floodwater through a cellar floor, fifteen years of dammed-up power demanding, with a voice like a struck bell, *enough*.

They crossed the square. They did not decide to cross the square; their body decided, and their gift decided with it, and by the time conscious thought caught up they were already standing between the warden and the child, already speaking words in a language that predated the crown, already *burning*.

Light went through them and out of them. Not the sly, guttering charm-light they had rationed out for years in trinkets and hidden sigils—this was the full current, unmasked, pouring down their arms and off their fingertips in visible rivers of gold. It wrapped the warden's reaching hand and stopped it—not violently, not breaking anything, but with the absolute finality of a river stopping a stone. The man cried out and staggered back, shaking his arm as though he'd plunged it into snowmelt. The chain fell ringing to the cobbles.

The girl's own shimmer sank back into her skin, soothed quiet, and Zephyrine knelt and gathered her up—one arm, effortless, she weighed nothing—and turned to face the square.

Every eye in Hollow Court was on them. The musicians stood frozen with bows raised. Lantern-light and emberlight mixed on hundreds of upturned faces, and in every face Zephyrine read the same thing, the thing they had spent fifteen years hiding from: *recognition*. Gifted. Here. Now. Visible.

The four wardens had drawn steel. The oldest, the one whose hand they had stayed, was breathing hard and staring at Zephyrine with an expression that was no longer greed or duty but something colder and more careful—the look of a man recalculating, a man realizing that what stood in front of him was worth far more than a child's bounty.

"Name," he demanded. "Give me your name, witch, and the crown may be merciful."

Behind him, at the mouth of the square, Zephyrine saw a fifth figure arrive—a courier, mud-spattered, breathless, pressing a folded paper into the warden's second's hands. Even at this distance, even upside-down, Zephyrine recognized the format of the document, because they had seen a hundred like it in fifteen years of studying how the crown marked its prey.

A likeness. A posted likeness. Someone's face, rendered in ink, wanted.

They could not see whose.

Brannoc's shadow fell over them both, vast and sudden, and Odile's voice cut across the square like a whip-crack: "That's enough! Wardens drawing blades on children at a festival—in front of forty witnesses? Hollow Court remembers faces, gentlemen. And Hollow Court *trades* with everyone." A murmur of assent ran through the crowd, ugly and low, and the wardens' certainty wavered—four swords were formidable, but four swords against a whole square full of merchants who hated interference with business was arithmetic even crown men could do.

The oldest warden lowered his blade an inch. His eyes never left Zephyrine. "This isn't finished," he said. "The hunt comes for everyone. And it comes soonest for the ones who make us look."

Zephyrine set the girl down. She pressed her paper crown into their hand—solemnly, the way children confer honors—and ran to a woman in the crowd who swept her up, weeping, and vanished into the press of bodies.

The wardens withdrew. The music did not resume. The festival, Zephyrine understood, was over—not because anyone announced it, but because the thing that festivals require, the collective permission to be careless, had been burned out of the square as surely as if they had dropped a torch in dry grass.

* * *

They walked back to the barge in silence, the four of them, the lanterns glowing mockingly festive above empty streets. Zephyrine still held the paper crown. Their hands had not stopped trembling since the fountain, and they suspected they would not stop for some time. Fifteen years of discipline, undone in thirty seconds. Fifteen years of *almost*, ended in front of a hundred witnesses.

At the gangplank, Odile turned.

"You used it," she said. Not angry. Worse than angry—careful. "In the open. In a town full of eyes."

"A warden was going to chain a six-year-old."

"I know. I *know*, Zeph." She dragged a hand down her face, and in the lantern-light Zephyrine saw how tired she was, how the red festival coat suddenly looked like a costume she'd been foolish to put on. "I'm not saying you were wrong. I'm saying we all wear your choice now. Those papers Sef bled three months of profit for? Worthless by morning. That route through the marsh we haven't run yet? Our only road, and every warden between here and the border will know a gold-handed witch is traveling it." She exhaled slowly. "I'm not saying you were wrong. I'm saying I need to know what else you haven't told me. Because whatever you are—whatever that *was*—it wasn't a hedge-witch's trick. I've seen hedge-witches. Hedge-witches don't stop a grown man's arm with a thought and light up a square doing it."

"Sef already asked me questions," Zephyrine said quietly. "I answered what I could."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is heavier than you want."

Odile studied them for a long moment. Somewhere across the water, the last festival drums had gone silent, and the river lapped against the hull with the patience of something that would carry on long after all of them were gone.

"Heavy things sink ships," she said finally. "But I'd rather take on the weight honest than find it at the bottom of my hold." She stepped aboard. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we run the marsh, and tomorrow night—" she nodded grimly toward the dark square, toward the memory of the courier and his folded paper, "—tomorrow night I want to know why a crown courier rode three days through mud to bring a warden a picture of somebody's face. Because I've been in this trade fifteen years, Zeph, and I've never seen the crown spend that much ink on anybody."

Zephyrine looked down at the paper crown in their hands, at the small crayon sun drawn on its front, and felt the Ember turn over in their chest like something waking in a warm room—slow, patient, and no longer willing to pretend it was asleep.

Neither, they suspected, was the crown.
