## 10. The Inquisitor's Eye

They left Hollow Court before dawn, the way the guilty leave, though Zephyrine had never felt less guilty and more hunted in their life.

The river barge slid out through the mist with its lanterns shuttered, Odile at the tiller, Brannoc poling them past the sleeping fisher-stakes, Sef below deck pretending to sleep and failing at it audibly. The festival square was behind them now, and somewhere in it a girl of perhaps nine years was being questioned by men in grey, and somewhere in it Zephyrine's face had been seen doing what faces were not permitted to do in Highmoor: shining.

Zephyrine sat at the bow with their knees drawn up and watched the mist peel off the water in long gray ribbons. Their hands still smelled of chalk and candle-smoke from the charm they had thrown — the little light-work, the simplest thing in their repertoire, the thing a child could do. *A child had done it,* some traitorous part of their mind kept insisting. *The girl you saved did it first, and she'll hang for it if they trace her, so be grateful your face is on the walls instead of hers.*

Gratitude and terror made an uncomfortable pair. They sat together in Zephyrine's chest like two passengers who disliked each other and refused to change seats.

"You should sleep."

Sef had come up without sound, which was her way; she lowered herself onto the cargo hatch and began unbraiding her hair, shaking out the night's smoke and sweat. She didn't look at Zephyrine when she spoke. That was also her way.

"I slept," Zephyrine said.

"You sat with your eyes closed and flinched every time a bird went up. That's not sleeping, that's praying badly." Sef pulled a comb through a knot with more force than the knot deserved. "Odile says we make for the old quarry cut by midday, then north along the ridge. She says it like the ridge is a friend of hers. I say we're three days from anywhere warden-free and our passenger is famous now, so maybe someone should ask the famous one what they intend."

"I don't intend anything. I intend to be small."

"Well." Sef snapped the comb shut. "You've had fifteen years of practice. How's that working?"

The question landed harder than Sef probably meant it to — or exactly as hard as she meant it to; with Sef it was difficult to tell kindness from cruelty because she wrapped each in the other's clothing. Zephyrine didn't answer. Behind them, below decks, Brannoc's low voice rumbled through the planks, telling Odile something in the half-language the two of them used for serious things, the words too soft to catch but the rhythm unmistakable: *trouble, trouble, trouble.*

* * *

They saw the first likeness at the quarry cut, nailed to the toll-post where the road bent down toward the water.

It was good work. That was the worst of it. Not the crude charcoal smear Zephyrine might have hoped for, not a wanted-poster's usual slander of wrong noses and imagined scars, but a likeness — the sharp cheekbones, the ink-stained fingers rendered almost lovingly, the eyes caught in that particular wary look Zephyrine knew from mirrors and hated seeing reproduced at this scale, four feet tall, staring down the empty road at no one.

UNDER EDICT OF THE CROWN, the header read. THE PERSON DEPICTED IS SOUGHT FOR CRIMES OF ENCHANTMENT. REWARD: FIVE HUNDRED CROWNS SILVER.

Five hundred crowns. Zephyrine did the arithmetic without wanting to. Five hundred crowns was a fishing boat. Was a shop. Was a year of a family fed and then some. Somewhere downriver there were people who had bought Zephyrine's "lucky trinkets" for coppers, who would look at this face and feel the pleasant click of recognition, the small golden thrill of a fortune assembling itself in their memory.

"Odile," Sef called, and the barge came about.

Odile stood at the rail studying the poster with her arms folded, her jaw working the way it did when she was chewing on something she couldn't swallow. Up close, the likeness was worse. There was a seal at the bottom Zephyrine hadn't been able to read from the water: not the crown's ordinary hunting-lion, but a different device — a circle bisected by a single vertical line, like an eye seen edge-on, or a door seen closed.

"That's not the wardens' mark," Odile said. "That's the Inquisition's personal seal. He's posting these himself. Not the crown. Him."

"Lord Aldric Vane," Zephyrine said quietly.

"My brother," Odile said, in the flat voice she used for facts that had teeth. "Yes."

Brannoc pried the poster off the post whole, board and all, and broke the board over his knee with a sound like a small bone snapping. It didn't help. Zephyrine appreciated it anyway.

* * *

They saw the second likeness at Miller's Reach, and the third at the ferry town of Ashford Sluice, and by the fourth, at the market cross of Dunlow, Zephyrine had stopped flinching and started studying them, because that was what they did — they studied. Maps, faces, patterns. And the posters were telling them something, if they could bear to read it.

The first poster had said CRIMES OF ENCHANTMENT. The second, posted only two days later, had changed. THE PERSON DEPICTED IS SOUGHT FOR QUESTIONS PERTAINING TO THE OLD BLOOD. The third: ...PERTAINING TO MATTERS OF SUCCESSION AND STATE. The fourth said nothing about crimes at all. It said:

THE BEARER OF THE EMBER IS ENTREATED TO COME FORWARD. AMNESTY IS OFFERED. THE THRONE EXTENDS ITS HAND IN GOOD FAITH.

"Amnesty," Sef read aloud, spitting the word like gristle. "That's new."

"That's bait," Odile said.

"It's worse than bait." Zephyrine stood before the market-cross posting in the gray drizzle, hood up, hands buried in their sleeves, and felt the cold walk up their spine one vertebra at a time. Around them the market went on — fishwives arguing, a dog stealing a pie, children chasing each other between the stalls — ordinary life, magnificently indifferent to the paper nailed above its head. "Look at the wording. Not 'the criminal.' Not 'the enchanters.' The Ember. He's not casting a net anymore. A net catches whatever swims into it. This is a hook. There's one fish he wants."

Odile came to stand beside them, close enough that her shoulder made a wall of itself, an unconscious gesture she probably didn't know she was making. "You keep saying *he*. You talk about my brother like you've met him."

"I haven't. But I've met his handwriting." Zephyrine touched the edge of the poster, careful not to tear it. "I spent fifteen years reading documents for the cartographers' guild. Charters, grants, edicts. Every hand has a weather. His is precise. Cold front moving in. And this—" they tapped the eye-and-door seal, "—this device isn't on any crown register I ever copied. I'd have remembered it. Which means it's new, or it's old and secret, and either way it means Aldric Vane is not running the crown's hunt. The hunt is running his."

Sef had gone very still. "Say what you mean, mapmaker."

"I mean he's looking for something specific." Zephyrine heard their own voice go quiet, the way it did when the truth got too large for a normal volume. "The proclamation at the gate was general — all the gifted, root and branch. That's how these things start, wide as a flood. But floods recede. Look at the sequence. Enchantment. Old blood. Succession. Now amnesty for the Ember alone. In nine days he's narrowed fifteen years of persecution down to a single word." They turned and looked at Odile, and then, because there was no way around it, at both of them. "He knows what he's hunting. He knew before the hunt began. Maybe the hunt exists *because* he knows."

The rain ticked on the market awnings. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, unhurried, stupidly peaceful.

"And what does he want it for?" Sef asked. Her voice was light. Her eyes were not.

"I don't know yet." It was almost true. Almost. The prophecy of the thirteen turns sat in Zephyrine's chest like a swallowed coal, and next to it, older and darker, a memory from the archive beneath Hollow Court — the charter, the founding lie, the king who stole rather than conquered. *A king who stole the Ember's power.* If the throne had been built on stolen fire, then whoever held the fire held the deed to the whole kingdom. "But I know what I'd want it for, if I were him. Everything."

* * *

They ran north after that, off the river and onto land, because Odile said the waterways would be watched now — every lock, every toll, every ferryman with five hundred reasons to look closely at hooded passengers. They walked by night and lay up by day in places Brannoc seemed to produce from memory: a charcoal-burner's abandoned platform, a shepherd's bothy with a roof like a broken ribcage, once a hollow under a yew tree that Brannoc insisted, with total seriousness, was drier than most inns.

It was in the bothy, on the third night, that Zephyrine finally said it out loud.

Not everything. Not the Ember-in-the-blood, not the thirteenth moon, not the thing coiled behind their sternum that had been waking a little more each night, warm as a hand pressed flat against a banked fire. But enough.

"Aldric Vane isn't hunting the gifted," they said to the small circle of firelight. "Or he is, but they're cover. Noise. You sweep a city for pickpockets when you're really looking for one thief. The gifted are the sweep. I'm the thief."

Sef looked up from her mortar, where she was grinding willow bark into bitter dust. "And how does he know to look for you? Specifically you?"

"I don't—" Zephyrine started, and stopped, because the answer arrived while they were denying it, the way answers do. "The girl. In the square. When I used the charm-work publicly, it wasn't just seen. It was *felt*. Anyone with old blood within half a mile would have felt it, the way you feel a struck bell in your teeth. And if Aldric has gifted informants — coerced ones, bought ones — then what they reported wasn't 'an enchanter in the square.' It was the flavor of the working. The signature." Their hands had gone cold; they folded them tighter. "Every gift has a grain to it. Mine comes down a long line. If even one of his informants is old-blooded enough to recognize the lineage..."

"Then he's not following your face," Odile said slowly. She was sharpening her knife, though it did not need it. "He's following your fire."

"He's following my fire," Zephyrine agreed, and hated the sentence, and said it anyway, because Sef had taught them something in the weeks since the Saltmarket: that honesty, spoken early, was cheaper than honesty extracted. "Which means the posters are theater. The reward, the amnesty, the edicts — those are for the crowds. For us, he has something better. He has my own magic, announcing me everywhere I go, like a lantern I can't put out."

The fire popped. Outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea remembering itself.

Brannoc spoke for the first time that evening. "Then we move you where your fire can't be read." He said it simply, the way he said everything, as though arranging the world were merely a matter of large hands and patience. "Stone country. High passes. Old wards in the mountain shrines — my grandmother's people raised them, before. Fire reads poorly through stone and older spells. We go up. We go quiet. We let his eye pass over."

"The mountains," Odile said, and something crossed her face too fast to name. "Of course. The shrines."

"You know them?" Zephyrine asked.

"I know *of* them." She tested the knife's edge against her thumb and put it away. "Everyone from the river country knows of them. Places the gifted hid during the last purge. Places the crown never found." She looked up, and her eyes caught the firelight and held it, green going gold. "Places my brother has wanted to find for twenty years."

Zephyrine lay awake long after the others slept, watching the bothy's broken roof stitch together the stars.

*A lantern I can't put out.* That was the shape of it now. Fifteen years of learning to be small, to be gray, to be a trinket-seller with ink-stained fingers and no history — and it had taken one act of conscience in a festival square to make them visible across half a kingdom. Conscience, it turned out, was the loudest magic of all.

And beneath the fear, quieter and more terrible, another thought kept surfacing, patient as water finding a crack: *he knew before the hunt began.* Which meant someone had told him. Someone who knew what the Ember was, and where old blood ran, and what slept under the skin of the world waiting for the thirteenth moon.

Someone who had read the same archive Zephyrine had. Or written what was in it.

The Ember stirred behind Zephyrine's sternum, warm and slow, like something turning over in its sleep. For the first time it felt less like a burden they carried and more like a companion that had opinions — and one of those opinions, unmistakably, was that the mountains were a good idea, and that they should hurry.

Zephyrine closed their eyes. Three days south, a man with cold handwriting was nailing their face to the world. Above them, the moon hung two nights shy of full — the twelfth turn nearly done, the last turn beginning.

Whatever the Ember was waking toward, it was waking fast. And Lord Aldric Vane, with his eye-shaped seal and his offers of amnesty, intended to be standing underneath it when it opened.
