## 12. Fracture

The archive's dust still clung to everything—Odile could taste it in her tea, gritty and old as sin, the flavor of a truth that had followed them out of the dark like a dog that wouldn't be shooed.

She stood at the barge's stern rail and watched the river carry Hollow Court away behind them, its crooked spires sinking into the morning haze. Somewhere back there, her brother was sharpening his knives and calling it justice. Somewhere ahead of them, the Wraithmarsh waited to swallow the unwary whole. And somewhere on this barge—three feet away, maybe four, close enough to touch if Odile were fool enough to reach—stood the person she'd begun, against every instinct she owned, to call family.

Who had lied to her.

Not with words. That was the worst of it. Zephyrine hadn't looked her in the eye and sworn anything false. They'd simply let her believe a smaller truth while the larger one sat coiled in their blood like a lit fuse.

*The Ember lives in you.*

Sef had said it quietly, last night, in the low lamplight of the cabin, after the charter's revelation had stopped ringing in all their ears. *They have the gift, yes. But they're more than gifted, Odile. The prophecy—the thirteenth turn—the Ember of the old bloodline. It's them. It's been them the whole time.*

And Zephyrine had not denied it. Had stood there with their hands folded and their face gone pale and smooth as fresh vellum, and said only, "I was going to tell you."

*Was going to.* As if there were ever going to be a good moment for that particular confession. As if any moment existed in which *by the way, I am the living weapon the crown has hunted for fifteen years, the thing your brother is searching for, the reason wardens are dying on our trail* would land gently.

Odile drank her tea and let the bitterness sit on her tongue where it belonged.

"Captain." Brannoc's voice came soft from the deck behind her, careful as a man approaching a snare. He lowered himself onto the bench beside the rail without being asked, which was itself an act of courage; the bench groaned under him, and so did the silence between them. "You haven't slept."

"I slept fine."

"You're holding the rail hard enough to bend it."

She looked down. Her knuckles were white around the wood. She made herself let go, finger by finger. "Go away, Brannoc."

"No." He said it mildly, the way he said most things, his great hands resting loose on his knees. His sister's charm—a knotted cord, faded almost colorless—was wound twice around his left wrist, as it always was. "You'll want someone here when you decide what kind of captain you're going to be."

"And what kind is that?"

"The kind who hasn't decided yet." He turned his head and looked at her, and his eyes were gentle and absolutely unyielding, the eyes of a man who had buried family once already because the crown decided what they were before asking what they'd done. "I just want you to know I'm watching which way you lean."

Odile laughed, and it came out ugly. "You think I'd sell them?"

"I think you're angry enough to consider it. And I think anger's honest, and I'd rather see it than watch you pretend it isn't there." He rose, joints cracking like green branches, and squeezed her shoulder once—brief, warm, unbearable—and went forward to check the lines, leaving her alone with the river and the taste of dust.

* * *

By midday the wind had turned contrary, and by afternoon so had the crew.

It happened in the galley, which was too small for the conversation and exactly the right size for a fight. Tam started it, because Tam always started things he couldn't finish—he was pacing between the stove and the table like a cat in a sack, and his voice had the high, cracked edge it got when his debts were talking louder than his sense.

"We should just say it plain," Tam said. "There's a bounty coming. There's always a bounty coming now—we've seen the postings, we know what Aldric Vane pays for information about the Ember. A king's ransom. An actual king's ransom, Odile. We could pay off every debt from here to the Salt Sea and never run cargo again."

"You want to sell them," Sef said flatly. She sat at the table with her mending in her lap, though she hadn't taken a stitch since she'd sat down. Her needle hung motionless in the cloth like a struck match.

"I want us to *live*," Tam snapped. "That's all. That's all I've ever wanted. You think I like saying it? You think I don't—" He stopped, jaw working. "Zeph's going to get us all killed anyway. That's what the Ember does. It starts wars. We read the charter same as you did. Everything that thing touches burns."

"That's not what the charter says," Sef said. "That's what the throne says. There's a difference, and you know there's a difference, and you're standing in this galley pretending you don't because fear makes liars of us all."

"Fear keeps people breathing!"

"Not always," Brannoc rumbled from the doorway. He filled it entirely, and the temperature of the room changed the way it always did when he chose to speak instead of listen. "My sister breathed just fine until someone decided what she was worth dead."

Tam flinched. "Brannoc—"

"Don't." One word, quiet, and Tam sat down as though his strings had been cut.

Which was when Odile noticed that Zephyrine was standing at the galley's far end, half in shadow, a bundle of charts under one arm, having heard every word.

The silence that fell was total. Even the river seemed to hold its breath outside the hull.

"Well," Zephyrine said at last, and their voice was steady, which must have cost them something enormous. "At least no one's pretending anymore."

"Get out," Odile said.

"Odile—"

"*Get out.*" She heard her own voice crack on it, and hated that, hated the crack more than the anger, because it showed the seam underneath—the place where the lie had split her open. "Take your charts. Take them anywhere but here."

Zephyrine didn't move. Their eyes found hers across the little room, gray-green and calm and terribly tired, and Odile thought: *you knew. All those weeks on this barge. All those nights sharing watch, sharing food, sharing the small stupid jokes that make a crew into a family—you knew what you were carrying, and you let me moor my heart to it.*

"I trusted you," Odile said, quieter now, which was worse. "I don't trust anyone. Fifteen years running this river, and I trust exactly three people, and I counted you among them. I told you about my brother. Do you remember that? I told you about Aldric, and what he was before, and you sat there and listened and you *knew*—"

"I knew I was the reason he's hunting us," Zephyrine said. "Yes."

"And you said nothing."

"What would you have done?" For the first time their composure slipped, just a hairline fracture, and something raw showed through. "If I'd told you in Highmoor, on the first night? Would you have taken me aboard? Would any of you?"

"Yes," Brannoc said.

Zephyrine closed their eyes. "You might have. But Odile would have weighed it. And she would have been *right* to weigh it. I was a death sentence walking up your gangplank, and I knew it, and I wanted—" Their voice caught. "I wanted passage out of Highmoor. I wanted to keep breathing. I told myself I'd tell you when we were safe. And then safe kept not arriving, and the telling kept getting harder, and I—" They stopped. Drew breath. "I'm not going to stand here and make excuses. I lied by omission, and omissions are still lies. I'm sorry. It doesn't fix anything. But it's true, and I owe you truths."

The galley was very still. Sef's needle finally moved, one slow stitch, another.

Tam broke it. "See? See, that's exactly it. 'I owe you truths.' Well, I owe forty crowns to a man in Hollow Court who breaks fingers, and the crown owes *us* nothing, and Zeph owes us everything, and there's a bounty out there big enough to square all our accounts at once." He stood, palms flat on the table, looking at Odile with pleading, desperate eyes. "Captain. Captain, I'm asking you. Not selling them to the wardens—I'm not a monster—but we put them off at the next town, we take the barge and go, we wash our hands—"

"They'd be taken within a day," Sef said.

"Then that's not our weight to carry!"

"It became our weight," Brannoc said, "the night we pulled them out of the Saltmarket."

"Because of a *debt*! One map! One favor from years ago—that's what started this, and nobody voted on it, and now we're running from inquisitors and getting shot at in marshes and for *what*?" Tam's voice climbed toward breaking. "For a person who looked us in the eye for weeks and never said *oh, by the way, I'm the reason the sky's falling*—"

"Enough." Odile pushed back from the rail she'd been leaning against—when had she come inside? she didn't remember—and the room went quiet again, because whatever else she was, she was still captain, and the word *enough* in her mouth still carried iron.

She crossed to Zephyrine. Stood close enough to see the exhaustion carved into their face, the fine tremor in their hands they were trying to hide by gripping the chart roll tighter.

"If you'd told me," she said, very low, "that first night. If you'd said *I'm the Ember*, what do you think I'd have done?"

Zephyrine held her gaze. "I think you'd have thrown me off the barge."

"I think so too." She let that hang. "And I think that's why you didn't tell me. Not because you're a liar. Because you knew me better than I liked." She stepped back, and the distance felt like a mile. "It doesn't make it hurt less. It doesn't make it *not* a betrayal. But I'm not going to pretend I wouldn't have done the same in your position, and I hate you a little for making me honest about that."

"Odile," Sef said softly.

"I'm not done being angry," Odile said, to the whole room, to the river, to herself. "Understand that. Anyone expecting me to forgive and forget can swim home. But we are not selling anyone. We are not putting anyone off at some town to be collected like a bounty. That's not what this crew does, and if that's suddenly what this crew does, then it's not my crew anymore, and I'll row the rest of the way alone." She looked at Tam last, longest. "Is that plain enough for everyone?"

Tam's face worked through several expressions—relief, resentment, shame, and something that might have been gratitude, or might have been the shape fear leaves when it retreats. "Plain," he muttered, and slunk out past Brannoc into the daylight.

* * *

Evening came down purple and cold over the water. Odile took the second watch alone, as she'd asked, though the asking had been more of an order, and no one had argued.

Zephyrine came up anyway. Of course they did. They moved like a person who had learned that silence was safety and had never quite unlearned it, settling at the far end of the stern rail with a respectful arm's length of wood between them.

For a long time neither spoke. The river murmured against the hull. Somewhere on the far bank a nightjar called, three notes, over and over, like a question nobody answered.

"My mother used to sing that," Zephyrine said finally. "The nightbird song. She said it was how the gifted talked to each other across distances, once. Before the outlawry. Three notes means *I'm here. Are you there?*" A pause. "Nobody ever answers anymore."

Odile kept her eyes on the water. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked me for truths." Zephyrine's breath fogged in the cooling air. "And because I've spent fifteen years learning every way there is to hide, and I'm finding that the hiding is heavier than the thing I'm hiding. That's all. I'm not asking you to forgive me tonight. I'm asking you to know that I understand what I did. I made you care about someone who wasn't entirely real, because the real one was too dangerous to be."

"That's not—" Odile stopped. Started again, slower. "That's not what happened. The person I cared about was real. That's what makes it sting. If you'd been a stranger, I'd have put you off at Hollow Court and never lost a night's sleep. But you weren't a stranger. You were the one who fixed Brannoc's netting without being asked. The one who taught Sef the marsh-herb names. The one who—" She exhaled through her nose. "You drew my brother's face from memory, Zeph. From a description. Perfectly. Because you thought it might help me grieve. People don't do that unless they mean it. So either you meant it, or you're the finest actor on five rivers, and I've watched you draw maps, and nobody acts that well."

Zephyrine said nothing. In the dark, their profile was all sharp lines and held breath.

"So here's where I am," Odile said. "I'm angry, and I'm going to stay angry for a while, and you're going to have to live with that. But you're on my barge, and while you're on my barge, no one sells you, no one gives you up, and no one—including you—gets to decide alone anymore. Whatever the Ember is. Whatever it wants. Whatever happens at the thirteenth turn. We do it together or not at all. That's the price of staying. Full truth, shared weight. Can you live with that?"

Zephyrine turned to look at her. In the faint light off the water their eyes were wet, though their voice, when it came, was level. "I can try."

"Trying's all any of us manage." Odile faced the river again. Ahead, far downstream, the Wraithmarsh waited with its fog and its drowned roads, and beyond it the mountains, and beyond those—Highmoor, and the thirteenth moon, and whatever her brother was building in the dark. "Now get below. You look like something the marsh coughed up."

Zephyrine hesitated at the companionway. "Odile. The nightjar. Three notes."

"What about it?"

A pause, long enough that Odile almost turned. Then, softly:

"*I'm here. Are you there?*"

And they were gone below before she could answer—which was answer enough, and not answer enough, and exactly the kind of unfinished thing she supposed she'd have to learn to live with.

She stood her watch alone under the turning sky, counting moons toward the thirteenth, and did not sleep, and did not quite regret it.

It was near dawn, the fog rising off the water in slow gray banners, when Sef came up the steps two at a time with a lantern swinging wild and a face gone white as bone.

"Odile." Breathless. "Tam's gone. Took the skiff, took the strongbox, and left this pinned to the mast."

A scrap of paper, damp with river-fog, the ink already bleeding.

*I'm sorry. They'll pay more for where you're going than for what you're carrying. Head east. Don't follow me.*

Odile read it twice. Behind her, she heard Zephyrine come quietly up the stairs, and stop, and read it over her shoulder, and go very still.

"He knows about the archive," Zephyrine whispered. "He knows where we're headed. Odile—he's riding straight to Aldric Vane."
