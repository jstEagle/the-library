## 13. The Betrayer's Road

Sef had always trusted her hands more than her heart. Hands could be washed, bandaged, steadied. Hearts were treacherous things—they kept beating for people who didn't deserve it, kept hoping past all evidence, kept aching in the small hours when there was nothing left to do but listen to the rain.

The rain came down on Hollow Court like a judgment nobody had bothered to pronounce. It drummed on the barge's canvas awning, pooled in the seams of the deck planking, turned the river to a churning brown ribbon that swallowed the lantern-light of the free-town's docks. Sef sat under the awning with a mortar and pestle, grinding dried feverfew into powder she probably wouldn't need, because grinding gave her something to do with the restlessness that had lived under her ribs since they'd fled the city.

Brannoc slept below, or tried to. The wound in his side had closed, thanks to Zephyrine's charm-work and Sef's own stubborn needlecraft, but the fever came and went like a tide, and each time it crested, Sef counted his breaths and hated how slow they'd grown. Odile stood at the bow in the wet dark, arms crossed, watching the water as if it might confess something. And Tam—

Tam was gone.

Not gone-gone. Not yet. His bedroll was still rolled against the mast, his tin cup still hanging from its hook, his boots—no. His boots were missing. Sef stared at the empty space where his boots should have been for a long moment before the truth of it settled into her stomach like a swallowed stone.

Tam never went anywhere without his boots. He complained about them constantly—the left one rubbed, the sole was cracked—but he wore them like a second skin, because Tam had been a street child before he'd been a smuggler, and street children learned early that bare feet on cold cobbles was the first way a city told you it didn't want you.

Sef set down the mortar. She listened. Under the rain: the creak of the mooring lines, Brannoc's labored breathing, Odile's boots shifting at the bow. No fourth set of footsteps. But the gangplank's rope rail was wet on the underside too, which meant someone had gripped it recently enough that the rain hadn't yet erased the warmth—or hadn't fully soaked the fibers where hands had been.

She didn't wake Odile. That was the decision she would turn over and over in the years after, the one she'd defend and condemn in equal measure: she didn't wake Odile, because if she was wrong, she'd have raised an alarm against a crewmate for nothing, and if she was right—

If she was right, then every minute mattered, and Odile's grief would only slow her down.

Sef pulled her oilskin hood up, took the short knife from her kit—the one she used for cutting poultice cloth, though it would cut other things—and slipped down the gangplank into the rain.

* * *

Hollow Court at night was a different animal than Hollow Court by day. By day it was noise and haggling, fish guts and forged papers, a town built on the principle that no crown's law reached past its last tollgate. By night it became what it had always secretly been: a place where people went to disappear, and a place where people went to sell the disappeared.

The rain drove most of the taverns' spillage indoors, so the streets belonged to Sef and the puddles and the few figures hunched against doorways. She moved the way she'd learned to move in the plague camps of her youth—fast, low, reading the ground. Mud held prints. The cobbles near the fish market held a print she knew: Tam's cracked left sole left a distinctive crescent, like a tiny moon bitten by something hungry.

She followed the bitten moons east, toward the customs house, toward the warden barracks that the crown maintained in Hollow Court despite every treaty that said it shouldn't. Of course he'd go there. Where else? The silver the crown paid for informants was real silver, stamped and heavy, and Tam owed money to people whose collections methods made warden interrogations look gentle.

That was the thing about Tam that Sef couldn't stop turning over, even as she ran: he wasn't bad. That was the whole rot of it. He wasn't bad. He was the one who taught the dock kids dice games and always lost on purpose. He was the one who'd carried Brannoc's gear for three days without being asked after the Kessrin job. He sang badly and laughed easily and once, in a lean winter, had given away half his share of a haul to a widow on Candlerow Lane and lied about losing it gambling so no one would make a fuss.

And he owed two hundred crowns to the Gull Street syndicate, and they had sent him a finger in a small box as a reminder, and desperation did not care what kind of man you were. Desperation was a solvent. It ate through loyalty the way lye ate through grease—slowly, then all at once, leaving nothing behind but a clean, shining absence where the person used to be.

Sef caught sight of him at the mouth of Tanner's Row: a broad-shouldered shape in a dripping coat, walking fast, shoulders hunched like a man walking into wind even though the wind was at his back. Walking like a man trying to outrun his own shadow.

"Tam!" Her voice cracked out over the rain.

He flinched. She saw it even from thirty paces—the whole body flinch of a man caught—and then he ran.

* * *

The chase through Hollow Court lasted eleven minutes. Sef counted them later, in the way she counted everything when grief needed a container: eleven minutes of sliding cobbles and shouted curses and the rain coming down so hard the whole world seemed to be drowning.

He was faster than her—he'd always been, all whipcord and long stride—but she knew the town better. She cut through the tannery yard, vaulted the drainage ditch, came out ahead of him on Cooper's Lane just as he rounded the corner, and grabbed his coat.

They went down together into the mud. He was stronger, but she was angrier, and anger is a kind of strength that doesn't announce itself until it's already won. She got a knee across his chest and the knife at his throat—not pressing, just present, a cold comma of steel against skin.

"Say I'm wrong," she said. Rain hammered her hood, ran off the brim between them. "Say it. Say you're going to see your girl in Salt Row. Say you're buying medicine. Lie to me well enough and I'll believe you, Tam, I swear on my mother's grave I'll believe you, because I want to."

Under her, Tam wept. There was no other word for it. A big man, a smuggler, a survivor of a hundred close scrapes, lying in the mud of Cooper's Lane and crying like the child he'd been on some other city's streets, rain washing the tears away almost before they left his eyes.

"They have my sister," he said.

Sef went still.

"You didn't know that," Tam said, laughing, awful, wet laughter. "Nobody knows that. I keep her quiet, I pay for her room in Millbrook, I tell everyone my family's dead because dead family can't be leverage. But they found her, Sef. Gull Street found her, and they sold her name to the wardens, and a warden captain came to me three days ago and said—" his voice broke apart and reassembled itself around the words like bone knitting crooked— "he said, bring us the Ember-bearer or your sister hangs as a gifted witch, and I thought, I thought, it's just stories, the Ember, it's a fireside tale, nobody actually—"

"But you think it's Zephyrine." Sef's voice came out flat and strange. "You've watched them. You've seen the charms."

"I've seen them heal a wound that should have killed Brannoc." Tam's eyes were red-rimmed and desperate and terribly, horribly honest. "I've seen them do things maps can't explain. And I thought—if it's true, if they're really the Ember, then they're already doomed, aren't they? The whole crown is hunting them either way. One more informer doesn't change whether they burn. But it changes whether Mara burns. Don't you see? It doesn't change anything for them and it changes everything for her."

"That's not true," Sef said. "You know that's not true. You're not selling a dead man's name. You're selling ours. You're selling Brannoc while he sleeps with a fever. You're selling Odile, who took you off the docks when you were sixteen and starving—"

"I know." It came out of him like a sob. "I know, I know, I know—"

"Then get up," Sef said. "Get up and come back to the barge and we'll figure it out. We'll get your sister out of Millbrook tonight, all of us, we'll—"

"The wardens already have her." Tam said it quietly, and the quiet was worse than any scream. "They picked her up yesterday. She's in the barracks cellar right now. The exchange happens at dawn. If I don't walk through those doors with what they want, she hangs tomorrow, and if I warn you and run, they hang her anyway and hunt me besides." He looked up at Sef through the rain, and his face was the face of a man standing at the bottom of a well looking up at a coin-sized circle of sky. "There's no road out of this where everybody lives, Sef. I looked. I looked all night. There isn't one."

Sef knelt there in the mud with her knife at a good man's throat and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade of her own, that he was right. That was the heartbreak of it—not cruelty, not greed, but arithmetic. Desperation had done the sums for him and found that love, weighed in bodies, came out heavier on one side. He had chosen his sister the way any of them would have. The way Sef might have, if it were Brannoc bleeding below decks. If it were Odile in a cellar.

That was the unforgivable part: not that Tam was a traitor, but that he wasn't. Not really. He was just a person, and persons broke, and the crown had learned long ago that you didn't need torture to break a person—you only needed someone they loved and a deadline.

"Let me up," Tam whispered. "Please. If you stop me, I'll just find another way, and I'll hate you for it, and Mara will still hang. Let me up, Sef. Let me be the villain. It's easier for everyone if one of us gets to be the villain."

For a moment—a long, rain-drowned moment—Sef considered it. Considered letting him go, letting him carry the betrayal alone so the rest of them could carry the clean rage instead. Rage was lighter than grief. Villains made things simple.

Then she thought of Zephyrine's face when they'd shown the crew the archive charter, the terrible tenderness in their voice as they read aloud the founding lie. She thought of Brannoc's fevered breathing. She thought of Odile saying *we are not people who sell people*, back on the first night, when the question had been theoretical.

"No," Sef said.

She got off him. She pulled him up out of the mud by his collar, and she held onto that collar, and she put her forehead against his, rain streaming down between their faces like a curtain neither of them could part.

"I'm not letting you be the villain," she said. "Because you're not one. And because the moment you walk through those doors, you stop being someone we can save. Do you understand? Right now, tonight, in this mud—you can still be saved. Mara can still be saved. The dawn hasn't happened yet. Nothing has happened yet. Everything that's going to happen is still changeable, and I will be damned before I let you hand the crown the only family I've got because a warden captain wrote the sums for you."

Tam shook under her grip. "They'll hang her."

"Maybe," Sef said. "Or maybe we steal her first. We're smugglers, Tam. Stealing people out of cages is literally our trade. Give me until dawn. Give me the chance to be smarter than a warden captain. That's all I'm asking—not forgiveness, not absolution. Just a head start."

* * *

He gave her until dawn. That was the tragedy in miniature, the seed of everything that came after: he gave her until dawn.

They went back to the barge together through the rain, and Sef woke Odile, and Odile listened with a face like carved stone while Sef laid it all out—Tam, the debt, the sister, the barracks, the exchange. Tam stood dripping on the deck and did not defend himself, which was somehow worse than any defense.

Odile looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at Sef, and something passed between them that had no words in it—something about how the crown made traitors the way rivers made mud, patiently, from ordinary stuff.

"Millbrook," Odile said at last. "How many guards?"

"Two at the door, maybe three inside," Tam said hoarsely. "It's a lodging house. They're not expecting trouble."

"Nothing ever is," Odile said, and reached for her sword.

But the rain had other plans, and so did the wardens. Later—much later, in the years of retelling—Sef would understand that the moment Tam had failed to return by midnight, the warden captain had known. Wardens always knew. Doubt was a luxury the crown trained out of its men; suspicion was bred into them like a coat of fur.

The crew came off the barge in the black hour before dawn, moving fast through streets that had gone strangely, ominously quiet. And as they crossed the fish market, Sef heard it: the bell of the customs house, ringing out of pattern, ringing alarm.

Lanterns bloomed along the quay. Boots, many boots, on cobblestone. And above the rooftops, rising with the smoke-gray light of dawn, a herald's amplified voice, flat and official, reciting names:

"—the smuggler vessel *Kestrel's Debt*, wanted for harboring the Ember-bearer. Odile Vane. Brannoc Stone. Sef of the Marshlands. Tam of Highmoor, informant, to be rewarded. And the cartographer Zephyrine Whitlock, the Ember-bearer, sought alive—"

Sef stood in the rain in the middle of the fish market and felt the words go through her like cold iron. *To be rewarded.* They'd listed Tam among the wanted and the rewarded in the same breath, and she understood with sick certainty what that meant: the crown had never intended to honor the exchange. Tam's sister had been bait twice over—bait for Tam, and Tam himself was bait for the crew. The whole trap had been built the night the warden captain first knocked on Tam's door, and every road out of it had been false except the ones the crown had drawn.

Beside her, Tam made a sound like something breaking.

"They're going to hang her anyway," he whispered. "Aren't they. They were always going to hang her."

Sef didn't answer. Because down the quay, torches now—wardens in file, and at their head a figure in gray with a warden captain's insignia, and behind the warden captain, dragged in chains, a thin young woman with Tam's same dark hair and Tam's same stubborn chin, barefoot on the wet stones.

Mara.

The warden captain smiled through the rain, and it was the smile of a man holding all the cards and knowing it.

"Now then," the captain called out, voice carrying clear over the water. "Shall we talk about the Ember?"

* * *

Later—much later—Sef would count the cost of that night the way she counted everything: in minutes, in breaths, in the distance between what they'd been and what they were becoming. Eleven minutes of chase. One hour of mercy given and spent. And a dawn that arrived not with rescue but with a choice, laid out on the wet stones of Hollow Court like a knife on a tray: the Ember-bearer's freedom for a sister's life, and behind it all, patient as tide, the shadow of Lord Aldric Vane, who had never needed Tam's information at all—who had simply needed the crew to learn what fear could make of love.

The rain kept falling. Somewhere beyond the hills, the moon was turning toward its thirteenth face. And Sef, healer, pragmatist, keeper of the crew's broken bodies, stood with her knife still in her hand and understood at last that the crown's cruelest weapon had never been fire or chain or rope.

It was this: making good people betray each other, and then charging them for the privilege of grieving it.
