## 14. Crown and Cinder

The mountains announced themselves long before we saw them—a gray suggestion on the horizon like a held breath, then a blue-gray promise, and by the third morning after Hollow Court, a wall of stone and pine that scraped the belly of the sky.

We traveled light and fast, or as fast as Brannoc's bulk allowed, which was faster than any of us expected. He moved through the foothills the way water moves through a channel: unhurried, inevitable, finding the path that wanted to be found. Sef walked beside him most days, arguing about herbs. Odile ranged ahead with her spyglass, a silhouette against the ridgelines. Tam kept to the rear, quiet in a way that had begun to itch at me like wool worn too long.

And I walked in the middle of them, carrying a secret that grew heavier with every mile.

The Ember did not sleep anymore. That was the truth I could not speak. Since the archive beneath Highmoor—since I had read the charter with my own eyes and learned what my bloodline was, what it had always been—the thing inside me had begun to stir like a sleeper turning toward morning. It woke in small ways. My fingertips glowed faintly when I drew maps now, so I drew them at night, under blankets, sweating with the effort of concealment. When Sef brushed past me on the barge deck, the air between us crackled, and Sef frowned and rubbed their arms and said nothing. When I dreamed, I dreamed in firelight that belonged to people three hundred years dead.

I told myself: *after the mountains. After we're safe. Then I'll tell them.*

I told myself this the way a drowning man tells himself the shore is close.

* * *

It happened on the day the road narrowed.

The pass we'd chosen was an old smuggler's route—one Odile knew from years she didn't talk about—and for two days it wound upward through pine forest so dense that noon arrived as a rumor. The air smelled of resin and cold stone and, underneath it, something else. Something wrong.

I smelled it before anyone spoke of it. Cold iron. Oiled leather. The particular absence of birdsong that means men with weapons are standing very still.

"Odile." My voice came out flat and strange. "Stop."

She stopped. She'd heard it too—I saw it in the set of her shoulders, the way her hand drifted to the hatchet at her belt without hurry, without drama. Fifteen years of hiding had taught me to read danger; five days of traveling with Odile Vane had taught me she wore it like a second skin.

"Wardens," she said quietly. "Ahead. And—" her nostrils flared, head tilting, listening to the forest the way Brannoc listened to rivers— "behind."

Sef swore softly. Brannoc set down the pack he carried and rolled his shoulders, and there was nothing gentle in him at all just then, only a vast and patient readiness.

"How many?" I asked.

"Doesn't matter," Odile said. "What matters is the ridge to the east. There's a goat track. Steep, ugly, but it climbs, and wardens don't climb well in armor." She looked at me, and her eyes were hard and bright as coins. "You first. You're the one they want."

"I'm not leaving—"

"You're not *asking*. Move."

That was when the arrows came.

They came out of the trees in a hissing arc, three of them, and one took Tam through the shoulder and spun him into the bracken with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life. The forest erupted. Wardens in gray-and-crimson livery rose from behind fallen pines like the forest itself deciding to stand up, and their captain's voice rang out clear and cold across the clearing:

"In the name of the crown! Surrender the Ember-bearer!"

Not *surrender*. Not *yield*. *Surrender the Ember-bearer.* As if I were cargo. As if I were a thing that could be handed over, wrapped and weighed.

Odile moved. I have seen her move before—quick, decisive—but never like this, never with the whole crew folding around her motion like a single body. Sef dragged Tam upright and shoved him toward the goat track. Brannoc simply stepped between us and the wardens, and became a wall.

"Go," he said. Just that. His voice was calm as deep water.

There were more of them than I'd counted—eight, ten, twelve, pouring out of the green—and their crossbows sang again, and Brannoc caught one bolt on his pauldron and another on his forearm raised like a shield, and he *laughed*, low and terrible, and waded forward into them.

I should have run. Odile was screaming at me to run. Sef had Tam over their shoulders now, staggering up the track, and Odile had my wrist in a grip that would leave bruises, hauling me toward the rocks—

And then I saw the warden behind Brannoc. The one with the long knife, the one Brannoc couldn't see because he was holding two others at bay with his bare hands, the one who drew back his arm to throw.

Everything I have ever hidden, everything I have ever buried, everything I have starved and smothered for fifteen years—it all came up out of me at once.

I threw out my hand and the world went white-gold.

The knife never reached Brannoc. It hung in the air, trembling, its blade glowing cherry-red until the warden dropped it with a howl. Light poured off my skin like heat off a forge, and every warden in the clearing staggered, shielding their eyes, and I heard someone shout—Aldric Vane's captain, or Aldric himself, I couldn't tell—"*There! There, take it, take the Ember!*"

Take it. They thought I was a lamp to be carried away.

Brannoc turned. In the blaze of my own making I saw his face—awed, unafraid, *smiling*, gods help me, smiling at what I was—and then I saw the crossbowman on the ridge above, the one no one had marked, leveling his weapon not at me but at the giant standing between me and the world.

"No!" I screamed. "BRANNOC—"

He turned too late. The bolt took him high in the chest, below the collarbone, punching through with a wet heavy sound, and the great wall of him swayed—and did not fall. He put his hand over the wound as if he could hold himself together by will alone, and he turned his back to the wardens, deliberately, fully, and used the last of his enormous strength to be a door instead of a wall.

"Up," he rasped. "Girl—*up the track*—"

Odile hauled me. Sef appeared out of the rocks like a fury and got a shoulder under Brannoc's arm, and somehow—by miracle, by stubbornness, by the sheer mass of love that had accreted around this man in five short days—we got him moving. Arrows chased us. One split stone a hand's width from my cheek. Behind us the wardens' shouts fell away as the track steepened, as our pursuers' armor betrayed them on the scree, as the mountain closed around us like a fist.

Tam made it forty yards further before he collapsed. Sef knelt over him, hands red, face gray. And Brannoc sank down against a boulder with a sigh that seemed to empty half the mountain's air, and did not get up again.

* * *

Night came early under the pines, and cold with it.

Sef worked by lantern-light, and I worked beside them, and this was the first time since the barge that I let myself use the gift openly—no disguise, no trinket-craft, no lies stitched over the shining thing beneath. I pressed my palms to Brannoc's chest and felt for the damage the way I felt for the lines of unmapped countries: carefully, humbly, listening.

The bolt had missed his heart by the width of two fingers. It had not missed much else. A lung, bruised and bleeding inward. Splinters of bone where the shaft had shattered against rib. I could slow the bleeding. I could ease the drowning pressure in his chest. I could not mend him—not here, not with the Ember thrashing inside me like a netted fish, spilling everywhere, too raw and new to be aimed.

"He needs a surgeon," Sef said. Their voice was stripped down to wire. "A real one. With a table and clean linen and laudanum. What you're doing is a rope thrown to a drowning man. It isn't a boat."

"Then I'll keep throwing rope."

"That's not what I—" Sef stopped. Rubbed their eyes with the back of a bloody wrist. "No. Keep throwing rope. I'll hold the other end."

Above us, Odile stood watch on the rocks, her back to the firelight, her spyglass useless in the dark. She had not spoken to me since the clearing. Not during the climb, not while we cut boughs for Brannoc's bed, not while Sef stitched and I burned my gift down to embers trying to knit what stitches couldn't reach. When I brought her tea, she took it without looking at me. When I said her name, she looked at the treeline instead.

I understood why. Understanding did not make it bearable.

Because I had seen her face, in that first white-gold instant. Before the arrow. Before the blood. I had seen Odile Vane look at me with wonder—and then, as Brannoc folded, with something else entirely. Not surprise. She had suspected; she had said so on the barge, weeks ago, with that crooked half-smile: *You're hiding more than a route, mapmaker.* But suspicion is a coin and knowledge is a debt, and I had let her carry the suspicion while I hoarded the knowledge, and now Brannoc lay bleeding because the debt had come due in the worst possible currency.

If I had told them. If I had said, on the barge, in Hollow Court, on any of a hundred ordinary evenings: *the crown isn't hunting the gifted, it's hunting me, and where I walk, the hunt follows*—would they have taken this road? Would Odile have chosen a different pass? Would Brannoc be breathing shallowly against a boulder instead of snoring in his bunk?

Guilt is heavier than chains. Chains you can pick at. Chains you can see. Guilt sits inside the ribs and wears your own heartbeat as its lock.

Near midnight, Brannoc woke.

His eyes found me first. Of everyone in the firelight—Sef with their needle, Tam gray and groaning, Odile a statue on the rocks—his eyes found mine, and crinkled, and he tried to smile around the pain.

"There she is," he rumbled. Each word cost him; I could hear the labor of it, the wet catch. "The mapmaker. Burning like a festival lamp." A breath, wheezing. "Knew it. Knew there was something... First day on the barge. You looked at the Wraithmarsh like you were greeting an old friend."

"Don't talk," Sef said.

"Never listened before. Won't start now." His huge hand fumbled across the blanket, found mine, engulfed it. His skin was fever-hot. "Zephyrine Whitlock. Listen. Whatever the little captain says—" a flicker of his eyes toward Odile's rigid back— "whatever she doesn't say. She's been carrying that brother of hers like a stone in her boot for twenty years. Don't you carry it for her. Carry *him* instead. He's lighter."

"Brannoc—"

"And you." His grip tightened, gentle even now, gentle always. "Whatever's burning in you. It looked like hope to me. Up there. All that light." His eyes were closing. "Looked like morning."

He slept. Sef checked the bandages twice, three times, and finally sat back against the boulder, spent, and looked at me across the dying fire.

"He'll live," Sef said. "Probably. If the wound stays clean. If we find shelter within two days. If the fever breaks." A pause. "Lots of ifs, Zephyrine. We're a crew made of ifs now."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't." Sef's voice cracked like a whip, and then softened just as fast, which was worse. "Don't apologize to me. I asked you once, on the barge, plain as plain: *what are you?* And you gave me half. Half was enough, then. It isn't enough now." They fed a stick to the fire. "But I watched you today. When you lit up like the sun coming early—you weren't thinking of yourself. You were thinking of *him*. So I'm not afraid of you. I want that understood. I'm not afraid of you. I'm afraid *for* you, and I don't know yet which is worse."

They went to check Tam's dressing. I sat with Brannoc's enormous hand still curled around mine, and I listened to him breathe—each breath a negotiation, each breath a victory—and I made promises to the fire that I intended to keep this time. No more halves. No more convenient silences. Whatever it cost.

* * *

Dawn found the pass wrapped in cloud, and Odile found me at the edge of camp.

She came silently, the way she did everything that mattered, and stood looking east through the thinning mist at the road we'd left behind. Far below, tiny as ants, gray shapes moved along the tree line. Wardens. Casting wide, circling, patient. Aldric Vane did not need to hurry. He knew where the mountains funnelled us. He had known before we crossed the river; perhaps he had known before we left Hollow Court.

"They'll sweep the lower passes," Odile said at last. Her voice was level, captain-flat, addressed to the horizon rather than to me. "Force us higher. There's one way through from here—the old shrine road. Ruins, halfway up. No cover, no water, but no wardens either. Too steep for armor." She paused. "Too steep for a wounded man, if I'm honest. But honesty's been scarce lately, so maybe I shouldn't start."

"Odile—"

"You had a hundred nights." Now she turned, and her eyes were terrible—not with rage, which I could have borne, but with the exhaustion of someone who has been lied to by everyone she has ever loved. "A hundred nights around my fire, Zephyrine. You watched me plan routes around warden patrols. You watched me bargain with informants. You watched me lie awake doing sums—how many silver, how many lives, how far—and the whole time, you were the sum. You were the number I couldn't see." Her jaw tightened. "Brannoc took a bolt meant for a secret. Do you understand that? Not for you. For the *secret*. If you'd trusted us with the truth, we'd have chosen different roads. We'd have run the river country, scattered, gone to ground. Instead we walked single file up a mountain with a beacon in our middle."

"I was going to tell you. After the mountains—"

"After." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only rust. "After. Everything with you is after. After the next town, after the next border, after the moon turns." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice, and somehow that was crueler than shouting. "Here's what I can't get past, mapmaker. It's not the lie. Lies I know. I've dealt in them my whole life. It's that you let us love you *while you hid*. You let Brannoc teach you river songs. You let Sef argue herbs with you till midnight. You let me—" she stopped. Started again, quieter, which hurt more. "You let us build a family around a hollow place, and you never once trusted us to hold its weight."

"I was protecting you."

"I know." And that was the worst of it—that she said it without scorn, with something almost like grief. "That's what my brother says, too. Every cruelty he's ever done, he does to protect the realm. Protection is just a lie we tell about control." She shouldered her pack. "Get Brannoc ready. We move in an hour. Shrine road."

She walked to the head of the column and did not look back.

* * *

The shrine road climbed through cloud the rest of that day and into the next, switchbacking up a spine of naked rock where the wind spoke in a language older than the crown. We carried Brannoc in shifts—Sef and I, then Odile and I, Tam limping behind with his arm bound to his chest, refusing, in his shame, to meet anyone's eyes. Twice we heard horns in the valleys below, thin and distant as memory. Twice we froze among the stones until the sound passed.

Toward dusk of the second day, the wind shifted, and the clouds tore open, and I saw it.

A shrine. Half-ruined, clinging to a shelf of rock above us—broken columns, a dome collapsed into a crown of rubble, walls stained black with centuries of weather. But even ruined, even half-swallowed by the mountain, it *glowed*. Faintly. The way coals glow under ash. The way my fingertips had glowed over maps for fifteen years, and I had called it candlelight, and lied.

The Ember woke in my chest like a bird hearing its own name.

I stopped walking. Odile, ahead of me with Brannoc's arm over her shoulders, felt me stop and turned, ready with another cutting word—and then she followed my gaze up to the ruin, and whatever she saw in my face made the word die unspoken.

"What is it?" Sef asked, coming up behind, breath ragged.

I could not answer. The light in the shrine was answering *me*—pulsing now, slow and steady, like a heart. Like a door. And beneath my feet, in the bones of the mountain, something vast and old turned over in its sleep and began, at last, to remember.

"It knows we're here," I said. My voice sounded far away, borrowed. "It's been waiting."

Odile stared at me—at the glow rising through my sleeves, my throat, the whites of my eyes—and for the first time since the clearing, since the arrow, since the silence, she spoke to me directly.

"To what?" she said. "Waiting for *what*?"

The shrine flared, gold against the darkening snow, and I started climbing toward it.
