## 15. The Ember's Memory

The mountain did not want them.

Zephyrine felt it in the way the wind came at them sideways, in the way the pines bent and hissed like a crowd murmuring behind a hand, in the way the path—no path at all, really, only a suggestion of one—kept trying to slide them back down toward the valley where the wardens' torches had gone out like swallowed stars. The stone underfoot was black and slick with old snowmelt, and every breath was a small cold knife. Somewhere below, Brannoc lay fevered in the hollow of a fallen tree, Sef's poultices dark against the ruin the warden's blade had made of him, and Odile stood watch with her back turned to Zephyrine as though silence were a wall she could build with her shoulders alone.

Zephyrine climbed anyway.

They told themselves it was for water, for the spring Sef had said might seep from the high rocks, but that was a cartographer's lie, a mapmaker's mercy, and they knew it even as they thought it. The truth was simpler and far more terrible: something above them was calling. It had been calling since the ambush, since the moment Brannoc's blood had soaked into the frozen ground and Zephyrine's own blood had answered it, singing up through their ribs like a struck bell. Since the moment the Ember had turned over in its sleep beneath their sternum and *noticed* them.

*Come,* it said, without words, the way heat says come to a shivering hand. *You have carried me long enough. Come and remember.*

So they climbed.

The shrine revealed itself the way secrets do—not all at once, but in pieces, each piece making the next inevitable. First a stair, half-swallowed by the mountain, its steps worn concave by feet that had been dead five hundred years. Then a lintel carved with interlocking rings, the old sigil of the gifted houses, defaced by chisels that had left the marks of their fury still sharp in the stone. Then the shrine itself: an open crown of broken columns on the mountain's shoulder, ringed by fallen statues whose faces had been hammered smooth, so that they knelt forever with the blank eyes of eggs. Snow had drifted through the gaps in the roofless colonnade and lay in pale ribbons across a floor of mosaic, and where the snow had melted away, the mosaic showed—stars. Thousands of them, gold and lapis and crushed shell, arranged not in constellations but in rivers, flowing inward toward a center that someone had tried to pry out of the floor long ago. They had failed. Whatever had been set there had been set with more than mortar.

Zephyrine stood at the threshold and their breath stopped in their throat, because they knew this place. Not from study. Not from any map they had ever drawn or sold or burned. They knew it the way you know your own heartbeat—without ever having seen it.

*I have been here before,* they thought, and then corrected themselves with the strange calm of someone standing at the edge of a very long fall: *No. I have been here always.*

They stepped inside.

The Ember woke.

It did not happen gently. There was no slow brightening, no tender unfurling. It was a door blown open in a storm—all the warmth Zephyrine had banked and buried for fifteen years roaring up at once, filling their chest, their skull, their fingertips, until they could not tell whether they were standing in the ruined shrine or kneeling in it, whether the snow around them was melting or whether it was simply fleeing. Light poured out of them, gold-white, casting their shadow huge and doubled against the broken columns, and Zephyrine had the distant, lucid thought: *so this is what I look like when I stop lying.*

Then the memory took them.

---

It began with fire, because all their bloodline's stories began with fire—but not the fire of destruction. This was older and stranger: a hearth-fire, tended. Zephyrine saw hands that were not their hands feeding split logs to a flame in a stone ring, and understood, with the wordless certainty of dream, that this was the first work of the gifted. Not dominion. *Tending.* The Ember had never been a weapon. It had been a hearth—the first hearth, the one from which every flame in the realm had been lit, carried from hearth to hearth by keepers who swore an oath so old it predated language: *that no fire should go out while a keeper lives; that no keeper should hold the fire for themselves.*

Generation upon generation, the memory flowed, and Zephyrine drowned in it gladly, terribly. They saw the keepers of the Ember through five hundred years—a woman with river-grey eyes passing the spark to her son at midwinter; twin brothers dividing the keeping between them so neither would grow proud; a child of eight receiving it too early because plague had taken everyone else, and growing up gentle anyway, which the memory seemed to consider the greater miracle. They saw the Ember passed hand to hand like a lantern down a dark road, and in every pair of hands it looked different—in some it burned quiet as coals, in some fierce as forge-light—but it was always the same fire, patient, warm, *given*, never taken.

And then they saw the king.

He came in winter, in ermine, with a retinue of men whose armor was new and whose eyes were hungry, and he knelt at the hearth of the last keeper—an old man, a gardener of flames, who offered the king warmth and bread and asked nothing. Zephyrine watched through the keeper's own eyes as the king ate the bread, warmed his hands, praised the fire. Watched as the king's gaze lingered on the hearth a moment too long. Watched him understand, in the way of powerful men, that what he had been given freely he could instead possess.

The theft happened at night. Zephyrine felt it happen *in their own chest*—the tearing of it, the Ember ripped from its keeper not by force of arms but by a rite the king had bought from a frightened scholar, a chain-work of binding-sigils that closed around the living flame like a snare around a bird. The keeper did not die quickly. He burned from the inside for nine days, and the memory held every one of them, and Zephyrine wept tears that fell steaming onto the ancient mosaic, and the memory did not stop, because the dead do not know mercy and memories are the dead speaking.

But here was the thing the histories had burned, the thing the crown had spent five centuries ensuring no one learned:

*The Ember could not be kept by a thief.*

It dimmed in the king's iron casket. It guttered. And the king—who had stolen fire because he feared the dark, who had bound the gifted because he feared their tending—grew afraid that his theft would be discovered, that the flame would die in his hands and be found empty. So he did what fearful kings do. He made a story. He sent out criers and chroniclers with a single tale: that the gifted had risen in rebellion, that they had coveted the throne, that the war that followed—the war that scorched the borderlands and emptied whole valleys—had begun with gifted treachery. That magic itself was rebellion. That the only safety for the realm was the eradication of the ember-bloodlines, root and branch.

And the gifted, leaderless, grieving, their hearth-keeper murdered and their sacred fire stolen, had fought back—and so had proven the lie true in the only way that mattered. The war wrote its own justification in ash.

Five hundred years of purges. Fifteen years of outlawry in Highmoor. A neighbor dragged from her home in Chapter—dragged from her home in chains while informants counted silver. Every pyre, every proclamation, every frightened family teaching children to hide their small bright gifts—it had all been built on a king's fear of being caught. The persecution was not policy. It was *concealment*. The crown had been hiding its crime for five hundred years, and every gifted soul burned was another witness silenced.

Zephyrine came back to themselves kneeling on the mosaic, snowmelt soaking through their trousers, light still leaking from between their fingers like a held breath escaping. The stars under their hands glowed faintly in answer, gold waking along the old rivers of the design, and Zephyrine understood that the shrine had recognized them—that this place had been waiting, keeperless, for longer than kingdoms.

And in the settling quiet after the vision, one truth assembled itself out of all the others, cold and precise as a compass needle:

Aldric Vane knew.

He was not hunting the gifted. The gifted were noise, cover, the broad net that hid the narrow hook. He was hunting the *Ember*—and not to destroy it. No inquisitor burns what he intends to use. He wanted it for the throne: the stolen fire returned to the crown's hand, the binding renewed, the power that had let one king rewrite the world placed once more in royal grasp. The hunt, the purges, the thirteenth moon with its old prophecy ticking down like a clock in a locked room—Aldric meant to be standing beside the throne when the Ember woke, and take it as his predecessors had taken everything.

*Not a weapon,* Zephyrine thought, and the thought was steady now, settled, theirs. *Not a treasure either. A hearth. Mine to tend—or mine to give. But never again to be taken.*

That was the difference, they realized, between destiny and a cage. Destiny asked everything of you and still left you the choosing. The cage only asked that you stay small.

Zephyrine rose. The light folded back into them, obedient, intimate, a coal cupped in cupped hands, and for the first time in fifteen years they did not feel smaller for hiding it. They felt like a keeper.

They turned toward the descent, toward the fallen tree and the fever and Odile's wall of turned shoulders, and paused at the threshold of the broken columns. Behind them, in the heart of the mosaic where the thieves had failed to pry the center loose, the star-rivers pulsed once, softly, like a heart deciding to keep beating.

Below, far down the black flank of the mountain, a line of torches had appeared on the valley road. Dozens. Moving with the unhurried confidence of those who already know where the quarry sleeps.

Wardens. Coming for the shrine. Coming for them.

Aldric had known about this place, too.

* * *

Zephyrine took the stairs three at a time, light spilling unhidden from their skin, past the defaced lintel, down the swallowed stair, the cold air streaming past them like a river running the wrong way. Their mind was already mapping—reflex, refuge, the oldest habit of a life built on concealment. The valley road curved east around a shoulder of scree; the wardens would reach the tree-line in an hour, perhaps less if they had a gifted tracker with them, and Aldric always had a gifted tracker. The barge was gone, burned at the landing two nights ago. Brannoc could not walk. Odile would not run.

Which left, Zephyrine thought, sliding down the last stretch of scree in a hiss of gravel, exactly one road, and it went straight through the truth.

They smelled the woodsmoke before they saw the camp—Sef's bitter green fire, the one that gave no light, the healer's trick—and then the fallen tree loomed up out of the dark, and Odile was on her feet with a knife drawn before Zephyrine's boots touched level ground.

"You were gone half the night." Her voice was flat as ice on a pond. "I don't care what you are anymore, Whitlock. But if you've led them here—"

"I didn't lead them." Zephyrine held up their hands, and the light between their fingers made Odile's eyes widen despite herself. "They're coming regardless. Down the valley road. An hour, maybe less."

Odile looked at the light. At Zephyrine's face. Something moved behind her expression—fear, fury, and underneath both, unwillingly, awe—and she hated it, Zephyrine could see her hating it.

"What did you find up there?"

"The truth." Zephyrine crouched by Brannoc, whose fever-bright eyes found them and crinkled with something like a smile. Gently, Zephyrine laid two fingers against the great man's brow and let the smallest thread of the Ember's warmth flow through—not healing, Sef's art was beyond them, but easing, the way a hearth eases a cold room. Brannoc sighed, and slept, truly slept. "All of it. The charter is a lie, Odile. The war was started by a king who stole our fire and blamed us for the burning. Five hundred years of purges to hide one theft."

Sef emerged from the shadows of the roots, herb-stained and skeptical. "And Vane knows this?"

"Vane knows better than anyone. He's not hunting the gifted. He's hunting *me*—not to kill. To take. To put the Ember back in the crown's fist before the thirteenth moon." Zephyrine stood, and the light steadied in them, calm as banked coals. "There's a shrine up there that's been waiting five hundred years for a keeper. He means to make sure the next keeper is a king."

Odile sheathed her knife slowly. She looked at Brannoc sleeping, at Sef, at the distant torches beginning to crawl up the valley road like a slow spill of embers, and Zephyrine watched her weigh it—flight, surrender, fight—with the speed of a woman who had been weighing impossible odds her whole life.

"An hour," she said at last. "One hour, one bad leg between us, and a mountain full of wardens." A grim ghost of her old grin. "Well. You're the mapmaker. Get us off this mountain."

Zephyrine looked up at the shrine glowing faintly on the heights, then down at the torches, and felt the Ember turn over inside them—not in fear, but in readiness, like a keeper hearing a knock at the door.

"There's a way," they said. "But it runs through the one place on this mountain even the wardens fear."

Above them, on the silent heights, the shrine's star-mosaic flared once—gold answering gold—and somewhere in the dark below, a warden's horn sounded the first note of the hunt.
