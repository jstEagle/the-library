## 16. What Odile Carries

The shrine had no name that Odile knew of, and the mountain around it did not seem to want one. Wind came off the peaks in long, gray breaths, moving through the broken colonnade where the crew had made their camp, and it carried with it the smell of old stone and older snow. Somewhere below, past the tree line, the world went on burning—Highmoor, the hunt, Aldric's wardens sweeping the low country like a comb through tangled hair. Up here there was only the wind, the wounded, and the waiting.

Odile sat on a fallen pillar with her back against a column stump worn smooth by centuries, a whetstone in one hand and a knife in the other, though she hadn't sharpened anything in an hour. The work was for her hands. Her mind was elsewhere, down in the dark of the shrine's inner chamber, where Brannoc lay.

He was sleeping now. Sef called it healing-sleep, said it was the body doing its honest work while the mind got out of the way, but Odile knew what she'd seen when they'd carried him in: the warden's blade had opened him from collarbone to ribs, and the blood had come out of him the way water leaves a breached hull—fast, indifferent, unstoppable. She had pressed her palms into the wound and felt his life pushing up between her fingers like a tide that would not be argued with. For three days he had burned with fever, muttering names Odile didn't know, and Sef had not slept, and Zephyrine had knelt at the edge of the sickroom like a penitent at an altar, hands folded, silent, useless in the way that only guilt can make a person useless.

On the fourth morning, Brannoc had opened his eyes, looked at Sef, and asked—in a voice scraped down to gravel—whether the others were safe.

That was Brannoc entire. A man built like a siege engine who woke from the edge of death asking after other people.

* * *

She remembered her brother the way you remember a house you grew up in: imperfectly, tenderly, with all the furniture rearranged by time.

Aldric before the crown was a boy who could not pass a locked door without wanting to know what was behind it. He was eight years older than Odile, which meant he was seventeen when their father died and she was nine, and she remembered him standing at the graveside in his borrowed black coat, too big in the shoulders, holding her hand so tightly her fingers went white. He had looked down at her then and said, *I'll take care of everything now.* And he had. That was the thing people forgot about Aldric Vane—he had kept every promise he ever made. He just kept making new ones to worse people.

Their mother had been a laundress in the palace quarter, and their father a locksmith, dead of a lung fever at thirty-four. They had been poor in the particular way of the crown city's lower terraces: clean, proud, and always one bad winter from ruin. Aldric had been brilliant the way a lamp is bright—everything near him lit up, and everything far away went dark. He read everything. He memorized everything. By fourteen he was correcting their parish priest on points of canon law; by sixteen he had caught the eye of a minor clerk in the Ministry of Edicts, and that clerk had been the first rung of a ladder Aldric climbed with both hands and never once looked down from.

But Odile remembered the boy underneath the ambition. She remembered him teaching her letters by writing them in spilled flour on the kitchen table because paper cost money. She remembered him carrying her on his shoulders through the Midwinter Fair, high above the crowd, telling her the names of all the constellations wrong on purpose so she'd correct him and feel clever. She remembered the night the wardens came to their street hunting a gifted dyer—a woman two doors down who could make colors sing—and Aldric, fifteen years old, had stood in the doorway with a fire poker in his hand and told the wardens the woman had already fled north. He had lied to the crown's face to protect someone with magic in his blood.

Odile had forgotten that night for years. Then she remembered it again, later, when it became unbearable to have known it.

The change came slowly, the way rot comes to timber—from the inside, invisibly, until one day the beam gives way. It was not one moment. It was the scholarship to the Ministry academy. It was the promotions. It was the fine gray coat replacing the rough brown one. It was the dinners he stopped coming home to, the questions he stopped asking, the way his eyes developed a habit of sliding past beggars and gifted-marked doorways as though noticing them might cost him something. And then, one winter, he came home wearing the white sash of an inquisitor's apprentice, and he stood in the kitchen where he'd once written her name in flour, and he said, "It's better this way, Odile. Order is a kindness. You'll understand when you're older."

She was eleven. She understood immediately.

The last time she saw him before she ran—the last time before Hollow Court, before the forged papers, before learning his name was on every proclamation nailed to every gate from here to the coast—they had fought in that same kitchen. She had been nineteen, already running salt and charms through the river trade, already half a smuggler, and he had found her packing and told her she was throwing her life away. She had told him he'd already thrown his away, he'd just done it slowly enough to call it a career. He had raised his hand—not to strike her, never that, but the gesture had been there, aborted, trembling—and she had seen in his face, for one naked instant, the boy with the fire poker, buried alive under thirty years of gray coats and edicts, screaming.

Then the mask came back down, and he said, coldly, "When they catch you, I won't be able to help you."

And she had said, "You couldn't help yourself," and walked out, and the door had closed behind her with the soft, final click of a lock she had turned herself.

Now he led the hunt. Now his wardens had put a blade into Brannoc, the gentlest man she had ever known, because Brannoc had put his body between a warden's steel and a person Odile had begun, without deciding to, without permitting herself to think of it in those words—

Family. The word arrived in her chest like a splinter working toward the heart.

* * *

Brannoc slept. Sef dozed sitting up beside him, chin on chest, finally surrendering to exhaustion. Tam was gone—had been gone since the rain, since the road, since Sef came back alone with wet eyes and mud to the knees and said only, "He wouldn't stop." The wound of that was still open in the crew, still bleeding quietly. Odile turned the knife over in her hands and did not sharpen it.

Zephyrine appeared in the colonnade doorway the way they seemed to do everything now: carefully, apologetically, as though taking up space were a crime. They carried two cups of willowbark tea, steam curling off them into the cold.

"I brought you one," Zephyrine said. "You haven't eaten properly since—"

"Since your Ember nearly got my best friend killed." Odile said it flatly, without heat, and watched Zephyrine flinch anyway. She took the cup. Their fingers brushed; Zephyrine's were cold as river stones. "Sit down, cartographer. I'm not going to bite you."

Zephyrine sat, perched on the far end of the pillar like a bird ready to fly. For a while neither of them spoke. The wind moved through the broken columns, playing them like the pipes of some enormous dead organ.

"Tell me about him," Zephyrine said at last. "Your brother."

Odile almost laughed. "Why? So you can map him? Chart his weaknesses?"

"So I can understand what's coming for us."

That was honest, at least. Odile wrapped her hands around the cup and let the heat soak into her palms, and heard herself begin to talk before she had decided to.

"He was the one who protected the gifted, once. Did you know that? When we were children. There was a dyer on our street—Maren, her name was—and when the wardens came, Aldric lied to them. Stood in our doorway with a fire poker and lied to the crown's face. I used to think that boy was still in there somewhere. Buried. Waiting." She stared into the tea. "I don't think that anymore. I think we bury our own dead, Zephyrine. We don't get to dig them up when it's convenient."

"You dug me up," Zephyrine said quietly. "In the Saltmarket. You owed me a debt and you paid it, and then you kept paying it, long after the debt was spent."

"That was different."

"Was it?"

"Yes." Odile set the cup down harder than she meant to. "Because you lied to us. Not the little lie either—I could forgive the little lie, the gift, the charms, all of it, gods know this crew runs on secrets. But you let Brannoc bleed for you without telling him what he was bleeding for. You let Sef stitch him closed and stand watch over a stranger. You let me—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through. "You let me start calling you family in my head. And family doesn't carry a secret that size. Family carries each other."

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned to face them fully. "Do you actually know what it cost? Because I've been doing the arithmetic, cartographer, every night since the ambush, and it keeps coming out the same. One Brannoc, nearly lost. One Tam, gone to the crown, gods and silver help him. One crew, marked men and women now, hunted across half the realm. All of it bought with your silence. And the worst part—the part I can't make peace with—is that I'd do it again. Take you in again. Rescue you again. Because some stubborn piece of me decided you were ours before I ever gave permission, and now I'm stuck with the decision."

Zephyrine's eyes were wet. They didn't wipe them. "I'm sorry."

"I know you are. And I'm not here for the apology." Odile stood, and the wind pulled at her coat, and she planted her feet the way she did on the deck of the barge in rough water. "Apologies are cheap currency. What I want is this: from tonight forward, no more silence. Whatever wakes in you, whatever the moon does, whatever the Ember wants—you tell us. First. Before you decide. Before you act. You've spent fifteen years carrying everything alone, and look what it's earned. You're not alone anymore. Either you believe that or you don't, but you don't get to believe it halfway—not with Brannoc lying in there with a wound the length of my forearm."

"And if what I tell you frightens you?" Zephyrine asked. "If the truth of what I am is more than—"

"Then we'll be frightened together, which is how families do it." She said it before she could soften it, and found, saying it aloud, that she meant it—that forgiveness wasn't a feeling that had arrived in her chest overnight, warm and complete. It was nothing like that. It was a decision, made the way she made decisions at the helm: reading the current, weighing the risk, and turning the wheel anyway because the alternative was drifting. "I'm choosing this, Zephyrine. Understand that. I'm not forgiving you because the hurt's gone. I'm forgiving you because I've decided you're worth the trouble, and Vane women decide things once."

For a long moment Zephyrine simply looked at her, and Odile watched something shift behind their eyes—the same look she'd seen on drowning sailors hauled aboard, the moment they understood the rope would hold.

"The moon turns thirteenth in nine days," Zephyrine said. "And there's something I have to tell you. Something I found in the archive, and something the Ember showed me in the shrine. About Aldric. About what he actually wants."

Odile felt the cold slide down her spine. "Say it."

Zephyrine glanced toward the inner chamber, where Brannoc slept and Sef dozed, then back at her, and when they spoke, their voice was low enough that the wind nearly stole it.

"Aldric isn't hunting the gifted to destroy the Ember," they said. "He's hunting it to *use* it. The throne has been starving without it for three hundred years—and he means to feed it before the thirteenth turn completes. Which means, Odile, that in nine days, your brother intends to make me kneel in the palace square and take what's in my blood."

The wind screamed through the colonnade. Down in the chamber, unseen, the Ember stirred in Zephyrine's veins like a coal breathed upon—and far to the south, past the tree line, past the marsh, past the walls of Highmoor, a man in a gray coat looked north at the mountains and smiled.
