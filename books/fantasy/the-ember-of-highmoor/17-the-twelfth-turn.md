## 17. The Twelfth Turn

The moon rose over the shrine that night, and Zephyrine counted its scars like a debtor counting coins.

Twelve turns. Twelve full moons since the Ember had first stirred in their blood, since the light beneath their skin had begun to answer the sky. One turn remained. They could feel it now even in daylight—a warmth behind the sternum, patient as a banked fire, growing with every dusk. When the thirteenth moon crested, it would wake fully. And Aldric Vane would be waiting for it.

Zephyrine sat alone on the shrine's broken steps, the charter's copy spread across their knees despite the cold. They had read it so many times the vellum had gone soft at the folds, the old ink worn to ghosts where their thumb kept returning: *the Ember's power, taken by royal decree, to be held by the crown in perpetuity.* Taken. Not given. Not won. Stolen, and then written over with a lie so old and so grand that an entire realm had built its laws upon it.

Fifteen years of hiding. Fifteen years of small charms sold as trinkets, of maps drawn in secret, of flinching from every warden's gaze. All of it resting on the crown's founding lie—and all of it ending in one moon-turn, whether Zephyrine willed it or not.

The question was no longer whether the truth would come out. The question was who would tell it, and how, and whether anyone would live long enough to hear it.

Behind them, inside the shrine, Brannoc coughed. A deep, wet sound that Sef answered with the clink of a bottle and a low word of instruction. The wound in Brannoc's side had closed, but the fever came and went like a tide, and each time it receded it left him thinner, grayer, his great hands restless on the blanket as though they missed holding something up.

Zephyrine folded the charter and went inside.

* * *

The shrine had been a place of healing once, before healing became heresy. Its vaulted roof had half fallen, letting the sky in through a wound shaped like a river delta, and moss climbed the walls in green rivers. Someone had carved prayers into the pillars—old words in the gifted script, the letters still faintly warm under Zephyrine's fingertips, as if the stone remembered being loved.

Brannoc lay beneath the largest breach, where moonlight pooled on his chest like spilled milk. Sef sat beside him, grinding something bitter-smelling in a mortar, their sharp face softened by exhaustion into something almost gentle.

"He asked for you," Sef said without looking up. "Kept saying your name between the shivers. Wanted to know if you'd decided."

"Decided what?"

"Don't play the fool, cartographer. It doesn't suit you." Sef set down the mortar and finally looked at them, eyes bright and tired. "You've been pacing that courtyard for three days like a fox in a snare. You've read that charter until it's falling apart. You think none of us can see what you're building toward?"

Zephyrine said nothing. Outside, wind moved through the pines, and somewhere below the mountain a night bird called three times and fell silent.

Sef wiped their hands on their apron and stood. "He's sleeping. Come outside when you're ready to stop lying to yourself. We'll all be around the fire." A pause, and then, quieter: "Whatever you've decided—we've already decided too. You just haven't heard us say it yet."

* * *

The fire was small, the way Odile liked them—enough to warm the hands, not enough to draw the eye. She sat cross-legged before it, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, and Tam sat apart from the rest, hunched, his shadow long and guilty against the rock. Since the rain-soaked road, since Sef had come back alone with mud to the waist and failure written across their face, Tam had not spoken unless spoken to. He was here still. That was either loyalty or shame, and Zephyrine suspected it was both, braided together too tightly to separate.

Odile looked up as Zephyrine approached. Her face was still guarded—the fracture of those weeks ago had healed crooked, the way bones do—but she did not look away.

"Well," she said. "You've got the look. The one you get when you're about to hand someone a map and ruin their whole life."

Zephyrine almost smiled. Almost. "I need to show you something."

They unrolled the charter across the flat stone between them, weighting the corners with pebbles, and turned it so the firelight fell across the old script. Then they told it plainly, without ornament, the way a map should be read: the theft. The lie. The war blamed on the gifted so that no one would ever look upward, at the throne, where the true crime sat wearing a crown. And above it all, the arithmetic of the moon—one turn remaining, and an inquisitor who knew precisely what would wake when it filled.

When they finished, the fire cracked and spat. No one spoke for a long moment.

"So," Odile said at last. "In one month, Vane gets what he wants. Or we do." She tapped the charter with her knife's point. "This is real? This will hold?"

"It's the original seal. I copied every line myself. It will hold—if there's anyone left willing to look at it."

"There's always someone willing to look," Odile said. "The trick is making them unable to look away."

"That's why I'm not going back to Highmoor." Zephyrine heard the words leave their own mouth and felt, strangely, lighter for having said them. "Not to hide. Not to run. If I stay hidden, the Ember wakes anyway—in some mountain hollow, in some warden's cell—and Aldric takes it, and the lie stands another five hundred years. There's nowhere left to flee to. So I'm going back to stand in the open, in the city that outlawed everything I am, and make them all see it. The charter. The theft. All of it, under the thirteenth moon, where no one can pretend they didn't."

Tam made a sound—not quite a laugh. "Back to Highmoor. Back to the wardens and the gallows and the man who's already hung your face on every post from here to the sea." He shook his head slowly. "You're mad. All of you, if you follow."

"You tried to sell them, Tam," Sef said quietly. "And you couldn't do it fast enough to matter, because here you are, sitting at our fire. Desperation makes strange choices. So does courage. I know which one I'd rather be counted in."

Tam looked at the fire for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice was rough. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking what use I am."

"You know the smuggler routes better than any of us," Odile said. "Every gate, every grate, every blind wall in Highmoor. If we're going in, we go in the way nobody watches." She sheathed her knife at last, decision settling over her like a cloak. "So. We go in."

Brannoc's voice came from the doorway of the shrine, low as a landslide. "We go in."

They all turned. He stood leaning against the frame, gray-faced, swaying slightly, but upright—upright for the first time in days, one huge hand pressed to the bandage at his side. Sef was moving toward him before he'd finished speaking, scolding and steadying all at once, but he waved them off and took another step into the firelight.

"My sister died in the last purge," he said simply, looking at Zephyrine. "Nobody stood in the square and told the truth about her. Nobody even said her name after. If we go back—if this works—then maybe the next girl like her won't die as a secret." He lowered himself carefully onto a stone, breath whistling. "I'm coming. Tie me to the mast if you have to."

"No masts on a river barge," Odile said, and her voice cracked just slightly on the joke, and everyone pretended not to notice.

* * *

They talked until the fire burned to embers and the stars wheeled westward. Plans layered over plans: Tam's routes through the old salt tunnels beneath the Gateward; Sef's list of the hidden gifted, the ones whose fevers Zephyrine's charms had cooled, whose children's coughs their knotwork had eased—people who owed the crew nothing and might still listen; Odile's colder accounting of wardens' shifts and patrol roads, knowledge bought over years of running contraband past the very men who would hang her.

And through it all, Zephyrine felt the thing in their chest answering the turning sky, warming as the plans warmed, as if the Ember itself understood that it was going home.

Near dawn, Odile found Zephyrine at the courtyard's edge, watching the east pale above the pines.

"When we were in Hollow Court," she said, without preamble, "at the festival—you danced like someone who'd forgotten they had a body. Like you were apologizing to it for using it." She studied the horizon rather than Zephyrine's face. "You won't dance like that in Highmoor Square. Whatever happens under that moon, you stand like you belong there. Can you do that?"

"I spent fifteen years learning to disappear, Odile. Belonging is a language I only half speak."

"Then let us speak it for you." She said it flatly, as fact, as cargo manifest, as route. "You don't stand alone in that square. That's the whole point of what we're doing. That's the point of all of it." She turned then, and her eyes in the pre-dawn gray were steady and fierce. "I called you family once and then wanted to strangle you for lying to me. Both things were true. Only one of them lasted. Understand?"

"I understand."

"Good." She clapped Zephyrine's shoulder—harder than necessary, which was its own kind of tenderness—and walked back toward the fire.

Zephyrine stayed a moment longer. Below the mountain, far to the south, Highmoor lay invisible under the fading dark: its walls, its gallows, its proclamation posts bearing a charcoal likeness of a face Zephyrine had spent a lifetime hiding. In fifteen days, the thirteenth moon would rise over it all.

They had spent fifteen years mapping realms that existed only through magic—coastlines of dream, mountains of the gifted dead, cities lit by no honest sun. Now the hardest map of all waited to be drawn: the short, dangerous road from fear into purpose, from hiding into standing, from one person's buried spark into a whole city's waking.

Zephyrine pressed a hand to their sternum, where the Ember burned quiet and certain, and turned back toward the fire, toward the voices planning in the dawn.

They would need a barge, a forged seal, and a miracle.

Two of the three, at least, the crew could provide.
