## 18. The City That Outlawed Magic

The river gave them back to Highmoor in the grey hour before dawn, when the fog lay on the water like a sheet drawn over a corpse.

Sef stood at the barge's rail and watched the city assemble itself out of the mist: first the black teeth of the harbor cranes, then the slate roofs stacked up the hillside like scales on a sleeping beast, then the spire of the Chantry of the Veil, its bell silent now—silenced, Sef knew, the same year the crown outlawed enchantment, because a bell that rang true could be made to ring otherwise. Fifteen years of quiet bells. Fifteen years of a whole city holding its breath.

"You're doing the face," Brannoc said beside her.

"What face?"

"The one where you're diagnosing the city."

Sef huffed. "It has symptoms. A city that outlaws magic is like a body that's decided pain is illegal. The pain doesn't stop. It just goes somewhere worse." She flexed her fingers inside her gloves. Her hands always ached when they came back to Highmoor, though she'd never been able to decide whether it was the cold or the memory.

Below them, Odile crouched at the tiller, steering the barge into the shadow of the old salt piers with the tenderness of a woman handling something breakable, which was unlike her, which meant she was afraid. Tam's absence sat between all of them like a fourth passenger. Three days since Sef had chased him through the rain and lost him to the crown's silver, three days of knowing that somewhere ahead of them, a man who'd shared their bread was describing Zephyrine's face to whoever paid him for it.

"He'll have told them about the routes," Sef said quietly.

"Tam doesn't know the smuggler routes," Odile said from the tiller, not turning. "He knows the taverns and the dice tables. The routes were never his." She let the barge kiss the pier fenders soft as a moth landing. "That's why we're still alive."

* * *

They went down into the city through the Undercut, the way the Saltmarket crews had moved contraband since before Sef was born: a door behind a tannery that smelled of lime and rot, then stairs worn hollow by generations of boots, then the long brick throat of an aqueduct abandoned two hundred years ago when the river changed its mind about where it wanted to go. Brannoc went first with a hooded lantern, his shoulders brushing both walls. Zephyrine came after him, moving carefully, one hand trailing the bricks as though reading them.

Sef watched them do it and felt the old ache sharpen. She had known Zephyrine Whitlock for the better part of a year now. She had stitched the knife wound in their shoulder, argued with them over fever tonics, taught them which mushrooms in the Wraithmarsh would kill you politely and which would kill you rudely. And for most of that time she had told herself she knew them. Then the charm-work under the floorboards, then the partial confession—the gift, yes; the Ember, no—and then the archive beneath the mountain, where the truth had come out of Zephyrine like a splinter finally worked free, ugly and relieving all at once.

She didn't resent them anymore. That was the strange part. What she felt instead, walking through the dark under Highmoor, was a kind of professional horror—not at Zephyrine, but at the city itself. Because Sef was a healer, and healers understood something that kings did not: that a wound bound too long doesn't heal. It learns to live under the bandage. It grows its own secret weather.

Fifteen years of secret weather. And tomorrow night, the thirteenth moon.

"Here," Zephyrine said.

They stopped before a wall that looked like every other wall—brick, damp, indifferent—and Zephyrine pressed their palm flat against it and closed their eyes. For a moment nothing happened. Then Sef saw it, the way you see a fish under brown water: a faint warmth spreading out from Zephyrine's hand through the brick, a bloom of light that wasn't light, tracing lines that might have been mortar joints or might have been writing. The wall shivered. A section the size of a door swung inward on hinges that had been oiled within the last month.

Zephyrine opened their eyes and looked almost embarrassed. "I've been maintaining the wards," they said. "All these years. Someone had to."

"How many doors are there?" Odile asked.

"In Highmoor?" Zephyrine's mouth did something complicated. "Forty-one. I stopped counting the people."

* * *

The first safehouse was above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, and it was full of people who should not have been there.

Sef counted eleven before she stopped counting: a laundress with burn-scarred forearms whose hands glimmered faintly blue when she wrung cloth; a boy of maybe sixteen whose shadow moved a half-second behind him; an old man mending nets by lamplight, except the knots in his net tied themselves while his hands rested idle in his lap. They looked up when the crew came through the trapdoor, and Sef watched the whole room flinch as one body—the flinch of creatures that had been hunted so long that any opening door was a verdict.

Then the laundress stood, squinted at Zephyrine, and said, "You're the trinket-seller."

"Marta," Zephyrine said, and something in their voice cracked open. "Your daughter—"

"Is here. Behind you. She's been wearing your luck-charm since she was six." Marta's voice was steady but her eyes weren't. "It's the only reason the wardens' dogs never caught her scent. Do you know what that's worth? Do you have any idea—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. Started again, quieter. "We thought you were dead. Or gone. You sold us charms for fifteen years and never once stayed for thanks."

"I couldn't stay," Zephyrine said. "Staying is how they find you."

"Well," said the old net-mender, not looking up from his work, "you're staying now, I hope. Since the moon's nearly full and the whole city's about to learn whether hiding was worth anything."

* * *

By midmorning the safehouse had become something Sef had no clean word for. Not a refuge exactly. Not a garrison. It was more like what happened in her surgery when a bad fever broke in a household and everyone started telling each other the truth.

Word moved through the Undercut the way water moves through chalk—slowly, invisibly, everywhere. Zephyrine sent messages through forty-one doors, and by noon there were thirty people in the chandler's attic and by dusk there were sixty, and Sef found herself working, because of course she did, moving through the crowd with her satchel checking hands and pulses and the particular exhaustion she'd learned to recognize in people who had spent years clamping down on the thing that made them themselves. Suppression, she wrote in her head, making a diagnosis of a city. Presents as fatigue, tremor, insomnia, and a silence so complete you mistake it for peace.

A girl of nine sat on the stairs watching her work, her eyes too old for her face. When Sef crouched beside her, the girl held out her cupped palms, and in them a small flame burned without heat, steady as a pilot light.

"It won't go out," the girl whispered. "Mama says hide it. But it won't go *out*. It just gets smaller and smaller and it hurts, like holding your breath."

Sef looked at that little flame for a long moment. She thought of Zephyrine at fifteen, drawing maps of realms that didn't officially exist, folding them small enough to swallow. She thought of Brannoc's sister, whom she had never met, who had been taken in the last purge for a gift no larger than this girl's.

"Show me how you make it smaller," Sef said gently.

The girl breathed in, and the flame shrank to a bead.

"Now don't." 

The girl blinked. Let the breath out. The flame rose again, warm and unashamed, and the girl stared at it, and then at Sef, and her face did something that Sef knew she would carry a long time: it unclenched. Nine years old, and already her face had learned to clench.

"It doesn't hurt," the girl said, wondering.

"No," Sef said. "It never did. The holding was the part that hurt."

* * *

Odile ran the war council off the chandler's worktable, because someone had to and because planning was the only language she spoke fluently when she was frightened. Sef stood at the edge of it with Brannoc, listening.

"The palace square," Odile said, tapping the map Zephyrine had drawn—of course Zephyrine had drawn it, from memory, every gutter and archway rendered in that precise, loving hand. "Tomorrow night, when the moon clears the Chantry spire. Aldric will want an audience; he's a man who performs his cruelty. So we give him one. We put the charter's truth in front of the entire city at once, and we do it with the Ember, because there's no arguing with a light that writes itself across the sky."

"And if it goes wrong," said Marta the laundress flatly, "we all hang."

"If we do nothing," Odile answered, "we hang slowly, one neighbor at a time, bought off with silver until there's no one left to inform on anyone. I've watched that arithmetic my whole life. I don't recommend it."

There was a rumble of assent, not joyful, but real. Sef drifted toward the window while they argued timings and escape routes, and looked out over the rooftops toward the palace hill, where lanterns were already being strung for the festival of the thirteenth moon—the crown's own festival, ironically enough, a celebration of the realm's founding, of the charter, of the lie. The whole city would be in the square tomorrow. The whole city would be watching.

Behind her, Zephyrine had come to stand at the table's edge, saying very little, the way they always did in crowds. But Sef caught what the others missed, because she was watching for it: every few minutes, some small magic moved through the room—a knot tying itself, a candle steadying, a child's flame blooming—and every time, Zephyrine's shoulders came down a fraction of an inch. Like a man hearing a language he thought dead, spoken freely, all around him.

Fifteen years of maintaining forty-one doors, Sef thought. Of selling luck to children and never staying for thanks. And all that time, the gift hadn't starved. It had gone to seed, quietly, patiently, all through the city—charms in pockets, wards on walls, small mercies scattered like a sower who never stopped walking even after the law said the field was forbidden.

The invisible, becoming visible. Not tomorrow, when the Ember blazed. Tonight, here, in a chandler's attic: sixty people discovering they were never alone.

* * *

Near midnight, Sef found Brannoc sitting alone on the loading dock behind the chandler's shop, whittling something small, which he did when his hands needed to be busy so his heart could think.

She sat down next to him. For a while neither of them spoke. Below the dock, the canal slid past, black and patient, carrying the reflections of festival lanterns being hung on the far shore.

"My sister used to say the city was a person," Brannoc said eventually. "Before they took her. She said Highmoor was sick, and the sickness was pretending, and one day it would stop pretending and either die or get well." He turned the whittling over in his huge fingers. It was a small boat. It was always a small boat. "I used to think getting well would look like a battle."

"And now?"

He nodded upward, toward the attic, toward the murmur of sixty voices that had spent fifteen years learning silence and were, tonight, unlearning it. "Now I think it looks like this. Sick people finding each other. That's most of medicine, isn't it? You told me that once."

"I tell everyone that once," Sef said. "Nobody listens."

"I listened."

From inside came a burst of laughter—one of the gifted children, showing another how her flame could dance—and then Odile's voice cutting through it, organizing the laughter into watch rotations, because Odile could organize anything, including joy.

Brannoc smiled at the sound. Then his smile faded into something more careful. "Sef. Tomorrow. If Aldric takes the Ember—if it goes wrong—"

"Then we get them out," Sef said. "That's the whole plan. Everything else is decoration."

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only kind I've ever believed in." She bumped her shoulder against his arm, which was like bumping a dock piling. "We came back for this. All of us. Even Odile, who hasn't forgiven anyone yet, and even me, who swore off causes after the last purge buried everyone I treated. We came back. That's not nothing, Brannoc. That's the cure starting to work."

* * *

It was in the smallest hour, when the attic had finally gone quiet, that Sef heard the knock.

Three taps, then two, then three—the smuggler's rhythm, but wrong, hurried, stripped of its usual lazy confidence. Brannoc opened the trapdoor with his knife already loose in his grip, and the man who climbed through it was soaked to the skin, mud to the knees, and gray-faced with running.

Tam.

Every voice in the attic stopped. Sef saw Odile's hand move toward her belt and stop, deliberately, the way you stop yourself from touching a hot iron. Zephyrine went very still, the stillness of prey.

"I didn't tell them about the routes," Tam said hoarsely, before anyone could speak. "I want that understood. I took their silver and I gave them the taverns, the faces, the names I knew—and then I sat in a warden's barracks all night listening to them plan, and I heard what they mean to do tomorrow, and I couldn't—" His breath hitched. "I couldn't stay bought. So I'm spending everything I stole to pay it back. Listen to me. *Listen.*"

"Talk," Odile said, in a voice like a closing door.

"Aldric isn't setting his trap in the square," Tam said. "He knows you know he wants an audience. He's letting you come. The square is theater—he'll take the Ember there if he can, but if not, he doesn't need to. He's seeded warden-captains through the whole crowd, plainclothes, and he's done something else. Something the officers wouldn't explain to the likes of me, but I heard the word they kept using." He swallowed. "*Vessel.* They keep saying vessel. There's a rite, an old one, dug out of the Chantry archives. If they can't take the Ember willing, they mean to take it bleeding. And they've got the executioner's block set up in the square tomorrow dressed as a festival stage, and the warrant is already signed, and the name on it—" He looked at Zephyrine, and his face was terrible with pity. "It's not yours. It's mine. They're going to hang me in front of the whole city unless the Ember gives itself up. That's the trade he's offering. Your life for mine, in front of everyone, so the city sees the gifted abandon one of their own."

Silence. Somewhere below, the canal lapped against a piling, keeping its own count.

Sef looked at Zephyrine and saw the Ember-bearer weigh it—that arithmetic, that cruel, royal arithmetic—in the space of a single breath. Then she saw them set it down.

"No," Zephyrine said quietly. "He doesn't get to price people. That's the whole lie, right there. That's what we came back to end." They turned to Odile. "So we change the order of things. We don't wait for his stage."

Odile's mouth curved, slow and sharp, the first true smile Sef had seen on her since the rain. "Captain's privilege," she said. "Somebody wake the attic. We've got a hanging to ruin."
