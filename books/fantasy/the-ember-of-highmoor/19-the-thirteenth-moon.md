## 19. The Thirteenth Moon

The moon rose over Highmoor like a wound in the sky.

Zephyrine had watched it climb for twelve turns now—each full moon a little brighter, a little heavier, as though the sky itself were filling with something that could not be held much longer. Tonight it came up vast and white behind the palace spires, so close to the earth that it seemed to lean against them, and every rooftop in the city caught its light like the scales of a sleeping serpent.

From the bell tower of the Saltmarket chapel, where Sef had hidden them through the last hours of dusk, Zephyrine could see the whole of the capital laid out below: the crooked lanes of the low quarter glowing silver, the merchant terraces bright as bone, and at the center of everything, the palace square—a great pale circle of flagstones that had been swept clean of snow by order of the crown, though no one had said why.

They all knew why.

"He wants an audience," Odile said. She stood at the tower's narrow window, her arms crossed, her breath fogging the cold air. In the moonlight her face looked carved from something older than flesh. "Aldric doesn't trap people in squares to kill them quietly. He traps them there to be *seen*."

Below, the square was filling. Not soldiers—civilians. Fishwives and clerks and coal-heavers, drawn out of their houses by criers who had walked the streets since sundown announcing that the crown would make a great revelation before the thirteenth moon reached its height. Wardens lined the square's edges in ranks of grey, pikes angled inward, and at the far side, on the steps of the palace itself, someone had erected a scaffold draped in black cloth.

Zephyrine's hands were shaking. They pressed them flat against the stone sill and made themselves breathe.

"They'll come for us at midnight," Brannoc rumbled from the shadows near the stair. He had insisted on coming despite the wound in his side, despite Sef's fury, despite the way he moved now like a man carrying a sack of wet sand where his strength used to be. "Tam told them everything. Where we'd surface from the smuggler routes. When. He's been waiting."

"Then we don't go," Sef said sharply. She was packing her kit with more force than the task required, vials clicking against each other like small angry teeth. "There are a hundred ways out of this city that don't run through his square. We take the river gate, we take the—"

"Sef." Odile didn't turn from the window. "Look at the square. Look at what he's built."

Sef looked. The silence stretched.

"He's not building a trap for four smugglers," Odile said quietly. "He's building a stage. And the only thing worth staging—" she turned then, and her eyes found Zephyrine's, and held them, steady and terrible and true, "—is you."

* * *

They had argued about it for three days, in the cellar beneath the chapel, in whispers, while the city above them tightened like a fist.

Odile had wanted to run. It was the only time Zephyrine had ever seen her argue for retreat—the woman who had steered her barge down the Wraithmarsh with wardens' arrows falling around her like rain, who had stared down her own brother across a forge-fire in Hollow Court and called him a stranger to his face. But she had stood in that cellar with her palms flat on the table and said, *We leave tonight, we're over the border in six days, and whatever wakes in you wakes somewhere he can't reach.*

And Zephyrine had looked at the map spread between them—the honest map, the one they'd drawn in the archive's lamplight, showing the old charter's seal and the lie written beneath it—and had heard themselves say words they hadn't known they were carrying.

"If I run, the thirteenth turn completes anyway. The Ember wakes anyway. And Aldric finds me eventually—he's been hunting my bloodline for fifteen years, Odile; do you think a border will stop him? But if I run, no one ever learns why. No one learns what the crown did. The persecution doesn't end. It just gets quieter, and deeper, and it swallows the next child the way it nearly swallowed me." Their voice had cracked, and they had let it. "I spent fifteen years learning how to disappear. I'm done disappearing."

Brannoc had said nothing at all. He had simply come to stand beside Zephyrine, the way he had stood beside them in the marsh, in the ambush, on the road—his silence a wall, his choice already made.

And Sef—sharp-tongued, unsentimental Sef, who trusted nothing she couldn't stitch or set—had looked at Zephyrine for a long moment and said, "If you do this, you do it knowing exactly what you are. Not a symbol. Not a spark. A person. A person who comes back to us after. That's the price. You promise me that."

Zephyrine had not promised. They had not lied to her again. But they had taken her hand, and she had not pulled away, and somehow that had been answer enough.

Now midnight was one hour away, and the argument was over, and there was only the waiting.

* * *

Zephyrine went down into the chapel alone while the others readied the escape route they all knew none of them would use.

The chapel had been deconsecrated decades ago, when the purges began—its saints chiseled off the walls, its windows replaced with plain glass—but the old gifted stonemasons had left their signatures in places the crown never thought to look: a spiral worked into the base of the font, a knot of interlaced light carved where the altar once stood, worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands. Zephyrine knelt beside it and laid their palm against the stone.

It was warm.

It was always warm. Fifteen years of hiding, and this small stubborn warmth had survived every purge, every proclamation, every winter of the crown's long night—as if the building itself remembered what the city had been ordered to forget.

"I don't know how to do this," Zephyrine whispered to it. To the stone, to the dead masons, to whatever remained of the old bloodline listening in the dark. "I know how to hide. I know how to draw maps of places no one can follow me to. I don't know how to stand in front of ten thousand people and be the thing they've been taught to burn."

The stone answered nothing. But the warmth spread up their arm, slow as dawn, and in it Zephyrine felt—not words, never words, but something older: the accumulated weight of everyone who had carried the Ember before them. The healer-queens and storm-singers and nameless cartographers of the old blood, each of whom had also, surely, stood at some threshold of terror and stepped across it anyway.

*You are not the first,* the warmth seemed to say. *You are only the next.*

When Zephyrine rose, their hands were steady.

* * *

They came out of the smuggler tunnels at half past eleven, through a coal cellar two streets from the square, and the city swallowed them whole.

Highmoor on the thirteenth moon was a city holding its breath. Every window along the processional route held candlelight—hundreds of them, thousands, though open flame was taxed beyond what any laborer should afford, because tonight even fear made people want to see. The crowds moved toward the square in slow rivers, murmuring, and Zephyrine moved among them with their hood up and Odile's hand locked around their arm like a shackle, and no one looked twice, because everyone in Highmoor had learned long ago how not to look at anything.

The square opened before them like a held note.

Ten thousand people, perhaps more, packed shoulder to shoulder inside the ring of wardens. The snow had been swept from the flagstones and the stones themselves had been scrubbed pale, and under the full moon they glowed like the floor of some vast temple. At the center stood a single iron brazier, unlit. On the palace steps, beneath the draped scaffold, Lord Aldric Vane waited in robes of white and gold, and even at this distance Zephyrine could see how still he stood—stillness like a blade's, stillness like a man who has finally stopped pretending to be patient.

"There," Sef breathed. "There's the side passage past the granary—if we move now, before the—"

"No," Zephyrine said.

Their voice came out quiet, but it carried, because Odile had stopped walking, and Brannoc had stopped, and the three of them formed a small island in the moving crowd, and Zephyrine stepped out of it, away from them, into the open.

"Zeph—" Odile's hand closed on empty air.

"I have to be seen arriving," Zephyrine said, without turning around. "That's the only way this works. He needs me to come to him. So I come to him." They made themselves look back—one look, fast, burning, taking in all three faces: Odile's jaw tight as a cable, Sef's eyes wide and wet and furious, Brannoc's hand pressed flat over his bandaged side as if he could hold himself together by will alone. "Whatever happens on those steps—get to the crowd. Get them to look. That's all I need from you. Make them *look*."

"Zephyrine Whitlock." Odile said their name the way other people swore oaths. "You come back. You hear me? Whatever you are, whoever you were before us—you come *back*."

And Zephyrine smiled—really smiled, for the first time in longer than they could measure—and said, "I'm a cartographer, Captain. I always find my way home."

Then they turned and walked into the square.

* * *

The crowd parted for them without understanding why.

It moved the way water moves around a dropped stone—slowly, reluctantly, a ripple of turned heads and murmurs spreading outward, ten thousand small confusions feeding one another. *Who is that. Why are they walking forward. Move, let them through.* The wardens at the inner ring raised their pikes, and a captain's voice rang out—*Hold, hold, let them come, those are orders*—and Zephyrine understood, with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, that Aldric had given those orders hours ago. Perhaps days. He had known exactly which tunnel Tam would sell. He had known exactly when they would surface. He had built his stage and left the door open, and now here they came, walking through it, straight into the light.

The moon touched its zenith just as Zephyrine reached the center of the square.

Up close, Aldric Vane was taller than his likeness on the proclamations, and thinner, as if the years of hunting had burned him down to pure intention. His eyes—grey, Odile's grey, but scoured of everything warm—moved over Zephyrine's hooded face with the particular hunger of a man reading a document he has chased across fifteen years.

"You came," he said. His voice carried effortlessly; he had been trained for courts, for pronouncements, for distance. "The last of the Ember line walks into my square of its own free will. How very convenient for you all." This last to the crowd, a smile flickering across his face like sun on ice. "Citizens of Highmoor! Behold the creature your grandparents were promised had been destroyed. Behold the spark of the old rebellion, living hidden in your midst—selling you charms in the Saltmarket, drawing your maps, passing among you for fifteen years wearing a human face!"

Murmurs rolled through the crowd like wind through wheat. Somewhere in it, Zephyrine knew, Odile and Sef and Brannoc were pushing forward, working toward the front ranks, doing what they'd promised. Zephyrine kept their eyes on Aldric.

"Say your name," Aldric commanded. "Let the city hear it."

"Zephyrine Whitlock," Zephyrine said, and lifted back their hood, and let ten thousand strangers see their face. "Cartographer. Charm-maker. Neighbor. Friend—to anyone who ever needed one and couldn't ask why."

"Liar," Aldric said softly, and the word cracked across the square like a whip. "Traitor. The charter of this realm names your bloodline the enemy of the throne. The war that burned the old world began because *your kind* rebelled against the crown—because the gifted could not bear to serve. Every law, every purge, every hard and necessary thing this kingdom has done in three hundred years rests on that truth. And tonight, before this city, I will prove it." He gestured, and two wardens wheeled forward a covered object the size of a coffin. "Tonight I take the Ember from you, and the age of magic ends forever, and Highmoor is finally safe."

"That's not what the charter says."

Zephyrine's voice rang out clear, and the crowd's murmur faltered.

"I've read it," Zephyrine said, louder. "Not the copy in your libraries—the original, the sealed one, the one your kings buried beneath the archive and hoped the world would forget. Shall I tell this city what it says, Lord Vane? Shall I tell them how the war *actually* began?"

"*Seize them,*" Aldric said, and his voice, for the first time, was not perfectly calm.

But the Ember was already waking.

It had been waking for twelve turns, gathering itself turn by turn like a tide, and now, under the full thirteenth moon, with ten thousand hearts beating around it and the truth straining at Zephyrine's ribs like a bird against a window—it came up through them like sunrise.

Light first: a glow beneath the skin, gold-white, rising through Zephyrine's chest and throat and hands until their whole body seemed lit from within, until the crowd gasped as one creature and fell back in a great ragged wave. Then memory—the ancestral flood, the stolen power, the king's theft, the burning of the old world, all of it pressing upward, demanding to be spoken, to be *seen*—

And Zephyrine, standing in the center of the square with wardens closing from every side and the inquisitor's face gone white as his robes, did the only thing left to do.

They opened their arms. And let it blaze.

The light went up off them like a pillar, higher than the palace spires, higher than the moon, and the whole sky over Highmoor turned to molten gold, and in that light—every citizen saw. Saw the founding lie written across the heavens in letters of fire, the truth of the stolen Ember pouring out of Zephyrine and into ten thousand upturned faces at once. The square erupted—not into panic, not yet, but into a sound Zephyrine had never heard before: ten thousand voices crying out at the same instant, a sound like a city being born.

Through the blaze, Zephyrine saw Aldric Vane reaching for them, screaming orders no one obeyed. Saw Odile breaking from the crowd, running toward the light. Saw Brannoc's huge silhouette turning to shield the fleeing, and Sef's hands already moving, already healing, already *working*—

And high above the palace, the thirteenth moon hung full and white in a sky that was no longer dark,

as the Ember rose.
