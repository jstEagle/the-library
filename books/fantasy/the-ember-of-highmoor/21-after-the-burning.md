## 21. After the Burning

Brannoc woke to the smell of woodsmoke and bread, and for a long, drifting moment he could not remember why the smell made his chest ache.

Then he moved, and the wound in his side answered for him—a deep, hot pull beneath the bandages, like a hook set in muscle. He lay still on the narrow cot in the back room of what had been a chandler's shop and was now, by the look of the crates stacked against the wall, a field station. Someone had hung bundles of dried herbs from the rafters. Someone had folded his coat—his good coat, the one Odile won off a river-baron in a card game three years ago—over a chair with a care that was not Sef's and not Odile's either. A stranger's kindness. The city was full of it now, apparently. Strangers being kind in broad daylight, out loud, where anyone could see.

He tested the thought the way he'd test ice before crossing a frozen ford, and found it held: it was over. Or if not over—if nothing that big was ever simply *over*—then at least the long night of it was done, and the thirteenth moon had wheeled away west, and Zephyrine Whitlock had stood in the palace square with light pouring out of them like water through a broken dam, and every soul in Highmoor had seen the truth written across the sky in letters of ember and gold.

*The king stole it first.* That was what the light had said. Brannoc hadn't read the words so much as felt them, the way you feel thunder in your teeth. Fifteen hundred years of history, rewritten in one night, in front of everyone.

He sat up slowly. His side screamed its opinion. He let it scream, because Sef said pain meant the flesh was knitting, and Sef was never wrong about flesh.

Through the door's warped panels he could hear the city. Not the old Highmoor—that hushed, sidelong murmur, everyone speaking half-volume, doors closing soft as confessions—but something rawer and more alive. Hammers. Voices raised not in fear but in argument, the ordinary bickering of people building things. Somewhere a fiddle, badly played, joyfully played. And underneath all of it, constant as a tide, the sound of rubble being cleared.

He found his boots by the door. They'd been cleaned. That undid him a little, sitting there with his boot in his hands, his throat going tight over a pair of cleaned boots. He was a large man and had learned young to keep his feelings in a small room behind his ribs, but lately the room kept overflowing.

His sister would have loved this, he thought. Maren, who used to make the well-water rise just to watch the fish swim up to look at her. Maren, who died in the last purge with her hands tied and her gift drowned out of her in the square by the old bell tower. She would have stood in that crowd under the burning sky and laughed until she cried, and then she would have rolled up her sleeves and started fixing things, because that was Maren.

Instead he was here, alive, in a city that had finally stopped pretending, and grief and hope were sitting together inside him like two old friends sharing a bench, not talking much, just keeping each other company.

He put on his boots and went out into the morning.

* * *

Highmoor smelled different already. Three days since the Ember, and the smoke of the old fear was lifting off the streets like fog off the marsh at sunrise.

Brannoc walked slow, because walking fast was beyond him, and because he wanted to see everything. The Saltmarket was half wreckage—the wardens' charge through it during the battle had taken out two rows of stalls and the fountain with the leering stone dolphin—and the merchants were already rebuilding, hammering planks, arguing about who owed whom for a burned awning. A woman was selling apples off a cart with no cart-wheels, the cart propped on stones, and nobody was checking papers, because there were no papers anymore, or rather there were too many, all of them suddenly worthless, all of them suddenly just paper.

Children were chalk-drawing on the cobbles. Brannoc stopped when he saw what they were drawing: not hopscotch grids, not ships. Maps. Lopsided, glorious maps of the city with a great golden star over the palace square, and stick figures with their arms raised, and one small boy carefully coloring in a figure with hair like fire who was clearly, unmistakably, Zephyrine.

"That's not right," Brannoc rumbled, crouching with a grunt. "Their hair's darker."

The boy looked up, unafraid, which would have been unthinkable a month ago. "It glowed," he said, as though that settled it. "It glowed yellow."

"Aye." Brannoc touched the chalk star gently, leaving a smudge. "That it did."

He bought three apples with a coin that might or might not still be legal tender and no one cared, and carried them toward the river, toward the barge, toward home—or whatever the *Vane's Fortune* was now that home had stopped being a thing you fled to and started being a thing you built.

* * *

Sef met him at the gangplank, arms crossed, in the way that meant *stand still and be examined.*

"You're supposed to be resting."

"I rested."

"For three days?"

"I'm a fast healer."

"You're a stubborn mule with a hole in your side that I sewed shut with thread I'd have liked finer." But Sef's hands were already on him, peeling back the bandage with a gentleness that contradicted the voice entirely, and after a long moment they grunted—the satisfied grunt, the one Brannoc had learned to prize above applause. "Knitting clean. Sit down before you fall down. There's broth."

The barge looked strange in daylight without cargo. It had been stripped for the battle—every smuggled bolt of silk and cask of untaxed brandy gone, traded, spent, or given away—and now its hold held something else entirely: blankets, bandages, sacks of grain Odile had bought with the last of the crew's silver, and crates marked with the seal of the new council, whatever a new council was when it was nine days old and meeting in a guildhall with a leaking roof.

Tam's crate was the one nearest the plank. Brannoc saw it and his hands went still around the bowl of broth.

Because Tam was there. At the far end of the deck, coiling rope that didn't need coiling, doing it badly, doing it the way a man does work when he's really waiting to be noticed. He was thinner than he'd been. There was a healing cut above his eye and a bruise along his jaw, and he flinched—actually flinched—when a gull cried overhead.

"He came back last night," Sef said quietly, beside him. They watched Tam together. "Walked up the plank in the rain, soaked through, wouldn't come below. Said he'd take whatever we decided. Said—" Sef's mouth twisted. "Said he'd told the wardens about the mountain route, and that Aldric Vane's men nearly killed Brannoc because of it, and that he knew what that was worth and he wasn't asking forgiveness, only a place to stand while we decided."

"And Odile?"

"Hasn't decided. Hasn't spoken to him. Hasn't thrown him off either." Sef took the empty bowl from Brannoc's hands. "She says it's not hers alone to decide. Says the crew decides together, like always." A pause. "She says it's mostly your call. It was your blood on the deck."

Brannoc looked at Tam a long time. He remembered the man Tam had been two years ago—quick, funny, hopeless with dice, the first to sing and the last to stop. He remembered the debts, the quiet desperation that had crept over Tam like damp creeping into timber, the way he'd stopped meeting anyone's eyes. Desperation corrodes, Sef had said once, after chasing him through the rain. It doesn't excuse. It just explains.

"Maren would say give him a rope," Brannoc said at last. "Not a long one. But a rope."

Sef nodded slowly. "And what does Brannoc say?"

Brannoc set down the bowl. He crossed the deck, each step pulling at his wound, and stopped in front of Tam, close enough that the smaller man had to tilt his head back to meet his eyes. Tam stood his ground, barely. His hands had gone white on the rope.

"I nearly died on that road," Brannoc said. Not loud. He never needed to be loud.

"Yes." Tam's voice cracked on it. "I know."

"The warden's blade went where my heart is because someone I broke bread with sold the road we slept on." He let that sit. Let Tam carry it a moment, the full weight, because some weights deserved carrying. Then: "You came back. Nobody made you. You could've run to the coast and been rich."

"There's no rich," Tam said miserably. "There's no anywhere. I watched the sky burn and I understood what I'd helped try to put out." His eyes were wet. "I don't want forgiveness. I want work. Give me the worst work you've got. Give me the latrines and the rubble and the graves. Just—let me dig."

Brannoc considered him. Then he picked up the coil of rope Tam had been failing to tie and, with two economical movements, showed him the correct hitch.

"Start with that," he said. "You've been doing it wrong for an hour and it's hurting my heart."

Tam made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and tied the knot, and got it right, and Brannoc turned away before his face could do anything embarrassing, and found Odile watching from the wheelhouse door with an expression he couldn't read and didn't need to. She gave him a single nod. The matter was decided the way things were decided on the *Fortune*: not with speeches, but with knots and nods and one person willing to go first.

* * *

The guildhall had been the Woolmongers' Hall before the wool trade dried up, and it still smelled faintly of lanolin and old money. Now its long trestle tables were crowded with the strangest assembly Highmoor had ever seen: guildmasters and gutter-rats, a dowager countess seated elbow to elbow with a hedge-witch, two former crown wardens looking deeply uncomfortable among the very people they'd hunted, and at the head of the table, Zephyrine Whitlock, mapmaker, liar, beacon, looking like they hadn't slept in a week and had never been more awake.

Brannoc took a seat at the back, as was his habit. Large men at the back; it was where he could see everyone.

"—the question isn't whether magic returns to the law," Zephyrine was saying. Their voice carried without effort; Brannoc suspected that was the Ember's residue, or maybe just fifteen years of learning to speak precisely. "The question is what the law looks like when it stops lying. The old charter was built on theft. We can't amend a lie. We have to write a new one."

"A new charter needs signatories from both worlds," said the dowager countess, whose name was Lady Isolde and who had, according to Sef, personally hidden eleven gifted children in her wine cellars over the years while publicly donating to the hunts. Hypocrisy wore many coats; some of them, it turned out, had been armor. "Gifted and ungifted, in equal number. Which means someone must decide who counts as gifted, and I submit that the last people who should make that list are the ones who made lists before."

"Then we don't make lists," said a weathered woman Brannoc recognized as Mother Quill, who ran the orphanage by the tanneries and had, it emerged over the past days, been quietly teaching hedge-craft to orphans for thirty years under the nose of four separate inquisitors. "We make oaths instead. No registry. No rolls of names for the next tyrant to seize. If the charter protects the gift, let it protect *all* gifts, named or unnamed."

Murmurs. A warden objected that oaths without registries were unenforceable. Mother Quill observed that registries without oaths had gotten fifteen hundred children burned, and the warden subsided.

Brannoc listened for an hour, the way he listened to rivers—patiently, feeling for the current under the noise. And what he felt, underneath the arguments about clauses and councils, was something he had not felt in Highmoor in his whole life: people saying true things out loud, in public, to strangers. Ugly truths, hard truths, frightened truths. A dyer admitting she'd informed on a neighbor in '41 and asking, voice shaking, how she could ever make amends. The neighbor, alive, standing up and saying: start by helping me rebuild my stall. It was not graceful. It was not pretty. Half the room was crying at any given moment and the other half was furious about something. But it was *true*, end to end, and Brannoc thought: this is what Maren died believing was impossible.

When the session broke for the evening meal, Zephyrine found him at the back, as he'd known they would. They sat down heavily beside him, a bowl of stew between them, and for a while neither spoke. That was another thing Brannoc valued about Zephyrine: they never needed to fill silence. Silence, to a mapmaker, was probably just unmapped country.

"You should be resting," Zephyrine said finally.

"Sef says the same. I've decided they're both of you conspiring."

"We are. There's a committee." Zephyrine's mouth quirked, then faded. They turned the stew spoon over in their fingers. "Brannoc. You took a blade for me. On the Betrayer's Road. I never—I haven't said—"

"You don't owe me words."

"I owe you everything."

"No." Brannoc shook his head, slow and certain. "You gave the city back its sky. I gave you one blade's worth of meat. We're square." He looked at them then, fully, and let a little of the room behind his ribs open. "But I'll tell you what I do want. When they build the school—the cartography school, the one you keep pretending you're not planning—I want the marsh routes taught. All of them. Every safe path through the Wraithmarsh, written down honest, so no smuggler ever has to buy a route from a scared kid with chalk-stained fingers again."

Zephyrine stared at him. Then they laughed—wet-eyed, startled laughter—and gripped his forearm, careful of the wound, and said, "First lesson. I promise. First lesson of the first class."

* * *

Evening came down over Highmoor gold and gray, and the crew gathered on the barge's deck as they had gathered a thousand nights before, except that now the lanterns burned openly at the rail, bright and unashamed, and now the songs from the shore drifted across the water instead of screams.

Odile had brought out the good bottle—the one saved for shipwrecks and miracles, and she declared this qualified as both. Sef cooked on the little stern stove, cursing creatively at a pot of river fish. Tam worked the galley as the lowest form of scullery life and did it without complaint, and when he brought out the plates, Odile took hers from his hand and said only, "Mind the chip in this one," and Tam's face did something complicated and grateful, and that was all the pardon ceremony there was, and all there needed to be.

They ate. The moon rose—not the thirteenth moon, just an ordinary moon, thin and new, starting its count again from nothing—and Brannoc leaned against the rail with his side aching and his heart full almost past bearing, and thought about the word *after*. People said *after the war*, *after the purge*, as if after were a place you arrived. But standing here he understood that after was not a place. It was a practice. It was tying correct knots and feeding a traitor and drafting charters and cleaning boots for a wounded giant. After was a thing you did every morning, imperfectly, until it became a life.

"Brannoc." Odile, at his elbow, passing him the bottle. "You're brooding. Drink."

"Not brooding. Navigating."

"Hm." She looked out at the dark water, at the city's ten thousand new lights. "Council wants us to run relief upriver. Grain, medicine, word to the free-towns. Hollow Court first—they'll want to hear it from us that the world didn't end." Her jaw tightened slightly at the name of the town where her brother had grown strange and then cruel, and Brannoc said nothing, because some currents a friend doesn't comment on. "We sail day after tomorrow. Assuming the giant holds together."

"The giant holds."

"Good." She clinked her cup against his. Then, quieter, watching the new moon: "Fifteen years I ran cargo around the truth. Feels strange, sailing straight into it."

"Feels right, though."

She was quiet a moment. "Aye," she admitted. "Damn it all. It does."

Down the deck, Zephyrine sat sketching by lantern-light—not a map of anywhere real, Brannoc saw when he wandered over, but of somewhere new, coastline and rivers drawn in confident lines, blank spaces left deliberately open and labeled, in Zephyrine's small precise hand: *to be discovered.*

Beyond them, Highmoor glittered, scarred and mending, and somewhere in it a child was sleeping under a roof where no one whispered anymore, and somewhere else a council worked late by candlelight on the first honest laws the realm had ever known, and the river carried the barge gently at its moorings, patient as all rivers, ready for whatever came next.

Brannoc finished his cup, and let the hope settle into him—not the blazing kind, not the thirteenth-moon kind, but the slower, sturdier kind that lives in bread and knots and mornings—and turned toward the sound of Sef calling that the fish was burning, and the night went on, and it was enough.
