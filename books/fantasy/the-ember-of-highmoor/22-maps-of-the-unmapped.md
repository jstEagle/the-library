## 22. Maps of the Unmapped

The morning Zephyrine Whitlock opened the doors of the Whitlock School of Cartography, they woke before dawn out of habit rather than necessity, and lay for a long while in the narrow bed above the schoolhouse listening to Highmoor breathe.

It was a different city than the one they had hidden in for fifteen years. That was not to say it was healed—cities did not heal the way flesh did, cleanly and all at once. Highmoor still bore its scars: the scorch-mark on the palace square where the thirteenth moon's light had struck stone and left it glassed and pale; the empty tenements in the Saltmarket where families had fled or been taken and never returned; the new scaffolding on the old warden barracks, which the council had voted to tear down and rebuild as granaries, though the work went slowly because half the masons kept stopping to argue about politics. But the fear was gone from the air, or at least thinned enough that you could breathe without tasting it, and that alone made it feel like a different city entirely.

Zephyrine rose and dressed in the dark. They had learned, over a year of slow unlearning, that some habits deserved keeping. The early hours were theirs—the quiet ones, when the river mist came up off the Vane like breath held and released, when the gulls argued over the fish-market scraps and the first carts rattled over the cobbles with their loads of ice and milk. In those hours, before anyone else arrived, Zephyrine could stand at the great drafting table in the center of the schoolroom and work without an audience, and there was one map in particular that had been waiting a long time for their hands.

It hung now on the eastern wall of the schoolroom, pinned at the corners, half-finished: the honest map of Highmoor.

Not the crown surveyor's version, all straight avenues and proud monuments, the one that had hung in every schoolhouse and guard post for two hundred years, the one Zephyrine had memorized as a child and quietly despised. Not even the smuggler's version Odile had once traded for, all back doors and bolt-holes and water-gates, useful and true in its way but incomplete. This was something neither had ever dared to be. It showed the city as it actually was—all of it. The grand quarters and the slums drawn at the same scale, given the same loving detail. The hidden gifted households marked with small silver stars, forty-one of them, each one a person Zephyrine had protected with charm-work through the long years of silence, each one now living openly, several of them already enrolled as students. The old archive beneath the records hall, its entrance inked in gold. The marsh-routes and mountain passes. Even the palace square, with its pale scar, rendered honestly: *here is where we almost destroyed ourselves, and here is where we chose otherwise.*

Zephyrine worked on it by lamplight, adding the last quarter-inch of the riverfront, when they heard footsteps on the stairs—not the creak-and-thud of Brannoc, who announced himself to every building he entered, nor the quick light tread of Sef, but a slower, deliberate climb, someone taking the stairs the way other people took a stage.

Odile Vane appeared in the doorway with two paper-wrapped bundles under one arm and an expression she probably thought was neutral.

"You're early," she said.

"You're earlier."

"I don't sleep anymore. Haven't since the war." She said it dryly, but her eyes moved over the room—the rows of desks Sef had helped build, the shelves of atlases donated by three kingdoms' worth of merchants who wanted very much to be seen supporting the new order, the half-drawn map on the wall—and something in her face softened by a degree she would have denied under torture. "Huh," she said, which from Odile was a speech.

She set the bundles on the nearest desk. "Bread from Marta's. And the spiced buns Brannoc likes. He'll eat six and claim it was four."

"He always claims four."

"It's his number. Four wounds, four years on the barge, four buns." She shrugged. "A man should have a consistent mythology."

* * *

They came, as Zephyrine had known they would, though knowing did nothing to dull the ache of it, the good ache, the one they were still learning to trust.

Brannoc arrived mid-morning, filling the doorway the way weather fills a valley. His limp was nearly gone now; the healers of Hollow Court had done what the crown's surgeons never could, mostly because they'd been allowed to use magic, and Brannoc walked with only the faintest hitch, a souvenir of the wardens' ambush in the mountains. He carried a crate of books on one shoulder as if it weighed nothing and set it down among the others with the gentleness of a man handling eggs.

"From Tam," he said. "He sent them up from the coast. Said—" Brannoc cleared his throat, embarrassed by the sentiment even secondhand. "Said he hopes the school teaches better maps than the ones that got him lost."

Tam. A year ago the name had been a wound in the crew's side; now it was mostly a scar, which is what Sef said scars were supposed to become. Tam had come back in the spring, hollow-eyed and soaked from the same rain he'd betrayed them in, and stood in the Saltmarket mud outside the barge until Odile came out and looked at him for a long minute and said, "You'll start with the bilge, and you'll hate it, and then maybe in a year we'll talk about trust." He'd been hauling bilge-water and running coastal routes ever since, sending books and letters and, once, a truly hideous carved duck that Brannoc refused to throw away.

Sef arrived last, as always, sweeping in on a tide of complaints about the ferry, the price of ink, the council's new trade tariffs, and the state of the world generally, carrying a potted plant that Zephyrine suspected had been stolen from a municipal garden.

"For the windowsill," Sef said, thrusting it at them. "Schools need plants. Something green so the children don't think knowledge is all dust and dead men's opinions."

"Is it stolen?"

"It's *liberated*. There's a difference. Ask any smuggler."

By noon the schoolroom was full—not with students yet, classes began next week, but with neighbors, council members, curious strangers, and a scattering of the gifted who had spent years hiding behind Zephyrine's charms and now came to see what their protector had built. Old Maren from the dye-works, whose fire-hands had warmed her family's rooms for twenty secret winters, brought her granddaughter. The twins from Cooper's Lane, who could speak to river-things, arrived dripping and grinning. A gray-haired man Zephyrine didn't recognize shook their hand with both of his and said, "Your maps got my brother out through the Wraithmarsh in the bad year. I never knew your name. I just want to have known it."

Zephyrine stood in the middle of it all and felt something they had no word for, except perhaps this: for fifteen years they had poured themselves into hiding, into smallness, into the exhausting labor of being no one, and now all that concealed light was coming back to them changed—reflected, multiplied, returned.

* * *

In the afternoon, when the crowd thinned, Odile found Zephyrine at the drafting table, looking at the honest map of Highmoor.

"It needs one more thing," she said.

"It's finished."

"It's not." She tapped the wall where the map ended at the city gates. Beyond the parchment's edge, the world simply stopped—the Wraithmarsh fading into blank paper, the mountains trailing off into nothing, the whole unmapped horizon left white. "You've drawn everything inside the walls. But you taught me once that a map isn't a record of what's known. It's a promise about what can be known." She took up a pen, and in the blank space beyond the mountains, in her bold, careless captain's hand, she wrote:

*Here be wonders.*

Then she set down the pen and stepped back. "There. Now it's finished."

Zephyrine looked at the map—at the scarred square and the silver stars, the archive in gold, the marsh-routes and the river, and beyond it all those three words standing in the white—and felt their throat tighten. Fifteen years of silence, and this was what it had bought: a room full of open doors, a city learning its own true shape, and friends who understood that the edge of a map was never an ending.

Below, in the street, the first enrolled students were arriving early, peering through the windows at the atlases and the strange instruments, and one small girl pressed her nose to the glass and mouthed, *is it magic?*

Zephyrine smiled, and reached for a fresh sheet of parchment, and began to draw the coastline of a realm no one had ever charted.

"Yes," they said, to the girl, to the room, to the wide white world waiting past the map's edge. "Come in and find out."
