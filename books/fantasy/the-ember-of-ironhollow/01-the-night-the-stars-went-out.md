## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The wards over Thornfell Pass had a sound, if you knew how to listen for it — and Kestrel Sandoval had been listening since she was seven years old, riding pillion behind her mother with her ear pressed to the woman's shoulder blade like a child at church.

It wasn't a sound so much as a hum beneath sound. A held note in the bones of the mountains, steady as a millstone turning. Her mother had called it *the long chord*, and she'd taught Kestrel to feel it through her teeth, through the soles of her boots, through the reins of a nervous mule. When the chord was true, the border was closed to anything that flew without feathers or walked without shadow. When it wavered, you turned back. No cargo was worth what came through when the wards thinned.

Kestrel had crossed Thornfell two hundred and eleven times in her twenty-six years. She'd never once heard the chord go silent.

She heard it now.

Or rather — she heard it stop. Not fade, not thin, not fray at the edges the way it did during the spring storms when the ward-stones drank lightning and groaned. It simply ceased, the way a heart ceases, between one beat and the next, and the silence that followed was so complete that her own pulse sounded obscene against it.

She pulled up short on the switchback trail, one hand raised, and behind her the caravan ground to a halt in a clatter of hooves and muttered curses. Nine wagons. Forty-one souls, counting the children sleeping under oilcloth in the third wagon's bed. A last run before winter closed the pass — tin and salt going out, medicine and iron nails coming in, and three crates of nothing anyone would ever write down. The kind of freight that kept the free cities fed and the guild fed and everyone pretending the world worked by gentler rules than it did.

"Kess?" That was Dara Hult, her second, riding up through the dark with her lantern shuttered to a coin of light. "What is it? You've gone white as bone."

"The wards just died."

Dara laughed, because there was nothing else to do with a sentence like that. "Wards don't die, girl. They're older than the kingdom. They'll outlast—"

"I know what they'll outlast." Kestrel didn't mean to snap. She did anyway. She was staring up, and the words came out of her flat and strange, like someone else speaking through her mouth. "Dara. Look up."

* * *

There were no stars.

Not clouds — she knew cloud-cover the way she knew her own hands, could read its weight and texture and the smell of rain an hour off. This was not weather. The sky above Thornfell Pass was simply *empty*, black from horizon to horizon, a darkness so total it had texture, the way deep water has texture. Where the Wheel should have burned silver over the eastern peaks, where the Shepherd and his Dogs should have wheeled slow and patient around the pole, where even the faint dust-glow of the river of heaven should have smeared its pale light across the dark — nothing. A ceiling of absolute black, pressed down close enough to touch.

And it was moving.

That was the thing Kestrel's mind kept refusing, the thing she circled and approached and shied away from like a horse at a snake. Darkness didn't move. Darkness was an absence; absences didn't drift and fold and *lean*. But this darkness leaned. She watched it settle over the pass the way a great bird settles over a field, wings spreading not to fly but to cover, and where it touched the ridgelines the frost on the stones stopped glittering. The night itself seemed to drink the little light their shuttered lanterns leaked, drinking it politely, patiently, the way a guest drinks who intends to stay.

"Mama," said a small voice from the third wagon. One of the children, awake now. "Mama, the sky's gone."

Kestrel swung down from the saddle because standing still had become unbearable. Her body knew things before her mind agreed to them — that was the whole secret of smuggling, the whole inheritance her mother had left her: trust the animal first, argue with it later. And every animal instinct she owned was screaming that they needed to be off this exposed trail, off the high switchbacks, down into the tree-line where stone walls rose on either side and the sky became something you could ignore.

"Dara. Get the wagons moving. Down-slope, no lights, no noise. We make for the Hollow Chapel waystation."

"It's four hours down—"

"Then we'd best start." She was already unhooking the lead mule's tether, checking girth straps by touch. "And Dara — if anything asks you what you saw tonight, you saw weather. Clouds came in early. Understood?"

"What did you see?" Dara asked quietly.

Kestrel looked up once more. The darkness had finished settling. It lay over the pass like a lid on a pot, and somewhere far above — or perhaps not far at all, distance meant nothing to it — something in that blackness turned over, slow and vast, the way a sleeper turns.

"Nothing," Kestrel said. "I saw nothing. Let's keep it that way."

* * *

They went down blind.

No lanterns. No torches. The moon was three days past new and offered nothing, and the stars — the stars that had guided every Sandoval since before there were roads, the fixed and faithful lamps her grandmother had navigated by, singing their positions like old friends calling names across a valley — were gone, and Kestrel discovered within the first mile that she had never actually known how to find her way anywhere. She had only ever known how to read the sky, and the sky had been taken away, and underneath all her competence was a howling little girl lost in her own backyard.

So she did it the hard way. Hand on stone. Counting paces. Reading the trail through her boots the way her mother had taught her to read the ward-chord — the dip where water crossed in wet seasons, the rise of root-laced clay, the particular hollow knock of the bridge-planks at Miller's Cut. She called the turns back to Dara in a whisper, and Dara passed them forward, and the caravan crawled down the mountain like a funeral procession, forty-one people breathing carefully in the dark.

The horses hated it. That was the worst part, in those first hours — worse than the cold, worse than the blindness. Animals had no talent for lying to themselves. The mules went stiff-legged and trembling, and old Sorrel, Dara's gelding, kept stopping to stare back up the trail with his ears pinned flat, at a darkness he could smell but Kestrel couldn't. Twice she felt it too, on the back of her neck: a pressure, a *regard*, like being watched through a keyhole by something that had all the time in the world.

"You feel that?" Dara murmured beside her, somewhere around the second hour.

"Yes."

"What is it?"

"I don't know." The words tasted like ash. Kestrel Sandoval knew everything about Thornfell Pass. She knew which ward-stones had been re-cut in the reign of Aldric's grandfather and which still bore the old kings' marks. She knew the smuggler-cuts and the tollman's blind spots and the shrine where travelers left copper nails for luck. Her knowledge was a map drawn over thirty years and three generations, and tonight every line of it had been erased, and all she could say, all she had, was *I don't know*, and the saying of it made her want to put her fist through a tree.

Her expert knowledge counted for nothing. That was the truth of it, arriving in her chest like a swallowed stone. Whatever had come over the pass tonight, it hadn't come through a gap in her map. It had burned the map.

* * *

At Miller's Cut, the bridge was gone.

Not broken — gone. The planks, the rope rails, the stone footings, all of it simply absent, and beyond the gap the road continued on the far side as if the bridge had been lifted out of the world rather than washed away. Kestrel crouched at the edge and ran her fingers along the cut-off end of a footing stone and found the surface smooth. Not shattered. Not worn. Smoothed, the way sugar smooths in water.

"Flash flood," said Tam Wicker behind her, the carter, wanting it to be true. "Happens. Whole bridges, gone in an hour."

"There's been no rain," Kestrel said. "Not for nine days. I'd know. I've been walking in it."

Silence behind her. Then the small voice again, the child from the third wagon: "Mama, why does the dark breathe?"

Everyone heard it. Everyone pretended they hadn't. But Kestrel stood very still at the edge of the missing bridge and admitted to herself what she'd been refusing to admit since the switchbacks: the darkness wasn't sitting over the pass. It was *moving through* it. Slowly. Deliberately. Like a man searching a house room by room, opening drawers, looking for something specific. She'd felt it pass over them twice already and pass on, unsatisfied, and each time the pressure lifted she'd told herself it was wind, it was exhaustion, it was fear making shapes.

But the child was right. The dark breathed. In, and the frost died on the stones. Out, and somewhere upslope a nightbird that had been crying all evening stopped mid-note and did not start again.

It was hunting something. And whatever it was hunting, it wasn't forty-one smugglers and a load of tin.

"Rope bridge," Kestrel said aloud, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "We've got coil-line in the second wagon. Anchor points on both sides — I know where they are. We cross single file, stock last, and nobody looks up. Nobody looks up at any point tonight. Can you all agree to that for me?"

Nobody answered, which was agreement enough. She heard Dara moving back down the column, low-voiced, organizing, and she heard Tam Wicker whisper a prayer to the Shepherd — except the Shepherd was gone from the sky too, and the prayer trailed off into confusion, a man addressing a letter to a house that had burned down.

Kestrel found the anchor stone by memory and touch, wedged her line, and hauled it taut across the gap while the darkness breathed overhead. And as she worked — knots her fingers tied without instruction, knots older than the kingdom — a thought surfaced that she would spend the rest of her life trying to drown:

*The wards didn't fail. They were silenced.*

You don't trip over a chord that's been holding for three hundred years. Something had reached into the bones of Thornfell and put its hand over the long chord, gently, the way you quiet a dog before you enter its master's house. And whatever wanted into the kingdom badly enough to do that — whatever had eaten the stars to travel by — had come through *quietly*. On purpose. Unannounced.

Which meant the danger wasn't the thing in the dark above them.

The danger was wherever it was going.

* * *

They made the Hollow Chapel waystation in the grey hour before dawn — though *dawn* had become a word without a referent, because the east stayed black past the hour when the sun should have bled its first light over the peaks. The waystation was a smuggler's refuge, half chapel and half barn, built into the hillside with a turf roof and a door that locked from inside. Kestrel got the wagons under cover, got the animals settled, got fires lit in the deep hearth where the smoke wouldn't show, and then stood in the yard a moment alone, looking up.

Still nothing. Black, seamless, close. Somewhere in it, faintly, that sense of vast attention rolling onward — southward, she thought. South and west. Away from the free cities, toward—

Toward Vellmark. Toward the capital road. Toward the crown.

"No," she said aloud, to herself, to the dark. "Don't be stupid. You're a smuggler, not a scholar. Go home, count your coin, drink until the sky makes sense again."

But her hand had gone, without her permission, to the letter in her inner pocket — the letter Old Brann had pressed on her at the last waystation, the one he'd written in cipher, the one he'd told her to deliver to no one but to *keep*, with an urgency in his eyes she'd filed away as an old man's nerves. *If the sky ever goes wrong, girl, you burn that letter before you read it.* He'd laughed when he said it. He hadn't meant it as a joke.

Inside, the caravan was bedding down, children wrapped in every blanket they owned, adults sitting up in shifts because no one could sleep under a sky with nothing in it. Dara found her at the door with two cups of the terrible waystation tea and pressed one into her hands.

"We'll wait for daylight," Dara said. "However late it comes. Then push for Vellmark, get these people to walls, and you and I are going to find out what in the name of every dead god happened up there."

"Every dead god," Kestrel repeated softly. "What if it's not the dead ones we need to worry about?"

Dara snorted and went inside. Kestrel stayed in the doorway a moment longer, tea cooling in her hands, and let herself look south along the road she'd traveled a hundred times, the road to Vellmark, the city of her birth and her guild and everything she'd ever stolen or saved.

Far off — miles off, days off, impossibly far and yet unmistakably near — the darkness over the southern hills pulsed once. Gently. Like a heartbeat. Like something pleased.

Like something that had found what it was looking for.

Kestrel went inside, barred the door, and took out Brann's letter. She didn't open it. Not yet. But she sat with it in her lap by the banked fire, listening to forty-one people breathe, and outside the window the sunless morning came on slow and wrong, and the dark breathed, and breathed, and breathed.
