## 3. The Priest Without a God

The temple of Saint Vessia had been closed for eleven years, and Ivo Reyes had been living inside it for nine.

He knew every hour of the building the way a blind man knows his own hands. He knew how the dawn came through the eastern rose window in a slow red spill across the flagstones, staining the feet of the saint's statue as though she were standing in wine. He knew the sound the roof made at noon when the sun warmed the old lead — a low ticking, like a patient clock counting toward nothing. He knew that at dusk the pigeons left through the broken clerestory in a gray rush, and for one moment the whole nave seemed to breathe out.

He had not said Mass in nine years. He had not heard confession, anointed the dying, or spoken the words *the Light keeps you* to another living soul. The Church of the Radiant Flame had stripped his orders from him with the same ceremony it used for everything: precise, cold, and thorough. They had taken his stole, his ring, his name from the rolls. They could not take the building, because no one wanted it anymore — a temple in the poor quarter of Vellmark, its congregation scattered by the levy wars, its relics deemed too minor to cart away when the diocese consolidated.

So they left him here, like a coin dropped behind a cathedral altar. Worthless, and therefore safe.

He was kneeling before the statue on the night the stars went out — he still knelt sometimes, out of habit more than faith, or perhaps out of stubbornness, which he had learned was often the same thing — when he heard the bolt cutters.

The side door of Saint Vessia opened onto a mortuary lane, and the lock on it had rusted into decoration. But the sound that reached him was not the creak of old iron giving way. It was the clean, deliberate snap of tempered steel, twice, three times, and then the whisper of a door moving on hinges someone had oiled recently and quietly.

Ivo rose from his knees without hurry. He had spent nine years learning not to be afraid of sounds in the dark, mostly by being afraid of them thoroughly and often until fear wore smooth as river stone.

He moved along the shadowed aisle, past the empty offering boxes, past the votive racks stripped of candles years ago, and stopped behind the great pillar near the north transept, where the dark was deepest.

A figure slipped inside and pulled the door shut. Small. Hooded. Moving like water finding its level — low, quick, silent, checking corners in a sequence Ivo recognized. He had seen soldiers do it badly. This person did it well.

Then the stranger pushed back her hood, and moonless starless gloom or not, Ivo knew her face.

Not her actual face — he'd never seen Kestrel Sandoval before in his life. He knew the *kind* of face. The guild-mark tattooed small behind her left ear, the smuggler's ink that the Church called the thief's brand and the guilds called the road-sign. And in her hand, wrapped in oilcloth but unmistakable even so, a letter sealed with wax pressed by a signet ring Ivo would have known anywhere, because he had buried the man who wore it.

Old Brann's sigil. The interlocked wheel and key.

Ivo stepped out of the shadows. "You're either very lost," he said, "or Brannoc Hale is dead."

She spun, knife already out — a good knife, held correctly — and froze at the sound of his voice. Her eyes raked the darkness trying to find all of him and finding only a tall shape in a cassock gone gray with dust.

"Who's asking?"

"A priest." He let the word sit there, let her hear the hollowness in it. "Or what's left of one. Third question tonight will cost you, so choose carefully."

"The sigil," she said. Her voice was steady but her breathing wasn't; she'd run far and fast to get here. "You know it."

"I know it." He struck a light — flint and steel against the stub of a candle he kept hidden in the pulpit, one of his few luxuries — and lit the lamp beside the statue of Saint Vessia, patroness of lost things. Fitting, he thought. She had certainly delivered one to his door.

In the lamplight the woman resolved out of the dark: mid-twenties, sharp-boned, a fresh burn blistering along her jaw where something hot had passed too close. Her eyes were the eyes of someone who had watched a world end tonight and hadn't yet decided whether to grieve or fight it.

"You're the defrocked one," she said slowly. "Reyes. The one who argued the wards were sacrilege."

"I'm *a* defrocked one. There were several of us. We had a club." He crossed the nave, unhurried, hands visible. "And you're Sandoval. Which means Brann is dead, because that man would have eaten glass before sending you to a stranger with that letter."

Her jaw tightened. The burn along it whitened. "This morning," she said. "The guild hall burned with him in it. Inquisitors. He got out long enough to give me this." She held up the letter. "He told me to find the man who remembered what the wards were *for*. He wouldn't say who."

"No," Ivo agreed softly. "He wouldn't. Brann never trusted a truth to survive being named aloud." He looked at the letter for a long moment without touching it. "Read it to me. Not here — the walls of churches have ears again, these days. The vestry. There's tea, if you don't mind it tasting like bark."

* * *

The vestry of Saint Vessia had once held vestments worth a bishop's ransom. Now it held a cot, a brazier, three crates of books Ivo had rescued from the diocese bonfire, and a kettle. He made the tea while she read the letter aloud, and by the third paragraph his hands had gone still on the kettle handle.

"—*the Ember is not fed by the crown, girl, it IS the crown, it has been eating the roads we kept for six generations, and it knows your blood now, God forgive me for what we let them do to your mother—*" Kestrel's voice caught on that line and went over it like a cart over a rut. "*Find Reyes. He read the same texts I did, before they burned them. He'll hate that I'm right. Make him help you anyway.*"

Silence. The kettle ticked as it heated. Somewhere above, a pigeon resettled.

"He told you about your mother?" Ivo asked quietly.

"He told me my family didn't abandon me." Her voice was flat in the way voices go flat when feeling has been amputated to keep the patient alive. "That's new information. I'm going to need a minute before I have feelings about it."

"Take ten. I'll want at least that long myself." Ivo set down two chipped cups and poured. "Because here is my problem, Sandoval. Nine years ago I stood in the Chapter House of Vellmark and told the assembled clergy that the border wards of Thornfell were not protection. That they had never been protection. That they were a *mouth*, built to funnel something toward the crown, and that the Church had helped build it and then helped everyone forget. For that I was defrocked, excommunicated, and invited — politely, with paperwork — to never speak again." He sat down across from her. "Brann was the only person who wrote to me afterward. One letter a year, unsigned, always the same closing line: *the wheel still turns.* So yes. I know the sigil. And yes, I will hate helping you. And yes, I will do it anyway, because I have spent nine years being right in an empty church, and being right alone is just a slower way of being wrong."

Kestrel looked at him over the rim of her cup. Something in her posture eased by a single degree — not trust, he noted, but the decision to postpone distrust.

"What did they take from you?" she asked. "When they defrocked you. Besides everything obvious."

"My library. My ordination. My—" He paused. "There was a text. A codex, pre-Founding, recovered from Ironhollow itself three centuries ago. *The Ledger of the Hollow Flame.* It documented the forging of the Ember — not as legend, as procedure. Who made it. Why. What it was *before* it was a crown-thing." His thumb moved absently over the scar on his palm where a ring had sat for twenty years. "The Inquisition confiscated it the day after my trial. I've spent nine years trying to learn where they put it. Six months ago I finally found out."

"Where?"

"The Reliquary Vault under the Bastion Chapel. Same building where they store everything they don't want the faithful thinking about." He smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Including, as of four days ago, a certain defrocked priest's file. Which means they haven't forgotten me either. Which means, Sandoval, that when the inquisitors burned your guild hall this morning, they were carrying orders with my description somewhere in the stack."

"They're hunting both of us."

"They're hunting everyone who ever knew. That's rather the point of the Ember, if Brann's letters were honest. It doesn't just eat magic. It eats *memory*. It eats the evidence of itself." He leaned forward. "Now. Before I agree to anything, tell me what happened tonight. On the road. Tell me about the sky."

For the first time since entering his church, Kestrel Sandoval looked genuinely frightened.

"The stars went out," she said. "All of them, at once, like snuffed candles. And the dark — Father, the dark *moved*. It came down the valley like it was looking for something. Looking for *me*, maybe. I felt it pass over the caravan like a hand." She set down her cup. Her hand was not quite steady. "What kind of thing puts out the stars?"

Ivo was quiet for a long time. When he answered, he answered the way he had once answered from the pulpit — slowly, weighing each word, knowing some of them might be the last ones anyone believed.

"One that's getting hungry," he said. "One that has finished with the borders and started on the sky. The wards were its fence, Sandoval. Tonight it broke its own fence, because it doesn't need a fence anymore. It needs a door." He nodded at the letter in her lap. "And according to a dying smuggler, you're the key."

* * *

They talked until the candle burned to a thumbnail and the tea went cold twice. He told her about the wards — what little the forbidden texts had survived to teach him: that the Founders had bound something at Ironhollow, something forged rather than born, and that the binding required keepers, blood-bound, generation after generation. He told her how the crown had converted the keepers' roads into smuggling routes, how the guilds had become unwitting couriers of their own prison's maintenance, and how, somewhere in the last century, the arrangement had inverted — the Ember had begun feeding on the roads instead of being fed by them.

"And the keepers?" Kestrel asked. "What happened to them?"

"What happens to every lineage the powerful find inconvenient." Ivo looked at the dead fire in the brazier. "Scattered. Renamed. Forgotten. Or in your family's case, apparently, whittled down one death at a time until only you were left."

She absorbed that in silence. He watched her do it — watched grief and fury move through her face like weather, neither allowed to land.

"So Ironhollow," she said at last. "You can get us there."

"I can get us *toward* there. Whether we arrive is a different sacrament entirely." He stood, joints cracking, and went to the third crate of books — the one he never opened in front of anyone. From beneath a false bottom he drew a folded map, hand-copied across nine years from fragments, rumor, and one unforgettable afternoon in the diocesan archive before they caught him. "The mountain roads are watched. The river roads are watched harder. But there are ways underneath — old ways, miner's ways, ways the Church sealed and forgot. I know the entrances. I don't know what's in them."

"Nothing's ever in them," Kestrel said. "That's why they're forgotten."

"That," said Ivo, "is the most theological thing you've said all night, and I mean it as a compliment."

But when he turned back to her, she wasn't smiling. She was watching him with the flat assessing look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"You want something," she said. "Nobody helps for free. Not priests, especially not priests. What's the price?"

"The text," he said simply. "*The Ledger of the Hollow Flame.* It sits forty feet below the Bastion Chapel, behind a vault door that opens to an inquisitor's seal. I can forge the seal. I cannot walk into that building. Every guard in Vellmark has my face pinned above their ale." He met her eyes. "You break into places for a living, Sandoval. Get me my book, and I will walk you into the earth itself and out the other side of the kingdom."

"And if the book's a trap? If they're waiting for you to come crawling back?"

"Then we'll be caught together, which is more companionship than I've had in nine years, so I'll count it a win regardless."

Something almost like a laugh escaped her — brief, rusty, surprised out of her. Then it died, and she looked down at Brann's letter, at the wheel-and-key sigil, and Ivo saw her make the decision the way he had once watched sailors make decisions in storms: quickly, completely, with no looking back permitted.

"All right, Father. One book for one road." She stood and slung her pack. "We move tonight. The inquisitors burned the guild hall at dawn; they'll sweep the poor quarter by morning. Where's this chapel?"

"Half a mile east. Which brings us to the difficult part." Ivo took his cloak from its hook — gray wool, hooded, the cassock's last disguise — and paused at the vestry door with his hand on the frame.

Behind him, Kestrel waited. "The difficult part being?"

"Being that for nine years, the hardest prayer I ever said was the one where I asked God to forgive me for still believing in Him." He pushed the door open onto the starless night, onto streets darker than any street had a right to be, and found that his heart was hammering like a novice's. "Tonight I find out if He was listening. Stay close, Sandoval. And whatever you see in that vault — whatever the Ember shows you, because it *will* show you something —"

He stepped out into the dark. Above them, where the Pleiades should have hung over Vellmark's rooftops, there was only a blackness with texture to it, a blackness that seemed, at its edges, to be listening.

"—don't answer it."

They went east together, priest and smuggler, two people with nothing left but what they'd lost, toward a chapel full of the things the kingdom most wanted forgotten.

Neither of them saw the figure detach from the shadow of the bell tower and follow — soft-footed, patient, wearing the gray of the Inquisition — nor the way it paused at the temple door, touched the wood once, almost gently, and whispered a word in a language that predated the crown.

The word meant *found*.
