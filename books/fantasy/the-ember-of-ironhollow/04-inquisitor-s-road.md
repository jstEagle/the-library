## 4. Inquisitor's Road

The rain had not stopped in three days, and Maren Voss had begun to suspect it was not weather.

It fell too evenly. It fell without wind. It sheeted down over the garrison at Cael's Rest in gray curtains that never thickened and never thinned, as though the sky had been given a single instruction and lacked the imagination to vary it. She stood at the arrow-slit window of the interrogation block and watched it drown the courtyard below, where her soldiers moved like figures in a bad dream, hunched and silent, their lanterns burning with a light that cast no warmth on anything.

Behind her, in the second cell, a smuggler was screaming.

Not from the irons. The irons were cold-forged, warded, the kind that bit only when they needed to. The screaming had started an hour ago, when Voss had asked her first real question, and it had a quality she recognized — that raw, unraveling terror of a man who has understood something about his situation that the irons alone could never teach him.

She turned from the window. Her boots left wet prints on the stone; the rain followed her indoors these days, or something did. She had stopped trying to decide which.

The corridor to the cells was lit by a single lamp, and its flame burned a color she refused to name. Not quite gold. Not quite the white of fever. Somewhere between, the way a bruise is somewhere between purple and yellow. The flame bent toward her as she passed, the way grass bends toward a thing that walks through it, and she kept her eyes forward because looking at it directly made the scars ache.

She had eleven scars now. She'd had four when she took Cael's Rest for the crown.

They ran along her left side, from collarbone to hip, thin and silvered and old-looking, though the youngest of them was three weeks old. They did not hurt, exactly. What they did was *listen*. When the whispering came — and it came more often each week, seeping up through her bones like damp through mortar — it came loudest through the scars, as if they were seams in her skin and the voice behind them was pressing its mouth to each one in turn.

*The Sandoval girl,* it had said last night, at the hour when even the watch fires drowse. *Find me the Sandoval girl.*

Voss pushed open the door to the second cell.

* * *

The smuggler's name was Denny Hale, and he had been a fool long before tonight. He hung from the wall-irons by both wrists, toes barely scraping the floor, a broad soft man gone suddenly narrow with fear, his smuggler's leathers dark with sweat. He had stopped screaming when the door opened. That was the pattern. They always stopped when she entered, as if her face were worse than the pain.

Perhaps it was. She had been told, once, before, by someone who loved her — the memory arrived blurred at the edges, a name attached to it dissolving like sugar in water — that her eyes had gone wrong. That they looked at a person the way winter looks at a field: patiently, with no malice at all, certain of what would come.

"Master Hale," she said. "You've been quiet."

"Please." His voice was shredded. "General. I don't know anything. I run cloth, mostly. Cloth and salt—"

"You ran cloth and salt," Voss agreed. She dragged the room's single stool across the flagstones, the scrape of it enormous in the small space, and sat facing him. Up close she could smell him: sweat, old beer, river mud, and beneath it all a faint char-smell, the smell of a man whose guild hall had recently been ash. "Past tense is correct. Your guild is dead, Master Hale. Burned in Vellmark two nights ago. You were taken on the east road with a wagonload of nothing but salt, which tells me you were either the guild's most honest man or its most expendable."

He wept then, quietly, the way men do when they've stopped hoping anyone will be merciful and started hoping only that it will be brief.

Voss waited. Patience was the whole of her method. Anyone could turn a screw; the crown had a hundred men who could turn screws, and most of them enjoyed it, which was why they were not given important work. But patience — sitting in the silence with a man until his own mind began working against him, until every minute of quiet became a question he had to answer just to break it — patience was rarer, and it cost her nothing, because time was the one thing the thing in the crown had given her plenty of.

"The Sandoval girl," she said at last.

Hale flinched so hard the irons rattled.

"There it is," Voss murmured. "You know the name."

"I don't—"

"Your guild hall burned on the same night the stars went out. Every guild hall in the free cities burned within a day of it — did you know that? Coordinated. Precise. Someone wanted the smugglers scattered or dead, and someone else wanted one particular smuggler found." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and let the lamplight fall across her face so he could see what was in her eyes. "Kestrel Sandoval. Young woman. Runs the Thornfell routes. Her family is dead or scattered. And my orders say she is to be taken *alive*, Master Hale, unharmed, and delivered to the capital. Do you understand what that means?"

"No," Hale whispered. It was almost true. He understood it in his gut, Voss could see; he simply hadn't assembled it into words yet, and the words were the worst part.

"It means," Voss said, "that in a kingdom where the general order is burn and take, one girl is worth more breathing than any ten of us are worth obeying. It means the crown wants her for something. And the crown—" she paused, and here she permitted herself honesty, because honesty frightened people more than cruelty ever had, "—the crown is not entirely the crown anymore, is it? You've seen the king's levies. You've seen the soldiers come back from the capital with that look. Something sits behind Aldric's eyes, and whatever it is, it wants your Kestrel Sandoval the way a starving man wants bread."

"I haven't seen her," Hale said. "I swear it on my mother. On the salt itself. I'm a low-route man, General, I've never even met her—"

"But you've heard things." Voss settled back. "Smugglers hear everything. It's the one trade skill that survives a burned guild hall. So. Tell me what you've heard, and I'll tell you how you leave this room."

That was a lie, of course. There was only one way out of the room, and it ended in the courtyard, in the rain, under the ward-post where the bodies went. But Hale needed to believe in a door, and belief made men generous.

He talked.

Mostly it was dross — route gossip, toll bribes, which wardens in Graywater took coin and which took worse. Voss let it wash past her, cataloguing, waiting. Then, near the end, wrung out and babbling, eager to have said enough, he gave her the thing.

"—they say she was on the Thornfell road the night it happened. The night the wards broke. Some of the boys from the north route swore the darkness out there wasn't empty, it was *looking* for something, and then word comes down that the Sandoval girl walked out of it alive when nobody else did. And Old Brann—" Hale's voice cracked. "Old Brann always said the Sandovals were marked. Said it half-joking, over drinks, but he meant it. Said there was a reason the Ember's men never touched their routes. Said they were *kept*. Like — like you keep a thing you're going to need later."

The whispering rose.

It came up through the floor first, Voss felt it in the soles of her feet, then through the scars, each seam in her skin warming like a struck match held close. *Kept.* The word echoed strangely, doubled, as if something far away had heard it too and leaned closer to listen.

"Yes," Voss said softly, and did not know if she answered Hale or the other listener. "Like a thing you're going to need later."

*Alive,* breathed the voice through her scars, and this time it was not a memory of last night's orders. It was present. It was *here*, in the cell, in the lamp, in the rain falling without wind outside. *Bring her to me alive and whole. Bring me the blood that remembers the forge.*

Hale was staring at her. "General? Are you—"

"Quiet," Voss said.

For a moment she let herself feel it fully — the attention pouring through her, vast and cold and patient, wearing her scars like finger-holes in a puppet. This was the truth of her service that no one spoke aloud: that the usurper-general Maren Voss, terror of the border roads, breaker of Cael's Rest, was not the crown's instrument. She was its *aperture*. A hole shaped like a woman, through which something older reached into the world.

And the horror of it — the true horror, the one that woke her at night sweating in a body that no longer felt entirely hers — was not that she served it.

It was that some part of her *liked being chosen*.

* * *

Afterward, she stood in the courtyard and let the rain fall on her.

Denny Hale had been led to the ward-post. He had gone quietly, which she noted with a distant professional satisfaction; the loud ones were harder on the soldiers. Before the end he had asked her one question, not the one she expected — not *why*, not *how could you* — but: "Is it true the stars are coming back?" And Voss had said, "No," and he had nodded as if that settled something, and then it was done.

Now the courtyard was empty except for her and the rain and the ward-post, still warm.

She flexed her left hand. Beneath the glove, the newest scar — a thin line across her palm she did not remember earning — pulsed gently, in time with something that was not her heartbeat. Three weeks ago it had been a wound from a smuggler's knife, shallow, forgettable. By morning it had closed silver and smooth, and the whispering had changed key. Closer. More familiar. As if the thing in the crown had moved another room nearer inside her house.

*You wonder,* it said now, conversational, almost kind, *whether you were ever anything else.*

"I wonder whether you'll keep your bargain," Voss said aloud, to the rain, to the dark above the rain where stars should have been and were not. Her breath smoked. "You promised me my son."

*Did I?*

"You did. In the crown chamber, the first night. You said the boy lives as long as I serve."

*And he does live.* A pause, savoring. *As long as you serve.*

The sentence had a shape to it, and Voss turned the shape over the way she'd once turned captured dispatches, looking for the trap in the folds. *As long as you serve.* Not *while*. Not *so long as*. As long as. Service without horizon. Service unto — she would not finish the thought. Finishing thoughts was dangerous now; the thing read her mind the way she read a map, easily, and it rewarded certain conclusions with warmth and punished others with the ache in her scars, training her, patiently, the way she trained men.

She thought of Tam anyway. She couldn't help it. Eight years old, left in Vellmark with her sister, a gap-toothed boy who drew armies in the dust and always gave himself the banner with the hawk. Was he real? Was he fed? Did he ask for her? Or had the Ember simply reached into her memory, lifted the image of her son the way a pickpocket lifts a purse, and promised it back to her one day at a time?

She could not check. Letters went to the capital and answers came back in her sister's hand, but the handwriting had grown subtly wrong over the months — the letters spaced too evenly, the way the rain fell too evenly — and she had begun to suspect that the replies were written by the same patient absence that wrote her orders.

That was the machine, she thought. That was the whole of it. Not armies, not inquisitors, not the hollow-eyed king on his throne of light. The machine was *doubt, administered daily*. You served because you could not prove you shouldn't. You obeyed because disobedience might cost the one real thing you had left, and you could no longer verify that the one real thing existed anywhere but in the promise. The Ember did not devour kingdoms first. It devoured the evidence. It ate the stars, and it ate the certainty, and it ate the faces in old letters, and by the time it came for the rest of you, there was nothing left to resist with.

*I chose well,* the voice murmured through her scars, pleased, proprietary. *You see clearly. That is why you will find her.*

"And when I do?" Voss asked. "When I bring you this girl — this blood that remembers the forge — what happens to her?"

Silence. The particular silence of a parent declining to answer a child.

"What happens to *me*?"

The rain fell. Somewhere beyond the walls, out on the drowned roads, the darkness that was not weather shifted its weight, and every lantern in the garrison guttered at once, and Voss felt the scars pull taut like harp strings being tuned by hands she could not see.

Then, distantly, from the direction of the capital — a sensation rather than a sound, arriving through the seams of her skin — the thing in the crown turned its attention fully upon her, and for one vertiginous moment Maren Voss saw herself as it saw her: a vessel three-quarters full, a lamp burning the wrong color, a name (Maren, her mother's voice said, Maren, come in out of the rain) already half-eaten.

*Bring her to Ironhollow,* it said. *Bring her to the forge. And you will want for nothing ever again.*

The attention withdrew. The lanterns steadied. The rain went on falling its single, obedient note.

Voss stood in the courtyard a while longer, because going inside meant sleeping, and sleeping meant dreams, and lately her dreams had a witness.

Then the horn sounded from the east gate — two notes, urgent, the signal for a rider under seal — and she turned, and watched a mud-spattered courier walk his lathered horse through the gate bearing the crown's black wax.

She broke the seal on the steps. The orders inside were written in a hand she knew, though she had never seen it: elegant, precise, each letter identical to its brothers, the hand of something that had learned writing the way a mimic learns birdsong.

*The Sandoval girl moves south. She travels with a defrocked priest and carries the sigil of the old ledger. Take the Graywater crossing. Take her alive. The priest may be broken. The blood must not be spilled.*

Voss read it twice. Then she looked up at the sky where the stars should have been, and felt the scars pulse their slow, borrowed rhythm, and thought: *a priest, and a sigil, and a road south* — and beneath the dread, beneath the horror of the machine she served, something small and stubborn and still hers stirred, the way a coal stirs when the ash over it shifts.

She gave the order to ride within the hour.

Graywater, she thought, as the column formed in the drowning rain. There was a toll bridge at Graywater, and a flooded cave system beneath the bluffs that every smuggler in the borderlands knew and every crown soldier's map showed as solid rock.

Someone had taught her that once. Someone with a laugh like a hinge, years ago, before the scars, before the crown, before the rain forgot how to stop.

She could not remember his name. But she remembered the caves.

And she rode.
