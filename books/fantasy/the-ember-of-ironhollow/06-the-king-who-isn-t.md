## 6. The King Who Isn't

The garrison at Coldmere sat where the Thornfell road narrowed between two shoulders of hill, and it had grown fat on the king's war. Kestrel counted the changes from the ridgeline above: new palisade stakes, black with pitch; a gallows frame that hadn't been there in spring; cookfires in numbers that meant a levy was being processed, not merely housed. Smoke rose straight up into a sky the color of wet slate — no stars even at midday now, if you knew how to look, which she did. The dark had a texture to it lately, like gauze drawn over everything bright.

"You're sure about this," Wren murmured beside her. The scholar had insisted on coming to the overlook, hood up, ink-stained fingers worrying the strap of her satchel. "A levy means census rolls. Census rolls mean clerks who write down faces."

"I need to see the supply trains." Kestrel kept her voice flat and low. "If Voss is moving grain west, the free cities have weeks. If she's moving iron—" She didn't finish. They'd all heard what iron meant.

Below them, past the terraced barley fields stripped half-harvested by soldiers' hands, the road funneled farmers and fisherfolk and one unlucky thread of peddlers toward the garrison gate. Kestrel had watched levies before, under the old king's regency and the year of regents after. They'd been orderly then. A clerk, a scale for the household goods, a stamped chit and a weeping mother, but orderly. This was different. This moved like a slaughter line dressed in bureaucracy.

Ivo crouched behind them among the gorse, his big hands folded as if in prayer he no longer believed in. "There's a priest's collar in my pack," he said quietly. "And a writ of inspection I forged in Vellmark that says I'm auditing tithes for the Crown's charity offices. It's thin. But it gets us through the gate, and through the gate is the only place you'll learn anything worth knowing."

"It's more than thin," Wren said. "It's gauze over a wound."

"Most faith is." Ivo's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "Well, Kestrel? Your roads, your call."

Kestrel looked down at the smoke, at the line of the desperate shuffling toward the gate, and felt the letter from Brann shift against her ribs where she'd sewn it into her coat. *The Ember eats. It has always eaten. Ask yourself what it wears.* She'd read those lines so many times the ink was going soft.

"I want to see him," she said.

Both of them went still.

"The king travels with the levy seasons now — everyone says so. He inspects. He *chooses*." She spat the word. "I've spent three weeks running from inquisitors and reading dead men's letters, and I still don't know what we're walking toward. So I'm going to look at it. At *him*. Through a gate, behind a forged writ, in and out before the clerks finish their noon bread." She stood, brushing dirt from her knees. "You can wait on the ridge if you like."

"Absolutely not," said Wren.

"God has abandoned me," said Ivo, shouldering his pack, "but apparently curiosity hasn't."

* * *

The gate took them.

It shouldn't have. That was the first wrongness Kestrel noted, cataloguing it the way she'd once catalogued patrol rotations along the Thornfell run: the guard barely glanced at Ivo's writ, barely glanced at their faces, waved them through with the bored brutality of men who had stopped seeing people as people days or weeks ago. Inside, the garrison yard was a study in organized misery. Families knelt in rows on the trampled mud, sorted by chalk marks on wooden boards hung around their necks. *Fit. Partial. Exempt.* Clerks moved down the rows with ledgers chained to their belts, and behind the clerks walked soldiers with short iron rods that hummed faintly when they passed near certain people — Kestrel saw a boy flinch as one went by, saw the rod glow dull orange near his shoulder, saw the clerk chalk something new onto his board.

Wren made a small sound. "They're testing for affinity," she breathed. "That's ward-craft. Crude, brutalized ward-craft. Those rods are resonance gauges — they were instruments, once, for calibrating boundary stones." Her knuckles had gone white around her satchel strap. "They're measuring children, Kestrel. For what?"

"For harvest," Kestrel said, and hated how easily the word came now.

They played their parts. Ivo in his stolen collar moved through the yard with a defrocked priest's particular genius — authority without accountability, blessing without doctrine — and clerks parted for him because no one wanted to be the one who delayed the Crown's charity. Wren kept her eyes down and her mouth shut, which cost her visibly. And Kestrel did what smugglers do: she drifted, unremarkable, reading the yard the way other people read books.

She found what she was looking for at the far end, where the ground rose toward the old watchtower and the garrison's original keep. There was a cleared lane there, roped off, swept clean of mud. Soldiers lined it at intervals, facing outward, and every one of them wore the same expression: not vigilance. *Fear*, carefully managed, the way a man manages a burn.

At the top of the lane, beneath a canopy of grey silk, stood a chair that was almost a throne, and in the chair sat King Aldric.

Kestrel had seen him twice from a distance in her old life — once at his coronation, a broad-shouldered man laughing on the steps of the Hall of Concord, and once years later at a distance across a market square, greyer, quieter, already changed. She remembered dark eyes that moved when he looked at things. She remembered a man who seemed to occupy space generously, the way large personalities do.

The thing in the chair occupied nothing.

He sat perfectly upright, hands resting on the arms of the chair, and he did not fidget, did not shift, did not perform any of the hundred small motions a living body makes when it belongs to itself. His face was Aldric's face — older now, hollowed at the cheeks, the skin gone the color of candle wax — but it was the eyes that stopped Kestrel's breath. They were open. They were aimed at the yard below, at the kneeling families, at the clerks and the humming rods. And there was nothing behind them. Not cruelty, not emptiness-as-absence, but emptiness-as-*displacement*: the look of a house whose owner has gone out and left the windows lit.

Then, as she watched, the light in the windows flickered.

It was small. It was everything. One moment the king's gaze swept the yard with that terrible smooth vacancy, and the next — a hitch, a stutter, a fraction of a second where the eyes *caught*, the way a horse catches its stride on broken ground — and Kestrel, twenty yards below him and hidden among clerks' carts, would have sworn on Brann's grave that someone looked back at her. Someone behind the glass. Someone pressing both palms against the inside of the window of his own face.

His lips parted. No sound came out. But she saw the shape the mouth made, saw it repeat, saw the jaw tremble with an effort like a drowning man kicking for surface:

*Run.*

The window closed. The vacancy poured back in like cold water filling a footprint, and the king's head turned — smoothly, horribly smoothly — toward the section of yard where Kestrel stood.

"There," he said.

His voice carried. It wasn't loud, but the whole yard bent toward it the way grass bends under wind, and Kestrel understood in one lurching instant that this was the first time in the entire morning the king had spoken. The clerks froze. The soldiers stiffened. And the voice — it was Aldric's voice, she could hear that, worn and rusted — but underneath it ran another sound, a harmonic too low to hear properly and impossible to unhear once noticed, like a bell struck in a room next door.

"One of these," the king said, "is mine."

Chaos, of the quiet kind. Clerks began checking their boards. Soldiers began moving down the rows with their humming rods raised. And Kestrel's body made decisions her mind hadn't authorized yet — she was already turning, already pulling Wren by the sleeve, already murmuring *walk, don't run, walk* — when a rod swung past her hip and flared orange as a bonfire.

The soldier holding it looked at the gauge. Then at her. Then at the king's chair on the rise.

Kestrel hit him.

Not elegantly — there was no time for elegance — just a smuggler's short brutal shove-punch to the throat that dropped him gagging into the mud, and then she was moving, hauling Wren one way while Ivo, magnificent and terrible in his false collar, stepped directly into the soldiers' path and boomed in his best pulpit voice, "In the name of the Crown's charity, stand where you are and be *counted*!" — buying three seconds, five, enough. They went over a cart of salted fish, through a gap between a tannery shed and the palisade that only existed because Kestrel's eye had catalogued it an hour ago, and out through the drainage culvert beneath the wall, into the alder scrub beyond, running now, all pretense burned, as horns began to sound behind them.

* * *

They didn't stop until full dark, eight miles north in a fold of hills where a shepherd's bothy leaned against the slope like a tired animal. No fire. They sat close together in the black, sharing hard cheese and colder water, listening to the distant horns give up their search one by one.

"He spoke," Wren said finally. Her voice had the careful steadiness of someone holding a cracked vessel very level. "Levies don't make kings speak. The clerks reacted like they'd never heard it. Like it *never happens*."

"It doesn't happen," Kestrel said. "Which means whatever's wearing him spent something to say it. Or—" She stopped. Started again. "Or he spent it. Himself. From inside."

Ivo was silent a long time. When he spoke, his voice had lost its pulpit thunder entirely. "I've served in courts, in my first life. I've seen possession — or what the Church called possession, which was usually epilepsy and cruelty. This wasn't that. Possession erases. What I saw today..." He turned his cheese rind over in his fingers. "What I saw was a man signaling from a locked room."

"*Run,*" Wren quoted softly. "He told her to run. Before the thing caught him again."

"And then it said *one of these is mine*," Kestrel said. "It felt me. The way the dark on the road feels me. The way the inquisitors keep finding my trail no matter how I break it." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Brann wrote that it eats our line. Eats it *slowly*, generation by generation, like a man nursing a cellar of wine. And now it knows exactly which bottle is left."

The silence that followed had weight. In it, Kestrel found herself back at the top of that swept lane, staring into the empty windows of the most powerful man in the kingdom, hearing a word shaped by starving lips.

When she'd set out from Vellmark, she had carried a simple arithmetic. The Ember was a monster. Monsters get killed. The king was the monster's meat and mask, and masks break. She had rehearsed the mission in those terms on long stretches of road: reach Ironhollow, find the forge, destroy the thing, save the kingdom. Clean. Killable.

But she had seen the hands on the arms of the chair. She had seen the effort it took that ruined face to shape a single warning — the cost of it written in the trembling of his jaw, the way a man pays his last coin. Whatever the Ember was, whatever it had done to the wards and the stars and her family's generations of dead, it was doing this too: it was keeping a living man buried alive inside his own crown, conscious enough to care, awake enough to warn a stranger.

"This isn't just a monster to kill," she said aloud, testing the words and finding them true. "It's a hostage to free."

Wren looked at her sharply. "You understand what that does to the problem. Destroying the binding might destroy the bound. If the Ember is woven through him—"

"Then we don't destroy it blind. We learn what it is first. Truly." Kestrel pulled Brann's letter from her coat, though in the dark she couldn't read it and didn't try; she just held it, the way you hold a compass. "Ironhollow. The forge. Everything starts there — the Ember, the wards, my family's whole cursed inheritance. And somewhere in that story is the difference between killing a king and saving one."

Ivo shifted in the darkness. "There's someone who might know that difference better than any ledger," he said slowly. "Someone who stood in the court six years ago and screamed about the crown until they called him mad. Someone who was exiled for it — stripped, named, driven into the mountains." A pause. "Prince Tomas. The king's brother."

Kestrel went very still.

"They say he drinks now," Ivo continued. "They say he raves. But madmen who are right are the most dangerous witnesses in the world, and the crown let him live — which has always struck me as either mercy or fear." She could hear the grim smile enter his voice. "I know which taverns exiled princes haunt. The mining towns past Graywater. If anyone can tell us what Aldric was like *before* — what the man inside the window looks like when the windows are his own — it's the brother who watched him change."

Outside the bothy, the wind moved through the alders, and for a moment — Kestrel would remember this, would carry it the rest of her life — the wind sounded like a voice saying *run*, and beneath it, deeper, patient as roots, something else saying *mine*.

She stood, and slung her pack.

"Graywater, then," she said. "We're going to collect a prince."
