## 7. Brother of the Hollow King

The mountain had a name once — something old and holy in a language nobody spoke anymore — but now it was just called the Maw, because that was what the miners had dug into its flank: a wound wide enough to swallow carts whole. The town at its foot was called Cinderhatch, and it smelled like every mining town Tomas Aldricson had ever been thrown out of. Sulfur and sweat, coal smoke and cheap ale, the sour breath of men who spent their days underground and their nights trying to forget what the dark felt like.

He loved it here, in the way a man loves a bruise.

Tomas sat in the back corner of the Drowned Lantern with his back to the wall — an old habit, one of the few pieces of his former life he'd managed to keep — and watched the lamplight swim in the bottom of his cup. Third cup? Fourth? The numbers had stopped mattering around the time the barkeep started giving him looks that were more pity than patience. He'd traded his last silver ring for credit two nights ago. Tomorrow he'd trade something else. The day after that, maybe nothing would be left to trade, and then the mountain could have him too.

"You're drinking early," said the barkeep, a woman named Hess with forearms like ship rope and a face that had never once smiled at him.

"I'm drinking late," Tomas said. "From yesterday."

Hess snorted and moved on down the bar. That was the arrangement between them: she let him drink, he let her insult him, and neither of them pretended it was anything other than what it was. A prince of the realm, rotting gently in a town of four hundred souls who didn't know his name and wouldn't believe it if they heard it.

*Prince of the realm.* He turned the phrase over like a coin from a dead country. There was no realm anymore, not really. There was a crown, and there was the thing that wore it, and there were the people who looked at King Aldric on his throne and saw a king instead of a suit of flesh with something else behind the eyes.

Tomas had seen. Eleven years ago, he had *seen*, and he had done the one thing you must never do in a court: he had said so out loud.

*Madness,* they'd called it. The physicians had used gentler words — melancholia, an affliction of the humors, a fever of the mind brought on by grief for their dead mother. His father had wept while signing the papers. Or someone wearing his father's face had wept; Tomas had never been able to decide, afterward, whether the tears were real or whether the thing in the crown had learned to make them, the way it had learned everything else about being a man.

He drank. The ale tasted like ash, which was fitting, because everything tasted like ash now. It had for eleven years.

The door of the Drowned Lantern banged open, letting in a knife of cold air and the smell of snow. Three figures came in out of the dusk, shaking frost off their cloaks, and Tomas noted them with the automatic attention of a man who had spent a decade learning which strangers meant trouble. A woman, young, dark-haired, moving like she owned the floor and was only lending it to everyone else. A tall scholar-type with ink-stained fingers and the haunted look of someone who'd read too many forbidden books. And a priest — no, not quite a priest anymore, judging by the missing collar and the way he kept touching the doorframe like he expected it to be barred against him.

Smugglers, probably. Or refugees. Cinderhatch got both, this far from Vellmark, this close to nothing.

They weren't his problem. Nothing was his problem. That was the entire architecture of his life now: build the days small enough that nothing in them could matter.

He went back to his cup.

But the woman had seen him. He felt it the way you feel weather changing — a pressure behind the ears. She crossed the room without hurrying, weaving between tables, and when she reached his corner she didn't ask permission. She just pulled out the opposite bench and sat, setting her elbows on the scarred wood, studying him with eyes the color of wet slate.

"Tomas Aldricson," she said. Not a question.

The name landed in his chest like a dropped stone. Around them the tavern noise went on — dice clattering, a fiddle wheezing somewhere near the fire, miners laughing too loud — but for Tomas the sound seemed to come from very far away, through water.

"Wrong man," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Never heard of him. Sad story, though. You should ask someone."

"The exiled prince," she pressed on, as if he hadn't spoken. "Committed to the care of the Priory of Saint Vessal at nineteen. Escaped at twenty-two. Seen since in Graywater, in Marrow's Ford, in—" she ticked the towns off on her fingers, and each name was a year of his life laid open on the table like a gutted fish, "—and now here. Drinking himself toward the grave one copper at a time."

"Flattering." He made himself meet her eyes. "And you are?"

"Kestrel Sandoval."

The name meant nothing to him. He showed her that, too, letting his face go blank and bored, the mask he'd worn through a hundred interrogations by people who wanted to know if the mad prince was still mad. Behind her, the priest and the scholar hovered near the door, pretending to warm their hands at the fire, pretending very badly.

"Sandoval," he repeated. "Should I know you?"

"No," she said. "But you should know why I'm here." She glanced around — at the miners, at Hess, at the fiddler — and lowered her voice. "Your brother is dying. Slowly. From the inside. And I think you've known it longer than anyone alive."

The tavern tilted. Or perhaps it was only the ale, finally catching up with him, finally collecting its debt. Tomas set the cup down with exaggerated care, because his hands had begun to shake and he did not want her to see.

"I don't have a brother," he said quietly.

"You have a king."

"I have a *crown*," he snapped, and the word cracked something open in the room, in him, in the eleven careful years of his exile. Several heads turned. He dragged his voice back down to a whisper, to the gravel-bed where he kept everything that still hurt. "Crowns sit on heads, girl. What sits on my brother's head isn't a crown. It's a mouth."

For a long moment Kestrel Sandoval said nothing. Then, softly: "I know."

* * *

They ended up outside, because he couldn't bear to say the rest of it under a roof full of strangers, and because the cold helped. Cold always helped. Cold was honest. He led them up past the last row of miners' huts to the spoil-heap at the edge of town, a black hill of crushed stone where the wind came off the Maw smelling of iron and old fires, and there he stood looking at the sky — at the sky that had no stars in it, none, not one, the same starless wound it had been for weeks now — and told them things he hadn't said aloud since the night they carried him out of the palace.

"I was twenty," he said. "Aldric was twenty-six. Our father had been dead eight months, and Aldric had just been crowned, and he was — he was *good*, understand? That's the part nobody believes. He wasn't weak, he wasn't cruel, he wasn't ambitious in the ugly way. He used to sneak me out of the palace to watch the river barges load at dawn. He remembered the names of kitchen boys. When Mother died he held me all night in the chapel because I was crying and he thought someone should be strong, and he decided it should be him." Tomas's breath fogged and tore away on the wind. "That's who put the crown on. That's who it found."

Wren Ashvale — the scholar had introduced herself on the walk up, in a rush of words that suggested she'd rehearsed — shifted her weight. "When did you first notice something wrong?"

"Three weeks after the coronation." He didn't have to think. Some dates you don't get to forget; they're carved into you like names on a cell wall. "He was holding audience. A delegation from the free cities, complaining about tariffs. And mid-sentence he stopped. Just — stopped. Stood there like a lamp with the flame blown out. For maybe ten heartbeats. Then he finished the sentence exactly where he'd left off, same tone, same gesture, perfect. Nobody else noticed. Courtiers see what they want to see." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "But I'd grown up with him. I knew how his mind moved, the little pauses, the way he'd tap two fingers when he was deciding something. Whatever stood up from that throne finished the sentence, but it didn't tap its fingers. It never tapped its fingers again."

"And you told the court," Ivo Reyes said. The defrocked priest's voice was gentle, and that gentleness was worse than accusation.

"I told the court." Eleven years fell away and he was standing in the great hall again, twenty years old, voice cracking, holding up the letters he'd stolen from his brother's private desk — pages written in Aldric's own hand that said things Aldric would never say, *the light is hungry, the light is patient, bring me more of the old blood* — and watching the faces of the most powerful people in the kingdom arrange themselves into pity. "I told them my brother was possessed. I told them the crown itself was the prison, that it had been forged for something else, that whatever was inside it had gotten out and was eating him from the inside. I stood in front of two hundred lords and ladies and I begged them to look at his eyes." He wrapped his arms around himself. The wind cut through his coat like it wasn't there. "Do you know what they did? They wept. Half of them wept, for poor Prince Tomas, grief-mad over his father, seeing demons in his brother's shadow. The physicians came with their leeches and their prayers. And Aldric—" his voice caught, "—Aldric stood at the edge of the hall and watched them take me away, and his face was so *sad*, and I couldn't tell — even then, even screaming — I couldn't tell if it was him in there, watching, or if it was the thing, enjoying it. Wearing his sadness like a coat."

Silence, except the wind and, far below, the groan of the mine machinery turning over for the night shift.

"They gave me a year in the Priory of Saint Vessal," he went on, because now that the dam had cracked he couldn't stop, the words coming up like floodwater, eleven years of them. "Kind place, actually. Quiet. Then I climbed out a window and spent three years trying to prove I wasn't mad — gathering evidence, finding witnesses, writing to anyone with a title who might listen. Do you know what happened to the witnesses?" He looked at Kestrel. "Dead. All of them. A chamberlain drowned in a shallow stream. My old tutor burned in a house fire with no cause anyone could find. A guard captain who'd seen the king stop mid-sentence — took his own life, they said, though he'd just been promoted and had a new granddaughter he doted on. Every person who might have confirmed what I saw, gone, one by one, neat as a ledger being balanced." He shook his head. "So I stopped. I ran. I figured a mad prince alive in exile was worth more than a dead prophet in a ditch. And I told myself that was wisdom." His mouth twisted. "Eleven years of wisdom. Eleven years of ale."

"You were nineteen," Kestrel said. "You were a child, and they broke you for telling the truth, and then they taught you to call your survival cowardice. That's not wisdom or cowardice. That's just what surviving does to people."

He wanted to believe her. That was the cruelest part — some starving thing in him leaned toward her words like a plant toward a window, and he had to physically stop himself from nodding along. Trust was a door he'd nailed shut a long time ago, and he wasn't going to let a stranger with kind eyes and a smuggler's swagger pry it open in one conversation on a slag heap.

"Why now?" he asked. "Why come find the mad prince after eleven years? What's happened?"

Kestrel exchanged a glance with Wren. Then she reached into her coat and drew out a folded piece of paper, creased and stained, handled soft as cloth with age.

"Three weeks ago the border wards at Thornfell shattered," she said. "Same night, every star in the sky went out. My guild hall was burned. My mentor — the man who raised me — died in my arms with this in his hand. He told me the wards were never built to keep magic out. They were built to feed something. And that something has been culling my family for generations, keeping us small, keeping us blind, until it needed one of us again." Her jaw tightened. "It needed me."

She unfolded the paper and held it out.

Tomas almost didn't take it. His hands hung at his sides, heavy as anchors, and every instinct honed over eleven years of exile screamed at him not to reach — because hope was the thing that hurt worst, hope was the hook they'd baited him with before, hope was how they'd caught the witnesses. If he took the paper, he'd start believing. If he started believing, he'd start caring. If he started caring—

He took the paper.

At first, in the thin moonless dark, he could make out only the shape of the writing — cramped, hurried, the script of a man writing fast because he wasn't sure he'd live to finish. But at the top of the page, drawn large and deliberate, was a sigil. A circle broken by a vertical line, with seven small marks radiating from the break like rays, and beneath the circle a single horizontal stroke.

The world went very quiet. Not the quiet of the wind dropping — the wind was still tearing at his coat, the mine still groaning, the town still murmuring below — but a quiet *inside* him, the absolute stillness of a man standing on ground that has just shifted.

"That's not possible," he whispered.

"It's Brann's mark," Kestrel said. "Guild sigil. Why? What do you—"

"My tutor." His voice came out strange, flattened, distant. "Master Corvane. The one who died in the fire. He drew this. On the last day I ever saw him, he pressed a paper into my hand in the corridor and said, *when you're ready, find the ones who carry this mark.* I never understood it. I thought it was a merchant's seal, some smuggling ring he trusted. I lost the paper crossing the Marrow. I looked for that symbol for three years — in books, in churches, on ships' flags — and then I stopped looking, because looking was making me madder than they already thought I was." He looked up at her, and his eyes were burning, and he didn't care. "Who is Brann? Who are the Sandovals? What *are* you?"

"We're jailers," Wren said quietly. "Or we were. We just didn't know it. Neither did you, I think. Your tutor did."

Tomas read the letter then, right there on the spoil heap, holding it close to his face, lips moving over the words. *The Ember wakes. The wards were its feeding-troughs, not its cage. The blood remembers what the crown forgot. Ironhollow. Before the coronation feast, before it's too late — find the others, find the forge, find the key.* And at the bottom, half-legible, a line that made his knees go soft: *Tell the king's brother he was right. Tell him we believed him.*

"He believed me," Tomas said. The words barely made it out. "Corvane believed me. He *knew*. He was trying to help me, and they burned him for it, and I never—" The paper trembled in his grip. "I spent eleven years telling myself I was the only one. That I'd seen true and everyone else was blind or bought, and I was alone with it, alone forever, and that was the price of being right. And the whole time there were *others*. People who carried the same knowledge, the same mark, dying one by one just like my witnesses, and I was hiding in a mining town getting drunk because I couldn't bear to be wrong again."

"Hey." Kestrel's voice was low and steady. "Look at me. You were nineteen. They had the whole kingdom, the church, the physicians, and a monster that kills witnesses. You survived. That's not nothing. That's the only reason any of this can happen now — because the king's brother is alive to stand up and say *I told you so*."

"I don't want to say I told you so." He laughed, and it broke halfway through and became something else, something wet and ugly that he couldn't swallow down. "I want my brother back. I want to hear him tap his fingers when he's thinking. I want—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Gods, I want to apologize. For running. For leaving him in there alone for eleven years while I drank myself stupid in towns like this one. He's been buried alive in his own body for a decade and I never once tried to dig."

"You're digging now," Ivo Reyes said.

Tomas lowered his hand. The priest stood a little apart, collar-less, wind-whipped, watching him with an expression Tomas recognized because he'd worn it himself for years: the look of a man cataloguing his own failures and finding the list too long to finish. Something passed between them — not comfort, nothing so easy, but recognition. Two wrecks acknowledging each other's hulls.

"If we do this," Tomas said, "if I come with you — understand what I am. I'm not a prince. I haven't been in a room with power in eleven years unless I was sweeping its floors. I'm a drunk with a famous name and a head full of useless history. I'll slow you down. I'll doubt you at every crossroads, because doubting is the only skill I've kept sharp. And if the Ember gets its hooks into me the way it got them into Corvane's killers, into Voss's soldiers, into—" he swallowed, "—into my brother, then you should put me down yourself rather than let it use me. Promise me that. All of you. Promise it now, while I mean it."

Nobody answered right away. Then Kestrel stepped forward and held out her hand — not to shake, palm up, open, the gesture of a dockworker sealing a bargain or a friend offering a rope.

"I promise you'll slow us down," she said. "And I promise we'll carry you anyway. That's how this works. Nobody on this road gets to be dead weight alone."

Tomas looked at her hand for a long moment. Then he took it, and her grip was strong and callused and warm, and something in his chest that had been clenched for eleven years loosened by a single degree — not much, not enough to call freedom, but enough to notice.

"All right," he said. "Ironhollow. The forge. Let's go wake up my brother."

They started down the spoil heap together, four shadows against the black flank of the Maw. And it was Wren, bringing up the rear, who stopped first — stopped so suddenly that Kestrel nearly walked into her — and turned, and looked back the way they had come, up the valley, toward the high pass where the road from the south came over the shoulder of the mountain.

"What is it?" Tomas asked.

"There's no wind up there," Wren said slowly. "There's supposed to be wind. There's always wind on that ridge." She pointed, and Tomas followed her finger, and his stomach turned to ice. Along the crest of the pass, where the last grey light was failing, the darkness was gathering — not the ordinary dark of evening, but a darkness with texture, with *motion*, pooling in the saddle of the mountains like water finding a drain. Like something pouring itself downhill toward the town. Toward them.

"It knows," Kestrel breathed. "It knows he's with us now."

Below them, across Cinderhatch, the mine whistle began to scream the shift-change — and every dog in town started howling at once, at the ridge, at the dark, at the thing coming down the mountain with the stars in its throat.
