## 9. The Toll Bridge at Graywater

The river came on them like a rumor made real.

Ivo Reyes heard it before he saw it — a low, grinding hush beneath the birdsong, the sound of gray water worrying at gray stone — and his heart did something it had not done in six years: it lifted. Old habit. Rivers meant towns, and towns meant bells, and bells had once been the whole architecture of his life. He had measured his days by them. Matins at dawn, vespers at dusk, the great bronze voice of the Cathedral of Saint Verene rolling over the rooftops of Vellmark like a tide that never failed.

The bells were silent now. The temple was shuttered. And Father Ivo Reyes walked toward the water with a smuggler's rope coiled over one shoulder and no god to pray to, feeling that old lift in his chest anyway, and hating himself for it.

"Graywater," Kestrel said, cresting the bank beside him. She stood with her weight on her back foot, the way she always did when she was reading a landscape, as if the ground might lie to her and she wanted to catch it at it. "Toll bridge. One road across for thirty miles either way, unless you fancy swimming in current that'll fold you in half." She squinted downstream. "There's a ferryman's landing under the bluff. Or there was, ten years ago."

"And if there wasn't?" Wren Ashvale asked. The scholar had come up last, breathing hard, arms full of Brann's letter and three days of notes tied in a bundle that they guarded like an infant.

"Then we swim," Kestrel said, "and we die."

Tomas laughed — a short, cracked sound, not quite mirth. The prince looked better than he had in the mining town, scrubbed and fed and walking upright, but there was still something loose about him, something unmoored, a man who had taken apart the frame of his own life and not yet found the joints to reassemble it. "Cheerful company you keep, priest."

"They're honest," Ivo said. "Honesty is cheerful, compared to the alternative."

He said it lightly. He did not feel light. Because he had seen what Wren had seen two nights past, crouched over the fire with a stick, drawing lines in the ash: the dark behind them was not weather. It kept pace. It paused when they paused. It hung at the edge of the trees like a congregation waiting for the sermon to end, patient as only hunger can be patient, and it was drawn to Kestrel Sandoval the way iron filings are drawn to a lodestone.

The Ember's attention. That was what Wren called it. Ivo thought of it differently. He thought: *the eye of God, if God had gone ravenous.*

He had spent his life believing in a watching presence. He had lost that belief in a single afternoon, kneeling among the relics of a shuttered temple, reading the decree that stripped his orders from him. And now some vast and hollow thing watched them through the dark, and Ivo found the irony so complete it circled back around to comedy. He had wanted to be seen by something greater than himself all his life. He should have been more specific.

* * *

They came down to the bridge at midmorning, and Ivo understood immediately why the crossing had been named for its tolls.

Graywater ran wide and fast and the color of gunmetal, churning between banks of wet black stone. The bridge itself was old imperial work — five arches of fitted granite, moss-hung, sturdy as sin — and it funneled every cart, every herd, every pilgrim and peddler into a single lane where a tollhouse squatted at the far end like a spider in a web. Even now, in a kingdom going quietly mad, there was traffic: a drover with six oxen, a tinker's cart, two women leading a mule loaded with salt.

And at the tollhouse, four soldiers in the black-and-silver of the usurper's regulars, checking papers. Behind them, picketed horses. Behind *them*, on the ridge above the far bank, the glint of a helmed line — a full garrison detachment, maybe sixty men, camped exactly where a garrison detachment would camp if it had been told to watch this bridge.

Kestrel lay flat against a gorse brake on the near side and looked for a long time without speaking. When she finally did, her voice was flat and quiet. "That's not a checkpoint. That's a net. They're not stopping people. They're *sorting* them." She pulled back. "Someone told them who to look for."

"The description will be vague," Wren whispered. "'A woman, a priest, a scholar, a man of noble bearing.' We could split up—"

"No." Kestrel's jaw worked. "Splitting up is how you lose people. We cross together or not at all." She turned, and Ivo watched her do the arithmetic she was always doing — routes, weights, risks, bodies — and watched the arithmetic fail. There was no route. The flooded caves under the bluff were a maybe; the ferryman's landing was a decade dead; the river was a coffin. Sixty men on the ridge. Four at the tollhouse. A bridge with one way on and one way off.

"We go back," Tomas said. "Circle north. Lose a week—"

"A week puts us in the open passes with the dark still on our heels and Voss's outriders ahead of us." Kestrel closed her eyes briefly. "We don't have a week. Whatever's following us, it isn't following us for our health."

Ivo looked at the bridge. At the tollhouse. At the four soldiers lounging around their little fire, bored, cold, stamping their feet. Ordinary men. Men with mothers, probably. Men who would check his papers and wave him through, because a priest traveling alone was nothing, nobody, a beggar in a faded cassock.

An idea came to him the way ideas had once come to him in the pulpit — whole, sudden, terrifying, certain. He recognized it and almost flinched. He had not felt certainty since the day he lost everything. He had told himself certainty was a vice, the root of every fanaticism, the thing that had let him preach with conviction about a god who apparently wasn't listening. But here it was again, arriving uninvited, and it did not feel like faith in anything above.

It felt like faith in the four people crouched in the gorse beside him.

"I'll cross alone," he said.

They all turned.

"I'm the least of us," Ivo went on, before the objections could form ranks. "Listen. A lone priest is invisible. A group of four — one of them a woman matching the smuggler's description, one of them wearing a face half the border provinces remember from court — that's a spectacle. But me? I'm a vagrant in holy orders that were struck off years ago. Half these soldiers were raised by mothers who'd still kiss a cassock's hem. I walk across, I bless somebody's oxen, I ask too many questions about the garrison on the ridge — loudly, stupidly, the way a defrocked drunk would — and every eye on that bridge comes to me." He met Kestrel's stare. "You take the bluff path down to the old ferry landing while they're counting my teeth. If the landing's gone, you take the caves. Wren says the limestone under the bluff runs through to the far side. You come out beyond the garrison's picket line and you're gone."

"That's insane," Kestrel said.

"It's arithmetic," Ivo said. "Yours. I learned it from watching you."

"You'd be captured."

"I might be captured."

"You'd be *tortured*—"

"Ivo." It was Wren who spoke, and the scholar's voice was strange — thin, careful. "What happened at the temple in Vellmark. When the inquisitors nearly had us. You didn't run. You stepped *between*." Wren's eyes searched his face. "You keep doing this. Why?"

Ivo looked out at the river. The truth was simple and he had never once said it aloud, not even to himself, not even in the dark of the shuttered temple where he hid among other men's discarded gods.

"Because I spent eleven years telling people that faith meant trusting in something they couldn't see," he said. "And then I stopped believing, and I discovered that I had built my entire self out of that trust, and when it went, there was nothing left of me but the shape it left behind. A hole shaped like purpose." His hand found the small wooden saint at his throat — Saint Verene, patron of lost causes, which he had kept out of spite and never managed to throw away. "But you four—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "I can't believe in heaven anymore. I've tried. It won't take. But I can believe in getting you across a river. That's a small enough miracle. I might manage it."

Nobody spoke. The river filled the silence, grinding its gray stones.

Then Kestrel reached out and gripped his forearm, hard, smuggler-fashion, wrist to wrist. "You come out the other side of this," she said. "That's not a request. That's a toll. You want to pay it, fine. But the toll works both ways, priest. You get yourself captured, you get yourself *back*. We'll be at Ironhollow. Don't make me come find you."

Ivo laughed — really laughed, for the first time in six years — and it hurt his face, the muscles had gone so unused. "Wouldn't dream of it."

* * *

He crossed the bridge the way he had once processed down the nave: slowly, visibly, with the particular dignity of a man who owns nothing and therefore cannot be robbed.

The morning fog off the river helped. It came off Graywater in slow gray ribbons, softening the far bank, blurring the garrison on the ridge into a smudge of steel. Ivo walked into it with his hood back and his hands open and let himself be seen.

"Hold! In the name of the Crown Regent!" One of the tollhouse soldiers — young, freckled, nervous — stepped onto the bridge with a spear held at an angle that suggested he hoped never to use it. "State your business."

"Business," Ivo said, and put a wine-bright slur into his voice, thickening his accent toward the southern parishes where he'd done his novitiate. "My business, son, is that I'm walking to Marrowdale to beg forgiveness of a bishop who doesn't want it, and the river's between me and my humiliation, same as it's been between everyone and everything since the world began." He swayed slightly. "Might I trouble the Crown Regent's men for a cup of hot water? I've got the leaf. Just not the heat."

The soldier blinked. Behind him, another — older, sergeant's stripes — came out of the tollhouse wiping grease on his trousers, and looked Ivo up and down with the weary expertise of a man who had processed ten thousand travelers and been surprised by none of them.

"Priest, are you?"

"Was." Ivo produced his papers — genuine, at least; the defrocking decree, which he had carried for six years like a wound and which now served him better than any forgery. The sergeant read it, and something almost like pity crossed his face. Good. Pity loosened tongues.

"Defrocked. What'd you do?"

"What every priest does eventually, sergeant. I noticed something true, and I said it out loud." He let his gaze drift, deliberately, theatrically, up toward the ridge. "Speaking of saying things out loud — that's a lot of soldiers for a toll bridge, eh? Expecting an invasion? Or expecting *someone*?"

The sergeant's face closed like a door. "Move along."

"Oh, I'll move along, I'll move along." Ivo shuffled forward, then stopped, struck by a thought, and turned back with the artless loudness of the half-drunk. "Only — you hear anything, up at the garrison? About the stars? I ask because I've been walking since Thornfell and I haven't seen a single one, and a man starts to wonder whether the sky's been *collected*. Whether someone's holding it. And whether whoever holds it might send men to a bridge like this one, hunting for—" he waved a vague hand, "—well. Hunting."

Every soldier at the tollhouse was looking at him now. On the ridge, faintly, he could see movement — figures standing up along the helmed line, drawn by the commotion below. Somewhere behind him, down the bluff, he knew, four shadows were moving along a goat track toward a dead ferryman's landing, quick and silent as water finding its level.

Keep them, he thought. Keep your eyes on me. I am the most interesting thing on this bridge. I have been training for this my entire life. Every sermon I ever preached was rehearsal for standing in front of armed men and making them look nowhere else.

"Funny kind of talk," the sergeant said slowly, "for a man wanting to cross a bridge."

"I'm a funny kind of man." Ivo smiled, and it was the smile he'd used on grieving widows and skeptical merchants and once, memorably, a bishop. "Give me my cup of hot water, sergeant, and I'll be out of your hair."

It was working. It was actually working — and then the fog shifted.

It rolled back off the river in a long breath, and Ivo saw, at the near end of the bridge, perhaps forty paces behind him, a rider who had not been there a moment ago. Black armor, unadorned. A pale scarred face beneath the helm's rim, and eyes that were wrong — not empty, worse than empty, *occupied*, like windows with something standing just behind the glass.

Inquisitor Maren Voss. He knew her from the descriptions Wren had dug out of old trial records. And he saw, in the instant before she spoke, that she had not been fooled at all. She had simply been waiting to see who would walk out of the fog and offer themselves.

"Well," Voss said. Her voice was quiet and carried anyway, the way a blade carries light. "Father Reyes. The temple in Vellmark reported a break-in. Stolen texts. A missing priest." She dismounted, unhurried. "You've been a difficult man to find."

Ivo set down his cup of hot water very carefully, as though it were a chalice.

"Sergeant," he said pleasantly, "I don't suppose those men on the ridge heard anything I said?"

"Every word," said a new voice from the tollhouse door, and a lean officer stepped out with a signal mirror already flashing up toward the ridge. "Every word, Father."

* * *

They took him at the tollhouse, and they took him gently, which frightened him more than roughness would have. Roughness was for criminals. Gentleness was for assets.

Voss questioned him in the tollhouse itself, dismissing the soldiers, and for an hour she circled him with a patience that was almost tender, asking about the temple, about the text, about the woman called Kestrel Sandoval. And Ivo — who had prepared lies the way he'd once prepared sermons — found himself doing something stranger instead. He told the truth in shapes that misled. Yes, he had met the Sandoval woman. No, he didn't know where she was bound. Yes, she had saved his life. When Voss asked why a defrocked priest would follow a smuggler toward Ironhollow, Ivo looked at her scarred face — at the thin white lines running up from her collar, the marks he'd read about in Wren's records, the scars the crown's own inquisitors bore — and answered honestly:

"Because she believes in something, Inquisitor. It's contagious. You should be careful."

Something moved behind Voss's occupied eyes. For a moment — one moment — her expression fractured, and Ivo saw underneath: horror, exhaustion, a woman drowning and aware of the water. Her hand rose halfway to her own throat, to the scars, and stopped.

"Where," she said, and her voice was different, smaller, "*is* she?"

And Ivo understood, with a clarity that felt like grace and probably wasn't, that whatever wore this woman was not wearing all of her. That somewhere inside the hollow there was still a person, and the person was screaming.

"I don't know," he said softly. "But I know she'll come back for me. That's the kind of person she is. Perhaps that's the kind of person you were."

Voss stood very still. Then the occupation slid back over her face like a shutter, and she said, in the flat voice of the crown: "Bind him. He rides with the column. The Ember wants the priest's companions found, and grief makes men careless." She turned to the door, then paused. "Pray, if you like, Father. It bothers no one."

"I will," Ivo said. "Just not to whom you think."

They led him out into the gray morning, wrists bound, up the road toward the garrison on the ridge. Behind him, the river ground on over its stones, and the fog was closing again over the bluff, over the dead ferry landing, over whatever darkness might or might not have swallowed four fleeing shadows.

Ivo did not look back. Looking back was for men with gods to appeal to. He had something better now: a promise with a toll attached, and four people foolish enough to honor it.

*Ironhollow,* he thought, as the soldiers marched him up into the hills. *Wait for me. All of you. I'm coming — slowly, in chains, and late.*

*But coming.*
