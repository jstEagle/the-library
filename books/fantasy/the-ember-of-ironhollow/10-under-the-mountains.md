## 10. Under the Mountains

The dark in the flooded caves was not the same dark as the dark above.

Kestrel had known darkness her whole life — smuggler's dark, the friendly kind that hid you, the kind you learned to read with your fingertips and the soles of your feet. This was different. This was a dark with weight to it, a wet black pressing against her eyes like a thumb against a bruise, and somewhere behind it, always, the sound of water moving through stone: patient, enormous, indifferent as a sleeping god.

They had been walking for what felt like two days and might have been four. Time went strange down here. The phosphorescent moss Wren had scraped from the walls of the first gallery gave them light enough to see each other's faces and nothing more, and it dimmed when they stopped moving, as if even the light were tired.

"Left fork," Kestrel said, though she wasn't sure anymore which way was left. "The water flows toward us on the right side, which means we're still going deeper along the vein. That's good."

"That's *good*," Tomas repeated flatly, from the back of their little procession. He had a miner's lantern with no oil in it and carried it anyway, swinging it at nothing, a habit from a life he didn't talk about. "Deeper is good."

"Deeper is under the mountains instead of inside them," Kestrel said. "Inside them is where Voss's soldiers are."

Ivo was not with them. Every time she thought of it — and she thought of it constantly, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth — the loss opened again. Father Ivo Reyes, who had walked into the toll bridge square with his hands raised and his voice carrying that ridiculous, magnificent calm, saying *gentlemen, I am a priest without a parish, surely you have use for one*, while Kestrel dragged Wren down into the culvert and Tomas covered their backs with a stolen crossbow he barely knew how to hold. She had heard the shouting start as the water closed over her head. She had not let herself imagine what came after.

So they had gone under. And now they were lost.

Wren stumbled behind her, arms wrapped around the satchel of notes and instruments she refused to put down even to sleep. Her spectacles had cracked on the first day below, one lens webbed white, and she kept pushing them up her nose as if the gesture could fix them. "For the record," she said, "the water flow argument assumes the vein hasn't been rerouted by three centuries of mine collapse, which—"

"It hasn't."

"You can't know that."

"I can feel it." Kestrel said it before she could stop herself, and then wished she hadn't, because Wren went quiet in that particular scholar's quiet that meant *interesting, tell me more*, and Tomas lifted his empty lantern like a man scenting rain.

They walked another hour before anyone spoke again.

* * *

They made camp in a gallery where the ceiling rose high enough that the moss-light couldn't reach it, and the darkness overhead felt less like a lid and more like open sky, if sky were something that could drown you. Kestrel got a fire going from damp wood and old habit — smugglers learned to burn things that shouldn't burn — and the small orange circle of it made the rest of the world seem farther away than it was.

Tomas ate in silence, tearing dried meat with his teeth. Wren spread her cracked notes across a flat stone, pretending to study them, which was how Wren pretended not to be afraid. And Kestrel sat with her back to the warm rock and listened.

Not to the water. To the other thing.

It had started the night the stars went out. At first she'd taken it for shock, for grief, for the ringing left in the ears after the wards shattered — a sound like a bell the size of the horizon being struck once and never quite finishing its note. But bells don't speak. Bells don't know your name.

*Kestrel.* It said her name the way her mother used to, low and warm, across a kitchen table in a house that had burned eleven years ago with everyone in it but her. *Kestrel, you're so tired.*

She realized Tomas was watching her.

"You do that," he said. Not unkindly. He was drunk less often now, which meant he noticed things more, which was not entirely an improvement. "When it gets quiet. You go somewhere. Your eyes do this thing like you're listening through a wall."

"I'm listening for pursuit," she said.

"No," Tomas said. "Pursuit has a shape. You flinch differently when it's pursuit. I spent six years around people listening for my brother's inquisitors. I know the flinch." He set down the meat. "What is it?"

Wren looked up over her ruined spectacles.

Kestrel stared into the fire. She had built her whole life on the principle that you never told anyone anything they could use to hurt you, and here were two people she'd known for weeks, and the fire was warm, and the dark was full of water, and she was so tired of carrying it alone.

"The night the stars went out," she said, "I started hearing a voice."

The words fell into the firelight and lay there. Neither of them moved.

"It knows my name. It sounds—" She laughed, short and awful. "You'll think I'm mad. Everyone thinks Sandovals are mad; it's practically our coat of arms. It sounds like my mother. Not exactly. Like someone doing an impression of my mother from a description written by someone who loved her very much and only met her twice. Warm. Patient. It says things like—" Her throat worked. "It says, *you're so tired, Kestrel. You've been running routes since you were nine years old because there was no one else. You slept in doorways the winter after the fire. You buried your guild brothers with your own hands and then went back to work the next morning because the work doesn't wait. I can give you the house back. I can give you the kitchen. I can fill it with the smell of bread and your mother's voice calling you in from the rain, and no one will ever leave it again.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere far off in the black, water dripped, counting seconds nobody wanted counted.

"It's been offering me my family," Kestrel said quietly. "Every night since the stars went out. Every single night. And every night it gets a little better at it. A little more specific. Last week it described the crack in the third stair, the one I used to jump over. There's no way it could know about the stair. Unless it can reach into whatever's left of me and pull the details out like splinters." She finally looked up. "That's what I'm listening for. That's the flinch you saw. I'm checking whether it's still talking."

"And is it?" Wren asked. Her voice was careful, the voice she used on unstable ward-matrixes.

"It's always talking. It just waits until I'm weak." Kestrel wrapped her arms around her knees. "There. Now you both know the smuggler's cracked. You can decide what to do with that information."

Tomas moved first. He crossed the small circle of firelight and sat down beside her, close enough that his shoulder touched hers, and said nothing at all, which was somehow the exact right thing. He had spent years being called mad by a court that didn't want to hear him. He knew what it cost to say the unsayable and have it met with silence.

It was Wren who spoke, and when she did, she was already pulling a charcoal stub and a scrap of hide toward her, because Wren Ashvale processed horror the way other people processed weather — by diagramming it.

"Say the offers again," she said. "Exactly. Word for word, as much as you remember."

"Why?"

"Because of what you just told me. It gets *more specific*. More accurate. Do you understand what that means?" Wren's eyes were bright behind the cracked glass. "A corrupting influence doesn't need accuracy. Fear doesn't need accuracy. If the Ember wanted to hollow you the way it hollowed Aldric, it could simply press — pain, despair, erosion, the slow grinding-down. That's what corruption does. It doesn't *craft*. It doesn't learn the crack in your mother's third stair." She began to sketch as she talked, quick jagged lines. "Accuracy means desire. It's not showing you a lie, Kestrel. It's showing you a *catalogue*. It finds the truest want in you and builds the offer out of it, because the offer has to be real enough to take."

"So it lies beautifully," Tomas said.

"No. That's the terrible part. I don't think it lies at all." Wren looked up, and her face in the firelight was pale and certain and frightened. "Think about what we know. The wards weren't built to keep enemies out — I said that at my trial and they stripped my title for it, but I was right, and Brann's letter proves it. The wards were a cage. The Ember has been sealed for three hundred years, and everything it touches, it *fills*. Aldric isn't destroyed; Voss's reports — the ones we intercepted — describe him as serene. Content. The hollowed ones don't scream. They smile." She turned the hide over, drew a circle, filled it in. "Here's my theory, and I hate it. The Ember doesn't corrupt. Corruption implies it changes what you are. I think it grants wishes. Perfectly. Completely. Whatever you most deeply want, it gives you, and keeps giving it, and keeps giving it, until the wanting is all that's left — until there's no Kestrel remaining who wanted a kitchen, just a kitchen wearing Kestrel's face, endlessly warm, endlessly fed, and *empty*. It doesn't eat your soul by force. It trades you out of it, wish by wish, fairly, honestly, and you thank it the whole way down."

The silence after that had texture. Kestrel felt it settle on her skin like the cave-damp.

"The hollowed ones smile," she repeated. "Gods. I've seen them. On the levy road. I thought it was the horror of it — the wrongness. But it's not horror at all, is it. They got what they wanted."

"Or they're so far gone they can't remember wanting anything else," Tomas said roughly. "My brother used to argue with me. Shout. Slam doors. Aldric had a temper like a summer storm — loud and over fast. Now he stands in receiving lines like a coat on a hook." His jaw tightened. "If your theory's right, Wren, then somewhere inside he's standing in a perfect kitchen too. Or a perfect throne room. Whatever Aldric wanted."

"What did he want?" Kestrel asked.

"To be a good king," Tomas said simply. "That was the whole of him. It's why he wouldn't listen when I tried to warn him about the crown — because the warning required admitting the crown might harm the kingdom, and he couldn't hold both thoughts. And the Ember was listening, and it said, *I can make you a good king. Forever. Every harvest full, every border quiet, every petition answered.*" He stared into the fire. "And he said yes. Because it was true. Everything it promises is true. That's the trap."

Kestrel thought of the voice in the dark, warm as banked coals, offering her a house that had burned eleven years ago. Offering her bread and rain and her mother's voice. And the worst part — the part she'd never said aloud, the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal — was that some nights, in the last hour before waking, she *wanted to say yes*. Some nights the offer was so beautiful that the Kestrel sitting in a wet cave with two half-strangers felt like the poor copy, the rough draft, and the woman in the warm kitchen felt like the real one waiting to be restored.

"If it's granting wishes," she said slowly, "then it needs the wisher intact long enough to wish. Which means it wants me whole. Which means—" She stopped.

"Which means Voss's orders to take you alive aren't cruelty," Wren finished softly. "They're *care*. You're not prey, Kestrel. You're a delivery."

Tomas swore, low and heartfelt, in the mining-town dialect he usually kept buried.

But Kestrel was already past that, following the thought down into its cold cellar, because once you gave a smuggler a map she couldn't stop reading it. "Then it's afraid," she said. "Of me. Of my blood. Wren — Brann's letter said the binding runs in the Sandoval line, that we were the ones who—" She faltered on the word, still not ready to carry its full weight. "The ones who put it in the crown. If it's been culling us for generations, if it's been whispering to me since the night it broke loose, then it's not hunting me the way a wolf hunts. Wolves don't talk to sheep. It's negotiating. It's trying to buy the one thing that can unmake it, and it's paying in kitchens and mothers' voices because it has nothing else to pay with except truth."

"Or," Wren said, "it's grooming you. Softening you until you walk into the forge-heart voluntarily and ask it to take you."

"Yes," Kestrel agreed. "That too. Both things can be true. It can be afraid of me and hungry for me at the same time. Most dangerous things are."

She got up and fed the fire, and the act of moving helped, the way motion always helped. Above them — miles of stone above them — the starless sky pressed down on the whole world, and somewhere in it the Ember wore a king like a glove, and somewhere behind her an inquisitor with half a face was dragging a priest toward Ironhollow, and none of it could be reached from here, tonight, in this wet throat of the mountain. All she could do was the next true thing.

"The next true thing," she said aloud, "is sleep in shifts. Then we follow the water vein south until it forks at a fallen pillar — if my grandmother's maps were honest — and from there the air should start moving, and moving air means a way out."

"And if your grandmother's maps weren't honest?" Tomas asked.

"Then we're lost in the dark with a creature that grants wishes," Kestrel said. "Same as every other day lately."

It got the ghost of a laugh out of both of them, which had been the point. She took first watch because she always took first watch, and the others rolled into their cloaks, and within minutes Wren was breathing in the arrhythmic pattern of a scholar asleep mid-thought and Tomas was snoring with the practiced efficiency of a man who'd slept in ditches for six years.

Kestrel sat with her back to the rock and watched the fire and waited.

It came the way it always came, sliding in under the sound of the water, gentle as fog off a river.

*Kestrel. You told them. That was brave. I felt it — the weight coming off you. See how good that feels? See how much lighter you are when you stop carrying things alone?*

Her hand found her knife and stayed there, resting on the hilt, not drawing. "Go away," she breathed.

*I don't want to hurt you. I have never wanted to hurt you. Hurt is such a crude instrument. I want to give you the kitchen, Kestrel. The bread. The rain on the roof. Your mother humming while she works. I can make it so real you will never doubt it, and you will never have to be tired again, never have to be the strong one, never lose anyone else, because no one in that kitchen ever leaves—*

"I said go away."

*One day,* the voice said, tender and vast and terribly patient, *you will say yes. And when you do, I will keep my promise to the letter, and you will be so happy, and it will be the last thing you ever feel, and you will thank me. Just as he did. Just as she did.*

"Just as who did?" Kestrel whispered. "Who else? Who else did you give a kitchen to?"

The voice paused. And in that pause, for the first time since the stars had gone out, Kestrel heard something underneath the warmth — thin and high and buried deep, like a note held under water.

Someone screaming. Far away. Getting closer.

The fire guttered. Down the southern passage, where the water-vein ran toward the fork and the fallen pillar and the way out, the darkness changed — thickened, gathered, leaned toward them like a head turning — and the moss on the walls dimmed all at once, every frond, as if the mountain itself had flinched.

Wren sat bolt upright. "Kestrel," she whispered. "The moss. Why did the moss—"

"Pack everything," Kestrel said, already on her feet, knife out, heart hammering against the cold knowledge that the voice had never once screamed before — and that whatever was coming down the passage toward them, the Ember was afraid of it too.
