## 11. The Free Cities Close Their Gates

Tomas Aldricson had learned to read armies the way other men read weather. It was a skill his father had drilled into him on the parade grounds of Vellmark's citadel — *watch the baggage train, boy; the banners lie, but the wagons tell you how long they mean to stay* — and he had thought the skill dead in him, pickled in wine and exile like everything else. But standing at the ridge above the Graywater road with Wren's spyglass pressed to his eye, he felt it come back to his hands like an old scar aching before rain.

Below, where the valley narrowed toward the toll bridge, the usurper's column moved without hurry. That was the thing that made his stomach turn. Armies that marched fast were afraid of something. Armies that marched slow believed nothing could stop them.

"Count the supply wagons," Wren said beside him. The scholar's voice was flat with concentration, her ink-stained fingers steady on the ridge stone. "I want numbers."

"Forty," Tomas said. "Maybe forty-five. Grain, tent-leather, and—" He adjusted the glass. "Siege frames. Disassembled, but I know the shape. They're not chasing us anymore."

Kestrel crouched lower against the rock, her dark hair tied back with a strip of leather, her face turned toward the distant glitter of spearheads. "Then what are they doing?"

"They're taking the country." Tomas handed the spyglass back to Wren. "You don't need siege engines for four fugitives. You need them for walls."

The word settled over the three of them like a cold draft. Walls. The free cities had stood open for two hundred years — that was what *free* meant, in the old charters: no gate barred to any soul born under the open sky. Tomas had grown up reciting the charter at his father's knee, had watched Aldric swear it renewed each midwinter with a crown of oak leaves instead of iron. His brother had loved that ceremony. Tomas was almost sure of it. Almost sure there had been a man under all that ceremony who loved something.

Now the cities would close their gates, and the countryside — the farmsteads, the river villages, the mining towns like the one where they'd found him drunk and useless — would be left outside them like coins spilled from a torn purse.

"We should move," Kestrel said. "Tonight, while their vanguard is still south of the bridge. If we cut through the high passes we can reach Ironhollow's foothills in nine days."

"Eight," Wren said, "if we don't sleep."

"Seven if we don't eat," Kestrel said, and for a moment the ghost of her smuggler's grin surfaced — that quick, crooked thing that made her look younger than she was and older than she'd ever admit. Then it faded. She was looking down the valley again, past the army, past the bridge, to where a thread of smoke rose from the far side of the river. Not the clean gray smoke of a chimney. The dirty brown of a burning barn.

Tomas saw it too. So did Wren. None of them said anything for a long moment, because saying it aloud would make it a decision, and decisions had costs, and they had all already paid so much.

It was Kestrel who finally spoke, and her voice had gone quiet in the way Tomas had learned meant she was angriest. "That's Harrowmere."

"You know it?" Wren asked.

"I ran goods through it for six years. Salt up, wool down. There's a ferryman named Doss who cheats every traveler but me because I once carried his daughter out of a fever-country when she was small." Kestrel's jaw worked. "There are maybe two hundred people there. And that army is going to walk through them on its way to wherever it's going, and take whatever it wants, and burn whatever it can't carry, because nobody is left to stop them. Because the cities are closing their gates."

"The cities have to close their gates," Wren said, and there was no cruelty in it, only the terrible precision of a mind that followed logic wherever it led. "If Vellmark stays open, it falls, and then there's nowhere left to rally. Sealing the walls is the correct strategic—"

"I know it's correct." Kestrel's voice cracked like ice on a pond. "I've spent my whole life being told what's correct by people whose correctness never cost them anything. The wards were correct. The culling of my family was correct. Somebody always has the arithmetic ready when it's other people's blood going into the sum." She stood, brushing grit from her knees. "I'm not asking anyone to follow me. I'm telling you I'm going down there."

Tomas looked at her — this small, sharp-edged woman carrying a burden none of them fully understood yet, hunted by half the kingdom, marked by something ancient and hungry — and he felt something shift in his chest that he hadn't felt in years. Not pity. Pity he'd drowned in wine long ago. It was recognition. He knew what it was to be the one person who'd seen the truth and been called mad for it. He knew what it was to watch doors close while you stood outside them.

He also knew, with the clarity that only came to him now, sober, that this was the moment the chapter of his life turned or didn't.

"The village is a day behind us if we push hard," he said. "Two days ahead of the main column if they don't detach outriders." He met Kestrel's eyes. "They'll detach outriders."

"So we get there first, get them moving, and get gone." Kestrel nodded slowly. "A day. Maybe two. We lose our lead on Ironhollow."

Wren made a sound low in her throat — frustration, or fear, or both. "Every hour we spend playing shepherds is an hour Voss gains. An hour the Ember feeds. You're carrying the thing's attention in your blood, Kestrel, and you want to stand still in an open field and wave?"

"Yes," Kestrel said simply.

And that, Tomas thought, was the whole of her. No speech, no justification. Just *yes*, the way a door stands open.

Wren looked from Kestrel to him, waiting — and Tomas realized with a start that she was waiting for *him*. For the prince. For the vote that mattered, or at least the vote she wanted to matter. He hadn't been needed by anyone in eleven years. The sensation was like sunlight on frost-burned skin: painful, and warm underneath the pain.

"My brother burned villages," Tomas heard himself say. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Not with his own hands — he never touched anything with his own hands anymore — but they burned all the same, in the border wars, and I signed the requisitions because signing was easier than arguing. I told myself the arithmetic was correct." He picked up his pack. "I'm done with correct. Harrowmere first."

* * *

They came down out of the hills in the last grey light, and Harrowmere was worse than the smoke had promised.

The village sat in a bend of the river like a cupped hand, forty-odd houses with thatched roofs gone silver with age, a mill, a shrine with a bell that hadn't rung since the stars went out — because what was the point of ringing a bell at a sky with nothing in it? Half the fields lay harvested; the other half stood in stubble rows going soft with rot, because there was no one left with the heart to finish. The burning barn was the ferryman's, and Doss himself stood in the yard of it watching the embers with the blank face of a man who had stopped counting losses.

When Kestrel walked into the village square, an old woman recognized her first — shouted her name, *Sandoval girl!*, as if the name were still a promise of safety — and then the square filled, and Tomas understood the scale of what they'd walked into.

Not two hundred souls ready to march. Two hundred souls with nowhere to march *to*. Old men who couldn't walk ten miles. A woman nine months pregnant sitting on a cart tail with her hands folded over her belly like a closed book. Children — gods, so many children, barefoot in the autumn mud, staring up at the strangers with the flat patience of those who had learned that adults' plans rarely included them.

"There's a column coming," Kestrel told them, plain and unsoftened, because Tomas was learning she did not know how to lie except when it mattered most. "Soldiers. Three days, maybe less. They'll take your stores and your strong backs and they won't ask twice. You have to leave tonight."

"Leave to where?" Doss asked. His voice was ash. "Vellmark's shut its gates. We had word two days past. Corvane's turning folk away at the wall — says they can't feed them. Where does a man go when every door is barred?"

The silence that followed was the truest answer, and everyone in the square knew it.

Tomas stepped forward. He had rehearsed nothing; he had no rank to invoke, no crown to show, and even if he had, the name Aldricson would buy him more stones than bread. What he had was eleven years of watching crowds from the outside, learning what they feared and what they'd follow.

"You go east," he said. "Into the hills. There are old shepherd roads above the tree line — my family's maps mark them, roads the crown forgot because no tax collector could climb them. They're hard, and they're cold, and they're ugly, and they're alive. The army won't take carts up goat tracks. In the high country there are caves that go back further than any kingdom, and water that runs clean, and when this war finds its ending — and it will find its ending — you'll still be there to see it."

"And who are you," Doss said, not cruelly, just tiredly, "to promise endings?"

Tomas opened his mouth and found nothing in it. Who was he? A drunk. A madman. The brother of the thing wearing the king. He had spent eleven years perfecting the art of having no answer to that question.

"He's the man who's going to carry your grandmother's cart over the ford himself," Kestrel said, "if it comes to that. We all are. Four sets of hands tonight, and more tomorrow if we find them. That's who he is. That's who we are."

She said it like it was already true. Like *we* was a word with weight and history, though they'd known each other mere weeks. And the strange thing — the thing Tomas would turn over in his mind for years after — was that hearing her say it made it begin to be true. Words were small magic. He'd forgotten that, in the citadel, where words were only ever large and hollow.

The village began to move.

* * *

They moved all night, and the night taught Tomas things about himself he had not wanted to learn.

He learned that he was stronger than he'd been in the mountain town — the wine had been gone from him for weeks now, and his body remembered labor the way a riverbed remembers water. He learned that Kestrel organized a panicked exodus the way other people breathed, her voice never rising, her hands always finding the next falling thing before it fell. He learned that Wren Ashvale, for all her cold arithmetic, worked until her fingers bled lashing axles, and that she flinched whenever a child cried, and hid the flinching badly.

And he learned, somewhere in the black hours between the ford and the tree line, with a screaming infant wrapped against his chest and the mother too exhausted to carry her, that he had missed this. Not the nobility of it — he'd had nobility stuffed into him like goose liver until he gagged on it. This. Usefulness. The plain animal goodness of a body doing what another body needs. He had spent his whole princely life being *for* something abstract — the realm, the line, the crown — and had never once been simply *for* a frightened woman and her child on a dark road.

The baby's name was Lark. She gripped his finger with a grip like a small iron clamp and screamed at the starless sky as though filing a formal complaint, and Tomas found himself laughing — actually laughing — for the first time since the night he'd tried to warn the court and been dragged from the hall by men who used to bow to him.

"She's got a good set of lungs," he told her mother.

"She's got a good set of everything," the woman panted. "She'll need them."

Above them, the sky stayed empty. Tomas had stopped looking up weeks ago; it was Kestrel who couldn't stop, her eyes flicking upward at every rest halt like a tongue returning to a broken tooth. Tonight, though, as they climbed into the shepherd roads and the village strung itself out along the track like beads on a fraying cord, he caught her staring east, toward the mountains, toward Ironhollow, and her expression was one he recognized from mirrors in worse years.

Guilt. The specific guilt of a person doing right at the wrong speed.

"We'll make it," he said, falling into step beside her.

"You don't know that."

"No. But I know we wouldn't have made it anyway, not really, not if we'd left these people to burn. We'd have reached Ironhollow faster and arrived as smaller people. Whatever's waiting for us down there—" he gestured at the dark earth beneath them, at the forge-city sleeping under the stone, "—it eats wishes, Wren says. It grants them until nothing's left. So maybe the only defense is wanting something bigger than yourself." He shrugged, awkward, the old courtly eloquence failing him exactly when he wanted it most. "I don't know. I'm making it up as I go. I've been making it up since the night the stars went out."

Kestrel was quiet for a long moment. Then: "My mentor used to say the roads remember who walks them honestly." Her mouth twisted. "I thought he meant smuggling routes. I'm starting to think he meant something else."

Ahead of them, the column of villagers snaked up the switchbacks, torches bobbing, a fragile constellation of their own making since the real ones had abandoned them. Somewhere in it, old Doss was singing — a low, rough harvest song, tuneless and stubborn, and one by one other voices joined it, not because the song was good but because singing together was a way of holding hands across distance.

Tomas sang too. Badly. It didn't matter.

* * *

They lost the race on the third morning.

Wren spotted it first — she'd climbed the shoulder of the hill to take bearings with her battered brass instruments, and when she came back down her face had the color of old paper. "Riders," she said. "Six of them. Light cavalry, moving fast along the river road. They've found the empty village."

"How long?" Kestrel asked, already tightening her pack straps.

"At their pace? They'll reach the foot of these tracks by midday. And Kestrel—" Wren hesitated, and Tomas watched her decide to say the hard thing, watched her choose honesty over comfort the way she chose it every time, as if it physically cost her. "They're not following the villagers' trail. They're casting about. Quartering the ground. The way hounds do when they've been given a scent-cloth." She looked directly at Kestrel. "They're not hunting refugees. They're hunting *you*. Something about your blood leaves a sign, and Voss's witch-finders know how to read it."

The words dropped into the group like a stone into a well, and Tomas heard the splash of it in everyone's silence — in Kestrel's white-knuckled grip on her strap, in Wren's defiant lift of her chin, in his own heartbeat, which had gone strangely calm. Of course. Of course she was the scent. The Ember had been reaching for her since the night the stars went out; he'd seen it in her face in the caves, heard it in the pauses where her voice should have been. Every hour they lingered was an hour the net drew tighter around the one person among them the enemy could not afford to lose.

"Then we split the trail," Tomas said.

Everyone turned to him.

"The villagers keep climbing — Doss knows the high caves, I showed him the map myself. We four go north along the ridgeline, loud, visible, leaving a trail any fool could read. The riders chase the brighter fire." He heard himself say it and marveled, distantly, at how easily the words came now, how little they sounded like the man who'd once calculated requisitions in a warm office while villages burned. "We're the bait. We've been the bait since Graywater. At least this time we choose it."

"That's suicide arithmetic," Wren said sharply. "Four against six, on open ground—"

"Four against six on ground *I* choose, with a river at our backs and a fog coming off it — look at the air, scholar, you can smell the weather. And six riders report to someone. Which means six riders can be made to report something false." He allowed himself the thin smile that had once disarmed ambassadors and now mostly annoyed Wren. "I was trained for this part. Let me finally be useful at the thing I was trained for."

Kestrel studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face seemed to settle something in hers. "Nobody dies," she said. "That's the price. Nobody dies buying time, not today, not after Ivo. We run, we mislead, we live. Swear it."

"I swear it," Tomas said, and found, checking himself carefully, that he meant it — that some buried part of him that had wanted, for eleven years, to end dramatically and be forgiven posthumously, had quietly died in the night on a mountain road, holding a baby named Lark.

Far below, faint on the wind off the river, they heard it: a horn. One note, then two. Not a hunting call.

A summons.

And threaded through the horn's dying echo, so faint that only Kestrel went rigid and only Wren whispered it aloud, came the other sound — the one that had been following them for days, the pressure behind the air, the attention like a thumb pressed against the world's skin.

*"Keeper,"* the darkness seemed to murmur, in a voice like coals settling. *"Little keeper. Why do you waste your light on shadows?"*

Kestrel's eyes went distant. Tomas stepped closer to her — deliberately, bodily, putting himself between her and the eastern sky — and after a moment she blinked, and shuddered, and was back.

"Midday," she said hoarsely. "They reach the tracks by midday. Let's go give them something to chase."

They turned north along the ridge, four small figures on a vast grey spine of stone, walking loudly toward the hunters — and behind them, high in the shepherd roads, two hundred souls climbed toward the caves and the clean water and the stubborn, unbowed morning, carrying with them a harvest song and the memory of four strangers who had stopped when stopping cost them everything, and stopped anyway.

Tomas did not look back. He had spent enough of his life looking back.

But he smiled as he walked, and the fog came up off the river to meet them, and somewhere below, the horns sounded again — closer now, and hungry, and right on time.
