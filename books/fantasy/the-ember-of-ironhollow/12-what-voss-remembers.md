## 12. What Voss Remembers

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Maren Voss had learned to count her days by the dripping.

One drip from the ceiling seam to the puddle in the corner: roughly two heartbeats. The puddle took an hour to fill and an hour more to drain through some crack in the floor she had never found. She had been in this cell—her cell, though she was not certain anymore whether *prisoner* or *keeper* was the truer word—for eleven days, or perhaps fourteen. Time went strange when you stopped sleeping properly, and she had stopped sleeping properly the night the thing in her scars began to whisper back.

She sat with her spine against the wall because lying down invited the dreams, and the dreams were where it spoke loudest. In the waking hours it only murmured, a sound like wind through a keyhole, like a fire very far away. It lived along the lattice of scars that climbed her left side from hip to throat—the marks the Ember's light had burned into her the day General Voss knelt before the crown and swore herself body and breath to the king's service. She had thought, then, that the burning was a test she passed. She understood now that it had been a door, and she had held it open herself.

The cell beside hers had been occupied for three days.

She knew who it was before the guards said his name. She heard him praying—not to any god, because defrocked priests did not pray to gods, but in the low, steady cadence of a man reciting something memorized, something that kept his hands from shaking. Litanies. Old ones. She recognized fragments: the Verses of the Sealed Door, the Canticle of Ash. Things that had been burned out of the temples years ago on the crown's order. Her order. Her signature at the bottom of the decree, ink still wet with pride.

"You're awake," the voice said through the wall. Not startled. Conversational, almost. As if they were two travelers sharing a fire instead of two prisoners sharing a drain.

"I am always awake," Voss said. Her own voice surprised her—rough, unused, smaller than the voice that commanded vanguards.

"So am I," he said. "It's one of the things I'm best at."

She should have called the guards. There was a bell-pull, rusted but functional, and a guard named Pellit who came at all hours with his lantern and his fear-sweat smell. She should have summoned him and reported that the priest Ivo Reyes was attempting conversation with the Inquisitor-General of the Crown, which was either madness or strategy, and both were punishable.

She did not call the guards.

"What is your crime, priest?" she asked instead, and hated how much the question sounded like curiosity.

"The usual. Knowing too much and saying it." A pause, the drip of water filling the silence. "And yours, General? What does one have to do to be locked in the same dungeon as one's own soldiers?"

The question slid between her ribs with the ease of a blade that knew the way. Because that was the truth of it, wasn't it—the Ember's orders had come down through channels that bypassed her entirely now. She had woken three nights ago to find her command seal revoked, her escort doubled, and herself marched down into the undercroft of her own garrison like contraband. *Hold her until the mountain,* the courier had said, not meeting her eyes. *Until the king comes down.*

"They are not my soldiers anymore," she said.

"No," Ivo Reyes agreed softly. "I don't suppose they are."

* * *

On the fourth day, he said her name.

Not *General*. Not *Voss*. Not the title she had built like armor over thirty years of service, first to the old marshal her father served, then to the crown, then to the thing wearing the crown. He said:

"Marenka."

The word went through her like cold water through a cracked dam.

No one had called her that since she was nine years old, running barefoot through the terraced gardens above Lake Sorrel, her grandmother's voice chasing her up the hillside—*Marenka, Marenka, the bread will be gone and you'll have nothing but regrets.* No one living knew that name. It existed nowhere in any record. It had belonged to a woman dead twenty years and a house burned in the border wars and a girl who had believed, with the whole ferocious simplicity of childhood, that she would grow up to protect places like that one. Gardens. Lakes. Bread that would still be there when you came down the hill.

"How do you know that name?" Her voice came out wrong. Thin. The whisper behind her scars stirred, attentive, and she pressed her palm flat against the stone as if she could push the sound deeper down.

"A man confesses before he dies," Ivo said. "It's the last useful thing priests do. Your grandmother came to my temple in Vellmark, the year before the end. Old, ill, frightened for you. She'd heard what you'd become—the general who burned the granaries at Corren's Ford—and she couldn't believe it. She wanted me to tell her it was lies." A long breath. "I told her I didn't know. She gave me the name anyway. Said if I ever met you, I should use it. Said names were doors, and someone had to leave yours unlocked."

Voss discovered she was crying.

It was an absurd, animal thing—tears without sobbing, sliding hot down her face while she sat perfectly straight against the wall like a soldier at inspection. She could not remember the last time she had wept. She suspected the Ember had taken it from her early, along with her appetite for honeyed wine and her ability to sleep past dawn and her memory of her mother's face. It took things gradually, politely, the way water takes a shoreline. You woke one morning and could not recall what grief felt like, and you were grateful, because grief had been heavy, hadn't it? And then one morning you could not recall why you had ever wanted to feel anything at all.

But the name had gone through the hollow like a spear through rotten wood, and everything behind it was pouring out.

"I burned Corren's Ford," she said. The confession came out of her the way blood comes from a wound—under pressure, unstoppable. "There were four hundred people in the granary district. We told ourselves they were harboring smugglers. They were harboring *grain*, priest. Winter grain. The Ember—" she caught herself, the word foreign in her mouth after so many months of thinking it only in the dark, "—the crown required the stores moved south, and the carts were slow, and slow carts were treason, so I made the grain into ash and the ash into an example."

"I know," Ivo said gently. "I buried some of them."

She laughed then—a horrible, cracked sound—and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Why are you telling me this? Why give me the name? Are you trying to save my soul? I don't think there's enough left of it to bother with."

"I'm not trying to save your soul," Ivo Reyes said. "I'm trying to reach the rest of you. There's more left than you think, Marenka. Hollowed things don't grieve. Whatever is happening to you right now—that's you. That's the part it couldn't eat."

* * *

For one hour—one hour exactly, she would count it later by the drip of the water—Maren Voss was herself.

It was not a gentle hour. It was an hour of standing naked in the wreckage of her life while the whisper behind her scars snarled and clawed at the edges of her mind like a dog shut out of a house it had owned for years. But she was *in* the house. She was in it, and she looked at everything.

She remembered the levy at Thornfell, the farm boys marched south with rope halters because their villages had missed quota, and how she had watched them go with nothing in her chest at all, and how she had thought at the time, *how efficient I've become*, when the truth was that something had eaten the part of her that would have refused.

She remembered Inquisitor Dane, who had begun asking questions about the crown's new decrees, and who had died of a fever so sudden and convenient that no one dared investigate. She remembered signing the order that sent his widow north. She remembered the night the stars went out—she had been on the walls of the garrison, and every soldier around her had gasped and crossed themselves, and she had felt *nothing*, only a faint warm pulse along her scars like a cat purring, and she had thought: *this is fine. This is fine.* It had not even occurred to her to wonder why she thought that.

She remembered Kestrel Sandoval's file. Smuggler. Guild-born. The interrogation records of the man they'd taken on the Graywater road—the one whose screams she had listened to through the door because the Ember wanted her to listen, wanted her accustomed, wanted her *hungry* in some way she refused to examine. The prisoner had said the same thing over and over until his voice broke: *the Sandoval girl, bring the Sandoval girl alive, alive, alive.* And Voss had written it in her report in her precise, beautiful hand, and beneath the words, in a place she had not known was still there, something small had whispered: *run.*

"Marenka." Ivo's voice again, careful now, the way you speak to a person standing at the edge of a high place. "Where are you?"

"Corren's Ford," she said. "I'm counting them. Four hundred and six. I never counted them before. It wouldn't let me count them."

"It's not letting you do anything right now," Ivo said. "That's the point. Listen to me. The party I came in with—they're going to Ironhollow. The Sandoval girl among them. When the crown comes down the mountain, there will be a moment—I don't know what it looks like yet—but there will be a moment when the hold on everyone it wears goes thin. It has to concentrate. It has to spend itself. Do you understand? When that moment comes, you'll have a choice. It will feel like drowning and surfacing in the same breath. Take the surface."

"And do what?" she asked, and her voice broke on it. "What am I, Ivo Reyes, besides what it made me? I have no god. My king is a hollow shell. My army follows the crown because the alternative is following me, and look at me. If you cut the strings, I don't stand. I fall."

"Then fall toward something," he said. "That's all faith ever was. Falling toward something, and finding out too late whether it catches you. I fell toward a god for forty years and it turned out to be people. People catch better than you'd think. They're closer to the ground."

She laughed again, and this time it was almost a real laugh, wrecked and wet and human. Through the wall, in the dark, she heard him laugh too—an old man's chuckle, tired and warm.

They talked for the rest of the hour. Small things. The bread at Lake Sorrel—rye and caraway, she remembered suddenly, caraway, she could *taste* it. His mother's habit of salting the stew twice because she never trusted the first time. The way snow sounded coming off pine boughs. The inventory of ordinary lives, laid out between them like treasure across a prison wall, and every item of it a wound and a mercy, proof of everything the Ember had taken and everything it had somehow failed to take.

The whisper behind her scars grew louder as the hour aged. It had stopped snarling. That was worse. Now it was patient, and it spoke in her own voice, and it said: *you know this changes nothing. You know what you are. Put it down, Maren. Put it down and rest.*

Near the end, she said, "When it comes back—and it will come back—I won't remember this. Will I?"

"Some residue stays," Ivo said quietly. "A splinter. A name said at the right moment. I've seen it pull people back from further under than you are."

"If I hurt you," she said, "after—if whatever's left of me in there is made to hurt you—"

"You'll leave a door unlocked," Ivo said. "That's all anyone can do. Leave doors unlocked and trust the lockpickers."

* * *

She felt it return the way you feel a tide turn: not suddenly, but inevitably, a cold pressure rising through the cracks, seeping up through the floor of her mind. The hour ended. The tears dried on her face and the salt of them tightened her skin, and the last human thing she did with that hour was press her palm flat against the wall between their cells, and hear him press his palm against the other side.

Then the hollow closed over her like dark water.

She sat against the wall. She counted drips. Somewhere far away, a thing that wore her voice whispered plans about mountains and kings and a girl with smuggler's blood, and Maren Voss listened to it from underneath, from the bottom of the lake of herself, hands pressed against the underside of the ice.

But.

But her body had habits older than the Ember, drilled into it by thirty years of soldiering, and the hollow wore her body and used her hands and did not think to check them, because why would it check the hands of its own instrument?

In the deep night, when the guard Pellit made his round and stood outside her bars with his lantern, the thing wearing Maren Voss rose and demanded to be escorted to the interrogation level—to see the priest, to finish what the crown had begun, to ask him questions with her beautiful precise voice while her hands did their work. Pellit fumbled the ring of keys with his fear-sweat fingers. The hollow watched him with her cold gray eyes and felt nothing at all.

And her hands—her hands, quick and sure, muscle remembering what the mind had drowned—took the ring from him, found the third cell's lock by touch alone in the dark of the keyring, and turned it, smooth and silent, before handing the keys back.

Pellit never noticed. The hollow never noticed. It walked back down the corridor wrapped in its purposes, and behind it, in the third cell, a defrocked priest sat listening to the lock he had not touched click softly open, and began, very quietly, to pray—not to any god, but to a smuggler, a scholar, and a drunkard prince somewhere out in the dark, hoping the roads were kind and the timing was better than his faith deserved.

Above them both, far to the east, under a sky with no stars, the armies of the crown began to march for Ironhollow.
