## 13. Ironhollow Gates

Wren Ashvale had spent eleven years studying the wards of Thornfell from the inside of a cellar, and in all that time she had never once imagined she would stand before the thing they were built around.

Ironhollow rose out of the mountain's shoulder like a black tooth. Even from two miles off, across the scree fields and the last stunted pines, she could see that something was wrong with it — or right, depending on how you read a horizon. The peaks behind it caught the dying light and burned copper and rose. The city itself did not. Light went into Ironhollow and did not come back out, the way sound goes into a wool-lined room. Three centuries of rain had not softened its edges. Three centuries of snow had not gathered on its roofs. It simply sat there, sealed and patient, like a mouth holding back a word.

"Stop," Kestrel said, and her voice came out strange — flattened, as though the air itself were thinning ahead of them.

They stopped. The four of them stood in a ragged line on the ridge: Kestrel with her hand resting on the strap of her pack the way other people touched talismans; Tomas with his hood up and his jaw set against whatever the mountain was saying to him; Wren herself, ink-stained fingers already itching for her journal; and the space where Ivo should have been, which had been an ache between them since Graywater.

"He'll be there," Kestrel said. Not to anyone. To the mountain, maybe. Or to herself.

"You don't know that," Tomas said gently.

"I know it."

Wren said nothing. She had learned, over these weeks on the road, when her precision was welcome and when it was cruelty dressed up as honesty. But privately — in the ledger she kept behind her eyes, the one where every claim was entered with its probability — she gave Kestrel's certainty perhaps one chance in five. Priests who offered themselves as decoys at river crossings did not walk away from inquisitor cages. And if they did walk away, they did not come back whole. She had read enough interrogation transcripts in the Archive to know what the Ember's people did with a captured priest who carried their enemy's secrets in his head.

They descended through the last of the light, and the cold came up to meet them, and Wren felt the wards before she saw anything at all.

That was the part no one had warned her about — not Brann's letter, not her own years of study, none of it. She had expected Ironhollow's defenses to be *dead*, the way a wall is dead, stone and mortar and nothing else. Instead what met her at the tree line was a pressure, faint and enormous, like standing at the edge of a held breath. Her teeth ached. The fillings in her molars — old lead work, cheap garrison dentistry from her Academy days — hummed at a frequency just below hearing.

"Wren?" Kestrel had turned. "You've gone white."

"The wards are live." Wren worked her jaw, trying to make the humming stop. "Not active. Live. There's a difference. A dead ward is a broken lock. This is—" She searched for the word and hated the only one that fit. "This is a held breath. Something is keeping these wards running, and it has been for three hundred years, and it is *tired*."

"Tired how?" Tomas asked.

"Tired the way a man holding a door shut is tired. With his shoulder. For three centuries." She pulled out her journal, mostly to have something to do with her hands. "The founding texts called Ironhollow the Anvil Seat. They never said what sat on the anvil."

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, and Wren did not sleep. She sat up with her back against the cold stone and watched the sky, because the sky over Ironhollow was doing something she needed to see again to believe.

The stars were visible here.

Not all of them. Not properly. They hung above the dead city in a rough oval, perhaps forty of them, frozen exactly as they had been on the night everything went dark — mid-vanish, half-dissolved, their edges fraying into nothing like embers eaten by paper. Wren had charted the vanishing from her cellar window in Vellmark, hour by hour, and she recognized the pattern instantly: this was the sky of the first night, preserved. A snapshot. The darkness had rolled across Thornfell like a tide, and here, directly above the place where the tide had come from, it had stopped — or been stopped — and left these few stars hanging in suspension, neither alive nor dead.

Like the wards. Like everything here. A held breath.

She wrote until her fingers cramped, and then she wrote more, because writing was the only prayer she had ever been any good at.

* * *

They found Ivo at dawn, and he was waiting for them, and that was the first wrong thing.

He sat on a boulder beside the road — the ancient paved road that ran up the valley toward gates tall enough to swallow a cathedral — with his hands folded in his lap and his face turned toward the sunrise that never quite arrived. He looked, at first glance, untouched. Same grey-streaked beard, same broad shoulders under the same travel-ruined coat. Then he turned his head toward them, and Wren saw it.

His eyes moved *wrong*. Not the empty-window blankness of King Aldric — thank every dead god for small mercies — but slow, deliberate, each motion arriving a half-beat after it should have, like a puppeteer working a marionette whose strings had gone slack. And when he smiled at Kestrel, the smile was Ivo's smile, warm and crooked and real, but it took too long to build, assembling itself piece by piece like a man remembering how the expression went.

"Ivo." Kestrel was running before anyone could stop her. She dropped to her knees in the frost and gripped his hands, and Wren watched him look down at her grip with an expression of faint scholarly interest, as if hands were a phenomenon he was relearning.

"Kestrel Sandoval," he said. His voice was his voice, but the cadence had changed — pauses in the wrong places, words set down carefully like stones across a stream. "You came. Good. That is — good. I told them you would."

"*Them?*" Tomas's hand had gone to his knife. "Ivo, who—"

"They asked me questions." Ivo's gaze drifted past them, up the valley, toward the gates. "For nine days. Or nine years. It is difficult — the counting was difficult. Then the questions stopped being questions and started being *offers*, and I understood that they did not want information. They wanted my consent. Consent makes the hollowing faster. Cleaner." He looked back at Kestrel, and for one flickering instant the slowness fell away and his eyes were sharp and wet and entirely his own. "I did not consent. I want that recorded. Someone should write it down. Write it down, scholar."

Wren's breath caught. "How did you—"

"You are Wren Ashvale. You take notes when you are frightened. Your thumb is white where you press the spine of your book." He said it without accusation, without anything at all, and then the warmth came back into his face, slowly, like dawn climbing a wall. "I escaped. Or I was released. I have thought about which, every hour since, and I cannot tell the difference, and that is the worst of it. The cell was unlocked. The guards were asleep. The gate stood open, and the road led here, and I walked, and I do not know if I chose any part of it."

"Then we go around," Tomas said flatly. "We find another way in. We don't walk through a gate he may have been sent to open."

"There is no other way in," Wren said quietly. All three of them turned. "There hasn't been for three hundred years. The founding texts list seven gates. Six were sealed from the inside during the Binding. The seventh—" She looked at Ivo, hating what she was about to say. "The seventh was left as the throat. One way in, so that whatever was bound would always know where its keepers stood. If Ivo was released rather than escaped, then yes, he may be a key someone is using. But if we turn back now, we lose the only season of the year the passes stay open, and Voss's army is three days behind us, and I would rather walk through a suspicious gate than be caught in the open by the vanguard. At least the gate's intentions are *old* intentions. Voss's are current."

"Spoken like a true archivist," Tomas muttered.

"Spoken like someone who has read what happens to armies that camp on the Anvil Seat's doorstep." She closed her journal. "Besides. Look at him. Whatever they did to Ivo, they didn't finish it, and they didn't finish it because *he didn't let them*. Nine days of offers and he kept enough of himself to walk out the wrong direction. That's not a key. Keys don't choose which lock they open."

Ivo was watching her with that slow, assembling smile. "The scholar defends me," he said. "I am — I am glad. I think I am glad. It is hard to be sure of my own weather now. But when you spoke just now, something in me said *yes, that is true, trust that one*, and I have decided to believe that something, because it is the only part of me that still sounds like me."

Kestrel stood. She had not let go of his hands. "He vouched for me in a burning temple when he had every reason to hand me to the inquisitors," she said. "He carried Wren's pack through the flooded caves when she'd cracked two ribs. He gave himself up at Graywater so the rest of us could breathe. Whatever they left in him, whatever they took out of him — I'm vouching back. Anyone who wants to leave him on this mountain can tell me now, and then explain it to me again every day for the rest of my life."

No one spoke. The wind came down the valley, smelling of cold iron and old smoke, and somewhere far above them the frozen stars held their positions, patient as nails.

Tomas exhaled through his nose. "Fine. But he walks in the middle, and if his eyes go window-blank, I knock him down and we run. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Kestrel said.

"Agreed," Wren said, and meant it, and hated the small traitor voice in her that whispered: *you agreed too easily, Ashvale. You wanted to believe the best of him because the alternative means the Ember wins even when it loses.* She silenced the voice. She was very good at silencing voices. It was, after all, most of what scholarship was.

* * *

The gates of Ironhollow were a quarter mile high, and Wren spent the entire approach cataloguing the ways that statement failed to prepare her.

They were not built so much as *grown* — or forged, if forging could be done at geological scale, which the evidence suggested it once could. The metal of them was dark and shot through with veins of a dull red luminescence, like cooling slag, like the memory of heat that had never fully died. As they drew closer Wren saw that the veins formed patterns. Letters. No — older than letters. Sigils, thousands upon thousands of them, layered over one another in strata, the way a hillside shows the bones of every forest that ever grew there. Generations of ward-work, written and rewritten and written again, each layer a generation of keepers adding their hand to the seal.

Her family had studied wards her whole life. The Academy had taught her that wards were walls — static, complete, finished things. Standing beneath the gate, craning her neck until it hurt, Wren understood for the first time that everything she knew was a footnote. This was not a wall. This was a *sentence*, three centuries long, still being spoken.

"It's beautiful," Kestrel breathed.

"It's terrified," Wren said.

The others looked at her.

"Look at the newest layers. The topmost sigils." She pointed with her journal. "The strokes are shaking. Whoever wrote last — and someone *did* write last, within living memory, decades at most — their hand wasn't steady. Fear does that to ward-script. It bleeds into the lines. Every keeper who ever added to this gate believed, absolutely, that the thing behind it was going to get out." She lowered her arm. "And the oldest layers aren't afraid at all. Look at the base. Confident, clean, almost casual. The founders weren't scared of the Ember." She swallowed. "They were scared of *needing* it."

Nobody answered that. There was nothing to answer it with.

The gate stood open. Of course it did. A gap barely wide enough for two abreast, cut in the vast metal like a keyhole, and through it a road of black iron ran inward, and beyond the road — Wren saw it and her mind refused it and then accepted it in the space of a heartbeat —

Stars.

Inside the walls of the dead city, the sky was full of stars. Hundreds of them, thousands, spread across a darkness that should not exist inside a roofed and walled enclosure, and every one of them frozen mid-vanish, frayed at the edges, hanging in the exact posture of their dying three hundred years ago. It was as if the night of the Binding had been taxidermied and hung overhead. As if the sky itself had been caught mid-scream and pinned.

"Oh," said Kestrel, very softly.

"That's not possible," Tomas said. "That's not — the city has *roofs*, there are buildings, there should be—"

"The roofs don't matter." Wren's voice came out steady, which surprised her, because nothing about her felt steady. "Neither does the sky. This isn't astronomy. It's a *record*. The Binding didn't just seal the Ember — it sealed the moment. Everything inside these walls is preserved at the instant of binding. The wards, the gate, the stars. The whole city is a wound that was closed around the knife." She made herself keep looking up. It was the hardest thing she had done since Graywater. "Which means the knife is still here. And wounds that are closed around knives fester."

They walked in.

The road ran straight through streets of silent houses, and the silence was the second-worst thing. Not emptiness — Wren had known empty places, ruined places, places where the people had died or fled. This was different. The houses were *furnished*. Doors stood ajar. A child's wooden horse lay on its side in a doorway, one wheel still turning, very slowly, as if it had been knocked over three hundred years ago and had not yet finished falling. On a windowsill, a bowl of water had not evaporated. A loaf of bread on a table, visible through a shutter, had not molded.

Nothing here rotted. Nothing here finished. The whole city was suspended in the last moment of its life, and walking through it felt like trespassing in a photograph, like disturbing mourners who had been kneeling so long they had become statues.

"We shouldn't touch anything," Wren whispered, and her whisper sounded obscene in that silence.

"I wasn't going to," Tomas whispered back.

"You were absolutely going to."

"...I was absolutely going to."

Ahead of them, Ivo walked with his head tilted back, staring at the frozen stars, and he was speaking — low, continuous, half to himself. Wren drifted close enough to hear, and wished she hadn't.

"—the third bell, the third bell always rang before the shift change, father worked the deep forge, he smelled of cinders and tallow, mother said the bells were the city's heartbeat, listen, listen, you can almost hear—"

"Ivo." Kestrel touched his arm. "Ivo, those aren't your memories."

He looked down at her. The slowness was worse here, under the held sky; his blinks came long and reluctant, like a man fighting sleep. "No," he agreed pleasantly. "They aren't. Isn't that remarkable? The city remembers, and it is — leaking. Into me. I am porous now, Kestrel. They made me porous. I contain multitudes and none of them are mine." He smiled his slow, assembled smile. "But some of them are kind. I try to hold on to the kind ones."

Wren wrote that down. Her hand shook, and she let it.

They made camp that night in a forge-square at the city's heart, around a fountain that had not flowed in three centuries and yet stood full to the brim, its surface perfectly still, reflecting the frozen stars with terrible fidelity. They ate cold rations and did not light a fire, by unspoken agreement; no one wanted to see flame in this place, or to learn whether the city would notice.

Kestrel took first watch. Tomas slept with his knife drawn. Ivo sat awake by the dry fountain, murmuring memories that belonged to dead strangers, and Wren sat beside him because someone had to, and because she had decided — deliberately, methodically, the way she decided everything — that watching him was more useful than fearing him.

After a long time, without turning his head, Ivo said: "You still don't trust me."

"No," Wren said. "I don't."

"Good." He nodded slowly, approvingly. "Keep it. Keep a little distrust, like a coin in your shoe. It reminds you the ground is real." His eyes drifted upward, to the fraying stars. "They left the door open for me, Wren. Nine days of questions and then an unlocked cell and an open gate and a road pointing here, and I walked it, and I told myself I chose to. But tonight, sitting here, listening to this city remember — I understood something." He turned to her at last, and his eyes were wet, and for this one moment they were fast and human and entirely Father Ivo Reyes. "If they wanted me to open the way for them — why would they need to? The gate has been open for three hundred years. The throat doesn't need a key, scholar. It needs a *voice*. Someone to call down into the dark and say the words the keepers used to say." His voice dropped to a whisper. "And I know the liturgy. God help me, I know it by heart. I have known it since I was six years old. What if that's why they let me go? Not to open the door. To *pray* it open."

Wren stared at him. Around them the dead city held its breath, and above the fountain the frozen stars hung in their ancient agony, and far below — she felt it rather than heard it, a vibration coming up through the iron road, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her — something turned over in its sleep.

Or began, very slowly, to wake.

"Ivo," she said. "When the Ember spoke to you. In the cell. Did it ever say what it wants?"

He was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, the slowness was back, and worse than before, and underneath it, faint as a bell rung in another room, his voice had company.

"It wants," he said, "to be *finished*."

Below their feet, in the dark beneath the city, something began — softly, patiently, note by note — to ring the third bell.
