## 15. The Voice in the Forge

The stairs went down farther than the mountain went up.

Tomas counted them at first — a habit from his soldiering years, when counting steps kept panic at arm's length — but he lost the number somewhere past three hundred, and after that there was only the descent: the worn black stone underfoot, the walls sweating cold mineral light, and the sound. A hum beneath the world, low as a struck bell that never finished ringing. He felt it in his teeth. In the old scar over his ribs. In the place behind his eyes where the court physicians had once said the madness lived.

They had been walking for hours. The dark was not empty.

"Talk to me," Kestrel said ahead of him, her voice pitched low, meant only for the group. "Anyone. I don't like how quiet it's getting."

"It's been quiet for six hours," Wren said. "That's what quiet is."

"Not this kind of quiet." Kestrel's hand found the wall, dragged along it. "This kind listens back."

Tomas knew what she meant. He had felt it since they passed beneath the frozen stars — those impossible constellations hanging mid-vanish above Ironhollow's dead streets, pinned like moths to a collector's board. Something down here had noticed them the way a man notices a fly on his wrist. Not alarmed. Interested.

The stair opened without warning into a gallery — a vast ribbed hall where the ceiling arched overhead like the inside of a throat, and veins of dull red light pulsed in the stone, slow as a sleeping pulse. Once, Tomas understood, this had been a road. Now it was an artery.

And the voice began.

It did not come from anywhere. It came from *in*, unfolding behind his eyes with the intimacy of memory, warm as brandy, patient as winter:

*You could have him.*

Tomas stopped walking.

*Your brother. Aldric. You could have him back — not the hollow thing wearing his face, not the puppet Voss leads on its strings of light. Him. The boy who taught you to swim in the mill pond until your lips went blue. The prince who stood up in council and said the crown was wrong about the northern levies because you asked him to. You could have all of it again. Every year the crown took from you. Every year exile took. One word, Tomas Aldricson. One small surrender.*

He saw it so clearly he swayed. Aldric laughing, whole, his eyes his own. Their father's hall full of light instead of dust. His name cleared — not *mad*, not *traitor*, just *brother*. The word he had been starving for for eleven years.

"Tomas." Kestrel's hand on his arm. Her grip was iron and callus. "Whatever it's saying — it's lying."

"It's not lying." His voice came out cracked. "That's the horror of it. It's telling me exactly what I want."

"Then want it louder," she said. "Want it so loud it drowns out everything else. But keep walking."

He kept walking. And the voice stayed with him, gentle now, wounded, like a lover left standing in the rain — and Tomas understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, that this was how it worked. Not fire. Not chains. Just the thing you wanted most, held just far enough away to follow forever.

* * *

Ivo Reyes walked at the rear, as he always did, and the voice came to him dressed as a hymn.

*You never stopped believing,* it murmured, in a tone like candlelight on old gold. *You only stopped being believed in. Come home. The vestments still fit. The incense still rises. There is a god who hears you, Ivo — he has heard you every day of your exile, every night you slept in the temple among relics that no longer answered, and he wept that they cast you out. Take my hand and be a priest again. Take my hand and never doubt.*

Ivo's breath fogged in the cold air and hung there too long.

He had spent nine years learning to live without the certainty that had once filled him like water fills a cup. Nine years of waking at dawn out of habit and reaching for prayers that came back echoless. He had told himself he'd made his peace with the silence. Standing in the Ember's artery-road, hearing divinity pour itself into his ears like it had never left — hearing forgiveness, *forgiveness*, offered freely by something vast enough to mean it —

"I know what you are," he whispered.

*I am what you've always wanted. That is all anything holy has ever been.*

"That's blasphemy."

*Yes,* the voice agreed, tenderly. *So is everything else you've ever believed.*

His hands shook. He clasped them together the way he had at ten thousand altars, and found the gesture still steadied him — not because any god watched, but because his body remembered what it was to stand in a shape of devotion, and shapes can hold a person even when the contents have spilled out.

Ahead of him, Wren stumbled. Behind her words, Kestrel had gone rigid, jaw set, marching like a woman wading against a river. And Tomas — Tomas walked like a man listening to two conversations at once, one aloud and one beneath the world.

Ivo looked at the four of them, his strange ragged congregation, and made himself a promise he wasn't sure he could keep: *If it offers me heaven, I will choose these people instead.* 

He did not notice, then, how easily the thought bent. How much room there already was inside him for bending. The Ember had held him once before, at Graywater, in the cells beneath the toll bridge — had poured itself through the cracks in him the way water finds cracks — and though Voss's hour of mercy had freed his body, some thread of the thing had come away with him, fine as spider silk, singing still.

He would remember this later. He would remember it too late.

* * *

Wren Ashvale had spent her whole life wanting one thing, and so the voice barely had to search.

*You were right.*

She stopped so hard Kestrel nearly collided with her.

*Every paper they burned. Every lecture they laughed out of the hall. The wards were built to contain — you said it at twenty-three, alone against the entire Academy, and they stripped your credentials and called you a crank and a coward, and you were right, Wren Ashvale, you were right, and I can give you the proof. The original ward-charters. The names of every scholar who buried the truth. Your vindication written in a hand three centuries dead, entered into the record where no one can ever laugh again. They will teach your name. They will apologize in textbooks.*

Her eyes stung. She hated that. She hated that her body responded to the fantasy before her mind could veto it — the great hall of the Academy, silent, every face turned toward her, the Provost rising from his chair to say *we were wrong, Ashvale, forgive us* —

"You're offering me a library," she said aloud, and her voice sounded thin and furious in the pulsing dark. "You're offering me *paperwork*."

*I am offering you justice.*

"Justice doesn't need me to want it badly enough to hand you my soul. Justice is work. Justice is boring and slow and done by people with dirty hands." She started walking again, faster, talking faster, because if she stopped talking the vision would close over her head like water. "My mother used to say the devil doesn't tempt you with lies, he tempts you with your own diary read back to you in a flattering voice. Well, I've read my diary. I know what's in it. Keep your charter."

But her hands wouldn't stop trembling, and she shoved them deep in her coat pockets where no one could see, and she catalogued the vein-lights in the walls to steady herself — cadence, interval, wavelength — because scholarship was her prayer and her armor both, and right now she needed every scrap of it.

Because here was the thing she did not say aloud, the thing that crawled in her stomach like a swallowed coal: the voice had quoted her own dissertation. Word for word. A text that existed in exactly one copy, which she had memorized and burned herself, eleven years ago, rather than let the inquisitors find it.

Nothing should have known those words.

Something down here had eaten them out of her memory like marrow from a bone.

* * *

Kestrel heard it plainly.

Not dressed as a hymn, not dressed as a brother, not dressed as a vindication. Plainly — a voice like her own voice, speaking in the register of the dark between stars, and it had been speaking to her since the night the sky went out, and tonight, descending into the forge-heart's throat, she finally heard the thing underneath the words.

Fear.

*Little keeper,* it said, as the gallery narrowed and the red veins brightened and the heat began to rise up through their boot-soles like breath from a furnace mouth. *Turn back. There is nothing here for you. There never was. Your guild smuggled trinkets and lies; your mother abandoned you in a tavern town because you were a burden; your blood is worth nothing but trouble. Go home. Rebuild your little roads. Forget Ironhollow.*

"Liar," Kestrel said softly.

*Am I? What do you actually remember of her? A woman on a horse, riding away. A sigil pressed into your palm and no explanation. Twenty years of silence while you starved and stole and learned to smile with a knife behind it. I offer you the truth, Kestrel Sandoval. Isn't truth what you've wanted all along?*

"No." She laughed, and the laugh came out jagged. "You're bad at this. Do you know why? Because everyone else got a beautiful lie, and I got *insults*. You're not tempting me. You're trying to make me turn around."

Silence. The veins pulsed. Somewhere below, vast and slow, something turned over in its sleep.

*You hear me plainly,* the Ember said at last, and the warmth drained out of it, leaving something older and colder and very, very careful. *Do you know how long it has been since anyone heard me plainly?*

"No. Tell me."

*No.*

And there it was. Kestrel felt it land in her chest like a dropped stone. The thing beneath the world — the entity that had hollowed a king, devoured her guild generation by generation, snuffed the stars themselves — had just refused to answer her. Had just, in the grammar of ancient and terrible things, flinched.

*You are afraid of me,* she said, wonder making her voice almost gentle. *Aren't you. All this power, all this hunger, and you're scared of a smuggler with a dead guild and a stolen letter.*

*I am not—*

"My blood predates the crown. Wren decoded it. Brann died carrying it to me. My mother died holding your door shut." She was walking faster now, down, toward the heat, toward the voice, and she realized she was smiling and couldn't stop. "You didn't cull my line because we were thieves. You cull us because every few generations, one of us is born who can hear you *plainly* — and plain hearing is the first step to unbinding. You've been murdering midwives' work for three hundred years, and you missed one."

The heat surged. The veins flared white-red, and the whole gallery groaned like a ship in a storm, and the voice came back stripped of every disguise, vast and furious and — yes, unmistakably now, even through the rage — *afraid*:

*COME NO FURTHER, KEEPER.*

Behind her, Tomas cried out and clutched his head. Wren dropped to one knee. Ivo made a sound she had never heard from him before, high and broken, a man's faith tearing down the middle.

Kestrel planted her feet in the burning dark and shouted back, in the voice she'd used to talk down customs officers and cutthroats and once, memorably, a river-god's minor cousin:

"Make me."

* * *

The gallery ended at a door.

There was no door, really — that was the wrong word for a slab of black metal three men tall, seamed with ward-script that glowed faint gold and flickered, flickered, like a lamp running out of oil. Beyond it, the hum became a roar. Heat rolled off the stone in visible waves. The air tasted of iron and cinders and, underneath, something sweet and rotten, like fruit left too long in a closed room.

The five of them stood before it, breathing hard, holding each other up. Tomas had blood on his lip from biting it. Wren's hands were still shaking. Ivo stared at the door with an expression Kestrel couldn't read — or could, and wished she couldn't.

"It knows us," Tomas said hoarsely. "All of us. It was inside our heads like it owned the deed."

"It's been reading us for days," Wren said. "Since Smuggler's Roads, at least. Maybe since the stars went out. It follows her blood; the rest of us are just... luggage it's learning to open." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears. "It quoted my own burned dissertation, Kestrel. It reached into my *memory*. If it can do that, what else can it reach?"

"Then we go in fast," Kestrel said. "Before it finishes reading."

Ivo hadn't spoken since the shout. Now he stepped forward, and his voice, when it came, was steady in a way that raised every hair on Kestrel's arms — too steady, smoothed flat, like ice over moving water.

"The rite," he said. "Wren, walk me through it again. Everything we'll need."

"Chalk of the old rite — I have it. Blood of the keeper's line — obviously. And the words, which are in the letter, which I have memorized twice over." Wren frowned. "Why? You were there when I—"

"Humor me," Ivo said. "Every word. From the beginning."

Kestrel looked at him. At the sweat on his brow despite the forge-heat. At his hands, folded in the shape of prayer, knuckles white.

Later — much later, in the ashes of everything — she would try to remember whether there had been a sign she missed. Whether the silk-thread crack in his voice had been audible, if only she had listened. Whether the Ember had already begun, in that moment outside the door, to spend him like a coin it had been keeping in its pocket since Graywater.

But in that moment there was only the door, and the heat, and the fear of the thing behind it, and her own heart hammering the oldest rhythm she knew: *forward, forward, forward.*

"All right," she said. "From the beginning."

Wren unslung her pack. The ward-script on the door guttered, dimmed — and for half a heartbeat, in the failing gold light, Kestrel saw the script rearrange itself, letters crawling like ants, spelling out a single word in a language older than the crown.

She couldn't read it.

But Ivo could. She saw it hit him — saw his whole body flinch as though struck — and saw him hide it, smooth and quick, the way a man hides a wound.

"Ready?" Kestrel asked.

"Yes," said Father Ivo Reyes, in a voice that was almost entirely his own.

Almost.
