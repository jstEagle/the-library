## 16. Betrayal at the Threshold

Tomas had learned to measure a man by his silences.

His brother had once been full of them — the long quiet of Aldric listening in council, weighing every word before he spent it. Voss's silence was different: the silence of a blade sheathed but not put away. And Ivo Reyes — Father Ivo, who talked like a river that had forgotten how to freeze — had been silent for most of the last day of their descent into Ironhollow.

That was the first wrongness. Tomas had told himself it was awe. The dead city did that to people; it had done it to him. Three centuries of sealed gates and frozen stars would hollow out anyone's chatter.

But there were other things, smaller things, that he had catalogued without knowing he was doing it — the old habit of a prince who had survived a court where noticing was the difference between living and dying.

Ivo no longer blessed himself before eating. He had done it every meal since Graywater, an absent little gesture, thumb to brow to breast, even defrocked, even drunk on grief for the god he'd lost. Tonight, at the cold camp in the archive antechamber, he had simply taken his bread.

Ivo had stopped humming. He used to hum hymns without words, half-melodies from a liturgy Tomas didn't know, when the dark pressed close. The last two days: nothing.

And once — only once, in the deep stair where Wren's lantern made shadows jump — Ivo had looked at Kestrel with an expression Tomas could not name, and it had lasted too long, and it had not been kindness.

*He's tired,* Tomas had told himself. *We're all tired. The mountain is eating us.*

He should have known better. He of all people should have known what it looked like when something wore a person like a coat.

* * *

The forge-heart lay below everything.

They reached it near what Tomas guessed was dawn, though dawn had become a rumor since the night the stars went out. The last stair opened into a cavern so vast that Wren's lantern-light died a few feet from its source, swallowed by a darkness that seemed less like absence and more like appetite. The air tasted of hot iron and old smoke and something sweeter beneath it — burnt sugar, almost, the smell of a feast burning.

And at the center, visible anyway, as if it made its own light by refusing light: the forge.

It was not a forge as Tomas understood forges. It was a ring of black stone pillars around a basin of coals that had not gone out in three hundred years, and above the coals hung a shape that hurt to look at directly — a knot of ember-glow, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart, wrapped in chains of script that crawled across its surface like insects. The binding. Wren had described it a dozen times on the road, drawing diagrams in ash and spilled tea. Seeing it was another thing entirely. It looked like a wound that had been stitched shut by someone who loved it very much and knew they were going to die.

"It's beautiful," Wren whispered, and then, horrified at themselves: "I didn't mean—"

"You meant," Kestrel said quietly. "That's how it works. That's how it gets you."

She stepped forward, and the ember-pulse quickened.

Tomas saw it happen — saw the great knot of fire above the coals shift its rhythm to match her heartbeat, saw the crawling script brighten along one chain like a vein filling. Kestrel Sandoval stood in her dead ancestors' house, and the house recognized her.

"Kestrel." Her name came out of Ivo's mouth, and it was wrong.

Tomas turned.

Ivo was standing at the edge of the lantern-light, and his face was coming apart. Not literally — worse than literally. It was the face of a man watching himself from a great distance, hands raised slightly from his sides as though someone else were operating them. His eyes were wet. His eyes had been wet for days, Tomas realized now; he'd thought it was grief for his god, for his cell, for the hour of freedom Voss had given him before the hollow took it back.

"Father?" Wren said. "Ivo?"

"I can't—" Ivo's voice cracked down the middle. "I need you to know that I am so sorry. I need you to hear me say it while I still—"

Then his hand closed around Kestrel's arm.

He moved fast — faster than a man his age had any right to move, faster than Ivo had ever moved, with the terrible economy of something that had worn many bodies and knew exactly where the joints went. He spun her off her feet, one arm barreled across her throat, and dragged her back toward the stair, toward the dark, away from the forge.

"No!" Kestrel's voice came out strangled. She twisted, drove an elbow into him, and it landed — Tomas heard the breath punch out of him — and it changed nothing. Ivo held on. His grip did not tremble. Whatever was holding her was not using Ivo's strength alone.

"Ivo, fight it," Tomas said, and drew his sword.

The sound of steel leaving leather rang through the cavern, and the ember-thing above the coals pulsed brighter, and for one absurd moment Tomas thought: *it likes this. It's watching.*

"I have fought it," Ivo gasped. His cheek was pressed against Kestrel's hair; his eyes found Tomas over her shoulder, and they were the worst thing Tomas had ever seen, because they were *his*, they were human, they were begging. "Every hour since Graywater. Every single hour. Do you know what it showed me? It showed me my congregation. All of them. Alive. Singing. It said — it said if I brought her to the crown, it would give them back, it would un-burn the temple, it would let me be a priest again, and God help me, God forgive me, some part of me *believed*—"

"Then don't believe it now!" Kestrel snarled. She got her boot against a pillar of fallen masonry and shoved, and they staggered together, and still he held her. "You told me faith was choosing! You said — you said your whole life was learning that faith wasn't certainty, it was *choosing*, over and over—"

"I lied about being able to keep choosing forever!" Ivo screamed, and the scream tore something in Tomas's chest, because it wasn't rage, it was grief, it was a man mourning himself while he still had a mouth to mourn with. "Everyone has a limit, child. Everyone breaks. I just wanted — I wanted to break *later*. I wanted one more year. Is that so much? Is that such a sin?"

"Yes," said Wren Ashvale, in the flat clear voice they used for facts, and threw the lantern.

Not at Ivo. At the floor, at the seam of ancient tile where the forge-wards ran — the boundary line Wren had been mapping since the gates, muttering measurements, chalking marks no one had understood. The lantern burst. Oil spread fire in a blue-edged sheet along the ward-line, and the script carved there woke up, drinking flame, and the whole threshold of the forge-chamber became a wall of light between them and the stair.

Between them and escape.

"You're trapping us," Tomas said.

"I'm trapping *him*," Wren said. "Wards don't care whose side you're on. Nothing crosses that line while it burns. Not him. Not whatever's riding him." Their voice shook on the last word. "Nothing gets delivered to the crown tonight."

* * *

What happened next, Tomas would spend the rest of his life failing to describe properly.

Ivo carried Kestrel toward the burning ward-line — because of course he did; the Ember had never been interested in possible outcomes, only in obedience, and obedience does not do arithmetic. And Kestrel, who had spent her whole life learning how bodies moved and how to move inside them, went limp in his arms at exactly the right moment, dropped her whole weight, hooked her heel behind his knee—

—and they both crashed down short of the fire, rolling, and Tomas was already moving.

He did not think about it. Later, he would try to reconstruct the thinking and find none there, only motion: his brother's training, twenty years old and buried under exile and drink, rising up through his arms like water finding an old channel. He hit Ivo at the shoulders. They went down together. The sword skidded away across the stone. Ivo's fist caught him under the ribs and the world went white at the edges.

"Tomas—" Ivo's face above him, streaming tears, contorted. "Don't make me hurt you. Please. It will use what I do to you. It will show it to me forever. Please."

"Then stop," Tomas wheezed.

"I *can't*—"

"You can." Tomas grabbed the front of his coat, hauled him close, forehead nearly touching forehead. He could smell the oil-smoke, the sweat, the sweet burnt-sugar reek of the thing underneath. He made himself look into those drowning eyes. "I know what it is to be ridden, Ivo. I know what it is to have your own mouth say things you'd die before meaning. I stood in my brother's court and called him mad for three years because it was easier than calling myself a coward. So believe me when I tell you — I know the exact weight of this moment. And I am telling you that you are *still here*. It has to work this hard because you are *still here*."

Ivo shuddered. His grip on Tomas's collar loosened — a finger's width, a breath's width.

"The Ember doesn't corrupt," Kestrel said. She was on her feet, blood at her lip, breathing hard, and she had picked up Tomas's sword, and she was pointing it at the priest she loved like a father, and her hand was steady, which must have cost her more than anything else that night. "Wren figured it out. It grants wishes until nothing of you remains. So I'm asking you, Father — what's left? When it gives you your congregation back, who walks into that temple? Who sings? Because I've met what's left after it finishes. I've seen the king's eyes. Is that what you want for them? For yourself?"

The silence stretched.

Above the coals, the ember-thing pulsed — faster now, arrhythmic, and Tomas felt rather than heard a pressure build in the cavern, a note below hearing, and the crawling script along the bindings began to move *faster*, and somewhere in the walls of the mountain stone groaned like a ship in weather.

It was afraid. Wren had said it, chapter and verse, in the dark of the caves: *only Kestrel hears it plainly, and it's afraid of her.* But Tomas could feel it now, everyone could, the way you feel a storm change its mind. The thing in the forge had bet everything on Ivo's hands, and Ivo's hands were stopping.

"Ivo," Kestrel said, softer. She lowered the sword. "You gave yourself up at Graywater for people you'd known three weeks. You told me that was the first real prayer you'd ever said. Don't take it back. Don't let it be the last."

Ivo made a sound that had no words in it at all.

His hands opened.

He sat back on his heels in the firelight, an old man in a ruined coat, and wept — not the controlled leaking of the last few days but the ugly, whole-body weeping of a man wrung out, and Tomas knelt beside him and did the only thing he knew to do, which was to put an arm around his shoulders and hold on, the way no one had held onto Ivo Reyes in a very long time, perhaps ever.

"I'm sorry," Ivo kept saying, into Tomas's shoulder. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I could feel myself doing it, I was screaming behind my own eyes—"

"I know," Tomas said. "I know. I believe you."

Because that was the terrible mercy of it, the thing Tomas would carry and carry and carry: Ivo had not betrayed them. Something had betrayed Ivo, using his love as the handle. And the difference mattered, and it also didn't matter at all, and both truths had to be held at once or you became either a fool or a monster, and Tomas was tired of the court's way of choosing between those.

* * *

The wards burned low. Wren fed them oil from the second flask, methodical, hands only shaking a little, and explained in a voice sanded flat by adrenaline: "It'll hold maybe an hour more. Then the line gutters and anything can cross. Including whatever it sends next, because I promise you, it knows we're here now. It's been *calling*."

Tomas heard it then, for the first time with his own ears instead of through Kestrel's confessions — far above, filtered down through miles of stone, a sound like a horn, like a bell, like a voice trying to be all three. The crown, coming down the mountain. Or something answering it.

"We don't have an hour," Kestrel said. She was standing at the edge of the forge-ring, looking up at the bound heart of the Ember, and the light of it played across her face, and she looked — Tomas searched for the word and hated the word when he found it — she looked *recognized*. Like a portrait meeting its subject. "The rite's in the ledger. I memorized it. It needs blood that predates the crown, spoken at the basin, and it needs—" She stopped.

"It needs what?" Wren asked.

"Nothing," Kestrel said, too quickly, too smoothly, and Tomas watched her lie to them with a smuggler's clean craft, and something cold walked down his spine. "It needs me to start. That's all. Start, and keep going, and don't stop no matter what it shows you."

"That's not nothing," Wren said. "Rites that say *don't stop no matter what* always cost something. I've read enough of them to know the grammar."

"Then read it yourself when we're done." Kestrel turned, and smiled at them, and it was a good smile, warm and crooked and real, and that was the worst part, that it was real. "Whatever happens down here — thank you. For coming. For staying. Nobody's ever—"

The horn-voice sounded again, closer, and this time it was joined by another, and another, a chord of them, and dust sifted from the cavern roof.

"They're bringing him," Tomas said quietly. "Voss is bringing Aldric to the forge. It wants its vessel and its key in the same room."

"Then we finish this before they arrive." Kestrel rolled up her sleeve. Old scars there, guild-marked, family-marked. She stepped toward the basin of coals that had burned for three hundred years, toward the knot of chained fire that had eaten her mother's mother's mothers, and the ember-light rose to meet her like a tide.

Behind them, at the guttering edge of the ward-fire, Ivo Reyes lifted his head, tears still wet on his face, and said in a voice that was only half his own:

"She's lying to you. About the price."

The ember-thing pulsed once, furious, and every chain of script blazed white —

— and Kestrel, without turning around, without stopping, said: "I know. Keep him back, Tomas. Whatever I say. Whatever I do. *Keep him back.*"

And she stepped into the forge-light.
