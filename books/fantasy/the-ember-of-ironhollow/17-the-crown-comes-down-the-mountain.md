## 17. The Crown Comes Down the Mountain

The order came at dusk, written in a hand that was not the king's.

Maren Voss knew this because she had spent eleven years reading the king's hand — the careful, clerk-trained script of Aldric the Third, the way he pressed too hard on his descenders, the little ink-blots where his pen hesitated over hard decisions. She had burned enough of his warrants to know them by texture. This one was smooth. Effortless. The letters flowed like water finding a crack in a wall, and every stroke of it made the skin of her scars itch as though something beneath were turning over in its sleep.

*Bring the king to Ironhollow,* the warrant said. *The forge must be fed.*

It did not say who would do the feeding. It did not need to. Voss had stopped asking questions of the crown two winters ago, back when the questions still formed fully in her mind before something behind her eyes could eat them. Now they came apart halfway up her throat like wet paper.

She stood on the high road above Graywater with the warrant in one hand and the vanguard at her back — four hundred picked soldiers, the Hollow Guard's finest, men and women whose eyes had gone glassy in ones and twos over the past year until now not one of them met her gaze with anything behind it but patience. They waited the way furniture waits. She hated how well they waited. She hated more the small, traitorous part of her that envied them their emptiness, because emptiness did not ache.

Behind them, on the ridge, the king's pavilion glowed.

They had brought him down from the capital in a litter draped in black silk, twelve bearers who never slept, and Voss had watched him the whole way — the way you watch a wound to see if it will close or spread. Aldric sat upright in that litter like a man carved from tallow left too near a flame: still shaped like a person, still faintly warm, but softening by degrees into something that would run. His hands lay folded in his lap. His eyes stayed open. He did not blink often enough for a living man, and when he did blink, it was slow, deliberate, like a door being tested for its lock.

Tonight, though, tonight was different.

Tonight the pavilion's light moved.

Voss turned as the tent flap parted without a hand touching it, and the king came out.

He walked unaided for the first time since Graywater. That was the first wrongness. The second was what trailed him: threads of light, fine as spider-silk and pale as the memory of stars, running from the crown on his head back into the dark of the tent — no, not the tent, *through* the tent, up, back toward the sky that had no stars in it anymore. Strings. He was strung like a marionette, and the puppeteer was somewhere overhead, in that vast blind dark where the constellations used to hang, and it was reeling him in.

"Your Majesty," Voss said, and knelt, because the form was all that was left of her, sometimes. Form was a rope you could hold onto when the current pulled.

Aldric looked down at her. For a moment there was nothing in his face at all — a mask of kingly calm stretched over a hollow, the way a glove keeps the shape of a hand after the hand is withdrawn. Then, deep down, at the very bottom of his eyes, something *moved*. A flicker. A drowning man's hand breaking the surface of black water.

"Inquisitor." His voice was his own. That was the worst part. The Ember wore his voice the way it wore everything else, and it fit so well because it had been wearing him for years. "You have the warrant."

"I do."

"Then we go down." He gazed past her, past the fires of the camp, to where the mountains folded together around a valley of dead stone. Ironhollow. Even from here, even in starless dark, Voss could feel it — a pressure behind the eyes, a hum in the teeth, like standing near a struck bell that would not finish ringing. "It is time I went home."

*Home.* The word snagged on something in her, some splinter of the woman she had been. Kings did not call ruined forge-cities home. Kings did not walk out of their own tents trailing strings of light. And kings — she remembered this suddenly, vividly, a memory rising through the murk like a body through a lake — kings did not once, three years ago, grip her wrist in a corridor of the palace and whisper, *if ever I look at you and do not know you, burn the crown off my head*, before his face smoothed over and he thanked her courteously for her service and walked on.

She had told herself she'd imagined it. She had gotten very good at telling herself things.

"Your Majesty," she said carefully, "the descent is steep. Perhaps—"

"We go down." No anger. No emphasis. Just the sentence again, laid down like a stone in a path, and behind it the patient weight of the thing that had laid it. The threads of light shivered. Somewhere far above, in the dark, something adjusted its grip.

Voss bowed her head. "We go down."

* * *

They descended at dawn, if dawn could be said to exist anymore.

There was a grey hour, still — the world insisted on it, a thinning of the dark that passed for morning — but no sun broke the east. The mountains stood up against a sky the color of old ash, and the army moved down the switchback road into the valley like a bruise spreading across pale skin. Four hundred Hollow Guard in their lacquered black. Two hundred regulars who still had shadows under their eyes and fear in their step, the last unclaimed soldiers of the vanguard, kept together where Voss could watch them. The supply train. The priests of the Crown Rite, chanting the new liturgy, the one about the Ember Eternal and the King Who Is the Kingdom. And at the center, the litter, and the king inside it, and the threads of light running up out of it into nothing.

Voss rode at the column's head and felt the valley noticing them.

That was not a figure of speech. She had learned, these past months, to feel things she had no vocabulary for — the Ember's attention moving across the land like weather, gathering here, thinning there. And Ironhollow's valley had a quality of attention to it, a held-breath quality, as though the mountains themselves were leaning in. As they dropped below the ridgeline, the hum in her teeth became a tone, and the tone became almost a word, and the word was almost her name, and she dug her nails into her palms until the pain gave her something else to be.

Her scars ached in rhythm with it. They always did now. Seven of them, raked across her collarbone and shoulder by a smuggler's knife in the Toll Road riots, years ago — an ordinary wound, long healed, until the night the stars went out. Then they had opened like mouths. Not bleeding. *Listening.* She had lain awake that whole first night feeling the dark pour through them, feeling it taste her, feeling it find the shape of her ambition and her guilt and her terrible obedient love of order, and say, in a voice like her own voice heard underwater:

*I can use this.*

And she had said yes. That was the thing she could never unsay. Whatever else happened, whatever mercy the world might someday offer her, she had said yes, and the yes lived in her scars like a brand lives in hide.

"General."

Captain Dree fell in beside her horse — a broad, reliable woman, one of the last half-herself officers Voss had. Half-herself was a relative term. Dree's hollowness showed only in small ways: she no longer sang on the march; she no longer flinched at anything; yesterday she had eaten a hardtack biscuit while watching a deserter flogged and her expression had not changed at all.

"The scouts report movement in the lower valley," Dree said. "Small party. Five, maybe six, coming up the pilgrim road from the south. They'll reach the gates by midday."

Voss's heart did something complicated and painful that she chose not to examine. "Armed?"

"One carries a priest's staff. One walks like a soldier. The others—" Dree paused, and for a flicker of a moment something crossed her face that might, in another life, have been unease. "The dark follows them, General. Like a cloud that won't come loose. The men are saying the starless sky has a leash, and they're holding the other end."

The Sandoval girl. It had to be. The Ember wanted her alive — the orders had been explicit, repeated, obsessive, *the bloodline comes to the forge, the bloodline comes willingly or carried* — and where the girl went, the Ember's attention pooled like oil. Of course the dark followed her. The dark followed its own heart.

"And the king?" Voss asked.

"Resting. If you can call it that." Dree lowered her voice, which was pointless — the Hollow Guard heard everything and cared about nothing. "He stood all night, General. Facing south. Toward the gates. He didn't move until the grey hour, and when the bearers tried to fold him back into the litter he said—" she hesitated, and the hesitation itself was remarkable, a crack in the glass, "—he said, *not yet, she isn't here yet.*"

*She isn't here yet.* The king, waiting for the smuggler. Or the thing in the king waiting, the way a spider waits, with the web already spun and trembling.

"Double the pickets on the pilgrim road," Voss said. "No one touches the girl. No one speaks to her. If she reaches the gates, let her reach them. Report to me and no one else."

Dree's eyebrows rose a fraction. "The warrant says—"

"The warrant says the bloodline comes to the forge. It does not say broken." Voss heard her own voice come out flat and cold, the voice of the Inquisitor-General, and beneath it, faintly, the other voice, the underwater one, purring approval. *Yes. Keep her whole. Keep her fresh. We want her to walk in on her own feet, wanting what we want her to want.* Voss clenched her jaw until her molars sang. "See it done."

Dree saluted and dropped back. Voss rode on alone at the head of the column, and the valley closed around them, and the hum in her teeth resolved at last into a word, and the word was not her name after all.

It was *soon.*

* * *

They reached the overlook above Ironhollow in the afternoon, and the whole army stopped, because there was a city in the valley floor that no map admitted to.

Three centuries dead, the scholars said. Sealed since the Sundering, since the last of the forge-lords died or fled or were made into whatever the archives refused to name. And yet it rose out of the valley whole: walls of black iron-streaked stone, gate-towers like cupped hands, and above it all — Voss felt her breath stop — *stars.*

Stars. In a sky that had none.

They hung over Ironhollow in a frozen constellation, mid-vanish, each one a smear of silver light caught in the act of going out, like rain suspended before it strikes the ground. The scholars in her train began weeping. The priests began chanting louder. And Voss sat her horse and stared at those dying captive stars and understood, with the cold clarity the Ember allowed her precisely because understanding changed nothing, that she was looking at the moment of its making. Held. Preserved. The sky's last breath, kept in a bottle over the place where the bottle was blown.

Everything converges here, she thought. The girl. The king. Me. Whatever is left of any of us.

"General." Dree again, at her stirrup, pointing down. "The gates."

Below, at the base of the great sealed doors, figures had gathered. Small at this distance, five or six, one leaning on a staff. And among them — Voss narrowed her eyes, disbelieving — a seventh figure, standing apart from the others, robed in travel-worn white, arms open, facing the army on the mountain as though he had been waiting for it specifically.

She knew that stance. She had put prisoners against walls in that stance. She had seen condemned men take it, once, in the old days, when the crown still bothered with trials.

"That's the priest," Dree said. "Reyes. The one we took at Graywater."

"He escaped."

"No, General." Dree's voice had gone strange. "The cell was unlocked. From the outside. And the warden swears the king's own seal was on the release order — except the king was on the road with us, and the seal was *warm* when they found it."

Voss looked at the priest standing in the shadow of the gates, arms open, and at the frozen stars above him, and at the thread of light that ran, when she finally thought to follow it, from the king's pavilion behind her, down the switchbacks, across the valley floor, to end — she traced it twice to be sure — at the priest's chest.

The pieces were moving. All of them. Every piece on the board, hers and theirs and the board's own, sliding toward the same square, and the square was a forge, and the forge was hungry, and the clock —

The clock. She could hear it now. Not metaphorically. A slow, vast ticking under the earth, under the city, under her scars, keeping time with her pulse.

"Maren."

She went rigid. That was the king's voice, close, directly behind her — but the king was in his litter a hundred yards back, she had *seen*—

She turned anyway. Slowly. The way you turn toward a sound in a dark house.

Aldric stood at the edge of the overlook, having crossed the distance without her hearing, without anyone hearing, his bearers staring blankly at nothing. The threads of light ran taut from his crown up into the ash-colored sky. And his face — his face was *struggling*. Muscles fighting muscles. The mask cracking along lines no one had drawn. His mouth worked twice before words came, and when they came they were low and fast and ragged, a man shouting up through thirty feet of black water:

"*Inquisitor. Listen to me. Whatever it tells you to do at the forge — whatever it promises — the price is a vessel. It always needs a vessel. Do you understand? It cannot be destroyed and it cannot be free, it can only be* worn —"

The threads flared white. Aldric's spine straightened like a snapped bow. The struggle drained out of his face between one heartbeat and the next, leaving the smooth tallow calm, and when he spoke again his voice was mild and kingly and terrible.

"General Voss. Make camp. At moonrise — such as it is — bring my guard down to the gates. It is time to greet our guests."

He turned and walked back to his litter, unhurried, strings singing faintly in the wind like a harp touched by a dead player's hand.

Voss sat her horse a long moment, nails buried in her palms, the king's broken warning ringing in her skull alongside the vast slow ticking under the earth. *The price is a vessel.* She looked down at the gates, at the small figures gathered there, at the priest with his arms open and the thread of light ending in his chest — and saw, now that she knew to look, that the thread did not end there. It continued. Faint as breath on a mirror, it ran from the priest to the girl beside him, and from the girl it split, four ways, into the dark.

Five vessels, then. Or four. Or one.

The Ember whispered through her scars, soft as a lover, patient as rot: *Soon, Maren. Soon you may rest.*

Down in the valley, the great gates of Ironhollow began, for the first time in three hundred years, to open.
