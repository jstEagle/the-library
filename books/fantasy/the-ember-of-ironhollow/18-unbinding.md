## 18. Unbinding

The stairs down to the forge-heart had been cut for someone taller than Kestrel, or perhaps for something that did not walk the way people walked. The steps were too shallow by half, each one a half-measure, so that descending felt less like walking than like sinking — a slow submersion into stone that grew warmer with every turn of the spiral.

Behind her, boots echoed. Tomas first, heavy and deliberate, his sword wrapped in cloth so it would not sing against the walls. Then Wren, whose breathing had gone quick and shallow since they'd passed the last of the archive doors. Ivo came last, silent as spilled ink, and Kestrel did not let herself think about why she'd chosen to keep him at her back where she could hear him best.

The heat arrived before the light. It pressed up through the soles of her boots like a heartbeat, like a second pulse laid over her own and slightly out of time, so that her body kept trying to reconcile the two rhythms and failing. Sweat beaded at her hairline. The air tasted of iron and hot ash and, beneath both, something sweeter — woodsmoke from a hearth in a house she had not stood inside since she was nine years old.

*It knows what you miss,* Brann's voice said in her memory. *That's how it gets its hooks in.*

*Then it can miss me right back,* she answered him, silently, and kept descending.

The last stair opened without warning into a cavern that swallowed sound. Kestrel stepped out onto a gallery of black stone and stopped, because there was nothing in her smuggler's catalogue of wonders — no vaulted vault, no dragon-hoard, no cathedral of the old faiths — that had prepared her for this.

The forge-heart was a bowl of rock half a mile across, and at its center burned the Ember.

Not fire. Fire moved, leapt, died. This *stood* — a column of light the size of an ancient oak, gold shot through with veins of deep red, rooted in a bed of coals that had not cooled in three hundred years. It gave off no smoke. It made no crackle. It simply burned, patient as a debt, and the light it cast did not fall away into shadow at the edges of the cavern but hung in the air instead, thick as honey, so that every surface — the gallery rail, the rusted chains taller than a man, the great anvil-block of fused glass at the column's base — seemed lit from within.

And above it all, impossibly, through three hundred feet of mountain: stars.

Kestrel's breath caught. She had seen them frozen like this once before, at the gates — the sky caught mid-vanish, constellations hanging like flies in amber. But here, above the thing that had eaten them, the stars burned whole and bright, reflected in the Ember's light the way a face is reflected in the eye of the thing watching it.

"It keeps them," Wren whispered. She had come up beside Kestrel at the rail, and her scholar's hands gripped the stone until her knuckles went white. "All of them. Every star it swallowed, it didn't destroy them, it — oh, saints, it's *wearing* them." Her voice cracked on the last word. "Kestrel, look at the pattern. That's not reflection. That's a cage. It built itself a ceiling out of the sky."

"Can you read the floor?" Kestrel asked quietly.

Wren tore her eyes upward and looked down. The bowl of the cavern was inscribed — rings upon rings of script, some in letters Wren had spent her disgraced career proving existed, some older than letters at all, grooves cut deep and filled with metal that still shone after centuries. At the center, around the base of the Ember, the rings tightened into a spiral, and at the spiral's heart lay a depression shaped almost — *almost* — like a person lying down.

Kestrel had known it would be there. Brann's letter had told her, in the cipher only a Sandoval could read, in words her mother had chosen three hundred years too late to soften: *the keeper lies down in the light.*

She had known. Knowing had not prepared her for the shape of it. The depression was exactly her height.

"Well," said Tomas, behind her, in the flat voice he used when feeling too much, "I've seen worse chapels."

"You haven't," said Ivo.

"No," Tomas admitted. "I haven't."

* * *

They made camp at the gallery's edge because there was no other choice; the rite required preparation, and Wren insisted — demanded, with the full brittle authority of her ruined academic career — that the binding-words be transcribed and checked three times before anyone spoke them aloud in this place. So they worked by the Ember's light, which never flickered and never dimmed and never, ever let them forget it was watching.

Kestrel copied the final ring of the rite onto the back of Brann's letter while Wren recited translations under her breath. The old ward-scholar had gone quiet in the way she went quiet when she was afraid — more precise, more clipped, each sentence a small fortification.

"The vessel clause is here," Wren said, for the third time, tapping the page. "I want you to hear it again. *The binding requires a living vessel; the unbinding requires the vessel's keeping.* That's the literal translation. There's ambiguity in the second phrase — *keeping* could mean guarding, could mean holding, could mean—"

"Wren."

"—could mean a great many things, and I will not have you walk down those stairs on a mistranslation—"

"*Wren.*" Kestrel set down the pen. "I heard you the first two times. Go check the outer rings. If the spiral's damaged anywhere, I need to know before I'm standing in it."

Wren held her gaze a moment too long — long enough that Kestrel saw the suspicion kindle there, the scholar's instinct for a text that wasn't saying everything — and then, mercifully, turned away toward the far arc of the gallery with her lantern and her notebooks.

Tomas sat sharpening a knife he did not need to sharpen. He had barely spoken since the gates. When he did speak now, he didn't look up.

"My brother used to say the crown was heavy," he said. "Everyone thought he meant it as a figure of speech. Kings say things like that. But he'd touch the back of his neck when he said it, right here—" he touched the place below his own skull "—like something was sitting on him. I should have understood. I was *there*, Kestrel. I saw what it was doing to him and I let them call me mad rather than keep shouting." The whetstone paused. "Whatever happens down there, I want you to know I'm not letting anything else get called mad. Or lost. Or left behind."

"I know," Kestrel said.

Because that was the cruelty of it. That was the knife-edge she'd been walking since the caves, since the night by the flooded pool when she'd confessed about the voice and Wren had looked at her with such gentle, terrible understanding. They would not let her do this alone. They would try to stop her. And if they learned the truth —

She looked down at the rite again, at the line Wren had translated three times, and at the smaller annotation beneath it in her mother's hand, in cipher, which Wren could not read and which Kestrel had not offered to share:

*The vessel does not survive the keeping. Tell no one who would carry it for you. This is the last mercy a keeper may give.*

Her mother had lied to someone, once. Had walked into some forge or shrine or doorway with a smile fixed in place, and Kestrel had grown up calling that woman an abandoner, a coward, a woman who chose the road over her daughter. All those years of anger, and the truth was that her mother had been giving her the only gift left to give: a childhood free of the knowing.

Kestrel closed her eyes. Below, the Ember burned. In her blood, faint as a pulse through a wall, it sang.

*I can give them back,* it murmured, in the voice it always wore for her now — her father's voice, roughened by pipe-smoke, warm with the laugh she'd forgotten until the Ember reminded her. *Your guild hall, rebuilt brick by brick. Old Brann young again, hale, telling his terrible jokes. Every name on your dead ledger walking back through the door. You have only to stop fighting what you are, little keeper. You were made for this. You were bred for this. Lie down in the light and be loved.*

"That's the problem with you," Kestrel whispered, so softly even Ivo, ten feet away, could not have caught it. "You keep offering me everything except the one thing I actually want."

*And what is that?*

She watched Tomas sharpening his knife. Watched Wren bent over her rings of ancient text, muttering, alive with purpose for the first time since her disgrace. Watched Ivo sitting apart with his broken god's medallion in his hands, praying to nothing, or to everyone.

"Them," she said. "Tomorrow morning. And the morning after that. You can't give me mornings. Nobody can. They're not for sale."

For the first time since the night the stars went out, the voice in her blood had nothing to say.

* * *

They finished the preparations an hour past midnight, if midnight still meant anything in a land without stars. Wren chalked the activation-glyphs along the gallery rail. Tomas cleared the descent path and tested each step down to the anvil-floor with his weight, cursing the shallow cuts. Ivo unpacked the rite-oil from Brann's kit and anointed the four anchor-points of the spiral, moving with the unconscious grace of a man who had once done this every day of his life in temples grander than this cavern and holier than anything the Ember had ever touched.

And Kestrel stood at the top of the last stair, dressed in her guild leathers with her mother's letter folded over her heart, and rehearsed her lie.

It had to be small. That was the trick of smuggling — the best false bottom was barely false at all, just true enough to bear weight. She could not lie about everything. She could only lie about the one thing.

"Kestrel." Wren came to stand beside her, hugging her elbows against the heat. Below them the Ember's light pooled and swayed. "When you're down there, and the rite takes hold — the texts say the vessel feels everything. Every thread of it. Three centuries of what it's eaten. The wards. The guilds. The king." Her voice dropped. "You don't have to be brave about it with us. Whatever it does to you, we'll be right here. We'll pull you back."

"I know," Kestrel said, and found, to her horror, that she meant it, and that meaning it made the lie heavier, not lighter. "Wren — listen to me. Once I start the words, the spiral locks. No one crosses the outer ring until it's done. Not for any reason. If you come down the stairs mid-rite, the binding could catch *you*. Do you understand? Whatever you see, whatever happens — you stay behind that chalk line. Promise me."

Wren frowned. "That's not in any text I've read."

"It's in mine." She tapped her temple. "Keeper's knowledge. Family business."

It was the first outright lie she'd told them, and it slid home clean as a pick in a lock, because of course Wren believed it — Wren had spent her life being locked out of exactly that kind of inherited knowledge, and her hunger to finally be trusted with its edges was the softest spot she had. Kestrel hated herself with a thoroughness that surprised her. Then she put the self-hatred away, the way her mother must have, the way every keeper must have, and smiled.

"One hour," she said. "However long it takes. Keep the line."

Tomas embraced her first, brief and crushing, his beard rough against her ear. "Aldric is still in there," he said. "If there's any way — any way at all —"

"I'll find him," she promised, and that much was true.

Ivo took her hand in both of his, priest-fashion, though he had no church to bless her with. His eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "I have buried one faith," he said. "I find I cannot bury another. Come back up these stairs, Kestrel Sandoval. That is my whole sermon."

"Amen," she said, and stepped past them, and went down.

* * *

The anvil-floor was hot enough that the air shimmered. Up close, the Ember was worse than it had been from the gallery — the light had texture, layers, depth, like looking into a bonfire and seeing, briefly, faces. She tried not to look. She failed. There were faces in it. Some of them she knew.

The spiral depression waited at its base, worn smooth as a riverbed, shaped by three hundred years of waiting for a body to fill it.

Kestrel knelt at the first anchor-point and began the words.

They came easier than they should have — in the old tongue she'd never been taught, syllables rising out of her blood like silt stirred in water, and she understood suddenly that this was the last inheritance, the final layer of the family business: the rite lived in the Sandoval line the way songs live in throats. Her mother had spoken these words. Her grandmother. Thirty generations of keepers, lying down in the light so the kingdom could keep its sky.

The first ring lit. Gold fire ran the groove like dawn running along a mountaintop, and the Ember shuddered — actually shuddered, the column of light bending away from her like grass from wind.

*No,* it said, and for the first time it did not wear her father's voice. It wore its own: vast, cracked, ancient, and thin with fear. *Little keeper. Little thief. We can talk. We can trade. Name your price —*

"You already named mine," Kestrel said, and spoke the second ring.

The second ring lit. The heat doubled. Somewhere above, muffled through stone, she heard Wren shout something — alarm, maybe, or triumph — and Tomas's answering bellow, and she fixed her eyes on the spiral's heart and did not look up, because if she looked up she would see their faces, and she was not strong enough for their faces.

*We showed you your father,* the Ember said, and its voice frayed at the edges. *We can show you his death. We can show you it was quick. Is that not worth —*

Third ring. The light climbed her arms like warm water. She could feel it now, all of it, the way Wren had warned — three centuries pouring into the channels of her, the wards' shattering, the guild halls burning, the hollowed king, the eaten stars, grief beyond counting, and underneath the grief, everywhere, everywhere, the wanting. Everything it had ever been offered. Everyone who had ever traded a piece of themselves for a wish.

*Stop,* said the Ember. *Please.*

It begged. A thing older than the crown, older than Ironhollow, begging a smuggler in borrowed leathers kneeling in its own forge. And Kestrel Sandoval, who had spent her whole life taking what was behind locked doors, felt the last ring rise to her lips and understood, with perfect clarity, the thing she would carry into the light with her:

It was not a monster. It was a prisoner that had become its own prison. And she was the jailer, and the key, and the door.

"I'm not trading," she said gently. "I'm keeping."

She spoke the fourth ring.

The world went white. The spiral blazed, the chalk line on the gallery above flared like a struck match, and the binding closed over her like a hand closing over a candle-flame — and in the last instant before the light took her wholly, Kestrel heard two things at once, from two directions, neither of them the Ember.

From above, tearing through stone and silence, a scream that was not Wren's and not Tomas's — a man's voice, ragged and royal and *awake*, screaming down through three hundred feet of mountain and three hundred years of hollow dark:

*"NO — girl — don't — I won't be freed into another cage — TAKE MY HAND INSTEAD—"*

And from the tunnels behind her, distant but coming closer, the unmistakable cadence of marching boots — hundreds of them — and a voice like a drawn blade calling orders in the dark.

The crown was coming down the mountain.

Kestrel lay back into the light, and the light closed over her like water, like wings, like home.
