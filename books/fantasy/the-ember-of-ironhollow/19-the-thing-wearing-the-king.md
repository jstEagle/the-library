## 19. The Thing Wearing the King

Aldric had not dreamed in nine years.

He had thought about that, sometimes, in the small stone rooms of himself that still belonged to him — the memory of a childhood bedroom in the summer palace, the smell of cut grass through an open window, the particular weight of sleep after a long day of riding. Dreams had been the last thing to go. The Ember had taken them first, actually, before it took his hands, his voice, his name. It had eaten his dreams the way a fire eats the softest wood: quietly, completely, and without any sound he could protest.

So when the dream came back, he did not at first understand what it was.

He was standing in a corridor of white light, and somewhere ahead of him a woman was burning.

Not dying. Burning the way a lamp burns — steadily, purposefully, giving off light because she was being consumed and had chosen it. He knew her face. He had seen it three days ago, or three years ago, or perhaps only moments ago; time inside the crown was a corridor with no doors. The smuggler girl. The Sandoval girl. She stood in a circle of forge-light with her arms open and her head bowed, and around her the air itself was coming apart like wet paper, and she was saying something he could not hear, and the thing that wore him *screamed*.

It screamed through his mouth, wherever his mouth was. It screamed through the roots of him, through the hollowed channels where his soul had been, and for one impossible instant the scream tore the walls of his prison wide — and Aldric fell forward into himself like a man falling into cold water after years in a desert.

He was awake. He was *in*. His hands were his hands. They were raised above his head, bound in threads of light that ran up through the shattered roof of Ironhollow's great forge-hall and out into a sky he could not see, and they were not his anymore than the crown was his, but he could feel them, feel the ache in his shoulders, feel the stone under his knees, feel the heat of the forge rising through the floor like a heartbeat.

And he could hear her.

The girl's voice came thin and clear through the light, reciting words in a language older than the crown, older than the wards, older than the kingdom his grandfather's grandfather had built on top of all of this and called theirs. And beneath her voice, beneath everything, the Ember was afraid.

Aldric knew its fear the way a man knows the fear of a wolf he has lived beside for nine years. It had never been afraid before. It had been patient, and hungry, and vast, and occasionally — when some part of him surfaced too far, when he had clawed too hard at the walls — it had been *amused*. But now it poured itself down the threads of light toward the burning woman and found something there it could not drink, and its terror rang through every hollow place in him like a bell struck in a tomb.

*No,* it said, in the voice it used for him, which was his own voice, worn smooth as river stone. *No, no, this is not the bargain, this is not—*

"Let her go."

His mouth. His voice. His words.

For nine years he had not spoken. He had screamed inside the crown until screaming became silence, and silence became sleep without dreams, and sleep became the long gray nothing in which the thing wore him out into the world like a glove wears a hand. And now, with the last of whatever he was, with strength he did not have and could not account for, King Aldric the Third of Thornfell opened eyes that had been windows with no one behind them and spoke aloud:

"*Let her go.*"

The light shuddered.

*You are nothing,* the Ember said, and there was panic in it now, real panic, the first honest thing it had ever said to him. *You are a coat I have worn so long you have forgotten you were ever a man. You cannot even hold this moment. You will be gone before she finishes the next line of her little song.*

"Then I will spend it," Aldric said, "on her."

He did not know where the strength came from. Later — if there was a later — he would wonder. Perhaps it was the girl herself, the keeper's blood, the old lock finally turning. Perhaps it was simply that a man can carry anything for nine years if someone, once, tells him the carrying has meant something. But he felt the hollow places in him filling, not with the Ember but with *himself*, with everything it had drunk and stored and hoarded the way a miser hoards coin — his mother's laugh, the weight of a sword hilt, the taste of honeyed wine at his coronation, the faces of the councilmen he had sentenced to death with a nod he had not chosen to give. All of it, rising.

He turned his head. His neck obeyed him. Below the dais, in the ruined nave of the forge-hall, soldiers knelt in ranks — Voss's vanguard, brought down the mountain in the night, their faces slack with the same gray nothing he knew from the inside. Between them, held upright by two guards whose grips were more ritual than necessity, hung a figure in a defrocked priest's cassock, head lolling, lips moving in what might have been prayer or delirium.

"Ivo Reyes," Aldric said, and the name felt strange in his mouth, a stranger's name, a free man's name. "Priest. Listen to me."

The man's head lifted. His eyes were wrong — half here, half elsewhere, the pupils blown wide with something that was not entirely the Ember's doing. But they found the king, and they narrowed.

"You're in there," Ivo rasped. "God help us. You're actually in there."

"There is no god in this room," Aldric said. "There is me, and there is *it*, and there is a girl who is about to do what I should have done nine years ago." The threads of light around his wrists were tightening; he could feel the Ember gathering itself, coiling its attention away from its fear and back toward its oldest possession. He did not have long. He had perhaps a minute. He had perhaps less. "When this ends — however it ends — tell my brother I am sorry. Tell him I heard him. That night in the council chamber, when they dragged him out screaming about the crown — I heard every word. I was pounding on the inside of my own skull trying to reach him. Tell Tomas I heard him."

Ivo's face broke. Not dramatically — a small crack, a hairline fracture across the jaw, the kind of break that ruins a whole structure. "Your Majesty—"

"No." Aldric shook his head, and the crown's weight shifted, and for one vertiginous moment he felt the thing inside it recoil from him, press itself against the far wall of his skull like a rat in a corner. "No majesty. Majesty is what it wore. My name is Aldric. Use that."

The light flared. The girl's voice rose. And the Ember stopped being afraid and started being furious, and the fury came down the threads like a landslide, and Aldric braced himself in the last room of himself and set his feet against the door—

—and held.

Not won. Not escaped. But *held*, the way a dam holds, the way a dying man holds a door against soldiers, knowing the holding is the whole of the victory and the victory is measured in seconds. The rite needed time. The girl needed time. He would buy it with the only coin he had left.

"Kestrel Sandoval," he shouted, and his voice cracked across the forge-hall like a whip, raw and human and *loud*, the first loud thing the hall had heard in three hundred years. "Whatever it offers you — whatever it shows you — it lies by telling the truth! It gives you exactly what you want until there is no *you* left to want it! I know! I wished for a kingdom worth ruling, and it gave me one, and it ate me in the eating!"

Below him, the kneeling soldiers stirred. Somewhere in the ranks, a man began, very softly, to weep.

"Hold the door," Aldric said, to her, to all of them, to no one. "Hold the door. I'll hold mine."

The Ember came for him then, all of it at once, a flood pouring backward into the vessel it had thought was empty — and Aldric closed his eyes, and smiled, and held.

* * *

Maren Voss had learned, over eleven years of service, to read the Ember's moods the way sailors read weather. It did not speak to her often — she was not crown-bound, only scar-bound, marked along the left side of her body in ridges of pale tissue that ached when the thing in the king was pleased and burned when it was angry. Tonight it was incandescent. Tonight her scars felt like brands pulled fresh from the fire, and she stood at the head of her vanguard in the upper camp above Ironhollow with her jaw clenched so hard her teeth ached, watching the mountain breathe light.

Something was going wrong below. Something was going gloriously, catastrophically wrong, and the Ember's fury was pouring up through the ground and through her scars and into the hundred and forty soldiers she had marched down from Graywater, and they were all of them feeling it, and none of them could name it.

She had felt the crack come an hour ago. A single moment — she had been reviewing the watch rotations by lantern-light, her adjutant reading names aloud — and between one name and the next, the world had gone *quiet*. No whisper behind her eyes. No pressure of the vast attention that had leaned on her since the day General Korran had pressed the brand-iron to her shoulder and spoken words in the old tongue. Just Maren, alone in her own skull, holding a list of names, and the sudden vertiginous horror of realizing she could not remember writing the order that had burned the granaries at Millbrook.

It had lasted four minutes. She had spent them shaking, silently, while her adjutant watched her with concern and asked twice if she was well. Then the pressure had returned, heavier than before, punitive, and the whisper had resumed its litany of duty, duty, the kingdom, the crown, the *hunger* — and she had straightened her spine and given the order to advance, and hated herself with a clarity that was almost a comfort.

But now. Now the mountain was screaming light, and her scars were on fire, and the Ember's attention was *elsewhere* — wholly elsewhere, wrenched downward into the deeps of Ironhollow by whatever the smuggler girl was doing — and Maren Voss stood alone in her own head for the second time in eleven years, and this time she did not waste it shaking.

"Captain Dree," she said. Her voice came out level. Eleven years of practice at level. "How many of our people took the crown's oath, and how many took mine?"

Dree blinked. He was a good soldier, Dree, a miller's son from the Ashvale marches, and his face in the lantern light wore the particular blankness she had stopped noticing years ago and now saw everywhere at once — the gray look, the hollow look, the look of men whose obedience ran deeper than their thoughts. "Yours, General. All of them. The crown's guard stayed in the capital."

"The crown's guard," she repeated. "And the Inquisition's wardens?"

"A dozen. Camped on the east ridge."

"And if I ordered you to arrest them?"

The pause was a hair too long. "Those would be treasonous orders, General."

"Yes," Maren said softly. "They would. Answer the question."

And Captain Dree looked at her, and something moved behind his eyes — something frightened and waking, a man surfacing from deep water — and he said, "I would follow them, General. God forgive me. We'd all follow them."

She looked out across the camp. Two hundred fires. Fourteen hundred men and women who had marched for her because she was Voss, the Hammer of the Marches, the general who fed her troops before she fed herself, who buried her dead with their names spoken aloud — and who had also, in the Ember's service, razed four villages, executed ninety-one smugglers, and signed the levy orders that had emptied the borderlands of every child over fourteen. She had told herself the crown commanded it. She had told herself the whispers were conscience. She had told herself so many things, for so many years, in a voice that was not entirely her own.

Below her, the mountain flared again, and this time the light was different — gold shot through with red, the color of a forge, the color of the old banners in the histories, the color of the kingdom *before*. And through her scars she felt the Ember shriek, felt it thrash against something, felt — unmistakably, impossibly — its *fear*.

It could be hurt. It could be fought. It could, perhaps, be *unmade*.

"General?" Dree said. "Your orders for the descent?"

Maren Voss looked at the lantern flame for a long moment. Then she reached up, slowly, and touched the oldest scar, the one that ran from her collarbone to her jaw, the mark she had stopped seeing in mirrors because seeing it meant remembering the day she had chosen this.

"Dree," she said, "when you were a boy, did your father ever take you to the Gilding Fair in Vellmark? The one where they float the lanterns on the canal?"

He stared at her. "...Every year, General. Until the levies."

"Do you remember what you wished for? Over the lantern?"

Another pause. Longer. When he answered, his voice had changed — roughened, unsteady, the voice of a man hearing himself speak for the first time in years. "That the mill wouldn't fail. That my father wouldn't—" He stopped. His hand went to his chest, pressing flat, as though holding something in. "General, why are we here? Why are we *here*?"

"Because I led us," Maren said. "And I was led. And tonight, for about an hour, I intend to be neither." She drew her sword — not the ceremonial blade, the plain one, the one she'd carried since she was nineteen — and turned to face the assembled staff, and raised her voice to the register that had carried across a dozen battlefields.

"Officers! To me!"

They came at a run, twenty of them, hollow-eyed and lantern-lit, and she watched the gray look on their faces flicker as the Ember's attention strained elsewhere, watched the cracks spread through them like frost across a window. She had perhaps minutes. Perhaps less. The thing below would notice eventually; it always noticed; it had eyes in the back of her scars.

"Inquisitor Halven and twelve wardens are encamped on the east ridge," she said. "By dawn, they will have orders to execute every person taken alive in Ironhollow — the priest, the scholars, anyone the crown's guard finds below. I have seen those orders drafted. I have *carried* those orders before." Her voice did not shake. She was proud of that, in a distant, disconnected way, proud as one is proud of a stranger. "Tonight I am countermanding them. Tonight the Fourth Vanguard arrests the Inquisition. Anyone who cannot follow that order — step out of the line now, and no harm will come to you."

Silence. Wind off the mountain. The flare of the forge-light below, pulsing now like a heartbeat running too fast.

And then Captain Dree stepped forward, drew his sword, and turned to face the east ridge.

"For the mill," he said, absurdly, terribly, his voice cracking on the word, and a hundred voices took it up — *for the mill, for the march, for the names, for the names* — and the Fourth Vanguard of the usurper's army turned its blades upon its masters with a roar that rolled down the mountainside and into the gates of Ironhollow.

Maren Voss stood at the head of them and felt, for the first time in eleven years, the scars on her body go quiet.

It lasted, of course, exactly as long as it lasted. She could already feel the pressure rebuilding behind her eyes, the vast attention wrenching itself back from the depths, the whisper gathering itself into a word — *traitor, traitor, traitor* — and she knew what would come for her, and she found she did not care. Some debts you pay on your feet.

"Move!" she bellowed. "East ridge! Take them alive if you can, and if you can't—"

Below her, the mountain went white.

Not flare — *white*, total, the forge-light blowing upward through three centuries of sealed stone like water through a broken dam, and in the heart of it Maren saw — or dreamed she saw, or would swear to her dying day she saw — five figures standing in a ring around a single point of burning, holding hands, holding *on*, and the sky above Ironhollow tearing open like a seam.

The first star came back directly overhead.

It hung there in the torn dark, small and fierce and impossibly blue, and across the camps of fourteen thousand soldiers, men and women dropped their weapons and fell to their knees and pointed upward and *wept*, and Maren Voss felt the whisper behind her eyes dissolve mid-word like sugar in rain.

The crown, she understood, distantly, watching the star burn — the crown had just come down.

And somewhere beneath her feet, in the deeps of Ironhollow, a smuggler's daughter was either dying or refusing to, and both of those things, somehow, were the same victory.

"To the ridge," she said quietly, and the army moved.
