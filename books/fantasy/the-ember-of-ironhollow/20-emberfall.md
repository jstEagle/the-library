## 20. Emberfall

The forge-light took her the way water takes a stone.

Kestrel had expected fire. She had braced for pain, for the scream she'd been told to expect from every martyr's story Old Brann ever told her by the guttering light of smuggler-lamps. But the Ember did not burn. It *poured* — up through the soles of her boots, along the channels of her blood like a river finding its old bed, and it was warm, gods help her, it was warm the way a house is warm, the way a hand held in the dark is warm.

*You came,* said the voice that had been whispering since the night the stars went out. *Little keeper. Little key. You came home.*

She stood in the heart of the forge beneath Ironhollow, in the bowl of a basin carved before the crown existed, before the kingdom, maybe before the word for kingdom. The walls around her were veined with metal that still remembered being molten — rivers of iron frozen mid-flow three centuries ago, and threaded through them all, the faint gold shimmer of wards laid by hands with her same blood. Her mother's hands. Her mother's mother's. Generations of Sandovals had walked down these stairs, and every one of them had walked back up alone or not at all.

*I can give you everything,* the Ember murmured, and its voice was her mother's now, and that was the cruelest thing it had done yet. *The guild hall rebuilt stone by stone. Brann young again, laughing on the docks of Vellmark. Every name you've ever mourned, breathing. I only ask for a vessel. I only ask for you.*

"I know what you ask," Kestrel said aloud, and her voice echoed strangely in the basin, doubled and tripled, as if the forge itself were learning to speak. "I know exactly what you ask. That's why I'm here."

She spread her arms. The rite was already moving through her — Wren's chalk-lines burning silver on the floor, the words of unbinding rising in her throat in a language older than the free cities, older than the crown. She could feel the shape of the binding closing around her like a door swinging shut. It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. It felt like being *wanted*. Like belonging. Like the family she'd never had opening its arms, and all she had to do was stop being Kestrel Sandoval and become instead an eternal, obedient warmth.

*No one will ever leave you again,* said the voice in her mother's timbre. *No one will ever die on you again.*

"That's how you got Aldric," she whispered. "Isn't it? He just wanted to be loved without conditions."

The Ember said nothing, which was answer enough.

She thought of Tomas above, fighting Ivo at the threshold. She thought of Wren's rigged wards burning somewhere in the dark. She thought of Voss turning her soldiers against their own guard, of Aldric screaming through the link like a man drowning behind glass. And she thought: *someone has to hold this door.* Her whole life she'd carried things across borders in the dark — silk, salt, secrets, stolen relics — and here, at the end, the cargo was herself.

The binding closed.

Light took her. Not white — ember-colored, deep orange shot through with gold, the color of the last coal in a dying hearth, the color of every lamp she'd ever lit to guide a caravan home. She felt herself spreading, thinning, becoming vast and patient and terribly, terribly lonely —

— and then three sets of hands seized her.

* * *

Later, she would try to describe it and fail. There were no words in any language Wren knew, and Wren knew dead ones. But in the moment itself, Kestrel understood it bodily, instantly, the way you understand a rope going taut.

Tomas had come down the stairs with Ivo half-restrained and half-free, weeping and fighting himself, and Wren had abandoned the rigged wards because rigging wards against the Ember was pointless if the Ember simply swallowed Kestrel whole. Three people who loved her had looked at the forge-light eating their friend and made the same decision at the same instant, without discussion, the way her old crews used to move when a ward collapsed mid-run — no orders, no debate, just bodies answering the emergency together.

They stepped into the light and took hold of her.

"NO—" Kestrel tried to say, and couldn't, because she no longer quite had a mouth. *Let go. Let go, you idiots, it needs a vessel, it'll take you—*

"No," Tomas said aloud, gripping her wrist with both hands, his knuckles white, his eyes streaming tears he didn't seem to notice. "It doesn't need *a* vessel. Wren?"

"The rite says 'the binder,'" Wren shouted over the roar of the light. Their hair was lifting off their shoulders, their chalk-stained fingers hooked into Kestrel's belt. "It says *the* binder! Singular! Grammar, you ancient furnace — grammar is not destiny!" And they laughed, wild and terrified, the laugh of a scholar who has just bet their life on a footnote.

Ivo's hands found her shoulders. His lips were moving — prayer, or apology, or both. "I tried to give you to it," he choked. "Gods forgive me, it never let me go, it wore me like a glove — but I am *here*, Kestrel, I am here now, and I will not let it have you *alone*." His grip tightened. "That's the faith I lost. Not in heaven. In this. In holding on."

The Ember screamed.

It was not a sound; it was a *rearrangement*, the whole forge-heart shuddering as the thing that had been pouring into one soul found itself poured into four. Kestrel felt it thrash against the shape of them — felt it probe Tomas and find his hunger for vengeance, his hunger for a brother restored, and find those hungers *held*, named, refused. Felt it slide toward Ivo and meet a man who had already been hollowed once and knew the taste of it and gagged on it. Felt it reach for Wren and find a mind that had spent ten years in a cellar studying containment and had opinions, extensive cited opinions, about improper bindings.

And underneath all of it, holding them together like a keel holds a ship, Kestrel felt something she had never once felt in her life as a smuggler, an orphan, a survivor:

She was not carrying this alone.

"*Unbind,*" she gasped, and the word came out in four voices, hers and Tomas's and Ivo's and Wren's braided into one, and the rite — confused, delighted, starved for exactly this — *obeyed*.

* * *

The crown cracked first.

Kestrel felt it happen from inside, the way you feel a branch break in a distant wood — a sharp wrong note in the great web of the Ember's feeding. For three hundred years the Ember had drunk the kingdom through that circlet of iron and gold, sipping Aldric's will, sipping the wards' strength, sipping smuggler-magic generation by generation, always careful, always slow. Now the drinking reversed. Now it *drained*.

Above them — she knew it without seeing it, felt it in the new shared sense flooding her — King Aldric collapsed in the arms of Voss's mutinous soldiers, and the hollowed emptiness behind his eyes filled like a well refinding its spring. Somewhere in the camp above Ironhollow, hundreds of half-touched soldiers staggered as the whispering in their scars went suddenly, blessedly silent. Maren Voss fell to her knees in the mud and began, witnesses would later swear, to laugh and weep in equal measure.

And the sky—

Oh, the sky.

Kestrel felt the stars reignite the way you feel sunrise on your eyelids. One by one, then in cascades, then all at once, the lights that had been snuffed out on the worst night of her life came back — and they came back *angry*, blazing, as if they'd been holding their breath for a year and meant to make up for it. The darkness that had hunted her caravans, that had followed them through the mountain passes like weather with intent, tore away from the world like a sheet ripped off furniture in an empty house.

Below Ironhollow, the vanguard of the usurper's army looked up.

Kestrel saw it through a hundred borrowed pairs of eyes in the last instant of her shared sight: thousands of faces tilting back, thousands of soldiers who had marched under a starless black ceiling for months, who had told themselves the dark was temporary, tactical, survivable — seeing the constellations of their childhoods blaze back overhead. The Hunter. The Weaver's Loom. The Salt Road of lights that sailors steered by and smugglers swore by and lovers wished on.

Some of them cheered. Some of them dropped their weapons. Some simply sat down where they stood, in the trampled snow, and looked at the sky until their officers stopped bothering to shout at them.

An army cannot fight under a miracle. It can barely even stand.

* * *

Then the four-way grip broke, gently, like hands releasing after a long dance, and Kestrel fell.

She hit the floor of the forge-basin on her side, cheek against cold iron, and lay there gasping like a landed fish. Everything hurt. That was the first miracle — everything *hurt*, which meant she had a body left to hurt with. But it was a smaller body than the one she'd walked down the stairs with. She could feel it. Something vast had been in her and was no longer, and the space it left ached like a tooth pulled, like a room emptied of furniture, like a name crossed off a ledger.

But she was *alive*. Diminished. Alive.

"Kestrel." Tomas's face swam into view above her, soot-streaked, tear-tracked, younger-looking than she'd ever seen him. "Kestrel, look at me. Stay with me."

"'m looking," she managed. "You look terrible."

He laughed — a broken, sobbing, magnificent laugh — and gathered her up against his chest, and then there were more arms, Wren collapsing against her other side chattering something about resonance coefficients and *did you feel the harmonic split, four-fold distribution, it actually WORKED*, and Ivo kneeling with his forehead pressed to her hand, murmuring fragments in liturgical cadence, though whether he was praying to a god or to the three people in front of him, Kestrel couldn't tell and suspected he couldn't either.

"You lied to us," Wren said, pulling back to glare at her, eyes red-rimmed and furious and shining. "You lied about the cost. We read the rite, Kestrel. We read it while you were walking down the stairs lying to our faces."

"I know."

"You were going to *die* down here. Alone. Again. After everything—"

"I know." Her voice cracked. She looked at the three of them — the defrocked priest, the disgraced scholar, the mad prince — and found she couldn't assemble anything cleverer than the truth. "I've spent my whole life making sure nobody else paid my tolls. It's the only inheritance I thought I had."

Tomas brushed the hair off her forehead, gentle as handling a wounded bird. "Well," he said thickly, "it turns out your inheritance also includes three people too stubborn to let you."

Above them, through the shattered throat of the forge-city, the stars poured down light onto Ironhollow for the first time in three centuries. The frozen moment at the gates — the stars caught mid-vanish like a held breath — had released. Snow glittered. Far below, the armies were dissolving into campfires and confusion and, here and there, spontaneous singing, because soldiers are soldiers in any age and a returned sky makes even killers sentimental.

Kestrel started to laugh. It hurt her ribs and she kept doing it anyway, and then Tomas was laughing, and Wren, and finally even Ivo, a rusty sound like a gate opening after years shut, and the four of them sat in the cooling heart of the greatest forge in the world, filthy and bleeding and diminished, laughing under the returning stars.

* * *

They climbed out slowly. Kestrel's legs shook; Tomas carried her piggyback for the last stretch of stairs, refusing all offers otherwise, and she was too tired to pretend she minded. At the threshold where Ivo had betrayed her — where he had seized her arm with hollow-eyed purpose and begun dragging her toward the crown's men — he stopped, and looked at the spot, and could not go forward.

Kestrel slid down off Tomas's back, steadied herself against the wall, and put her hand in Ivo's.

"You came back," she said. "That's the part I'm keeping."

His jaw worked. "I don't know if it will always be the part that wins," he said quietly. "What it put in me — the crack it used — I felt it close when we bound it, but cracks scar, Kestrel. Scars remember."

"Then we watch each other's scars," she said. "All of us. That's the job now. That's the whole job."

Wren, ahead of them at the great gates, made a strangled noise. They were staring upward through the gap in Ironhollow's roof, at the sky, at the stars — and at something else. Their face had gone scholar-pale, the particular gray of a person whose footnote has just grown teeth.

"Kestrel," they said. "The binding worked. Four vessels, distributed essence, no single point of failure — it worked *perfectly*. Which means the Ember isn't destroyed. It's *quartered*." They turned, and in the starlight their eyes were wide and bright and terrified and thrilled, all at once, the way only Wren's eyes could be. "Four shards. Four keepers' worth of vigilance. And I think — I *think* — I know where each of them settled."

Tomas exhaled slowly. "Tell me one of them isn't in the crown."

"It's in the crown," Wren said. "Or what's left of it. All four shards flew home to the things they'd been feeding on longest." A pause. "One of them is in a man who just got his soul back. One is in a woman kneeling in the mud outside. One is..." They swallowed. "One is in the guild ledger. In the Sandoval archive. In *your* founding book, Kestrel. Your family didn't just write the binding. They became its first shelf."

Kestrel looked down at her own hands — thinner than they'd been a month ago, scarred, steady. She thought of Brann's letter. Of her mother, dying at a door she'd never explained. Of a burned guild hall in Vellmark and a name that needed to mean something new.

"Well," she said, and heard her own voice settle into the register she used at the start of every run, the one that made frightened crews believe the road was passable. "Then we'd better get to work. We're keepers now."

Above Ironhollow, the stars burned on — and far to the north, over the road where a broken king was being carried gently down the mountain, one star flared brighter than all the rest, like a lamp lit in a window.

Like someone, somewhere, saying: *you are expected. Come home.*
