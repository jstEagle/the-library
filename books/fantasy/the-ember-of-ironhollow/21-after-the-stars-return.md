## 21. After the Stars Return

The stars came back on a Tuesday, and the world did not know what to do with them.

Tomas Aldricson stood in the ruined courtyard of Ironhollow and watched the first of them kindle — a hard white point above the eastern ridge, then another, then a hundred, then all of them at once, as if some vast hand had swept ash from a fire and found the coals still living. Below him, in the terraced camps where Voss's army had been encamped when the sky broke open, ten thousand soldiers looked up. He heard the sound they made. It was not cheering. It was smaller than that, and stranger: a long, ragged exhale, like a city-sized held breath finally released, like grief remembered all at once.

He had not realized he'd been holding his own breath for eleven days.

Beside him, Kestrel Sandoval sat wrapped in two blankets she clearly hadn't asked for, gray-faced, her hands in her lap like something borrowed. The binding had taken something out of her — Wren said it was like a river giving up a quarter of its water; the bed of her was still there, but shallower, and things moved through it differently now. She had slept for two days. When she woke, the first thing she'd done was ask about Ivo.

Ivo Reyes sat apart from all of them, near the broken gate, speaking to no one, his hands folded in his lap in the old attitude of prayer even though there was nothing left in him that prayed. He had wept, after. They had all seen it — the half-hollowed man kneeling in the forge-light, sobbing like a child while the Ember's last residue drained out of him through the shared binding, four ways, so that no single soul would be consumed. What remained in Ivo was mostly himself. Mostly. There were hours, Wren said, when the fragments came back. When he called Kestrel by his sister's name. When he stared at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger who had done terrible things with them.

They had. That was the accounting no one wanted to speak aloud. Ivo had turned at the threshold. Ivo had seized Kestrel and delivered her, half-strangling, toward the crown's men. And Ivo had wept while he did it, which was either mercy or the cruelest thing of all.

Tomas looked at the stars and thought: my brother is alive, and I don't know how to love him anymore.

* * *

Aldric abdicated nine days later, in the great hall of Vellmark, before a council assembled from every free city that still had walls and a government to stand behind them.

Tomas stood at the back. He had been offered a place at the front — *the king's brother*, the heralds kept saying, as though the word could be polished into something it wasn't — and he had refused. He had spent seven years being the mad prince, the drunk prince, the cautionary tale told in mining-town taverns, and he did not trust himself to stand in front of a crowd without wanting to burn the whole theater down. Better the back of the room. Better the shadows. He knew how to live in shadows; they were the only country that had ever granted him citizenship.

Aldric walked to the dais unaided, which was itself a kind of miracle. Three weeks ago he had been led across the mountains like a puppet on strings of light, his eyes empty windows, the Ember wearing him the way a hermit crab wears a stolen shell. Now he walked, and his eyes were his own, and that was almost worse. Because behind those eyes Tomas could see everything that had been done with them. Every levy. Every hanging. Every village burned on Maren Voss's orders with the king's seal attached. Aldric carried it all now, awake, and he carried it the way a man carries a wound he knows will never close.

He wore no crown. The crown lay on a cushion beside him, cracked clean through, the fracture running from brow to nape like a lightning scar. Wren Ashvale had examined it for two days and pronounced it dead metal — "a shell," she'd said, "the way a cicada husk is a cicada" — but no one wanted it near them, and eventually it had been locked in an iron box and sunk in Graywater, and everyone had pretended to feel better.

"My lords and ladies of the free cities." Aldric's voice carried. It had always carried; even as boys, he'd had the voice that made servants stop in doorways. "I am told the law says a king who has been enthralled may keep his throne, that what was done was not done by him. I am told the council has debated this for nine days."

He paused. His hands gripped the edge of the dais, and Tomas saw the knuckles whiten, and thought: *he would rather be anywhere else. He would rather be dead.* And also: *he is going to do this anyway,* and felt something in his chest crack along an old seam.

"I am here to tell you the law is wrong." A murmur ran the length of the hall. "I remember everything. Do you understand? I was in the crown the way a drowning man is in the water — present, screaming, unable to move my own hands. And my hands did what they did. My seal signed what it signed. Whether I willed it or not, it was my face the people obeyed, and a king who cannot answer for his face should not keep his throne." He straightened. "I abdicate. Not to my brother — he will tell you himself he is unfit, and for once in our lives we agree — but to you. To a council of the free cities. Let Thornfell be governed by many hands, so that no single head can ever again be hollowed and call it rule."

The silence afterward lasted long enough that Tomas counted his own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Then the Lord Protector of Vellmark, an old woman named Casserly with a voice like gravel in honey, rose and said, "The council accepts," and the hall broke into sound — not quite cheering, not quite weeping, that same strange exhale he'd heard on the mountain, grief and relief tangled past separating.

Afterward, in the antechamber, Tomas faced his brother for the first time since the garrison. Since the levy. Since the day he'd looked into those empty windows and understood that the monster had a hostage inside it.

"Tomas." Aldric said it carefully, the way a man steps onto ice.

"Your Majesty," Tomas said, and immediately hated himself for it.

"Don't." Aldric's composure flickered. Up close he looked terrible — hollowed at the cheeks, gray at the temples where there had been no gray before, a man aged a decade in a season. "Please. I have spent three weeks being addressed by people deciding what I am to them. I would like, just once, to be spoken to by someone who knew me before."

Tomas looked at him. Seven years of exile sat between them like a wall — the court laughing at his warnings, the cell they'd put him in, the ship they'd put him on, the bottle he'd crawled into and lived in until a smuggler with her mentor's sigil had dragged him out. He had rehearsed this conversation a thousand times in a thousand taverns. In most versions he shouted. In some he wept. In one memorable version he hit him.

What came out instead was: "I tried to warn you. Before the crown. You laughed at me, Aldric. In front of the whole court. You called it *Tomas's melancholy* and you sent me away, and if you had listened—"

"If I had listened, I would have been crowned anyway, and the Ember would have eaten me a year earlier, and you would be standing here blaming yourself instead of me." Aldric's voice was quiet. "You want someone to be guilty. I know. I have a list myself; mine is longer. But brother—" and here his composure went entirely, and Tomas saw the boy underneath, the one who used to sneak him honeycakes during sermons, "—the only person whose guilt I will accept is mine, and I have already given it to the council to weigh. Don't take it back from them. Don't take it into yourself. It doesn't fit you. It never fit you. That was always your tragedy."

Tomas stood there with his rehearsed fury dying in his throat and nowhere to put it, and finally, awkwardly, like a man setting down luggage he'd forgotten he was carrying, he embraced his brother.

Aldric shook against him. Neither of them said anything else. There was nothing else, yet. But when they parted, Aldric said, "The council means to try Voss publicly. In the square. They've asked for witnesses." A pause. "They've asked for you."

"I know," Tomas said. "She wrote to me herself."

* * *

Maren Voss surrendered on the steps of the Vellmark garrison with her sword presented hilt-first and her hands bound by her own second, because she refused to let anyone else touch the knots.

They gave her a cell in the old temple gaol — the same building, though none of them remarked on it aloud, where a defrocked priest had once hidden among relics waiting for a smuggler to break in. And on the third night, she sent for Tomas.

He went expecting a monster. He had seen her work. He had seen the levy fields outside Harrowgate, the rows of dead, the precision of it. Half-hollowed, Wren had explained once, tapping her temple — the Ember took her gradually, left enough of her running the machinery to make it efficient. A woman slowly eaten who kept serving the meal.

She sat on the cell bench with her chains loose — they'd allowed her that much dignity — and when she looked up at him, Tomas saw what he had not been prepared to see: a tired woman in her forties with a scarred face and eyes that were, unmistakably, her own.

"You came," she said. Her voice was rough, disused. "I gave it fifty-fifty."

"You're difficult to refuse. It's apparently a family trait of the Ember's servants."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. "Sit, if you're going to loom. Looming is for generals."

He sat. For a while neither spoke. Somewhere above them, water dripped through the old temple roof, patient as a clock.

"I'm not going to ask for forgiveness," Voss said at last. "I want to be very clear about that, because I know why you're here, and I know what you think you'll find. People come to cells hoping the monster will turn out to be small. I won't make myself small for your comfort. I did what I did. Some of it I chose before the hollow ever touched me, and the hollow only made the choosing easier. That's the truth, and I'll say it in the square too, if they let me speak."

"Then why send for me?"

"Because there's one thing I need done, and you're the only one who might do it." She leaned forward; the chains whispered. "The council wants to judge me by the law. Treason, atrocities, the whole litany. Fine. But the law will make it clean. It will make it *about the crown*, about orders, about the Ember — a machine malfunctioned and here is the part we punish. And the people I actually hurt — the smugglers' quarter in Vellmark that I burned, the villages on the levy roads, the families — they'll watch from behind barriers while lawyers talk." Her jaw tightened. "I don't want the law. I want the people. Bring me before them — the ones who survived me — and let them say what I owe. Judgment outside the law. Their judgment. Even if what they decide is the rope."

"And if they decide worse than the rope?"

"Then I'll have earned a mercy the law wouldn't give them."

Tomas studied her. This was the woman who had hunted Kestrel across half a kingdom. Who had interrogated prisoners with methods the records didn't name. And here she sat, asking — not begging, *asking*, with the flat pragmatism of a soldier requisitioning supplies — to be handed over to the people she'd broken.

"Why?" he said. "Truly. The hollow is out of you. You could lawyer your way to a comfortable imprisonment. Why hand yourself to a mob?"

"Because I remember what it was like inside it." For the first time her voice wavered. "Do you know what the Ember does? Everyone thinks it corrupts. It doesn't. It *grants*. It finds the want under your want and gives it to you until there's nothing left but the having. Mine was order. I grew up in a border town that burned twice before I was twelve — raiders, then tax-war, then raiders again. I wanted a world where nothing burned. And it gave me that. It gave me a kingdom where nothing would ever burn again because everything flammable would already be ash." She looked at her scarred hands. "For one hour, after your priest said my childhood name — Mira, my mother called me Mira, nobody had said it in twenty years — for one hour I was myself, and I saw the ledger. And then it closed over me again, but I left the cell unlocked, and I have been trying to earn that hour ever since. You can't earn an hour. But you can spend the rest of your life paying for it. That's what I'm asking. Let me pay where the debt is owed."

Tomas sat with it. He thought of his own list — the court that laughed, the brother who didn't listen, the years in the bottle — and how badly he had wanted someone to be guilty, and how Aldric had refused to let him carry it. Voss was doing the opposite. She was picking up a weight that wasn't hers to set down.

"I'll speak to the council," he said, standing. "I can't promise what they'll decide. Or what the people will."

"I know." She settled back against the wall. At the door he paused, and without turning around, he said: "Mira."

Behind him, very quietly, Maren Voss began to cry.

* * *

They buried Ivo's old life on a hillside above Vellmark, in a graveyard that served the small chapel of Saint Verene-of-the-Roads, patron of travelers and lost causes — a saint, Ivo noted dryly, whose cult had been suppressed forty years ago for insufficient deference to the crown, which meant the ground was cheap and the company congenial.

It was not a body they buried. Ivo was alive; diminished, fragmentary, but alive, and every morning he woke and checked, visibly, that he was still himself. What they buried was everything else. The cassock. The stole. The breviary with its water-stained pages of offices he would never again lead. The letters of defrockment, which he had kept folded against his heart for six years like a wound he couldn't stop touching. And the ring — his ordination ring, which he had pried off his finger that morning with soap and a silence that made Kestrel look away.

"The church won't take me back," he'd said, holding it. "Even if they would, I can't — I don't know what I believe anymore. I served a god who stayed silent through all of this, and then I served a thing that never stopped talking, and between the two of them I have learned that faith is just love with nowhere to put itself." He'd turned the ring in the light. "But I can't wear it and I can't throw it in a river. It deserves ground."

So they buried it, with the cassock and the breviary and the letters, in a small box Wren built from archive shelving, and Tomas — who had volunteered before anyone could assign it, because someone had to and because he was tired of speeches delivered by other people about him — read the words.

Not scripture. He didn't know any that fit. Instead he read from the guild ledger, the true ledger, the one Kestrel had found in Ironhollow's archive: the founding oath of the Sandoval line, written three hundred years ago by the first keepers. *We hold the door. We hold it together, for a door held by one hand is a door already opening. We do not hold it for glory, nor for gold, nor for the gratitude of kings. We hold it because someone must, and we are someone.*

"It seemed fitting," Tomas said, closing the book. "For a priest without a god, and a guild without a guildhall, and a prince without a kingdom. We are all of us someone. Apparently that's the whole liturgy."

Ivo laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, the first in weeks — and then wept, and Kestrel put her arm through his, and Wren pretended very hard to be interested in the gravestone inscription while crying openly, and the box went into the ground, and the earth of Saint Verene's hillside closed over a life that was ending so another could begin.

Afterward they did not go back to the city. It was evening, the kind of clear autumn evening that had become precious since the stars returned — people still stopped in the streets to look up, still pointed children skyward, still treated each cloudless night as a small resurrection. They sat on the hillside with a bottle of rough red wine Kestrel produced from somewhere (Tomas declined to ask; some professional habits deserved respect) and passed it around, and for a long time nobody said anything at all.

It was Kestrel who finally spoke.

"I've been thinking about what comes next. About the Keepers." She had the wine bottle cradled in both hands like a lantern. "The guild's refounding papers are drawn up. Four shards, four watches, roads between the free cities. Wren's drafted the wards. Ivo's agreed to train the novices — he says teaching theology to smugglers can't be harder than smuggling theology to priests. And I keep coming back to the ledger. *A door held by one hand is a door already opening.*" She looked around the circle: Wren, sharp-eyed and sleepless; Ivo, quiet and present; Tomas, still smelling faintly of grave-dirt. "That's the whole reason we're alive. Any one of us alone — I'd be ash in the forge, Ivo would be the Ember's glove, Tomas would be dead in a ditch, Wren would still be in that cellar. We only worked because there were four of us. Five," she corrected, and her voice caught, thinking of the hour in the flooded caves, of Voss's unlocked cell, of debts even justice can't settle cleanly. "Five."

"So the question," Wren said slowly, catching it, "is whether this is a guild or a family."

"Is there a difference?"

"For a Sandoval, historically? Yes. Your family held doors and died holding them, alone, generation after generation, and their children found out from ledgers." Wren's voice was gentle. "I'm asking whether we repeat that."

The wind moved through the grass. Down in the valley, Vellmark's lamps were coming on one by one, and above them the stars were fully out now, thick as spilled salt, so many that Tomas wondered how he had ever breathed under a sky without them.

"I spent seven years walking away from everything," Tomas said. "From the court, from my name, from my brother, from every obligation that ever had teeth. It's the one skill I truly mastered. And then four people dragged me out of a mining town and refused — *refused*, repeatedly, against all evidence and my best efforts — to let me walk away from them." He took the bottle, drank, passed it on. "So here is my vow, such as it is. I'm bad at staying. I know it. But I'm not going anywhere. If anyone wakes at three in the morning with the Ember whispering in whatever shallow places it left behind — and it will happen, we all know it will happen — you wake the rest of us. Nobody holds their piece alone. Nobody carries their hour alone. That's the whole liturgy, right? We are someone. Together."

"Together," Ivo said. It sounded, from him, like an amen.

"One condition," Wren said, and there was a gleam in her eye that Tomas had learned to fear slightly. "Someone has to tell Kestrel that 'keeper' is a terrible name for a smuggling-adjacent organization and we are absolutely rebranding."

"We are not rebranding," Kestrel said.

"The letterhead writes itself, Kestrel. *Keepers of the Hollow.* People will think we're a funeral society."

"People will think we're a funeral society and therefore leave us alone, which is the entire point of good branding."

They were laughing — all four of them, on a hillside over a buried cassock, laughing at letterhead under a full sky — when Ivo went suddenly still.

He was looking east, past the city walls, toward the road from the mountains. Tomas followed his gaze and saw nothing at first: dark hills, a scatter of torchlight at the gates, ordinary traffic of an autumn evening. Then he saw what Ivo saw. A rider, small with distance, moving fast, and riding the way no courier rode — leaning forward, driving the horse, as if the message were chasing them rather than carrying them.

Wren stood. "That's a Graywater saddle-mark," she said quietly. "That's one of ours. From the toll bridge watch."

The rider reached the city gate. Even from the hillside they heard the horn — three short notes, repeated, the signal Wren had designed herself for exactly one purpose: *a shard has moved.*

Kestrel was already on her feet, the laughter gone from her face, replaced by something older and harder and steadier — the look of a woman who had held a door once and knew the weight of it. She looked at each of them in turn, and no one looked away.

"Well," she said. "Keepers. It seems the door isn't finished with us."

They went down the hill together, five shadows against the lamplight, toward the gate and the rider and whatever the night had decided to ask of them next.
