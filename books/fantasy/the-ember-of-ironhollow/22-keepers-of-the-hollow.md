## 22. Keepers of the Hollow

The guild hall at Vellmark had been rebuilt on the bones of the old one, and Kestrel had insisted the charred beams stay where they'd fallen.

"Let them see it," she'd told the masons when they offered to clear the ruin away. "Anyone who swears an oath under this roof should know what happened to the last people who kept secrets here."

So the new hall rose around the old wound — pale timber and clean mortar wrapped about a blackened skeleton of rafters, like a living tree grown through a dead one. On the night of the anniversary, a year to the day since the stars had gone out over Thornfell, the hall was full for the first time. Not with smugglers. With keepers.

Kestrel stood at the back of the room, hood down, watching them come in out of the rain. Farmers' sons and fisher-daughters, a defrocked seminarian's whole graduating class gone quietly rogue, two of Wren's former students from the archive at Callowmere who'd walked three hundred miles rather than take the usurper's coin. Forty-one of them now. She'd expected a dozen, maybe. She'd expected no one.

"They're staring at you," said a voice at her shoulder — low, dry, familiar as an old scar.

"I'm staring at them."

"That's different." Ivo Reyes leaned against the doorframe beside her, his hands folded around a cup of mulled wine he hadn't touched. He still spoke in fragments sometimes, when he was tired, when the hollow places in him ached against cold weather. But there were more whole sentences every month, and tonight his voice sounded like himself. "You look like a woman about to tell forty-one people they've made a terrible mistake."

"Aren't they?"

"No." He considered it. "They've made the only mistake worth making."

* * *

The ceremony was simple, because Kestrel had written it herself and she distrusted ceremony the way she distrusted locked doors — useful, occasionally, but never to be trusted blind.

No oaths to kings. No oaths to gods. The old smuggler's creed had been *the road remembers*, and she'd kept that much, but everything else she'd burned down to the studs and rebuilt.

Wren Ashvale read the charge from a slate, because Wren had insisted that words spoken from memory could drift, and drifted words became doctrine, and doctrine became the kind of rot that had hollowed out a king. Her voice carried through the hall, precise and unadorned:

"You will watch the four shards. You will watch each other. You will watch the roads between the free cities, and the people who walk them, and you will ask nothing in return but bread and honest news. You will not hoard what you guard. You will not wield what you watch. And if ever the sky goes dark again — you will not wait for orders. You will move."

Forty-one voices repeated it back. Some of them stumbled. One boy near the front, no older than sixteen, said *wield* wrong twice and went red to the ears, and Kestrel caught herself smiling before she could stop.

Tomas Aldricson stood at her left hand during the recitation, because protocol demanded someone of royal blood witness the founding, and because Tomas had refused — loudly, drunkenly, then soberly and with real force — to let the moment pass without a reminder of why it existed.

"My brother gave a kingdom to a crown," he told the assembled keepers afterward, in the speech nobody had asked him for and everybody would remember. "I am asking you to give your vigilance to no crown at all. Watch the watchers. That includes me. That includes her." He pointed at Kestrel with the crooked finger he'd broken escaping the garrison at Graywater, and grinned his ruined grin. "*Especially* her. She lies beautifully. It's how she got me up a mountain."

Laughter rolled through the hall, and Kestrel let it, because Tomas had earned the right to make jokes about mountains, and because laughter in this room was itself a kind of oath — proof that what they were building here wasn't built on fear.

* * *

Later, when the wine was gone and the fire had settled to its red bones, the five of them climbed the hill behind the hall.

It was the same hill where Old Brann had taught her the constellations, thirty years ago, back when the guild's business was goods and the worst thing a Sandoval had to fear was a customs man with a good memory. Brann was buried at the crest now, under a cairn they'd built from the fallen chimney stones of the old hall. His marker bore no sigil and no name. Just four small stones set in a diamond, one for each shard, and a fifth stone at the center that meant nothing to anyone but her: *he kept the door.*

The rain had stopped. The clouds were tearing open in long silver rags, and through them —

Kestrel stopped walking and just looked.

The stars. All of them. A year since Emberfall, and she still couldn't see the sky without doing inventory, counting constellations like coins, checking each one against the map in her memory of the night they'd vanished. The Hunter stood where the Hunter should stand. The River ran bright from horizon to horizon. And between them, faint as breath on glass, the four new stars that weren't stars at all — the points of light where the shards slept, one in the deep vault beneath this hill, one in Ironhollow's sealed forge-heart, one carried in trust by the council at Vellmark, and one that Tomas wore on a cord around his neck and refused, absolutely refused, to give to anyone else.

"The astronomers hate those," Wren said, settling onto the grass beside Brann's cairn with a scholar's groan. "Four lights that don't chart, don't drift, don't obey anything. There's a paper going around Callowmere arguing they're atmospheric phenomena."

"What do you argue?"

"That the sky is allowed to have secrets." Wren pulled her cloak tighter. "That used to be heresy. Now it's just Tuesday."

Aldric came last up the hill, moving slowly, leaning on a staff of gray ironwood. A year ago he had been a puppet of light with a man buried alive behind his eyes; six months ago he had stood before the council of the free cities and abdicated with a speech so quiet the hall had leaned forward to hear it; and now he was simply Aldric — thinner, grayer, given to long silences, but present in a way that made every silence feel inhabited rather than hollowed. He lowered himself down beside the cairn with the care of a man who had learned exactly how heavy a body could be.

"I used to know all their names," he said, looking up. "Every star. My tutors thought it a waste of a prince's time." His mouth curved. "It was the only lesson I ever loved."

"Name some," Kestrel said.

And he did — the Weaver, the Ox, the Sisters Weeping, the Lantern at the World's Edge — his voice low and steady while the rest of them lay back in the wet grass and followed his finger from light to light. Voss would have known them too, once. Kestrel thought of her sometimes, in the work-camp at Marrow Ford where she served her sentence — a sentence she had demanded from the people she'd wronged rather than accept the council's pardon, because, she'd said, *a debt forgiven is still a debt, and I want mine paid in full.* They wrote to her. She wrote back, rarely, briefly, always in a hand that grew a little steadier with each letter. In the last one she'd asked whether the boy from the levy at Thornfell had survived. He had. He was one of the forty-one.

Kestrel looked around the hilltop — the scholar arguing gently with the ex-king about whether the Lantern was one star or a cluster; the priest with his untouched wine, humming an old hymn under his breath, not praying, just remembering; the prince wearing a shard of a devouring god like a common pendant, guarding it with his life and complaining constantly about the weight.

A year ago she had been alone on a dark road with a dying mentor's letter and no sky.

"You're doing it again," Ivo murmured.

"Doing what?"

"Counting us like coins."

She huffed a laugh. "Old habit."

"It's a good habit," he said. "Just count out loud, sometimes. People like knowing they're counted."

Below them, the lights of the hall were going out one by one as the new keepers found their beds. Somewhere in the dark, a sentry called the hour — the first watch of the first night of the second year, passed hand to hand the way the old guild had passed contraband, except now what moved along the roads was news, and warning, and care.

Kestrel took out the ledger — the true ledger, the founding book of her line, recovered from Ironhollow's archive and rebound in green leather by her own hands. She didn't open it. She just held it, feeling the weight of every keeper who had come before her: the mother she'd mourned as an abandoner and honored now as a martyr; the grandmother whose name had been struck from family stories; the long chain of Sandovals stretching back three centuries to the forge, each one paying the price in secret, each one dying so the door would hold.

Her mother hadn't abandoned her. Her mother had died holding the door. And the door was held now — not by one bloodline bleeding in the dark, but by forty-one strangers on a hill, by a broken king naming stars, by a priest learning to have faith in people, by a scholar who believed the sky could keep secrets, by a prince who guarded a god's heart and complained about the weight.

Power shared was power survived. She'd learned it in the forge-light with three pairs of hands seizing hers at the last moment, refusing to let her go alone into the binding. Everything since had been the practice of it.

"One more year," Aldric said softly, "and no one will remember the dark."

"No," Kestrel said. "We'll remember. That's the whole point of us." She tapped the ledger. "First law of the Keepers: remember the night the stars went out. Not to be afraid. To be ready."

The wind moved through the grass, carrying the smell of rain-wet earth and woodsmoke from the valley below. Above them the four false stars burned their small stubborn lights, and the true stars wheeled slow and certain around them, and nothing in the sky or on the road or in the deep vault under the hill stirred against its bindings.

Then Wren sat up so fast she spilled her ink-horn.

"Kestrel." Her voice had gone thin and sharp, the voice she used for a mistranslation that changed everything. She was staring east, past the cairn, past the valley, to the far black line of the Thornfell border. "Look at the eastern sky. The Hunter — the Hunter's belt —"

Kestrel followed her gaze.

The three stars of the Hunter's belt were still there. All three. Burning steady and true.

But between them, where no star had ever hung in any map of any sky, in any age, in any of Wren's thousand charts —

a fifth light was rising.

Red. Small. Getting brighter.

And in the vault beneath the hill, faint as a heartbeat through three hundred feet of stone, Kestrel felt one of the four shards turn over in its sleep — and begin, very softly, to sing.
