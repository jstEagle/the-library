## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The stars were still there when Wren Oyelaran last checked, and that was the trouble with them — they never warned you.

He crouched at the lip of the Hollowmere Pass with his palm flat against cold granite, reading the mountain the way other men read ledgers. Below him, the smugglers moved in single file along a shelf of scree no wider than a coffin, their lanterns shuttered to slits of amber. Nine of them tonight, plus Dov at the rear with her hand resting easy on the pommel of her knife, the way she always did when the cargo was worth more than the people carrying it. Salt-iron from the Steppe side, bound for Thornfell's freeholds. Ordinary contraband. Ordinary night.

Wren had been guiding runs through these passes for six years, long enough that the mountains had stopped feeling like exile and started feeling like address. He knew which ledges held frost past midsummer, which ravines sang back your own voice in a stranger's timbre, which stones remembered heat from the day before and gave it up grudgingly after dark. He knew the border wards too — not as most smugglers knew them, as weather to be dodged, but as something closer to kin. When he passed beneath them he felt a faint pressure against his skin, like the memory of a hand on his shoulder, and he had learned not to flinch from it. Whatever the wards remembered of his family, they had never told him. He had stopped asking.

"Storm-caller." The whisper came up the line, one of Dov's new hires, a boy called Fen with a face full of questions. "Why do they call you that? I haven't seen you call so much as rain."

"You haven't seen me try," Wren said.

"But could you?"

The question hung in the thin air. Six years ago the answer would have been a demonstration: a sky split open, thunder answering him like a hound answering its name. Now the storm lived in him the way an old injury lives in a joint — felt only when the weather turned, aching at nothing.

"The mountains don't like noise," Wren said. "Keep moving."

Fen subsided, but Wren felt the boy's curiosity trailing him like a burr. It was the same curiosity everywhere in Thornfell, patient and slightly hungry. They knew he'd come from somewhere else. They knew he'd been sent away from somewhere else. In the freeholds, exile was a common enough trade that nobody pressed for details; you took a man's work and let him keep his silences. But the young ones always asked eventually. And Wren always answered the same way: with less than the truth, shaped like the truth.

The truth was a name he hadn't spoken aloud since the wardens put him across the border with a week's bread and a warning never to return. The truth was a grandfather whose signature he'd seen on exactly one document, half-burned, in a house that burned with it. The truth was that Wren Oyelaran had built himself a small life out of dark passes and unasked questions, and he guarded it the way a man guards a fire in wind — cupped, careful, always aware it could be taken.

They made the shelf below the Widow's Tooth by midnight. Here the pass narrowed to a throat, and above them the border wards ran their ancient course between two standing stones — visible now, as they almost never were, as a faint silver lattice strung across the dark like a spider's web holding up the sky. Wren paused beneath it out of habit, the way you pause under a doorway older than your grandmother's grandmother. The lattice hummed. It had hummed every night of his six years here, a sound below hearing, felt in the teeth more than the ears.

Tonight the hum was wrong.

Wren stopped dead. Behind him, boots scuffed to a halt, one by one, until nine smugglers stood in silence on the scree.

"What is it?" Dov's voice, low, at his shoulder. She was a compact woman with grey coming into her black hair and eyes that had priced everything she'd ever looked at. "Wards shifting?"

"They don't shift."

"Everything shifts."

"Not these." Wren lifted his hand toward the lattice without touching it. The silver threads were vibrating, yes, but underneath the vibration was something worse — a slackness, a loosening, the way a rope goes wrong just before it parts. He had felt this once before, years ago, on the far side of the border, in a life he didn't talk about: the sensation of a great structure deciding whether to stand or fall. "Get the cargo off the shelf. Now."

Dov didn't argue. That was the thing about Dov — she spent fear like coin, carefully, and only when the exchange favored her. Her whistle cut the dark twice, sharp, and the line began doubling back toward the wider ground below the Tooth.

Wren stayed.

He told himself it was to watch the wards, because watching was his job and the wards were his section of road. But his feet wouldn't move, and some older part of him — the part that had once stood in warden halls learning what the covenant was and why his family's name was written into it — understood that this was not a moment a man walked away from. The lattice above him brightened. Not steadily. In pulses, like a heartbeat climbing toward something.

Then the pulses stopped.

For three heartbeats the pass was utterly silent — no wind, no scree-slide, no breath of the mountain at all, as if the world had drawn in air to scream.

The wards shattered.

There was no explosion. That was the horror of it. The silver lattice simply came apart, thread by thread, unravelling upward like a knitted thing pulled from its hem, and where each thread failed the darkness behind it deepened — not shadowed, but *emptied*, as if the night itself had been a fabric and someone was picking it apart. Wren felt the failure go through him physically, a snap along his spine, a bell breaking inside his chest. He cried out without meaning to. His knees hit stone.

And then the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. Every star in the sky over the Thornfell passes vanished at once, all together, the way a room goes dark when a door closes — total, immediate, absolute. Wren raised his head into a blackness so complete it had texture, pressing on his open eyes like water. He had known dark his whole life. Mountain dark, cellar dark, the dark behind closed eyelids. This was none of those. This was a dark with weight and appetite, settling over the peaks like a creature coming home to a den it had been locked out of.

Somewhere down the line, Fen began to scream. Then someone else. Then Dov's voice, cutting through it like a blade through wet canvas: *"Lanterns! All of them, light everything you have!"*

Amber blooms opened along the shelf, ragged, trembling. In their light Wren saw the others' faces and understood his own must look the same: not afraid of robbers or rockfall or any enemy with a body, but afraid the way children are afraid, the way animals are afraid, of a thing that has no shape yet.

"Wren." Dov reached him in four strides and hauled him upright by the collar. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "What happened. You know something. I've watched you walk under those wards for six years like a man walking past his own grave, and I've never asked, but I'm asking now."

"I don't—" He stopped. Swallowed. The lie he'd used for six years rose to his lips and died there, because the broken wards were still humming through his bones, and lying in this dark felt like whistling past a cliff edge. "I know what they were," he said. "I don't know why they fell. But the wards weren't just Thornfell's fence, Dov. They were a covenant. A bargain, sealed by signatories on both sides of the border. My family—" His voice caught on it, the first time in six years he'd said it aloud. "My family signed for the human side. If the wards are gone—"

"Then the border's gone."

"Then the border's *open*." He looked up into the starless black, and the black looked back with the patience of something that had waited a very long time. "And whatever the covenant kept out is no longer kept."

The lanterns guttered, all together, though there was no wind. For a moment every flame shrank to a blue bead, and in that bead-light Wren saw the pass behind them — the way they'd come — filling with a cold that moved. Not snow. Not mist. A slow, deliberate white, pouring down the switchbacks like milk poured into ink, and where it touched the stone, frost bloomed in patterns far too regular to be weather. Patterns like script. Patterns like a language he almost recognized, from a burned house, from a half-page his grandfather had hidden and died for hiding.

The cold reached the first lantern and snuffed it. Then the second. The smugglers broke, running downslope toward the tree line, and Wren ran with them, Dov's grip on his arm hauling him along, Fen sobbing somewhere ahead, the frost-scripted dark flowing after them at the pace of something that had all night and all the world.

They made the trees as the last of the high lanterns died. Under the pines the dark was merely dark again — ordinary, survivable — and the company collapsed against trunks and stones, breathing hard, nine people and their abandoned salt-iron and their ruined ordinary night.

Wren leaned against a pine and pressed his shaking hands flat against his thighs and made himself think. Exile had taught him that much: when everything cracks, find the one thing still standing and build from it. The wards were gone. The stars were gone. The border was open, and something from the Glass Steppe was already crossing, writing its intentions on the mountainside in frost.

But he was alive. Dov was alive. The crew was alive. And he — he was the grandson of a signatory, the last person alive who carried that blood, that name, that half-burned page's promise. He had spent six years building a life small enough to hide in.

The night had just come and torn the walls down around him.

"Dov," he said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "I need to get to Sefa Ilori. The old warden, up at Greyhollow. Tonight."

"She's defrocked. Half-mad, they say."

"They say." Wren looked up through the pine branches at the empty places where the stars should have been, and felt, beneath his fear, something kindle that he hadn't felt in six years — anger, hot and clean, like the first strike of lightning over dry plains. Someone had done this. Covenants didn't unravel on their own. "She'll want to see what I saw. Because I think whatever buried the truth about my family just dug itself up — and I think it's walking down this mountain after us."

Far above, at the mouth of the pass, the frost-script flared once, briefly, like a signature being completed.

Then the dark began to move downhill.
