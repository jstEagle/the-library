## 2. What the Wardens Buried

The dark had weight now. Wren felt it pressing against the shutters of Sefa Ilori's cottage like a living thing, patient and enormous, testing for gaps.

He stood at the window with a candle cupped behind his hand — an old smuggler's habit, shielding light from eyes on ridgelines — and watched the snow fall upward. That was the wrongness of it. The wind came down off the Glass Steppe in long slow exhalations, and instead of driving the flakes against the glass it lifted them, unhurried, as though the sky were drinking the world back into itself. No stars to steer by. No wards humming along the border stones, that faint taste of copper he'd felt at the back of his throat his whole life and never once questioned until last night, when it simply stopped.

"You'll wear a groove in my floorboards." Sefa's voice from the hearth, dry as old vellum. She sat wrapped in three blankets despite the fire, a woman built like a fence post left too long in weather, gray-braided hair pinned with what looked suspiciously like a warden's signal-pin repurposed. "Sit. Drink. Staring won't bring them back."

"Nothing will bring them back," Wren said. He didn't sit. "That's rather the point."

"The point," she said, "is that you've been awake for thirty hours and you're no use to anyone dead of exhaustion. And you *are* useful, Wren Oyelaran, whether you like it or not. Sit down."

There was something in her voice — not command; defrocked wardens had no rank left to command with — but the particular gravity of a woman who had spent forty years being obeyed and hadn't entirely lost the habit. Wren sat. The tea was bitter, brewed from pine needles and something sharper he chose not to identify. Smugglers drank worse.

"You knew," he said. "When I stumbled into your yard half-frozen, you looked at me like you'd been expecting me."

"I looked at you like a woman seeing a ghost." She turned her hands toward the fire. Her knuckles were ruined, old breaks badly set — the hands of someone who had once held a warden's staff through storms that would have snapped a lesser grip. "I knew your grandfather, boy. Tunde Oyelaran. I stood post beside him at Highmoor Gate for six winters before you were born. You have his jaw. His stubborn way of standing when the wind argues with him."

Wren set the cup down carefully, because his hand wanted to shake and he refused to let it. "My grandfather was a goat farmer."

"My grandfather was a king's cook," Sefa said. "People are more than their last trade. Tunde Oyelaran was the last Voice of Thornfell — the one who spoke for the human realm when the covenant was sealed. And you are his blood, which makes you the last living signatory of the accord that just failed." She looked at him across the firelight, and her eyes were very steady. "Which means the night the stars went out is not an ending. It's a summons."

* * *

Sefa Ilori had kept her ledgers.

That was the thought that circled Wren as she hauled the cedar chest out from beneath her bed, breathing hard, waving off his help with a glare. Defrocked wardens weren't permitted to keep official records — that was part of the defrocking, the stripping of papers as well as rank — but Sefa had copied everything by hand before they took her commission, page after page in a small fierce script, because she had known even then that the day would come when the official record would be needed by someone the official record had been written to deceive.

"Read this," she said, and put a leather folio in his lap.

It was the covenant itself. Not a copy of the public text — every schoolchild in Thornfell could recite the public text, the pretty version about friendship between realms and shared watchfires — but the full instrument, with its annexes and its private clauses, signed in the old way: not with ink but with names spoken into the ember and bound there. Two columns of names. On the fae side, titles mostly — Lady of the Long Frost, Keeper of the Pale Court — because the fae guarded their true names like knives. On the human side, seven names in a descending line of inheritance, each generation's Voice re-signing the bond.

At the bottom of the seventh column, in handwriting Wren recognized with a lurch because he'd seen it on seed sacks and tax receipts all through his childhood: *Tunde Adeyemi Oyelaran.*

"Adeyemi," Wren said. The name tasted strange in his mouth, like a key found in a wall.

"His middle name. The name of his own inheritance." Sefa crouched by the fire, feeding it a split log. "The covenant isn't paper, boy. It's a chain of names, freely given, generation to generation. Each Voice speaks their whole name into the ember at the shrine, and the wards drink it, and the border holds. Your grandfather was the last to speak. After him—" she hesitated, and Wren saw, for the first time, something like shame move through the old woman's face— "after him, the chain was supposed to pass to his heir."

"I never heard of any of this. My father never—"

"Your father renounced it. Formally, publicly, before witnesses. He wanted a quiet life and he got one, gods rest him. So the chain should have passed to the next heir." She looked up at him. "You."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the wind lifted another slow breath of snow off the ground, upward, into the starving sky.

"They took my calling," Wren said slowly. "Eleven years ago. The wardens' tribunal stripped me of storm-calling and put me beyond the border for 'unlicensed invocation.' One lightning strike on a night I was trying to save a caravan, and suddenly I'm an exile." He heard his own voice flatten, go cold. "There was a man at that tribunal. Warden-Commander Hesk. He pushed the sentence harder than anyone. Argued for permanent exile, past the border stones, where I couldn't ever come home."

"Hesk," Sefa said. "Yes. Read the annex, third leaf. The one marked with the frost sigil."

Wren turned the brittle pages. The annex was short. It was also, he understood within four lines, the most important document he had ever held. *In the event of the covenant's failure,* it read, *the signatories' heirs bear the burden of reforging, and the winter court shall have claim upon any heir found beyond the border stones, per the terms of the Long Accord.* And beneath it, in different ink, different hand — a marginal note, undated, unsigned except for a single letter *H*:

*The boy must be sent beyond the stones before the old man dies. An heir inside Thornfell can be found. An heir in exile belongs to the Steppe.*

Wren read it twice. Three times. The words did not change. The room did not change. Only the shape of his entire adult life altered around him, quietly, the way a landscape alters when the fog lifts and shows you the cliff you've been walking toward.

"They didn't exile me for the lightning," he said.

"No."

"They exiled me so that when the wards fell — when *they* let the wards fall — I'd be standing on the wrong side of the border. Property of the winter court. A signatory's heir, delivered." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Eleven years. Eleven years I built a life out there. Routes, contacts, a reputation, a *name* — I made myself somebody new because I thought I'd shamed myself. And the whole time I was cargo. Placed. Like a stone moved on a board."

"Yes," Sefa said again, gently now. "I'm sorry. I tried to warn your grandfather's friends. I wrote letters. Hesk's people intercepted every one, and then they came for my commission, and I learned how far the rot goes up." She settled onto the bench across from him, blankets and all, and fixed him with those steady eyes. "I've waited eleven years for you to walk out of that dark and knock on my door. I confess I expected you sooner."

"I didn't know this house existed."

"I know. That's the tragedy of it. Everyone who could have told you is dead or scattered." She leaned forward. "So. Now you know what you are. What will you do?"

And here was the thing Wren discovered about himself, sitting in a defrocked warden's kitchen with his grandfather's name drying on his tongue like salt: the anger didn't scatter him. He had expected it to. He had spent eleven years practicing the art of not caring, of keeping his head down and his heart locked, and he had assumed that if the lock ever broke it would break him with it.

Instead the anger settled. It cooled. It became something with edges and a handle, something that could be carried, something that could be *used*.

"Hesk is still alive?"

"Warden-Commander now. In Thornfell city, fat and honored."

"And the winter court — whatever ate the stars last night — they'll come looking for me. For their property."

"They're already looking. The riders crossed the passes before dawn. I saw their lights on the high road." Sefa said it the way she reported everything, flat and factual, and somehow that made it worse. "You have perhaps two days before they trace the smuggling routes to this valley. Less, if they have a tracker."

Wren stood. He went to the window again, but this time he didn't stare at the upward-falling snow. He stared south, toward the low country, toward Thornfell city where a fat honored traitor slept warm under a roof that should have been struck by lightning eleven years ago.

"I want the records," he said. "All of them. Copies. The annex, the signature rolls, anything with Hesk's mark on it."

"You're planning something."

"I'm planning justice," Wren said, and heard how strange the word sounded — how large, how unfashionable, how much like something from the old stories his grandmother used to tell. "Not the tribunal kind. Not the kind that gets decided in a marble hall by men who buried the evidence. The other kind. The kind you make yourself, out on the road, with whoever's willing to stand beside you." He turned back to face her. "You spent forty years holding a border that belonged to liars, Sefa. Are you willing to help me build one that doesn't?"

For a long moment the only sound was the fire and the wind drinking the snow. Then Sefa Ilori smiled, and it transformed her whole ruined face into something young and dangerous.

"I've been waiting eleven years for someone to ask me that," she said. "But understand what you're proposing. Justice beyond the law means no courts to appeal to, no wardens to call, no rules but the ones we swear among ourselves. You'd need people. Good people and desperate ones, which are usually the same people." She began counting on her ruined fingers. "There's a smuggler captain runs the Salt Road — Dov, calls her ship a mule train but she's got forty blades who'd die for her. There's a hedge-witch in Millbrook, Bramble, who knows more about the old shrine-roads than any living warden. And there are six other defrocked wardens scattered through these valleys, men and women they broke for asking the same questions I asked."

"You've been building this list for eleven years."

"I've been *waiting* for eleven years. Building implies I believed anyone would come." She rose, groaning, and pulled a key from around her neck — iron, old, shaped like a flame. "First, though, there's something you need to see. Something I couldn't show you until you knew whose blood you carry."

She unlocked the cedar chest's inner compartment and lifted out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, unwinding it with the care of a woman handling relics. Inside lay a warden's staff — but not a standard issue staff. This one was black wood shot through with veins of silver, and along its length ran a groove filled with something that glowed, faintly, stubbornly, like a coal refusing to die.

"This is a Voice-staff," Sefa said. "Tunde Oyelaran's. It's been waiting for an heir almost as long as I have." She held it out across the space between them. "And Wren — before you take it, you should know why the wards really failed. Because the annex tells us *who* inherits the burden. But the shrine-records tell us *when* the next reforging must happen, and how little time we have."

Outside, far to the north, across the dead border and the starless steppe, something howled — a long, harmonized cry, many voices braided into one, hungry and patient and unmistakably *directed*.

Sefa glanced at the shuttered window and lowered her voice.

"They've found the valley."
