## 3. A Deserter from the Winter Court

Ysolde had been running for nine days when the mountains finally caught her.

She had crossed the Glass Steppe in the dark that was no longer night — the starless dark, the court's own element now, thick as wool and tasting of iron. She had crossed it on feet that left frost-prints behind her, because everything she touched bloomed cold now; the court had seen to that when they gave her her commission. She had crossed rivers that froze at her approach and a village where the people barred their doors against her without ever seeing her, because they had learned what came after the scouts. And she had crossed the border itself, through the wound in the wards, feeling it close behind her like a mouth.

The human realm was supposed to be warmer. It was not. The winter had followed her over, or perhaps she had carried it, or perhaps — this was the thought she could not stop thinking, the thought with teeth — there had never been any difference between the two realms at all, only a line drawn by dead men and a fire that everyone had forgotten to feed.

She smelled woodsmoke in the ninth hour of the ninth day and understood, with the flat clarity of exhaustion, that she was about to be captured.

They were good at it, whoever they were. She heard nothing until the net came down — woven rope, iron-shot at every knot, and iron sang against her skin like a struck bell, dropping her to her knees in the snow. Faces appeared between the pines: a broad woman with a smuggler's rolling gait and a knife already out; a small hedge-witch whose eyes went wide and then narrow; an older woman in the faded grey of a warden's coat, defrocked or dead or both, holding a staff that hummed.

And a man with storm-light still fading off his hands, looking at her the way one looks at weather coming in.

"Fae," the witch said. "Scout-class. See the frost-sign? She's been marking the valley."

"I'm not marking anything," Ysolde said. Her voice came out cracked. Nine days. "I'm done marking."

The man crouched to her level. Up close he was younger than his hands, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Then what are you?"

"Deserting," she said, and laughed, because it sounded absurd even to her, a word for soldiers in stories. "I'm deserting."

* * *

They tied her in the warden's way — Sefa Ilori, the grey-coat, knew the knots from a war Ysolde had only heard sung about — and sat her by their fire, which was the cruelest thing they could have done. Warmth that close and untouchable. The heat moved against her skin and could not get in, and beneath the rope her hands ached with wanting it.

They questioned her for hours. She answered everything, mostly because lying required energy she did not have.

Her name. Ysolde, of the Unnumbered House, scout-third of the Winter Court's outer vigil. How long since the stars went out. Nine days, same as her flight. What the court wanted in Thornfell. Names, at first — the court always wanted names, names were kindling — and then something else, something the Lady Vess had begun calling *the heir*, though the court did not know yet where the heir slept.

Wren Oyelaran went very still when she said it.

"You know that name," Ysolde observed.

"It's mine," he said.

"Ah," Ysolde said. "Then I'm glad I found you before they did." And she meant it, which surprised her. Somewhere in the last hundred miles, somewhere around the frozen river or the shuttered village, the part of her that had served the court had quietly died, and what remained felt a fierce, unaccountable relief at sitting across a fire from the person her former masters wanted, instead of kneeling behind him with a blade.

"Why?" the witch — Bramble — demanded. "Why'd you turn? What'd they do, pass you over for promotion?"

"No," Ysolde said. "They ate my brother."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke.

"The court is cold," she said, because it was easier to say it plainly than to say it well. "You understand that much, I think. When the stars went out, we lost the light we fed on — the old light, the covenant light, the warmth that leaked through the wards whether anyone admitted it or not. We told ourselves we didn't need it. We told ourselves the dark was ours, that we were made for it. And for a while that was true, and the court grew strong, and Lady Vess rode out singing." Her voice flattened. "But dark isn't food. Dark is just the absence. You can't live on absence forever. So Vess started feeding us to each other. First the low houses, the ones nobody would miss. Then the Unnumbered — my house, the servants, the scouts. A scout burns slow, you see. All that endurance worked into the blood. One scout keeps a hearth-hall warm for a month." She looked at her roped hands. "My brother was scout-second. They took him at the last high feast. I stood in the hall and felt the room get warmer and everyone smiled and thanked the Lady, and I understood that I was watching my brother become comfort. That's all the winter court is now. A long house where we eat each other and call it glory."

Bramble had gone pale. Dov, the broad one, had stopped turning her knife.

"So I ran," Ysolde finished. "And then I heard the howls cross the border, and I knew Vess had found the heir herself, and I thought — better the enemy who doesn't know what he is than the family that does."

Silence, except the wind working at the shutters.

It was Sefa who finally moved, Sefa who knelt and began untying the warden's knots, finger by finger, as if unwrapping something fragile rather than freeing something dangerous.

"You could be lying," the old warden said. "Every word could be bait."

"Yes," Ysolde agreed. "But you've read the shrine-records, haven't you? I can smell ember-ash on your sleeves. So ask me the question that matters, and see if my answer costs me anything."

Sefa's eyes narrowed. "Where can the covenant be reforged?"

"At the Ember Shrine," Ysolde said. "Only there. Only in the original fire. Anywhere else and you're lighting a candle and calling it dawn." She flexed her freed hands, watching the frost on her knuckles catch the firelight. "Vess knows it too. That's why she's moving now, before the heir learns what he is. She doesn't want to kill you, Wren Oyelaran." She met his eyes, and saw the dread land in them, and was sorry for it, and said it anyway. "She wants you alive at her hearth. An heir given freely would warm that court for a century."

* * *

They did not kill her. She had half expected it, and prepared a speech for the occasion, and never got to use it.

Instead they argued — Dov for the ice and the river and a quick drowning dressed up as misfortune; Bramble for keeping her leashed and useful; Wren pacing the length of the warden-house with the Voice-staff in his grip like he didn't trust himself to put it down. Ysolde sat by the fire she still could not feel and listened to them decide her life, and found, strangely, that she was not afraid. Fear had been burned out of her somewhere on the steppe, along with most other things. What remained was a stubborn, exhausted curiosity: *what will these strange soft people do with me?*

What they did was let Wren decide.

He stopped pacing. He looked at her for a long moment, and she looked back, and something passed between them that was not trust — trust was a coin neither of them owned — but was its rough draft.

"When they took your brother," he said, "did anyone speak for him?"

"No," Ysolde said. "No one speaks in the court anymore. Speaking is how they find the next meal."

"Then no one's going to speak for you either, probably," Wren said. "Not the wardens — they're gone. Not the law — the law ended at the border. So." He turned to the others. "We're outside the law out here. Always have been, most of us. Which means whatever justice happens tonight, we make it by hand." He looked at Ysolde again. "You want to defect? Then defect. You carry your weight, you tell us everything you know about Vess and her riders, and when this is over — if there's an over — you answer for what you did as their scout. To the people you marked. Fair?"

It was more than fair. It was absurdly, painfully fair, and Ysolde found her throat tight around words that should have been easy.

"That's not how courts work," she managed.

"No," Wren said. "Thank the stars it isn't. Or used to be." A ghost of a smile. "We'll get the stars back first."

Dov threw up her hands. Bramble muttered something about storm-callers and fae and trouble arriving in threes. But Sefa simply nodded, once, the nod of a woman adding a name to a roster she had been keeping for thirty years.

* * *

Later, near dawn, Ysolde stood at the window of the room they had given her — given her, a cell with a bed, a miracle — and watched the east.

There was no sunrise. There had been no sunrise for nine days; the sky stayed a bruised grey, starless above and sunless below, and the snow fell straight down in the windless way that meant the world itself was holding its breath.

Behind her, a knock. Bramble, with a bowl of broth and a strip of iron wire held ostentatiously in her other hand, in case.

"Eat," the witch said. "You'll burn faster if you don't."

"Burn?"

"You're all frost and no fuel. Hedge-craft, not court-craft, but heat's heat." Bramble set the bowl down and lingered, studying her with frank, unafraid curiosity. "Your brother. In the hall. Did you feel him go into the fire?"

Ysolde wrapped her hands around the bowl. For the first time in nine days, warmth reached her — thin, secondhand, borrowed through clay — and she held onto it like a rope.

"I felt him everywhere," she said.

Bramble nodded slowly, and Ysolde watched her decide something, watch the wariness in the small face shift by a single degree toward something else.

"Well," Bramble said. "First thread's the hardest to spin. Rest."

When she was gone, Ysolde drank the broth and looked north, where beyond the passes the winter court was riding, patient and harmonized and hungry, carrying her brother's warmth in its walls.

They would reach the Ember Shrine in six days, if the weather held, if the toll roads were clear, if no riders found them first.

Outside, far down the valley, a bell began to ring — three quick notes, pause, three more. The signal Sefa had taught them all that evening.

*Riders on the road.*

Ysolde set down the bowl, and for the first time since she had deserted, she reached for her frost the way a soldier reaches for a sword — not for the court, not for Vess, but for the ragged, impossible company sleeping under this roof.

The bells rang on, and below she heard Wren Oyelaran's door bang open, and boots on stairs, and Dov swearing cheerfully as she armed herself in the dark.

Six days, she thought, pulling on her boots. If we live that long.
