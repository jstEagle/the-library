## 4. The Smuggler's Oath

The first freehold was a day's ride down the Kettle Road, and Wren spent every hour of it rehearsing.

He had not asked anyone for anything in eleven years. That was the whole architecture of exile: you built your life so that no one owed you, and you owed no one, and then the world could not take from you what you had never claimed. He had been very good at it. He had been so good at it that when Sefa Ilori had spread the buried warden-records across her table three nights ago — the covenant signatures in their faded iron-gall ink, *Adeyemi Oyelaran* written in his grandfather's careful hand — Wren's first instinct had not been anger but embarrassment. As if the dead man had caught him out. As if he'd been found with his pockets turned inside out, all his careful emptiness on display.

Now he was riding to ask for everything.

Saltmere Freehold came up out of the valley like something the river had coughed up and decided to keep: tar-paper roofs, drying nets strung between chimneys, boats hauled up on rollers above the flood line. It smelled of pitch and fish guts and woodsmoke, and Wren felt his shoulders drop an inch the moment he smelled it. Smuggler towns always did. There was an honesty to them that lawful places never managed — everyone knew exactly what everyone else was, and no one pretended otherwise.

Dov Ashkani was mending a net on her own porch, which was how she received most visitors, most days, for most reasons. She was sixty if she was a day, broad as a door, with hands like knotted rope and a face that had stopped bothering to have expressions years ago. Her hair was going white at the temples in two sharp wings, like frost creeping across a windowpane.

"Wren Oyelaran," she said, without looking up. "Thought you were dead."

"Everyone does. It's useful."

"Mm." She pulled a knot tight. "You're not dead. You're worse. You're *here*."

"I need a ship."

"You need a lot of things, from what I hear." Now she looked up, and her eyes were pale river-gray and missed nothing. "Word travels strange since the stars went out. Faster than it should. I've had riders through here asking after a storm-caller gone quiet, offering silver for word of him. Winter men. They wear gray under their furs, and they don't blink enough."

Wren kept his voice level. "I know."

"Do you? Because they're asking after you by description, not name. Which means someone gave them your face." She set down the net at last, slowly, the way other people laid down weapons. "So before you tell me what you need a ship for, you'll tell me who sold you."

"I don't know yet."

"Hm." She considered this, chewing on it the way she chewed salt-pork. "Honest answer, anyway. Sit down. Have tea. Tell me about the wards."

He told her. All of it — the night the stars went out, the shattering of the border wards like glass struck with a hammer, Sefa's records, the covenant, his grandfather's name on the line, the exile arranged by people who had wanted the wards to fail. Dov listened the way old smugglers listen, which is to say she listened for the lies and seemed disappointed to find none.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment. Down in the harbor someone was singing, badly, a work song about a drowned man who got better.

"You understand," she said finally, "that what you're describing is treason. Against whichever court claims this ground now. Possibly against both."

"There isn't a court claiming this ground. That's rather the point."

"No," Dov said. "There isn't. And there hasn't been, not really, not since the wardens were defrocked and the tolls went to whoever could hold a bridge longest. We've been governing ourselves out here for years, badly, by argument and debt and the occasional knife. What you're proposing is just more of the same, except with fire in it." She stood, joints cracking like green wood in a cold snap. "I'll come. But not for the covenant. I don't care about your grandfather's ink. I'm coming because the winter courts eat freeholds first when the dark gets long, and I've buried too many neighbors these past months to sit on my porch and mend nets while it happens."

"That's reason enough."

"It's the only honest reason there is." She jerked her chin toward the water. "Take the *Kestrel*. She's slow but she doesn't sink, which is more than can be said for her captain's promises. We sail on the morning tide."

* * *

Bramble was harder to find and easier to convince, which worried Wren more than the reverse would have.

The hedge-witch kept a crooked house at the edge of Fenmarsh Freehold where the road dissolved into reeds, and her garden grew things that had no business growing this far north — feverfew blooming in frost, a rosemary bush the size of a pony that hummed faintly when the wind touched it. She answered the door before he knocked, which meant she'd seen him coming from a long way off, which meant she'd known who he was before he'd known he was coming.

"Storm-caller," she said. She was small and brown and quick, somewhere past fifty, with dirt permanently worked into the creases of her knuckles and eyes the color of strong tea. "Come in before the reeds do. They get ambitious at dusk."

Inside, the house was all hanging bundles and stoppered jars and a ceiling low enough that Wren had to stoop. A kettle was already on. Of course it was.

"Sefa sent word ahead," Bramble said, pouring. "She and I go back further than either of us likes admitting. So. You're collecting people. For the shrine."

"For the Ember Shrine, yes. To reforge the covenant."

"Mm. And you're asking me because?"

"Because the passes are watched, the roads are worse, and I need someone who knows what grows in the dark now. The starless night has changed things. Plants that never used to open are opening. Things that used to sleep aren't sleeping. Half the old herb-lore is wrong and nobody's written the new half yet."

That interested her; he could see it land, see the hunger behind the wariness. Hedge-witches lived and died by knowing what the land was doing before the land did it.

"And the rest?" she asked. "The part you're not saying. Why does a man who spent eleven years building a life with no hooks in it suddenly want to hang himself back on the wall?"

Wren looked into his tea. "Because the wall fell down. Because there's nothing left to hang apart from."

Bramble snorted. "Better answer than I expected." She drank, set down the cup, and started pulling jars off shelves with the brisk economy of a woman packing for a journey she'd already taken in her head a dozen times. "I'll come. But I'm bringing the whole garden's worth of the useful ones, and you're carrying the heavy crate, and if you die stupidly I'm taking my herbs back off your corpse."

"Fair terms."

"They're the only kind I offer." At the door, she paused, and something in her face softened by a degree — a degree being, for Bramble, a great deal. "Eleven years is a long time to spend being no one, boy. Be glad you get to stop."

* * *

Sefa's disgraced wardens were the hardest ask of all, because they had lost the most.

They gathered in the burned-out shell of the old warden-house at Millbrook Freehold — six of them, what remained of a garrison of thirty. The roof had fallen in years ago and they'd never had leave to mend it, so they met under open sky, around a fire pit that had once been a hearth. Their former captain, a heavyset woman named Okonjo with a scar splitting one eyebrow, sat on a broken lintel like a throne and regarded Wren with the flat patience of a soldier waiting for orders that never come.

"Sefa says you want us," Okonjo said.

"I want what you were."

"We weren't anything. That was the finding of the tribunal. Defrocked for dereliction — for failing to hold the border the night it broke." Her jaw tightened. "Thirty of us against whatever came through, with orders to stand down even as the wards failed, because some clerk in the capital had countermanded the alert. We stood down. The border broke. And then we were tried for it, as though we'd broken it ourselves."

"I know. I read the records. Sefa showed me."

"Then you know we didn't fail. We were *made* to fail. Someone wanted the wards down, and someone needed someone to blame, and we were convenient." Okonjo looked at him for a long moment. "And you're the heir of the man whose signature held those wards. So I'll ask you straight, storm-caller, because soldiers get one question and deserve one answer: is this justice, or is this revenge wearing justice's coat?"

Wren thought about lying. Then thought about what Sefa would say, and didn't.

"I don't know yet," he said. "I know my exile was engineered. I know the wards were sabotaged. I know the same hands likely did both. Whether the people who did it can be brought to account by any law that still exists — I genuinely don't know. The capital's writ runs thin out here and thinner every night. What I can offer isn't a trial. It's the shrine, and the reforging, and afterward a border that belongs to the people who actually live on it instead of to clerks who've never seen it."

Silence, except for the fire. One of the younger wardens, a wiry man named Tessin, spoke up. "The capital won't honor a reforged covenant. Not one signed out here, by us, by *him*."

"No," Wren agreed. "It probably won't."

"Then we'd be oath-breakers twice over. Formally."

"Or," said Okonjo quietly, "we'd be the only ones keeping the actual oath. The one underneath the paper." She stood. She looked at her five remaining wardens, and then at Wren, and something passed over her face that was not quite hope but was its close kin — the feeling of a compass needle swinging at last toward something. "We were sworn to hold the border. Nobody ever unswore us from that. The tribunal took our badges, our pay, our names off the rolls. It never took the oath. Oaths don't live in tribunals."

She put out her hand. Wren took it.

* * *

They swore it two nights later, on the shingle beach below Saltmere, under a sky with no stars in it at all.

Dov had chosen the place — neutral ground, freehold ground, belonging to no crown and no court. The tide was out and the shingle gleamed wet and black under a moon that hung thin and wrong-colored, like a coin rubbed nearly blank. The company stood in a rough crescent: Dov and two of her crew, Bramble with her impossible crates, Sefa, Okonjo and her five wardens, Ysolde standing slightly apart in her gray winter-court furs, watchful as a heron. Fourteen souls, counting Wren. It was not an army. It was barely a raiding party. Looking at them, Wren felt the cautious hope in his chest war with the arithmetic and mostly lose.

But then Sefa spoke, and the arithmetic stopped mattering.

"There's no law for what we're about to do," she said, her voice carrying flat and clear over the water. "No court will recognize it. No clerk will record it. If we fail, history will call us bandits, or fools, or nothing at all. So we will swear outside the law — not against it, not around it, but outside it, in the older country where oaths were between the swearer and the sky and the ground underfoot, and no third party was needed."

She had them each speak their piece, freely given, the way the old covenants had been made. Dov swore on her ship. Bramble swore on her garden's roots. Okonjo swore on the oath she'd never been released from. Tessin swore on his dead comrades. Even Ysolde, hesitating, chose something — "on the light I was born under, before it was taken" — and her voice cracked on the last word, and no one laughed, and something shifted in the circle then, some small tectonic settling, strangers becoming a company.

Last of all, Wren stepped forward. He had planned to swear on the covenant, on his grandfather's name, on the ember itself. Instead, hearing the others, he found himself saying something plainer.

"I swore eleven years ago to be no one," he said. "I'm taking it back. I swear on the name I'm going to have to say at the end of this road — the one I've been running from since I was nineteen years old. I don't say it tonight. But I swear I'll be able to, when the time comes. Freely."

The tide breathed in and out. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered in a cloudless sky — his old gift stirring uneasily, like a dog that scents a journey in its master's packing.

Dov broke the silence by spitting ceremonially into the surf, which by freehold custom sealed everything. "Right," she said. "Ship sails on the morning tide. Shrine's nine days hard travel if the passes hold. Sleep while you can."

They were breaking camp when Ysolde came to him quietly, her breath fogging in air that should not have been cold enough for fog in high summer-that-was-no-longer-summer.

"The passes won't hold," she said. "My lady's riders are already moving. Vess knows where you're going — has known since the scout I killed failed to report. She won't fight you at the shrine. Too much old power in that ground, even dying. She'll take you on the road." Ysolde's pale eyes fixed on his. "There's a toll station at Grey Larch Pass. Narrow. Bad ground. Perfect for ambush. She'll be there in four days, maybe five."

Wren looked at the thin, wrong moon, and at his ragged fourteen, and felt the cautious hope in his chest grow teeth.

"Then we'd best be worth ambushing," he said.
