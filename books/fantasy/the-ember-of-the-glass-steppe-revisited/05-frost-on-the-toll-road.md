## 5. Frost on the Toll Road

Dov Ashkane had run the Grey Larch toll forty times in her life, and she had never once liked it.

The pass was a throat of stone where the old kings of Thornfell had once collected their dues from every caravan crawling between the freeholds and the Steppe. The station still stood — a squat tower of black granite with a gatehouse straddling the road like a jaw, its portcullis long since rotted to lace. Snow came down through the gap in fat, silent flakes, filling the wheel ruts, erasing the world beyond twenty paces. On either side the larches rose pale and bare, their needles long since dropped, so that the whole slope looked like a forest of bones standing at attention.

"Bad ground," Sefa had said, and Sefa was right. But it was also the only ground. The southern switchbacks were buried under a slide; the northern trail would cost them six days they did not have. So they would take the throat, and pray nothing waited in the teeth.

Dov did not believe in prayer. She believed in cargo weight, axle grease, and knowing exactly how many men it took to hold a choke point. She counted her people again as they came up the last stretch of road: Wren at the fore with his hood back despite the cold, storm-light flickering faintly under his skin when he thought no one watched; Ysolde gliding behind him like a shadow that had learned to walk; Sefa and her three disgraced wardens, armor patched, spears held with the grim economy of soldiers who had nothing left to prove and everything left to lose; Bramble at the rear with the mules, chattering to them the way she chattered to everything — herbs, hexes, hedge-lore poured out in a stream that never quite stopped.

Fourteen souls. It had seemed like an army around a fire. On this road, in this snow, it seemed like a handful of matches.

"You're doing the face," Bramble said, falling into step beside her. "The one where you're counting coffins."

"I'm counting mule feed."

"You're counting coffins." Bramble's grin was crooked, gap-toothed, unkillable. She'd worn a garland of dried rowan berries around her neck since dawn — "for the frost-fairies, keeps 'em honest" — and the berries clicked together as she walked. "Cheer up, Captain. If we die here, I'll haunt you gently. Nothing worse than a smell of burnt sage and the occasional rude noise."

"Bramble."

"Captain."

"If there's trouble, you get behind the wardens. You're not a fighter."

Bramble's grin faded into something softer, rarer. She touched Dov's sleeve — the only person alive who dared. "Neither are you, love. You're a smuggler. We're all just people who got good at running. Doesn't mean we stop walking."

Dov said nothing. Twenty years on the roads had taught her that the last thing anyone heard before bad news was usually a friend's voice, and she wanted to keep Bramble's voice a while longer.

* * *

The ambush came an hour past midday, when the snow thickened enough to swallow sound and the light went the color of old pewter.

It began with the birds. Or rather, with the absence of them — the silence spreading down the slope like ink in water. Ysolde stopped first, head tilting, nostrils flaring. Then Sefa's hand came up, fist closed, and the column froze.

"They're here," Ysolde breathed. "Above the tree line. More than I—" Her face went strange. "They've been *waiting*. They let us come."

The first rider came out of the snow like something carved rather than born: a tall shape on a horse the color of glacier ice, antlered helm streaming white ribbons, a lance of black frost couched at rest. Then another. Then a dozen, sliding down the slope without disturbing a single branch, and behind them — Dov's stomach turned over — ranks of footmen in gray mail, faces hidden, moving with the terrible patience of things that had never once been cold.

"To me!" Sefa roared, and the wardens snapped into a spear line across the road with a discipline that made Dov's chest ache. Defrocked, disgraced, stripped of their oaths — but their bodies remembered what their order had beaten into them, and for ten heartbeats it was almost beautiful.

Then the world broke open.

Frost-lances fell like hail. The warden on Sefa's left took one through the shoulder and went down screaming, his arm blackening where the cold bit flesh. Wren stepped forward and *called*, and the storm answered — lightning ripping out of a sky that had no stars to hide behind, splitting a rider and his horse into smoking halves. For a moment Dov dared to hope. Storm-calling was a weapon out of legend. What could winter do against—

The lead rider raised one gauntleted hand, and the falling snow simply *stopped*. Hung in the air. Every flake between the trees hung motionless, and the cold coming off them was sudden and absolute, a wall of stillness that ate Wren's second bolt before it crossed half the distance. He staggered. Blood ran from his nose.

"They're drinking the storm," Ysolde shouted, fighting toward him through the frozen air. "Wren! They were sent for *you* — they want your power, they'll pull it out of—"

A net of white fire arced over the wardens' line and settled over Wren like a shroud. He screamed once, and the lightning under his skin guttered, dimmed, went dark.

"No," Dov heard herself say. "No, no—"

What happened next happened fast and badly, the way real battles do. Sefa ordered a retreat to the gatehouse. The wardens gave ground by inches. Ysolde fought like a creature out of the old stories, all silver fury, cutting two riders out of their saddles before a frost-lance caught her leg and folded her into the snow. Dov dragged her behind a deadfall, put three crossbow bolts into a gray-mail footman's visor slit, and lost sight of everything in the churn of snow and shouting.

She found Bramble by hearing her.

The hedge-witch had gone forward instead of back — of course she had, curse her loyal reckless heart — because the mules had panicked and someone had to quiet them or they'd bolt with half their supplies, and because Wren was down and Bramble had never in her life left anyone lying in the road. Dov saw her kneeling beside the netted, twitching storm-caller, hands glowing green-gold, rowan berries scattering from her torn garland, singing some hedge-charm against the cold.

The tall rider — their commander, Dov understood later, though she didn't know it then — looked down at Bramble the way a man looks at a fly on his cup.

"Don't," Dov screamed, already running, already knowing she was too far, too slow, too late, the way she had been too slow at her brother's trial and too slow at her father's funeral and too slow her whole cursed life. "*Don't you touch her!*"

The rider gestured. Ice took Bramble from the feet up — not violently, that was the horror of it, gently, a bloom of white climbing her body while she kept singing, kept her hands on Wren, until the frost sealed her lips and the song stopped and the green light in her palms went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

Then the riders withdrew. All of them. As one. They took the frozen hedge-witch up across a saddle like a bundle of kindling, and the commander turned his antlered helm toward the survivors and spoke in a voice like wind through a keyhole.

"Wren Adeyemi Oyelaran." The full name, spoken slowly, tasting it. Wren, half-conscious in the snow, went rigid. "Her ladyship bids you come to her court when you are able. She has questions about your grandfather. And she returns your witch when the answers please her." A pause, and Dov could have sworn the thing was smiling under all that white. "Alive. She wants you alive, heir of both realms. Everything else is weather."

They rode up into the falling snow and were gone, and the birds came back, and the silence they left behind was worse than the battle.

* * *

They made camp in the gatehouse because no one could walk further. Dov sat apart, cleaning a crossbow she had already cleaned, because her hands needed work or they would start shaking and she did not know if they would ever stop.

Ysolde limped over and lowered herself onto the stone beside her, frost-burned leg wrapped in Bramble's own spare bandages. Neither spoke for a long time.

"She went forward," Dov said finally. "I told her to go back. She went forward. To help him. To help *any* of us." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "Twenty years I've run cargo through wars and wards and winters, and I never lost a soul I swore to carry. Not one. And now—"

"Now you have fourteen minus one," Ysolde said quietly. "And one of those is a fae who should have died on your rope the day you caught her. Grief doesn't count fair, captain. It never has."

"She's not dead." Dov stared into the middle distance. "That thing said *returned*. Which means Vess is keeping her breathing like a coin in a purse. Which means every hour we sit here grieving, that court has time to decide a frozen hedge-witch isn't worth the keeping."

Across the fire, Wren sat wrapped in every blanket they owned, hollow-eyed, his hands empty in his lap. When he caught Dov looking, he didn't look away. Good, she thought. Don't you dare look away. But his voice, when he spoke, was low and steady and had teeth in it.

"The shrine's two days hard march from here. Vess expects me to come begging terms." He stood, swaying, and Sefa moved to steady him and he waved her off. "So we don't beg. We march tonight, we reforge the covenant, and when the stars come back and her stolen dark burns off her court like fog off a river—" His jaw tightened. "We get Bramble back. I don't care what it costs. That's not a promise, Dov. That's arithmetic."

Dov set down the crossbow. Outside, the snow went on falling in a world with no stars, and somewhere north a woman made of winter was holding a gap-toothed girl who sang to mules, waiting to see what a man was worth when he stopped running.

"All right," Dov said, and reached for her pack. "Arithmetic."
