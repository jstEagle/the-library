## 6. The Court That Ate Its Own Light

The ice-hold did not look like a fortress. It looked like a wound.

Wren crouched on the ridge above the valley with Ysolde beside him, watching the court's seat rise out of the frozen lake below — a spire of black glass sheathed in ice, its walls drinking what little light the starless sky offered and giving none of it back. Around it, the lake had frozen in rings, like ripples caught mid-motion, as though something enormous had struck the water once, long ago, and the cold had preserved the moment of impact forever.

"It eats light," Ysolde said quietly. Her breath came out silver and hung in the air too long before fading. "That's not a figure of speech, Wren. The hold was built over a place where a star fell — one of ours, one of the Steppe's, back when the covenant was young. It burned itself out falling. The court hollowed out the crater and made it their throne-room floor. Everything the hold is comes from that dead star's hunger. It wants to burn again, so it takes whatever fire passes near it."

"And your court feeds it."

"My court *is* it." She said it flatly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Lady Vess doesn't rule the winter court. She administers it. There's a difference, and the difference is that an administrator can count us. We're inventory. When the dark got long and the old ones' warmth ran thin, Vess started feeding the hold our own people. First the criminals. Then the debtors. Then anyone whose name wasn't written down somewhere useful." Her jaw tightened. "Bramble will be in the counting-house. They always keep the living ones there until they're needed."

Wren looked at her. In the weeks since the toll road ambush, since Dov had carried Bramble's dropped satchel for three days rather than let anyone else touch it, Ysolde had become something Wren didn't have a clean word for. Not ally. Allies were chosen from strength. Whatever she was had been chosen from ruin, hers and theirs both.

"You're sure you want to walk back into that," he said.

"I'm sure I want to walk back in with *you*," she said. "It's different."

* * *

They went in through the drainage of the dead star itself — a fissure in the lake ice where meltwater from the hold's warm heart bled out and refroze in layers, leaving a tunnel of glassy ice that rang faintly when touched, like a struck bell. Sefa had argued against it. *The heart of the thing will hear you,* she'd said. But Dov had looked at the satchel she still carried and said nothing at all, and that silence had decided it.

Inside, the hold was warmer than it had any right to be, and that was the horror of it. The heat had to come from somewhere. Wren walked corridors of blue-black ice lit by veins of pale glow — stolen light, he realized, light the hold had swallowed and never digested, leaking back through the walls like blood through a bandage. And threaded through everything, faint but unmistakable, a sound like breathing. Slow. Enormous. Patient.

"The star," Ysolde whispered. "Don't listen to it. If it notices you listening, it starts asking questions."

They passed the counting-house on the second ring: a vaulted chamber where fae of the winter court sat at desks of clear ice, writing names into ledgers that steamed faintly, each page warmed by whatever remained of the person named. Wren saw a clerk pause, press her hand flat against a ledger as if soothing a fevered child, and turn the page. He made himself look away. Bramble wasn't there. Ysolde read the room in one sweep and pointed downward. *The deep cells. Where they keep the ones worth keeping.*

They found the cells by following the sound of someone humming.

It was Bramble's voice, thin and cracked, working through a hedge-witch's charm-song — the kind sung to keep frost off seedlings. She sat cross-legged in a cell of solid ice, her hands pressed to the wall, and where her palms touched, the ice had gone cloudy and soft, thawing in a slow circle no bigger than a dinner plate. She'd been at it a long time. Her lips were blue. When she saw them through the wall her face did something complicated — relief and fury arriving at once — and then she pressed two fingers to her lips and pointed up and behind them, and mouthed a single word:

*Vess.*

* * *

Lady Vess found them before they could find her.

She came down the corridor of the deep cells alone, unarmored, wearing grey silk and a circlet of black iron set with a single shard of fallen star, and she moved through the breathing dark of her own hold the way a hostess moves through her own house — unhurried, certain, already pouring the tea. Two of Sefa's wardens flanked Wren with drawn steel; Ysolde went rigid as a snared animal; and Vess looked at all of them the way one looks at weather.

"Storm-caller," she said. "Or should I say Oyelaran? You have his hands. Your grandfather's. I knew him when those hands were young."

"You knew my grandfather?" The words came out of Wren before caution could catch them.

"I signed the covenant beside him." Vess clasped her hands. "I was not always this. Sit. No — stand, if you prefer. But hear me, because you have come all this way through my dark to hear something, and it would be rude to send you home hungry."

"Bramble first," Wren said.

"Bramble is alive because I ordered her kept alive. Ask her, when we're done, whether her cell has been comfortable enough. I do apologize for the accommodations. We are short on warmth these days." Something flickered across her face then — fast, quickly buried — something that was not cruelty. "You believe I broke the wards. You believe I engineered your exile. Your warden friend has been very busy with her buried records, and most of what she found is true. Yes, I wanted the border open. Yes, I arranged for the last signatory's grandson to be cast out before the failing began, so that no heir would stand at the Ember Shrine when the hour came. All of that, I did."

"Then—"

"But you have the order of sins wrong, Wren Adeyemi Oyelaran." His full name in her mouth landed like a stone dropped into still water. "Your grandfather broke the covenant first. I only stopped holding up my half of a roof the other side had already set fire to."

"That's a lie."

"Is it?" She drew something from her sleeve: a tablet of grey-green stone, older than any script Wren knew, its surface crowded with dense carved lines. "This is the original accord. Not the copy in Thornfell's archives — the true one, cut before either realm had learned to lie about the other. Read the seventh clause. Or better — let me spare you the years of study. The covenant was never a wall between realms. It was a *treaty of exchange*. Thornfell gave the Steppe its storms — rain, thunder, the wild weather of the south — and the Steppe gave Thornfell its stars, hung low and bright over the border mountains, so that human travelers would never lose their way. Storms for stars. Each side kept the other's treasure in trust. And each side swore that if ever it took back what it had given — if Thornfell called its storms home, or the Steppe pulled down its stars — the covenant would die, and the wards with it."

Wren felt the ground tilt beneath him, slow and vast, like the deck of a ship in deep water.

"Sixty years ago," Vess said, "your grandfather called the storms home."

* * *

They brought him proof in the only way that mattered: they let him feel it.

Ysolde took the tablet in both hands and held it out to him, and when Wren's skin touched the stone, the accord spoke — not in words but in weather-memory, the way a storm remembers every coast it has broken against. He saw the signing: two circles of figures on the Glass Steppe under a sky so thick with low-hung stars it seemed the constellations had come down to graze. He saw his grandfather — younger than Wren was now, storm-grey eyes, the same restless hands — standing among the human signatories with a lightning-scar running down one arm.

And then he saw the drought.

He saw the southern freeholds cracking dry, wells gone to salt, children carried to rivers that were beds of pale stones. He saw his grandfather stand before the covenant circle and ask — beg — for the storms to be released early, all of them, a full generation's share of rain, because the south was dying. And he saw the Steppe refuse. Not cruelly. Carefully. *The exchange is balanced to the year,* the fae signatories said. *To give more now is to take more later, and the later belongs to children not yet born.* They were right, probably. They were right the way arithmetic is right, and arithmetic does not weep for dying fields.

So his grandfather had reached into the treaty itself and torn out his half. Called the storms home. Every storm, all at once, out of the Steppe's keeping and back into Thornfell's sky — and the rains had come, and the south had lived, and the covenant had begun, that same hour, to die. Not shattered. *Dying.* Sixty years of slow collapse, each season the wards a little thinner, each winter the dark a little longer, while both courts pretended the roof wasn't burning because neither side would admit who had struck the match.

Wren came back to himself on his knees on the ice, and did not remember kneeling.

"He saved ten thousand lives," Vess said, and her voice had changed — quieter now, and very tired. "I have never denied it. I voted to release the storms, actually. I was overruled. And when he tore the covenant rather than watch the south die, I understood him completely. That is why I did what I did next. If the covenant was dying regardless, I wanted the death to come on my terms. I wanted the dark gathered and stored, held in reserve, so that when the last star went out my people would have something to live on. Your grandfather spent the future to buy the present. So did I. The only difference between us, storm-caller, is that his theft fed humans and mine feeds fae, and your histories will call his act sacrifice and mine atrocity."

"That's not—" Wren started, and stopped, because he didn't know how to finish it. *That's not the same* was what he wanted to say, and he could not find, anywhere in himself, the reason why it wasn't. His exile, Sefa's records, the engineered failure — all of it had given him a shape to his anger, a villain, a single door to break down. Now there was no door. There was just a long corridor of choices, each one made by someone who could not bear the cost of doing right, and at the end of it, a boy in the snow with stolen lightning in his blood, believing he had inherited a war when he had inherited a *debt*.

"Wren." Ysolde's hand on his shoulder. Her voice low. "Whatever's true — the shrine doesn't care who broke it first. A name freely given reforges it. That hasn't changed."

"No," Vess said softly. "But something else has. Come and see, storm-caller. Both of you. Bring your witch her boots; she'll want to walk."

They opened Bramble's cell, and Bramble stood on legs gone wooden with cold and looked at Lady Vess with pure hatred, and Vess inclined her head to her as if to an honored guest.

"You'll want to know why I'm releasing her," Vess said. "Because I need you to reach the Ember Shrine, Wren Oyelaran. I need the covenant reforged as much as you do — more, perhaps, because when it dies fully, the hunger in this hold wakes up, and there will be nothing left anywhere to feed it but everything." She turned toward the stair, her grey silk whispering against the ice. "But understand what you carry now. The covenant cannot simply be remade as it was. The old exchange is broken past mending — Thornfell's storms are spent, and my stars are ash. To reforge it, the ember will demand a new price. A greater one."

She paused at the foot of the stair, and looked back at him, and for one unguarded moment Wren saw what sixty years of feeding her own people to the dark had left inside the administrator of the winter court.

"Ask your firekeeper what a freely given name costs," she said, "when the giver is heir to both realms. And then decide, quickly, child — because the final snows have already begun to fall, and they are falling toward the shrine faster than you can walk."

Outside the hold, when they emerged onto the starless plain, the eastern horizon had turned the color of bruised flesh, and the air tasted of iron and endings, and far away — impossibly far, and yet coming — Wren heard the first whisper of the long winter finishing its fall.
