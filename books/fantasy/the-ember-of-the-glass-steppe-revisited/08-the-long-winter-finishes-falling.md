## 8. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

Ysolde smelled the snow before anyone else did.

It was not the smell of snow — snow had no smell, or none that mortals could catch — but the smell beneath it: cold iron and crushed juniper and the faint sweet rot of things that had been alive too long in the dark. It was the scent of the Winter Court on the march. She had worn that scent for two hundred years like a second skin, and she had thought herself free of it, and now it came down the mountain toward the Ember Shrine riding the wind like a debt collector.

She was on the shrine's eastern wall when she caught it, watching the last light of a starless dusk pool in the valley below. She went still, the way she had gone still in the court's antechambers when Lady Vess walked past — stillness as survival, stillness as prayer.

"Dov," she said. "Dov, wake up."

He was below her in the courtyard, arguing with Bramble about whether hedge-witchery counted as honest work. He looked up at Ysolde's voice and whatever he saw in her face made him stop mid-sentence.

"How long?" he asked.

"Hours. Maybe less." Ysolde dropped from the wall, landing soft as frost. "She's not sending riders this time. She's bringing everything."

Bramble's hands had already gone to the charms at her belt, fingers moving over them like a woman counting coins she knew were short. "Everything," she repeated. "Define everything."

"The whole court." Ysolde heard her own voice go flat, the way it went flat when she spoke of home, because flatness was the only way to carry it. "Every knight who hasn't frozen solid. Every hound made of hunger. Vess herself. When the last snows begin, they have to feed them all, and there is only one fire left in the world worth eating."

The Ember Shrine sat in its bowl of black stone like a coal cupped in a dead hand. Behind it, the mountains climbed into darkness where stars should have been — where stars *had* been, once, before the wards broke and the sky went blind. In the shrine's heart, the old firekeeper tended the ember that had burned since the first covenant, feeding it splinters of consecrated oak and, lately, stranger things: Sefa's warden badges, surrendered one by one; a lock of Bramble's hair; three drops of Dov's blood, given grudgingly and with commentary.

And Wren Oyelaran, sitting beside the fire every night like a man keeping vigil at his own grave.

Ysolde found him there now, as the first flakes began to fall — fat, slow flakes, wrong somehow, falling too straight, as if even gravity had grown tired. He was sharpening a knife he would never use. Storm-callers did not fight with knives. They fought with the sky, and the sky was gone.

"It's coming," she said.

"I know." He didn't look up. "I felt it in my teeth an hour ago. Old habit." A ghost of a smile. "You'd think I'd have lost the habit by now."

"Wren." She crouched beside him, close enough to see the ember-light moving in his dark eyes. "When she comes — if it goes wrong—"

"Don't." Now he looked up, and there was something in his face she recognized from the ice-hold, from the moment before he'd turned back for Bramble instead of running: that stubborn, mule-headed refusal to count costs out loud. "We don't do eulogies before the battle. That's a mortal superstition I refuse to adopt."

"It's a fae superstition too."

"Then we're both superstitious." He set down the knife. For a moment his hand hovered near hers — not touching, never quite touching, though in two hundred years of courts and their careful distances Ysolde had learned that the almost-touch said more than any touch could. "Get some rest, deserter. You'll want your legs tomorrow."

She went, because there was nothing else to do, and because he had asked. And because some small cowardly part of her wanted, just once more, to sleep without hearing the sound of the court coming.

* * *

They came at moonrise, which was no moonrise at all, only the pale suggestion of one behind the cloud-bank, a bruise of light where light should have ruled.

Ysolde stood on the pass above the shrine with Dov and eleven of Sefa's disgraced wardens, and she watched the winter come up the valley like a tide.

It did not look like an army. It looked like weather. Snow moved against the wind, flowing uphill, and inside the snow were shapes: the tall thin knights in their armor of grey ice, their faces hidden behind visors frosted from within; the hounds, low and many-legged, whose bodies were mostly absence, holes in the world shaped like wolves; and above them all, borne on a palanquin of black glass, Lady Vess, robed in white so deep it hurt to look at, her crown a circlet of frozen breath.

"She brought the whole rotten household," Dov muttered beside her. His knuckles were white on his axe haft. "Good. Saves us the trouble of hunting them down later."

"You are going to die saying something like that," Ysolde said quietly.

"Not today." He grinned at her, that crooked smuggler's grin that had talked its way through a dozen toll-gates and customs houses. "Today I'm going to be insufferably alive. Watch me."

Sefa's voice carried down the line of wardens — calm, clipped, the voice of a woman who had been stripped of her rank but never of her authority. "Hold the narrows. Let them bunch. Nothing gets past you to the shrine. Nothing. If the line breaks, fall back to the second wall, and if that breaks—" a pause, the smallest hitch, "—then make them pay for every step."

Ysolde drew her blade — fae-forged, court-stolen, the one possession she had carried out of her old life besides her name, and even that she had left behind. Around her, the wardens set their spears. Bramble crouched at the line's edge with her charms strung out before her like a fisherman's net, muttering words that made the snow flinch.

The tide reached the narrows.

What followed was not a battle so much as a collision between two kinds of cold: the cold of the court, which devoured, and the cold of exhausted mortals standing shoulder to shoulder because there was nowhere else to stand. The hounds came first, pouring through the gap like spilled ink, and the wardens' spears found them — or didn't, because a spear through a hole in the world is a spear wasted — and Bramble's charms blazed and snapped and held, held, held.

Ysolde fought in the style of the court, because it was the only style she knew: economical, merciless, every motion costing exactly what it needed to cost and no more. Her blade drank the grey knights' attention and gave back ruin. She heard Dov laughing somewhere to her left, actually laughing, roaring something obscene about the parentage of ice, and his axe rose and fell like a ship's anchor in a storm.

For an hour — or a year; time had gone strange since the stars died — they held.

Then Vess grew bored of watching.

Ysolde saw it happen. Saw the palanquin tilt forward. Saw the Lady of Winter lift one hand, delicately, the way a woman lifts a teacup, and speak a single word in a language older than the covenant.

The temperature fell through the floor of the world.

The snow stopped falling and started *arriving* — everywhere at once, driven sideways, a white roar that erased the sky and the ground and the difference between them. Wardens froze where they stood, mid-swing, becoming statues with terrified eyes. Bramble screamed something and her charm-net flared gold and then guttered brown, burnt out, spent. Through the white blindness Ysolde heard Dov bellowing the retreat — *fall back, fall back to the shrine, that's an order, I'm the captain, fall back* —

And then she heard him stop.

She found him afterward, or rather she found the place where he had made his stand, because the fighting had swept past and the narrows behind him were choked with broken ice-armor and the shapes of hounds dissolving into ordinary shadow. He had planted himself in the throat of the pass with his axe buried to the eye in a knight's chest, and the cold had taken him from the feet upward, and he had not let go. He was frozen upright, axe and all, his grin still fixed — insufferably alive, forever.

Ysolde knelt in the snow beside him. She did not weep. The court had taught her that trick too well: grief without water, grief like dry ice, burning.

"I'm sorry," she told him, in the old tongue, the tongue of the court he had hated, because it was the only language she had for sorry. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

Somewhere below, the shrine fire answered her — a flicker, a pulse, as if it had heard.

* * *

They fell back to the shrine in ones and twos. Of the twelve who had held the narrows, four walked down the mountain. Sefa came last, carrying a wounded warden on her back, her face grey with exhaustion and something worse than exhaustion. Bramble stumbled in empty-handed, her belt bare, her charms all ash, her hands shaking so badly Ysolde had to hold them steady while the hedge-witch tried to bind wounds.

Wren met them at the doors.

He looked at the survivors. He counted them, Ysolde saw him count them, and she watched the arithmetic land in his eyes like stones into deep water. Four wardens. One witch. One deserter. One defrocked warden-captain. Himself.

"And Dov?" he asked, though his voice already knew.

"Held the pass," Sefa said simply.

Wren closed his eyes. For a long moment he stood in the doorway with the snow swirling around him, and Ysolde thought he might break — might finally, after all these miles and betrayals and buried names, break open. But when he opened his eyes again they were clear, and terrible, and calm.

"Inside," he said. "All of you. It's time."

The shrine's heart was a round chamber of black stone, and at its center the ember burned — smaller now, Ysolde realized with a lurch, smaller than it had been yesterday, smaller than it had been that morning. The final snows were falling outside, the last snows of the long winter, and they were drinking the fire's strength with every hour. The dying firekeeper knelt beside it, ancient and translucent, more smoke than flesh.

Vess's voice came through the stone walls then, soft as sleet against a window, and everywhere at once:

*"Little heir. I know what you mean to do. I knew your grandfather, and I will tell you what he learned too late: the covenant does not save. It only chooses whom it devours. Come out. Give me the ember willingly, and I will let your friends see the thaw."*

"Liar," Bramble whispered.

"Of course she's lying," Wren said. He rolled up his sleeves, unhurried, a man preparing for ordinary work. "But she's not wrong about the price." He looked at each of them in turn — Sefa, Bramble, Ysolde — and Ysolde saw that he was memorizing them, and her heart cracked along a fault line she hadn't known was there. "Whatever happens next, I need you to know it was worth it. All of it. Every mile."

"Wren—" Ysolde started.

The doors burst inward.

Not opened — *burst*, torn from their hinges by a hand of packed snow and malice, and through them poured the white tide, knights and hounds and the cold itself, filling the shrine chamber like water filling a lung. Above them, borne on the shoulders of her knights, Lady Vess descended, and her frozen crown glittered, and her voice was almost gentle:

*"Last chance, Wren Adeyemi Oyelaran."*

His full name. His true name, spoken aloud in that devouring voice, and Ysolde flinched to hear it — but Wren did not. He stepped forward, past the wardens' raised spears, past Bramble's empty hands, past Ysolde's outstretched fingers, until he stood between the court and the ember, a single figure backlit by a fire the size of a fist.

"Yes," he said. "That's my name."

He knelt at the ember. He cupped his hands around its failing light. And he began to speak.
