## 9. A Name Given Freely

The ember was the size of a sparrow's heart.

Wren knelt before it on the cold stone of the shrine floor, and it seemed impossible — obscene, almost — that everything should come down to something so small. Behind him the shrine doors groaned against Lady Vess's assault; he could hear Dov's voice raised in a battle-cry that cut off too soon, and Ysolde's answering shout, and the shriek of ice against iron. The firekeeper lay dying three paces away, Sefa's hands pressed to the old woman's wound, and the light that had burned in this place for nine hundred years was guttering down to that single coal.

*Speak your name,* the firekeeper had said. *Freely. All of it. The ember will know if you lie.*

Wren had spent eleven years teaching himself not to say it.

Not the way other exiles avoided their names — he'd never been ashamed of being Wren, never flinched when smugglers and freeholders shouted it across tavern rooms. It was the rest of it he'd buried. The middle name his mother sang over his cradle. The surname that opened doors in Thornfell's capital and closed them just as fast in the border towns. The lineage written into ward-stone and covenant-scroll, the inheritance he'd renounced at nineteen with a forged writ and a stolen horse, riding east into the passes where nobody asked who your grandfather was because everybody was running from something themselves.

He had told himself it was freedom. He had almost believed it.

Now the ember pulsed faintly, like a thing listening, and the dark beyond the shrine walls pressed in with the weight of an ocean, and Wren understood that he had never been free at all. He had only been hiding, and hiding has a shape, and the shape of his hiding had been carved by other people's hands.

"Wren." Sefa's voice, low and ragged. She didn't look up from the firekeeper's wound. "Whatever you're waiting for — don't."

He wasn't waiting for courage. He was waiting for the last moment when he could still be no one.

The doors shuddered again. Through the gap beneath them, frost crept inward like fingers, delicate and patient, and where the frost touched the stone the shrine's warmth died with a sound like a sigh. He thought of Bramble, taken at the toll road, her hedge-witch charms still hanging crooked from the lintel where she'd nailed them for luck. He thought of Dov laughing on the deck of the *Kestrel's Debt*, saying *debts are just promises that learned to swim, Wren — you'll call yours in someday.* He thought of Ysolde standing in the snow outside the ice-hold, starlight-less and shaking, saying *they eat the dark, Wren. They eat it, and it eats them back.*

And he thought of his grandfather's hand on his head, one summer when Wren was small enough to be lifted, saying words he hadn't understood until Sefa spread the buried records across her table: *Someday this will be yours to hold or yours to break, little storm. No one else can carry it for you.*

They had tried. That was the whole rotten truth of it. They had engineered his exile so that no heir would stand at the Ember Shrine when the wards failed, so that the covenant would die unwitnessed, unclaimed, and the winter court could drink the starless dark forever. They had made him a fugitive to make him absent. Every mile of his exile had been someone else's design.

But this — kneeling here, choosing this — this would be his.

Wren rose.

His legs ached from the climb through the pass; his hands were split from the ice-hold bars; there was Vess-frost in the seams of his coat that would never melt out. None of it mattered. He stepped forward until the ember's heat touched his face, thin as a candleflame now, and he felt the old power stir in his chest — the storm-calling, the gift that had gotten a boy whipped in Thornfell's schoolyards and gotten a man banned from three freeholds, the lightning that lived coiled behind his sternum like a second heart.

It had always felt like a curse. That, too, had been a lie someone taught him.

"The doors," Sefa said behind him, and there was terror in it now, real terror, because the wood was splitting.

Wren closed his eyes.

"I am Wren Adeyemi Oyelaran," he said.

The words came out strange in his own mouth — not because they were hard to say, but because they were easy. Because they fit. Eleven years of half-names and false papers, and the true name slid home like a key cut long ago for exactly this lock.

"Son of Adaeze Oyelaran, daughter of the steppe-folk, and Tomas Oyelaran, warden of Thornfell." His voice grew steadier. "Grandson of Kofi Oyelaran, signatory of the Covenant of Ember and Star. Heir of both realms — of the human stone and the fae glass, of the mountain and the steppe, of every word my grandfather spoke and every word he failed to speak."

The ember flared.

Not much — a breath's worth of brightness, gold bleeding up through the red — but the whole shrine answered it. The dying wall-murders of light along the dome stirred; the firekeeper drew a rattling breath; even the frost at the doorstems paused, as if the dark itself had turned to listen.

"I was made to run," Wren said, "so that I would not be here. I ran. I hid. I called myself nothing and I let myself believe nothing was a kind of freedom." He opened his eyes. The ember reflected in them, small and fierce. "I am done believing it. My name is mine. My inheritance is mine. And what I do with them is mine alone — freely chosen, freely given."

He pressed both palms flat above the ember.

"Now take the storm."

* * *

It did not feel like losing.

That was the thing he would try to explain afterward, to Sefa, to anyone who asked, and never quite manage: that giving away the storm-calling felt less like amputation than like exhaling after holding a breath since childhood. The power rose through him — he felt it gather in his ribs, crackle along his arms, taste of ozone and rain on hot stone — and instead of fighting it, the way he had fought it all his life, he opened every door inside himself at once.

The storm left him like a river reaching the sea.

It poured down through his palms into the ember, and the ember drank it, and the shrine *roared*. Light climbed the walls. The dome-murals blazed — nine hundred years of painted stars waking one after another, and Wren realized with a jolt that they weren't decoration. They were a map. A ledger. Every star that had ever hung over the Glass Steppe and Thornfell both, recorded in fire-gold paint, waiting for someone to pay them back.

The payment began overhead.

Through the shattered dome, through the smoke and the snow and the screaming wind of Vess's assault, the first star reignited.

It was small, that first one — a pinprick, almost shy, as though it wasn't sure it was wanted. Then a second caught beside it, and a third, and then the sky remembered what it was for. Star after star bloomed back into being, each ignition sending a silent shockwave rolling across the heavens, and where the light fell, the dark that the winter court had hoarded and fed upon simply *ended*. Not pushed back. Ended — unraveled like thread pulled from a hem, like a lie meeting its witness.

Wren heard the change reach the battlefield.

The sounds outside shifted — the shriek of ice becoming the crack of ice *breaking*, the howl of the riders faltering into confusion, then panic. He heard Ysolde cry out, and it took him a heartbeat to understand that it wasn't grief. It was relief, raw and disbelieving, the sound of a deserter watching the court that had hunted her come apart in the sudden light.

He stayed kneeling. The last of the storm drained out of him, and what remained underneath was startlingly ordinary: a tired man with empty hands and aching knees and frostbitten ears. He felt lighter than he had since he was a boy. He felt, absurdly, hungry.

"Wren." Sefa's hand on his shoulder, gripping hard. Her face was streaked with ash and blood that might have been the firekeeper's. The old woman was gone — he saw that in Sefa's eyes before he saw the still form — but Sefa was looking past the body, past the doors, up through the dome at the sky filling with stars. "Look. *Look.*"

He looked.

Above the pass, the snow was turning to ordinary snow — falling straight, glittering, harmless, lit silver by a hundred reborn stars. And in the light of those stars, the winter court's host was coming undone. The riders' shapes thinned to smoke. Their armor of black frost sublimated into glittering mist. Whatever they had been made of — stolen dark, devoured names, the court's long hunger given legs — it needed the starless night to hold its shape, and the night was gone.

Somewhere on the mountainside, thin and furious and fading, a woman's voice screamed a name that was no longer hers to command.

Lady Vess did not die, exactly. She *unraveled* — Wren watched it from the shrine doorway, Sefa and Ysolde flanking him, Bramble leaning gray-faced against the doorframe with Dov's cloak around her shoulders. The lady of the winter court stood in the starlight with her army dissolving around her, and the darkness went out of her the way frost goes off a window in the morning, and what was left was small, and old, and briefly, terribly human. She looked up at the stars with an expression Wren would carry the rest of his life. Not hatred. Hunger, ending. The look of someone who had been cold for a very long time and had finally, finally stopped feeling it.

Then she was snow, drifting, and the wind carried her apart over the pass.

Nobody spoke for a while. The stars kept arriving, patient as seeds, until the whole sky was sewn with light and the Glass Steppe beyond the broken wards glimmered like its name.

Bramble broke the silence first, because of course she did. "You look terrible," she rasped at Wren. "You gave away your magic and it didn't even fix your face."

A laugh tore out of him — cracked, wet, helpless. Ysolde caught his arm as his knees buckled, and Sefa caught the other side, and for a moment the four of them stood swaying together in the shrine doorway like drunks, holding each other up under a sky full of returned stars.

Dov was not among them. The laughter died in Wren's throat, and he looked down the pass toward the toll rocks where the captain had made her stand, and the stars were bright enough now, cruelly bright, to show him everything.

"We have to bring her home," he said.

Sefa nodded once. "Tomorrow," she said gently. "Tonight we bury the firekeeper and we sleep, if any of us remember how. Tomorrow we go get our captain."

Wren looked back once at the ember as they turned to the work of the dead. It was burning steady now — not the guttering sparrows-heart of before but a true flame, gold-hearted, self-sustaining, fed by a name freely given and a storm freely surrendered. It would burn, he understood, as long as someone tended it. As long as someone stayed.

He found, examining himself in the ember's light, that he already knew he would be the one to stay.

Outside, the long winter finished falling — soft, ordinary, starlit snow — and somewhere below the shrine, in the valley where law had never reached, the people who had endured the dark were opening their doors to look up.
