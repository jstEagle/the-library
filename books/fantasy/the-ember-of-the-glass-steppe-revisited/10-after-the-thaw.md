## 10. After the Thaw

The first star came back on a Tuesday.

Wren remembered it that way because Dov had always kept count of the days by market weeks, and the morning the sky cracked open with light over the Glass Steppe, the smuggler had been three days dead and could not see it. Wren had stood on the shrine's broken steps with his hands empty — truly empty for the first time since he was nine years old, when the storms had first answered him — and watched the stars return one by one like lamps being lit in a house long abandoned. Each one hurt. Each one was worth it.

That had been forty-one days ago. He knew because Bramble counted too, scratching tallies into the doorframe of what had been the tollhouse and was now, improbably, becoming a home.

The thaw came slower than the stars. The snow did not melt so much as surrender, retreating up the passes in gray, slushy stages, uncovering the world beneath it the way a hand uncovers a face: the black pines first, then the slate roofs of Hollowmere, then the toll road itself, rutted and starved-looking but real, running east toward Thornfell proper and west into a fae country that no longer ate its own light. Wildflowers came up through the ice in places where nothing should have grown, pushing white blossoms through old battlefields, through the frozen mud where Dov had made her stand. Ysolde said the Steppe was grieving and blooming at once, which was the fae way of doing most things.

Wren spent the mornings learning how to be a man without a storm in his chest.

It was harder than he'd expected. Not the grief of it — he had expected the grief, had braced for it the way you brace for a blow — but the sheer strangeness of quiet weather. For twenty years his body had been a barometer, a tuning fork struck by every front that moved across the mountains. Now the wind blew and it simply blew. Rain fell and he got wet, ordinary water on an ordinary coat, and there was something almost obscene about it, something that made him laugh out loud alone on the ridge above town until the laughing turned into the other thing and he sat down in the wet grass and let it move through him like weather.

Sefa found him there. She didn't say anything, just lowered herself onto the slope beside him with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions, and they watched the clouds go by like strangers.

"First spring I've seen in eleven years," she said eventually. "The wardens' garrison at Kestrel Mark faced north. You never saw the sun come around."

"You could go back," Wren said. "They'll un-defrock you now. Half the realm owes you its winter."

"They'll offer," Sefa agreed. "And I'll refuse." She picked a stem of grass and shredded it with her thumbnail, a habit Wren had learned meant she was assembling words she didn't like the shape of. "I spent my whole life keeping a border, Wren. Walls, wards, lists of names allowed through and names turned away. And do you know what I learned? The law never once asked whether it was *right*. It only asked whether it was written down." She looked at him sideways. "I'd rather build something here. Something we make by hand."

Hollowmere had been a border town the way a scar is skin — technically whole, functionally a mark left by violence. Three hundred souls before the long winter; fewer than two hundred after. The wardens' waystation stood roofless. The shrine on the hill was a shell of scorched stone with a cold hearth at its heart, and Wren could not yet bring himself to climb past the threshold where he had spoken his name and felt himself become, all at once, everything he had spent a lifetime refusing to be.

But the people stayed. That was the thing that undid him daily, quietly: they *stayed*. Farmers who had buried children under the starless dark walked out into the new sunlight and planted barley anyway. A widow named Marta Osei took over the tollhouse ledger and started charging fair prices, which scandalized everyone who remembered the old tolls, which was to say everyone. Children who had never seen stars were being taught their names by lamplight, pointing up through the windows at a sky full of them.

*Justice here is made by hand,* Sefa had said, and Wren was beginning to understand what she meant. There was no magistrate within a hundred miles. No court would ride this far for a stolen goat or a cheated widow or a man beaten in the night over a debt. So the town was learning to hold its own weight — councils in the tavern on rest-days, Sefa arbitrating disputes with the patience of a woman who had spent decades de-escalating armed men, Bramble brewing salves and, when persuasion failed, very persuasive itching powders.

Wren built things. It turned out his hands wanted work even if his blood no longer carried lightning. He re-roofed the waystation with Ysolde, who learned carpentry with the alarming speed of someone determined to be good at everything her old court had forbidden her. He dug graves in the softening earth — Dov's last, beside the pass where she fell, marked with a cairn and her own iron knife driven into the stones, because she'd have hated a prayer and loved a weapon. He mended fences. He hauled timber. On the worst nights, when the silence where his storm used to be ached like a pulled tooth, he split wood until dawn, and the rhythm of it — lift, swing, crack — was enough.

Mostly.

* * *

Ysolde still flinched at mirrors.

Wren noticed it in the third week, in the way she angled herself away from the polished shield Sefa had hung by the door, in how she braided her hair by touch alone. He understood better than anyone what she saw when she caught her reflection: not a deserter's face but a stranger's, someone remade so thoroughly that the person she'd been before the winter court had gotten lost somewhere underneath.

He found her one evening on the shrine hill, sitting outside the threshold with her knees drawn up, watching the cold hearth through the broken doorway.

"It doesn't feel finished," she said, without looking at him. Fae hearing; she'd known his footsteps from the bottom of the path. "The fire went out. The stars came back. But the covenant — I can feel the seam of it, like a scar under a bandage. It holds, but it isn't healed."

"Is that possible? To heal it?"

"The old firekeepers tended it for generations. Feeding it, singing to it. A covenant isn't a wall, Wren. It's a relationship. Your grandfather thought it was a wall. That's why—" She stopped. She had been there at the ice-hold; she knew what Lady Vess had shown him, the proof that complicated everything: the ledgers in his grandfather's hand, the tolls levied on fae travelers, the small cruelties that had given Vess her grievance and her opening. "That's why it cracked. Walls don't need tending. Relationships do."

Wren sat down beside her. Below them Hollowmere was lighting its lamps one window at a time, and above them the stars were doing the same, and for a moment the town and the sky looked like each other.

"I keep waiting to regret it," he said. "The name. The calling. I keep waiting to wake up furious that I gave it away." He flexed his hands, empty, calloused now from rope and hammer instead of crackling with borrowed thunder. "I don't. Some mornings I miss it so badly I can't breathe. And underneath that, all the time, there's this — *rightness*. Like I set down a weight I forgot I was carrying."

"That's what a true name does," Ysolde said softly. "When it's given freely. It doesn't take something from you. It tells you what you actually are." Her mouth curved, wry and sad. "My court never understood that. They thought names were leashes."

"What will you do? When the Steppe opens fully. You could go home."

"This is home," she said, simply, and the word landed in Wren's chest like a struck bell. "Besides. Someone has to teach your hedge-witch not to put foxglove in the fever tea."

From the darkness below, faintly, came Bramble's voice raised in argument with the foxglove in question.

* * *

Summer came early that year, or so the old-timers said, though Wren suspected the old-timers said that every year. The barley came up green along the toll road. Marta Osei's ledger filled with honest numbers. Two families arrived from the east in June, refugees of a different kind — fleeing debts, fleeing a magistrate, fleeing a life that hadn't fit — and Sefa met them at the bridge with bread and questions, and by week's end they had roofs over their heads and stakes in the common field.

On midsummer's eve the town held its first festival in living memory. Bramble brewed something illegal in three realms and legal nowhere, which everyone drank anyway. Sefa danced twice, stiffly, and pretended afterward that it hadn't happened. Ysolde taught the children a fae circle-dance that was probably a blessing and possibly a mild curse, and refused to clarify. Wren stood at the edge of the firelight with a cup he wasn't drinking and watched his strange, ragged, impossible family scattered through the crowd, alive, laughing, *here*, and let the ache and the joy sit together in him without trying to separate them.

Later, when the fires burned low, he climbed the shrine hill alone. This time he crossed the threshold.

The hearth was cold ash. But kneeling, pressing his palm flat against the ancient stone, he felt it — faint as a pulse in a sleeping wrist, faint as starlight. The seam Ysolde had spoken of. Holding. Waiting to be tended.

"We'll come back," he told it. Not a vow, exactly. An introduction. "Every year. We'll sing to it, if that's what it wants. We'll feed it. We're not good at walls, any of us. But relationships—" He smiled at the dark. "We're learning."

Behind him, boots on stone: Sefa, Ysolde, Bramble, drawn up the hill by whatever instinct binds people who have bled together. They didn't ask what he was doing. They just sat down around the cold hearth, four silhouettes against the doorway and the stars beyond it, and began, unhurried, to talk about tomorrow — the roof on the east barn, the road crew, the letter Sefa intended to write to Kestrel Mark refusing her reinstatement in language that would make a bishop blush.

Wren listened, and added his voice, and understood at last that exile had never been a place. It was a name you refused to answer to. He answered to his now — Wren Adeyemi Oyelaran, heir of both realms, keeper of a cold hearth in a town that made its own justice by hand — and the stars over Thornfell burned steady, and the long winter was over, and the work, the good work, the work of staying, had only just begun.
