## 1. The Weight of Ink

The ink was the first thing Ansel Quintana learned to fear, and the last thing they ever stopped loving.

It sat in its stone well at the corner of the copying desk, black as a pupil, and it drank the light of the tallow lamp in a way ordinary ink did not. Guild ink. Ledger ink. It was ground from soot and gall and something else the apprentices were never told, and it had a weight to it that no other substance in the Glass Steppe possessed — a weight that settled into the wrist after an hour of writing, into the shoulder by the third hour, into the spine by the end of a shift, until a scribe who had served twenty years walked with a permanent stoop, bent like a question mark over a page that never asked to be answered.

Ansel had been bending for nine years now.

The copying hall of the Ledger Guild stretched away in long ranks of desks, each one lit by its own lamp, each one occupied by its own scribe, and the silence between them was not the silence of empty rooms but the silence of held breath. Two hundred pens moving. Two hundred nibs whispering against vellum in the particular rhythm of transcription — not composition, never composition; scribes did not compose, scribes *copied* — and beneath it all the faint creak of the building itself, the guildhall settling its old bones around them like a patient animal.

Ansel dipped the pen. Drew it along the lip of the well so that no drop would fall where drops should not fall. And began again.

*Received, one bottle, sealed in blue glass: thunder, taken from the storm of the ninth of Harvesttide, as it broke over the Cindermere hills. Condition: furious. Buyer: the Lady Vess of the Salt Terraces. Price: three hundred crowns and the forgiveness of her husband's gambling debts.*

Ansel wrote it out twice — once for the master ledger, once for the duplicate that went into the fireproof vault below, where the true records lived behind iron doors that required three keys and a confession to open. The words came out even and small and perfect. They always did. Ansel's hand was the finest in the hall, though saying so aloud would have been the fastest way to make enemies of every other scribe on the night shift, and so Ansel kept the knowledge folded up inside like a letter never sent.

*Condition: furious.*

That was the part that caught in the throat, if you let it. The ledger did not deal in metaphors. If the entry said the bottled thunder was furious, then somewhere in the vaults below there was a bottle of blue glass with a storm inside it, beating itself against the walls, wanting out. Ansel had seen the vaults exactly once, as an apprentice, during the tour they gave to new indentures — a corridor of shelves receding into dark, jars and boxes and caskets and coffers, each labeled in that same heavy ink, each humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has supposedly ended. One shelf had been nothing but bottles of luck. Another had held, if the label was to be believed, the last honest laugh of a dead king.

You could sell anything, if you wrote it down properly. That was the first lesson of the Ledger Guild, delivered by the Guildmaster himself to every intake of debt-bound children, in his voice like dry paper turning: *The world is full of things that have no price. Our work is to give them one. Once a thing has a price, it can be owned. Once it can be owned, it can be sold. And once it is sold—* here he would pause, and smile, and the smile never reached anywhere near his eyes, *—it stays sold.*

Ansel's own entry was on page four hundred and eleven of the indenture rolls. They had copied it themselves, years ago, under supervision, because the Guild believed — correctly, Ansel had to admit — that there was no lesson in ownership quite like writing out the terms of your own captivity in your best hand.

*Debtor: Quintana, Ansel. Debt incurred: funeral costs of Maren Quintana, mother, deceased; apothecary's fees, outstanding; interest, compounded quarterly. Term: twenty years. Collateral: the debtor's labor, all of it, and the debtor's hand, which is judged exceptional.*

Eleven years left. Eleven years, two months, and some number of days that Ansel refused to calculate precisely because calculating it precisely meant admitting how much of a life could be measured in quarters and compounding. Nine years served. Nine years of lamplight and vellum and the slow accumulation of that ink-weight in the bones. Twenty-nine years old, or nearly — birthdays were not observed in the guildhall, being unprofitable — and the sum total of what Ansel owned outright was a tin cup, a wool coat with patched elbows, a hidden talent for reading other people's handwriting, and a memory good enough to be dangerous.

The dangerous part was the memory. Ansel had never told anyone about it, not in nine years, because the guild rewarded useful talents and disappeared inconvenient ones, and a scribe who remembered everything they transcribed was both. Every entry Ansel had ever copied lived somewhere behind the eyes, filed and cross-referenced, retrievable at will. It made Ansel the fastest corrector in the hall. It also meant that Ansel knew things about the Steppe's great families that no living person outside this room should know — who had bought whose grief, who had pawned whose courage, which lordling's famous victory had been purchased secondhand from a mercenary captain down on his luck.

Knowledge like that was a knife you couldn't put down. So Ansel carried it carefully, alone, and trusted no one with the hilt.

Trust was expensive. Everything was expensive. That was rather the point of the world.

* * *

The night shift ran from the lighting of the lamps to the bells of dawn, and somewhere past midnight the entries began to change character, the way they always did. Daytime ledgers recorded the respectable trades — grain futures, ward-tithes, the leasing of memories too painful to keep. But the deep hours belonged to the impossible things, and the impossible things came in from the counting-rooms upstairs written in the hands of brokers who were paid very well to have no curiosity whatsoever.

Ansel dipped, wiped, wrote.

*Received, one year of luck, belonging to Tammas Reeve, cooper, of the Tannery Quarter. Condition: middling, with one notable misfortune already spent (see appendix). Buyer: anonymous. Price: waived — trade conducted against future consideration.*

Future consideration. Ansel had copied that phrase eleven hundred times and still did not know what it meant, only that whenever it appeared, the entry was flagged with a small mark in the margin — a circle with a line through it, like an eye being closed — and the flag pages were collected each morning by a man called Corvane, who wore the grey livery of the Guildmaster's own office and who had once, without any change of expression, picked up a dropped pen from Ansel's desk and set it back down again with the nib facing Ansel, as a courtesy, while looking directly into Ansel's eyes the entire time.

Corvane collected the flagged pages now, making his rounds through the hall with his soft, deliberate steps. Ansel kept writing. The trick with Corvane was to be boring. Boring scribes kept their hands. Interesting ones got reassigned, and everyone in the hall knew what reassignment meant, even though no one said it: a transfer to the lower vaults, where the work was the sealing of things rather than the recording of them, and from which scribes returned changed, or quiet, or not at all.

The flagged page left Ansel's desk in Corvane's gloved fingers. Ansel did not watch it go. Watching was interesting.

The lamps burned lower. Around the hall, scribes finished their quotas and signed out, their footsteps fading down the stone stairs, until only the skeleton crew remained — six desks, six pools of light, six bent shadows thrown enormous against the high dark ceiling. Someone coughed. Somewhere far above, wind moved across the glass flats and made the whole guildhall sing faintly, a note like a wet finger on a bowl's rim, which was why the Steppe was called the Glass Steppe, and why the stars here had always seemed closer than stars ought to be, lying just beyond the panes of the sky like coins at the bottom of shallow water.

Ansel looked up at them sometimes, on the walk between the dormitory and the hall. Counted them, without meaning to. There had been a fixed number once — the ward-keepers' almanacs listed them, named them, tracked their slow wheeling — and Ansel's memory, that cursed accurate thing, had noticed something over the past season that it kept trying to bring to attention and that Ansel kept declining to examine.

Some of the stars were missing. Not clouded over. Not dimmed. Missing, the way a tooth is missing — a precise absence with edges.

There was no one to tell. Or rather, there was no one to whom telling would not be a form of suicide. So Ansel counted the absences instead, privately, the way one counts money owed, and wrote the impossible trades, and waited for the eleven years to run out, and told no one anything.

* * *

It was in the fourth hour past midnight, with the hall down to three scribes and the silence gone thick enough to lean on, that the runner came down from the counting-rooms with a single sheet.

Runners were common enough. What was uncommon was the runner's face — a boy of perhaps fifteen, white around the mouth, holding the sheet the way one holds something that might bite. He brought it not to the senior scribe on duty but directly to Ansel, which was itself wrong, and said, in a voice pitched low enough to be deniable:

"From Master Ellery-vault. Says it's to be entered tonight, before the bells. Says—" the boy swallowed "—says to copy it fast and forget it faster."

Then he was gone, up the stairs, at a pace just short of running.

Ansel unfolded the sheet.

The handwriting was Master Ellery's, and Master Ellery was the keeper of the inner vaults, and his hand was famously immaculate — but this was not immaculate. This was hurried, the letters leaning like drunks, the ink blotted in two places as though the pen had been pressed down hard enough to hurt the page. Whatever Ellery had been writing, he had been afraid while writing it, and Master Ellery did not frighten, or had never previously been known to.

The entry read:

*Received, this hour, into the deepest hold: one moon, whole. Seller: withheld. Buyer: withheld. Price: [entry suspended]. Note: the item is not to be catalogued, appraised, copied, or spoken of. This entry to be destroyed upon completion of transaction. By order of the Guildmaster's own office.*

Ansel read it three times.

The hall was very quiet. The two remaining scribes scratched on at their distant desks, oblivious, and the lamps burned, and the wind sang its thin note over the glass flats, and Ansel sat with a page in their hands that said the guild had taken possession of the moon.

Not a piece of moon-glass. Not a shard, a sliver, a fallen fragment such as prospectors occasionally pried out of the flats and sold to collectors. *One moon, whole.* Which was impossible, which was worse than impossible, because the moon was still in the sky — Ansel had walked under it not six hours ago, that pale worn coin hanging low over the wards, close enough that its light lay on the Steppe like spilled milk. You could not receive into a vault a thing that was still visibly overhead. Unless—

Unless what hung overhead was not the whole of it. Unless what hung overhead was what remained.

Ansel's mind, that treacherous accurate machine, began assembling the pattern without permission. The missing stars, precise-edged, increasing by ones and twos across the seasons. The flagged entries marked *future consideration*, collected by Corvane, destination unknown. The ward-tithes rising year over year, though the wards were older than the guild and needed nothing. The buyer: withheld. The seller: withheld. A transaction so large it had no counterpart in nine years of total recall — except that it must have had counterparts, must have been assembled piece by piece, entry by innocent entry, and Ansel had copied every one of them and filed every one of them and never once looked up from the page to see what was being built.

*This entry to be destroyed upon completion of transaction.*

Ansel looked at the sheet. Looked at the blank master-ledger page waiting open on the desk. The rules were absolute: everything received was entered; everything entered was copied; everything copied existed forever in duplicate, in iron, in ink. But this entry ordered its own erasure, and orders from the Guildmaster's office were not rules, they were the thing rules were made of.

The safe thing — the survivable thing, the boring thing, the thing that kept hands attached to wrists — was to write it out, flag it, let Corvane take it, and spend the rest of a short life not thinking about it.

Ansel dipped the pen. The ink welled up black and patient, drinking the lamplight, weighing on the nib.

Ansel wrote the entry. Twice. Evenly. Perfectly. In the finest hand in the hall.

And then, because the memory behind their eyes had already done the unforgivable thing and *kept* it — because the entry was now filed in the one vault no enforcer could raid, no fire could reach, no debt could claim — Ansel did the second unforgivable thing. On the reverse of the sheet, in a hand deliberately unlike their own, small enough to hide beneath a thumbnail, they copied it a third time. Then they folded the sheet along its original creases, flagged the ledger pages with the closed-eye mark, and sat back, heart going hard, listening to the sound of their own breathing and the scratch of the two distant pens and the singing of the wind.

The entry would be destroyed within the hour. Corvane would see to it, or Ellery would, or the Guildmaster's own grey hands. By dawn there would be no record anywhere in the guildhall that the moon had ever been received.

Except in the iron vaults below, where the true duplicates slept behind three keys and a confession.

And except in the head of a debt-bound scribe with eleven years left, who had just become the most dangerous kind of witness in the Glass Steppe: the kind who did not yet know it.

Ansel worked on until the bells of dawn. Nothing else unusual came down the stairs. At the sign-out book, the duty clerk took Ansel's quota sheet, frowned at the flagged entry, and initialed it without comment, and Ansel walked back across the hall past the other two scribes, who nodded the nod of people who share a cage and do not discuss the bars.

Outside, the sky was doing something it had never done in nine years of walking this route at this hour.

The moon was setting — that was normal, the moon set at dawn half the month. But it was setting wrong. It went down behind the ward-towers pale and thinned, like a coin rubbed between fingers, like a thing that had been handled too much. And as Ansel stood in the grey cold watching it go, the last sliver of it flickered. Briefly. The way a lamp flickers when someone, somewhere, lifts its cover to check how much oil is left.

Then it was gone below the horizon, and the eastern sky began to lighten, and the stars — Ansel counted without meaning to, that cursed machine behind the eyes tallying the absences — the stars were fewer than they had been yesterday. Three fewer. Precise-edged. Gone.

Behind Ansel, up in the counting-rooms, a bell rang once — the transaction bell, the one that rang when a sale was completed and an entry was closed. Then it rang again, which it never did, and a third time, and Ansel understood, standing alone in the cold on the guildhall steps, that somewhere below in the deepest hold, a page was burning.

They went inside. They washed their pen. They reported for the day shift, and bent over the vellum, and wrote out the morning's first entry in a hand that did not shake, because shaking was interesting, and interesting scribes got reassigned.

But all that day, and all through the next night, and every night after, in the space between one entry and the next, Ansel Quintana thought about the deepest hold, and the thing sleeping there that had once been overhead, and the eleven years stretching ahead like a sentence being served in a cell with a window.

And on the fourth night, the summons came — a grey-liveried runner, a slip of paper, the Guildmaster's seal — requesting the presence of Scribe Quintana in the upper offices, regarding *a matter of transcription*.

Ansel folded the slip into their sleeve, and followed the runner up the stairs, and did not look back at the hall of bent backs and burning lamps, because whatever waited above, it was already too late to be anything but interesting.
