## 2. Thirteen Turns of Nothing

The ward-stone at Kiln's Rest had begun to drink.

Sefa knew the stones the way a shepherd knows a flock — every one of them by name, by grain, by the particular hum they made against her palm at dawn inspection. She had walked the border circuit for nineteen years, from Saltmere Gate to the Ashen Ford, and in all that time she had never once felt a stone pull *inward*. The wards were built to channel starlight, to catch it as it fell through the thin hours of night and weave it into the long lattice that kept the Steppe's edges sealed. Light went *through* them, the way water goes through a millwheel, and the turning of that wheel was the whole quiet machinery of their safety.

But when she laid her hand on the Kiln's Rest stone that morning, frost still clinging to its carved channels, she felt something she had no word for. A hunger. A slow, patient drawing, like a mouth at a well. The stone was not passing the starlight on. It was keeping it.

She knelt there for a long time in the grey cold, her breath smoking, her fingers pressed flat against the granite, and told herself there was an explanation. There was always an explanation. Stones settled. Channels silted with dust and old light-residue. Nineteen years of service did not make her infallible; it only made her tired enough to imagine things.

Except the stone was warm. Ward-stones were never warm. They ran cool as riverbeds even in high summer, because they gave away everything they gathered. This one was warm as a sleeping animal, and when she pulled her hand back, her palm tingled with a numbness that took several minutes to fade.

She wrote it up anyway. That was the discipline of ward-keeping: you reported what you found, not what you feared. She spent the evening at the waystation desk composing her report in the careful, unadorned hand her superiors had drilled into her two decades ago — *anomalous retention observed at Kiln's Rest station, stone warm to touch, suspected channel blockage, requesting senior assessment* — and she sealed it with wax and sent it east with the courier before doubt could make her soften the language.

Then she climbed the tower and waited for dark, because if the stone was drinking, she wanted to see where the light was going.

* * *

The stars over the Glass Steppe had always seemed close enough to harvest. Sefa's mother used to say they hung low out of courtesy, so the wards could reach them. On clear nights the whole sky above the border looked like spilled salt across black cloth, dense and glittering, and the ward-lattice caught it all — every keeper learned to read the lattice the way sailors read currents, seeing in the faint silver flow above the stones which channels ran strong and which had gone thin.

That night, the flow above Kiln's Rest was wrong.

Sefa stood at the tower rail with her charts pinned under a river-stone and watched starlight bend. Not scatter, not dim — *bend*, curving down toward the ward-stone below like rain finding a gutter. The lattice, which should have run straight and level from horizon to horizon, sagged visibly toward the point where she stood, a great slow concavity in the fabric of the night, as though the sky itself were being drawn into a funnel.

And at the center of the funnel, the star directly overhead — Vessel, the bright one, the one every apprentice learned first — flickered.

She watched it for an hour. It did not go out. But each flicker lasted a little longer than the last, each recovery a little weaker, like a lamp running low on oil, and Sefa stood in the cold with her knuckles white on the rail and understood, with the certainty that comes not from evidence but from the body, that she was watching something die.

She wrote a second report that night. She did not soften this one either. *Starlight deflection confirmed. Lattice integrity compromised within radius of three miles. Recommend immediate suspension of all ward activity along the eastern circuit pending investigation.* She signed it, sealed it, and put it in the courier box beside the first, and then she sat alone in the waystation kitchen drinking tea gone bitter from steeping too long, listening to the wind move through the empty bunks of the apprentices' quarters.

There had been four apprentices assigned to the eastern circuit at the start of the season. There were three now. Tam had left in the spring, without ceremony, his bunk stripped and a single line left on the table: *This work is dying and I won't die with it.* She had reported his desertion, as required. She had also, privately, envied him.

* * *

The reply came back in nine days, which meant someone senior had actually read it, and it was the worst reply she could have received: not a summons, but a correction.

*Warden Sefa,* it began, in the Guildmaster's own office-hand, dry and precise as ledger ink. *Your reports have been reviewed. No anomalous retention has been recorded at any other station on the eastern circuit. The lattice shows normal flow. We note that your station is the oldest on the circuit and that its stones predate the current calibration standard by forty years. We further note that Warden Sefa has filed six anomaly reports in the past year, all of which resolved upon inspection or proved attributable to equipment age.*

*You are directed to recalibrate your instruments, re-inspect your circuit using standard methodology, and refrain from further speculative filings. Repeated unsubstantiated reports are themselves a form of negligence. The wards are the Steppe's oldest covenant. They do not fail.*

*They do not fail.* Sefa read the sentence four times. It was not an argument; it was a creed, recited the way one recites a prayer precisely because no one wants to examine it. And beneath the creed, in the space between the lines, she heard the thing her superiors never said aloud: *the wards cannot be allowed to fail, because we have no second plan, and a woman who says otherwise is more dangerous than any failure.*

She thought about the six reports. The cracked channel at Saltmere that "resolved" after a junior warden spent an afternoon chiseling the evidence smooth. The resonance drift at the Ford that turned out, when she finally got permission to dig, to be a buried stone — buried deliberately, its glyphs scored flat, as though someone had wanted it silent. She had filed that finding too. She had never received a reply.

She was not a hysterical woman. She knew what she was: a careful one, in a profession that had stopped wanting care and started wanting compliance. And she knew — the way she knew the weight of a stone under her hand — that the difference between those two words was about to matter very much.

So she did what careful people do. She began keeping her own records.

* * *

For eleven nights she measured. She rigged a simple light-catch above the Kiln's Rest stone — a bowl of polished tin that would fill with gathered starlight and show, by its brightness at dawn, how much the lattice had passed through. On the first night the bowl filled to a depth she marked with a scratch. By the fourth night the mark sat higher than the fill. By the seventh, the bowl held barely a skin of light, and the stone beneath it was warm enough that frost melted around its base in a ring, so that every morning the Kiln's Rest ward sat in a circle of bare earth like a thing breathing.

And the stars. She charted them nightly, obsessively, mapping the lattice-flow against the sky. The deflection cone widened. Two more stars joined Vessel in its failing flicker. On the tenth night she noticed the pattern she had been dreading: the failures were not random. They moved. Night by night, star by star, the drinking spread westward along the circuit, following the line of the stones, orderly as a row of lamps being snuffed by a walking hand.

Someone or something was going down the border, star by star, and it was doing it *methodically*.

On the eleventh night, her last apprentice came to her at the tower door. His name was Odo, sixteen years old, earnest, freckled, the best of the three — the only one who still asked questions instead of just performing the drills. He stood in the doorway holding his kit-roll already strapped, and Sefa looked at him and knew before he spoke.

"I'm sorry, Warden," he said. "My father got a letter. From the Guild. They're offering transfer to the capital. Interior posting. Warm rooms, real pay." He swallowed. "He says I'd be a fool to stay on a dying circuit."

"A dying circuit," Sefa repeated.

"That's what the letter calls it." Odo wouldn't meet her eyes. "It said — it said the eastern stations are being consolidated. That the ward-work there is winding down." He finally looked up, and his face was full of a misery he clearly didn't understand himself. "Warden, if it's winding down, why does it need winding? If nothing's wrong—"

"Go," Sefa said quietly. "Take the transfer. Don't throw away your life standing next to me while I shout at a wall."

"But the stars—"

"The stars will still be wrong tomorrow whether you're here or not." She softened it with a hand on his shoulder, the closest thing to tenderness her profession permitted. "You asked good questions, Odo. Keep asking them wherever they post you. That's all I ever really taught you."

He left before dawn. She watched him go down the road toward the capital with the sunrise behind him, a small figure carrying everything he owned, and then she turned back to her tower, her charts, her warm dead stone, and her empty station, and she felt the isolation settle over her like a coat of lead.

That was the shape of it, she thought. That was how the truth died — not with a refutation, but with a transfer offer. You didn't argue with the lone truth-teller. You just made sure everyone else had somewhere better to be.

* * *

On the thirteenth night, Vessel went out.

Sefa was awake for it — she had been awake for all of them now, sleeping in snatches through the day like a woman keeping vigil at a deathbed — and she saw it happen, and it was nothing like she expected. There was no flare, no final struggle. The star simply dimmed between one heartbeat and the next, smoothly, the way a hand closes, and the patch of sky where it had been was not black but *absent* — a hole with no depth to it, a place where looking yielded nothing, as though the sky had been cut and something behind the sky was showing through.

The lattice screamed.

Not in sound — in the only language the wards had. Every stone on the eastern circuit rang at once, a harmonic shriek that came up through the ground and through the tower's bones and through Sefa's teeth, and the light-flow that remained tore loose from the broken anchor-point and whipped westward like a banner in a gale. She felt it pass through her, that fleeing light, a pressure and a grief together, and then the sky over Kiln's Rest had one fewer star than it had held since before her grandmother's grandmother, and the darkness where Vessel had been seemed to lean down over the land like a face bending close.

Sefa did the only thing left to a keeper whose warnings had been refused. She lit every lamp in the station, dragged her charts and her tin bowl and her private records into a satchel, and sat down to write the report that would end her career.

*To whoever reads this,* she wrote. *I am done asking permission to be believed. The eastern lattice is failing by design, not decay. The stars are being taken, one per turn, thirteen in all — I have charted the sequence and it ends at midwinter, at the new moon, at a turn of nothing. Whatever is drinking the light is being paid for it. Nothing drinks this carefully for free.*

*I am taking my records west. If the Guild will not hear me, I will find someone who can read them.*

She signed it, weighed the page under the river-stone on the empty desk where four apprentices' bunks had gone bare one by one, and shouldered her satchel.

At the tower door she paused and looked back once — at the warm stone in its ring of bare earth, at the hole in the sky where Vessel had been, at nineteen years of service hanging around her like a shed skin. Then Warden Sefa of the eastern circuit walked out of her station and did not lock the door behind her, because there was nothing left inside worth stealing except the truth, and she was carrying it with her.

Behind her, far to the west over the capital, the second star began to flicker.
