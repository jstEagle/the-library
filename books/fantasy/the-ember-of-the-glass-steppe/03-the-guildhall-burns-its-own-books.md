## 3. The Guildhall Burns Its Own Books

The summons came at the ninth bell, folded into a routine delivery of blank vellum, which was how the Guild delivered everything that mattered. Ansel found the slip tucked between two sheets of unmarked foolscap — a single line in the Guildmaster's own hand, which no one below the rank of Under-Ledger ever saw and lived to describe:

*Copy Room Three. Bring your burn-box. Come alone.*

Ansel read it twice, then a third time with their thumb pressed flat against the paper as though they could hold the words still long enough to mean something other than what they meant. Burn-box. The phrase sat in the stomach like a swallowed coin. Every scribe learned about burn-boxes in their first year — the iron-lined cases with the hinged lids and the little brass vents that let smoke out but let nothing else escape — and every scribe learned, in the same breathless lesson, never to wonder too loudly why the Guild kept so many of them.

They went down through the guildhall's morning traffic with the slip crushed in their fist. The building was already awake around them: clerks hauling trolleys of bound volumes toward the Assessment Halls, ink-mixers carrying their reeking pails up from the cellars, a pair of Assessors arguing over whether a bottled thunderstorm counted as perishable goods for tax purposes. Ordinary things. Ledger things. Ansel had walked these corridors for eleven years and had once found the rhythm of them comforting — the great machine of debt turning over and over, everything entered, everything owed, everything accounted for. Lately the machine had begun to sound different to them. Not louder. Hungrier.

Copy Room Three occupied a corner of the sub-levels where the daylight never reached and the oil lamps burned behind green glass, so that everything done there happened in a light like pond water. It was the room where pages went to die. Misentries, voided contracts, records of trades the Guild had decided had never happened — all of it came down to Room Three to be struck through, dissolved, or burned, depending on how thoroughly it needed to have never existed.

Two people were waiting when Ansel arrived.

The first was Under-Ledger Corvane, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the apologetic posture of someone who had spent thirty years being sorry on behalf of his superiors. He would not meet Ansel's eyes.

The second was Vess.

Everyone in the guildhall knew Vess without knowing her name. She was the Guildmaster's enforcer, though *enforcer* was far too warm a word; she was the reason the euphemisms worked. When the Guild said *debt reassignment*, it was Vess who did the assigning. When an apprentice vanished from the rolls overnight, leaving a bunk bare and a name struck through in the master register, it was Vess who had signed the strike-through. She stood by the cold hearth with her hands folded in front of her, wearing the grey traveling coat she wore when she expected to be outside after dark, and she watched Ansel come in the way a cat watches a door it has already decided is closed.

"Scribe Quintana," Corvane said, and cleared his throat. "Thank you for coming promptly."

"Was I given a choice?" Ansel asked, and immediately wished they hadn't, because Corvane flinched and Vess smiled very slightly, the way one smiles at a small animal that has just discovered teeth it cannot yet use.

"There's been," Corvane said, "an irregularity. In the recent entries." He gestured at the worktable, and Ansel saw the box.

It was a document case, plain grey board, sealed with the Guildmaster's wax. And beneath it, stacked in neat bundles tied with ledger-twine, lay perhaps forty loose pages — and Ansel knew those pages. They had copied some of them. They had copied *all* of them, over the past six days: the entries that should not have existed. *One moon, whole.* The bottled thunder sold twice to the same buyer. A year of a ferryman's luck, purchased by a woman whose name had been written in a hand that shook. The fragment-entries, each one a sliver of something vast described in the small cold language of commerce — *lot seven, remainder, condition: singing.*

"Room Three has been given the task of correcting the record," Corvane said. "These pages are misentries. Errors of transcription. They will be destroyed today, and the destruction itself will be entered and certified, and then the matter will be closed."

"Misentries," Ansel repeated.

"Clerical error," Corvane said. "It happens."

It does not happen, Ansel thought. That was the whole point of the Guild, the entire architecture of the place: nothing happened that was not entered, and nothing was entered that had not been verified three times by three hands. A moon could not be misentered. A moon could not be *entered at all*. Which meant these pages were not errors. Which meant the Guild had recorded true things and now wanted them unwritten — and the Guild only unwrote things that were dangerous, never things that were false.

"You'll supervise the burning," Vess said. Her voice was quiet and even, a voice used to being obeyed in whispers. "Corvane will certify. You'll sign as the destroying hand. Standard form."

Ansel looked at the hearth. It was cold, but the wood was laid and the kindling beneath it was dry, and beside the grate sat the burn-box with its lid open like a patient mouth.

"And if I refuse?"

Vess tilted her head, genuinely considering the question, as though it were an interesting one. "Then I do it," she said, "and you watch. Either way you learn what happens to pages the Guild regrets. The difference is only whether your hands are on them."

There it was. No choice offered, none intended; the summons had not been a request for a service but a test of compliance, and possibly — Ansel felt this understanding arrive coldly, like water finding a crack — something more than a test. Because the Guildmaster did not summon third-rank scribes to burn important papers. The Guildmaster sent people who could not read what they burned.

Unless the Guildmaster wanted a scribe who *could* read them. Who could confirm, with a professional eye, that every page was accounted for. That nothing had been copied out beforehand.

Ansel crossed the room and picked up the first bundle.

* * *

The work took four hours, and it was the worst work Ansel had ever done.

Not because it was hard. Because it was easy. That was the obscenity of it — the sheer procedural smoothness. Each page was checked against the day-book, struck through with the red voiding-ink, folded once along its spine, and fed into the burn-box, where the coals took it with a soft grey breath. Corvane certified each batch. Vess stood by the door and watched, and between batches she made small talk in her mild way, asking after Ansel's quarters, their health, whether the dormitory noise disturbed their sleep — questions that were not questions, each one a finger pressed gently against a lock to see if it rattled.

Ansel answered in monosyllables and kept their hands steady and their face arranged into the bored competence of a scribe doing tedious work, and underneath that arrangement, page by page, something was hardening.

Because here was the thing Ansel discovered as the fire ate: they remembered. Every entry. Eleven years of copying had built a memory like a ledger itself, indexed and cross-referenced, and no fire in any box was going to reach it. *Lot seven, remainder, condition: singing.* *One moon, whole — buyer's seal pending — hold for the thirteenth turn.* The words were in them now, and the numbers, and the strange recurring notation in the margins that Ansel had pretended not to notice when they first copied these pages and could pretend no longer: a sequence of turns, thirteen of them, counting down.

The Guild wasn't destroying evidence of a crime. The Guild was destroying the *receipts* — and keeping the goods.

By the twentieth page Ansel's fear had changed shape. It had started the morning as a cold animal thing, crouched in the chest, whispering *be small, be dull, survive*. But fear, Ansel was learning, is like ink: left standing, it separates. The dark sediment sinks and stays, but the clear part rises, and the clear part was saying something else entirely. It was saying: *they brought you here because you remember. They need you to certify the forgetting. Which means the forgetting matters more than they want anyone to know — and a thing that matters can be fought.*

The last bundle was the thinnest, and Ansel saved it for last because they had recognized the twine. It was the binding from Copy Room One, the senior room, the Guildmaster's own scriptorium — pages Ansel had never touched, never seen, that had been carried down here ahead of them. They untied it with hands that were almost steady.

Three pages. The first was a contract of sale, half-finished, the buyer's clause left blank. The second was a schedule of holdings — a list of vaults, and against each vault a number of fragments, and against the fragments weights, and against the weights a column headed simply *TURNS*, descending from thirteen to one. The third page was different. It was written in the old hand, the pre-Guild hand, the flowing antique script that appeared only in documents concerning the founding — and it was not an entry at all. It was a warning, or the fragment of one, cut off mid-sentence as though torn from a longer book:

*—that which is weighed against the night must be paid for in night, and the ledger that holds the moon holds also the hand that wrote it, and no debtor escapes the ink that remembers—*

Ansel read it twice. Then, with Corvane watching and Vess watching and the fire breathing softly in its box, they struck it through in red ink, folded it along its spine, and fed it to the flames.

And while the page curled — while both watchers' eyes were on the burning, as eyes always are, because fire is honest and paper is beautiful in its dying — Ansel's left hand, resting on the table beside the day-book, moved.

Underneath the day-book lay the blotting folio. Every scribe kept one: a pad of soft grey sheets used to press fresh ink dry, replaced weekly, the old ones discarded. Nobody counted blotting sheets. Nobody ever had. But a page written in the old hand, written wet, laid on a blotter even briefly, leaves a ghost — a mirror of itself, faint as breath on glass — and Ansel had laid these very pages on their own blotter six days ago, in Room One, before they were ever summoned down. They had told themselves it was habit. They had known, even then, in the place where habits keep their secrets, that it was not.

Ansel slid the top sheet from the blotting folio, folded it small, and closed their hand around it.

"The record is corrected," Corvane said, signing the last certificate with a flourish that trembled only slightly. "Scribe Quintana, your mark."

Ansel signed. Their hand did not shake. They were distantly proud of that, and distantly horrified at themselves for being proud of anything on this day.

"You'll return to your regular assignments," Vess said, as Ansel gathered their tools. "Nothing changes. You copied errors; the errors are gone; the Guild thanks you for your discretion." She paused at the door, holding it, and looked back at them with an expression that might have been kindness in a better-built face. "You're a careful scribe, Quintana. Careful scribes go far here. Careless ones—" she smiled again, that small closed smile, "—well. The Guild has ways of settling careless accounts."

"I'm sure it does," Ansel said.

* * *

That night the guildhall slept, and Ansel did not.

Their cell was narrow and cold, one of forty along the Scribes' Gallery, and eleven years of living in it had taught Ansel every sound the building made: the settling groan of the archive stacks, the night-watch's boots on the upper stair, the wind worrying the gutter above the window. They lay in the dark with the folded ghost-pages sewn into the lining of their second-best coat — three hours' work with a needle by lamplight, the stitches deliberately crooked, the work of a tired scribe mending a worn seam, nothing worth a second glance — and listened to the building breathe, and waited for the boots that would come for them.

They came at the hour before dawn.

Ansel heard them stop outside the cell — two sets of boots, unhurried — and had time for exactly one full breath, in which the whole future presented itself at once: the bare bunk, the struck-through name, the stories the older scribes told in the bathhouse when they thought the juniors weren't listening, about reassignments who came back wrong, or didn't come back, or came back as Assessors with new names and old eyes. Then the knock, soft as courtesy.

"Scribe Quintana." Vess's voice, pitched for privacy. "You're wanted downstairs. There's been a question raised about your blotting inventory."

So. Not the coat. The folio — the count of sheets, noticed at last, or noticed all along, the trap sprung late to see where the mouse would run. Ansel sat up in the dark and dressed with fingers that were, finally, shaking — and found, somewhere inside the shaking, a stillness that had not been there yesterday. Because running was what the guilty did, and hiding was what the afraid did, and Ansel had done both for eleven years and it had bought them nothing but the debt and the dark and the sound of stars going out over a Steppe they'd never been allowed to visit.

They had copied a moon once. Whole. They had held the words of it in their hands, and the words had hummed.

Ansel picked up their satchel — tools, spare nibs, the little knife scribes used for scraping errors, the coat with its crooked seams — and opened the door.

Vess stood in the corridor with a lamp and two wardens of the internal watch, and behind her mild face Ansel saw, clearly now, what had always been there: not malice, but function. A hinge. A mechanism. The Guild's hand, doing what the Guild's hand did.

"Blotting inventory," Ansel said. "Of course. Shall I bring my burn-box?"

Something flickered across Vess's face — surprise, or its professional cousin — and in that flicker's width of time, Ansel understood two things at once. First, that they were going down those stairs, and that what waited at the bottom wore the shape of a question and the teeth of an ending. Second, that the ghost-pages against their ribs were not evidence anymore. They were a debt — the only honest one Ansel had ever carried — and debts, in the Glass Steppe, were the one thing everyone agreed must be paid.

They went down into the green-lit dark, carrying the light of a dead star next to their heart, and behind them, far above, through the high windows of the gallery, the sky over the capital was missing two stars, and a third was beginning to flicker.
