## 4. What Falls With the Wards

The last star died at the hour of the ledger's closing.

Ansel did not see it happen — no one inside the guildhall could have, with its shuttered windows and its lamps trimmed low to protect the vellum — but they felt it. Everyone did. It came as a small, wrong silence, like a bell that had been ringing so long no one remembered it until it stopped. The ink in Ansel's well shivered. The lamp-flame leaned east, though there was no wind.

They were copying an entry for six hundred jars of preserved birdsong when it happened, and their pen dragged a long gray scar across the page before they understood what had changed.

Then the shouting started.

It rose from the counting floor below like water coming up through floorboards — first one voice, then a dozen, then the particular roar of a great building discovering it was afraid. Ansel set down the pen. The hidden folio sat beneath the loose floor tile where they had kept it for nine days now, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a living thing against all reason. Nine days of copying pages that should not exist. Nine days of *one moon, whole* and *the border wards, renewed by payment* and *thirteen turns of nothing, terms negotiable*.

Nine days of waiting for the enforcer Vess to come back through their door.

The shouting resolved into words. *The stars. The stars are gone.* Someone on the stair was sobbing. Someone else was laughing, which was worse.

Ansel knelt, pried up the tile, and took the folio into their coat, against their chest, where their heartbeat could lie to them about what it was.

* * *

Sefa felt the wards break the way a mother feels a child fall in another room — not a sound but a certainty, sudden and total, arriving in her teeth.

She had been on the tower walk above the Ninth Gate for six hours, watching the dark where the stars used to be, when the ward-stones began to sing. That was the wrong word and she knew it; ward-stones didn't sing, they *hummed*, a low patient note like a held breath, the sound of order holding. But tonight the stones were singing — high, thin, rising — and then, all along the border from the Ninth Gate to the Salt Reach, they began to crack.

Not metaphorically. She heard them. Stone splitting in the cold, one after another after another, a sound like the world clearing its throat to say something terrible.

"Down!" she shouted to the two juniors on the walk below her. "Get off the — "

The Ninth Gate's anchor-stone went first. She saw it happen: a pillar of pale light that had stood for four hundred years folding inward like a knee buckling, and then the darkness behind it — the true darkness, the outer dark that the wards were built against, or perhaps merely built to *suggest*, because Sefa had spent three years suspecting the wards drank more than they defended — poured through the gap like smoke finding an open window.

Except it wasn't smoke. It was simply night, complete night, the kind of night the Steppe had never known, pressing against the broken gate with a patience that frightened her more than violence would have.

The junior named Oskar made it down the stairs. The other one — Pell — froze on the walk, staring up, and Sefa understood why a half-second too late. Above the shattered gate, where the sky should have shown the first gray suggestion of dawn within the hour, there was nothing. Not cloud. Not haze. Nothing. A hole in the shape of the sky.

"Pell." She grabbed his collar. "Pell, look at me."

"They went out," he said. His voice was very young. "Warden Sefa, the stars went out. Stars don't go out."

"No," she agreed. "They're taken. Move."

He moved. She took one last look at the breach — at the outer dark standing politely in the gateway like a guest who has arrived too early — and then she ran, down the tower stairs, past the empty duty room where her resignation letter still lay unsigned on the desk, out into the ward-yard where the horses were screaming.

Every horse in the yard was screaming. Every dog in the district was howling. And above the rooftops of the ward-town, the people were coming out of their houses with lamps they did not need to light, because there was nothing left to light them against. The dark was total and democratic. It fell on warden and refugee alike.

Sefa stood in the middle of the yard and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for three years.

The wards drank starlight. She had proven it twice, with instruments, with measurements, with reports filed and returned stamped *insufficient basis*. The wards drank starlight the way a debt drinks wages — slowly, invisibly, always more than promised. And now the guild had sold the source. Now the wards had nothing left to drink, and so they had begun to drink themselves, and so they had cracked like old glaze, and the thing wearing the king's crown in the capital would sign the final entry and the Steppe would have thirteen turns of nothing and then nothing forever.

Unless someone did something about it.

She had reported it. She had been dismissed. Her apprentice had deserted rather than be tainted by her questions. Every proper channel had closed in her face like water closing over a stone.

So she took the warden's satchel off the hook in the armory — technically theft, technically desertion, technically treason under the eleventh clause — and she walked out the south postern with the last working hand-ward humming in her pocket, and she pointed herself toward the capital, toward the guildhall, toward whatever was left to save.

Behind her, the Ninth Gate's light folded out entirely, and the dark stepped through.

* * *

By midnight the guildhall was a drowned ship.

Ansel had never seen fear move through the guild before. Fear was inefficient; the guild priced it, taxed it, amortized it across a debtor's remaining years. But tonight fear moved through the marble halls like floodwater, and the ledgers — the sacred, inviolable ledgers — sat open on their desks, abandoned, their entries half-finished, their columns running dry.

In the atrium, a junior factor was nailing boards over the windows. "Keep the dark out," he kept saying, to no one, to everyone. "Keep it out, keep it out." As if the dark were a creditor come to collect.

Ansel moved through it all with their head down and the folio burning against their chest. The plan — if a plan made of panic deserved the word — was simple: reach the records annex, take the service corridor, go out through the loading dock where the paper wagons came in, and vanish into the refugee crowds already gathering on the Processional. Every instinct said the guildhall was the worst place in the Steppe to be tonight. The guild had just lost everything it had secretly bought. Desperate institutions did desperate bookkeeping, and Ansel knew exactly which column they were entered in.

They were halfway across the counting floor when the Guildmaster's voice stopped them.

It came not from the dais but from the speaking-tubes, the brass horns mounted in every wall, and it was calm in a way that made the hair on Ansel's arms rise. The Guildmaster's voice was always calm. It was the calm of a man reading numbers aloud.

"Personnel of the Ledger Guild. The wards have failed. The stars are extinguished. These are material events, and material events are opportunities. Effective immediately, the hall is sealed. No member leaves. There is work to do, and there will be time to grieve when the accounts are balanced."

A pause. Then, softer, almost gentle:

"Scribes assigned to the anomalous folios will report to the Master's office. You know who you are."

Ansel knew who they were. They turned, slowly, and found Vess standing at the far end of the counting floor, framed in the doorway of the Master's stair, holding a lantern that cast her shadow enormous up the wall. She did not move toward them. She only watched, the way a cat watches a mouse that has noticed the cat, and tilted her head toward the stair.

*Come,* the tilt said. *We both know how this ends.*

And Ansel — eleven years on their debt, nine days with stolen pages against their heart, twenty-three years of being small and careful and owed — felt something in them refuse. Not courage, exactly. Something meaner and more durable. The part of a person that says: *no. Not this way. Not today.*

The lamps chose that moment to fail.

All of them, every flame in the guildhall, guttering at once as if inhaling — and in the blackness came a sound Ansel had read about in a hundred ledger entries but never heard: glass breaking somewhere far above, high in the guildhall's crown, where the observatory dome held the guild's oldest instrument, the star-glass, the lens through which the first moon-fragment had supposedly been found.

The star-glass was shattering. Floor by floor, the guildhall was drinking the same poisoned air as the border wards, and the building itself was beginning to die.

Someone screamed. In the stampede of bodies toward the doors — doors that were sealed, that groaned and held — Ansel went the other way, shoulder-down, through the clerks' aisle, over a fallen desk, into the records annex where the service corridor breathed cold paper-smelling air. Behind them Vess's lantern swung wildly, and her voice cut through the din, sharp and furious: "*Stop them! The folio — stop the scribe!*"

Ansel ran.

* * *

The loading dock was chaos wearing livery. Paper-wagons stood abandoned, their drivers fled; the dock gates had been forced from outside by refugees seeking shelter, and now the guildhall's back entrance poured people inward while Ansel tried to pour outward, upstream, a single salmon in a river of terror.

They were nearly through — one more cart, one more gap, the night air already touching their face, vast and starless and terrifyingly open — when a body slammed into them from the side and they went down together behind the wagon, tangled, striking, and then both freezing.

Because the person who had collided with them was wearing a warden's coat, and wardens did not flee *into* the guildhall's shadow. Wardens were the law out there. This woman had dust in her hair and blood on her knuckles and a hand-ward glowing faint blue in her fist like a caught firefly, and her eyes, when they fixed on Ansel, did the thing that eyes did when they were deciding whether you were danger or shelter.

"You're guild," she said. Accusation and question in one breath.

"You're warden," Ansel answered. Same tone.

For a heartbeat neither moved. Somewhere beyond the dock gates, the crowd was chanting something — *open the wards, open the wards* — and somewhere above, another piece of the guildhall's crown came loose and rang off the marble like a funeral bell.

"I need to get to the capital," the warden said. "There are people I have to reach. Evidence. Things the guild buried."

"I need to get *out* of the capital," Ansel said. "I have evidence. Things the guild buried." Their hand, without their permission, had gone to their coat, to the folio's edge. The warden's eyes tracked the movement and narrowed.

"What kind of evidence?"

"The kind that gets you reassigned." Ansel held her gaze. "Permanently."

Something passed between them then, quick and wordless, the recognition of one drowning person by another — not trust, nothing so generous, but the cold practical knowledge that two swimmers make a better raft than one. The warden — Sefa, she said, former Warden of the Ninth Gate, the *former* bitten off like gristle — hauled Ansel up by the arm.

"Then we want opposite things," she said, "which means we can't rob each other. Come on. The crowd's about to break the gates, and I'd rather be on the far side of them when it happens."

They went out through the gap behind the last wagon, two figures pressed together against the press of bodies, and the starless dark took them the way the Steppe took everything: completely, and without comment.

* * *

They made perhaps a mile before the city taught them what the night meant.

The Processional was a river of torches moving away from the walls — except there was nothing to burn toward anymore, no light anywhere ahead, and the torches only carved little trembling rooms out of the blackness, each one a family, each family a rumor. *The wards fell at the Salt Reach. The wards fell everywhere. The king has proclaimed eternal night a holy season. The king is dead. The king was never alive.* Ansel listened and said nothing and gripped the folio and thought: *I copied those rumors' originals. I know which ones are true, and worse.*

Sefa walked like a blade through the crowd, and people parted for her without knowing why.

It was near the tanners' arch that the screaming started again — a different flavor this time, sharper — and Ansel looked up and finally, truly, saw the sky.

Where the moon should have been, there was a seam.

Faint, silver-gray, like a crack in glazed pottery, like the fault-line on a broken plate held up to no light at all. It hung above the northern hills, and it was shaped — Ansel's stomach turned as the scribe's mind did what scribes' minds did — it was shaped precisely like the removal-marks described in the anomalous folio. Like the place where something had been cut away. The moon was not gone. The moon was *missing*, and what remained was the wound of its absence, and it was turning, slowly, terribly, beginning its thirteenth turn around a Steppe it no longer lit.

"It's true," Sefa whispered beside them. She had seen it too. Her hand-ward had gone dark in her pocket; Ansel could tell because her whole posture had changed, the last certainty gone out of her spine. "All of it. Everything I measured. They've been selling it piece by piece, and we *guarded the shop*."

"Not all of it," Ansel said. The words came out of them before they decided to let them, and once out, they could not be taken back, and some deep exhausted part of them was glad. "There's a fragment left. The last one. It's in the master ledger as collateral against the final trade. Thirteenth turn. Terms negotiable." They pulled the folio from their coat and held it between them, oilcloth pale in the torchlight. "Everything's in here. Entries, dates, prices. What was sold, and to whom, and what's left to buy."

Sefa stared at the folio. Then at Ansel. Then, briefly, up at the seam in the sky, as if checking that it was still there — as if the proof overhead weren't enough.

"They'll kill you for that," she said.

"They'll kill me anyway," Ansel said. "I'm marked for reassignment. I ran." A breath. "I'd rather be killed for the truth than processed for a lie."

Something almost like a smile touched the warden's mouth, grim as a scar. "Then we're going to need help," she said. "People who know the city's underside. People the guild doesn't own." She paused, and her voice dropped, reluctant, as if the name cost her. "There's a counting-house on Candle Street with vaults under it. Guild collateral storage. If anyone knows a way through the dark and out of the capital, it's whoever lives down there."

"Collateral storage," Ansel repeated. "You mean — people? Stored as — "

"Debt-children," Sefa said quietly. "Collateral raised as security. The guild calls them *assets in care*." Her jaw tightened. "If the lights are out and the wards are down, I'd wager some of those assets are taking stock of their situation right about now."

Above them, the seam in the sky turned another invisible degree. Below them, under Candle Street, in the dark of the vaults, something stirred that the guild had numbered instead of named — and neither of them yet knew that the smallest, sharpest ally they would ever find was already awake, already listening, already done waiting to be owned.
