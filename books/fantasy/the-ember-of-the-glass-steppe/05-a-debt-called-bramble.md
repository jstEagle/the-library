## 5. A Debt Called Bramble

Bramble had never seen the dark before.

Not true dark, not the kind that lived in the counting-house cellar where the debt-children slept — that dark was only a dimmer version of the guildhall's ever-burning lamps, the ones the Guildmaster said must never go out because *a ledger is read by light and judged by fire*. But this was different. This was the night the stars died, and the lamps with them, and the whole of the Glass Steppe went black as the inside of a closed book.

Bramble stood in the corridor outside the collateral dormitory and felt the dark press against their skin like cold water.

They were eleven years old, or thereabouts. No one had written down the day they were born; they had been entered into the ledger instead, on a page in the third vault, under *Assets, Human, Minor: one (1), value assessed at maturity*. That was how Bramble had learned what they were. Not from cruelty — the tally-clerks were not cruel, exactly, no more than a scale is cruel to what it weighs — but from the flat, patient arithmetic of it. Bramble was a number. Numbers did not have birthdays. Numbers had *maturity dates*.

The lamps had gone out an hour ago, all together, like a held breath finally released. In that hour, the guildhall had come apart around them. Bramble heard it through the walls: running feet, the crash of the great doors being barred, someone screaming about wards and borders and *it's all falling, it's all falling*. The tally-clerks who usually patrolled the dormitory corridors had vanished into the panic upstairs, and for the first time in Bramble's remembered life, nobody was watching them.

Nobody was watching the assets.

Bramble pressed their palm flat against the cellar door and felt, beneath the wood, the faint hum they had always been able to feel and had always assumed everyone could feel — the deep, slow vibration of the vaults below, where the guild kept its impossible things. Bottled thunder. Jars of luck. And, three floors down behind seven locks, something old and round and cold that the clerks never spoke of above a whisper, if they spoke of it at all.

Tonight the hum was wrong. Tonight it stuttered, like a heart missing beats.

"Right," Bramble said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud made them real, and Bramble had decided somewhere between the last lamp dying and now that tonight was going to be real. "Right."

They went back into the dormitory first. Not for themselves — well, partly for themselves — but mostly for the little ones. There were six other debt-children sleeping in the long room, curled in blankets that smelled of lamp-oil and dust, and when Bramble shook the nearest awake, a small girl named Wren-after-no-one-in-particular, she opened her eyes on the darkness and began to cry without sound, the way children do when they have learned that noise brings the clerks.

"It's all right," Bramble whispered, which was a lie, but a kind one, the sort of lie Bramble intended to spend the rest of their life telling. "It's all right. Everyone up. We're going down."

*Down* meant the cellar, and past the cellar, the old delivery tunnel that ran from the counting-house kitchens out under the wall — Bramble knew it the way other children knew the backs of their own hands, because Bramble had been sent through it a hundred times carrying sealed boxes no one would explain, small enough to fit where grown clerks couldn't, cheap enough to lose if the tunnel ever collapsed. *Asset, human, minor: expendable.* They hadn't put it in those words. They hadn't needed to.

Getting six children through the dark halls of a panicking guildhall was the hardest thing Bramble had ever done, and Bramble had once carried a jar of bottled thunder up four flights of stairs while it argued with itself. They moved by touch and memory, hand on shoulder, shoulder on hand, a little chain of warm fingers in the cold. Twice they froze against the wall as torchlight swept past — clerks with lamps relit from some reserve, hauling armfuls of ledgers toward the burning-room, saving the books before the people, which told Bramble everything about where they stood in the order of things.

The cellar door was unlocked. Of course it was unlocked; who would steal from the guild on the night the world ended?

Inside, the dark was total, and the hum was louder, and it was definitely wrong — a grinding note underneath the steady tone, like a wheel turning over gravel. Bramble got the little ones settled in the far corner among the sacks of rice-paper, told them the rules — *quiet as mice, stay here, I'm coming back* — and then stood a moment longer than they needed to, listening.

Because there were voices in the cellar. Two of them. Low, exhausted, arguing in whispers near the old wine-rack that hid the tunnel entrance.

Bramble picked up the heaviest thing within reach — a brass counterweight off the broken scales, heavy as a sleeping cat — and crept toward the sound.

* * *

Ansel Quintana had never been so tired in their life, and Ansel had once copied out the entire customs schedule for the salt-fairs twice because the Guildmaster didn't like his handwriting on the first pass.

Now they sat on an overturned cask in the counting-house cellar with a hidden folio of impossible entries pressed against their chest under their coat, watching a stranger relight a stub of candle for the third time because her hands wouldn't stop shaking.

Sefa. Ward-keeper, disgraced, half-dead on her feet. She had come barreling out of the border roads into the city the same hour Ansel came barreling out of the guildhall proper, and they had collided at the top of the counting-house stairs — literally collided, folio and ward-charts scattering across the flagstones — and spent one long, mad moment each deciding whether the other was a threat. Something in the way Sefa had looked at the folio — not greedy, not afraid, just *recognizing*, the look of someone who had also seen the truth and been punished for it — had tipped the balance. Now they hid together in the one place in the guildhall where the chaos hadn't reached, waiting for the shouting overhead to die down, arguing about what to do next because neither of them knew.

"The tunnels," Sefa said for the fourth time. "You said there are tunnels."

"There are tunnels," Ansel said. "I don't know where they go. I'm a scribe, not a smuggler. I copy things. I know the words for everything and the way out of nothing."

"Then we're rats in a jar." She cupped the candle flame with both hands. In its light her face was gray with exhaustion, and there was a burn scar along her jaw that Ansel tried very hard not to stare at. "The wards are down. The stars are gone. Whatever the Guildmaster sold, he sold it, and whatever's coming next—"

The candle went out.

Not blew out. Went out, all at once, the flame simply ceasing, and in the same instant the whole cellar filled with a sound Ansel felt in their teeth — a low grinding hum, deepening, straining, like a millstone turning against its own axle.

And then a voice, small and hard and young, out of the absolute dark:

"Put your hands where I can see them and don't move. I've got a counterweight and I'm not afraid to use it, and also I'd really rather not, so please don't make me."

Ansel's heart slammed. Beside them, Sefa had gone rigid, and he could hear her breathing change — the breath of someone calculating distances in the dark.

"We're not thieves," Ansel said carefully, to the darkness. "We're hiding. Same as you, probably."

"Nobody hides in the counting-house cellar unless they're stealing or stolen." A pause. "Which are you?"

"...Stolen," Ansel said, and was surprised at how true it felt. "More or less. I'm a scribe. Debt-bound. Eleven years left on my indenture as of last month. I took some pages I wasn't supposed to take, and now the enforcer wants me reassigned, which is a word that means worse things than it says."

Silence. Then, slowly, grudgingly, a spark — flint on steel, struck by a small determined hand — and a second candle caught, and Ansel saw their interrogator for the first time.

A child. Small, thin, maybe ten years old, maybe twelve — hard to tell, because the face was one of those faces that age does not bother visiting. Close-cropped hair, practical. Guild-gray clothes with no name stitched anywhere, because property doesn't need a name. In one hand, a lit candle stub. In the other, raised and ready, a brass counterweight nearly the size of their head.

Behind the child, half-hidden in the shadow of the rice-sacks, six smaller faces peered out, wide-eyed.

"Oh," Ansel said softly.

"They're mine," the child said immediately, stepping sideways to block the view, chin up, counterweight still raised. "Not *mine* mine, the guild says they're guild assets, but I found them first and I'm keeping them, so if you're thinking of turning us in for a reward, you should know the reward for debt-children is nothing, because we're already owned, so there's no profit in it and you might as well let us be."

"I wasn't going to turn you in," Ansel said.

"Everyone says that right before they do."

"Sefa," Ansel murmured, without moving their lips much. "Are you seeing this?"

"I'm seeing it." Sefa's voice had gone strange — softer than anything Ansel had yet heard from her. She lowered herself slowly, deliberately, down onto her heels, making herself small and unthreatening, the way you'd approach a spooked animal. Or a spooked person. With ward-keepers, Ansel was learning, the distinction was thin. "What's your name?" she asked the child.

A flicker crossed the small face — something quick and complicated, want and wariness fighting over the same ground.

"Bramble," the child said at last. "That's what the clerks call me. It's not a real name, it's a — they said I was found in a bramble patch, so. Bramble." A shrug that tried very hard not to be hurt and failed, in the way of shrugs. "It's as good as anything. Names cost money here. Everything costs money here. That's the whole point of the place."

"What's in the jars?" one of the little ones piped up from the shadows, pointing a small finger at Ansel's coat, where the corner of the folio showed. "Is it treasure? Did you steal treasure?"

"Hush, Pip," Bramble said, but without much force, because Bramble was looking at Ansel too, with the same question in their eyes.

Ansel hesitated. Every instinct honed by eleven years in the guildhall said *show nothing, tell nothing, trust no one*. But the instincts had been honed by the guildhall, and the guildhall had just gone dark, and the person in front of them was a child holding a counterweight to protect six smaller children, and Ansel found — to their own considerable surprise — that they were tired of living by the guildhall's instincts.

"Not treasure," Ansel said. "Proof." They drew the folio out and opened it on their knee, tilting it to the candlelight. "Entries from the master ledger. Trades the guild made that shouldn't be possible. Bottled thunder. A year of someone's luck. And—" they turned to the page they had copied only days ago, the one that had appeared and vanished within the hour, the one that had started all of this, "—this. *One moon, whole.* Sold. And the buyer's mark is the crown."

Bramble stared at the page. The candle flame leaned between them.

"I knew it," the child said quietly. Not triumphant. Almost grieving. "I *knew* it. The thing downstairs — the big vault, three floors down, behind the seven locks — I've carried boxes to it my whole life. It hums. Did you know it hums? Nobody believes me. It used to hum like — like a bell, almost. Like something round. And lately it's been humming wrong, and tonight it's barely humming at all, and I thought—" Their voice cracked, and they hated it, visibly, furiously. "I thought maybe it was dying. Whatever it is. I thought it was dying and nobody would even write it down, because things only get written down here when somebody owes money on them."

Sefa made a small sound. When Ansel glanced over, the ward-keeper had pressed the back of her hand to her mouth, and her eyes were bright.

"You know the vaults," Sefa said. "All of them?"

"Better than the clerks do." Bramble said it flatly, factually, the way you'd state your own weight. "They can't fit in half the places I fit. I know every tunnel, every lock that sticks, every door the new clerks don't know exists because the maps burned forty years ago and nobody rewrote them. If you're trying to get out of this guildhall alive, you need me." The counterweight lowered, an inch. "But I want something back."

"Name it," Ansel said.

Bramble set the counterweight down entirely, turned, and looked back at the six small faces in the shadows. When they spoke again, their voice was careful, the voice of someone reciting terms they had rehearsed in their head for years without ever believing they'd get to say them.

"When we get out — wherever we're going — you don't carry me on any ledger. No debts, no assets, no *one (1), minor, value assessed at maturity*. You don't get to write me down. Not you, not the guild, not anybody. I'm not property that behaves well. I'm a person who's choosing to help you, and persons can stop choosing whenever they want, and you have to let me." A breath. "That's the price. Treat me like a person. All of you. Even when I'm wrong. *Especially* when I'm wrong."

The cellar was silent except for the failing hum of the vaults below, grinding slower and slower, like a great clock winding down.

Ansel looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at Ansel. And then Sefa did something Ansel did not expect: she extended her hand, ward-keeper's hand, scarred and steady, across the candlelight.

"Ward-keeper's oath," she said. "Old form. My grandmother taught me, back before the guild banned it. *No chain on the free, no leash on the found.* I swear it. You're a person, Bramble. You were always a person. The ledger was lying."

Bramble looked at the hand for a long moment. Then they took it, small fingers wrapping around scarred ones, and shook once, businesslike, though their eyes had gone suspiciously shiny and they blinked hard until that was fixed.

"All right," Bramble said, voice only slightly unsteady. "All right. Then here's what happens now. There's a tunnel behind the wine rack — third shelf, false back, mind the low beam. It runs out under the east wall to the tannery road. But—" and here the child's face changed, sharpened, the negotiator giving way to something else, something that had been building all night, "—before we go anywhere, you need to see what's in the big vault. Both of you. Because if your pages are right, if they really sold the moon piece by piece, then there's still a piece *left*, down there behind the seven locks, and tonight, with the lights dead and the clerks running around like headless chickens, is the only night anyone's ever going to be able to walk in and *look at it*."

Ansel felt the folio grow heavy against their chest. "Look at it and do what?"

"I don't know yet." Bramble was already moving, candle in hand, toward the wine rack, and the six little ones rose from the sacks and fell in behind them with the practiced ease of children who had learned to move when Bramble moved. "But I've been carrying boxes for that vault since I could walk, and I'll tell you something no entry ever wrote down. Every box I ever carried in was cold. Cold like winter. Cold like *night*. And every year, there were more boxes going in and less humming coming out, and I think—" the child pulled the false back open with a grunt, revealing black air and the smell of old stone, and glanced back at them, and for a moment all the hardness dropped away and they were only a small person standing at the edge of an enormous dark, "—I think we should see what's left of it. Before whoever bought the rest comes to collect."

Above them, muffled through floors of stone, something in the guildhall boomed — a door, a vault door, slamming shut under lock and key.

Bramble didn't flinch. They lifted the candle higher.

"Well?" they said. "Coming?"
