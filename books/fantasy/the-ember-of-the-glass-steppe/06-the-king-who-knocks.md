## 6. The King Who Knocks

The cellar beneath the counting-house had once held the guild's overflow of small mercies — bolts of luck-cloth, jars of bottled thunder gone flat, the pressed and flattened years of minor officials. Now it held three fugitives, a stub of candle, and the particular silence of people listening for boots on stairs.

Ansel had not slept. Sleep was a debt they could no longer afford to pay; every hour of darkness was an hour the enforcer Vess Corane might be closing the net, and Ansel had seen what Corane's nets looked like when they came in. They sat with their back against a shelf that still smelled faintly of ink-vinegar, the hidden folio open across their knees by candlelight, copying again from memory because memory was the only place left where the entries were safe.

*One moon, whole. Sold to: [entry struck]. Price: silence, perpetual.*

The words did not get less impossible with repetition. If anything they grew heavier, the way a stone grows heavier the longer you carry it without setting it down.

Across the cellar, Sefa sat sharpening a ward-knife against a whetstone with the patience of someone who had nothing else to give her hands to do. She had not spoken more than twenty words since they'd come down here two nights ago. Bramble, curled in the corner behind a stack of empty strongboxes, was asleep the way animals sleep — lightly, one ear open, one hand wrapped around a pry-bar nearly as long as their arm.

The knock came at the third hour past midnight.

Three raps. Evenly spaced. Almost polite.

Sefa's knife stopped mid-stroke. Bramble's eyes opened without any of the fumbling of ordinary waking, and in the space of a breath the child was on their feet, pry-bar up, positioned between the intruders-at-the-door and the candle as if the light itself were the thing worth defending.

"Nobody knows we're here," Sefa breathed.

"Nobody's supposed to," Ansel whispered back.

The knock came again. Three raps. Then a voice, low and roughened, pitched to carry through old wood and no farther:

"I can see your candle through the grate. I mean no harm. I have coin." A pause. "I have very little else."

Bramble looked at Ansel. It was, Ansel realized with a strange lurch, the first time anyone had looked to them for a decision — not the guild looking to see if they'd flinched, not a superior waiting for compliance, but two strangers weighing whether Ansel Quintana, debt-bound scribe, third-rate copyist, was someone whose judgment was worth following. The candle flame leaned in the draft from the stairwell, and Ansel found their mouth answering before their fear could vote.

"Ask what he wants," Ansel said. "Through the door."

"What do you want?" Sefa called, and her voice had the flat authority of a woman who had spent fifteen years telling the weather what to do and being ignored.

"Shelter until dawn. Nothing more. I'll sit on the stairs if you like. You can watch me the whole time."

"Who are you?"

Another pause, longer this time. When the voice came again it had changed — quieter, wearier, stripped of whatever performance the first answer had worn.

"That depends on who's asking. To the bailiffs I'm a vagrant. To the palace I'm dead. To myself—" a sound that was almost a laugh, "—I'm honestly not certain anymore. But I was a king. Once. Recently. And the men wearing my colors are hunting me through my own streets."

Silence in the cellar. Bramble's grip shifted on the pry-bar.

"He's mad," Sefa said quietly.

"Mad or lying," Ansel murmured. But even as they said it, their scribe's eye was doing what it always did — reading, cataloguing, comparing. *A king escaped his own palace.* The folio lay open on their knees. *Sold to: [entry struck].* The price of a moon paid out in silence, and somewhere above them a city under curfew, wards dark, stars dark, proclamations nailed to doors in a hand that everyone swore was the king's own.

"Let him in," Ansel heard themselves say, and did not know which part of them had decided it.

* * *

He came down the stairs slowly, one hand on the wall, and the candlelight did him no kindness.

He was tall the way winter trees are tall — everything drawn up and thinned out, as though some slow fire had been consuming him from the inside for years and had only recently been blown out. His hair was grey before its time and cut badly, hacked short with a knife rather than scissors. He wore royal colors, or what had been royal colors: a deep blue travelling cloak gone grey with road dust, silver thread at the collar picked half away — someone had tried to strip the heraldry and lost heart halfway through, or wanted the colors recognizable but deniable. Beneath the cloak, a plain soldier's shirt. Boots resoled twice. Hands that had been soft once and were hard now, knuckles scarred.

And hanging at his breast, on a cord of braided leather, a fragment of glass the size of a man's palm.

It was not pretty. It was clouded, milky, shot through with fractures like frost on a window — but it caught the candlelight and did something wrong with it. The light went in and came out cold. And Ansel felt it before they understood it: a hum, faint as a struck tuning fork heard through a wall, resonating in their back teeth, in the bones behind their ears.

They knew that hum. They had spent eleven years transcribing entries that carried it — the impossible trades, the bottled thunder, the year of luck, the *one moon, whole*. Every anomalous entry in the master ledger had a signature in the ink, a faint vibration the guild's accountants called *resonance variance* and pretended was a rounding error.

This fragment sang the same note.

"You're staring at it," the man said. His voice was steadier now that he was sitting — he'd taken the spot against the far wall, exactly where Sefa indicated, hands visible, the humility of a man well-practiced in being searched. "Everyone does. Would you like to hold it?"

"No," said Sefa.

"Yes," said Ansel, at the same moment, and immediately wished they hadn't, because both the ward-keeper and the king turned to look at them.

But the man was already lifting the cord over his head, and he crossed the cellar floor — three steps, unsteady, favoring his left leg — and set the fragment in Ansel's palms.

Cold. That was the first thing. Cold like river water in thaw-month, cold like the underside of a stone that has never seen sun. And heavy — far heavier than glass had any right to be, as though it were dense with something held inside it. The hum deepened against Ansel's skin, and beneath the hum, fainter, there was something else. A rhythm. Almost like breathing. Almost like —

Almost like handwriting. Like the cadence of a line of script, the rise and fall of a practiced hand moving across a page.

Ansel had read ledgers since they were six years old. They knew in their fingers what writing felt like. And this thing in their hands felt like writing that had been folded up very small and made to wait.

"It's a piece of the moon," the man said simply.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, the city groaned through its curfew hours — a cart, a whistle, the far-off clang of the palace bells tolling a count that meant nothing good.

"The moon is in the sky," Sefa said flatly. "Or it was. Before your palace put it out."

"The moon is in the sky," the man agreed. "What remains of it. What you saw go dark — that was the last of its light being called home. Called *in*. This—" he nodded at the fragment, "—is one of thirteen pieces. There were twelve turns of the moon, once. Everyone forgets the thirteenth. It was never meant to turn. It was meant to be *spent*."

Ansel's mouth had gone dry. In the folio, in the copied pages hidden against their ribs: *one moon, whole.* Not bought. Not stolen. *Sold* — and the buyer's name struck out so thoroughly the vellum was thin enough to read through.

"Spent on what?" Bramble asked. It was the child's first word in hours, and it cracked the tension like ice cracking — everyone turned, and Bramble stood their ground, chin up, pry-bar loose in their grip. "What costs a whole moon?"

The king looked at the child for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes — grief, Ansel thought, or recognition, though recognition of what, in a debt-child with a pry-bar, the king could not possibly know.

"Silence," he said. "The border wards were failing. Forty years ago, sixty — the records disagree, and I have learned that records disagree most loudly precisely where someone has paid them to. The wards kept the Steppe safe from what lies beyond the borders. Keeping them fed took starlight, and starlight was running thin, and the guild came to my grandfather's court with an offer." His hand rose, unconsciously, toward the hollow of his throat where the cord had hung. "Sell the thirteenth turn. Cut the moon into pieces. Feed the wards with moonlight instead of starlight, and buy the borders another century of quiet. The pieces would be held in trust. By the crown. By the guild. Together."

"And instead?" Sefa asked, though her voice said she already knew.

"And instead the pieces were sold off. One by one. Year by year. Bottled thunder. Years of luck. Whole seasons. Every impossible thing the guild ever traded — I've spent three years learning this, hiding in my own palace, reading my own archives at night — every impossible trade was a moon-fragment changing hands. The wards weren't drinking starlight, keeper. They were starving. And the ones who sold the moon were also the ones paid to keep the secret of why the sky was going out."

The cellar was very quiet. Ansel became aware that they were still holding the fragment, and that they did not want to put it down, and that this wanting frightened them more than anything Vess Corane had ever done.

Because Ansel knew the ledger. Knew it the way a jailer knows keys. And everything this ragged, ruined man was saying fit the entries — fit them *exactly*, fit the resonance signatures and the struck-out buyers and the pattern of dates that Ansel had noticed months ago and told themselves was coincidence, because the alternative was unthinkable.

Which raised the question Ansel's suspicion had been circling all along, the question that pity had been holding at bay since the man first knocked:

How did a king — a hunted, half-starved, officially-dead king — know things that the guild had killed to keep unwritten?

* * *

Dawn, such as dawn was now — a grey lessening rather than a rising — found them all still awake.

Sefa had not relaxed the entire night. She sat where she could watch both the king and the stairs, knife across her knee, and she asked questions in the flat relentless manner of a ward-inspector conducting an audit: Which gate did he leave the palace by? Who else knew? What was the name of the captain of his guard? The king answered everything, patiently, and his answers were consistent, and consistency proved nothing. Liars rehearsed. Ansel knew that better than anyone; Ansel had been rehearsing innocence into their face since childhood.

Bramble had fallen asleep again near morning, sitting upright, and the king had watched the child sleep with an expression Ansel couldn't parse and didn't try to.

It was Ansel who finally said it, because someone had to, and because the words had been building pressure behind their ribs all night like floodwater against a door.

"The fragment hums," they said.

The king looked up.

"All the guild's impossible entries hum. Same note. I've copied thousands of them — I feel it in my teeth. So either you stole that from a guild vault, which makes you a thief who happens to tell true stories, or someone gave it to you, which makes you a courier, or—" Ansel made themselves hold his gaze, made their voice stay level, "—or you're exactly what you say you are, and then I want to know how a dead king walked out of a palace that's been sealed for a month, past guards who report to the thing wearing his crown, carrying the one object in the world that matches the ledger I'm running for my life with. Because from where I'm sitting, sir, every one of those stories ends with us in chains."

The silence stretched. Sefa had gone very still. Even the city outside seemed to hold its breath.

"You think I'm bait," the king said at last. No anger in it. Something worse — understanding. "That they let me escape. That I'm leading them to whoever else is asking questions about the moon."

"I think," Ansel said, "that the guild once reassigned a junior archivist named Pell Owles for copying the wrong page, and that Pell Owles trusted a stranger in a tavern one week earlier. I think trust is the most expensive thing on any ledger, and I've never been able to afford it." Their hands were shaking. They pressed them flat against their knees. "So convince me. Not with your story — stories are ink, and I know how ink lies. Convince me with something a bait-man wouldn't carry."

The king was quiet for a long time. Outside, the false dawn thickened by a shade.

Then he reached into his shirt, and drew out not the fragment but a folded scrap of paper, creased and re-creased until the fibers were going soft, and held it out.

Ansel took it. Unfolded it. It was a decree — an official proclamation, the kind that had been nailed to every door in the capital this past month, announcing the coronation-feast, the eternal vigil, the new age of ordered night. Signed at the bottom in a flowing, confident hand:

*Aldric, King of the Glass Steppe.*

And beneath it, in different ink, in a hand that shook, in letters small and cramped and desperate — the same name. *Aldric.* Written over and over, a dozen times, each one slightly different, each one fighting to remember its own shape. Practicing. Relearning himself stroke by stroke like a child at a copy-desk.

"They sign my name better than I do now," the king said quietly. "The crown ate thirty years of me, keeper, scribe — my memories, turn by turn, year by year, and it wears what it ate. When I fled, this was the only proof I could take that I am still—" his voice caught, steadied, "—still *in here somewhere*. Still mine. I practice every day. Some mornings I lose words. Last week I forgot my mother's face for six hours." He looked at Ansel, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly clear. "You want proof I'm not their bait. Here is my only proof: I am a king who cannot reliably write his own name, begging shelter from a debt-scribe and a disgraced ward-keeper and a child with a crowbar. If I were theirs, don't you think they'd have sent someone *competent*?"

Despite everything — despite the fear and the suspicion and the floodwater pressure behind the ribs — Ansel felt something crack open, just slightly, in the region of the chest. Pity, arriving unwanted. They looked down at the paper, at the trembling practiced letters, and thought of their own eleven years of entries, of a self written and rewritten by other hands until the original was hard to find.

"Bramble," Ansel said. "Wake up. We're voting."

Above them, muffled by floors and stone, the counting-house bell began to ring — not the hour-chime. The alarm pattern. Three, three, seven.

Someone had found the cellar door unlocked.

And down the street, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Ansel had been dreading since the night they fled the guildhall: the measured, unhurried tread of boots too disciplined to run, and a voice like oiled steel calling through the grey morning air:

"Scribe Quintana. The Guildmaster extends her compliments — and her final offer."
