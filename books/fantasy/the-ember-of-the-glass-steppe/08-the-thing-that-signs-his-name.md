## 8. The Thing That Signs His Name

The decree arrived at the campfire on the third night of their flight, carried by a trembling courier who had ridden hard from the capital and looked, when he handed it over, as though he expected to be executed for the delivery.

Aldric read it by the light of Sefa's small ward-lantern, the only lawful fire they dared keep in a country where all fires might soon be counted. The parchment was heavy, cream-colored, stamped with the seal of the Glass Steppe's crown — his crown, though he had not worn it in two years. He read the words once. Then again. Then a third time, slowly, the way a man touches a wound to learn whether it still hurts.

*By order of the King, beloved of the Steppe, all granaries within three days' ride of the capital are hereby sealed against hoarders and traitors. Any who withhold grain from the crown's stores shall be assessed a night-tax of double measure.*

The hand was beautiful. That was the first thing that struck him, before the cruelty of the words, before the absurdity of *by order of the King*. The hand was beautiful because it was almost his.

Almost.

He knew his own script the way he knew his own gait, his own voice, the particular way he pressed too hard on downstrokes so that every *t* he had ever written bore a small dark scar at its crossing. This hand did not press too hard. This hand was flawless, fluid, confident — a hand that had never hesitated over a word in its life. And yet the letterforms were his letterforms. The tall ascenders. The peculiar open-bowled *g*. Someone had studied his writing the way a forger studies a face, and had learned everything except the flaw that made it human.

"Bad news?" Ansel asked quietly, from across the fire. The scribe sat with the hidden folio in their lap, as always, arms wrapped around it like a child around a bread loaf.

"It's signed with my name," Aldric said.

Bramble looked up from the pot of barley they were coaxing over the coals. "But you didn't—"

"No." He turned the decree toward the light so they could see the signature. *Aldric Vane, Rex.* His name in a stranger's perfect hand. "I haven't signed anything in two years. I haven't been permitted near ink in two years."

Sefa took the parchment and held it close to the lantern, her jaw tightening. "Ward-keepers used to verify royal decrees by the hand," she said. "There were marks only the true king's pen made. Pressure-marks. Ink-weight. We were trained to feel them under the vellum." She ran her thumb across the signature and frowned. "This would have passed inspection. It would have passed mine."

"That is rather the point," Aldric said, "of everything that has happened to me."

* * *

He remembered the famine. That much he could still remember, though the memory had gone soft at its edges, like cloth washed too many times.

It had been the ninth year of the dust-winds, and the Steppe had eaten itself down to the roots. He had been young then — young enough to believe that a king's grief could be spent like coin, that if he mourned loudly enough and long enough, the sky would take pity. He had opened the palace granaries until they were empty. He had sold the winter tapestries, the silver, his mother's rings. He had stood in the great square while people starved and let them look at him, because looking at him was all he had left to give them.

And then the Guildmaster had come.

He remembered the man perfectly — that was the bitter joke of it. The famine had blurred, but the Guildmaster remained sharp as a knife laid flat on a table: the grey robes without ornament, the voice like dry paper turning, the ledger he carried bound in leather so old it had gone the color of old teeth.

"Your Majesty cannot feed them," the Guildmaster had said, standing in the ruined throne room while dust sifted down through the broken skylight. "No one can feed them. But something can be done about the nights."

"The nights?"

"The border wards are failing. You know this. Every keeper knows it and no keeper will say it aloud. The wards drink more than they channel, and each year they drink deeper. Soon the things beyond the border will not be kept out by failing wards but invited in by hunger." The Guildmaster had set the ledger on the arm of the throne — set it there casually, familiarly, as though thrones were furniture like any other. "Unless the wards are paid."

"With what? The treasury is empty. You've seen the treasury. You audited it yourself."

"I did." Something that was almost a smile had moved across the man's face. "Which is why I bring an alternative currency. There is a fragment, Majesty. A piece of the old moon, cold glass, older than the Steppe, older than the wards. Set into your crown, it will hold the border for a generation. The wards will draw from it instead of starving. Your people will sleep safely through the worst years of the dust. All it costs—" and here the pause, the terrible courteous pause, "—is what crowns usually cost kings. Themselves."

"I don't understand."

"You will. Slowly. Turn by turn." The Guildmaster had opened the ledger to a fresh page and dipped a pen. "Sign here, Majesty. It is only a debt. Everyone in the Glass Steppe carries a debt. Even kings. *Especially* kings."

And Aldric — twenty-six years old, hollowed by three years of famine, desperate to give his people one unbroken night of sleep — had taken the pen.

He remembered the weight of it. He remembered thinking: *this is the smallest price anyone has ever asked of me.* He remembered the scratch of the nib, the smell of the iron-gall ink, the little dark scar his hand always left on the crossbar of the *t*.

He had signed. And the crown, when it came, had been beautiful — a circlet of pale gold with a single shard of moon-glass set above the brow like a frozen tear. When he put it on, the border wards had flared so bright that shepherds on the far marches swore the horizon glowed green for a full hour. The dust-winds broke that same season. The rains came back, thin but real. His people slept.

And turn by turn, the crown began to eat him.

* * *

"Aldric." Sefa's voice, careful. "You've gone somewhere."

He blinked. The fire had burned lower. Bramble was watching him with that unnervingly direct stare children raised as collateral develop — the stare of someone who learned early that adults' silences contain prices.

"I was remembering," he said.

"Does it help?" Bramble asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely curious, the way children ask the questions adults have agreed not to ask.

"No," Aldric said honestly. "But stopping doesn't work either. The memories go whether I hold them or not. That's how it works. That's how it *worked*." He touched his bare brow, where the ghost of the crown's weight still lived. "Every turn of the moon, it took something. Not all at once — nothing so honest as theft. It would be a small thing at first. The name of my childhood dog. The taste of the wedding-cake at my coronation feast. Then larger. My father's face. The sound of my mother's voice saying my name. The layout of rooms I'd lived in for thirty years. Each turn, a little less Aldric, and the glass a little brighter."

Ansel had gone very still. Scribes recognized ledgers even when they weren't written on paper; Aldric had seen it in their eyes since the first night. "Interest," Ansel said softly. "It wasn't taking randomly. It was compounding."

"Yes." He laughed, and hated the sound of it — a dry, rusted hinge of a laugh. "You understand faster than I did. I thought I was ill. I thought grief had worn grooves in me. By the time I understood what the crown was doing, I couldn't remember understanding it — do you see? Each realization was taken before I could act on it. I would wake certain something was wrong, certain I had known *what*, and find only smooth blankness where the knowledge had been. Like reaching for a stair in the dark."

"So how did you escape?" Bramble asked. "If it ate your knowing, how did you run?"

Aldric was quiet a moment. Out past the ring of the lantern-light, the Steppe lay black and starless, and somewhere far off a night-bird called and got no answer.

"There was a servant girl," he said finally. "In the last year. She brought me water at dawn, every dawn, and every dawn she said the same thing: 'Good morning, Your Majesty. The gardenias are blooming.' She said it because I had told her once, years before, that gardenias were my mother's flower, and she thought it a kindness. And every morning it landed like a stone in still water. Because I could not remember my mother. But I could remember that I could not remember. Do you understand? The crown could eat the memory, but it could not eat the *shape* the memory had left behind. The hole has edges. Edges are information."

He looked around the fire at their faces — the disgraced keeper, the debt-child, the scribe clutching their stolen pages.

"So I began keeping holes. I wrote down what I could not remember, and hid the writings, and forgot them, and found them again, and each time the finding shocked me back into myself. *You are Aldric Vane. You had a mother who loved gardenias. Something is eating you. Run.* And on the last night — the night the thing wearing my shadow finally stopped pretending — I walked out of my own bedchamber past four guards who bowed to me, because to them I was just a tired man in plain clothes, and the thing on the throne was the king. It wore my shape by then. Or I wore its. I confess there were moments, in the last turns, when I could not have told you which of us was the copy."

"And now?" Ansel asked. "Now that it's off you. Are the memories coming back?"

"No." He said it plainly, because they deserved plainness. "What it ate, it kept. What I have is the edges. I know I loved my mother. I cannot recall her face. I know I was happy once — there are hours I can feel the shape of, warm and specific, like a handprint in dust — and I cannot enter a single one of them. I am a house where every room is locked and the keys were sold." He folded the decree along its crease, precisely, the way he had once folded state documents. "That is why the signature matters more than the words. Let the words be cruel; words are cheap. But the hand — the hand is a claim. Whoever signs as me intends to *be* me, entirely, and finish the meal."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, as they always did now, moving through a country that had forgotten what its own sky looked like. Aldric walked at the rear, as he insisted on doing — a king guarding the rear of a column of four, which would have been comedy if any of them had the heart for laughter.

It was during the grey half-hour before sunrise-that-would-not-come that Bramble fell into step beside him. The child walked in silence for a long while, which Aldric had learned was how Bramble conducted serious business.

"You said the crown was offered as salvation," Bramble said at last.

"It was. And it was salvation, for a time. That's what I need you to understand. These things are never lies, exactly. They're truths bent into traps."

"The guild offered my parents a debt-forgiveness if they gave me up." Bramble's voice was flat, reciting. "They were going to lose the farm anyway. At least with the guild I'd be fed. That's what they told themselves. Fed and schooled and safe." A pause. "I was fed. Technically."

"Bramble—"

"I'm not asking for sorry. I'm asking the next question." The child looked up at him, and in the pre-dawn greyness their eyes were very steady. "When it took your memories. Did it ask first?"

Aldric stopped walking.

The others were some paces ahead, dark shapes against the darker land. He stood in the starless cold and felt the question go into him the way the servant girl's gardenia-sentence used to go into him — landing on the edge of a hole.

"No," he said. "Nothing in the Steppe asks anymore. That's what the ledgers did to us. Everything gets taken by paperwork. Nobody has to say please."

"Then when we get it back," Bramble said — *it*, meaning the crown, the throne, the whole stolen apparatus of his life, spoken of with the casual confidence of a child planning to steal eggs — "when we break it. Will you ask? Before you take anything back? From anyone?"

"I don't understand."

"You will." And Bramble trotted ahead to walk beside Sefa, leaving Aldric alone with the question, turning it over like a coin he suspected was clipped.

* * *

On the fifth night they sheltered in the shell of a waystation, and it was there, sitting watch while the others slept, that Aldric did the thing he had been circling for days.

He asked Ansel for paper.

The scribe surrendered a sheet and a stub of charcoal pencil without questions — scribes, he was learning, hoarded questions the way misers hoarded coin, spending them only when the return was certain. Aldric sat by the banked coals and set the decree from the capital beside the blank page.

Then he wrote: *I am Aldric Vane. I am the King of the Glass Steppe, or I was, or I am the man who owes that office a debt. I am writing this so that tomorrow I will know tonight was real.*

His hand shook. Two years of not holding pens had left tremors where fluency used to live. He pressed too hard on the downstrokes — of course he did, he would always do that — and every *t* he wrote bore its small dark scar.

He set the two signatures side by side in the lantern-light. His own, scarred and trembling, alive. The other's, flawless and dead as a coin's profile.

And it was there, staring at the pair of them, that the grief finally caught him — not the clean grief of loss but the ragged grief of *almost*: the almost-hand, the almost-name, the almost-life being lived in his palace by a thing that had studied him so closely it could reproduce everything except the flaw. Somewhere behind him in the dark, the capital sat under its new eternal night, and a creature wearing his face was signing decrees in a hand nearly identical to his, feeding the last fragment of the moon into the machinery of the thirteenth turn, and every soul in the Steppe would call it king because the hand was good enough, because the seal was right, because nobody — not one clerk, not one keeper, not one courtier — had ever been taught to look for the scar on the *t*.

"They'll know," Ansel said quietly. Aldric hadn't heard the scribe wake. They stood at the edge of the firelight, folio hugged to their chest, watching him with those ledger-reader's eyes. "The difference. People will know, when it matters. Flaws are how we recognize each other. It's the only way we've ever recognized each other."

"Is that comfort or doctrine?"

"Both. Scribes don't separate them." Ansel crouched, studied the two signatures, and tapped the forged one with a fingertip. "This hand is perfect because it's copied. Copies don't press. They trace. Whatever's wearing your crown has never once in its existence meant anything it wrote." A pause, and then, softer: "Your hand shakes because you mean it. That's the whole difference. That's maybe the only difference there is between a person and a thing that signs his name."

Aldric looked down at his own scarred, trembling letters, and for the first time since the famine-year, since the grey-robed man and the ledger and the pen, he felt something that was not grief.

Resolve has a taste, he discovered. It tastes like iron-gall ink.

"Then we go to the capital," he said. "Before the thirteenth turn closes. Before it finishes signing itself into me." He looked up at Ansel, and heard his own voice come out level and clear, a king's voice, or close enough. "Because there is one more thing I remember — one thing the crown never managed to swallow, because I hid it so deep even I'd forgotten I'd hidden it. The original contract. My signature on it. And a clause, Ansel — a rescission clause, written in the old style, in the margin, where arrogant men never look."

Ansel went rigid. "A clause that does what?"

"A clause," said the Hollow King, folding his trembling page with a king's precision, "that lets a debtor un-sign. If the original ledger still exists — and it does, it must, the Guildmaster keeps everything — then my name can come off the crown's bargain. And when my name comes off, the whole edifice comes down with it. Every turn. Every fragment. Every debt." He rose, and the lantern-light caught the hollows of his face and filled them, briefly, with something like the man he had been. "We go to the capital. And I am going to take my name back."
