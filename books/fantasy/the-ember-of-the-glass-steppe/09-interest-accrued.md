## 9. Interest Accrued

The rain came down in sheets of grey, and Ansel Quintana had learned, over the past weeks of running, to read weather the way they had once read ink — for what it concealed.

They were camped in the hollow beneath a fallen waystone, one of the old markers that had lined the Steppe's roads before the wards drank themselves dark. Sefa was three hundred paces out on watch, a silhouette against the bruised horizon. Bramble slept curled against the Hollow King's side like a cat against a stove, and the King himself sat with his back against the stone, eyes open, watching nothing. He did that more often now. Watching nothing. Or perhaps watching everything that wasn't there anymore.

Ansel should have been sleeping too. Instead they were copying.

The habit had survived the guildhall, the flight, the hunger. Their fingers itched without a pen in them, and so they'd taken to tracing entries into the dirt with a stick, or into their own palm with a fingernail, keeping the hidden folio's contents alive in muscle memory in case the paper itself was lost. Tonight they traced the entry that had started everything: *one moon, whole*, written in a hand so fine it might have been spun rather than inked.

That was when they heard the footsteps.

Not Sefa's — Sefa walked with the deliberate heel-strike of someone who wanted to be heard coming, a ward-keeper's courtesy. These steps were soft. Deliberately soft. The kind of softness that cost money to learn.

Ansel closed their hand around the stick and did not move.

"You've gotten thinner," said a voice from the darkness beyond the waystone, conversational as a neighbor asking after the harvest. "Debt reassignment does that to people. All that walking."

Ansel knew the voice. Everyone who had ever owed the Ledger Guild a copper knew the voice of Vess Coriander, the Guildmaster's enforcer — though *enforcer* was too blunt a word for what she was. She didn't break legs. She broke ledgers, and let the arithmetic do the breaking. A small woman wrapped in oilcloth the color of wet slate, with a face so unremarkable that witnesses could never agree on it afterward. Ansel had seen her twice in eleven years at the guildhall and could not have described her under oath.

"Coriander," Ansel said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "You're a long way from your desk."

"The desk travels these days." She stepped into the hollow's mouth, hands visible, palms out — an old debtor's gesture, *I carry no claims*. It was meant to be reassuring. It was not. "May I sit? My knees are fifty years old and this rain is personal."

Ansel said nothing, which Coriander took as invitation. She settled herself on the far side of the fire-pit — cold, thank all the extinguished stars; they hadn't dared light one — and produced from her coat a flask, a hard biscuit, and a folded document sealed with black wax. She set the biscuit on the stone between them with the care of an offering at a shrine.

"Biscuit first," she said. "Then business. You look like a strong wind would file you under *lost*."

Ansel's stomach made the decision before their pride could object. They took the biscuit. It tasted of nothing much, which after three weeks of green forage and river water was nearly a delicacy.

"Sefa will be back within the quarter hour," Ansel said around the crumbs.

"I know. I counted her rounds." Coriander smiled faintly. "I'm not here for the ward-keeper, or the child, or His Majesty. I'm here for you, Quintana. Alone. Which is why I waited until you were alone, which is why you're going to listen, because you're the only one of your little company who understands what listening costs."

The rain filled the silence. Ansel chewed slowly and gave her nothing.

"Full forgiveness," Coriander said.

The words landed in Ansel's chest like a coin dropped down a well — they heard it falling for a long time before it hit bottom.

"Eleven years," Coriander continued, ticking them off on unremarkable fingers. "Eleven years of copying other people's fortunes in a hall that smells of tallow and spite. Eleven years of eating what the Guild feeds you and sleeping where the Guild puts you and dying, eventually, owing the Guild for the shroud." She leaned forward, and for just a moment something almost human moved behind her forgettable face. "I read your original contract, Quintana. I've read every contract in that building, it's my trade. Yours is the worst one I've ever seen. The interest structure alone is a work of art. You were never meant to climb out. You were meant to die in the scriptorium with a pen worn down to the nib and a balance still owing."

"And now?" Ansel asked. Their voice was very quiet. "Now the Guild has developed a conscience?"

"Now the Guild has developed a *need*." Coriander sat back. "Which is rarer, and therefore more expensive, and therefore — for you — more profitable. Full forgiveness. Every page bearing your name, burned before witnesses. Your debt struck from the master ledger by the Guildmaster's own hand. You walk out of the guildhall a free person, with a letter of credit no bank in the Steppe would dare refuse. You could buy a house. You could buy a *life*."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange," Coriander said, "you tell me where the folio is."

There it was. Ansel kept chewing. Kept their face arranged in the expression they'd learned across eleven years of guild tables — attentive, neutral, slightly dim. The face of a scribe who understood figures but not implications.

"The folio," Ansel repeated. "Copies of pages you ordered destroyed."

"Copies of pages the Guildmaster ordered destroyed, yes." Something flickered in Coriander's voice — the faintest sour note, a servant's grievance. "I burned those pages myself, Quintana. Do you know what it's like to burn a ledger entry and feel it *resist*? No. Of course you don't. You're a copyist. But those pages are worth more than every debt in the building, and the Guildmaster wants them, and what the Guildmaster wants—" she spread her hands, "—I fetch."

"And if I say no?"

"Then in about nine days the world ends, and your debt dies with it, along with everything else, and I'll have wasted a perfectly good biscuit." Coriander shrugged. "But you won't say no. Not yet. You're going to consider it, because anyone who wouldn't consider it is either a saint or a liar, and I've met neither in the Ledger Guild."

It was, Ansel thought, the most honest thing anyone from the Guild had ever said to them.

So they considered it. Out loud. Carefully. Buying every second the way they'd once bought candle-ends — hoarded, precious, spent only when necessary.

"If I gave you the folio," Ansel said, "what happens to the others? Sefa. Bramble. The—the man."

"Nothing, from the Guild. The Guild has no interest in them." Coriander paused, and the pause was a hair too long. "The crown's people are another matter. That's above my seal."

"The crown's people." Ansel laughed — a short, ugly sound they barely recognized as their own. "There is no crown's people. There's a thing wearing the King's shadow signing decrees in a dead man's handwriting, and half the Steppe salutes it because the alternative is admitting the night doesn't end."

"Yes," Coriander said simply. "And in nine days it won't. Have you actually read the folio you're risking your neck for, Quintana? Truly read it? Or do you just like the shape of being important?"

Ansel set down the biscuit. This was the trap inside the trap — Coriander offering contempt as a lever, daring them to prove their importance by talking. Ansel had watched debtors destroy themselves this way for eleven years, gabbling out secrets to prove they mattered.

"I've read it," Ansel said mildly. "Tell me what the Guildmaster is paying. For the fragment. The last one."

Coriander's eyebrows rose — genuine surprise, quickly mastered. "You have read it."

"I copy things. It's what I'm *for*." Ansel hugged their knees, staring into the cold fire-pit. "Bottled thunder. A year of someone's luck. One moon, whole, cut into twelve pieces and sold to buy the border wards' silence. And there's a thirteenth piece, isn't there? There always is. The part nobody lists. The keystone. The turn that completes the circle." They looked up. "The crown-thing isn't buying the moon back out of nostalgia, Coriander. Twelve fragments make a broken thing. Thirteen make a *turn*. And a completed turn means the lightless moon stops circling and settles over the Steppe like a lid."

For a long moment Coriander said nothing. The rain drummed on the waystone above them, patient as interest accruing.

"Nine days," she said finally. "The thirteenth turn begins at the new dark. The Guildmaster signs the sale at the coronation-feast — the crown-thing is calling it the Feast of Eternal Night, which tells you everything about its flair for reassurance. Payment is to be made in starlight. Actual starlight, drawn from the wards' reserves, enough to refill the Guild's vaults for a century." Her mouth twisted. "The wards are empty, by the way. Has been for months. Your ward-keeper friend was right about that, and everyone who called her mad owes her an apology they'll never live to give."

"So the Guildmaster sells the sky," Ansel said, "to buy the dark."

"The Guildmaster sells the sky to *survive* the dark. There's a difference, in her arithmetic." Coriander rose, brushing rain from her coat, and picked up the document — the sealed offer, the full forgiveness, the whole weight of eleven years reduced to black wax and vellum. She held it out.

Ansel looked at it. Truly looked. Eleven years of tallow-smell and cold mornings and the particular loneliness of being valued only for a hand that could hold a pen. Eleven years, struck out. A house. A life. A name that belonged to no one's books.

Their fingers rose — and stopped.

Because behind the wanting, quiet as a ledger line, sat the image of Bramble asleep against the Hollow King's side. Of Sefa walking her rounds in the rain because someone had to. Of a gaunt man watching nothing, mourning a self he couldn't fully remember. None of them appeared anywhere in Coriander's offer. Forgiveness, Ansel realized, was just another word for *paid in full* — and the price on this page was written in a currency the Guild had never once failed to collect.

"Not tonight," Ansel said.

Coriander tilted her head. "Not *no*?"

"Not tonight." Ansel met her forgettable eyes and let the scribe's dull mask slip, just enough. "A debt this size deserves proper consideration. I want terms in writing. I want the clause concerning my companions spelled out — *all* of them, including the man in royal colors, and I want it witnessed. And I want to know exactly what the crown-thing intends to do with the Steppe once the turn completes, because I don't sell to buyers whose intentions aren't disclosed. I was trained by the best, Coriander. You should know how this works."

Something that might have been respect crossed the enforcer's face, brief as a star's return.

"Three days," she said. "There's a shrine to the Unnumbered Saint at the crossroads below Harrow Fell. Come alone, or don't come, and I'll tell the Guildmaster you died in the rain. She'll believe it. Everyone believes scribes die quietly." She tucked the document away and turned toward the dark, then paused. "Quintana. The biscuit — eat the rest. And the folio. If it comes to burning, burn it yourself. Don't let them take it intact. Trust me on that. I've burned enough to know."

She walked out into the rain and was gone between one step and the next, the way she did everything — completely, and leaving no record.

Ansel sat alone in the hollow for a long time, heart hammering, the half-eaten biscuit cold in their hand. Three days. Nine days until the turn. And somewhere between those numbers, a choice that wasn't a choice at all, dressed up as one.

Behind them, a floorboard of stone creaked.

"That was well done," said the Hollow King, from the shadow where he had been sitting awake the entire time, his voice low and rough as ash. "Though I confess I only understood half of it."

"How long—"

"Since she named the feast." He leaned forward into the faint grey light, and his ruined face was grim. "The Feast of Eternal Night. Ansel — I know that feast. I *instituted* it. Or... the thing that wears me instituted it, in the famine year, when the crown first whispered its price." His hand rose to his bare head, to the ghost of a crown no longer there. "It isn't a celebration. It never was. It's the *first course*."

Ansel went very still. "First course of what?"

The Hollow King looked east, toward the capital, toward the palace where his own signature was being practiced by something that had never been hungry, never been human, never been *him*.

"Of us," he said. "All of us. The whole Steppe, served up in thirteen pieces. And the last piece goes on the table in nine days — unless we reach the Guildmaster's collection first, and steal back the ink that was a star."

Far off, through the rain, Sefa's whistle sounded the all-clear — two notes, bright and oblivious, a ward-keeper's promise that the night held nothing worse than weather.

Ansel closed their fist around the biscuit, and around the truth, and got up to lie to the people they loved least badly in the world.

Three days, then. The crossroads below Harrow Fell.

They would keep the appointment — but not alone, and not to surrender anything. Ansel Quintana had spent eleven years learning how debts were constructed.

It was long past time to learn how they were broken.
