## 10. Where the Stars Were Kept

Bramble had never told anyone about the door behind the vinegar casks.

There had never been anyone to tell. In the counting-house cellar, where the debt-children slept in rows like ledger entries themselves — *one cot per collateral, one blanket per bond* — Bramble had learned early that knowing things was the only property they were allowed to keep. Knowing which floorboards sang under an enforcer's boots. Knowing that the third shift of auditors always left their lamps burning because they were too proud to admit fear of the dark. Knowing the door behind the vinegar casks, and what lay past it.

Now there were three people standing in the cellar's cold breath, watching Bramble work the cask loose with hands that still remembered being small enough to crawl inside one.

"You're certain this leads anywhere?" Sefa asked. She kept her voice low out of habit, the way ward-keepers spoke near working wards, though these wards had been dead for weeks and the whole Steppe knew it.

"It leads everywhere," Bramble said. "That's the point of it. The guild built its roads underneath so nobody would see them moving things at night. You don't move a bottled thunderstorm through the front gate." They hauled the cask aside with a grunt. "You move it down."

The Hollow King stood apart from them, as he always did, wrapped in his travel-stained royal colors that he'd tried to make look less royal by rolling in ash. The fragment of cold glass hung against his chest on a cord, and even here, even now, it hummed — a sound more felt than heard, like a tooth aching somewhere behind the world's teeth. Bramble felt it answer something in the walls. Felt the walls answer back.

"Behind the casks," Ansel said quietly. They had their hidden folio clutched to their ribs the way other people held children. "How did you find it?"

Bramble hesitated. This was the part they never said aloud, the part that lived in the same locked room as all the other parts: the nights when the vault-mistress counted them before bed, touching each head like coin, and Bramble lay awake listening to the cellar breathe. The cellar breathed differently than the rest of the guildhall. Slower. Older. Like something very large sleeping under the ledgers, and the ledgers only its blanket.

"I listened," Bramble said finally. "Are you coming or not?"

* * *

The tunnel beyond the casks was older than the guildhall above it. That was the first thing Bramble wanted them to understand, and the thing they couldn't say: everyone up top thought the guild was eternal, that the Ledger Guild and the Steppe itself had been born on the same morning, twins of order and account. But the tunnel stones were fitted without mortar, without marks, in a pattern no guild mason used. The guild hadn't built this road. It had found it, the way a magpie finds a ring, and claimed it in ink.

Bramble walked ahead with a stolen storm-lamp — a little bottled thunder that gave off light the color of rain about to fall. The others followed in a line, and their footsteps made four different sounds: Sefa's measured stride, Ansel's quick scuffing steps, the Hollow King's slow deliberate weight, and Bramble's own soft pads, the walk of someone who had learned to take up as little space as possible for eleven years.

Except now, walking into the dark with three people at their back who had chosen to follow, Bramble found their feet wanting to take up space. Wanting to stride. It was an alarming sensation, like discovering a new limb.

"How far?" Ansel asked.

"To the old vault? Half a night, if the collapses haven't gotten worse." Bramble ducked under a fallen beam, its wood gone silver with age. "The guild stopped using this route after the Third Reckoning. Something about the tolls. Everything in the guild is about tolls."

"And you know it because—"

"Because I was inventory." Bramble said it flatly, the way you set down something heavy so your arms are free. "Debt-children get moved through the under-roads when the auditors want a transfer nobody sees. I went through this tunnel four times before I was seven. The last time, I paid attention. After that, I came back on my own. There's nothing else down here that belongs to me, so I decided this does."

Nobody laughed. Nobody said anything at all, actually, and Bramble understood that silence too — it was the silence of people recalculating. They had spent two days with these three, long enough to share fire and watch each other sleep in shifts, and Bramble could feel them doing sums. Not cruel sums. The kind where you discover a person is worth more than their entry suggested.

"The vault," the Hollow King said. His voice came out of the dark like a draft under a door. "You said the moon was cut there. Cut *apart*."

"That's what the folio says," Ansel answered for Bramble, and Bramble let them. It was easier to have Ansel say the terrible things; Ansel had read the pages, had copied them in secret, had the words memorized in that scribe way where memory becomes a kind of scar tissue. "*One moon, whole,*" Ansel recited softly. "Entry dated the year the wards were raised. And then entries after it. Fragment by fragment. Sold to buy the border's silence. Thirteen pieces accounted for. Twelve sold." A pause. "One left."

"One left," the Hollow King repeated, and touched the glass at his throat, and the hum deepened like a struck bell deciding whether to ring.

Bramble walked faster.

* * *

They stopped once, in a chamber where the ceiling opened into a natural chimney of stone and, impossibly, a thread of grey daylight fell through — the first any of them had seen since the stars died. It wasn't starlight. It wasn't moonlight. It was just the sun doing its indifferent noon work, and all four of them stood in it like travelers at a well.

Sefa tipped her face up. "I used to chart the sky every dusk," she said. "Eleven years. Every dusk, same hill, same slate. You think you'll remember it perfectly, but you won't. Already the patterns are going soft in my head. I can't recall if the Weaver's Chain had six bright stars or seven."

"Seven," said the Hollow King.

Everyone looked at him.

"There were seven," he said again, and his voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "I watched them from the nursery window when I was small. Before the famine. Before—" He pressed his lips together. The crown had eaten his memories turn by turn, he'd told them, and watching him reach for one and find it was like watching a man pat his pockets for keys he'd already sold.

"Seven," Sefa agreed gently. "Thank you."

Bramble looked away, throat tight. This was the thing nobody warned you about the end of the world: it didn't arrive as thunder. It arrived as a king forgetting stars, a ward-keeper losing her charts, a child who had never once been asked what they remembered. The dark didn't fall all at once. It was deducted. Piece by piece. Just like everything else in the Steppe.

"We should keep moving," Bramble said, gruff, because if they stayed in the light-thread much longer they were going to say something unforgivably soft. "Daylight doesn't wait and neither do collapses."

* * *

The collapse came anyway, an hour later, where the tunnel narrowed between walls of fused glass-sand — the Glass Steppe's own bones, vitrified by some ancient heat no ledger recorded. A fall of rubble blocked half the passage, and beyond it the air moved wrong, thick and humming, and Bramble knew before they climbed the pile what waited on the other side.

They knew the way you know your own name.

"Careful on the far side," they called back, already scrambling up. "Don't touch the walls. Don't touch anything until you've seen it, and then — I don't know. Touch it carefully."

"What is it?" Ansel's voice, tight with dread and hope in equal measure.

Bramble crested the rubble and stopped, and the storm-lamp's rain-colored light spilled out across the vault, and for a long moment Bramble couldn't speak at all.

It was enormous. That was the first truth, and it undid every map in Bramble's head, because they had imagined a room, a strongroom, something scaled to ledgers and locks. This was a cavern the size of a cathedral turned inside out, hollowed from the living glass-stone, and its ceiling rose so high the lamp-light couldn't find it. The floor was smooth as a frozen lake. And the walls —

The walls were teeth.

That was the image that lodged in Bramble and refused to leave: great curved scars cut into the glass-stone, dozens of them, hundreds, each one the shape of a removed thing — a socket where something round and radiant had been pried free. Some were small as a fist. Some were vast as a chapel dome. Each scar still held a faint residue of light, a ghost-glow like phosphorescence in a wake, so that the whole cavern seemed lit from within by the memory of what had been taken. The removal-marks spiraled upward and around, following some pattern too large to see from inside, and Bramble understood suddenly, with a lurch, that the pattern was the moon. The scars were the missing pieces of a single shape. The vault itself had been carved to fit around something whole.

They had cut the moon apart in this room. Here. On this floor. And then they had carried it out through tunnels like this one, piece by piece, and sold it.

"Oh," said Ansel, very quietly, from the top of the rubble. "Oh, it's beautiful."

"It's a wound," Sefa said.

Both things were true, and that was the horror of it. The cavern was beautiful the way a cathedral is beautiful, the way a perfect equation is beautiful, and every line of that beauty had been made by theft. The ghost-glow along the scars pulsed faintly, slowly, in a rhythm Bramble's body recognized before their mind did — the rhythm of a heartbeat slowed almost to stopping. Whatever remained of the moon in these walls, it was still turning. Still trying to complete its circuit with most of itself gone.

The Hollow King descended the rubble last. He walked out onto the glass floor alone, his ash-grey royal colors stirring in a wind that shouldn't exist underground, and stopped in the exact center of the cavern, where the largest scar of all gaped overhead — a socket the size of the sky, empty, waiting.

He looked up into it for a long time.

"This is where they brought me," he said at last. "Or — no. This is where they brought the *crown*. I remember a table. I remember men in guild colors bowing. I remember thinking I was being given a gift." His hand rose to the fragment at his chest. "I was being fitted. Like a key into a lock. And I thanked them."

Bramble's chest hurt. They crossed the glass floor — their floor, theirs, the one place in all the world that belonged to them — and stood beside the king, and looked up at the great empty socket with him.

"They took everything," the Hollow King whispered. "And they made me sign for it."

* * *

They made camp at the vault's edge, where an ancient alcove held the bones of a workbench and rusted tools whose purpose Bramble chose not to examine. Sefa built a small fire from salvaged tunnel-timber, and Ansel spread the hidden folio open and began, by lamplight, to match entries to scars — walking the cavern's rim with a stub of charcoal, marking each removal-wound with its corresponding line of ink. *Fragment the first, sold to the border lords. Fragment the fourth, sold to buy the fleet's muteness. Fragment the ninth—*

Bramble sat apart, as they always did, but closer than they usually sat, and watched the firelight move on the walls, and thought about ownership.

Everything Bramble had ever owned had been assigned. One cot. One blanket. One name that wasn't quite a name — *Bramble*, the vault-mistress had said, because the child had been found tangled in the hedge like a bramble and it was faster than looking up a real designation. Even their memories of the tunnels, their one hoarded treasure, had been stolen knowledge, taken in secret, spent in fear. Nothing in Bramble's life had ever been freely given or freely held.

But this place. This wounded, glowing, magnificent place.

Nobody had assigned it to them. Nobody had entered it in any book. The guild had abandoned it after the Third Reckoning; the enforcers didn't come; the auditors' maps ended at the collapsed passage. For years Bramble had come down alone and lain on the glass floor in the ghost-light and listened to the slow pulse of the violated walls, and it had been the closest thing to being held that they had ever known.

And now there were three strangers camped in it, and instead of fury Bramble felt something terrifyingly like gladness.

"Bramble." Ansel had come over, folio under their arm, charcoal dust on their fingers. They sat down uninvited, which no one in the guild would ever have dared, and looked out at the scarred walls. "This is where the original ink would have been mixed. The star-ink. If the Guildmaster keeps a vial, there might be records here — the formula, the provenance, anything that tells us how it works. Will you help me search?"

"Why me?"

Ansel glanced sideways. In the firelight their face looked younger and older at once. "Because you know this place better than anyone alive. Because you found the door behind the casks. Because—" they hesitated, and then said it plainly, the way scribes write true things, "because you're the only one of us who isn't afraid of it."

Bramble looked out at the vault. At the teeth-marks. At the slow pulse of stolen light. At the Hollow King still standing beneath the great empty socket like a man keeping vigil at a grave, and Sefa feeding the fire stick by stick as though tending something that could be healed.

Wonder and violation, side by side, forever. That was what this place was. Maybe it was what all of them were.

"Yeah," Bramble said. "I'll help."

They got up together, and Bramble led the way toward the workbench alcove where the oldest records would be, and it was there — prying loose a panel Ansel couldn't budge, feeling the wall give with a sigh like a held breath released — that Bramble's fingers found the thing that stopped their heart.

A cavity behind the panel. And inside it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, a shape Bramble knew from a hundred ledger entries and had never once expected to hold:

A vial. Small, stoppered, heavy. And within it, a darkness that was not darkness at all — a blackness with depth and glitter in it, like a sky remembered, like the moment just before the first star goes out.

Star-ink. The original. Not in the Guildmaster's collection at all.

It had been here the whole time. Hidden in Bramble's place. Waiting in Bramble's keeping, in the only corner of the world that belonged to them — as if it had known, all these years, exactly whose hands it was waiting for.

From the center of the vault, the Hollow King cried out — a raw, wordless sound — and when Bramble spun, lamp raised, they saw him staggering back from the great empty socket above, both hands pressed to his temples, the glass fragment at his throat blazing white.

"He's here," the king gasped. "The thing wearing my crown — it knows we've found the ink. It's coming."
