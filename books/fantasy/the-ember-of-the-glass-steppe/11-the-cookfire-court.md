## 11. The Cookfire Court

Sefa had spent eleven years learning the wards and one night learning they were lies, and in the weeks since she had discovered a third truth she liked even less: that her hands remembered everything, even when her heart wanted to forget.

They were working again now, those hands, but not as they had been trained to work. She knelt in the dirt behind Maren's bakehouse with a chipped ward-stone balanced on her palm, and instead of the binding-cantons of the Guild — *contain, compel, collect* — she was carving something older, something the ward-towers had buried under regulation and oath. A calling-stone. An opening-stone. The kind of craft that assumed the person on the other side of it was a neighbor and not a debt.

"You're holding your breath," Bramble said beside her. The child crouched low over the stone's edge, watching her knife-work with the fierce attention of someone who had learned every trade by watching through doors. "You do that when you're carving. You'll faint into the trench."

"I don't faint."

"You did at the ford."

"That was fever."

Bramble shrugged, unbothered, and went back to grinding chalk for the inlay. Sefa almost smiled. Almost. Her ribs still ached from the crossing at Hollowmere, and smiling pulled at them.

The village was called Tarnwick, though half its signposts had been burned for firewood during the first dark weeks, and it held perhaps two hundred souls who had done an extraordinary thing: nothing. When the wards shattered and the stars went out, when the Guild's towers went silent and the crown-thing's heralds rode out with their proclamations of eternal night and lawful order, Tarnwick had simply declined. No riot. No rebellion. They had taken down the guild-post where debts were published, stacked the boards in the miller's yard, and gone on planting winter wheat.

"They'll come eventually," Sefa had warned Maren on their first night, huddled by the great cookfire in the village square where all disputes were now heard. "A place that refuses to pay will be made an example."

Maren, a broad woman with flour in the creases of her knuckles and the calm of someone who had raised six children through three famines, had only nodded. "Then we'd best be worth the example. Sit. You look like a woman who hasn't been fed by anyone else in years."

Sefa hadn't known how true that was until she tasted the bread.

* * *

The bailiffs came on the ninth morning, riding under a banner Sefa recognized and hated: the royal colors, but wrong — the silver thread of the crown worked in a pattern that hurt to look at directly, the way the ledger-entries had hurt to read. Nine riders, then a wagon, then twelve more men on foot with iron-tipped staves and the flat, bored faces of people doing arithmetic rather than violence.

The Hollow King saw them first. He had taken to standing watch on the mill roof at dawn, saying he slept badly inside walls these days, and his voice came down to the square thin and strained: "Riders. The shadow's livery." He still would not call himself king; he said *the shadow's livery* the way a man might say *my brother's killers*, which told Sefa more than any confession could have.

Ansel came out of the cottage they shared, folio clutched to their chest even in sleep, ink stains permanent as birthmarks. "Night taxes," they said grimly, reading the banner's canton from fifty paces. "It's on the new schedules. Every settlement pays a tithe against the cost of maintaining the dark. It's extortion dressed in liturgy."

"Can you fight?" Sefa asked the square at large, because there was no time for gentleness.

Twenty-odd faces looked back at her. Farmers, a smith, two grandmothers, a boy of fourteen with a woodcutter's axe too big for him. Maren stood with her arms folded. Old Tam the miller leaned on his staff. Nobody flinched, which frightened Sefa more than flinching would have.

"We can throw things," Tam offered. "We're very good at throwing things. Ask the crows."

"No." Sefa set down her satchel and opened it, and felt the old prohibition settle over her shoulders like a wet cloak. *The ward-craft is licensed. The ward-craft is bound by oath. Teach it to no one outside the towers, on pain of stripping.* She had been stripped already. Stripped of rank, of tower, of title, of the stars themselves — what was left for the oath to take? "No throwing things. If we fight them like bandits, we lose, and worse, we prove their story right — that without the Guild and the crown, the Steppe tears itself apart. We beat them the way I should have been taught to use the wards. As something shared."

She drew the chipped stone from her pocket and held it up.

"Who here has ever touched a ward-stone?"

Silence. Then, slowly, the smith's daughter raised her hand, and then the boy with the axe, and then, to Sefa's surprise, Maren herself.

"My mother kept a hearth-ward," Maren said. "Before the Guild licensed hearths out from under us. She sang to it. I thought that was just a song."

"It wasn't just a song." Sefa's throat tightened. Everything the towers had claimed to invent, the villages had once known — and been made to forget so it could be sold back to them at interest. "Then we start there. Everyone who wants to learn, come to the bakehouse tonight. Bring stones. Bring songs, if you have them. We have until those riders finish counting our granaries, and not much longer."

* * *

She taught them through the evening and into the night, and it was the strangest work of her life — stranger than the night the stars died, stranger than fleeing the towers with alarms howling behind her. Because it was easy. That was the obscenity of it. Eleven years of sealed instruction, of cantons learned by rote and techniques guarded like treasure, and when she finally opened her hands and gave it away freely, the villagers took to it like water into cracked earth.

The hearth-song Maren remembered was, structurally, a stabilization cadence. The rhythm threshers used to keep grain-flow even mapped onto a dispersion lattice. The lullaby Bramble contributed — a debt-child's song, hummed in vault corridors to feel less alone — carried a concealment interval that Sefa had needed two years of formal study to derive. The knowledge had never left the people. It had only been renamed, fenced, and taxed.

"You're crying," Bramble observed, not unkindly, handing her a whetstone.

"I'm angry."

"You're crying angrily. Here."

By midnight, nine villagers could hold a simple veil-steadying between them — enough to blur the sightlines around the granary and the cellar where Ansel's folio hid. By the second watch, the smith's daughter, whose name was Odile and whose hands were surer than Sefa's own had ever been, could raise a minor repulsion across the square's north lane. And Sefa herself sat among them with her stripped badge in her pocket like a stone she couldn't put down, teaching forbidden craft by cookfire light, and understood that this — this circle, this sharing, this complete absence of licensing — was what the wards had always been supposed to be.

Ansel watched from the edge, taking notes. "For the record," they murmured, "the Ledger Guild lists ward-craft instruction as a capital offense against guild property."

"Then let them bill me," Sefa said. "I'm told my debts are about to become negotiable."

That got a real laugh out of the circle, brief and startled, the first since the riders appeared.

* * *

The bailiffs came for the collection on the third day, at the gray hour before full light, which told Sefa the crown-thing's captains had studied the old Guild suppression manuals — strike settlements at waking, before the mind is armed. But Tarnwick had been waking before dawn for generations to bake and milk and thresh, and Odile's veil-steadying had been humming across the granary lanes since midnight.

Sefa stood in the square's center as the bailiff-sergeant dismounted, a man with a ledger-clasp at his belt and frost in his voice. Behind him, his men fanned out with practiced boredom toward the storehouses.

"Tarnwick," the sergeant announced, unrolling a schedule sealed in black wax. "Assessment of night-tax arrears. Three days' grace is ended. Payment in grain, stock, or labor-bonding. Refusal constitutes—"

"Refusal constitutes what?" Maren asked pleasantly, from the bakehouse door, arms still dusted with flour. "Say the whole sentence. I want to hear it in daylight."

The sergeant's jaw tightened. "Refusal constitutes sedition against the Crown Eternal, punishable by seizure of persons and property."

"There's the whole sentence." Maren looked around at her neighbors, and Sefa saw the moment the sergeant realized something was wrong — that the villagers weren't scattered or cowering, that they stood in loose clusters at precise intervals along the square's edges, that the air itself seemed faintly, wrongly steady, like a pond before rain. "Now here's ours. Tarnwick doesn't recognize the Crown Eternal. Tarnwick recognizes its own. You can turn around."

The sergeant's hand went to his stave. "Take the granaries. Bond anyone who resists."

What followed lasted less time than the reading of the schedule.

Odile dropped the veil and raised the repulsion in the same breath, and the two bailiffs nearest the granary found their charge turned aside as though the air had become a wall of deep water — they staggered, overbalanced, and went down into the churned mud of the thaw. The smith and Tam swung the mill-gate shut across the north lane behind a shimmer Sefa had anchored at both posts, and four more bailiffs found themselves neatly penned with their horses and nothing to arrest. In the square itself, the villagers did exactly what Old Tam had promised: they threw things. Not stones — Sefa had forbidden stones — but flour-sacks, dropped from the bakehouse loft with devastating accuracy, blinding and choking the sergeant's line until they were swinging staves at ghosts.

Nobody died. Sefa had built that into the design, and paid for it: she took a stave across the shoulder breaking through to shield the boy with the axe, whose name she finally learned was Perrin, and the blow drove her to one knee with white fire up her arm. She saw the sergeant looming over her, ledger-clasp glinting, raising the stave again —

— and then the Hollow King was there.

He moved like a man remembering a body he'd been exiled from. One hand caught the stave mid-fall; the other took the sergeant's collar, and for a moment the gaunt face and the bailiff's face were inches apart, and the sergeant went rigid, because whatever he saw in the Hollow King's eyes wore a shape he'd been trained to obey.

"You will ride back," the Hollow King said quietly, "and you will tell whoever signs your orders that this village was defended. Not raided. Defended. Let them make of that what they wish."

He released him. The sergeant stared, mouth working, some terrible arithmetic running behind his eyes — and then he sounded the retreat, hoarse, and the bailiffs limped to their horses and the wagon rolled out of Tarnwick with its tax-schedules unsigned and its cargo space empty.

The village cheered. Sefa stayed on one knee in the mud, cradling her arm, and did not cheer, because she was watching the sergeant's face as he rode away, and she had seen that expression before, in the towers, on the faces of men sent to deliver messages they didn't understand. He hadn't been defeated. He had been *informed*. And somewhere in the dark capital, the thing that signed the king's name was going to read this report and understand exactly what had defended Tarnwick: ward-craft, unlicensed, alive, spreading.

And a dead king walking free.

* * *

They dressed her shoulder by the cookfire that evening, the same fire where justice happened now instead of in courtrooms. Maren splinted and bound with a healer's economy while the whole village drifted past to look at Sefa's bruise as if it were a relic, which, she supposed, it was — the price of the lesson, paid in the only coin the law outside the law accepted.

"Perrin says you took the blow meant for him," Maren said.

"He's fourteen."

"He's fourteen and he'll remember it for sixty years. That's how this works, you know." Maren tied off the bandage. "Not decrees. Not ledgers. Somebody taking the blow. Word gets around."

Across the fire, Ansel sat with the folio open on their knees, but they weren't reading it. They were looking at Sefa with an expression she was beginning to recognize and didn't yet know how to survive — the look of someone deciding to trust her. Bramble was asleep against the Hollow King's side, a liberty no one in the old world would have survived attempting, and the Hollow King sat perfectly still so as not to wake them, staring into the flames with the face of a man memorizing something he expected to lose.

"The sergeant will report," Sefa said quietly, when Maren moved away. "By tomorrow, maybe two days. The shadow will know ward-craft lives, and that we carry it. It won't send bailiffs next time."

Ansel nodded slowly. "It'll send something that doesn't need to count."

"And we're out of time to hide." Sefa flexed her bandaged arm, testing, and winced, and kept flexing anyway. "Two turns left on the moon. Whatever we're going to do with that folio and that vial and that broken man, we do it moving. Tonight, if the arm allows."

Ansel closed the folio. When they spoke, their voice was low, meant only for her. "There's a place I've been trying to find in the old entries. The first vault. Where they cut the moon apart. Bramble thinks they know the way in."

Sefa looked up at the starless sky, at the blind black where the constellations used to stand guard, and felt the old tower-training rise in her one last time — *report, contain, comply* — and set it down like a badge she no longer owed.

"Then we go at first light," she said. "All of us. Before the shadow learns to aim."

Above the eastern ridge, where no star had shone for weeks, something flickered — faint, brief, unmistakable. A single point of light, trembling like a held breath, as if somewhere far beyond the broken border, the sky itself had begun to remember its debts were owed back.
