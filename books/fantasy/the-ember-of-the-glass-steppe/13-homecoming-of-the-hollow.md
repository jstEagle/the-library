## 13. Homecoming of the Hollow

The city of Vael Corrin had always smelled of beeswax and hot iron, and it still did, and that was the cruelest thing about it.

Aldric stood in the shadow of the Saltgate arch with a hood drawn low and a porter's yoke across his shoulders — borrowed from a dead man's cart two miles back, along with the man's indifference to being robbed, since the man had been a scarecrow dressed in a dead porter's coat, set out by the crown-thing's bailiffs as a warning against desertion. The barrels on his yoke were full of river water and river stones. Heavy enough to make his shoulders ache in a way he was grateful for. Pain was proof of a body. He had learned to collect proofs the way other men collected coins.

Above him, where the sky should have been, there was only the ceiling of night — seamless, starless, close enough some nights that he could feel it pressing down like a hand laid flat on the world. But tonight the ceiling had been decorated. Lanterns by the thousand hung from wires strung between towers, arranged in constellations that no longer existed, so that the people of the capital could look up and see the Ploughman, the Sisters, the Wolf — painted in flame, hanging from strings, exactly where the real ones used to stand.

*They gave you lanterns for stars,* Aldric thought, *and called it mercy.*

He walked through the gate with the evening traffic, past guards who checked faces against nothing, because everyone who mattered was already inside the palace or already bought. A crier stood on the fountain steps announcing the feast in a voice polished by practice: *"By grace of the Eternal Night, by will of the King Regnant, all loyal subjects are summoned to witness the Coronation Renewal — three days hence, when the last turn completes and the Steppe is sealed in blessed dark forever—"*

Three days. Two turns left. The arithmetic sat in Aldric's chest like a swallowed stone.

He had ruled this city for nineteen years before the crown began eating him, and in those nineteen years he had never once walked through the Saltgate on foot. He had never heard how his own name sounded in a crier's mouth — *the King Regnant*, they said now, not even pretending, not even using the word *regent*, because regents were temporary and whatever wore his shadow intended to be permanent.

The strange thing — the thing he would try, later, to explain to Ansel and fail — was that the city did not look conquered. It looked *fed*. Shopfronts gleamed. Bakers had flour. Children ran through the streets playing a game with knucklebones and black cloth over their eyes, chanting something about the Night-King who watches while you sleep. There were queues at the soup-houses, orderly and long, and the soup smelled of actual marrow.

*This is what I could never give them,* he thought, standing aside to let a cart pass, feeling the porter's yoke bite into his neck. *Nineteen years of reign, and I never made them fed.*

And then, underneath that thought, quieter, in the voice he was beginning to recognize as the crown's residue rather than his own: *Perhaps it was right. Perhaps the trade was just. Perhaps you were the failure and it is the correction.*

He gripped the yoke until the wood creaked.

"No," he said aloud, softly, to himself, in the middle of a crowd that did not hear him. It was becoming a habit, this arguing with the inside of his own skull. Sefa had told him once — in her flat ward-keeper's way, mending a strap by their fire without looking at him — "You talk to yourself like a man checking a fence line. Keep doing it. The day you stop answering is the day we tie you up." So he kept answering. Every hour. Every street.

*No,* he said again. *I know what it is. I signed for it. I remember signing.*

That was the terrible joke of him: he remembered almost nothing else, but he remembered the signature.

* * *

They entered the palace through the kitchens, which had been Bramble's idea, delivered in the child's matter-of-fact rasp: "Every castle forgets its kitchens. Kings think food comes from weather." Bramble had mapped the service corridors from memory — the debt-child had spent four years of collateral-servitude carrying ledgers through these halls, small enough and unpersoned enough to be furniture — and the memory held. A coal chute, a scullery door propped with a bone-handled knife, a passage behind the ovens that smelled of roasting goose and clove.

Ansel went first, folio clutched under their coat, ink-stained fingers flexing like a nervous bird's. Sefa came last, walking backward half the time, watching the dark behind them the way she watched everything — as if it owed her money.

"The feast is tomorrow night," she murmured, in the oven-passage. "Tonight the palace is half-empty. Servants seconded to preparation. Guards drinking early. If we're going to find the Guildmaster's private collection, tonight is the hole in the wall."

"I know these holes," Aldric said.

It came out stranger than he meant it to. Because he did — he knew the palace the way a man knows his own mouth, every hollow of it — and yet he had been gone eleven years, and eleven years is long enough for a house to become someone else's.

The kitchen itself stopped him at the threshold.

It was his kitchen. The same hearth-arch, wide enough to drive a cart through; the same row of copper pots that his mother — no. Not his mother. His predecessor's mother, a woman whose face he could no longer retrieve, only the fact of her, a fact like an entry in a ledger with the sum torn off. The same long scarred table where he had once, in his third year of reign, eaten bread and salt with a delegation of steppe-herders while they argued grazing rights until dawn, laughing, all of them, over some joke he could no longer remember either.

But the walls had changed. Where his huntsman's trophies and his grandmother's tapestries had hung, there were mirrors. Dozens of them, tall and narrow, silvered dark, angled so that anyone working in the kitchen was always reflected — always watching themselves work. And above the great hearth, where the crowned stag of his house had been carved into the mantel, the stag remained but the crown had been recut, deepened, made larger than the animal's whole head, so that the beast seemed crushed beneath it.

"Who does that?" Ansel whispered, staring at the mirrors. "Who makes servants watch themselves?"

"He does," Aldric said. "It did. Whatever pronoun fits." He found he could not look at the mirrors directly. In one of them, briefly, he caught his own reflection and it took him a moment too long to recognize the gaunt man in the hood, and when he did recognize him, the reflection seemed — he would swear this later, and Sefa would nod grimly, because she'd seen it too — the reflection seemed to keep moving after he had stopped.

They crossed the kitchen in the blind spot between the ovens and the scullery stair, and no one saw them, and Aldric grieved the whole way across.

* * *

The great hall was lit for tomorrow.

They reached the minstrels' gallery above it through a servant's corridor, meaning only to pass along it toward the west wing and the Guildmaster's apartments, but Aldric stopped at the gallery rail and could not make himself move on. Below, the hall stretched away in candlelight — five hundred candles, a thousand, arranged in the old festival pattern, the pattern his master-of-feasts had designed for midwinter, the one with the spiral of light at the center representing the turning year. Except the spiral had been altered. At its heart, where the white candle of dawn had always stood tallest, there was now a black obelisk of glass — a fragment, unmistakably, humming faintly even at this distance, a hum Aldric felt in his teeth, in the empty places behind his eyes where memories used to live.

Workers moved below among the candles, dwarfed by the hall, arranging place-settings of black-glazed ware. Banners came down from the rafters on pulleys: his banners, the crowned stag, but dyed midnight, the stag's eyes picked out in silver thread so that the animal seemed to be screaming.

"They're going to feast here," Ansel breathed beside him. "Tomorrow. While the turn completes."

"A coronation renewal," Aldric said. "I invented that ceremony. Did I tell you? Year six. A drought year. The country needed a ritual of continuity, so I renewed my own coronation vows publicly, at midwinter, and fasted alongside the farmers. It worked. People believed in it." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, dry as ash. "Now it believes in me instead."

Below, a steward in livery Aldric didn't recognize — grey and silver, the colors of frost — was directing the placement of a long table at the foot of the dais. Thirteen chairs. He counted twice. Thirteen, each taller than the last, the thirteenth a throne of black glass that two men were bolting together with hands wrapped in wool.

"For the fragments," Sefa said quietly. She had come up beside him without sound, as she always did. "Thirteen pieces. Twelve seated around. The thirteenth enthroned. It's a ward-array, Your— " She caught herself; she'd been training herself out of the honorific for weeks. "It's a ward-array built out of furniture. When the last fragment is placed, the thirteenth turn closes inside that circle. Sealed. Permanent."

"And the guests?" Ansel asked. "Twelve seats. Who sits at the coronation of eternal night?"

"Debtors," Aldric said. The knowledge surfaced from somewhere deep, some ledger-entry he'd absorbed without understanding, back when he still signed things. "The twelve greatest debt-holders in the Steppe. That's the trade's shape. It was always a trade. Silence for the border wards, night for the debts. Everyone who sold a piece of the moon was buying their obligations cancelled. The feast isn't a celebration. It's a settlement."

Silence in the gallery. Somewhere below, a worker dropped a fork and flinched at the sound, glancing up at the mirrors along the walls — the hall had mirrors too, Aldric saw now. Rows of them. The whole palace had grown eyes.

"We should move," Sefa said. "West wing. Before the night guard rounds."

But Aldric did not move.

Because the doors at the far end of the hall opened, and a procession entered — not grand, just a rehearsal, a steward walking the route with a stand-in carrying the train — and at the head of it, borne on a cushion by two pages, came the crown.

His crown. The Ironlight Circlet, forged from the first ward-stone ever raised on the Steppe's border, the one he had worn for nineteen years and fled without. Even from the gallery he could feel it — a cold radiance, a wrongness, like standing near a well that goes down further than the world. The pages carried it carefully, the way you carry something that carries you.

And following the crown, unhurried, reviewing the hall with his hands clasped behind his back, came the king.

Himself.

Not himself. Taller than Aldric remembered being, or perhaps the crown added height. Broader. Dressed in royal black with the silver stag, face smooth and composed and *almost* right — his jaw, his brow, the scar he'd earned at the ford of Ilse Water, reproduced faithfully, lovingly, the way a forger reproduces a signature after long study. The thing paused at the dais, ascended the steps, settled into the unfinished glass throne as though trying it for weight, and looked out over the hall.

Looked up.

For one heartbeat — one — its gaze passed across the minstrels' gallery, across the shadows where four intruders stood frozen, and Aldric felt the crown's cold attention land on him like a hand on the back of his neck, and inside his skull something whispered, in his own voice, worn smooth as a river stone:

*You could come home. You could sit at your own table. You could sleep in your own bed. You are so tired. Look how tired you are. No one would ever need to know which one of you won.*

And the horror of it — the true horror, worse than the recognition, worse than the mirrors — was that every part of him wanted to say yes. His legs ached. His bones ached. Eleven years of exile weighed on him like wet wool, and here was his own chair, his own hall, his own name spoken in his own voice, offering rest.

He gripped the gallery rail. The iron was cold. Cold was real. Sefa's hand closed on his arm — she had seen it, seen the sway in him, seen the way his body leaned toward the stairs — and her grip said nothing and everything: *Answer the fence line. Answer it now.*

"I am tired," Aldric whispered, to the thing below, to the thing inside, to both. "But I remember signing. And you don't. That's the difference. You have my face and my crown and my chair, and I have the one thing you'll never have."

Below, the thing tilted its head, listening.

"I have the debt," Aldric said. "And I intend to default."

The whisper in his skull went silent — not defeated; he knew better than to hope for that — but silent, recoiling, like a tongue touching a broken tooth. On the dais, the false king rose from the glass throne and turned away, dismissing the gallery, dismissing the shadows, and called out in a voice pitched for the workers, warm and kingly and utterly hollow:

"Bring the thirteenth chair closer to the light. We want our guest of honor to see everything."

* * *

They reached the west wing as the palace bells rang the ninth hour, and it was there, in the corridor outside the Guildmaster's apartments — where lamplight burned late behind a door guarded by two enforcers in grey — that Aldric finally broke.

Not loudly. That was the worst of it. He simply stopped walking, in an alcove between a suit of armor and a window that looked out over the inner garden — *his* garden, the one he had planted with white roses the year of his coronation because his dead wife had loved them, except he could no longer remember her face, only the roses, only the fact of love like a cancelled entry — and he put his hand against the cold stone and shook.

"Eleven years," he said. "I walked these halls for eleven years and I cannot tell you what I ate yesterday. I stood in my own kitchen and it was a museum. I saw myself on the dais and I wanted to kneel. Do you understand? I wanted—" His breath hitched. "There is a version of tonight where I walk down those stairs and take the crown back and none of you could have stopped me, and I would have told myself it was reclamation, and it would have been surrender, and I would not have known the difference. That version of tonight was *this close*."

Ansel stood in front of him. Small, ink-stained, exhausted, a scribe who had never held anything sharper than a pen-knife until a month ago. They looked up at him with an expression he had learned to read on the road — the expression of someone who had also been owned by paper, also been a signature in someone else's book.

"When I burn my page," Ansel said quietly, "it has to be me who burns it. Nobody can do it for me. That's the rule of the ritual — we read it four times to be sure." A pause. "You just did yours. Down there. In front of the whole hall."

Aldric stared at them.

"You chose the debt," Ansel said. "You're the only person in this palace who remembers what he owes, and you chose to keep owing it rather than let it forgive you wrong. That's not surrender. That's the most solvent act I've ever witnessed." The ghost of a smile. "And I've read ten thousand ledgers."

From behind the Guildmaster's door came the murmur of voices — two voices, one dry and precise, one that made the glass fragment in the room beyond hum audibly through the wall, a sound like a struck wineglass that never quite stops ringing. Sefa pressed her ear to the stone and then pulled back, eyes hard.

"The Guildmaster," she breathed, "and something that talks like the crown. They're finalizing terms. Tonight. Before the feast."

Bramble, who had been keeping watch at the corridor's end, slipped back to them, and the child's face in the lamplight was pale and set.

"Then we don't have two turns," Bramble said. "We have until that door opens."

Through the wall, clear now in the hush, the Guildmaster's dry voice read aloud a single line — and Aldric, who had spent a lifetime signing such lines, felt the words go through him like frost:

*"Item the last: one moon, whole. Seller: the Crown Regnant. Buyer: the Ledger Guild, in perpetuity. Witnessed and bound this thirteenth turn—"*

The sentence stopped. Pages turned. And Ansel's hand found the hidden folio beneath their coat, and Aldric saw the scribe's face change — saw fear become arithmetic, become resolve — and understood that somewhere in those stolen pages was the answer to a question none of them had yet known to ask.

"Get ready," Ansel whispered. "I know how we break the trade."
