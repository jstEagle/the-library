## 15. Escape by Candlelight

Bramble had spent nine years learning the archive wing the way other children learned their mother's face. They knew which floorboards sang underfoot and which kept silent; knew that the third shelf of the cartography alcove leaned a finger's width from true, and that a body could squeeze behind it if they exhaled all their breath and thought small thoughts. The guild had raised them as collateral — *asset B-114, minor, unvalued* — but an asset learns everything about the vault it is kept in, because the vault is the only parent it has.

So when the alarms woke the palace like a hive kicked open, when the enforcer's whistle split the dark and the Guildmaster's voice rose somewhere above them, thin and furious as a kettle, Bramble did not panic.

Bramble ran *toward* the fire.

* * *

The archive wing was already burning when they reached it. Not burning the way ordinary things burn — this was ledger-fire, ink-fire, the flame that ate writing first and wood second, so that the walls themselves seemed to be screaming in script, whole ledgers of debt dissolving into orange light before they could be read. Someone had set it deliberately. Ansel's face, when Bramble found them at the wing's mouth, said everything: *I didn't mean for it to spread. I only meant to burn my page.*

"You lit the match," Bramble said, not accusing. Just naming it, the way you'd name weather.

"I lit one page." Ansel's hands were black to the wrist. Their eyes were wide and wet and absolutely certain. "The rest of it decided on its own."

Behind them, the Hollow King stood with his back against a shelf of tax rolls from the Third Famine, watching the flames with an expression Bramble couldn't read — grief, maybe, or recognition. The crown-thing wore his face upstairs. The fire wore his history here. Everything the man had been was going up in smoke, and he was standing still for it, letting it happen, as if he owed the fire something.

"The vial," Sefa said. She'd come down the servants' stair two steps at a time, ward-chalk still dusting her knuckles from whatever she'd broken upstairs to buy them these minutes. "Where is it?"

"Private collection," Bramble said. "East end of the wing. Behind the glass case with the First Census."

Sefa stared at them. "You know the private collection."

"I know every room in this building that has a lock on it." Bramble bared their teeth, which was the closest thing to a smile they owned. "Locks are just debts with hinges. I've been picking them since I was six."

* * *

They went in together, because there was no other way, and because — and this was the part that kept catching in Bramble's chest like a splinter — nobody told them to stay behind. Nobody said *you're a child, wait here.* Ansel looked at them across the firelight and nodded once, the way you nod to someone whose help you need, and Bramble carried that nod into the smoke like a coal cupped in both hands.

The archive burned in sections. The older wings, built of glass-steppe stone and bound in star-tanned hide, resisted; the newer additions, paper and pine and cheap glue, went up like festival lanterns. Between the two, the air itself had become a thing you had to negotiate with — thick, hot, tasting of copper and burnt sugar. Bramble moved through it by memory with their eyes shut half the time, counting turns: left at the shattered case of bottled thunder (the bottles were popping now, one by one, each releasing a single furious crack of storm into the burning hall), right where the luck-ledgers used to be, straight until the floor changed from plank to tile beneath their boots.

The private collection door was iron, and locked, and hot enough to raise blisters.

"Stand back," Sefa said, and drew a circle on the metal with two fingers, chalk-white lines glowing faintly against the black. "Old craft. The kind they took my license for." She spoke a word that wasn't a word so much as a shape made of sound, and the lock forgot what it was for. It simply opened, the way a fist opens when the arm holding it goes numb.

Inside, the collection gleamed. Cases within cases, velvet gone grey with age, and at the center — exactly where Bramble remembered seeing it once, years ago, through a keyhole while carrying a tray they weren't supposed to be carrying — a vial no longer than a thumb, filled with ink that was not black. It was the color of the space between stars. It moved when you weren't looking directly at it, swirling slow and patient, and even through the glass Bramble could feel it humming against their teeth, the same note the cold glass fragment had sung in the Hollow King's pocket, the same note the ledger entries made when Ansel read them aloud.

"One moon, whole," Bramble whispered. "Cut into thirteen pieces. And this is what they wrote the bill with."

"Take it," Ansel said. "Quickly."

Bramble reached for the case —

— and the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. A section, a rib of burning timber and slate, crashing through the collection room's roof and slamming into the floor three feet away, close enough that the impact threw them all sideways and filled the air with a snowfall of embers. Through the new hole in the roof, Bramble could see the sky — the lightless sky, the thirteenth-turn sky, utterly black except for the fire's reflection — and for one horrible heartbeat they understood that if they died here, they would die under a sky with nothing in it, and no one would ever know their name had ever been anything but *asset.*

Then Sefa's hand closed around their ankle and hauled, and the moment passed.

"The vial!" Ansel screamed over the roar.

Bramble scrambled up, snatched the case — the whole case, no time for delicacy — and smashed it against the edge of a marble plinth. Glass spidered. They dug the vial out with bleeding fingers, felt it hum against their palm like a living thing recognizing a relative, and shoved it deep into the inner pocket of their coat, the one sewn against the ribs, the one they'd made themselves from a stolen scrap of sailcloth because everything else they owned belonged to the guild.

"I have it," they said. "I have it, I have it —"

"Then we *go.*"

* * *

The way they'd come in was gone. Fire had eaten the corridor between the collection and the main hall, and beyond the fire, Bramble could hear boots — many boots, the measured cadence of guild enforcers who had stopped pretending to be anything but soldiers. The whistle sounded again, closer, and this time it was answered by a second whistle from the far side of the wing. They were being closed around like a fist.

"There." The Hollow King pointed with the terrible calm of a man who had commanded armies once and never quite lost the habit. "The map room. It has an outer wall."

"The map room is on the *second floor,*" Sefa said.

"Yes."

"And the stairs are —"

He was already moving, and they followed, because the alternative was standing still in a burning building listening to whistles, and Bramble would rather die running toward something than standing inside a debt.

The grand stair of the archive wing was a thing of carved oak and iron balustrade, three flights rising through the open heart of the building, and it was dying beautifully — flames climbing its banister like ivy in fast-forward, each step groaning as the heat worked loose the pegs that had held it together for two hundred years. Halfway up the second flight, the flight below them let go.

It didn't collapse all at once. It peeled away, step by step, from the bottom, a cascade of burning oak falling into the dark, and the four of them froze on the landing between second and third flights while the structure shuddered beneath their feet like something waking from a long sleep.

"It's coming down," Ansel said. "It's all coming down."

"Cross it fast and light," Sefa said. "Single file. Don't —"

The enforcers came through the smoke at the base of the remaining stair.

Six of them, maybe seven, shapes more than faces, cudgels and hooked poles in their fists, and at their head the enforcer himself — the one who had marked Ansel for debt reassignment, the one whose voice Bramble knew from a hundred corridors, smooth as sealing wax. He looked up at them through the firelight and smiled the smile of a man reading a balance sheet that had finally come out in his favor.

"There you are," he called up. "All four. The Guildmaster will want the scribe alive. The others are negotiable."

Something in Bramble went very quiet and very cold. *Negotiable.* They had been negotiable their entire life. Asset B-114, minor, unvalued, transferable at the guild's discretion. They knew exactly what that word meant, and they watched it land on Sefa's shoulders and the Hollow King's shoulders and Ansel's shoulders, and they understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that there was no version of the world where they let these people be collected.

"Sefa," Bramble said. "How long can you hold a stair?"

Sefa looked at the groaning structure, then at the enforcers beginning to climb, then at Bramble. She was a disgraced ward-keeper, stripped of rank and license, trained in craft she'd been forbidden to teach, and she had been telling them for weeks that her skills were small ones, hedge-craft, the kind that kept rain off crops and ghosts out of cellars.

She rolled up her sleeves.

"Long enough," she said. "Run."

* * *

What Sefa did to the stair, Bramble never fully saw, because they were running — across the shuddering boards, two at a time, the Hollow King ahead of them moving with a speed that seemed borrowed from somewhere, some reservoir of strength that had to be nearly empty by now. Behind them, Sefa planted both palms flat on the landing's rail and *sang* — a low, grinding note, the sound of a millstone, of a glacier, of something old being asked to do one more impossible thing — and the stair answered her.

The boards beneath the enforcers' feet turned traitor. Pegs loosened. Iron brackets sighed out of stone. The whole remaining flight became a held breath, trembling on the edge of refusal, and the enforcers' climb slowed to a crawl as the structure swayed and cracked and threatened beneath them. One of them fell — a short scream, a crash of timber — and the others pressed back against the wall, suddenly devout.

"Hold the rail," Sefa gasped, to no one, to herself. "Hold, hold, hold —"

Bramble reached the second-floor corridor and turned to look back, and what they saw they would carry for the rest of their life, however long that turned out to be.

Sefa at the rail, arms shaking, chalk-lines blazing up both forearms like veins of lightning, holding a burning staircase together through sheer refusal. Ansel beside her, one hand on her shoulder, feeding her something — words, maybe, or just presence, just the proof that someone was waiting on the other side of her sacrifice. And above them both, stepping backward onto the landing they'd just cleared, the Hollow King turning to face the enforcers with his empty hands raised and his ruined crown-shadow flickering around his head like heat-haze.

"Aldric," Bramble breathed. They'd started using his name recently, when they remembered he wanted it used. "*Aldric.*"

"Go," the king said, without turning around. His voice was calm the way deep water is calm. "Take them out. I am right behind you."

He was not right behind them. They all knew it. The last of his borrowed strength was in his legs and his stance and the strange pressure filling the air around him — the pressure of a man spending memories like coin, paying out pieces of himself to buy thirty seconds. Bramble could see it happening, could see him dimming even as he stood there, growing thinner, more translucent, a candle burning down to give light to strangers.

"No," Bramble said.

"*Go.*" And then, softer, in the voice of a tired man and not a king: "Child. I have spent eleven years losing myself a piece at a time to a crown. Let me spend the last of me on something I *choose.*"

Bramble went.

They hated every step of it. They ran down the map-room corridor with tears cutting tracks through the soot on their face and the vial humming against their ribs like a second heart, and behind them they heard the stair finally surrender — a roar, a whiteout of sparks — and heard, impossibly, footsteps after it. Slow ones. Dragging ones. But footsteps.

* * *

The map room's outer window overlooked the kitchen court, two floors down, over a drain-spout and a trellis that hadn't held weight in decades. Sefa, ash-grey and swaying, broke the window with her elbow and went first, sliding down the spout with a ward-sign flaring under her to catch the worst of the fall. Ansel followed. Then Bramble, clutching their coat shut over the vial, dropping into the dark and hitting the ground hard enough to see stars that weren't there — the sky was still empty, still black, still the thirteenth turn — and rolling, and getting up, because stopping was death.

The Hollow King came last, through the window, down the trellis, and the trellis broke under him and he landed badly, and Bramble caught his arm before he hit the stones. He weighed almost nothing. That was the worst part — how little was left of him, how his borrowed strength had burned down to a stub, how his eyes struggled to focus on Bramble's face as though it were very far away.

"Did we get it?" he asked.

Bramble pressed his hand flat against their coat, against the vial, against the hum. "We got it."

He smiled. It was a small, cracked, human smile, and it was the most kingly thing Bramble had ever seen.

* * *

They escaped through the laundry tunnels — Bramble's contribution, the last gift of nine years spent memorizing a prison — and came out through a dyer's yard into the reeking dark beyond the palace walls, where the city slept under its stolen night and the fires of the archive wing glowed against the underside of the smoke like a wound seen through skin.

It was only there, leaning against a wall in the dyer's yard, hearts hammering, that Bramble took inventory.

The vial: safe. Inner pocket. Humming.

The maps: gone. Every one. Burned with the archive wing, along with the route-notes, the supply caches, the list of sympathetic contacts Sefa had spent weeks compiling. Gone, all of it, into the fire that Ansel's single page had woken.

Half the food: gone, abandoned in the scramble, left in a cellar they could never go back to.

Ansel realized it at the same moment. Bramble watched the understanding cross their face — the arithmetic of it, the scribe's horror of a column that doesn't balance. "Two days' food," Ansel whispered. "No maps. No routes. And the whole guild hunting us, and two turns left until the thirteenth, and we're standing in a dyer's yard with a bottle of starlight and nothing else."

"Not nothing else." Sefa slid down the wall to sit beside them, her burned hands cradled in her lap, her eyes half-closed with exhaustion. She nodded at the four of them, one by one. "This. We have this."

And then the Hollow King, who had been silent since the trellis, straightened slowly against the wall. His gaze had fixed on the eastern horizon, where the black of the thirteenth turn met the black of the city, and something in his face had changed — sharpened, focused, afraid.

"Bramble," he said quietly. "When I was on that stair. Facing the enforcer." He swallowed. "He spoke to me. Not to arrest me. He *offered* me something. In the Guildmaster's voice, through the man's mouth — the crown-thing speaks through its servants now, I think, or I've finally gone mad, I honestly can't tell anymore." His hand found Bramble's shoulder and gripped it, and his grip was shaking. "It offered me my memories back. My throne. My name. All of it, whole — if I bring it the scribe and the vial."

The dyer's yard was very quiet. Somewhere far off, the archive wing groaned and settled into its own ashes.

"And I need you all to know," the Hollow King said, and his eyes were wet and steady and terribly honest, "that for one moment — one — I wanted to say yes."
