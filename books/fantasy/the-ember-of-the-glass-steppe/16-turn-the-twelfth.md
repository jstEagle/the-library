## 16. Turn the Twelfth

The border was a wound that had learned to pretend it wasn't one.

Sefa stood at the edge of the Glass Steppe where the ward-line had run for three hundred years — a seam of standing stones marching from horizon to horizon like the teeth of some buried jaw — and looked at what remained of her life's work. Most of the stones had shattered when the wards collapsed on that last starless night. They lay in the frost-burned grass in pieces no larger than a fist, their carved sigils gone dull and gray, drinking nothing, giving nothing back. A few still stood, cracked but upright, leaning against the wind like old soldiers who hadn't heard the order to fall.

She had spent eleven years of her life tending those stones. She had spent four more being told she was wrong about them.

"They're dead," Bramble said behind her. The child's voice was flat, practical, the voice of someone who had learned early that sentiment was a currency they couldn't afford. "You keep looking at them like they're going to apologize."

"They're not dead." Sefa crouched and picked up a fragment, turning it in her gloved hands. The stone was cold in the way all glass-steppe stone was cold, but beneath the cold there was something else — a faint tremor, like a pulse felt through too many blankets. "Dead things don't hum."

Ansel looked up from the hidden folio, which they had been reading by the gray light of the false dawn, wrapped in every layer they owned. "It hums?"

"Everything here hums. That's what nobody understood." Sefa set the fragment down gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping animal. "The wards were never walls. The first ward-keepers knew that. The guild made us forget. A wall keeps things out. But these—" she gestured at the broken line stretching away into the murk, "—these were always meant to be a *voice*. Something you could call with. We just spent three centuries shouting *stay away* through them instead of *come home*."

The Hollow King — Aldric, though he flinched less at the title than he once had, which worried Sefa more than she let herself examine — stood apart from them, as he usually did, his travel-stained royal colors bleached to the color of old bone. He was watching the sky. Everyone watched the sky now. The thirteenth turn was coming; two turns had already passed since the stars went out, and the darkness overhead had thickened from absence into presence, a ceiling of velvet black that pressed down on the Steppe like a hand testing whether a thing was ripe.

"You can rebuild one," Aldric said. It wasn't quite a question. He had a way of speaking that made statements sound like questions and questions sound like verdicts, and Sefa had stopped trying to decide whether it was a king's habit or a broken man's.

"I can rebuild one," she said. "One is all I need."

* * *

They worked for three days.

Sefa had expected it to be harder. She had expected to need tools the guild had confiscated, rites the guild had locked away, knowledge the guild had spent generations burying under procedure and penalty. Instead she found that her hands remembered everything — the angle at which a shard must be set into its socket, the spiral of the calling-sigil that apprentices weren't taught until their seventh year because it was the sigil that could unmake as well as make, the way you had to breathe while you worked, slow and even, because the stones answered rhythm the way a drum answers a heartbeat.

Bramble proved unexpectedly useful. The child had grown up in the counting-house vaults among sealed crates and catalogued relics, and had an instinct for how objects fit together that bordered on the uncanny. When Sefa couldn't make two fragments seat properly, Bramble would take them, turn them over twice, and say, "This one's lying about its shape," and then show her why.

"That's not a skill," Sefa said, watching the child fit a curved shard into place as though the stone had been waiting twenty years for exactly this hand. "That's a curse."

"It's inventory," Bramble said. "Everything's inventory if you look at it right."

Ansel copied. Of course Ansel copied — give that scribe a blank page and a stick of charcoal and they would document the end of the world in a fair hand with margins. But Sefa noticed, without saying anything, that Ansel had begun drawing the sigils differently than the guild manuals prescribed: looser, rounder, with small annotations in the margins — *this stroke means welcome, not warning* — as though the folio were slowly becoming a different kind of book than the one it started as.

And Aldric — Aldric held the stones.

It was the only labor he was suited for, and he seemed to know it. His strength was borrowed and running out; everyone could see it draining from him turn by turn, the way water drains from a cracked jar. But he could still lift what three people together could not, and he did it silently, hour after hour, kneeling in the frozen grass with the great base-stone across his shoulders while Sefa directed its placement, and when it settled into the earth with a sound like a struck bell, he exhaled and said, quietly, so only she heard:

"My father built these. Did you know that? Not the first ones. The repairs, after the second famine. He used to say a kingdom was just a promise you kept repairing."

Sefa looked at him — this gaunt, hollowing man who had been a king and was becoming, painfully, something else — and said, "He was right."

"Yes," Aldric said. "I'm only sorry it took me losing everything to agree with him."

* * *

On the fourth evening, the ward-stone stood whole.

It was not beautiful, not anymore. Too many of its shards came from different stones along the line, mismatched like a face healed badly after a break. Its sigils were incomplete — Sefa had filled the gaps with her own improvisations, patterns she'd been forbidden to teach, forbidden even to sketch, and her hands had shaken the first time she carved them and steadied by the last. It rose perhaps nine feet out of the earth at the crest of a low hill overlooking the old border road, and when the wind moved through the grass around it, the grass bent toward it slightly, the way wheat bends toward the sun.

"Now what?" Bramble asked. "You going to tell it to keep things out?"

"No." Sefa laid both palms flat against the stone. It was warm. After three days of work, it was finally, blessedly warm, and she nearly wept at the feel of it, the way you might weep at the touch of a fire after years in the cold. She closed her eyes. She found the rhythm — the deep, slow rhythm the first ward-keepers had used before the guild taught everyone fear — and she spoke into the stone.

Not words, exactly. Ward-craft at its root was older than language. But if it had words, the words would have been these:

*You are not alone. Come to the light. Come home.*

The stone lit.

Not the harsh white glare of the old border-wards, the light that had meant *halt, identify yourself, state your business*. This was amber, deep and soft, the color of a cookfire seen through a window on a winter night, the color of the lamp left burning in the window of a house where someone is waiting up for you. The light climbed the stone's scars and pooled in its mismatched seams until the whole thing glowed like a piece of the moon that had remembered what it was for.

And then the light pulsed. Once. Twice. Slow, patient, steady as a heartbeat, rolling outward across the darkened Steppe in rings that none of them could see but all of them could feel — a shiver in the teeth, a warmth behind the breastbone.

"Oh," Ansel whispered. "Oh, it's *singing*."

"How far does it carry?" Aldric asked.

Sefa opened her eyes. Her breath fogged in the cold air, and for the first time since the stars had died, she smiled.

"As far as anyone needs to hear it."

* * *

The first to come was a family of salt-farmers, arriving on the sixth night: a broad-shouldered woman, her wife, and three children, all of them pulling a cart loaded with everything they owned, which was not much. They had seen the light from twelve miles off, the woman said, and had argued the whole way about whether it was a trap.

"Most lights are traps, these days," the woman said, arms crossed, chin up, daring Sefa to disagree.

"Most are," Sefa agreed. "This one isn't. But you were right to check. Check everything, from now on. That's the price of no one ruling you — you have to think for yourselves, and it's exhausting."

The woman studied her for a long moment. Then she stuck out a calloused hand. "Vess. Salt-flats, east fork. The guild burned our leases in the collapse — we're nobody's tenants now, which sounds better than it pays."

"We've got soup," Bramble announced from the cookfire, in the tone of a herald announcing a coronation. "It's mostly onions. Don't be ungrateful about it."

By the ninth night there were forty. By the twelfth, a hundred and sixty, and the hillside had sprouted tents and lean-tos and cookfires like mushrooms after rain, and the air smelled of woodsmoke and boiling grain and, improbably, someone frying fish, because a river-family had arrived from the north with nets and opinions about how everyone else was doing everything wrong.

Sefa had expected refugees. What she had not expected was what the refugees did when they arrived.

They organized.

Nobody ordered them to. Nobody could have — there was no one to order, no bailiff, no assessor, no guild-clerk with a stamp. And yet within days there were watch rotations for the periphery, agreed upon at a fireside meeting that Sefa attended and in which she spoke exactly once. There was a digging crew trenching a latrine line because Vess the salt-farmer had read enough plague-years in her grandmother's stories to know what unwashed camps became. There was a woman named Odji, formerly a guild toll-collector, who had stood up at the second fireside and publicly confessed that she had overcharged travelers for six years, and had offered her ledger-skills to anyone who wanted help reckoning what they owed each other — freely, she said, or not at all — and had been shouted down, argued with, and eventually, grudgingly, taken up on it, because it turned out that even free communities needed someone who could do sums.

"They're building a town," Ansel said one night, standing beside Sefa at the ward-stone, watching the camp glow with a dozen fires. "In twelve days. Without a charter, without a lord, without a single stamped permission."

"The guild would say it can't hold," Sefa said. "No enforcement structure. No hierarchy. They'd write a paper proving it."

"Do you think it'll hold?"

Sefa was quiet for a while. Down the slope, someone had started singing — an old planting song, its words half-forgotten, hummed more than sung, and other voices had joined in without any of them deciding to.

"I think," she said finally, "that I spent fifteen years enforcing order and never once saw it. I saw compliance. I saw fear wearing order's clothes. This—" she nodded at the fires, at the song, at Vess arguing cheerfully with the fish-people about where the well should go, "—this is the first time I've actually seen it. And it looks nothing like what I was trained to protect."

Ansel hugged the folio to their chest — Sefa had noticed they did that now, carried it the way Bramble carried the star-ink vial, close and constant — and said, "When this is over. If we win. Do you think it could be like this everywhere?"

"I think," Sefa said, "that it was like this everywhere, once, before someone figured out you could sell people the idea that they couldn't manage without you." She touched the warm stone. "We're not building anything new, scribe. We're remembering."

* * *

On the fifteenth night, the stone called to someone Sefa did not expect.

She was woken past midnight by a change in the ward-song — a discord, faint but unmistakable, the way a mother wakes to a wrong note in a familiar lullaby. She was up and moving before she was fully awake, knife in hand, and found the disturbance at the eastern edge of the camp: a knot of armed figures at the firelight's margin, maybe thirty of them, ragged and hard-eyed, led by a big woman with a scarred jaw and a ward-tower badge torn half off her coat.

Sefa knew the badge. She knew the woman, too. Keeper Dagny, Third Tower, senior to her by four years, who had once reported her for teaching unauthorized sigils to her apprentice.

"Stand down," Dagny said, and her voice was hoarse, as if she'd been shouting orders for weeks into a country that had stopped listening. "This is an unauthorized gathering. Disperse to designated relief points or be processed as vagrants."

"Dagny." Sefa stepped into the firelight, hands open. "Look around you. There's no one left to designate anything. There's no one left to process anyone. The towers fell. The guild sold the sky. Where exactly are you ordering these people to go?"

"To *order*, Winterbourne. That's the point. Without order—" Dagny's voice cracked, and Sefa saw, suddenly, the truth underneath the badge and the bluster: the woman was terrified. She had lost her tower, her post, her entire architecture of meaning, and she was marching her surviving keepers through the dark trying to reconstruct a wall that no longer existed, because the alternative was to admit that everything she'd given her life to was gone. "Without order, it's just — it's just everyone alone in the dark."

Sefa looked back at the camp. At the fires. At Vess's children asleep in a cart bed, at Odji teaching sums by lantern-light to a circle of farmers, at Bramble — her Bramble, theirs — showing three refugee kids how to identify edible roots with the gravity of a professor. At Aldric sitting quietly at the lowest fire, holding someone's baby because the parents needed sleep, with the tenderness of a man rehearsing for a life he'd never allowed himself to want.

"No," Sefa said. "Look again. That's not everyone alone in the dark. That's everyone *together* in the dark, which turns out to be a completely different thing." She extended her hand. "We rebuilt a ward-stone here, Dagny. First one in the Steppe. But we didn't build it to keep anyone out. We built it to call people in. Your keepers are starving — I can see it. Put down the pikes. There's soup. It's mostly onions, and Bramble will absolutely call you ungrateful, but there's enough."

For a long moment, Dagny didn't move. The wind moved the grass. The ward-stone sang its low amber note above them both, patient as a heartbeat.

Then, somewhere behind Dagny, one of her keepers — young, hollow-cheeked, a girl who couldn't have been more than sixteen — dropped her pike in the snow with a clatter, and the sound broke something open. One by one, weapons followed it, until the big keeper stood empty-handed in the firelight, and Sefa took her arm, and led her toward the soup.

* * *

Two hundred and forty souls, by the count Odji chalked on a slate hung from the ward-stone itself. Two hundred and forty, and the number grew daily, drawn across the black Steppe by a single amber light pulsing like a heart that refused to stop.

But on the morning after Dagny's keepers joined the fires, Sefa climbed the hill alone and found that the stone had changed.

A new sigil had appeared on its western face — carved fine and deep in a hand she almost recognized, in a style she definitely recognized, because she had spent fifteen years studying the guild's official registers and the hand that signed the Guildmaster's decrees. Except this carving was not a decree. It was a single word, in the old tongue, the tongue the first ward-keepers had spoken before the guild taught everyone fear:

*Hurry.*

And below the hill, far out on the dark plain where the old capital road ran south, Sefa could see lights. Not cookfire amber. Not the warm scatter of a traveling camp.

Lanterns. Rows of them, ranked and regular, moving in formation. Guild lanterns, green-flamed and cold, dozens of them, coming up the road toward the beacon — and behind them, at the head of the column, something that caught the lightless sky and threw it back wrong, something that walked like a king and cast a shadow that moved a half-step ahead of its body.

Sefa put her hand on the warm stone and felt its song stutter, just once, like a heart missing a beat.

"Bramble," she called down the hill, not taking her eyes off the road. "Wake the others. Tell them the twelfth turn is over."

Her throat tightened around the rest of it.

"And tell Ansel — the Guildmaster knows exactly where we are."
