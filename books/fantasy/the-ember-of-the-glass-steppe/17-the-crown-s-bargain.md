## 17. The Crown's Bargain

The crown-thing came to him in the hour when even the wind forgot to breathe.

Aldric — for he had learned to call himself that again, the way a man relearns the name of a village burned down behind him — sat alone at the edge of the broken border, where Sefa's rebuilt ward-stone glowed faintly against the dark like a coal cupped in two hands. The others slept. Ansel had fallen asleep over the hidden folio, ink-stained fingers still curled around a page. Bramble had wedged themselves against Sefa's side without asking permission, and Sefa had pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of permission. The Hollow King had taken the first watch because he no longer needed sleep as much as he needed to be alone with what remained of his mind.

That was when the voice arrived.

It did not come from any direction. It came from inside his own skull, wearing his own timbre, the way a thief wears a stolen coat.

*You look tired, brother.*

"I am not your brother," Aldric said aloud, quietly, so as not to wake them. "I am the thing you were cut from."

*And yet we share so much.* A pause, and then — this was the cruelty of it — a memory surfaced unbidden: the smell of beeswax candles in the palace chapel, the weight of his mother's hand on his seven-year-old head, the specific gray of rain on the eastern towers. His memories. Held up before him like jewels on a merchant's tray. *Do you remember her hands? I do. I keep them safe. I keep everything safe.*

"You keep nothing," he said. "You consume."

*Semantics.* The voice smiled; he could hear it smile, a sound like paper folding. *I have an offer, Aldric. I have waited to make it until you were tired enough to hear it honestly.*

The night pressed close. Beyond the ward-stone's small glow, the Steppe stretched black and starless, thirteen turns deep into the long dark. Somewhere far off, a wolf sang to nothing, or perhaps to the absence where the moon should have been.

*Here is my bargain,* the crown-thing said. *Deliver me the scribe. Deliver me the vial of star-ink. Do this, and I will give back everything I took.*

Aldric said nothing. He had learned that silence was the only armor left to him, and even it was full of holes.

*Your memories,* the voice continued, patient as interest accruing. *All of them. Not the fragments I let leak through your dreams — all of them. Your coronation. Your mother's face. The name of the man who taught you to ride. The words of the vow you spoke at nineteen, kneeling in the snow at Vellamarch. You cannot remember Vellamarch, can you? You know there was a vow. You know there was snow. But the vow itself is a locked room, and I hold the key.*

It was true. That was the unbearable thing. It was true, and the voice knew it was true, and it laid each truth down gently, one by one, like a physician setting out instruments.

*Your throne. The palace is cold without you. The halls miss the sound of your step — I have made certain they do not get it. Every servant who ever loved you has been reassigned. Every portrait of you has been turned to the wall. There is a hole in the world shaped exactly like you, Aldric, and I am offering to let you fill it.*

*And your name.* Here the voice softened, went almost tender, which was worst of all. *Not "the Hollow King." Not the story they tell in villages now, the ghost-king who wanders. Your name. The one your mother gave you before the crown ever touched your head. Do you even remember it?*

He reached for it, the way he reached for it every night, every hour, in the small hours when the fire burned low. And found — as always — the shape of a door with nothing behind it. He knew he had a name. He could feel the absence of it the way an amputee feels the missing limb: precisely, achingly, constantly.

"No," he whispered.

*I do,* said the crown-thing. *I say it every morning when I sign the decrees. Would you like to hear it?*

"No," he said again, harder, because some part of him — some last unlooted vault of himself — understood that hearing it spoken in that stolen mouth would be a wound no dawn could close.

*Then earn it,* the voice said, and all the tenderness drained out of it at once, leaving something smooth and transactional beneath. *Three days. The thirteenth turn comes. Bring me the scribe and the vial, and walk into the palace whole. Refuse, and when the turn completes, you will not even be a ghost. You will be weather. You will be the draft under a door. I will wear your face until the sun forgets there was ever another one.*

The presence withdrew the way a tide withdraws — slowly, reluctantly, leaving things stranded on the shore of his mind that had not been there before. A memory of his mother's hands. The smell of beeswax. The color of rain on the eastern towers. Gifts, or bait, or both.

Aldric sat very still until the sky began, imperceptibly, to consider lightening — though these days it never truly did, only passed from black to a bruised charcoal, the perpetual dusk of the thirteenth turn approaching.

* * *

He did not tell them.

That was the first decision, and it shamed him even as he made it. When Sefa woke and asked how his watch had gone, he said, "Quietly," and watched her study his face with those ward-keeper's eyes that read disturbances the way other people read weather. She saw something. She said nothing. He was grateful and hated being grateful.

They broke camp and walked. The Steppe in the false dawn was a country of ash-colored grass and stones that seemed to lean away from travelers, as if the land itself had grown wary. Bramble ranged ahead the way they always did, small and quick, cataloguing edible roots and old guild boundary-markers with the fluent expertise of someone raised inside the machine that had devoured this country. Ansel walked beside Aldric, as had become their habit, and said little, but their silence was companionable in a way that frightened him more than the crown-thing's voice. Trust was heavier to carry than suspicion. Suspicion weighed nothing. Trust could break bones if you dropped it.

At midday they sheltered in the ribs of a ruined waystation, and Ansel spread the folio open across a flat stone, and for two hours the four of them bent over pages of impossible trades while Sefa traced ward-diagrams in the dirt and Bramble argued about tunnel routes with the authority of a general twice their size. Aldric watched them and felt the offer sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone.

*Look at them,* the voice murmured, from wherever it went between visits. *The scribe who cannot stop copying. The keeper who cannot stop guarding. The child who cannot stop counting exits. And you, brother, who cannot stop grieving. None of them can save you. Only I can give you back to yourself.*

He pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw stars that no longer existed in the sky.

* * *

Night came — the real night, the long one — and with it the darkest hour, though he would not understand until later that the darkness was not the sky's doing but his own.

He walked out beyond the camp's edge, past the ward-stone's glow, to a shelf of rock overlooking the dead plain. He told himself it was to keep watch. He was becoming skilled at telling himself things.

And there, alone, he let himself do the arithmetic.

If he delivered Ansel, the scribe would be consumed — he knew now what consumption meant, had seen the thing's work in the palace, had watched servants return from audiences with the king-shaped creature looking subtly emptied, like cups drained and set back on the shelf. If he delivered the vial, the thirteenth turn would complete, and the Steppe would be sealed in permanent night, and every village like the cookfire court — every small brave experiment in governing themselves — would be taxed and bound and ledgered into silence forever.

Against that: his mother's hands. The vow at Vellamarch. His name.

It did not seem like enough. That was the horror. Laid honestly on the scale, the suffering of thousands upon one side and the wholeness of one broken man on the other, and the scale barely moved, because grief is greedy and memory is the dearest currency a soul holds. He understood, in that hour, exactly how the Guildmaster had been bought. He understood how anyone was bought. You did not purchase a person's evil. You purchased their longing, and the evil arrived as change.

*Well?* the voice said, gentle as frost forming. *You have done the sums. You were always good at sums. Shall I tell you your mother's name too, as a down payment?*

"Stop," Aldric said.

*One word from you,* the voice said, *and I will show you the vow at Vellamarch entire. Every syllable. You knelt in the snow and you swore — do you want to know what you swore?*

He wanted it so badly his teeth ached. He wanted it the way the drowning want air, thoughtlessly, totally, with the whole animal self. For one long moment he stood on the rock shelf with the dead stars above him and the dead plain below, and he was nineteen years old in the snow, and he was seventy years old in the dark, and he was nothing at all, and the word *yes* gathered itself in his throat like a stone gathering speed down a hill—

—and stopped.

Because a memory came to him that was not bait. One of his own. Small, worthless, untradeable: a kitchen in the palace, decades ago, a scullery boy — himself, before, when he had still snuck down to the kitchens because the kitchens were warm and the throne room was not — watching an old cook salt a pot of soup. *Too much salt and you ruin the pot for one good meal,* the cook had said. *A king should know that better than anyone.*

He laughed. It came out cracked and rusted, the first laugh in longer than he could measure, which was not long in itself but long in what it meant.

"You already showed me your price," he said to the dark. "You showed me your price the day you took my crown. Everything you offer is salt."

The voice said nothing. In its silence he heard, for the first time, something almost like uncertainty — the sound of a ledger discovering an entry it could not balance.

Aldric turned his back on the dead stars and walked back toward the small glow of the ward-stone, toward the sleeping shapes of the people he had decided, in the darkest hour, not to sell.

* * *

But deciding was not enough. He had learned that much about himself: that intentions eroded, that the offer would come again tomorrow night and the night after, each time wearing new tenderness, each time finding him a little more worn. A wall built once would fail. He needed something else. He needed proof — not to them, but to himself. Evidence, dated and signed, that on this night, in this hour, he had chosen.

He was, after all, still a king. And kings, whatever else the crown had eaten, remembered how to make a thing official.

There was no paper in the camp except Ansel's precious folio, and he would not touch that. But Sefa kept scraps of ward-parchment in her kit, offcuts too small for diagrams, and Bramble — who noticed everything and hoarded useful things the way magpies hoard — had a stub of graphite pencil half-chewed at one end, liberated from some guild counting-house in a former life. Aldric traded Bramble a button from his travel-stained royal coat for the pencil. Bramble bit the button, pronounced it genuine, and asked no questions, which was its own form of mercy.

By the banked cookfire, in the blue hour before the false dawn, Aldric knelt with a scrap of parchment on his knee and wrote.

His hand shook. That was the first hard thing — the crown had eaten his fine motor control along with his finer memories, and the letters came out crabbed, a ruin of the courtly script that had once signed charters. He wrote anyway.

*To the scribe Ansel Quintana,* he began, and then stopped, because flattery and preamble were the tools of the thing that wore his shape, and he would not use them. He crossed it out. He started plainer.

*Last night it offered me everything. My memories. My throne. My name. All of it, for you and the vial. I am writing this so that tomorrow, when it offers again, I will have proof of what I answered tonight, which is: no. Not because I am noble. Because I finally understood that everything it offers was mine before it took it, and a man does not buy back his own hands with someone else's blood.*

He paused. The fire ticked. Somewhere out in the dark, the wolf sang again, closer now, or maybe it was not a wolf.

*It knows my name,* he wrote. *It says it every morning when it signs decrees in my hand. I have not heard my own name spoken in a voice I trust since the crown closed around my head. If I fall — if the offer finds a crack in me that tonight it did not — then know that the man who betrays you will not be me. Test him. Ask him the color of rain on the eastern towers; he will know, because he keeps my memories like coins in a purse. Ask him instead what the old cook said about salt, and watch his face. That one is mine. No one offered it to me. No one can take it back.*

He looked at the letter for a long time. It was ugly — cramped, crossed-out, the handwriting of a sick man, nothing like the decrees posted in the capital in his stolen hand. And that, he realized, was the whole point. The crown-thing wrote beautifully. It wrote flawlessly, mechanically, the way a machine writes. This crooked, blotted, human scrawl was the one signature in all the Glass Steppe the thing could not forge, because forgery requires practice, and practice requires caring, and the thing cared about nothing it had not eaten.

He signed it. Not *Aldric*, which was the name he had rebuilt for himself out of scraps — and not his true name, which he still could not reach. He signed it the way kings sign, with intention rather than ornament: a single deliberate stroke, and beneath it, *the man who was king, choosing.*

Then he folded the letter, and hesitated, and did the hardest thing of all, which was to stand up and cross the camp to where Ansel slept curled around the folio like a door around its hinge, and kneel, and tuck the parchment into the folio's binding where the scribe would find it at morning.

Ansel stirred. Opened one eye. Saw the Hollow King of the Glass Steppe kneeling in the ash-light with chewed pencil smudges on his fingers, and — miracle of miracles — asked nothing.

"Read it when you wake properly," Aldric whispered. "And keep it. If I ever hand you the vial with a smile on my face, burn this instead of trusting me."

Ansel's eye stayed on him a moment longer, reading him the way Sefa read wards — looking for the disturbance underneath. Whatever they found there, they nodded once, slowly, and closed the eye, and slept.

Aldric returned to his place by the fire and sat watching the coals breathe.

Far away, in a palace lit for a feast of eternal night, something wearing his crown paused mid-signature and felt, along the thin thread that still connected them, a door close that had stood open for thirteen turns. It tried the handle once. Twice. Then it set down the pen, and looked at the decree it had been writing — *an order for the collection of one scribe, one vial, and one hollow remainder* — and for the first time since the famine year, it felt the cold emotion that ledgers are designed to prevent.

It felt doubt.

And in the camp by the broken border, a hollow man watched the embers and discovered that his hands, though empty, had stopped shaking — and that somewhere out under the starless sky, the thirteenth turn had begun to move, and it was coming for them all whether he was whole or hollow when it arrived.
