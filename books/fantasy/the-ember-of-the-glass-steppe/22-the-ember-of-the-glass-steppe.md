## 22. The Ember of the Glass Steppe

The school had been a granary once, and before that, if the old beams were to be believed, a watchtower against raiders who had stopped coming long before anyone now living was born. Its walls were thick glass-block set in pale mortar, and on mornings like this one — the first hard frost of the season, the sun climbing over the eastern scree — the whole building filled with light and threw it back out again in slow amber ripples, so that from the road it must have looked as though the structure were breathing.

Ansel Quintana had chosen it for that reason, though they would never have admitted so aloud. A building that breathed seemed a fitting place to teach people how to write.

They arrived early, as they always did, while the frost still silvered the window ledges and the village beyond the schoolyard was only a suggestion of chimney smoke and lowing cattle. The door stuck in cold weather; it had stuck all autumn, and Ansel had developed a particular way of lifting the latch while shouldering the frame at the same time, a small private choreography that no one else could manage without instruction. They rather liked that about it. There had been too many doors in their life that opened for anyone with the right seal.

Inside, the air smelled of woodsmoke and ink and, faintly, of the dried lavender Bramble insisted on hanging from every beam because *a room where children learn should not smell like a room where adults argue*. Ansel lit the stove, set the kettle on, and then performed the last ritual of the morning, the one they never skipped, the one that had become as automatic as breathing and as necessary.

They took down the jar.

It sat on the high shelf behind the teacher's desk, between a chipped mug of pens and a brass instrument of Sefa's devising that measured starlight and looked, to the untrained eye, like an elaborate eggbeater. The jar was ordinary glass, the kind pickles came in, with a cork stopper worn smooth by handling. Inside it lay a quantity of ash: fine, grey-black, shot through here and there with threads that caught the morning light and held it, glimmering faintly, like embers that had forgotten they were supposed to go out.

Master ledger ash. Or what remained of it after Ansel had carried it out of the burning archive wing in a satchel scorched nearly through, after Sefa had picked the cinders from their hair for three days running, after Aldric — he was Aldric now, stubbornly, insistently, a tired man with dirt under his fingernails — had suggested they keep some of it, *because things that are destroyed deserve witnesses*.

Ansel set the jar on the desk, dead center, where a ledger would once have sat, and regarded it for a long moment, as they did every morning.

Eleven years. That was what their debt had been, when they were young and the numbers had felt like the shape of the world itself. Eleven years of copying entries for bottled thunder and other people's luck, eleven years of watching impossible things move across the page in a clerk's careful hand and learning not to wonder where they went. And then one line — *one moon, whole* — appearing and vanishing within the hour, and everything after that: the folio hidden in their coat lining, the enforcer's chalk mark on their door, the flight through the collapsing guildhall with the stars going out overhead like lamps being snuffed room by room.

The Guildmaster had told them, in the end, in that moment of terrible clarity before the alarms woke the palace, that the debt had been manufactured. That Ansel had been bound on purpose, purchased at birth or near enough, raised as an instrument for reading a ledger written in the ink of a murdered star. For a long time afterward — through the thirteenth turn, through the breaking of the crown, through the long strange dawn — Ansel had carried that knowledge like a stone in the chest. *You were made for this. You were never free.*

But standing here now, in a breathing building full of morning light, with the ash of every debt in the Glass Steppe glowing gently on the desk before them, Ansel had come to understand something the Guildmaster never had.

It didn't matter what you were made for. It mattered what you made yourself for.

* * *

The students began arriving as the frost burned off.

There were nine of them this term, which was more than the benches properly held and fewer than had applied, and they came from everywhere, which was the point. Two were village children from the cookfire court — Sefa still couldn't say the name of that place without her voice doing something complicated, so she just called it *the village*, and everyone understood. One was a former guild apprentice whose family had been ward-keepers for six generations and who had never been taught a single thing about how wards actually worked until Sefa started holding open lessons in the market square. One was a grown woman, gray at the temples, who had spent thirty years keeping accounts for a wool merchant and had come to learn, in her words, "how to write things that are true even when they're inconvenient."

And there was a boy named Tam, perhaps ten years old, who had shown up on the first day with no letter of introduction, no fee, and no surname that anyone knew, and who had stood in the doorway with his chin up and said, "I heard you take anybody."

Ansel had looked at him for a long moment — at the patched coat, the hollows under his eyes, the fierce stubborn set of his jaw, which was so much like another face from another cellar that Ansel's throat had gone tight — and said, "We do. Come in out of the cold."

That was the whole curriculum, really, though it took a full term for students to believe it. *Come in out of the cold.* Everything else followed from it.

The rule of the school was simple and had been since the first day, when Ansel had stood before seven nervous strangers holding a piece of chalk and said: **In this room, you will write only true things.** Not pleasant things, not safe things, not things that would please whoever might read them. True things. If you did not know whether a thing was true, you wrote that you did not know, and that too was true, and that too was acceptable. If a thing was true and cruel, you wrote it carefully, and you thought about why it needed writing at all. If a thing was true and beautiful, you wrote it exactly, without embellishment, because beauty did not need your help.

"Under the guild," Ansel had told them, "the most valuable skill a scribe could have was the ability to make a lie look like arithmetic. I was very good at it. It took me most of my life to unlearn." They had tapped the chalk against the board. "You will not learn that skill here. If that's what you came for, the door is behind you."

No one had left. Not even the wool merchant's accountant, who had wept quietly into her sleeve during the second lesson and stayed anyway.

This morning's lesson was composition, and the prompt — Ansel had learned to call them prompts rather than assignments, because assignments implied authority and prompts implied invitation — was written on the slate board in Ansel's careful hand:

*Describe something that was taken from you, and tell the truth about whether you want it back.*

Ansel watched them work. Tam chewed his pen and stared out the rippling glass wall at the garden — Aldric's garden, the one planted over the scarred floor of the old vault, where winter wheat and memory-of-spring bulbs slept under the frost waiting for a thaw they had earned. The gray-haired accountant wrote steadily, her pen moving like a loom shuttle. The ward-keeper's child had covered half a page already and kept crossing things out and rewriting them, which Ansel privately considered the surest sign of a true writer they had ever seen.

Ansel let them work and drank tea and did not hover, and when the light shifted toward midmorning they gave the signal — two soft knocks on the desk, a habit borrowed from Bramble, who knocked twice on everything, doors and tables and the tops of jars, as if announcing themselves to the world — and asked for volunteers.

Tam volunteered, because Tam always volunteered, because silence was a thing he had known intimately and refused to practice anymore.

"I wrote about my name," he said. He stood up straight, the way Bramble had taught him, feet planted. "I don't have one. Just Tam. Before I came here I lived at a counting-house, kind of, and they had a number for me instead. Four-one-one-nine. And the truth is—" He stopped. His ears went red. Ansel nodded at him, gently, the way Sefa had taught them both: *let the pause do the work; truth needs room to walk*. "The truth is I don't want the number back. But I want to be the kind of person who gets to choose a name. So I'm choosing one. I'm going to be Tam Winterbourne."

The room went very quiet.

Ansel had prepared, as always, a composed and appropriate response. What came out instead was: "That's a good name. It's served me well." And then, because the class was looking at them with expressions ranging from delight to confusion: "Class dismissed early. Go help Master Aldric with the mulching, he'll pretend to be annoyed and he won't mean it."

They went, laughing, stamping into their boots, tumbling out into the bright cold. Tam paused at the door and looked back, and Ansel raised the teacup to him, and the boy grinned and was gone.

Ansel sat alone in the breathing room for a while, listening to the building's glass walls tick as the sun warmed them, and found that their eyes were wet, and did not particularly mind.

* * *

Sefa came by at noon, as she did most days, ostensibly to inspect the school's heating flue and actually to eat lunch and complain about her advanced students, whom she adored and described exclusively in terms of catastrophe. Today she brought bread and a report.

"The northern villages have agreed on a common calendar," she said, tearing the loaf. "Based on star-rise, not guild turns. They're calling it the Open Count. First year of it starts at spring equinox. Bramble wants us all to attend the marking ceremony, and before you ask, yes, there will be dancing, and yes, you will participate, I've already told them you would."

"You've doomed me."

"I've enrolled you. There's a difference. Doom implies you'd enjoy resisting." She passed over the bread and studied Ansel with the frank, assessing gaze that had once catalogued failing wards along four hundred miles of border and now catalogued her friends, relentlessly, out of love. "You've been crying."

"I have not."

"Your eyes are red and you're holding the bread like it's a handkerchief."

Ansel put the bread down. Outside the window, faintly, they could hear Aldric's voice instructing children in the proper depth for planting bulbs, patient and wry, the voice of a man who had been a king and had discovered he preferred being a gardener, and who spoke about it sometimes in the evenings in words Ansel kept close: *I wore the crown for forty years and it ate everything I planted inside myself. Now I plant things outside myself and nothing eats them. It seems a fair trade.*

"A student took my name today," Ansel said.

Sefa's eyebrows rose. Then she smiled — the rare full smile, the one that transformed her face from watchful to radiant — and reached across the desk and squeezed Ansel's wrist, once, hard, which was how she hugged.

"He'll do better with it than the guild ever did," she said.

After she left — taking the rest of the bread and promising to return with Bramble for supper, *and don't think you can hide in grading, I know all your tricks* — Ansel turned back to the desk, to the jar.

The ash shimmered. It always shimmered, a little, especially in direct light. Scholars had come, in the first months after the unbinding, asking to study it; alchemists had offered staggering sums; one trembling archivist from the dissolved Ledger Guild had knelt on the schoolhouse floor and begged to be allowed a single grain, swearing it could be rendered back into ink, that the ledger could be reconstructed, that the old order could be—

Ansel had helped the man up off the floor, given him tea, and told him no. Kindly, but no. Some things, once unbound, stay unbound. That was not the tragedy of them. That was the entire point.

But the ash was not nothing. Ansel had learned that in the years since. It remembered being a binding, the way a broken chain remembers having held something. On certain nights — the longest nights, the ones closest to the anniversary of the thirteenth turn — the threads in the ash would glow brighter, and if Ansel held the jar up to the returned stars, the glow would answer them, faintly, like a conversation conducted in a language almost forgotten.

Bramble had asked once, sprawled on the schoolhouse floor amid a fort of borrowed books, what Ansel thought would happen to it eventually. Whether it would fade.

"I don't know," Ansel had said. "Maybe it'll just keep glowing. Maybe that's what it is now. Not a record of debts. A record that they ended."

*Everything bound can be unbound,* Aldric had said, the night the crown broke in his hands and the shadow that wore his name unraveled into ordinary darkness and the moon finished its turn into dawn. He had said it kneeling in the wreckage of the ritual chamber, holding pieces of a thing that had eaten forty years of his life, and he had not sounded triumphant. He had sounded like a man setting down an enormous weight and discovering his legs still worked. *Remember that. Whatever comes after us, whatever new bindings get built — remember that it can be undone. That's the inheritance. Not the peace. The proof.*

* * *

The afternoon light was going gold and long when the knock came at the schoolhouse door — two knocks, Bramble's knock, but wrong somehow, hesitant, and when Ansel opened it there stood a girl of perhaps twelve, travel-stained, alone, with a satchel that had clearly walked a long way and a look on her face that Ansel recognized the way one recognizes one's own reflection in dark water.

She had come down from the north, she said. Her village had heard of the school. She had a letter, but she couldn't read it herself, and she handed it over with her chin up and her hands shaking, and inside, in a crude but determined hand, someone had written: *This is our daughter. We cannot teach her what she needs. Please teach her to write true things. We are not giving her away. We are sending her to grow.*

Ansel read it twice. Then they stepped aside and held the door wide, the way it was meant to be held, the way it had been held for them once in a counting-house cellar by a child who had asked only to be treated as a person.

"Come in out of the cold," Ansel said. "There's tea. And there's someone you should meet — she keeps the ash."

The girl looked past Ansel's shoulder, to the desk, to the jar where the ember-light of the burned ledger glimmered softly against the gathering dusk, and her eyes went wide.

"What is it?" she whispered.

Ansel smiled, and lifted the jar into the last of the light, and answered the same question they had answered a hundred times and expected to answer a hundred more, in this breathing room, for as long as they were given.

"That," they said, "is proof that everything bound can be unbound. And now it's your turn to learn how."
