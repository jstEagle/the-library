## 1. Ink That Remembers

The ink was still wet when Lena heard the criers in the square.

They had been working since before dawn on the commission—a survey of the harbor district for Magistrate Corvel, all clean lines and honest measurements, the kind of map that would hang behind a tax collector's desk and be consulted by men who believed that a town could be fully known if only you drew enough of its edges. Grayharbor clung to the lower shoulder of the Thornfells like a barnacle on a ship's hull, and the commission had taken Lena three weeks: every warehouse stair, every tide-gate, the crooked spine of Fishmonger's Row where the buildings leaned together like conspirators sharing a secret.

Lena blew across the parchment, watching the ink dull from black to the color of old iron, and felt the small satisfaction of finished work settle into their chest. It never lasted long. Satisfaction was a candle; it burned down no matter how carefully you cupped your hands around it.

Outside the window, the morning fog was lifting off the harbor in slow gray ribbons. Gulls argued over something dead on the pilings. Somewhere below, a fishwife was shouting about the price of cod with the particular fury of a woman who had been cheated before and would be cheated again, and would not go quietly this time. Ordinary sounds. The ordinary world, going about its ordinary morning.

Lena reached beneath the floorboard under the drafting table and drew out the other maps.

There were eleven of them now. Eleven pages of vellum, each one drawn in a hand no one else in the realm could imitate, because no one else in the realm could see what Lena saw when they closed their eyes and let the ember-gift rise up through their blood like heat through iron. These were not maps of roads or coastlines. These were maps of *between*—of the fold-paths that threaded through the Thornfell mountains like veins through a wrist, passages that existed a half-step sideways from the world, visible only to someone whose blood remembered the Ninefold Court. Smugglers called them the whisper-roads. They paid well for them, in coin and in silence, and Doss carried the pages out of town sewn into his coat lining, and for six years no one had died because of what Lena knew.

Six years. Lena laid the newest page on the table—the one they'd finished two nights past, showing a fold-path entrance behind the frozen waterfall at Cindermere—and looked at it the way you look at a lover you already know will betray you.

It was beautiful. That was the cruelty of it. The lines moved, very slightly, if you watched them long enough—not flickering, but breathing, the way all things touched by the gift breathed. A road drawn by an ungifted hand sat still on its paper like a dead thing pinned to a board. A road drawn by Lena's hand lived. And living things could be recognized.

The bell in the harbor tower began to ring.

Not the market bell—Lena knew its lazy double-stroke the way they knew their own heartbeat. This was the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one, and beneath it, cutting through the fog, came a voice amplified by one of those brass speaking-trumpets the crown's heralds favored, a voice with the practiced boredom of a man who had made this announcement in forty towns and would make it in forty more.

"By decree of the Regency Council, in the name of the Crown and the preservation of the realm—" the voice paused for the bell to finish its cycle "—a general inquiry is declared against practitioners of the forbidden ember-art. All persons harboring knowledge of such persons are bound by law to declare it. Rewards are offered. Concealment is treason."

Lena did not move. Their hands stayed flat on the table, on either side of the living map, and they watched the ink breathe and thought, with a strange detached clarity: *so it has finally come.*

They had known it would. Everyone gifted knew it would—the way farmers know the flood year is coming, the way old dogs know a storm before the sky turns. The crown had tolerated the ember-gift for a generation, grudgingly, the way you tolerate a scar. But scars remember being wounds. And lately there had been rumors down from the capital: purges in the universities, archivists disappearing from the royal libraries, a decree sealed in red wax that no smuggler would repeat aloud above a whisper. The Ninefold Court had fallen thirty years ago, but crowns have long memories, and the thing they feared most was not an army. It was a single person who could walk through walls.

The herald's voice went on, listing rewards with the precision of a butcher pricing cuts. Five hundred crowns for information leading to the arrest of a gifted practitioner. A pardon for any accomplice who confessed within the week. Lena listened to all of it while looking at the waterfall at Cindermere, rendered in ink that remembered being fire.

Then they opened the little clay brazier on the hearth and fed it a twist of oilcloth, and held the map over the flame.

It caught reluctantly, the way living things do. The lines writhed as they burned—the fold-path seeming, for one horrible instant, to flee the fire along its own length—and then the vellum curled and blackened and the waterfall at Cindermere ceased to exist anywhere in the world except in Lena's memory, which was, they understood now, the most dangerous place in the realm.

They fed the rest of the pages to the flame one at a time. It took a while. They did not hurry. Each map deserved its moment; each one had cost weeks of night work, of closing their eyes and walking the between-places until their nose bled, of waking with frost on their eyelashes and their heart hammering some rhythm that wasn't quite human anymore. Ten more small deaths. The smoke smelled of tallow and something sweeter underneath, something mineral, like hot stone—which was the smell of the gift itself, Lena had learned, the smell of their own blood working. They wondered if the neighbors would notice it drifting from the window. They wondered if noticing it would be enough for someone to inform.

*Five hundred crowns.* Half the street would sell them for fifty.

When the last page was ash, Lena swept the brazier's contents into the cold hearth and mixed the ashes into the old soot there, thorough as a murderer. Then they sat back down at the drafting table, picked up their pen, and went back to Magistrate Corvel's honest, lifeless, ungifted map of the harbor district, because a cartographer seen working at dawn on a lawful commission was a cartographer no one would question, and because their hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

* * *

The square was full by midmorning.

Lena went down with the crowd the way water goes downhill, unremarkable, hood up against the wind off the water. In the center of the square, beside the old customs house, the crown's men had raised a platform—not a gallows, nothing so honest—and on it stood a crier in royal blue and gray, flanked by four witch-hunters in their slate-colored coats with the crown's nine-pointed star stamped in ash-gray on the breast. Nine points. The court's own sigil, turned inside out and worn as a threat. Lena had always found that the cruelest joke of the new regime: they had taken the emblem of the Ninefold Court and made it the mark of the men who hunted its remnants.

The crier read the decree again, slower this time, letting each clause land like a dropped stone. Around Lena, the crowd did what crowds do: muttered, shifted, avoided one another's eyes. An old woman near the fish stalls crossed herself with the salt-splash gesture the harbor folk used and whispered something to a saint who had been dead five hundred years and had never once interceded in a witch-hunt. Two boys at the front were laughing about something, until one of the witch-hunters looked at them, and then they weren't laughing.

And then Lena saw him, and everything in the square went quiet in a different way.

Doss stood at the edge of the platform, between two hunters, his wrists bound in front of him with gray cord. He was not struggling. That was the first wrong thing—Doss, who had once talked his way out of a crown checkpoint by convincing the sergeant that the barrels of brandy were church candles, Doss who could charm the scales off a fish—stood perfectly still, and he was looking out over the crowd, and he was looking *for* something. For someone.

His eyes found Lena's across forty feet of frightened townsfolk, and Doss smiled.

It was not a brave smile. It was not a defiant smile. It was the smile he'd given Lena the day they'd met, fourteen years ago, when Lena—new to Grayharbor, friendless, so carefully invisible—had dropped a folded map in the mud and Doss had picked it up, glanced at it, glanced at Lena, and handed it back without a word, having understood in a single heartbeat exactly what it was and exactly what it would cost him ever to mention. *I know,* that smile said, the same as it had said then. *And I'll carry it.*

"No," Lena breathed, and no one heard, because everyone in the square was breathing the same word.

The crier was saying something about conspiracy. About a network. About how the crown knew that the gifted did not work alone—that they had couriers, confederates, *friends*—and how the prisoner had been taken with seditious materials on his person, and how the prisoner's associates had one week to come forward, after which—

Doss was pushed to his knees. One of the hunters stepped forward with a torch, and the fog-damp air seemed to contract around the flame, and Lena felt it before it happened, felt it in their teeth and their sternum and the backs of their eyes: the pull. The terrible, traitorous pull of fire on gifted blood, the way a drowning man feels the surface. Every ember-gifted soul in that square felt it too, wherever they were hiding, whatever faces they wore. That was the design. That was the whole hideous architecture of it. You did not burn a man in public to punish him. You burned him in public because somewhere in the crowd, his kind could not look away.

Lena looked away. It didn't matter. They felt him burn anyway—felt it as a wrongness in their own skin, a heat with no source, and around them the crowd made the sound crowds make, and the gulls went on screaming over the harbor, and the fishwife stopped shouting about cod.

Afterward, when the platform was being struck and the ash was being shoveled into a barrow like so much refuse, Lena stood in the shadow of the customs house with their hands pressed flat against the cold stone, holding themselves upright, holding themselves *quiet*, because the gift wanted to answer grief with fire and there was no faster way to die than to let it.

*He knew what I was, and he carried it, and now he is a barrow of ash, and it was my secret that killed him as surely as the torch.*

One week, the crier had said. Come forward, or the burning continues.

Lena thought of the eleven maps, ten of them ash, one of them still hidden—no. All of them ash. All but the one that mattered, the one they hadn't let themselves think about in years, the half-finished thing sewn into the lining of their coat these six years past: the route to the heart of Thornfell, the map Wren the archivist had begged them to keep, the map that led down to the thing the whole crown was hunting the realm to find.

The Ember slept beneath the mountains. And now the mountains were coming for Lena whether they walked or stayed.

They pulled their hood lower, turned from the square, and started toward the harbor road—toward Doss's boat, toward the pass, toward anything that was not here—while behind them the town bell rang three-and-one, three-and-one, counting down the week.
