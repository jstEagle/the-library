## 2. The Smuggler's Toll

The knock came at the hour when Grayharbor's lamps gutter low and honest folk pretend not to hear anything at all.

Lena knew the rhythm of it before they knew the meaning—three raps, a pause, two more. Doss's knock. The one he'd used since they were children stealing plums off Magister Corvel's tree, the one that meant *open the back door, I've got something to show you, don't ask how I got it.* Lena's hand was already on the latch before their mind caught up with their body, and then, in the space of a heartbeat, caught up and pulled them back.

Because Doss never came at this hour. Doss came at dawn smelling of brine and cheap tobacco, or at dusk whistling something tuneless off the tide. Doss did not come in the black belly of night, and he did not send three raps and a pause and two more unless—

Unless someone had taught his hands the rhythm.

Lena stood in the dark of their own hallway, bare feet cold on the boards, and did not open the door. Outside, boots shifted on wet cobblestone. More than one pair. The lamp above the door hissed in the drizzle, and beneath its small circle of light Lena saw the shadow of a man who was not shaped like Doss—too broad through the shoulders, too still, the way soldiers are still.

They backed away, one silent step at a time, heart hammering against their ribs like something caged. Through the window over the sink, past the crooked chimneys of Tanner's Row, they could see the harbor square, and in the harbor square, torches.

Torches, at midnight, in the rain.

* * *

Grayharbor had burned things before. Lena had seen it from a distance—a cartload of smuggled silk, once, and a ship too rotten to save, pushed out and set alight as a warning to the sea. Fire in Grayharbor was practical. Fire was for cargo.

This fire was not for cargo.

They heard the criers before they reached the edge of the square, though "heard" was a generous word for what the crown's men did; they bellowed, voices raw with official importance, drowning under the drumming rain and not caring. *"By order of the Regent's Justice! A trafficker in forbidden arts is taken! Let all who harbor the ember-gifted witness!"*

Lena pressed into the crowd at the square's rim, hood up, collar high, one shoulder against the wet stone of the customs house. Around them the town had turned out—not because it wanted to, but because towns always do. Fishwives with shawls over their hair. Dockhands still smelling of pitch. Children hoisted onto fathers' shoulders so they wouldn't miss, because missing it would be worse than seeing it, everyone understood that without saying.

In the center of the square, ringed by witch-hunters in their ash-grey cloaks, stood a scaffold of green timber, and tied to the timber, soaked to the skin and bleeding from one eyebrow, was Doss.

He looked smaller than Lena had ever seen him. That was the thing no one warned you about—that violence shrinks people. Doss, who could fill a room with his laugh, who once talked his way past a customs officer by selling the man his own hat back to him, hung against the wood like a coat someone had forgotten. His head lolged, then lifted. He found the crowd the way a drowning man finds the surface.

And then he found Lena.

It shouldn't have been possible, thirty yards through rain and torchlight and a hundred faces. But Doss had spent twenty years reading harbors and back doors and the exact weight of a purse, and he read the crowd the way he read everything, and his eyes stopped on the customs house corner and did not move.

His mouth moved. No sound carried. But Lena had known him long enough to read lips across a crowded tavern, across a hold full of contraband, across the whole width of their shared childhood.

*Run.*

Lena did not run. That was the first sin of the night, and they would carry it. They stood rooted to the wet stone while a witch-hunter in a steel half-mask stepped forward with a brand taken from a brazier, and the crowd made its sound—the low animal moan crowds make when they want to look away and cannot—and the brand went into the kindling stacked around Doss's ankles.

The flame caught. The rain tried to kill it and failed.

And Lena felt it.

That was the cruelty of it, the precision. The gift does not live in the mind; it lives lower, in the blood, in the marrow, in the place where your pulse keeps time. And fire speaks to that place. Every gifted soul within a mile feels a flame as a tug behind the navel, a warmth in the wrist, an answering flicker under the skin—the ember recognizing its cousin. It cannot be ignored. It cannot be refused. Wren had told her once—no. Not Wren. That memory belonged to a different life, a different name, a story Lena kept locked even from themselves. Someone had told them, long ago: *the gifted cannot help but feel the flame.*

So the witch-hunters built their fires and watched the crowd, and any man or woman whose face changed, whose breath caught, whose hand curled toward the heat—they marked them. That was why Doss burned in the square instead of a yard behind the garrison. He was not the sentence. He was the question, and every gifted soul in Grayharbor was being asked to answer with their own face.

Lena bit down until they tasted copper and made their face a map of nothing. They thought of ink. Of contour lines. Of the boring, endless labor of hatching coastline, stroke after stroke after stroke, until the world narrowed to the point of a pen and there was nothing in them but craft.

It almost worked.

But grief is also a fire, and it burns in the same place, and Doss looked at them through the smoke and mouthed *run* again, and then—he smiled. That was the unbearable part. He smiled the way he'd smiled when Lena showed him their first true map at fourteen years old, drawn in a stolen hour on the back of a chart, the passes through Thornfell rendered in lines no ungifted hand could hold steady. He'd gone quiet, which Doss never did, and then he'd said, *You keep that hidden, little cartographer. Some things the world isn't ready to carry. But I'll carry it with you if you need.*

He had carried it. For eleven years he had carried it—never speaking of it, never letting his eyes linger too long on the false maps Lena sold and the true ones they hid, standing between Lena and every suspicion like a wall built of jokes and smuggling routes and stubborn love.

And now the crown was burning him as bait for a secret that was not his.

The guilt arrived all at once, whole, like a wave that has traveled a long way underwater before it breaks. *He died protecting my secret,* Lena thought, and the thought had teeth. *No. He died protecting me. The secret just happened to be the shape of the knife.*

The smoke thickened. Somewhere in the crowd a woman began to sob, and a witch-hunter's masked head turned toward the sound, slow and interested, and Lena watched the woman's neighbor—an old dockhand with salt-white whiskers—put his arm around her and shake his shoulders with loud, drunken laughter until the mask turned away. Small mercies. Small, desperate choreographies of ordinary people keeping each other alive.

Doss stopped struggling somewhere in the second minute. Lena counted without wanting to. In the third minute the rain won, or the fire tired, and the light went down to embers, and the embers were the worst part, because embers are what Lena's gift was named for, and they glowed in the dark square like a thousand tiny eyes.

When it was done, the witch-hunters did not leave. That was the message inside the message. They posted men at the square's corners and began moving through the thinning crowd with lanterns and lists, asking names, asking questions, asking *where were you when the trafficker burned, and did you feel anything.*

Lena turned and walked away at the pace of a person going home to bed. Not fast. Never fast. Fast is a confession. They walked through the drizzle past shuttered windows, past the garrison where more grey cloaks were gathering, past the chandler's shop where Doss had once bought them a candle carved like a ship—"so you can find your way home even when you're lost"—and they did not weep until they were three streets gone, and then only for the length of one alley, one hand against wet brick, teeth clenched so hard their jaw ached for days after.

Then they went home, and they did not go to bed.

* * *

The floorboards came up easy; Doss had helped loosen them years ago, grinning, saying every honest house needs one dishonest floor. Beneath, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed leather, lay the true work of Lena Reyes's life: forty-one maps, each one impossible, each one drawn in lines that only a gifted hand could produce—the passes through Thornfell that appeared and vanished with the seasons, the ways beneath the mountains, the old court roads that predated every kingdom now claiming them.

Forty-one reasons to burn.

Lena knelt among them in the dark and understood, with terrible clarity, what Doss's death had purchased. Not safety. Attention. The witch-hunters had not caught him carrying contraband; they had caught him carrying *something*, and whatever it was had been enough to put him in the square instead of a cell. Which meant they would search his boat, his lodgings, his debts, his friendships. Which meant, by morning or the morning after, they would come knocking at the house on Tanner's Row with three raps and a pause and two more.

They took everything. Maps, pens, the good knives, the little tin of Wren's-old-ink that they'd never let themselves think about. They left the furniture. Furniture was innocent.

Last of all, from a hiding place inside the chimney breast, they took the page they'd finished burning just yesterday—or meant to finish. One sheet of the true maps, the one showing the deep roads beneath Thornfell's western root, half-consumed, its surviving lines still faintly warm to a gifted touch. Lena had lit it the day the criers came, had watched the edges curl, and had faltered—because you do not burn eleven years of your own hand lightly, and because some traitor part of them had whispered *not yet, maybe it will pass, maybe they'll tire.*

Doss had not faltered. Doss had stood in the rain and burned slowly so that no one else would have to.

Lena held the half-burned page over the candle and watched the last lines of the deep road curl and blacken and die. Then they pinched out the flame, wrapped the ashes with the rest, shouldered the pack, and wrote—in ordinary ink, on ordinary paper, in their ordinary hand—a note for whoever searched next: *Gone to my sister in Marrow Bay. Debts unpaid, forgive me.* A coward's note. A human note. Nothing a witch-hunter would look twice at.

At the back door, they paused. On the shelf sat the candle shaped like a ship, wick unburned, waiting. Lena took it. Some things you carry.

Outside, the rain had thinned to mist, and the mountains stood up black against the lesser dark of the sky, east and north, vast and patient. Thornfell. Everyone in Grayharbor lived their whole lives under that horizon and pretended it was scenery. Lena had spent eleven years drawing its hidden roads and telling themselves the same lie.

They looked at the mountains the way you look at a door you've been afraid of your whole life.

"I'm coming," they said aloud, to no one, to Doss, to the dark. Their voice cracked on the second word, and they let it. "You carried it long enough."

Then Lena Reyes walked north out of Grayharbor, past the last lamp, past the edge of every map they'd ever sold, into the country that had no honest roads—and behind them, far off, a bell began to toll, and did not stop.
