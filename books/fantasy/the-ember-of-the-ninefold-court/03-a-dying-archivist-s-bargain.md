## 3. A Dying Archivist's Bargain

The mountains did not want Lena.

Thornfell made that plain from the first switchback above Grayharbor, where the smugglers' path narrowed to a shelf of wet granite and the wind came off the high snowfields with the smell of old iron. Lena climbed anyway, pack straps biting into shoulders still stiff from the night's flight, and told themselves the ache in their chest was only the altitude. It was not. It was Doss's face in the fire, mouth open on a word they would never hear finished.

Three days up, and the world below had shrunk to a gray smear of smoke where a town used to be. The crown's criers would have moved on by now. The witch-hunters never stayed long where the burning was done; ash kept no secrets worth interrogating. But Lena kept climbing, because going back was unthinkable and standing still was death, and because of the thing Wren had pressed into their hands at the archive door—half a map, drawn in something darker than ink, wrapped in oilcloth and whispered over like a prayer.

*Find me if I fall,* the archivist had said, coughing blood into her sleeve. *The mountain keeps me.*

Lena had thought it madness then. Now, alone on a ledge with night coming down like a lid, madness seemed the only honest company left.

They made camp in the mouth of a cave whose walls sweated mineral light—veins of quartz catching the last sun so the stone glowed faintly green, like the inside of a bottle. Lena ate hardtack and dried plums and unrolled the half-map by quartz-light, careful as handling a wound.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Whoever had drawn it—and Lena knew archivist hands when they saw them, knew the ruled precision of borders and the loving curl of river-names—had worked in a medium that shimmered. The lines were not brown or black but a deep ember-red, and when Lena tilted the parchment they *moved*, slow as honey, crawling along their courses like living things. Blood. Drawn blood, gifted blood, laid down in lines the way Lena laid down charcoal: a map that could only be made by one of the ember-gifted, and could only be read—

Lena stopped tilting the parchment.

Because for one heartbeat, the lines had *answered* them. The rivers had brightened. The mountain's spine had pulsed once, faintly, like a heart under a blanket.

Their own hands were shaking. They looked at them—ink-stained, charcoal-blackened, ordinary-looking hands that had spent twenty-six years pretending to be exactly what they appeared. Hands that drew true maps no ungifted cartographer could draw, routes through Thornfell that bent where the mountain bent, that remembered paths no surveyor had ever walked. Hands that had burned every such page the moment it dried, all except the ones hidden under the floorboards in Grayharbor, which were ash now too, most likely, or evidence.

*I'm sorry, Doss.* The thought arrived whole, unbidden. *You died for a secret that wasn't even mine to keep.*

Sleep came thin and full of falling. In the morning there was smoke—not the black reek of a pyre but woodsmoke, domestic, threaded with something herbal and bitter. A thread of it rising from a fold of the mountain two ridges east, from a place no map Lena had ever drawn showed habitation.

But the half-map did.

Lena stared at it a long time. Then they folded it away, shouldered the pack, and went to find the woman who had said *the mountain keeps me*—because either she was alive, or whatever had killed her was worth knowing about.

* * *

The fold hid a valley no wider than a thrown rope, walled in scree and hung with pines bent sideways by centuries of wind. At its head stood a ruin wearing the disguise of a shepherd's hut: the proportions were wrong, the doorway too tall, and set into the lintel a stone carved with nine interlocking rings, worn nearly smooth.

The Ninefold Court. Even in ruin, even reduced to a lintel, the sigil made Lena's skin prickle—the mark that had been burned into Doss's chest before the fire, the mark the criers spat when they read the hunt-proclamation. *By order of the Crown: any harboring the gifted, any bearing the sigil of the old tyranny—*

The door opened before Lena knocked.

The woman in it was a skeleton upholstered in skin, gray-haired, one arm bound in filthy bandages gone rust-brown at the elbow. She held a knife with the weary competence of someone who had been holding it for days. Her eyes went to Lena's face, then to the pack, then—sharpening—to Lena's hands.

"You came," she said. Her voice was gravel in a paper bag. "I told you to come, didn't I. Before the end. Sit down before you fall down, cartographer. You look like your own worst survey."

"You're Wren."

"I'm what's left of her." She stepped aside. "Come in. And don't touch anything. Everything in here is older than your country."

* * *

Inside, the hut lied about itself. The single room was deep as a chapel, shelves climbing into shadow, and every shelf was books—real ones, leather and vellum, spines stamped with the ninefold ring. A brazier burned low. The air smelled of cedar oil and blood and, beneath both, something mineral and warm, like a forge banked for the night.

Wren lowered herself onto a stool with the care of a woman negotiating with her own body, and Lena saw then how bad the arm was. Not a wound. A *wound*. Something had burned her from the inside out, the flesh around the elbow charred in a pattern almost like a handprint.

"Raiders?" Lena asked, though they knew.

"Crown men. Witch-hunters." Wren smiled without humor. "They found the archive vault at Cael Morrow three weeks ago. I was the only one who ran fast enough and knew the roads well enough—which is to say, I was the only one who'd ever mapped an escape route for myself. An archivist's vanity. It saved my life and cost me everything else." She nodded at Lena's pack. "You brought it?"

Lena hesitated. Every instinct honed over a lifetime of hiding said *give this woman nothing, trust no one, the last person who trusted for you is dead.* But Doss was dead anyway. Hiding had not saved him. Perhaps nothing saved anyone, and the only choice left was what to be carrying when it happened.

They unwrapped the half-map and laid it on the table between them.

Wren looked at it the way the devout look at relics. Her good hand hovered above it without touching. "Yes," she breathed. "Yes. Half of it. She got half of it done."

"*She?*"

"Maerin Vos. Last cartographer of the Ninefold Court. Last ember-gifted mapmaker in the world—" Wren's eyes flicked up, sharp and terrible, "—until now. Don't bother denying it. I watched your face when you unrolled it. The lines moved for you. They've never moved for me, and I've had this parchment against my skin for thirty years trying."

Lena sat down slowly on a crate of vellum. "What is it a map *of*?"

"The way down." Wren leaned forward, and the brazier light caught the fever-sweat on her face. "Beneath Thornfell. Beneath everything. When the court fell—when your criers' grandfathers pulled it down and called it liberation—they couldn't destroy what made the court the court. So they buried it. Sealed it behind doors that only open to a map drawn in gifted blood, because the founders were clever enough to know that the gift would be hunted to nothing, and a hunted thing makes a fine lock." She tapped the parchment, once, beside the pulsing red spine of the mountain. "The Ember sleeps under Thornfell's heart, cartographer. Has slept for two hundred years. And this is the only key."

The word landed in Lena's chest like a dropped stone. *Ember.* The gift was called that in whispers—in Doss's voice, laughing over stolen wine: *they say the first of us lit our candles off the Ember itself, that's why we can't help flinching when things burn.* Lena had always taken it for metaphor. Metaphors did not sleep under mountains with maps for keys.

"Why give it to me?" Their voice came out rougher than intended. "You don't know me. I could be anyone. I could sell it."

"To whom?" Wren laughed, and coughed, and the cough went on too long. "The crown already knows it exists—that's why the hunts have started again, why they're burning smugglers in squares. They're not fishing for the gifted, child. They're fishing for the *key*. Someone talked in Cael Morrow before the raiders came, and now every hunter in the realm is looking for a mapmaker who draws too well." She fixed Lena with her fever-bright eyes. "I don't know you. But I know your work. I've seen three of your commissions pass through private collections—routes through Thornfell that bend where the mountain bends. I've known what you were for two years. I wrote to you twice. You never answered."

"I burn letters."

"I know. I'd have burned mine too." Wren's smile flickered and failed. "That's why it has to be you. Not because you're good—you might be monstrous, for all I can tell—but because you're the only living soul the lock will recognize. Maerin's line ended with her hands. Yours are the same hands, near enough. The map won't finish for anyone else." She pushed back from the table, and her breath caught, and Lena saw the blood seeping fresh through the bandage. Whatever burned her was still burning.

"You're dying," Lena said.

"I'm *spending*," Wren corrected. "There's a difference, and it matters to me." She dragged a blank sheet of heavy vellum across the table, and a knife—a thin, curved thing meant for trimming quills. "Maerin drew her half in her own blood and it nearly killed her; she told me so in the letter I keep under my tongue. The second half wants the same price. I can't pay it. My blood's just blood now." She held out the knife, handle first, and her hand was steady in the way of people who have passed the last place where trembling helps. "Finish it. Here, tonight, while I can still tell you what the lines mean. After that—" she gestured at the shelves, the ruined court, the whole buried century, "—after that it's yours. All of it. I'm sorry. I know what I'm handing you. I carried it thirty years and it broke me in the end. But the alternative is letting the crown dig it out with hunters' hands, and I would rather burn this whole valley and myself with it."

Lena looked at the knife. At the blank vellum waiting like an open grave. At the half-map, its red rivers crawling, patient as roots.

Everything in them that had survived twenty-six years of hiding screamed to stand up, walk out, climb higher, hide deeper. Hide until the world forgot embers entirely.

But Doss had burned in a square while a crowd sang hymns, and he had burned *smiling at Lena's window*, and hiding had bought him exactly nothing.

"What happens," Lena asked quietly, "if I finish it and open the door?"

Wren's face did something complicated—grief and awe and fear sharing the same small space. "Then you find out what everyone who ever served the court died wondering," she said. "Whether the thing under the mountain is a crown... or a curse... or just a lamp that somebody, someday, will have the courage to carry properly." She pressed the knife into Lena's palm and closed their fingers over it, her grip cold as the quartz caves. "Cut. While I'm still here to teach you the names of the rivers."

Outside, the wind turned, and far below—so far it might have been imagination—Lena thought they heard horns. Many horns. Coming up the mountain road.

Wren heard them too. Her eyes went to the window, then back to Lena's hands, and she smiled like a woman placing her last bet.

"Well," she said. "Best hurry."
