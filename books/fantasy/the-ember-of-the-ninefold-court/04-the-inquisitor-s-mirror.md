## 4. The Inquisitor's Mirror

The fire had been out for two days, but Grayharbor still smelled of it.

Vael Sarn stood in the square where Doss the smuggler had burned, and let the stink of char settle into his lungs like a confession he could not speak. Rain had come in the night—a thin, apologetic rain that turned the ash to gray paste and streaked the scaffold's blackened posts with lines like weeping. The townsfolk moved around him the way water moves around a stone: carefully, without looking, pretending he was not there.

He was very much there. He was always there. That was the whole of his purpose.

"Ser Inquisitor." The town reeve approached with his hat wrung soft in his fists. A small man, gray as the weather, with the particular stoop of someone who had spent his life bowing to whatever stood nearest. "The survivors are gathered in the tithe-house, as you asked. Though I'll say again—no one here wanted that man dead. He was a smuggler, yes, but—"

"No one ever wants them dead," Sarn said mildly. "That is what makes the burning necessary."

He walked on before the reeve could decide whether to be offended or relieved. Behind him, the square held its silence. Sarn had learned long ago that squares remembered nothing and everything: they forgot the screaming within a season, but the stones kept the shape of the crowd forever, worn faintly into their surfaces by all the feet that had gathered to watch.

His left hand ached.

It always ached when he was near a pyre. Not the hand itself—he had broken it twice in boyhood and once in service, and it had healed crooked and strong—but something deeper in it, in the old scar that ran from wrist to knuckle like a seam of buried wire. The ache was familiar now, almost companionable. It was the sound of a door held shut against a wind.

He flexed the fingers once, inside his glove, and went to question the survivors.

* * *

The tithe-house smelled of tallow and wet wool and fear, which was, in Sarn's experience, the truest incense any room could hold. Eleven people sat on benches beneath the eyes of the Ninefold Court's painted sigil—the nine interlocked flames, gold leaf flaking from the walls like dying embers themselves. They had arranged themselves the way frightened people always did: the guilty at the edges, the grieving in the middle, the liars closest to the door.

Sarn did not sit. Sitting invited conversation; standing invited confession.

"I will be brief," he said, "because you have suffered enough, and because brevity is a mercy I am one of the few men in this realm still permitted to give." He let his gaze travel the benches slowly. "Doss Merren was burned because he sheltered the ember-gifted. Perhaps knowingly. Perhaps not. Someone in this room knows which."

Silence, except for a child sniffling somewhere near the back.

"You mistake my purpose if you think I want names out of cruelty," Sarn continued, and this was even true, which made it worse. "The gifted are not criminals. They are unexploded shells. A gift that sleeps can wake. A gift that wakes can burn a granary, a garrison, a town. I am not hunting monsters. I am preventing fires." He paused. "You watched a man burn two days ago. Tell me you wish to see it again."

An old woman began to cry. A fisherman stared at his hands. And near the window, a girl of perhaps fifteen sat very straight, her jaw set, her eyes fixed on Sarn's face with an expression he recognized because he had worn it himself at her age: hatred so tightly controlled it looked like calm.

She would bear watching. But she was not what he had come for.

"Show me the dead man's things," he said instead.

* * *

They kept Doss's confiscated goods in the tithe-house cellar, in oilcloth bundles that smelled of tar and river mud. Sarn worked through them alone, by lamplight, with the patience of a man who had spent twenty years learning that guilt never announces itself—it hides in ledgers, in letters, in the margins of other people's lives.

Smuggling manifests. Salt brandy. Court silver stamped with a reign forty years dead. Nothing unusual. Nothing—

Then he found the maps.

There were seven of them, folded into false bottoms of cargo crates, drawn on good rag paper. Coastlines, passes, river fords. Competent work. And then the eighth map, tucked inside a hollowed prayer book, and Sarn stopped breathing for a moment without meaning to.

It was a chart of the Thornfell approaches—and it was wrong. Wrong the way a lie is wrong when the liar is very, very good. The passes marked were plausible. The elevations were consistent. The rivers flowed in sensible directions. Any cartographer in the crown's employ would have accepted it, filed it, used it.

But Sarn had grown up in the hill country above Cinder Vale, where the mountains did not behave, and he knew Thornfell's true shape the way a man knows his mother's face. This map showed him a route through the range that did not exist. Could not exist. Unless—

Unless the hand that drew it had felt the stone.

He spread the paper flat under the lamp and looked closer, and there it was, so faint he might have imagined it: a tremor in the contour lines. Not error. *Emphasis.* Certain ridgelines were inked with a pressure that varied, minute and rhythmic, like a pulse. Like the draughtsman's heartbeat had traveled down their arm and into the pen, and the pen had been listening to something under the mountain.

A gifted hand. An ember-gift, subtle as banked coals—perception, maybe, or communion with stone. Rare. Precious. Dangerous beyond measure, because a map like this was not a document. It was a key.

And keys, in Sarn's long experience, were always being sought by more than one lock.

"The cartographer," he said to the reeve, who had crept down the stairs with a candle and the expression of a man hoping not to be noticed existing. "Who drew these?"

"We—we don't know, ser. The smuggler kept his sources close."

"Someone commissioned maps. Someone paid for them."

"There's a... there's a mapwright up on Lantern Row. Reyes. Keeps to themself, does fine work, everyone says. Quiet." The reeve hesitated. "But they'd have gone, ser. Soon as the burning happened. Folk like that smell trouble coming."

Folk like that. Sarn almost smiled. Almost.

"Describe them."

"Smallish. Dark-haired. Careful, if you take my meaning—never says more than needed. Good eyes, though. The kind that seem to be measuring everything." The reeve shrugged helplessly. "Been here six, seven years. Kept a lodger once, a scholar. Old woman. Died last winter, they said."

Something cold slid down Sarn's spine and settled, patient as frost, at the base of it.

*A scholar. Dead last winter.* Or fled. Or hidden. Wren—the name surfaced from twenty years of memorized files, the court archivist who had vanished with half the Ember records the night the old library burned. If Wren had been living above a gifted mapwright in Grayharbor, then the smuggler, the archivist, and the cartographer were not three threads but one rope, and somewhere along that rope, the Ember waited at the bottom of Thornfell like a stone at the bottom of a well.

"Reyes," Sarn said, tasting the name. "Lena Reyes."

"You know them, ser?"

"I know their work." He rolled the false map closed with great care. "We are, in a sense, colleagues. We both spend our lives drawing the world as it truly is, and both of us must hide what we know from people who would punish us for it."

The reeve blinked, uncomprehending. Good. Comprehension was dangerous.

* * *

That night, in the tithe-house loft, Sarn took off his gloves and set his bare left hand flat on the table, palm down, the way he did every night, the way he had done since he was nine years old and a traveling friar had gripped his wrist and said, *boy, you're full of light, and light draws arrows.*

The ache in the scar was louder tonight. Near the pyre, near the hunt, near the memory of flame, the door in him strained at its hinges. He could feel it on the other side—not power, exactly, not anymore, just the ghost of it. A flicker. When he was a child in Cinder Vale he had lit candles by wanting them lit, had warmed his mother's hands in winter, had once, in terror, made a hearth roar hot enough to crack its stones the day the first witch-hunters came up the valley road. After that: suppression. Cold baths. Prayer. The friar's iron bracelet, worn until the skin beneath went white and dead. Twenty years of holding a door shut until the thing behind it forgot it was a door.

He had become the crown's best inquisitor precisely because he understood the gifted better than they understood themselves. He knew every trick of concealment because he had invented half of them. He knew how a gift felt when it hid—that low hum beneath the skin, that carefulness in the hands. He could walk into a room and find the gifted the way a blind man finds a fire: by warmth.

Which meant he knew, with certainty, that Lena Reyes had felt Doss burning.

That was the cruelty of the pyres, and Sarn had never pretended otherwise to himself, though he lied about it to everyone else. The gifted could no more ignore a death-fire than a mother could ignore her child crying. The crown called the burnings bait because that was what they were. Somewhere in the hills above Grayharbor, right now, a careful person with measuring eyes had felt an old friend die in the flames, and grief would do what Sarn's soldiers could not: it would make them run, and running leaves tracks, and tracks can be read like maps.

He opened his satchel and laid out his instruments. Ink. Calipers. His own collection of Reyes's work—three commissions traced over the years, each one flawless, each one hiding its pulse in the contour lines. He had admired them before he suspected them. He suspected them now, and admired them still, and the admiration sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

*You and I,* he thought toward the dark hills, toward the person fleeing through them, *we are the same animal. You draw maps to keep people alive. I draw lists. You hide your gift in linework; I hide mine in procedure. You watched your friend burn and ran. I watched my valley burn and stayed, and put on the crown's gray, and became the fire.*

He pressed his scarred palm harder against the table until the ache sharpened into pain, and pain was useful, pain was a nail driven through the door.

In the morning he would ride for Thornfell with twelve picked men and the false map in his breast pocket, following a dead smuggler's routes toward a living fugitive. He would find Reyes. He would offer mercy first—he always offered mercy first, and meant it, in the way that a knife means it before it is used.

And if, in some chamber under the mountain, the Ember woke and looked at him with its old golden attention—if it saw the flicker behind his ribs, the door straining on its hinges, the boy in Cinder Vale who had only ever wanted to warm his mother's hands—then Vael Sarn did not know, could not know, which side of the door he would finally be standing on when it broke open.

He slept badly. In his dream, the mountains were drawn in ink that pulsed like a heartbeat, and a voice that sounded terribly like his own said: *follow the line, follow the line, it leads home.*

At dawn, it rained again. He rode east, into the teeth of Thornfell, and the road ahead was empty—which meant, he told his sergeant, that they were exactly one day behind.
