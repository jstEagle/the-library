## 5. Roads Not on Any Map

The smugglers' roads did not exist, and Lena had drawn every one of them.

That was the joke they'd never told anyone: the finest cartographer in Grayharbor made a living mapping roads that were not roads—goat tracks widened by boot leather, dry creek beds that ran only when the snowmelt argued with the mountain, a seam of limestone where the rock itself had been pried apart by three generations of men who'd wanted to move things the crown said could not be moved. On Lena's official maps, commissioned by harbormasters and merchant guilds, those routes appeared as nothing. A hatching of cliff. A note: *impassable.*

In their true maps—the ones that lived folded against their ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, drawn in ink mixed with a single drop of their own blood so that only a gifted eye could see the lines at all—those same routes blazed like veins.

Lena walked them now for the third day, and the mountains had begun to forgive them. That was how it felt. Thornfell did not welcome; it *tolerated*, grudgingly, the way an old dog tolerates a stranger who feeds it. The pines closed overhead and cut the sky into coins of gray light. The air smelled of resin and cold stone and, faintly, of woodsmoke from a fire someone had built days ago in this same hollow—which meant other people used these roads, which meant the crown's hunters might too.

Behind them, Kess kept pace without a sound. The child moved the way water moves, finding the low places, pouring around obstacles. They hadn't spoken much since the waystation below Grayharbor, since Lena had found them crouched in the ruins of a burned farmhouse with a crown-issue shortbow across their knees and a brand on their wrist shaped like a nine-pointed star gone wrong.

"I didn't do it," Kess had said, before Lena even spoke. "The thing they say the gifted do. I didn't hurt anybody."

"I know," Lena had said, and meant it, and had no idea why the child believed them.

Now, on the third morning, Kess finally asked the question Lena had been dreading.

"Is it true what Wren told you? About the Ember?"

Lena stopped. Ahead, the trail bent around a shoulder of granite furred with moss, and beyond it the land dropped away into a valley full of cloud. Somewhere down there, a hawk cried once and gave up.

"Where did you hear that name?"

"The archivist. Before she—" Kess's hands did something complicated in the air, a gesture that wasn't quite a shrug. "I was hiding in her cellar two nights before you came. She talked to me. I think she thought I was already dead."

Lena looked at the child—at the sharp wrists, the cropped hair grown out unevenly where someone had sheared it with a knife, the eyes that watched everything the way soldiers watch, which is to say the way prey watches—and felt the old guilt shift its weight again. Doss dead in the square. Wren dead in the dark. Everyone who touched this secret ended up ash or earth, and here was a ten-year-old asking to touch it too.

"She told you about the Ember," Lena said carefully, "and you still followed me."

"You're going to it." Not a question. "I want to see it before they burn me too. If I'm going to burn, I want it to be for something."

The words landed like stones in still water. Lena turned back to the trail because there was nothing their face could safely do.

"We're not going to burn," they said. "Any of us."

Kess said nothing. But that night, when they made camp in the lee of a fallen fir, the child sat closer to the fire than the night before.

* * *

They met Brannoc on the fourth day, and he nearly killed them all.

It happened fast, the way violence does—one moment Lena was leading them through a defile where the rock walls pressed close enough to touch both sides, and the next a man stepped out of the shadow between two boulders with a sword already moving, and the world contracted to the geometry of survival. Lena threw themselves sideways. Kess dropped flat, rolled, came up with an arrow nocked—a child's body executing a soldier's reflexes, and something in Lena broke a little at the sight of it.

"Hold!" The man's voice cracked like a whip. And then, as his gaze found Kess—found the bow, the stance, the brand half-hidden under a ragged sleeve—his sword point wavered. "Ninefold preserve me. They're putting *children* in the hunt now?"

"They put children in everything," Kess said, flat as slate. "You're wearing court steel under that cloak. I can see the pauldron."

The man went very still. Then he laughed—not a pleasant sound, more like something tearing—and pushed back his hood. He was fifty, maybe older, gray going to white at the temples, with a face like a cliff that had taken weather. The brand of the Ninefold Court was inked on his jaw, faded almost to nothing: the mark of a knight sworn and unsworn.

"Brannoc," he said. "Formerly Knight-Captain of the Ninth Vigil. Currently a bandit, if you ask the crown. Which you shouldn't, because the crown lies."

"Lena," Lena said, after a pause long enough to be honest. "Cartographer."

Something flickered in the knight's eyes. "Reyes. The mapmaker." He sheathed the sword with a click that sounded final. "Sarn has your hand. Did you know that? He keeps sketches of your work pinned inside his coat like love letters. He showed them to my last crew before he hanged them. 'Find me the person who draws like this,' he says. 'Find me the hand.'"

Lena's skin went cold despite the fire they built that night. Sarn. The name had been following them for days like a wolf pacing just outside the firelight. The Inquisitor who had stood in Grayharbor's square with Doss's ashes still drifting past his face, whose expression had been neither cruel nor kind but *attentive*, the attention of a man reading a difficult text.

"Why would he want me?" Lena asked, though some cold part of them already knew.

Brannoc fed a stick to the flames and watched it catch. "Because your maps are the only ones that reach the deep roads. Because the Ember is waking—I can feel it, can't you? Like a toothache behind the world. And because—" he glanced up, and his eyes held something that might have been pity, "—because a man who hunts a thing usually knows its shape from the inside. Take that however you like."

Kess, curled against a root with the bow across their knees like a talisman, said: "He means Sarn's gifted too. Everybody knows. Nobody says."

"Kess—"

"It's true." The child's voice didn't rise. It never rose; that was the worst part about it. "You can smell it on him. Suppressed gift smells like snuffed candles. The whole company smelled it. Nobody said, because saying gets you burned."

Silence took the camp. The fire popped. High above, through the broken canopy, a star moved—or seemed to—and Lena thought about a boy somewhere in the crown's ranks, decades ago, learning to fold his own light down small and small and smaller until it fit inside a man-shaped box, and then building his life on top of the lid.

"He'll break eventually," Brannoc said quietly. "A gift suppressed that long doesn't die. It ferments. When it comes up, it comes up wrong. I've seen it happen." He looked into the fire. "I helped bury the Ember, after the Court fell. I know what power does when it's kept in the dark too long. It doesn't sleep. It waits."

"And you?" Lena asked. "What do you want out of this?"

The knight was quiet for a long time. When he answered, he addressed the fire rather than either of them.

"Absolution is not on offer. I know that. I buried the Ember and I buried the Court's sins along with it, and neither stayed buried. What I want—" his jaw worked, "—is to be standing in the right place when it ends. Whatever 'it' turns out to be. I've spent twenty years standing in the wrong ones."

Lena studied him: the faded brand, the sword worn smooth where a thumb had worried the grip for years, the careful way he'd placed himself between the fire and the darker treeline without appearing to have done it at all. Old habits of guardianship, surviving the man's disgrace like bones surviving the body.

"All right," Lena said.

Brannoc raised an eyebrow.

"You heard me. You walk with us. But we vote on the hard choices—all three of us. And nobody dies for my secret. I've had enough of people burning for things I never gave them leave to carry."

The knight considered this with the gravity of a man reviewing terms of surrender. Then he nodded once, slow, and something in the set of his shoulders eased by a degree no one else would have measured.

"Then we should move at first light," he said. "There's a waystation ahead—old Court road, one of the nine. The Court of Cinders, my men used to call it, back when my men existed. It's warded. The wards are old, but old is sometimes better than strong."

"How far?" Kess asked.

"A day and a half. Less, if the mountain's kind." Brannoc smiled without humor. "Thornfell is never kind. But it remembers who served it, and I served it longer than I've served anything."

* * *

They walked, and the walking became a language none of them had chosen to learn but all of them spoke fluently within the week: who scouted, who slept, who carried the heavier pack without being asked. Kess began to talk—not much, but in fragments, offered like coins found on a road. A training yard in the eastern garrisons. An officer who noticed the child's shadows moved wrong when they were frightened, and instead of reporting it, taught Kess to hide it better, because the officer had a sister who'd burned. A night flight through the garrison palisade with nothing but a bow and a stolen loaf.

"They'll list me as a deserter," Kess said one evening, watching Lena sketch the day's route by firelight. "Deserters get the rope."

"Not anymore," Lena said. "Not if I have anything to draw about it."

And Kess—Kess smiled. It was a small, crooked, unpracticed thing, a smile that had clearly not been used in years and had rusted in the interval, but it was real, and it transformed the child's face into what it should have been all along: a kid's face.

Lena felt the sight of it lodge somewhere beneath their ribs, next to the folded maps and the drop of blood-ink and the weight of Wren's dying words. *Found things*, they thought. *Found family. Found in the dark, of all places.*

On the ninth night, camped beneath an overhang that hummed faintly with residual warding, Lena woke to find Brannoc sitting watch, and sat up beside him without a word. For a while neither spoke. The mountain breathed around them.

"You draw maps," Brannoc said at last. "But you've never once drawn yourself into one. I've watched you work. Every road, every river, every hidden thing—and the cartographer stays off the page."

"That's how maps work."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "That's how hiding works. Maps work when the mapmaker trusts the reader." He glanced over, firelight moving in the seams of his face. "Whoever you're saving that page for, Reyes—they'd best deserve you."

Lena didn't answer. But later, alone with the true maps, they unrolled the half-finished blood-chart Wren had died holding, and looked a long time at the blank space at its heart where the final route should go—the line only a gifted hand could complete, the line that led beneath Thornfell to the sleeping Ember.

Then, in the margin, small and tentative, they drew a figure: three travelers on a road that wasn't on any map. A tall one. A small one. One carrying a sword.

It was the first thing Lena had ever put of themselves onto a page.

They slept, after that, deeper than they had in weeks—and woke to Kess shaking their shoulder, eyes wide, pointing north through the trees.

Smoke. Thin, deliberate, signal-smoke, rising from the direction of the Court of Cinders.

Someone knew about the waystation. Someone was already there.
