## 6. The Court of Cinders

Kess had learned to count in the crown's barracks. Count your steps, count your lashes, count the days since you last bled from training with wooden staves. Numbers were safe. Numbers didn't burn.

So Kess counted now, walking behind Lena and Brannoc up a switchback trail that wasn't on any map—Lena's maps or anyone else's. *Four hundred and eleven steps since the last stream. Three crows. One moon, half-eaten.* The counting kept the other thing quiet, the thing under Kess's skin that woke at night and pressed outward like a fist against a door.

"You're limping," Brannoc said without turning around. He always knew. He was the kind of man who noticed everything and said almost none of it.

"I'm not."

"Then walk straighter."

Kess straightened, jaw tight. The cut on their heel from yesterday's scree had opened again, but admitting it meant slowing everyone down, and slowing down meant being left, and being left meant—

*Count. Four hundred twelve.*

Lena glanced back, and their eyes did that thing they did—seeing too much, the way mapmakers saw coastlines in a person's face. They said nothing, but when they stopped to rest an hour later, they knelt by Kess's boot and held out a strip of linen without a word. That was the strange thing about Lena Reyes: they gave you things as if they were nothing, as if kindness were just another supply to be rationed out quietly so no one made a fuss about it.

The waystation appeared at dusk, rising out of the mountainside like a broken tooth. Kess had seen ruins before—the crown burned its enemies' buildings for practice—but this was different. This place hadn't been destroyed. It had been *abandoned*, which was somehow worse. Arched doorways stood open to the wind. A bell tower leaned against the sky, its bronze bell green with age, silent for years beyond counting. Over the entrance, letters carved in old court script had been chiseled away, leaving only shallow scars where words used to be.

"What was it called?" Kess asked.

Brannoc stood beneath the ruined inscription for a long moment. "Doesn't matter," he said. "It doesn't have a name anymore."

But his voice did something strange when he said it, and Kess, who had been trained to hear lies in grown men's voices, heard one.

Inside, the waystation opened into a great hall floored with mosaic tiles, most of them cracked or missing, but here and there a pattern survived: nine interlocking circles, worked in glass the color of embers. Dust lay over everything like grey snow. Someone had lived here once—a cot frame rusted in a corner alcove, a clay pot still sitting on a cold hearth as if its owner had only stepped out to fetch water and never come back.

"We'll sleep here," Lena said. "One night. We move before dawn."

"Why here?" Kess asked. "It feels—" They stopped, hunting for the word.

"Watched," Brannoc supplied.

"No." Kess shook their head. "It feels *remembering*."

Both adults looked at them then, and Kess wished immediately they'd kept their mouth shut. In the barracks, saying things like that got you extra drills, or worse. But Lena only nodded slowly, as if Kess had confirmed something they'd already suspected.

"Get a fire going," Brannoc said. "Small one. And Kess—"

"I know. Keep it down." Keep yourself down. Keep the gift down. It was the same order they'd given every day since the smugglers' road, and Kess carried it the way they carried everything: silently, obediently, and with a growing crack running through the middle.

*

The fire was small, but the hall was big enough to make even a small fire feel like a betrayal of the dark. Lena spread their maps across the tiles, working by candle stub, and Brannoc took first watch near the doorway, his sword across his knees, a shape of stone and patience.

Kess couldn't sleep.

That was how it started, most nights. Sleeplessness, then restlessness, then the feeling under the sternum—that heatless warmth, like holding your hand over a candle without touching the flame. In the barracks they'd called it *the itch*, and the handlers had beaten it out of recruits where they could, and where they couldn't, they'd chained them to iron posts until the itch starved. Iron didn't hurt Kess exactly. It just made everything go grey and far away, like watching your own life through smoked glass.

There was no iron here. There was only the dark, and the fire, and the nine circles glowing faintly under the dust when the firelight caught them right.

Kess sat up.

The warmth under their breastbone had become a pulse. Not fast—slow, deep, patient, like something breathing in time with the mountain itself. And the fire answered it. Every time the pulse swelled, the flames leaned toward Kess, all together, like flowers turning toward a sun that wasn't there.

*Stop,* Kess told themselves. *Put it down. Put it down.*

But you can't put down what's inside you. That was the first lesson of the barracks and the last lesson of everything. So Kess did what they'd always done: gripped it, squeezed it, shoved it down into the grey place—and felt it shove back.

The fire flared.

Not much. A hand's height, maybe less. But in that dead hall, in that silence, it was a shout. Shadows leapt up the walls and across the ruined inscription, and for one instant Kess saw the chiseled-away letters stand out in relief, every scarred groove lit sharp as fresh carving:

**THE COURT OF CINDERS**

And beneath the light, for one instant, the whole hall seemed to breathe. The mosaics glowed. The dust rose in slow spirals. Somewhere far above, in the leaning tower, the silent bell hummed—one low note, felt in the teeth more than heard.

Then it was over. The fire sank. The dark came back.

But something had gone out from Kess in that moment—Kess felt it leave, felt it pass through stone and mountain like water through sand—and somewhere out in the night, miles away or more, Kess knew with absolute certainty that someone had felt it too.

"Kess." Brannoc was standing. His voice wasn't angry. It was worse than angry. It was afraid. "What did you do?"

"Nothing. I didn't—I was trying to keep it *down*—"

"The bell," Lena said quietly. They were standing too, maps forgotten, face pale in the dying light. "I heard the bell."

"That bell hasn't rung in forty years," Brannoc said. "I would know. I was there when they silenced it."

The words dropped into the room like stones into a well.

Kess looked at him—at this grey, hard, quiet man who never spoke of anything—and saw him standing in the firelight with his past written suddenly all over him, plain as ink.

"You were *here*," Kess said. "You served them. The Court."

Brannoc's hand closed around his sword hilt—not drawing it, just holding it, the way a drowning man holds a spar. "Every knight of the Ninefold swore the same oath," he said. "To guard the Ember, and to guard the secret of it. When the court fell, some of us were ordered to make sure no one could ever find it again." His eyes went to Lena. "I helped bury it. Under Thornfell's heart. I helped seal the ways, and I helped silence the bells that were set to warn if any gifted blood came near." He looked down at his hands, huge and scarred in the firelight. "Forty years I've been listening for those bells. Tonight, a child rang one."

"It wasn't me," Kess whispered. "I mean—it was, but I didn't—"

"I know what you didn't do." Brannoc crouched to their level, and his voice gentled in a way Kess had never heard from him. "Listen to me. That flare went out through the old warning-ways like a spark down a fuse. There are trackers who serve the crown—some of them gifted themselves, turned against their own kind—who can read a flare like that the way Lena reads a coastline. They'll come. Maybe not tonight. Maybe not tomorrow. But they'll come, and they'll come *here*."

"Then we leave now," Lena said, already rolling their maps. "In the dark. Off the trails."

"And go where?" Brannoc asked. "Every way I sealed, I sealed well. But the Ember isn't the only thing sleeping under these mountains, and the way to it runs through places I swore I'd never open again." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice was rough. "The past is calling in its debts. From all of us. From the girl who can't hold her fire down. From the mapmaker whose blood opens doors. And from the knight who buried the treasure and thought he'd buried himself along with it."

Outside, the wind moved through the ruined arches, and for a moment it sounded almost like distant hoofbeats.

Kess hugged their knees and counted. *One fire. Two companions. Three days since anyone called me by name instead of number.* The bell's note still hummed faintly in their back teeth, and far below the mountain floor, something vast and old seemed to have turned over in its sleep—awakened, perhaps, by a child's unguarded flame.

And somewhere in the dark beyond the walls, patient as winter, the trackers were already turning toward the light.
