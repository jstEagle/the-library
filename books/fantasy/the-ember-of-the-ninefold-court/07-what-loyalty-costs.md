## 7. What Loyalty Costs

The order came at dusk, written in the Crown's black wax and smelling of tallow and iron.

*By command of the Regent's Hand: let the gifted be gathered, and let their deaths be made public. Let the cartographer see what her silence costs.*

Vael Sarn read it three times by the light of a guttering lamp in the governor's house at Grayharbor, while rain ticked against the shutters like a clerk counting out coins. The handwriting was not the Regent's. It was the Hand's—small, precise, joyless. A hand that had never once hesitated over a signature.

He set the letter down on the map-strewn table. Lena Reyes's work stared up at him from a dozen sheets: coastlines drawn with a lover's patience, mountain passes rendered so finely he could almost feel the cold coming off the ink. He had spent weeks among these papers now, learning the cartographer the way one learns a face in the dark—by touch, by repetition, by the small betrayals of habit. The way they doubled every *l*. The way rivers were always drawn slightly thicker than truth required, as if water deserved more weight than land.

And beneath all of it, in the pressure of the pen, something else. A heat. He had pressed his fingertips to those lines more than once and told himself he was imagining the faint warmth that lingered in the paper, the way a hearthstone holds the memory of fire long after the coals are raked.

He was not imagining it. He knew what ember-warmth felt like. He had been carrying his own small coal behind his ribs since he was nine years old.

*Let their deaths be made public.*

Sarn closed his eyes.

* * *

They held the families in the old salt warehouse at the harbor's edge, because the jail was full of debtors and drunks and there was no room for the Crown's newer harvest. Nine families. Thirty-one souls, though Sarn had stopped counting souls years ago and forced himself to start again this morning, standing in the doorway with his ledger open like a man taking inventory of everything he was about to lose.

A cobbler named Merric Voss who could make dead wood bloom with his bare hands—he'd done it to entertain children at market, stupidly, beautifully, until a neighbor sold him for thirty crowns. His wife, who had no gift at all and refused to leave him. Two sisters whose skin glimmered faintly when they sang. A fisherman who could call eels out of deep water. Children. There were always children. Seven of them under the age of ten, huddled together on the far side of the warehouse as if distance from their parents might dilute whatever poison the Crown believed ran in their blood.

An old woman sat apart from the rest, spinning thread from a distaff she'd been allowed to keep. Her gift, according to the arrest record, was the mending of broken things—not objects; *things*. Quarrels. Silence between estranged brothers. Once, allegedly, a marriage. She looked up when Sarn entered, and her eyes were the pale gray of weathered slate, and they did not fear him.

"You're the one who burns them," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the one who finds them," Sarn said. "Others do the burning."

"That's a fine distinction to carry." She drew the thread out long and thin. "How much does it weigh?"

He did not answer. He crossed the warehouse floor, boots ringing on salt-crusted boards, and stopped before her. Up close he could see the gift in her, or thought he could—a faint shimmer under the skin of her hands, like sunlight through ice. His own coal stirred in answer, traitorous, warm. He pressed it down the way he had pressed it down for twenty-five years: breath by breath, lie by lie, until it was only an ache again.

"The executions are tomorrow," he said quietly. "At dawn. In the square."

"I know. They told us. They wanted us to have a night to think about it." She smiled without humor. "I've had twenty years to think about it, Inquisitor. Every day I mended something I shouldn't have been able to mend."

"Then you'll understand me when I say—" He stopped. Started again. The words came out lower than he intended, almost beneath the sound of the rain. "When I say that not everyone in this room has to die."

The spindle slowed. Stopped.

* * *

He worked through the night, and the work was the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than suppressing his gift, harder than the interrogation rooms, harder than watching Doss the smuggler burn and feeling the flame in his own chest like a second heartbeat.

Because here was the arithmetic of it, laid out plain: thirty-one people, and one Inquisitor, and a Crown that would send replacements if he failed. If he simply refused, they would hang him and burn the families anyway. If he reported the order complete with false names, the deception would unravel within a season—the dead had relatives, creditors, graves that needed filling. No. It had to be real enough to survive inspection. Real ash. Real grief. Real graves.

Just not real deaths.

He began with the records. He was, he reflected grimly, well suited to this particular treason: he had spent his career making paper tell lies that felt like truth. He wrote out thirty-one execution certificates in his own precise hand. He invented a plague—there was always plague, in port towns, in autumn—and quarantined the warehouse retroactively, backdating documents with the ease of long practice. Fever, he wrote. The gray fever, come off the ships. Contagion. The bodies must be burned entire, unopened, for the safety of the town.

Burned entire. Unopened. Unexamined.

It was elegant. It was monstrous. It would work.

Then came the harder part: persuading thirty-one frightened people to play dead.

* * *

He gathered them at midnight, all nine families, and stood before them in the lamplight, and for a long moment he could not speak. The old woman with the distaff watched him from her corner, and he understood suddenly that she had known since dusk—that she had seen the shape of him turning over and over in his hands like a coin he couldn't decide to spend.

"My name is Vael Sarn," he said finally. "You know my name. You know what I've done. I'm not going to ask you to forgive it. I'm going to ask you to trust it—one last time, in the worst possible direction."

Merric Voss rose slowly, wood-blooms long since faded from his knuckles. "Inquisitor—"

"At dawn, a pyre will be lit in the square. Thirty-one coffins will go onto it, sealed, because of the fever I've invented for you. And thirty-one living people will already be gone—out through the salt tunnels under the harbor, north along the coast road, into Thornfell if you can make it that far." He laid the forged papers on a barrel between them. "There are smugglers' routes. I know them. I've spent months learning them, chasing a cartographer through country I should never have been able to read."

"And after?" One of the singing sisters, arms around her children. "After we're ashes, what then? We can't buy bread. We can't show our faces. Our names will be on your lists forever."

"Yes," Sarn said. "That is the cost. I won't pretend it isn't. You'll be dead on paper for the rest of your lives. You'll grieve yourselves, and attend your own funerals from a hillside, and start over with nothing but what you can carry." His voice caught—actually caught, and he was too tired to be ashamed of it. "But you'll carry it breathing."

Silence, except the rain.

The old woman set down her distaff at last. "Why?" she asked, and it was not accusation; it was worse. It was genuine curiosity, gentle as a blade. "What changed in you?"

And Sarn—who had rehearsed a dozen answers on the walk over, about duty and conscience and the limits of lawful orders—heard himself say none of them.

"When I was nine," he said, "a witch-hunter came to my village. My mother hid me in the flour chest and told me to keep very still, and I did, and the whole time he searched our house I could feel it—the thing inside me, the warmth—trying to get out. Trying to answer him. Because that's what the gift does. It wants to be seen." He looked down at his own steady hands, the hands that had signed so many death warrants. "She died two winters later, still hiding me. Still paying for me. And I grew up and put on this coat and became the man in the doorway." He raised his eyes. "I'm tired of being the man in the doorway."

The old woman studied him for a long moment. Then, softly, she reached out and touched his wrist—just touched it, skin to skin, the first deliberate contact anyone had offered him in years.

And her gift moved through him like water through a cracked wall, finding every fracture he'd spent a lifetime caulking, and he felt something in his chest *mend*. Not the coal. The coal stayed, banked and waiting. But the loneliness around it—the leaden shell he'd built to smother it—softened, just slightly, just enough to hurt.

"There," she said. "That's what it's for, Inquisitor. Remember that, wherever you end up."

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping. The pyre was built in the square as ordered, thirty-one coffins stacked in the Crown's ceremonial geometry, and the town turned out to watch because towns always turn out, hungry for spectacle even when the spectacle is grief. Governor's men in their livery. Priests mumbling the rites for the soulless. Criers reading the Regent's proclamation about the peril of the ember-gifted and the mercy of the Crown.

Sarn stood at attention in his gray coat and gave the signal, and the coffins went into the fire, and the smoke rose thick and black over Grayharbor, and the crowd murmured and wept and went home to breakfast.

Inside those coffins: sandbags weighted to a body's heft, salt-soaked wool to thicken the smoke, and the clothes of the dead-to-be, gathered that night, folded by their owners with terrible tenderness. Some had left notes tucked in the linens. Goodbyes to themselves.

The real thirty-one were eleven miles north by then, moving through the salt tunnels and up the coast road in the company of a disgraced ferryman Sarn had bought with gold and blackmail in equal measure. By noon they would be in the fells. By dark, invisible.

Sarn filed his report at midmorning. *Executions carried out per order. Ashes interred in the common pit. Note: the cartographer Reyes has not surfaced. Recommend pursuit continue into the Thornfell passes.* He wrote it in his precise hand, sealed it in black wax, and understood, watching the seal cool, that he had crossed a line from which there was no documented return. He was a traitor now. Quietly, bureaucratically, irreversibly.

It should have felt like falling. Instead it felt like setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

He packed his saddlebags himself—no orderly, no witnesses—and took down from the wall the one map he had kept back from evidence, the one bearing Lena Reyes's doubled *l*s and their rivers drawn too thick, their ink still holding, faint as a secret, its ember-warmth. He looked at it a long time.

Then he rolled it, slung it across his back beside the sword he hoped he would not need, and rode north into the fells—alone, off the roads, following not the Crown's orders anymore but the pull of that buried heat, toward the mountains where a cartographer was running out of map.

Toward whatever waited under Thornfell's heart.

Toward the choice he could already feel waiting for him there, patient as a coal.
