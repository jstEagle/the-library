## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

Bram Okafor had counted the hours until midnight for eleven days, and on the twelfth night, the sky went out.

He was standing in the yard behind Mistress Halden's cottage with his pack already strapped to his shoulders—bread wrapped in oilcloth, a coil of hempen cord, the good knife she thought he'd lost last spring—when it happened. One moment the stars were there, all of them, the whole spilled-salt scatter of them that he had memorized without meaning to, because a boy who means to run needs to know which lights to steer by. And then they were not.

It did not feel like clouds coming over. Clouds arrive; they have weight and intention, a gray tide rolling up from the mountains. This was subtraction. The stars were there, and then the stars had never been there, and Bram felt the difference in his teeth, the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expected it.

He stood very still in the dark of the yard. The well rope creaked against the stones. Somewhere down the lane a dog began to bark—not the ordinary complaint of a dog at shadows, but a low, continuous, frightened sound, like an animal trying to warn its house about something too large to name.

"Stars," Bram said aloud, just to hear whether his voice still worked. "The stars are gone."

The valley did not answer. The valley, as far as Bram knew, had never answered anyone. But he felt—this was the part he would not tell anyone later, this was the part that made his hands shake as he tightened the pack straps—the valley *notice*. It was a sensation like being watched from inside his own skin, like the ground beneath his boots drawing a slow breath. The wards. He'd grown up on stories of the wards: the great unseen net that held the vale safe, that kept out the devouring dark beyond the hills, that made Emberfall Valley the only true place left in the world. Every child learned the litany before they learned their letters. *Within is warm. Within is whole. Without is hunger.*

And tonight, for the first time in his nineteen years, Bram could feel the litany straining. Not failing—not yet—but shuddering, the way a bridge shudders when a weight too heavy crosses it. He could feel it because of the scar.

He looked down at his left palm even though he didn't need to. He had carried the mark since birth: a thin silver line across the heel of his hand, curved like a crescent moon lying on its side. Mistress Halden called it a birth-blessing when he was small. She stopped calling it anything when he got older, and started going quiet whenever he mentioned that it itched near the valley's edge, near the standing stones on the western march where no one was allowed to walk. Tonight the scar wasn't itching. Tonight it was *burning*, a thin wire of heat drawn from his wrist to his thumb, and through that heat he could feel the wards the way you can feel a fever coming before the sweat starts.

They were falling. Or being pulled. Or—

A light came on in the cottage behind him. Warm gold spilled across the yard from the kitchen window, and Bram's heart did a complicated thing, half guilt and half grief, because that window meant Halden was awake, which meant she had felt it too, which meant she would come outside and find him standing in the dark with a packed bag and there would be no running away after all, only explanations, and he had rehearsed none that would survive her eyes.

He stepped back into the shadow of the woodshed. Old habit. He had been stepping into shadows around her for two years now, ever since the night he'd asked her what lay past the western stones and she had looked at him with an expression he still couldn't name—something between fear and sorrow and a door closing—and said, *Nothing, Bram. Nothing lies past the edge. That's rather the point of it.*

She came out into the yard wrapped in her gray shawl, holding a candle that guttered in a wind he couldn't feel. She was small and square and sixty years old, with flour still dusting her forearms from the evening's baking, and she stood in the middle of her garden looking up at a sky with nothing in it, and Bram watched her lips move. He was too far away to hear the words. He didn't need to hear them. He knew the litany. Everyone knew the litany.

*Within is warm. Within is whole.*

Her voice cracked on the third line. He saw her shoulders do it, saw the candle flame bend as her hand shook, and something in Bram's chest tore along a seam he hadn't known was there. Because he loved her. That was the terrible engine at the center of everything: he loved her. She had taken him in as a foundling with a strange scar and taught him herbs and hedge-craft and how to set a bone and how to read the weather off the backs of birds, and she had lied to him about the shape of the world, and he loved her anyway, and tomorrow night—no, *tonight*, now, tonight had arrived while he stood dithering—he was going to walk out of her life forever.

Unless the world had just walked out first.

* * *

He should go in. He knew he should go in. The wards were shaking, the stars were gone, every sensible instinct said *get inside, get under a roof, get next to someone who knows things.* Instead Bram found himself doing the opposite of sense, which was, by now, the most reliable thing about him. He slipped along the fence line, past the goat pen where old Marigold pressed her blunt face against the rails and huffed at him in betrayal, and took the lane toward the village green at a pace just short of running.

Emberfall Vale at night was a place of small honest lights: windows and lanterns and the orange glow of the forge that never quite cooled. Tonight those lights seemed thinner somehow, as if the darkness pressing down from the starless sky had more substance than darkness ought to have. People were coming out of their houses. He passed the miller's family standing in their doorway in their nightclothes, the miller's youngest daughter pointing straight up and asking a question her mother couldn't answer. He passed Tam Wicker's workshop, where lamplight burned behind shutters that were usually dark by this hour, and heard, muffled through the wood, the frantic clatter of someone knocking tools off shelves in a hurry. Good old Tam. Even exiled to the village's ragged edge for asking too many questions, the ward-tinkerer would be the first one trying to fix the sky.

On the green, a crowd was gathering the way crowds gather at disasters: slowly, then all at once. Maybe forty people, faces tilted upward, breath steaming in the cold. Wardens moved among them in their slate-gray cloaks, the ones who kept the marches and spoke for the Council of the Vale, and their calm was the worst thing Bram had seen all night. They weren't looking up. They were looking *out*—toward the hills, toward the edges—with the tight, listening expressions of people checking a lock they already know has been forced.

"The stars will return," one warden was saying, a broad woman with a voice pitched to carry. "The wards have held for three hundred years. Whatever this is, it is weather, not ruin. Go home. Light your lamps. Trust within."

*Trust within,* murmured the crowd, the way they always did, the way children are taught to murmur it, and Bram stood at the green's dark margin with his pack digging into his shoulders and felt the words go through him like water through a sieve. Because he had spent eleven days planning to stop trusting within. Eleven nights of lying awake cataloguing everything Halden had never explained: why the maps in her cabinet showed the valley surrounded by blank parchment instead of other countries. Why travelers never came, not ever, not once in his life. Why the road east of the standing stones simply ended, worn grass giving way to bare rock, as if the whole world had decided to stop bothering. Why his scar burned when he walked the high pasture near the western march, and why Halden's face went gray when he told her so.

He had told himself he was ungrateful. He had told himself the valley was enough, that wanting more was a sickness of the blood, some restlessness inherited from whatever parents had abandoned him on a church step with nothing but a crescent scar. He had almost believed it.

Then the sky had gone out, and every suppressed question in him rose up at once like crows off a harvested field.

Because here was the vertigo of it, the thing he kept circling and could not land on: he had wished for this. Not this exactly—never this, never the dog barking and the miller's daughter crying and the wardens' locked jaws—but he had lain awake wishing the valley would *show its hand*, wishing the world would crack open and prove him right for wanting out. And now the world had cracked, and it turned out the crack ran straight through the ceiling of heaven, and everyone he loved was standing underneath it saying *trust within* in voices that shook.

A wish granted in the worst possible way. He laughed—a single helpless syllable that he strangled immediately—and a warden's head turned toward the sound, and Bram melted backward off the green before the man's eyes could find him.

* * *

He made it three lanes toward the cottage before the second shock came.

It came as silence. All his life, Bram had been able to hear the valley hum. He'd assumed everyone could: a deep, subterranean note beneath hearing, felt more than heard, steady as a heartbeat, the sound of the wards doing their endless work. He used to fall asleep to it as a child, that vast patient drone, and dream of nothing at all, dreamless as a stone.

Tonight, walking the dark lane with his scar blazing, Bram realized the hum had changed key. It had gone thin. High. A singing wire pulled toward snapping. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, was a rhythm that did not belong—a pulse from *outside*, slow and enormous, like something vast turning over in its sleep beyond the hills. Pressing. Testing. Finding, perhaps, the same weakness Bram had found: that a cage built to keep a boy safe will also teach him exactly where the bars are.

The wards were not merely shaking. Someone or something was leaning on them from the other side, and the valley—his whole world, the only world—was a hand cupped around a candle flame, and the fingers were opening.

Bram pressed his burning palm flat against a fence post and breathed until the lane stopped tilting. Then he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since the sky went dark. If the wards fell, whatever was outside would come in. If the wards held, the wardens would hunt for the cause, and they would find it, because the cause was standing in a hedgewitch's yard with a packed bag and a scar that sang to the border-stones. Either way, the quiet life he'd been suffocating in was over. The only choice left was whether it ended with him inside it or outside it.

Outside, then. God help him. Outside.

He cut through the herb garden, ducked under the trellis where the runner beans climbed in summer, and let himself in through the kitchen door he had opened ten thousand times. The cottage smelled of bread and dried lavender and the bitter green tang of Halden's tinctures, and the smell hit him so hard he had to stand in the dark with his hand over his mouth for a moment, because this was the last time. Whatever happened at the valley's edge, he would not smell this room again as a person who lived here.

He wrote the note by feel, barely daring to light the lamp. *Mistress—I'm sorry. I have to see. If I'm wrong, thrash me when I'm home.—B.* He weighed it down with the honey jar so she'd find it at dawn, and then, because he was a coward in exactly one direction, he took the stairs up to her sleeping loft and stood beneath the trapdoor listening to her breathe. Slow. Steady. Asleep, or pretending well enough. He put his hand flat against the wooden hatch, scar side down, and felt the heat of his own blood pulse against the grain.

"I'll come back," he whispered, knowing it might be a lie, knowing she might know it was a lie, knowing she had known for two years that this night was coming and had never once tried to bar the door. That was the thing he couldn't stop thinking about as he climbed down again. She could have locked him in. She could have taken the knife from his pack, burned the maps hidden in his mattress, told the wardens her apprentice walked in his sleep. She had done none of it. Whatever Halden knew about the valley's edge—and she knew something, he'd stake his life on it, he was staking his life on it—she had chosen, every day for nineteen years, to leave the door unlocked.

That was either love or a trap. He was leaving to find out which.

* * *

The western march was a two-hour walk if he pushed, and he pushed. He took the shepherd's track above the millpond, where the grass grew long and the valley's little lights fell away below him one by one, until he was walking in a darkness deeper than any he had ever known. No stars. No moon—had there been a moon? He wracked his memory and found, with a fresh lurch of horror, that he could not remember. Had there ever been a moon over Emberfall? There must have been. There must have been, and he could not picture it, and the not-picturing felt less like forgetting and more like the memory itself having been sanded smooth, the way old paint gets sanded, the way things get prepared for something new to be painted over them.

His scar led him now. That was the plain truth of it, strange as it sounded even inside his own head: the crescent mark pulled westward like a compass needle, heat rising and falling with each step, and the closer he came to the standing stones, the more the air changed. It thickened. It tasted of iron and rain that hadn't fallen yet. The hum in the ground was a scream now, or nearly, and the pulse beyond it had quickened to match, two heartbeats racing each other toward the same terrible dawn.

He crested the last hill an hour before sunrise, and stopped.

The standing stones rose against the eastern grayness—nine of them, ancient and lichen-eaten, arranged in a crescent that mirrored, he realized with a chill that had nothing to do with cold, the mark on his own hand. Beyond them the ground fell away into mist. Not ordinary mist. This mist glowed faintly, a pale pearl luminescence with no source, and it filled the whole pass between the mountains like milk poured to the brim of a bowl. The road ran into it and simply stopped being a road. The world ran into it and stopped being the world.

And above the mist, where the sky should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud. Not dark. *Nothing*—a blackness so complete that his eyes slid off it, a hole cut in the fabric overhead, and through the hole he could feel the outside pulse beating, patient and vast, patient and vast, patient and—

The wards screamed. There was no other word for it. The hum became a shriek that he heard with his bones rather than his ears, and the nine stones flared with blue-white light, veins of fire crawling up their flanks, and Bram dropped to his knees in the wet grass with both hands clamped over his ears and his scar searing like a brand. Above the mist, the nothing *pressed down*. He saw it happen—saw the darkness bulge inward like a membrane pushed by a finger—and the stones blazed brighter, held, blazed—

—and dimmed.

Not failed. Dimmed. The fire in the stones sank from white to blue to a bruised violet, and the shriek faded to a whimper, and the whimper faded to that thin high singing-wire note, and the mist at the valley's edge sighed and shifted, and somewhere in its depths, faint as a memory, Bram heard—or imagined he heard, he would spend years deciding which—a sound like a door being tested on its hinges.

Then, silence. The awful, total silence of a world holding its breath.

Bram knelt in the grass at the edge of everything, shaking, weeping without quite noticing it, his packed bag still absurdly strapped to his back. The wards had not fallen. But they had bent, visibly, undeniably bent, and everyone in the valley had felt it, and by morning the wardens would come to these stones to find out why, and they would find the ground churned by kneeling feet and they would find, eventually, a runaway apprentice whose cursed scar matched the curse-marks on the stones themselves.

There was no going back. There was no staying. Both doors had closed in the same hour, and the only door left was the one in front of him, glowing faintly at the bottom of the pearl-white mist, leading into a nothing that might be death or might be the rest of the world.

He thought of Halden asleep under the trapdoor. He thought of the miller's daughter pointing at the empty sky. He thought of the litany, and how it had never once, in nineteen years, answered a single question he'd actually asked.

"Within is warm," Bram whispered to the dying light of the stones. His voice cracked. He laughed at the crack, a wild small sound with terror in it, and stood up, and wiped his face, and stepped forward onto the road that stopped being a road.

The mist took him the way water takes a dropped coin—swallowing, closing, erasing—and the last thing he felt was his scar flaring once, bright as a struck match, as if something on the other side had recognized him.

Something was waiting in the mist. He could hear it moving. It was sketching.
