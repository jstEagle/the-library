## 2. Hedge-Witch's Boy

The night the stars went out, Bram Okafor was already running.

But to understand why a boy would run from the only home he had ever known—out into a dark that had just swallowed the sky—you have to go back further. You have to go back to the smell of rosemary and burnt honey, to a cottage with crooked shutters and a garden that grew things out of season, to the woman who raised him and lied to him in equal measure, and who loved him so fiercely that the lies were, she must have believed, a kind of shelter.

You have to go back to being a hedge-witch's boy.

* * *

He was seven the first time he asked about the edge of the valley.

It was autumn, and Mistress Halden had sent him up the hillside behind the cottage to gather late rowanberries before the frost took them. The berries hung in heavy red clusters, and the air smelled of woodsmoke and turning leaves, and from the top of the hill Bram could see the whole of the valley spread out below him like a quilt someone had stitched with enormous care: the river looping silver through the middle, the fields in their patchwork golds and browns, the village of Emberlow huddled around its market square with its chimneys already breathing thin gray ribbons into the cooling air. And all around it, ringing everything like the walls of a cupped hand, the mountains.

He had never been past them. No one had. He knew this the way he knew his own name, which is to say he had never once thought to question it, until that afternoon, when he noticed something strange about the light.

The sun was setting behind the western peaks, and its last rays were gilding the snow on the highest ridges—and then they simply stopped. Not faded. Stopped. As if the light itself had reached the boundary of something and found no permission to continue. Beyond that line the mountains went on, he could see them going on, but they were lit by nothing, flat as figures painted on a shutter.

"Mistress Halden," he said that evening, while she stirred a poultice over the fire and he snapped bean pods for tomorrow's soup, "what's past the mountains?"

Her spoon did not stop moving. That was the first thing he remembered afterward—not that she froze, not that she looked at him strangely, but that her spoon kept its slow, patient circle through the green sludge of the poultice, exactly as it had been doing, exactly as it always did, as though his question had been no more than a change in the wind.

"More mountains," she said.

"And past those?"

"More mountains still." She tapped the spoon against the pot's rim, twice, the way she did when a thing was finished. "The world is very large, Bram, and mostly made of stone. Eat your beans."

But he had seen the light stop. He was seven, not stupid, and he carried the image of that flat, unlit country home inside him like a splinter, working its way deeper every year.

* * *

She took him in when he was four. He had no memory of before the cottage—no memory of parents, of another house, of any life that did not smell of rosemary and burnt honey—and when he asked, once, older, braver, drunk on his own daring, she told him he'd been left on her doorstep in a basket, like a storybook foundling, and she had laughed when she said it, but her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere far away and cold, and he had learned not to ask again.

What she gave him instead was work, and work, in Halden's cottage, was a kind of love. She taught him which mushrooms would feed you and which would stop your heart between one breath and the next. She taught him the names of herbs in two tongues—the valley tongue and an older one she claimed was just "the way old women talk"—and made him repeat them until they lived in his mouth like teeth. She taught him to set bones, to draw fevers, to read weather in the behavior of birds and the ache of old scars. And she taught him the small magics: how to coax a flame to burn blue and steady for reading late into the night; how to whisper to a wound so it forgot to bleed; how to walk soft through a sleeping household carrying a full bucket without spilling a drop, because some nights you healed people in their homes and it was kinder not to wake them.

"You have the touch," she said when he was nine, watching him calm a lamed ewe with nothing but his palm flat against its heaving flank. There was something complicated in her face when she said it—pride and grief sharing a chair, neither willing to give up its seat. "You have more than the touch."

"Then teach me."

"I'm teaching you." She ruffled his hair hard enough to hurt, which was her way of ending conversations. "I'm teaching you everything you need."

Everything you need. Not everything there was. He was too young to hear the difference. He heard it later, in the dark years, and it rang in him like a struck bell that would not finish fading.

* * *

The scar came when he was eleven.

It was midsummer, and she took him farther from the cottage than she had ever taken him—a full day's walk north, up through the high pastures where the sheep went thin and wild, to a place where the trees stopped growing in a line so straight it looked drawn. On one side of the line, grass and pine and the ordinary clutter of the world. On the other side, grass too, and pine too, but somehow wrong, somehow quieter, as if the whole hillside beyond were holding its breath.

"Stay close," she said, and her voice had a weight he had never heard in it before. "And whatever happens, don't cross the line."

"What line? I don't see any—"

He stepped forward to look, and the world bit him.

There was no fence, no wall, no thorn hedge. But something met his skin like a wall of cold water standing on its end, and where it touched him—at the heel of his palm, along the soft flesh below the thumb—it burned, and he snatched his hand back with a cry, and there on his palm was a mark like a lightning-branch, white and fine, as if a thread of the invisible wall had seared itself into him forever.

Mistress Halden knelt so fast her knees cracked against the stones. She took his hand in both of hers, and her fingers were shaking.

"It doesn't bite children usually," she murmured, half to herself, half to him, half to something else entirely. "It doesn't mark them. It shouldn't—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. Wrapped his palm in a poultice of comfrey and something bitter he couldn't name, and walked him home in silence, and that night she sat by his bed until he slept, and he woke once in the deep hours to find her still there, staring at his bandaged hand where it lay on the blanket as though it were a letter she could read if only she tried hard enough.

"Why does it itch?" he asked her, because even half-asleep the scar itched, a deep maddening prickle, like the memory of ants.

"Scars itch," she said. "Go back to sleep."

But it didn't itch everywhere. It itched near the line. He learned this within the month, learned it the way you learn anything at eleven: accidentally, thoroughly, and in secret. Walking the northern pastures, the prickling would rise until it felt like his palm was full of nettles; walking home southward, it would fade to a whisper. The closer to the mountains' ring, the louder the scar spoke. His body, he understood without understanding, had become a kind of instrument, and the valley's edge was the note it was tuned to.

He never told her he knew. And she never asked why he stopped asking about the edge. Two people under one roof, each keeping their silence like a candle cupped against the wind, each believing they were protecting the other from the dark.

* * *

At fourteen, he asked her again, because at fourteen you cannot help yourself.

They were shelling peas on the doorstep in the long gold light of a June evening, and the question came out of him sideways, the way true questions do: "Did you ever want to leave?"

Halden's hands went still over the bowl of pods. A bee droned drunkenly among the foxgloves. Somewhere down in Emberlow a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"When I was young," she said at last, and her voice was careful, laid down stone by stone like a ford across a river, "there was a war. Far away. Over the mountains, over many mountains. Bad enough that some folk decided the only safe place was a place that couldn't be found." She opened a pod with her thumb, precise, unhurried. "So they made one."

"Made one how?"

"Bram."

"Made it how?" He heard his own voice crack and hated it. "Mistress, my hand burns when I walk north. There's a line in the hills where the trees stop straight as a rule. The light stops at the peaks like it's hit glass. I've *seen*. What is this place?"

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—he thought she was going to tell him. Her eyes were wet in the low sun, and her mouth opened, and the whole truth stood behind her teeth like a bird at a door.

Then she closed her mouth, and picked up another pea pod, and said, "This place is home. It's the only home you'll ever need, and one day you'll thank me for keeping you safe in it." And she smiled at him, and the smile was so tired and so tender and so full of love that he swallowed every follow-up question he owned, and shelled peas beside her until the light died, and hated himself a little for how much he wanted to believe her.

That was the ache of it, he thought later, lying awake in the loft with his itching palm pressed flat against the floorboards as if he could pin the feeling down. Not that she lied. That she lied *well*, and kindly, and that the lie was woven through with so much real love that pulling the thread might unravel everything warm he had ever known. Belonging to someone who lies to you is not like being deceived by a stranger. It is like living in a beautiful house and slowly noticing that the windows are painted on.

* * *

The years after that, he collected secrets instead of questions.

He learned that the valley had no winter worth the name—frost came, but never the killing kind, and snow fell soft and shallow and melted in days, and old Sef the fisherman swore over his nets that in his grandfather's time winters had been "proper winters, lad, with ice you could walk a horse over," and everyone laughed at him, and Bram, laughing too, filed it away.

He learned that no one ever arrived. Travelers' tales were all secondhand, all *my cousin saw*, all *years ago, before my time*. The road south out of Emberlow wound up toward the pass and dwindled to a footpath and dwindled to a game trail and dwindled to nothing, and the few who walked it looking for adventure always, always turned back, and could never quite say why. "Felt wrong," they said. "Got a bad feeling." His palm itched just hearing them say it.

He learned that maps were rare and strange things in Emberlow. There was one, inked on linen, hanging behind the bar at the Wayfarer's Rest, showing the valley and its ring of mountains and—nothing else. Not blank space beyond. Nothing. The linen simply ended, hemmed and stitched, as if the world had been cut to fit the frame. When he pointed this out once, at sixteen, to Tam the tinkerer's son—a sharp-eyed boy his own age who mended kettles and locks and, rumor said, ward-stones nobody admitted owning—Tam had looked at the map a long time and said, quietly, so only Bram could hear:

"Whoever drew this wasn't lazy. Look at the stitching. They *finished* it. They finished the world right here on purpose." And then he'd grinned his quick unsettling grin and said, "Makes you wonder what they were afraid of letting in," and Bram had felt his scar prickle so hard he had to put his hand in his pocket.

He learned, above all, to watch Halden. To catalog the small evasions: how she never let him gather herbs on the north slopes; how she paid her respects at the standing stone above the village every solstice, alone, and came back smelling faintly of ozone, like the air after lightning; how, on certain nights—nights he could never predict but always felt coming, nights when his scar hummed like a struck tuning fork—she would stand in the garden at midnight with her arms raised, muttering in the old tongue, renewing something, feeding something, tending the invisible.

Once, on such a night, he crept to the window and watched her, and saw her pause in her working, and turn, and look directly at his window across the dark garden. He ducked below the sill, heart hammering. When he dared peek again she was still looking, and she did not look angry or surprised.

She looked sad. Sad the way you are sad about weather, about seasons, about things that are coming regardless. Then she finished her working, and the humming in his palm faded, and she went inside, and in the morning she made porridge with honey and complained about her knees and never said a word.

* * *

On the last ordinary evening—the evening before the stars went out, though neither of them knew it yet—she taught him a new charm.

It was a warming charm, simple enough, the kind she'd taught him a dozen variants of since childhood. But this one had a line in it he didn't recognize, a phrase in the old tongue that made his scar flare so suddenly and so sharply that he gasped and dropped the mortar he was holding. It cracked on the flagstones, dried yarrow scattering like tiny green bones.

"Careful!" she snapped, and then, seeing his face, seeing him cradle his burning palm, she went very still. "Bram. What did you feel?"

"It itched. It—" He flexed his fingers. The prickle was already fading, retreating like a tide. "It sang. For a second. Like it does near the line, but here, inside the cottage, inside—" He looked up at her. "Mistress, what was that phrase? Where does the charm actually come from?"

Something moved behind her eyes, fast, like a fish under ice. He had spent ten years learning to read that face, and he caught it: fear, yes, but underneath the fear, guilt. Guilt with nowhere to hide.

"An old charm," she said. "From an old book. It's nothing. Cut your hand on the shards and mind you don't leave any in the dough tomorrow."

She turned back to the fire. He knelt picking up the pieces of the mortar, and his scar gave one last faint pulse, like a heartbeat slowing, and he understood three things at once, the way you sometimes understand things all in a single silent flash: that she knew what the phrase meant; that it frightened her; and that whatever was coming—whatever his own blood and bone were tuned to receive like a bell awaiting its strike—she had known it was coming for a very long time.

He could have asked. Standing there with the broken mortar in his hands and the firelight on his master's bent back, he could have asked her plainly, one last time, and maybe—maybe—on the edge of whatever was coming, she would finally have answered.

Instead he swept up the shards, and kissed her cheek goodnight, and climbed to the loft, and lay awake with his palm pressed to the floorboards, listening to her move below: bank the fire, bar the door, pause—longer than usual, longer than ever before—beneath the ladder as if she meant to climb up and wake him and say everything, and then continue on to her own bed, where the boards creaked once, twice, and went quiet.

He slept. He dreamed of a line of trees stopping straight as a rule, and beyond it a country lit by nothing, and in the dream his hand was open, and the scar was singing, and something on the far side of the flat dark light was singing back.

He woke at moonrise to a sky with no stars in it, and the wards of the valley screaming down every nerve of his body like a harp with all its strings snapping at once, and Mistress Halden's voice on the path outside, calling his name—not the way she called him to chores, but the way you call someone you are afraid you will never see again.

Bram Okafor did not answer her.

He took the satchel he had packed in secret weeks ago, and the coat she'd made him with the deep pockets, and the memory of every kindness she had ever done him folded up tight around the memory of every lie, and he went out the back window and down the garden and away, north, toward the line where the trees stopped, toward the itch and the song and the truth, running from the only home he had ever known with tears freezing on his face—

—because the stars had gone out, and the wish he had never quite dared to wish had come true in the worst possible way, and the world outside the painted windows was finally, terribly, open.
