## 3. The Cartographer of Nothing

Bram walked for two days before he understood that the valley was walking with him.

He had left the cottage in the black hour after the stars went out, when the wards had shuddered like a struck bell and Mistress Halden's voice had cracked through the dark—*stay inside, Bram, stay inside, whatever you hear*—and he had done the one thing she had never asked of him, which was to disobey her. He had taken the satchel with the bread and the flint and the little brass compass she kept locked in a drawer as if it were a sin, and he had run east, toward the edge, toward the place she had spent eleven years teaching him not to think about.

The valley did not want him to leave. He felt it in his boots first: the road bending when it should have run straight, the hedgerows leaning in like curious neighbors, the same crooked oak appearing three times on the same hillside as though the land were shuffling its own pages. By the second morning he was certain. He marked a stone with chalk from his pocket, walked an hour, and came upon the same stone again, his own mark facing him like an accusation.

"You're not real roads," he told the valley. His voice sounded small under the starless sky. "You're a story someone tells over and over."

The sky above him was wrong in a way he could no longer unsee. He had grown up beneath that darkness without knowing it was darkness; Halden had called it the Veil, had said all countries had one, that other places simply kept theirs kinder. But he had seen the night the stars went out—he had been awake, face pressed to the cold window, when the few pale lights that had hung over the eastern ridge for all his life guttered and died like candles pinched between wet fingers—and now the sky was not merely dark but *empty*, a ceiling instead of a heaven, close enough some nights that he swore he could feel it pressing down on the back of his neck.

So he walked against the grain of the land. When the road curved away from the east, he cut through bracken. When the hills folded themselves politely out of his path, he climbed them anyway. The scar on his palm—the old burn-shaped mark Halden had never explained—itched so fiercely by the second dusk that he had scratched it raw, and where his blood touched the soil, the grass grew green and eager, as though his body were arguing with the valley in a language neither of them would translate.

On the third morning, the land gave up its trick.

He crested a hill of grey gorse and there was no hill beyond it. There was no *beyond* beyond it. The world simply stopped—a line drawn across existence, soft-edged, shimmering faintly like heat off a summer road. Beyond the line was not darkness and not distance but a kind of pale nothing, the color of unwritten paper, and it stretched north and south as far as he could see.

Bram stood at the top of that hill with his heart hammering against his ribs like a bird in a closed room, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that everything he had ever known fit inside a box. And someone had built the box.

His legs carried him down the slope before his courage caught up. The air changed near the edge—it thinned, sweetened, tasted faintly of rain that hadn't fallen anywhere yet. Twenty paces from the shimmering line, his scar stopped itching and began to *burn*, a slow bright ache, and he wrapped his hand in the rag from his satchel and kept walking because stopping would have meant thinking and thinking would have meant turning back.

That was when he saw the woman.

She was sitting cross-legged in the gorse ten paces from the border of nothing, so still that at first he took her for a cairn or a scarecrow. She wore a long coat the color of dried mud, patched at the elbows, and her hair was iron-grey and cropped short as a soldier's. Spread across her knees was a sheet of heavy paper weighted at the corners with stones, and she was drawing—quick, precise strokes of a pen dipped in ink from a stoppered bottle beside her hip. Around her, arranged with the care of a surgeon's tray, lay instruments Bram had no names for: a brass circle etched with numbers, a plumb line swinging gently in wind he couldn't feel, a telescope no longer than his forearm, and three compasses, each pointing in a different direction, each trembling slightly, like animals that smelled a storm.

"Stop there," the woman said, without looking up. Her voice was dry and even, worn smooth like a river stone. "You're standing in my sightline."

Bram froze. "Your—"

"Sightline." She finished her stroke, blew on the ink, and only then raised her head. Her eyes were pale grey, the grey of the paper-world beyond the border, and they went through him the way a surveyor's chain goes through open ground—measuring, cataloguing, dismissing. Then they stopped. They fixed on his bandaged hand, then rose to his face, and something shifted behind them, some door opening onto a room she'd thought long empty.

"Well," she said. "You're not supposed to exist."

"Neither is that," Bram said, and pointed at the shimmering edge of the world.

The woman snorted—a short, involuntary sound, quickly suppressed, as though laughter were a tool she'd pawned years ago. "No," she agreed. "It isn't. And yet here we both are, existing at it." She gathered her weighted paper and stood, joints cracking, and Bram saw that she was tall and thin as a fence post and probably fifty years old, though her hands looked younger than her face, quick and sure and ink-stained to the second knuckle. "Come here, boy. Don't be shy. Shy people don't walk three days against a valley that doesn't want them gone."

The words landed in him like stones dropped into still water. *Three days.* She had been watching him. Or watching for anyone. He came closer despite himself, close enough to see the paper on her knees: it was covered in fine, dense linework, contours and hatching and tiny immaculate lettering, a map taking shape under her pen—and every line stopped dead at the shimmering border, and beyond it she had written, in letters small and furious, over and over until they ran out of room:

*NO DATA. NO DATA. NO DATA.*

"What is this place?" Bram asked. His voice came out hoarse. Three days of silence had rusted it.

"This place?" She tapped the paper. "This place is the reason I am no longer employed by the Royal Cartographic Office of Ilvaren, why my name was struck from the Guild rolls, why I have eaten gorse-berries and river trout for four months and talked to myself until talking to myself got boring." She rolled the map with a snap. "Ilsa Vane. Formerly Third Surveyor to the Crown. Currently the only person in the world who knows this valley exists, which is a problem, because the world does not."

"I don't understand."

"No," she said. "You wouldn't. You're from inside." She said it the way a physician says *you're from the plague country*—not cruelly, but with the flat certainty of diagnosis. She crouched, picked up one of the compasses, and held it out to him. "Look."

Bram took it. The needle spun lazily, aimlessly, a drunkard's finger. He had never held a compass before—Halden's had lived behind a lock—but he had read about them in her books, smuggled geographies with the maps torn out, and he knew what a needle was supposed to do. It was supposed to *choose*. This one refused.

"Now watch." Ilsa Vane took it back, stepped over the shimmering line—

And Bram's whole body lurched forward, his scar flaring white-hot, a cry tearing out of him before he knew he'd made it—"Don't!"

But she had already stepped back, unhurt, unbothered, holding the compass up between two fingers. On the far side of the border, for just that half-second, the needle had swung hard and settled, pointing south-southwest, steady as scripture.

"The moment I cross your line," she said quietly, "north exists again. South exists. My instruments remember their manners. Step back over here and—" she crossed, and the needle began its drunken spinning once more, "—everything forgets itself. There is no direction in your country, boy. No bearing. No distance that agrees with itself. I have triangulated this border from nine points on the outside, and every survey gives me a different shape. I have tried to fix its position against the sun, and the sun"—her jaw tightened—"the sun does not pass over this valley. I have watched the dawn come up *around* it, like water around a stone. Your home is a hole in the geometry of the world, and the world has healed over it so smoothly that no one ever thought to look."

Bram's mouth was dry. "There are maps," he said. "Mistress Halden has books. There's a map of the valley in the schoolhouse, and it shows the mountains, and the river, and—"

"A map of the valley, yes. Drawn by whom? For whom? Have you ever seen a map that shows what lies *beyond* the valley? One road leading out? One name past the ridgeline? One sea?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. He thought of every book in Halden's cottage, every chart in the schoolhouse, every traveler's tale told in the market at Lowmere—and realized, with a vertigo so complete it was almost physical, that every map he had ever seen ended at the valley's edge in faded, apologetic blankness, and that he had never once wondered why. The wondering had been sanded out of him. Or warded out.

"There's nothing," he whispered. "Every map just... stops."

"Because your maps aren't records," Ilsa said. "They're furniture. They tell you where your bed is and where your table is and they leave the rest of the room undrawn, because you were never meant to know there's a door." She slung her instrument case over her shoulder and looked at him fully, and for the first time he saw that beneath the surveyor's flatness there was something else—something banked and burning, the way coals hold fire after the flames are raked over. "I found this valley because of a ledger. Four hundred years ago, the Royal Office recorded a survey expedition dispatched to the northern marches. Nine cartographers. They reported back once, by courier, with coordinates and a promise of a full chart within the season. Then nothing. The expedition was marked lost. The coordinates were marked erroneous, because no such terrain existed at them. The page was marked *closed*." Her voice dropped. "But I pull threads. It's my one vice. And I pulled that thread for six years, through archives and grave-registers and the private papers of dead surveyors, and every thread led here—to a valley that appears on no map, in a country that swears it has no missing piece, at coordinates that everyone agreed were a mistake."

"And were they?"

"They were the most accurate coordinates in the history of my profession." She turned to face the shimmering nothing, and Bram followed her gaze. Up close, the border wasn't empty at all, he saw now. It moved. Faint patterns drifted through it like fish under ice—shapes almost forming, almost meaning something, dissolving before the eye could hold them. "Something erased this place," Ilsa said softly. "Not hid it—hid things can be found. Erased it. Wrote it out of the world so thoroughly that the world itself cooperated. And whatever did the writing was powerful enough that the wound never quite healed. That's what this is." She gestured at the drifting shapes. "A scar. The world remembering, badly, that something used to be here."

The wind moved through the gorse with a sound like whispering, and Bram hugged his arms against a chill that had nothing to do with weather. "Then how did you get in? If it's erased. If the world forgot."

"I didn't get in." She gave him a look that was almost pitying. "I'm standing *outside* it. So are you. That's the thing you haven't understood yet, boy, standing there with your burned hand and your three days of walking." She stepped toward him, and her pale eyes held his with the terrible gentleness of someone delivering a prognosis. "The border isn't behind you. It's around you. Your whole valley sits inside this line like a coin inside a ring. Everything you've ever seen—the mountains, the river, Lowmere, your mistress's cottage—is on the *inside* of the nothing. You didn't walk to the edge of your country. You walked to the edge of the world's memory, and you're standing on the last inch of it that still remembers your name."

Bram looked down at his bandaged palm. Through the cloth, the scar pulsed with a slow, hot rhythm, like a second heartbeat, like something knocking to be let out.

"Why doesn't it erase me too?" he asked. "If I'm from inside. Why can I stand here?"

For a long moment Ilsa Vane didn't answer. She looked at his hand, then at his face, then at the drifting, dreaming shapes in the border-wall, and Bram watched her arrive at a decision the way he'd watched her draw lines on paper: swiftly, precisely, with no wasted stroke.

"That," she said, "is the question I've been sitting in this gorse for four months hoping somebody from inside would eventually walk out here and answer." She shouldered her pack and nodded east along the shimmering line, toward a low fold in the hills where, Bram now noticed, a lean-to of oiled canvas and driftwood had been built against the slope—a camp, a life, a vigil. "I have tea. Well. I have hot water and opinions. Come and sit, and tell me who you are, and who raised you, and what they told you about your hand."

The word *tea* did more to undo Bram than all the revelations. Three days of hunger and fear and walking, and here was a stranger offering him warmth as if the world were still a place where warmth could be offered. He followed her toward the lean-to, and as he walked, he glanced back once at the valley behind him—its familiar hills, its hedgerows, its kind and crooked roads—all of it fitting, from this side, into a space no bigger than the span of his two hands held apart.

It was the smallest thing he had ever seen, and it was everything he had ever loved.

At the camp, while the water heated over a spirit-lamp, Ilsa unrolled a fresh sheet of paper and pinned its corners. Then she drew a single line across it, and at the center of the line she drew a small, careful circle, and inside the circle she wrote a word.

Bram leaned over her shoulder. "What's that say?"

"It's the oldest name for this place I could find," she said. "In a fragment of a fragment, a shipping record from before the Sundering wars. A captain wrote that his crew had sailed past a coast where 'the stars had been paid away,' and he named the bay after what he saw there." She tapped the word inside the circle.

*Sundered Sea.*

"The stars were paid away," Bram repeated. Something in the phrase snagged on him like a hook in wool. "Paid to whom?"

Ilsa Vane looked up at him across the little flame of the spirit-lamp, and her pale eyes were bright with the particular madness of a person who has finally, after years alone, found someone to tell.

"To the dragon," she said. "Whose heart, I believe, is still burning somewhere in your valley. And I think, boy, that it just heard you coming."

Behind them, at the border of the world, the drifting shapes in the shimmering wall had begun—very faintly, very slowly—to pulse. In time with the scar on Bram's palm.
