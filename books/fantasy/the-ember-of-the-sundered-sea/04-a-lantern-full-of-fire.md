## 4. A Lantern Full of Fire

Ilsa Vane had learned long ago that the best way to carry a secret was to treat it like a tool. A tool had weight, yes, but it also had purpose, and a thing with purpose could be endured. She carried the lantern the way she carried her guilt: close to the body, angled away from strangers, and never, ever discussed.

The lantern itself was unremarkable. That was the point. She'd bought it in a market town three hundred miles and one lifetime ago—pewter, dented, its glass clouded with the breath of every fire it had ever held. Any thief worth her salt knew that the finest hiding place for an extraordinary object was ordinary craftsmanship. Kings kept their treasures behind wards and walls. Nobody guarded a dented lantern.

But inside that lantern burned the last light of nine extinguished stars.

She felt it against her hip as she walked, a warmth that had nothing to do with flame. The Ember did not flicker. It did not gutter in wind or drown in rain. It simply *was*, steady as a heartbeat, and on the bad nights—the nights when she let herself remember—that steadiness was worse than any accusation. Fire that could not be put out was fire that remembered what it was owed.

The boy was following her.

She had known it since the valley's edge, since the moment her instruments had gone mad and this Bram Okafor had come stumbling out of the hedge-row with his eyes full of vanished stars and his whole face a question he didn't know how to ask. She should have lost him by now. She was, whatever the Royal Cartographic Society said in their polite, devastating letter, very good at moving through country that did not wish to be moved through. But the boy kept pace, half a mile back, dogged as a debt, and every time she crested a rise and looked back, there he was—a smudge of motion against the smothered fields, refusing to become nothing.

*Let him follow,* she told herself. *He'll tire. They always tire.*

He did not tire.

By the time the light failed—and it failed strangely now, the dusk coming down like a lid rather than a tide—Ilsa had run out of patience and, if she was honest, out of the particular loneliness that had been her only companion since Corvane's men had taken the Ember's true bearer and left Ilsa with a stolen god in a soup pot. She stopped at the ruins of a shepherd's cot, set the lantern down inside the broken circle of its wall, and waited for the boy to do exactly what boys did.

He came around the corner too fast, saw the firelight, and froze like a poacher caught in a lamp-beam.

"You're loud," Ilsa said, without looking up. She was sharpening her pencil knife against a whetstone, mostly for something to do with her hands. "You breathe like a bellows and you step on every dry twig between here and the ridge. If I'd wanted you dead, you'd have died at the second fence."

"I—" The boy swallowed. Up close he was all elbows and hollows, underfed in the way of apprentices whose masters fed their craft better than their bodies. His right hand was curled closed, and she noticed—he made sure she noticed—that he kept it that way deliberately. "I need to know what happened to the stars."

"Do you." She tested the blade's edge with her thumb. "And what makes you think I know?"

"Because you were drawing the border." He came closer, drawn despite himself, the way moths were drawn and the way boys were drawn to trouble. "At the valley's edge. You had instruments out. Surveyor's tools. Nobody surveys a place they don't care about."

*Sharp,* she thought, and hated him a little for it. Sharp things cut their handlers. "Sit down before you fall down," she said instead. "You look like a scarecrow that's lost its pole."

He sat. Of course he sat. He was starving, and exhausted, and the night outside the ruined cot was darker than any night had a right to be, and she was the only lit thing in a country gone blind. She pushed half her bread across the stones toward him and told herself it was strategy. Fed informants talked. Fed companions didn't collapse. There was no kindness in it at all.

None whatsoever.

"The stars went out at moonrise," the boy said, around the bread. "All of them. Like someone pinched them out. And then the wards—" He stopped. His closed hand twitched. "The hedges along the border shuddered. I felt it in my teeth. And then they just... weren't. The pressure was gone. Like a headache ending, except the headache had been my whole life."

"Your whole life," Ilsa repeated. She turned the pencil knife over in her fingers and studied him across the lantern-light. "How old are you?"

"Nineteen. Twenty in autumn."

"And in nineteen years, no one ever wondered why your valley had no roads out? No travelers in? No merchants, no pilgrims, no tax collectors?" She let the questions land one after another, gentle as falling axes. "No letters?"

"There were letters." He frowned. "From my mother. Before she died. And Mistress Halden gets... parcels. Sometimes. From her sister, she says, in a town called Lowmark." He paused, and Ilsa watched the thought arrive in his face like weather. "I've never met anyone from Lowmark."

"No," Ilsa said. "You haven't."

The silence stretched. Outside, the dark pressed against the broken walls like water against a hull. Inside, the Ember burned its steady, patient burn, and Ilsa found herself—against all sense, against every rule she'd built since the Society's letter—reaching for the lantern.

"Look at this," she said. "Tell me what you see."

He leaned in. The lantern's clouded glass softened the light into something almost domestic, almost kind. "A lantern," he said. "Pewter. Old. The flame's strange, though—it doesn't move. It's like..." He searched for the word, and Ilsa braced herself, because everyone reached for the same word eventually. "Like it's remembering how to burn."

*There it is.*

"It's not a flame," Ilsa said. "It's a shard. Dragon-fire, if you believe the stories, and I have spent two years believing them whether I wanted to or not. It was cut from the heart-fire of the dragon that—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Some truths were tools; others were knives you handed to strangers handle-first and hoped. "It's the last light of the stars that went out tonight. When they were extinguished, this is what remained. One ember, saved out of nine burning skies."

The boy had gone very still. "That's not possible."

"Neither is a valley with no roads," Ilsa said. "Neither is a border my compasses refuse to draw. Neither is you, sitting there with a scarred hand you keep fisted like it's going to bite someone. We've left possible behind us, Okafor. We're living in the part of the map where the cartographer writes *here be dragons* and means it literally."

"How do you know my name?"

"You talk in your sleep when you faint. You fainted at the valley's edge. Your master's mark is on your satchel—Halden's cottage has a reputation, even out here." She sheathed the pencil knife. "And because I've been asking about you longer than you've known I exist."

His eyes came up, wary and bright. "Why?"

And there it was. The question she'd been circling since the hedge-row, the question she'd have to answer eventually, and so she did what she always did with unavoidable pain: she made it efficient.

"Because I was hired to steal this," she said, and touched the lantern. "Two years ago. By a magister named Corvane. He wanted the Ember delivered intact, unlit by any other flame, unmapped by any road. I was the best thief-cartographer in the royal service, which is a sentence I can no longer say without laughing, and I took the contract because the fee would have bought me my own ship and a coastline to sail it along." Her voice stayed level. She'd practiced. Two years of practice. "What I didn't take was that the Ember already had a keeper. An old woman in the high passes who'd tended it for sixty years. Corvane's men killed her the night I lifted the lantern. I was three valleys away before I understood what the weight in my pack actually cost."

The boy said nothing. The Ember burned. Somewhere far off, something that might have been wind moved through the starless dark, though there was no wind anymore, not really, not since the sky had closed.

"I ran," Ilsa said. "Not out of conscience—conscience is a luxury for people with clean hands. I ran because I opened the lantern to see what I'd stolen, and it looked back." She laughed, short and ugly. "Fire shouldn't *look*. But it did. It looked at me like it knew exactly what I'd done and intended to wait, patiently, for as long as it took, until I did something about it. So I've been running with it ever since. North, south, into marshes, over mountains. Corvane's hunters behind me. The Ember getting heavier every year, though it weighs the same. And then, three weeks ago, my instruments started lying to me. Every map I drew came out wrong. Compasses spun. Distances folded. And every wrong line pointed the same direction—into a valley that appears on no chart ever made, guarded by hedges that hum, at the center of a country that does not exist."

"That's where I live," the boy said quietly.

"That's where you lived," Ilsa corrected. "Past tense. Whatever your valley was, it's coming apart at the seams, and the Ember led me here like a salmon leading a river home. Which brings me"—she leaned forward, and let him see, for the first time, how tired she was beneath the pragmatism—"to you, Bram Okafor, and the only question that matters tonight. When I took the lantern out at the valley's edge, while you were still unconscious in the nettles, the Ember did something it has never done in two years of carrying."

"What?"

"It flared. Once. Bright enough to throw shadows through solid glass. And it flared *toward you*." She watched his face carefully. "Fire remembers what it's owed, boy. Dragons especially. So either you're the most interesting coincidence in a country of impossibilities—or you're the reason my instruments broke, the reason the wards fell, the reason nine stars went out over your head on the eve of your escape. Either way, you're not a burden I've picked up. You're a thread, and I intend to follow you to whatever's at the other end."

"A burden," he repeated. Something flickered across his face—hurt, quickly buried, the hurt of a boy who had perhaps been called that before. "You're saying I'm useful."

Ilsa looked at him: the hollow cheeks, the fisted scarred hand, the stubborn spine that had followed her half a day through unraveling country. Useful. Yes. A key fit in a lock; that didn't make it less of a key. But she heard how the word landed, and some traitorous chamber of her heart—the one that had never quite healed from the old woman in the high passes—added, unbidden: *and you know exactly what it is to be used, Vane, so mind how you use him.*

"Useful," she agreed aloud, because the truth was heavier than the lantern and she wasn't ready to carry both. "You know the valley. I know what's hunting it. That's a partnership, not charity. Eat your bread."

He ate. Outside, the dark deepened by degrees that no longer obeyed the sun. And Ilsa sat with her back to the wall, the lantern warm against her hip, and told herself the familiar lie with the familiar fluency: *this is strategy. This is a tool being used for a purpose. Nothing more.*

The Ember flickered—once, softly, like a held breath released—and for a moment she could have sworn the light bent toward the boy again, patient as a creditor, waiting to be paid.

*Tomorrow,* she thought. *Tomorrow we find out what he is.*

Above the ruined cot, where nine stars should have been, the sky was a black page awaiting ink—and somewhere beyond the hills, she could feel it in her teeth the way the boy had felt the wards, something else was moving through the Hollow March, following the same thread she was.

Corvane had found the valley too.

* * *

They walked before dawn, because Ilsa trusted the dark she chose over the dark that had chosen the valley. The boy—Bram, she supposed she'd have to get used to the name—kept pace beside her now instead of behind, which she permitted because watching him was easier than listening for him.

"Your hand," she said, as the grey came up. "The scar. It itches near the wards, doesn't it. That's why you keep it fisted."

He glanced at her sharply. "How did you—"

"Because mine itches too." She held up her own palm, crossed with the pale seam of an old surveyor's cut, and lied with the ease of long practice: "Every cartographer's does, near strong wards. It's how we find borders that don't want finding." She closed her fist. The itch crawled anyway, deeper than skin, and she did not tell him that hers had begun only three weeks ago—only since the Ember had turned north, only since the maps had started pointing here.

Only since she'd started dreaming, every night, of a sea with no ships and a door standing open in the water.

"Hold the lantern a while," she said, and passed it to him before she could think better of it. "My shoulder's cramping."

It wasn't. But she wanted to watch what the fire did when it touched him—and when his fingers closed around the pewter handle, the Ember rose in its cage of glass like a glad thing waking, and Bram Okafor stared into it with his mouth slightly open, and the light lay on his scarred palm like a signature recognizing its name.

"Well," Ilsa said, into the ringing silence. "That answers several questions I was hoping wouldn't be answered."

Far off across the blank fields, something answered her—a sound like a bell struck underwater, rolling out of the Hollow March, and every hair on her arms stood up as she recognized it, from two years of nightmares, as the call-horn of Corvane's hunters.

"They've crossed the border," she said. "Run."
