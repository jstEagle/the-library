## 5. What the Valley Forgot

The cave Ilsa had chosen for the night was less a cave than a wound in the hillside, a place where the valley's edge had begun to gnaw at itself. Bram felt it the moment they stepped inside: a thinning, like walking into a room where someone had just stopped singing. The air tasted of chalk and cold iron. Behind them, the fields of the Hollow March still glowed faintly with their borrowed light—wheat that ripened without sun, rivers that ran in perfect silver loops—but here at the margin, the light came apart. It pooled in the wrong places. Shadows leaned against shadows.

"Sit," Ilsa said, not looking up from her instruments. She had laid them out on a flat stone with the reverence of a priest arranging relics: a brass theodolite with a cracked lens, a compass whose needle spun in slow, drunken circles, a bundle of surveyor's stakes tied with frayed red cord. "And don't touch anything. Especially not that."

"That" was the lantern.

It sat apart from the other instruments, set into a hollow of stone as though she'd buried it there. It was an ugly thing, honestly—a fisherman's storm lantern, its glass smoked black, its handle wrapped in leather gone stiff with age. But the light inside it was wrong for firelight. It didn't flicker. It didn't dance. It burned the way a memory burns: steady, patient, and utterly indifferent to whether anyone was watching.

The Ember. She'd told him about it on the first night, in the flat voice of someone reciting a confession she'd rehearsed until it stopped hurting. *A shard of dragon-fire. The last light of the stars your sky used to have.* He hadn't believed her then. He was beginning to believe her now, because his scar had started to sing.

It had been itching since the night the stars went out—that old crescent on his palm, the one Mistress Halden had never explained, the one he'd had since before memory. Near the wards it had always prickled, a cat's warning of weather. But since the border fell, the itch had become something else. A pull. A tuning fork struck against bone.

He sat where she'd pointed, hugged his knees, and watched the lantern.

"You're staring at it," Ilsa said.

"It's staring back."

That earned him a glance—one eyebrow raised, the look of a woman deciding whether to be annoyed or amused. She chose neither. "It doesn't stare. It remembers." She turned back to her theodolite, squinting through the cracked lens at nothing. "Dragons don't forget, boy. That's the whole tragedy of them."

Bram uncurled his hand and looked at the scar. In the Ember's light it seemed darker than usual, the crescent standing out against his palm like a moon against a night sky. Like a *night* sky. The thought arrived whole and unwelcome, and he pushed it away.

"I want to know what you're not telling me," he said.

Ilsa laughed, short and dry. "Where would you like me to start? The list is long, and we only have until dawn."

"The lantern. Why does my hand—" He stopped. Started again. "Since we left the cottage, it's been pulling. Toward the edge. Toward *that*. What is it about me that—"

"Give me your hand."

He hesitated. She noticed, and something flickered across her face—not hurt exactly, but the ghost of it, the expression of a woman long accustomed to being the last person anyone trusted.

"I'm a disgraced cartographer with a stolen relic and a warrant in three provinces," she said. "Whatever I might do to you, the world has already done worse to me. Give me your hand."

He gave it.

Her fingers were cold and ink-stained, a cartographer's fingers, precise even when they were gentle. She turned his palm toward the lantern, and Bram felt the pull sharpen into a note—a single clear tone below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the wet dark of the inner ear. The scar's crescent seemed to widen. To open.

"No," Ilsa said softly. Not to him. To herself. "No, no, no. That's not possible."

"What? What's not possible?"

She didn't answer. She reached for the lantern instead, and Bram watched her hands shake—actually shake, this woman who had walked the Hollow March with him for two days without a tremor—as she lifted the latch and opened the little smoked door.

Light poured out.

Not firelight. Nothing so kind. It was the color of the sky on the last night before winter, deep blue shot through with white, and it moved the way starlight moves if starlight could move—deliberately, anciently, like something turning over in a sleep centuries long. The cave filled with it. The chalk walls lit up. And the light bent, all of it, every ray of it, bending across the cave like iron filings to a lodestone, bending toward—

Toward Bram's open hand.

The scar drank it. There was no other word for what happened. The crescent on his palm caught the Ember's light and held it, and held it, and the pull in his bones resolved into recognition, into *welcome*, into the terrible intimacy of a lock finding the key cut for it. Warmth spread up his arm. Not burning. Worse than burning: *knowing*. For half a heartbeat Bram knew things he had no words for—the weight of wings, the taste of high cold air, the sound a mountain makes when it agrees to move—and then the moment passed, and he was only a boy in a cave with a shaking woman and a lantern full of impossible light.

He snatched his hand back. The light snapped off like a door closing. The Ember dimmed to its patient ember-glow, and the cave went ordinary again, and Bram discovered he was breathing hard.

"What did you do?" His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "What did *I* do?"

Ilsa Vane sat down slowly on her instrument case, the way old soldiers sit down, all at once, as if their legs have made the decision without consulting them. She looked at him for a long time. Outside, somewhere far off, the dying wards made a sound like a bell heard through water.

"Say the word 'ember' for me," she said.

"What?"

"Just say it. Slowly."

"*Ember.*"

"And 'star.' Say 'star.'"

"*Star.*"

"Now hold out your hand again and say 'sundered.'"

He almost refused. Pride, fear, some animal instinct older than both—he almost stood up and walked out of that cave and back into the valley and its warm lying light. But the pull was still there, faint under his skin, and curiosity is a hunger that outlasts nearly everything else. He held out his hand.

"*Sundered.*"

The Ember flared. The scar drank. And this time, when the knowing came, it brought words with it—an old tongue he had never learned, syllables that fit his mouth like they'd been cast from a mold of it, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that these were not words he was remembering.

They were words he had *inherited*.

He pulled his hand back and pressed it against his chest as if he could smother the scar there, as if it were a coal that could be banked. "Stop it," he whispered. "Make it stop."

"I can't," Ilsa said. Her voice had changed. All the dryness had gone out of it; what remained was quiet and level and very careful, the voice of a woman walking across ice she has already heard cracking. "Bram. Listen to me. The Ember answers to one bloodline only. One family. The family that paid for it. The family that—" She stopped. Started again, gentler. "The founders of this valley didn't just come here. Do you understand? They couldn't have. No one *finds* a country that isn't on any map. They *made* it that way. They hired the dragon. They paid it in heart-fire—the last of its fire, the last light of the true stars—to cut this valley out of the world and stitch the wound closed behind them. The Sundering. And the blood that sealed the bargain—"

"Don't."

"—runs in your veins. Your scar isn't a scar, Bram. It's a signature. You are descended from the Sunderers."

The silence after was the loudest thing Bram had ever heard.

He shook his head. Once, twice, mechanically, like a man trying to dislodge water from his ear. "No. You're wrong. My mother was a hedge-witch's daughter from Millbrook. My father—" He faltered, because here was the hole, the gap he had never been able to see around: he had no father. Mistress Halden had always said *your father died before you were born*, and when he'd asked how, or where, or what his name was, she had looked at him with such tired sorrow that he'd learned to stop asking. "My father died before I was born," he said, and heard how it sounded—how it had always sounded, if he'd ever truly listened. A sentence with nothing behind it. A wall painted to look like a door.

"Bram—"

"He *died*." Louder now, and the Ember flared at the anger in his voice, and that was worse, that was unbearable, the light leaning toward him like a dog toward its master's whistle. "I grew up in that cottage. She taught me letters. She taught me which mushrooms will kill you and which ones taste like chicken and she sang to me when I had fever and she—" His throat closed. When he got it open, the words came out flat and dead. "She never once told me where I came from. Twenty years. Twenty years and I thought the worst secret in that house was whatever she kept locked in the cellar."

"Maybe it was kindness," Ilsa said. "Maybe she was protecting you."

"From *what*?"

"From this." Ilsa gestured—at the cave, the Ember, the thinning air, the whole unraveling margin of the world. "From being the heir to a lie. From carrying the key to a cage. Because that's what you are, Bram. Don't look at me like that—I'm not the one who built it. But the wards, the borders, the forgetting—all of it runs on the founders' blood. Yours. You're not a citizen of this valley. You're part of its architecture."

*Part of its architecture.* The phrase went into him like a splinter driven under a nail.

He thought of his life, all of it, laid out suddenly like one of Ilsa's maps: the cottage, the lessons, the scar that itched near the edges, the way strangers in the market sometimes looked at him a half-second too long and then forgot why. The way he had never once, in twenty years, been able to walk past the third milestone on the north road without turning back. He'd thought it was habit. He'd thought it was *home*.

It was the leash.

"They erased themselves from the world," he said slowly, working it through, needing to say it aloud to believe it, "and then they erased themselves from *me*. Gave me a fake mother. A fake history. A fake—" He stopped. Laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Is my name even mine? Is *Bram Okafor* real, or did somebody write me onto a page the way you'd write a river onto a map?"

"I don't know," Ilsa said. "I genuinely don't. Names have power here—more than anywhere else in creation, maybe. If the wards needed an heir, they may have needed a name to hang the heirship on." She paused. "Or Halden named you herself, and loved you anyway, and the two things were never in conflict. People are capable of that. It's usually the only thing they're capable of."

"Don't defend her."

"I'm not defending her. I'm defending the possibility that love and lies can live in the same house. Believe me, I know the rent is ruinous."

He wanted to hate her for saying it. He wanted to hate everyone—for Halden, for the founders, for the dragon, for the valley itself with its soft green lie of a sky. Most of all he wanted to hate the scar, and he stared down at his own palm, at the crescent that had drunk starlight, and wished with a violence that surprised him that he could peel it off like bark.

But underneath the wanting, quieter, colder, another thought had already arrived and taken a chair by the fire:

*If I am the architecture, then I am also the architect.*

*If my blood holds the wards shut, my blood can open them.*

*Whatever was done, can be undone.*

The Ember pulsed once in its lantern—slow, vast, patient, like a heart counting centuries—and Bram had the sudden, skin-crawling certainty that it had heard the thought. That it agreed.

Ilsa saw his face change. Cartographers notice borders shifting; it is the whole of their trade. "Bram," she said carefully. "What are you thinking?"

He looked up at her. Beyond her, through the mouth of the cave, the valley lay glowing under its false and fading sky, every field and rooftop lit by light that was only a memory of light, and somewhere in it, Wren's mother was putting a starless child to bed, and Old Sef was mending nets for a sea he half-remembered, and none of them knew that the ground under their lives was a page in someone else's book.

"I'm thinking," Bram said, "that if my family built the cage—"

Far off in the dark, a horn sounded. Long, low, wrong—a note with no music in it, the sound the wards made when they were searching for something. Then a second horn answered it, closer, from the ridge above the cave.

Ilsa was on her feet, snuffing the lantern, gathering instruments with hands that had stopped shaking because there was no longer time for shaking. "Wardens," she breathed. "They've crossed the March. They're tracking *you*, boy—they'll have felt the Ember answer your blood from a mile off."

Bram closed his fist over his scar, over the warmth still sleeping in it, and followed her into the dark.

"—then I'm going to find out what else it opens."
