## 6. The Company of the Unmapped

The valley had never seemed so small as it did in the three days after the stars went out.

Bram felt it the way a bird feels the walls of a cage it has only just noticed: every hedgerow, every crooked lane, every familiar chimney-smoke became a line drawn around him. He and Ilsa Vane walked the old drover's road south with the lantern swinging between them, and inside its smoked glass the Ember burned with a light that was not quite fire—more like the memory of fire, amber shot through with veins of red that moved when you weren't looking directly at them.

"Three people," Ilsa said, for perhaps the tenth time since dawn. "You want to gather *three people* before we do anything else."

"Not anyone. Specific people." Bram counted them off on his fingers, the way he'd counted them in his head all night while sleep refused him. "Tam Ashdown, who used to keep the border wards on the north ridge until they exiled her for asking what was on the other side. Old Sef, who fishes the mere and talks about the sea like he's actually seen one. And—" his voice caught, and he covered it by adjusting the lantern strap, "—Wren. The Halloway baby. Born the night the stars went out."

"A child." Ilsa said it flatly, but Bram had spent two days learning her flatness, and underneath it he heard the question she wouldn't ask outright: *are you mad?*

"She's not just a child. She was born the exact hour the wards failed. Midwife swore the room went dark as a pocket and then Wren opened her eyes and there was light coming off her skin, faint as foxfire, for a full minute. Everyone in Hollowmere says it. They say it quietly, because saying it loudly gets you a visit from the wardens, but they say it."

"And you believe a village ghost story because—"

"Because my palm itched the whole time Ilsa told me about it, is why." He held up his right hand. In the grey morning light the scar was a pale crescent across the heel of his thumb, old as memory—he'd had it since birth, Mistress Halden said, though she'd never said how. It had itched near the wards his whole life. Now it itched constantly, a low insistent hum, worse when the Ember was near. "It knows things before I do. It's been pulling me south since the night the sky went black. And it pulled hardest when I thought about her."

Ilsa looked at his hand for a long moment, then at the lantern, where the Ember's veins of red had quickened, almost eagerly.

"Well," she said. "That's either a very good sign or a very bad one. Let's go find out which."

* * *

They found Tam Ashdown exactly where exile had left her: in a leaning hut of driftwood and scrap iron at the edge of the Mirefen, surrounded by the carcasses of ward-tinkering, half-dissected charms hanging from the rafters like gutted fish.

She answered the door with a crossbow.

"Put it down, Tam, it's Bram Okafor. Halden's boy."

"I know who it is." She didn't lower the crossbow. She was younger than Bram expected—thirty, maybe, with iron-grey streaking her black hair and burn scars laddering both forearms—and her eyes went past him to Ilsa, to the lantern, and narrowed. "I know who *that* is, too. Cartographer. You came through here four years ago with royal instruments and a royal warrant, asking questions about the north ridge. Questions are how I ended up out here."

"I remember," Ilsa said. "You asked what the wards were hiding. I asked what the maps were hiding. We were both punished for it."

"You got disgraced. I got *exiled*. There's a difference in degree, not kind." But the crossbow dipped an inch. Her eyes had found the lantern again, and something moved through her face that Bram recognized from his own mirror these last days: hunger fighting terror. "Is that—open the shutter. Just a crack. I have to see."

Bram glanced at Ilsa. Ilsa shrugged, which he had learned meant *your funeral*, and slid the smoked pane aside.

Light poured out—not bright, not warm exactly, but *true*, the way nothing in the valley had felt true since the stars vanished. Tam made a sound like a woman being told a death and a birth in the same breath. The crossbow clattered to the floorboards.

"That's dragon-fire," she whispered. "That's actual—gods below, that's the real thing. Do you know what I've been working with? Valley magic. Ward-magic. It isn't magic at all, not really—it's a *recollection* of magic, a copy of a copy, and the copies have been degrading for generations and nobody will admit it because admitting it means—" She stopped herself, jaw tight. "Why are you here? Nobody visits the exile unless they want something the honest folk won't give them."

"We're going to the edge of the valley," Bram said. "Past it, if we can. And we need someone who understands the wards—someone who understands them well enough to know how they break."

Silence. Somewhere in the rafters a dissected charm ticked as it cooled.

"They'll call it treason," Tam said at last. "Asking questions got me the fen. This would get me the hollow. That's what they do with traitors—they put them in the hollow under the warden-house, where the wards run thickest, and you stay there until you forget your own name, because the wards eat memories first. Everyone knows. Nobody says."

"I know," Bram said. "My master told me. Right before I ran."

Something shifted in Tam's face—the first crack, the first softening. "Halden told you? Old Halden, who taught half the hedge-witches in this valley to keep their mouths shut and their heads down?" She laughed, short and disbelieving. "Then either she's changed, or she wanted you gone. Either way." She looked at the lantern a long moment. Then she turned back into the hut and began pulling tools from hooks—a punch, a spool of copper wire, a leather roll that clinked. "Well. I've spent four years asking questions to a fen that doesn't answer. Might as well go ask them somewhere the answers can actually kill me."

* * *

Old Sef was harder.

They found him on the shore of the Grey Mere at dusk, mending nets by touch more than sight, his boat drawn up on the mud like a tired animal. He was older than old—older than the warden-captain, older than the oldest gravestone in Hollowmere, though no one could say by how much—and his hands were a topography of rope-burns and healed splits. When Bram said his name, Sef didn't look up.

"Halden's boy. Heard you ran. Heard the sky went out the same night." He tied off a knot. "People say those things happened together. People say a lot of things."

"They're true. Both of them."

"Are they." Sef set down the net at last and looked up, and his eyes were the pale washed-out blue of water over sand. "And what do you want with an old man, boy? Come to hear stories? The wardens don't like my stories. Say they unsettle folk. Say the sea isn't real, never was, that I'm a confused old man who dreamed it and won't let the dream go." His voice didn't bitter; it just went quiet, which was somehow worse. "Forty years they've been telling me the sea isn't real. Forty years. And I *remember* it, boy. I remember the smell of it. Salt and weed and something wild underneath. You can't dream a smell like that."

"The sea is real," Bram said. "I've seen proof. The valley itself is the lie."

Sef went very still. A curlew called somewhere out over the mere, and the sound seemed to come from very far away, from another country—which, Bram thought, it did.

"Say that again," Sef said.

So Bram said it. All of it—the wards failing, the stars going out, Ilsa and her instruments that refused to record the valley, the Ember in the lantern, the scar on his hand that knew things. He talked until the light died off the water and the first untrue stars—the painted ones, the ward-lights, dimmer than they'd ever been—flickered up over the fen. Sef listened without moving except once, when Bram mentioned the Sundered Sea, and then his hands closed slowly into fists on his knees.

"My grandfather's grandfather came from the sea," Sef said finally. "Family always said it like a joke. *From the sea, like a fish.* But he had a tattoo, boy. An anchor, here—" he touched his own wrist, the place where the ink would have been, long faded or never there, "—and he'd stand on this shore some nights looking east like a man looking at a door that used to be a door. My mother said homesickness. For what? He'd never seen anywhere but the mere." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "For *this*. He was homesick for this. Because this wasn't home. This was the place they hid him."

"Yes," Bram said softly.

Sef stood. It took him a while—old joints, old bones—but when he was up, he seemed taller than he had any right to be, and he began stowing his nets with brisk, furious purpose.

"Where's the boat going?" Ilsa asked.

"Nowhere. Boat stays. Boat's part of the lie too, isn't it—a fisherman on a pond, playing at the sea." He spat, precisely, into the water. "I'm done playing. Where do we walk?"

* * *

Hollowmere after dark was a different village than Hollowmere by day. The ward-lights overhead gave everything a submarine pallor, and doors were shut early now, shutters drawn, because everyone had heard the same rumor: the lights were failing, the edges of things were getting thin, and out past the mill-race there was a field that had gone *blank*—not dark, not empty, but absent, a hole in the world shaped like a field. Bram had seen it on their way through. He still couldn't describe it. His eyes kept sliding off it, refusing to hold the absence, and his scar had screamed the whole time they skirted it.

Wren lived in the last house before the blankness, with her grandmother, in a cottage whose garden grew strange and lush, as if the soil knew something the family didn't.

The grandmother met them at the gate with a fire-iron in her fist and Wren behind her skirts, peering out—six years old, solemn-eyed, hair the color of dark honey, and around her, faintly, unmistakably, even in the dark, a shimmer. Not light, exactly. More like heat-haze in winter. Like the air remembered her differently than it remembered other things.

"No," the grandmother said, before Bram had finished two sentences. "Whatever you're selling, whatever trouble you're carrying, no. They already watch this house. Warden-captain comes by every month, asks her questions, looks at her like she's a specimen. You think I'll hand her to strangers with a lantern full of—of *that*?" Her eyes had found the Ember and gone wide with old, inherited fear.

"Grandmama," Wren said. "Let them talk."

The grandmother's grip tightened on the fire-iron. Then, slowly, she stepped aside—because, Bram realized, watching the girl step forward out of the shadows, because Wren had asked, and in this house Wren's asking carried weight no six-year-old's should.

Wren stopped in front of the lantern and looked up at it. At the Ember. And the Ember—Bram saw it happen, saw Ilsa see it happen—*leaned*. The flames inside the glass bent toward the girl like stems toward sun.

"Hello," Wren said to it. Politely. As if greeting a shy animal.

The Ember flared, once, gently, gold flooding the garden, and every strange lush plant in it turned toward the light.

"What is it saying?" Bram asked, before he could stop himself, because it was obvious—obvious—that it was saying something, that the girl heard it the way he heard rain.

"It's sad," Wren said simply. "It's been alone a long time. Longer than Grandmama. Longer than the house." She frowned, concentrating. "It says it used to be part of something big. Something in the sky. And somebody put it in a little box so it wouldn't be lonely, but a box isn't the sky, is it." She looked up at Bram, and her eyes were ancient and six years old at once. "It says you're going to take it home. Is that true?"

Bram knelt. Up close, the shimmer around her was stronger, and his scar sang like a struck wire.

"I'm going to try," he said. "But it's dangerous. The wardens will come after us. The road goes through places that are coming apart. I shouldn't ask a child—"

"I'm not a child," Wren said, with the absolute conviction of the very young and the very correct. "I'm the only one born right. Everybody else was born *before*, when the lie was still working. I'm the only one who's never been fooled." She held out her hand toward the lantern, small and steady. "It wants to come with us. And Grandmama always says you don't leave a creature alone in the dark if you can help it."

The grandmother made a sound that was half sob, half surrender. "She's been waiting for you," she said roughly. "Since the night she was born. Telling me someone would come, someone with a mark on his hand. I thought it was dreams. Children dream." She wiped her face with her apron. "Take the food bag. Take the good cloak. And if anything happens to her, hedge-witch's boy, I will find whatever is left of you and haunt it."

* * *

They camped that night in the ruins of the old drover's chapel, halfway between Hollowmere and the south road, because Tam said the wards there had decayed enough that the wardens' scrying skipped over it like a tongue over a missing tooth.

It should have been miserable. Cold ground, thin blankets, the blank field a mile off humming its terrible absence into the dark. Instead Bram lay awake listening to the sounds of the company settling in around him—Tam muttering over her instruments, recalibrating a compass that kept trying to point at the Ember instead of north; Sef telling the low, rolling story of a storm at sea to Wren, who had fallen asleep against his shoulder halfway through and whom he hadn't moved; Ilsa sharpening her surveyor's knife and pretending not to listen to the story, and failing, her head tilted toward Sef's voice like a plant toward light.

A tinkerer who'd been thrown away for curiosity. An old man who'd been told his whole life was a mistake. A child the darkness had marked and the darkness had gifted. A disgraced mapmaker and a hedge-witch's boy with a scar that sang.

None of them belonged. That was the thing Bram understood, lying there under the failing painted stars. None of them fit the valley, and so the valley had pushed them to its edges, and here at the edges they had found each other, and it was—

It was the first time in his life he hadn't felt alone, and it had taken the end of the world to manage it.

He must have made some sound, because Ilsa glanced over. "Sleep, Okafor. Tomorrow we walk."

"Toward what?"

"Toward the Gate of Return." She said it carefully, testing the words. "If the old records are right—if anything in this valley's records is right—there's a gate in the southern hills where the founders came in. Sealed, presumably. But sealed things can be unsealed, and you're the key, whether you like it or not." She paused. "Get some rest. Keys need their sleep."

Bram smiled in the dark. Around him his strange, impossible, brand-new family breathed and murmured and kept watch, and above them the false stars flickered like candles in a draft, and far off to the south, unseen, the hills waited.

He was nearly asleep when Tam sat bolt upright.

"Listen," she whispered.

Bram listened. At first: wind, Wren's breathing, the Ember's faint crackle. Then, beneath it, threading up the south road through the dark—a sound he knew from village warnings, from childhood cautions, from every story mothers told to keep children home.

Hooves. Many hooves. Moving fast.

The wardens had found them.

"*Everyone up,*" Ilsa said quietly, and the company of the unmapped rose together into the dark.
