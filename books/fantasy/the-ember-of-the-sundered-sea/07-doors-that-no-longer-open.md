## 7. Doors That No Longer Open

Ilsa Vane had spent eleven years learning to distrust doors.

It began in the royal archive at Corvane's court, where she had discovered that every map is a kind of lie agreed upon by powerful people, and it had ended—she had thought—in a cell, in disgrace, in the long gray years of being nobody. But the habit of suspicion had stayed with her like an old scar that ached before rain. So when Tam led them up the eastern spine of the valley toward the Gate of Return, and the path grew too smooth, too deliberate, too *welcoming*, Ilsa felt her scar ache in sympathy.

"Who maintains this road?" she asked, crouching to press two fingers against the flagstones. They were warm. Not sun-warm—the day was overcast, the sky above the valley still a lidless gray where stars should have been—but warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. "Nobody walks here. The grass hasn't been cut back from the edges. And yet."

"And yet," Tam said. He walked with his tools slung across his back in a leather harness he'd built himself, forty small instruments for measuring things that were not supposed to be measurable, and he touched each of them absently as he talked, the way another man might worry a rosary. "The valley keeps its own roads. Or the wards do. A road that wants to be walked is a road that wants you somewhere."

"That's either very reassuring or very sinister."

"I've never managed to decide which." He smiled, but it was a thin smile, stretched over something older. Tam had been exiled from the tinkerers' quarter for asking questions—Ilsa had gathered this much in three days of travel—and she recognized the look of a man who had asked one question too many and been answered with silence. She knew that silence intimately. It was the sound a kingdom made when you looked at its maps too closely.

Behind them came Old Sef, leaning on his fish-gaff as though it were a pilgrim's staff, humming something tuneless and tidal. Wren rode on his shoulders, four years old and solemn as a magistrate, her small hands knotted in his gray hair. The child had not spoken since morning, which was unusual; Wren spoke the way rain fell, constantly and without apparent design. Her silence now made Ilsa uneasy in a way she could not name.

And Bram walked at the rear, because he always did, as though he were guarding them from himself.

Ilsa glanced back at him. Three days since the Ember had flared gold at the touch of his blood, three days since she had watched the color drain out of his face as she told him what she'd realized: *You're descended from the Sunderers. Your blood opened their lock.* Three days, and he had not once mentioned it again, which worried her more than weeping would have. Grief that goes quiet goes deep. She had buried enough of her own to know how it dug.

The road climbed through birch country, white trunks standing in the mist like the ribs of some enormous patient animal. The air smelled of cold stone and, faintly, of iron—a smell Ilsa associated with old fortifications, with gates, with the architecture of fear. She checked her instruments as they walked. The brass compass in her pocket spun slowly, uselessly, as it had since the night the stars went out. But the barometer held steady, and the little silver hypsometer she'd rigged from a broken theodolite gave her elevation readings that were almost sane, and this, more than anything, convinced her they were approaching something real. The valley's interior refused measurement. Its edges, apparently, remembered being measured once.

"The Gate of Return," Sef said suddenly, from under Wren. His voice had the quality of a man reading from a page only he could see. "My grandmother sang about it. *The door that opens outward, the door our mothers barred.* I thought it was a lullaby." He shifted the child higher on his shoulders. "You don't put a door in a lullaby unless it matters."

"People put everything in lullabies," Ilsa said. "That's how you smuggle truth past the people who would burn it. Nobody burns a lullaby."

Tam snorted. "The tinkerers' guild burned three of my songs."

"Then your songs were better than you thought."

They crested the ridge near midday, and there it was.

* * *

The Gate of Return stood in a saddle between two hills, and it was—there was no other word for it—magnificent.

Two pillars of veined white stone, each taller than three men, crowned with capitals carved into shapes that might have been waves or wings or hands, depending on the angle of the light. Between them, a lintel of dark basalt bearing an inscription so worn that only the deepest grooves survived, letters like the bones of a language. And beneath the lintel, filling the space between the pillars entirely, a wall.

Not rubble. Not collapse. A wall—fitted stones laid with loving precision, mortared with something that gleamed faintly silver when the light crossed it, seamless as the shell of an egg. Someone had built it from the inside. Someone had wanted it perfect.

Ilsa walked the length of it slowly, her cartographer's eye cataloguing without her permission: the masonry style, consistent with work perhaps eight or nine generations old; the absence of scaffolding scars on the outer face, meaning it had been raised from within; the way the silver mortar followed the joints in unbroken lines, meaning it had been laid by someone with time and skill and no fear of interruption. No siege builds like this. Sieges are hasty, ugly, functional. This was devotion.

"This wasn't done in panic," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded strange in the gate's acoustic shadow, flattened, private. "This took months. Maybe years. They had time to think about it the whole while, and they kept building."

"They sealed themselves in?" Bram had come up beside her. Up close, in the flat gray light, he looked younger than his twenty-odd years and older than them both at once. He put his palm flat against the wall, and Ilsa saw him wince—the scar, the itching scar, the one that woke near the wards. "Why would anyone—"

"Why does anyone build a wall?" Ilsa traced the silver seam with one fingertip. Cold. Deeply, unnaturally cold, colder than the stone deserved, as though the mortar were drinking heat out of the world. "To keep something out. Or to keep something in. And sometimes—" she hesitated, because the next word was a cruel one, "—sometimes both, and sometimes the thing inside doesn't get asked."

Bram pulled his hand back as though the wall had bitten him.

"It's keyed to blood," Tam said quietly. He had unpacked three of his instruments and set them on a flat stone, and he was watching the smallest of them—a needle suspended in oil, trembling—with an expression Ilsa had learned to read as alarm. "Look. The needle points at Bram no matter how I turn the housing. It did that at the valley's edge too, but weaker. This wall and those wards are the same craft. Same hand, maybe. Same family, certainly." He looked up, and his eyes were kind and terrible. "Your ancestors didn't just cast the valley out of the world, lad. They locked the door behind them. From the inside. With themselves on this side of it."

The wind moved through the birches below, a long shivering sigh, and for a moment nobody spoke.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the western pillar, because there was nowhere else and because, Ilsa admitted privately, none of them wanted to leave. There was something about the gate that demanded witness. Even Wren, who had found a game involving stacking pebbles on Sef's boots, kept glancing at the wall with the fixed, unblinking attention children gave to things that mattered.

Ilsa sat with her back against the white stone and drew.

She drew the way other people prayed. The pencil moved and the world became lines, and lines became sense, and sense became something she could carry. She sketched the gate from four angles, then the wall, then the inscription—or what remained of it—and it was while rendering the inscription's broken letters that her pencil stopped.

"Bram."

He looked up from the fire he was feeding. The Ember hung at his hip in its black lantern, and even banked it threw a light that was not quite light, a warmth that reached the eye before the skin. In the days since the valley's stars had gone out, the Ember had become the only fixed point in a landscape gone soft at the edges. People oriented by it. Ilsa caught herself doing it now, angling her sketchpad toward its glow.

"Come look at this. Tell me if I'm imagining it."

He crouched beside her, and she turned the pad so the firelight fell across the letters she'd reconstructed. She had worked from the deepest grooves, the ones erosion hadn't reached, and filled the gaps by inference the way she'd been trained at court—context, grammar, repetition. Nine words survived whole or nearly whole.

*We bar it gladly. We bar it in love. Forgive.*

Bram read it twice. His lips moved on the second pass.

"*Forgive,*" he said. "Forgive who?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Ilsa tapped the last word. "Forgive us, for what we're about to do? Forgive them, whoever they were, for making it necessary? Forgive the ones left outside?" She shook her head. "Inscriptions are contracts, Bram. Somebody stood right here with a chisel and decided these exact words were what they wanted the future to know. And the future they chose to address—" she looked up at him, and hated what she was about to say, and said it anyway, because she had spent eleven years learning that kindness delayed was cruelty compounded, "—the future they addressed is you."

He didn't flinch. That was the worst part. He had gone somewhere past flinching, into a flat quiet country she recognized from her own worst years.

"My mother used to sing me a song about a door," he said at last. His voice was level, careful, a man walking on ice. "Mistress Halden, I mean. She wasn't—it doesn't matter what she was. The song had a line: *the door is shut but not unkind.* I used to think it meant the cottage door. That she'd always leave it open for me." He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Now I think every adult I've ever loved has been singing me the same song. Shut door. Not unkind. As if that's supposed to help."

"Bram—"

"Don't." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Please. I'm not angry at you. You're the only person who's told me the truth since the stars went out. I just—" The stick snapped in his fingers. He stared at the halves. "I need to not be spoken to kindly for a minute. I need to stand in front of this wall and be furious at people who've been dead for two hundred years, and I can't do that if someone's being gentle at me."

So Ilsa did the hardest thing she had learned in her disgraced, map-haunted life. She picked up her pencil and let him be furious alone, three feet away, in full view, and she drew the wall instead of comforting him, and she understood—as the lines accumulated—that she was drawing a cage. A beautiful cage. A cage with an apology carved over its lintel. And she thought: *this is what my kingdom did to me, except they never bothered with the apology.*

* * *

Wren woke in the blue hour before dawn, and screamed.

Not a nightmare scream—Ilsa had heard those, from refugee camps, from her own throat in bad years. This was sharper, startled, the scream of a child who has put her hand on something hot. Ilsa was moving before she was fully awake, knife out, heart slamming, and found the girl standing at the wall with both palms pressed flat against the fitted stones, her whole small body rigid.

"Wren." Sef got there first—he was faster than a man his age had any right to be, and he swept her up and away from the wall as though lifting her out of surf. "Little fish, little fish, what is it, what did it do?"

"It's sad," Wren said, and burst into tears.

The camp came fully awake around those two words. Tam was up with a lamp, Bram with the Ember, and the lantern's glow washed the wall in shifting amber, and Isla saw—everyone saw—that where the child's hands had touched it, the silver mortar was glowing. Faintly. Fading even as they watched, like ripples closing over a thrown stone.

"It's sad," Wren repeated into Sef's shoulder, hiccupping. "It's been sad for ever and ever and nobody listens to it. It wants to be a door. Doors are supposed to open. It remembers opening." She lifted her head, tear-streaked and ferocious, and pointed one small finger at the wall. "It remembers *before.*"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Tam broke it, very quietly. "The wards are dying. We've known that since the stars went out. Everything the valley is made of—roads, weather, memory, walls—is running down like a clock with no winding key." He approached the wall the way he'd approach a wounded animal, lamp held high. "But a dying ward doesn't grieve. A dying mechanism just stops." He laid his palm where Wren's had been, and nothing happened, and he nodded as though confirming a diagnosis. "It answered *her.* Star-born. Born the night the sky went dark. Whatever's woven into this valley, she's woven into it too—more tightly than any of us. She's not hearing a mechanism." He turned, and the lamplight made hollows of his face. "She's hearing the Sundering itself. The spell. And the spell is sorry."

"The spell is sorry," Sef repeated. He had gone gray. He lowered Wren gently to the ground, keeping one huge hand on her back. "Grandmother's song. The third verse. I never understood it." He closed his eyes, and sang, low and rough, a fisherman's voice cracking on the old words:

*"We shut the door with tears upon it,*
*we shut the door with love inside it,*
*and oh, the knocking, oh, the knocking,*
*of all the hands we left behind it."*

Nobody moved. Above them the starless sky pressed down like a held breath, and the Ember in its lantern pulsed—slowly, twice, three times—like something sleeping turning over.

Bram stepped forward. He walked past all of them, past the fire, past the instruments and the sketches and the small crying child, until he stood directly before the wall, close enough that his breath fogged the ancient stone. And Ilsa, watching, saw him raise his scarred palm and hold it there—not touching, hovering a hair's breadth from the surface, the way you'd hover a hand over a sleeping face to feel whether it still breathed.

"For what?" he asked the wall. His voice cracked on the second word, and he steadied it, and asked again. "*For what? What did you see out there that was worth this?*"

The mortar did not glow. The wall did not answer. But the wind rose then, sudden and specific, funneling through the gate's pillars from the east—from outside, from beyond the valley's edge, from the direction of the real world—and it carried a smell Ilsa had not smelled in eleven years. Salt. Open water. The Sundered Sea, miles distant and unreachable, sending its breath through a crack in a cage built with love.

And beneath the salt, so faint that only Wren turned her head toward it, something else. A sound like a heartbeat. Like a heartbeat coming from the wrong side of the wall.

Tam's needle-instrument, forgotten on its stone, swung hard to the east and began, very softly, to ring.
