## 8. The Tinkerer's Testimony

Tam had always believed that everything could be taken apart.

It was the first lesson his father taught him at the workbench in Hollow March, before the exile, before the questions that cost him everything: *Anything made can be unmade, and anything unmade can be understood.* He had spent thirty years living inside that sentence like a house. He had taken apart clocks and locks and singing-chairs and the little brass orrery in the schoolhouse that turned without being wound. He had taken apart a warden's lantern once, when he was nineteen and reckless, and found nothing inside it but a sliver of glass that glowed because everyone agreed it should glow.

That was the night he began asking the wrong questions.

Now he sat on a fallen log at the edge of a meadow that was forgetting how to be a meadow, with a fire crackling in front of him that he did not entirely trust, and five strangers—no, not strangers anymore, not after the crossing at Miller's Ford, not after Sef's hand on his shoulder in the dark—and he was about to take apart the only home any of them had ever known.

The girl, Wren, slept against Old Sef's coat, her breath small and even. The fisherman himself sat sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, the way men do when their hands need something to lie to them. Ilsa Vane stood at the meadow's edge with her instruments laid out on a cloth, taking readings of a border that no longer held its shape. And Bram—the boy, the heir, the walking key with the itching scar—sat closest to the fire, watching Tam with the particular hunger of someone who has just learned his whole life is a sentence written by other people and wants, desperately, to read it for himself.

"Tell me," Bram said. "All of it. You said you'd explain what's happening to the valley."

Tam looked at his own hands. Tinkerer's hands: scarred knuckles, a missing half-finger on the left from a gear-press, grease worked so deep into the creases it would outlive him. Hands that had never once been afraid of the insides of things.

Until now.

"All right," he said. "But you won't thank me for it."

* * *

"I was born in Hollow March," he began, "which most of you know, and which none of you know, if you understand me. It's a town of tinkerers and ward-smiths. We keep the valley running—or we kept it running, back when there was something to run. My father repaired the boundary stones. Did you know there were boundary stones? Most folk don't. They walk past them every day of their lives and see nothing but mossy rocks, because the wards make sure they see nothing. My father saw them because he was one of the ones who maintained the seeing-not."

Ilsa looked up from her instruments. "Ward-smiths are a myth," she said. "In the capital, they say the valley has no craft guilds. No records. Nothing."

"We have records," Tam said. "We have wonderful records. That's rather the problem." He pulled a folded square of oilcloth from his coat and unwrapped it with the care of a man handling a relic, though what lay inside was only a tool—a slender brass probe tipped with a lens no bigger than his thumbnail. "This was my father's. A truth-lens. It shows you what a thing is made of, underneath whatever it's pretending to be. Point it at a lock, you see the tumblers. Point it at a lying man—" he shrugged— "well. You see the lie."

He held it up toward the fire.

Through the lens, the flames were wrong. Not fire at all, but a slow golden shimmer, like light through honey, like the idea of fire remembered by someone who had seen it once, long ago, and was doing their best.

"The valley runs on memory," Tam said. "Not metaphor. Mechanism. Every working thing here—every hearth that lights itself, every crop that ripens true, every river that stays in its banks—is powered by the wards, and the wards don't create anything. They *remember* things. Somewhere at the center of this country there is a great engine—I've never seen it, no one I know has ever seen it, but my father called it the Loom—and the Loom holds the memory of how the world works. Sunlight. Rain. Fire. Seasons. And it plays those memories over us, endlessly, the way a music box plays its one song."

"That's not possible," Sef said quietly. But he said it the way a man says a thing he has suspected for forty years.

"No," Tam agreed. "It isn't. That's rather the point. None of this is possible. That's why it takes so much work to keep it going."

* * *

He told them about the maintenance. About the crews who walked the boundary stones each season, feeding them drops of blood and songs whose words no one remembered the meaning of. About the weather-houses in the high hills where old women sat all day remembering rain, because the Loom's memory of rain had gone thin in places and needed reinforcing. About the year the winter lasted three weeks too long and the ward-smiths worked themselves gray trying to remember spring hard enough.

"You thought the valley was blessed," he said. "Fertile beyond reason. Peaceful beyond nature. It is. It's fertile the way a painting of an orchard is fertile. Beautiful. Convincing. And completely dependent on the painter."

Bram's face had gone pale in the firelight. "Mistress Halden's cottage," he said slowly. "The garden. It never had weeds. In twenty years I never pulled a single weed, and I never once wondered why."

"Because wondering is discouraged," Tam said. "Gently. Kindly. The wards don't just remember sunlight, boy. They remember *contentment*. They hold the whole country in a kind of agreement. Everyone agrees the valley is good. Everyone agrees the edge is dangerous. Everyone agrees not to wonder too loudly. And the ones who wonder anyway—" he tapped his own chest— "get exiled. Or worse."

"What happened to your father?" Wren asked. She wasn't asleep after all; she was watching him with those starless eyes that seemed to drink in every word and give nothing back.

"He asked whether the Loom had an off switch," Tam said. "Out loud. At a guild meeting." He let that sit in the firelight for a moment. "There's no grave. There's no record. As far as the valley is concerned, Aldous Tam was never born—which, given what I'm about to tell you, may be more literally true than any of us would like."

* * *

"Now," he said, and his voice caught, and he hated it, hated the tremor in a tinkerer's voice the way he'd hate a rattle in a finished clock. "Now the part that matters. Now the part about why the meadow behind you is coming apart at the seams."

He pointed with the truth-lens. Beyond the fire, past the log line, the meadow stretched silver-gray under the light of no moon. But through the lens, whole patches of it simply weren't. Not dark—dark would be something. Blank. Absent. Grass that had forgotten to be grass, earth that had forgotten to be earth, holes in the world shaped exactly like the world used to be.

"It started the night the stars went out," Tam said. "You felt it, Bram—you told us. The wards shuddering. The whole valley ringing like a struck bell. Here's what that bell was saying, if you knew how to listen: *the Loom is failing.*"

"Why?" Ilsa asked. She had abandoned her instruments entirely now and come to sit across the fire from him, the Ember-lantern hanging at her hip, its light steady and red and patient as a heartbeat. "What do stars have to do with it?"

"Everything." Tam wrapped both hands around his knees. "Think about what a star is. Out there—in the real world, the world we were cut out of—a star is a thing that burns whether anyone looks at it or not. It doesn't need belief. It doesn't need maintenance. It's the most honest object there is. And the Loom—" he laughed, a short bitter sound— "the Loom's memories of stars were the oldest, deepest memories it had. The founders carried them out of the war with them, burned into the spell like brands. For two hundred years the Loom played its memory of the night sky over our heads every single evening, and it was flawless, because the founders remembered the stars perfectly. Grief does that. You remember perfectly the thing you lost."

"But the stars went out," Bram said.

"And the Loom didn't lose them," Tam said. "*We* did. Or the wards did. When the sky went dark, the Loom reached for its memory of stars and found—" he searched for the words, found them inadequate, used them anyway— "found a hole where the memory used to be. Something took it. Something ate it. I don't know what. Maybe the spell is finally dying of old age. Maybe the dragon—" he glanced at the lantern at Ilsa's hip, and lowered his voice— "maybe the price came due. But the moment the star-memory failed, the Loom started failing everywhere else. Because a memory machine that forgets one thing will forget the next. It's unraveling. From the top down. Stars first, because stars were the thread it held most dear. Then the far places. Then the edges. Then—" he gestured at the blank patches in the meadow— "then everything."

Silence, except for the fire, which was not really fire, making sounds that were almost, but not quite, the sounds fire makes.

Sef spoke first. His knife had stopped moving. "The sea," he said. "When I was young, I fished the Mirrorwater, and my grandfather told me his grandfather had fished the Sundered Sea itself, before the Sundering. I always thought that was just a story. A fisherman's story." His hands were shaking. Tam watched the old man realize, decade by decade, what his stories had been. "The sea in my grandfather's tale had tides. Ours has never had tides. Forty years I told myself the valley's sea was a quiet sea. It's not a quiet sea. It's a *remembered* sea. It goes in and out because somebody remembered it going in and out, and whoever remembers it is getting tired."

"Yes," Tam said gently.

"And the fish?"

"Some days the nets come up full. Some days they come up empty, and the fishermen say the fish are scarce this season. They're not scarce. On the empty days, the Loom hasn't gotten around to remembering enough fish yet."

Sef put down the knife. He looked at his hands the way Tam had looked at his own—as if they belonged to a stranger, as if everything they had ever touched might be subtracted from them. Tam knew that look. He had worn it for thirty years. He wished, not for the first time, that knowing a thing in advance made it easier to give away.

* * *

Wren had climbed into Bram's lap at some point, or perhaps Bram had simply gathered her there without noticing, the way you gather something small and warm against a cold that's coming. She looked up at him.

"So the valley is a story," she said. "Somebody's telling us, all the time, and we're inside the telling."

"That's as good a way to say it as any," Tam said.

"Then when the telling stops—"

"We stop," Tam said. "Or we don't. That's the question nobody knows the answer to, child. The founders built the valley so that no one would ever have to find out. When the Loom forgets a place, the place doesn't burn or flood. It just... stops having been. The blank spots in that meadow aren't ruins. Ruins are what's left after. There's no after, in there. There's just the world deciding it never got around to that bit of grass."

Bram's arms tightened around the girl. "Then we're dying," he said. "The whole valley. Slowly. From the edges in."

"Faster than slowly, now," Tam said. "The unraveling is accelerating. My father's journals tracked the boundary erosion for years—it was fractions of an inch a season. Since the stars went out? Ilsa's instruments will tell you better than I can, but I'd say yards. Miles, soon. The Loom is not fading gracefully. It's panicking. A memory that knows it's forgetting grabs at everything it has left, and spends itself faster, and forgets faster still. It's a death spiral, and we're all inside it."

Ilsa had gone very still. Tam had learned, in the short weeks of their acquaintance, to read the cartographer the way he read mechanisms—by what moved and what refused to. Her stillness now was the stillness of guilt.

"The Ember," she said. "The last light of the extinguished stars. If the Loom needs its star-memory back—"

"Then that lantern might be the only piece of the original sky left anywhere in the world," Tam finished. "Yes. I've been thinking the same thing. Which means either it's the seed we replant the valley with—" he met her eyes across the fire— "or it's the last honest thing we own, and we spend it on getting out. Those are the only two choices I can see. Save the illusion, or spend the miracle."

"Is it truly an illusion?" Bram asked. His voice was very quiet. "The valley. Us. Wren. If the Loom remembers us—are we less real than people outside?"

And there it was. The question Tam had carried for thirty years, the question that had cost him his father and his name and his town, and he heard it now in a boy's mouth and found he still had no answer that didn't hurt.

"I don't know," he said. "Here's what I know. My mother's bread was real. I ate it. Her hands were real; I held them when she died. And if every bit of it was a memory being played by a machine—if she herself was a song the Loom learned from some founder's grief for a woman killed in the war—then I don't know what real is anymore, and neither do you, and neither does anyone. But I know this." He leaned forward, and the false firelight caught the truth-lens in his hand and made it blaze. "A homeland that has to be remembered into existence every morning is a homeland that can be forgotten. Ours is being forgotten right now. Whatever we decide—restore it, escape it, burn it—we decide *soon*, and we decide together, because there are no more seasons left for arguing."

* * *

They sat with it. That was the thing about truth, Tam thought, watching their faces around the fire: it weighed differently in different hands. Sef held his like a stone he'd been carrying so long he'd mistaken it for part of his body. Ilsa held hers like an instrument she hadn't finished calibrating. Wren held hers lightly, the way children hold everything, testing it for edges. And Bram—

Bram held his like a debt.

"My ancestors did this," the boy said at last. "Built it. Sealed it. Fed everyone into the machine." His scarred palm was open in his lap, facing up, as if he expected the answer to land in it. "Two hundred years of birthdays. Two hundred years of bread and harvests and—" his voice cracked— "of Mistress Halden teaching me letters. All of it a room someone locked from the outside because they were afraid."

"Because they were grieving," Tam said. "Those aren't the same thing. But they build the same cages." He thought of his father, and of the guild hall, and of the sentence that had ended a life: *anything made can be unmade.* "The founders loved the valley, boy. That's the horror of it. Love is the strongest binding agent there is. They loved so hard they nailed a country shut."

Before Bram could answer, Wren sat up straight in his lap, sudden as a struck match.

"Do you hear that?" she whispered.

Tam cocked his head. At first there was nothing—only the not-fire, the wind moving through grass that was mostly still grass. Then he heard it: a low hum from the east, from the direction of the old road, rhythmic and wrong, like a tuning fork pressed against the world's teeth. He knew that sound. Every ward-smith's child knew that sound.

Boundary stones. Dozens of them, singing at once. And boundary stones only sang like that when the wardens marched them—when the guardians of the valley came hunting whatever threatened the Loom.

"They've found us," Ilsa said, already reaching for the lantern.

Tam closed his hand around his father's truth-lens and rose, his old knees cracking, and thought: *so much for deciding together.*

"Put out the fire," he said. "Quietly. And whatever happens—nobody lets them touch the boy."
