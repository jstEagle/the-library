## 9. Riders of the Hollow March

The Hollow March earned its name honestly. Bram had heard Old Sef speak of it the night before, in the low voice he saved for things that frightened him: a stretch of country where the wards ran thinnest, where the grass grew pale as old bone and nothing sang, not crickets, not wind, not even the small internal music of one's own blood. A silence you could walk through like water.

They had been crossing it since dawn, and by midday Bram understood why Sef's voice had gone quiet.

It wasn't only the hush. It was the way the land seemed to be *listening*. The pale grass bent toward them as they passed, all in one direction, like a congregation turning its heads. The sky above—starless now for six days, a flat grey ceiling that pressed down like a lid—gave back no echo of their footsteps. When Wren stumbled and cried out, the sound fell dead a hand's breadth from her lips.

"Keep moving," Ilsa said, without turning around. She walked with her instruments wrapped against her chest like a swaddled infant, and her eyes kept going to the horizon in a way Bram had learned to distrust. "This is the kind of place where you don't want to be caught standing still."

"Why?" Tam asked. He was limping; his ankle had turned badly two days ago on the scree below the Gate of Return, and he'd refused all offers to stop. "Because of the wardens?"

"Because of everything." Ilsa's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "A place this quiet is either empty or full. I've never met anything in between."

Bram walked at the rear, beside Sef, who carried the lantern. The Ember inside it burned low and steady, a coal-colored light that threw no heat but somehow made the air around it feel *remembered*—as if the lantern were a small room where someone had once been happy. Six days ago it had flared at Bram's touch like a heart recognizing its own blood, and since then it had followed him with something that felt uncomfortably like attention.

"It knows where we're going," Sef said quietly, watching the light sway with his stride. "That's what I keep thinking. It's not being carried. It's *leading*."

"Then I wish it would lead faster," Bram said. "My master used to say the March was cursed."

"Mistress Halden said a lot of things about places she never went." Sef shifted the lantern to his other hand. His knuckles were white around the handle, though his voice stayed easy. "Fishermen learn which silences are empty and which are holding their breath. This one's holding its breath."

Ahead, Wren had stopped walking. She stood in the middle of the pale grass with her head tipped back, staring at the dead sky, and her small face wore an expression Bram had come to dread on her: the look of a child assembling an adult truth from pieces too heavy for her hands.

"Bram," she said. "Why does the ground feel sad here?"

He crouched beside her, ready with some gentle deflection—and then he felt it too. Through the soles of his boots, through the scar on his palm that had been itching since they entered the March, came a tremor. Faint. Rhythmic. Rising through the earth like a pulse through a wrist.

Hoofbeats.

"Ilsa," he said, and his voice came out wrong, thin and high. "Ilsa—"

She was already turning. Her hand went into her coat and came out with the short blade she pretended she didn't carry. "Off the road," she snapped. "Down. All of you, *down*—"

They scattered into the grass as the riders crested the low rise to the east.

There were nine of them, and Bram's first thought—absurd, hysterical—was that they looked like the valley itself had decided to ride. Their armor was not metal but woven wicker and bound reed, dyed the grey-green of the March, and their faces were masked with plates of pale bark carved into smooth, featureless ovals. No eyes. No mouths. Just the blank grain of the wood, and yet Bram felt *seen*, thoroughly and terribly, by every one of them.

Their horses made no sound. That was the worst of it. Nine great beasts, shaggy and grey as river-stone, thundering across ground that swallowed every hoofbeat, manes streaming, breath steaming—and the March gave back nothing. They moved like figures seen through glass.

"The wardens," Tam breathed beside him. He'd gone the color of tallow. "They don't leave the March. They *never* leave the March. Why are they—"

"They're not leaving it," Ilsa said grimly. "We brought it with us. Look behind us."

Bram looked. The pale grass stretched back the way they'd come, and beyond it, faint as a stain spreading through cloth, the greyness of the March had begun to bleed outward into the ordinary green country—the first sign, Tam had told them, of a valley dying from its edges inward. The wardens rode the border of that stain like shepherds riding the edge of a flock.

The riders drew up thirty paces away and fanned into a crescent. For a long moment nothing happened. The masked faces turned slowly, all of them, until every blank oval pointed at the company crouched in the grass.

Then the center rider dismounted.

He—or she, or it; the wicker armor gave away nothing—walked forward alone, each step silent, and stopped ten paces from where Bram lay flattened among the stems. The warden's head tilted, birdlike, and when it spoke, its voice came from somewhere behind the mask, muffled and strange, like words spoken underwater.

"You are far from your hearths, little ones."

Ilsa rose slowly to her feet, blade held low and loose. "Travelers," she said. "Bound for the coast. We mean no trespass."

"No one means trespass." The warden's mask regarded her without interest. "Trespass is what the world does to us. We are the ones who stand between." The blank face swung back to Bram, and though there were no eyes behind the bark, he felt the gaze settle on him like a hand. "You. Stand up."

Every instinct screamed at him to stay down. But the scar on his palm had gone from itching to burning, a hot wire pulled tight from his hand up his arm and into his chest, and some part of him—the part that had run from Mistress Halden's cottage under vanishing stars—understood that hiding was over. Hiding had been over since the night the sky went dark.

He stood.

The moment his feet left the ground, the Ember flared.

Light poured through the lantern's seams, gold-edged and furious, and the nearest horses shied and screamed—soundlessly, horribly, mouths wide and silent. The wardens' crescent tightened. And the lead rider went very still, the way a man goes still when a snake crosses his path.

"Show me your hand," the warden said.

"Don't," Ilsa said quietly, for Bram's ears alone. "Whatever it asks, don't—"

But Bram was already opening his fist. There was no point anymore. He understood, with the terrible clarity that had been arriving in pieces all week, that this was what the whole journey had been walking toward: the moment when what he was could no longer be folded away in a pocket, hidden under a glove, explained away as a birthmark.

He held up his right hand, palm out.

The scar stood livid in the Ember's light—not the small white seam he'd carried all his life, but a branching mark now, silver-dark, running from the heel of his palm up across his fingers in a pattern like a river delta seen from above. Like a map. Like the lines Ilsa drew when she charted borders no instrument could find.

The warden crossed the distance between them in three strides and took his wrist. Its grip was cold through the gloves, cold as well-water, and it turned his hand over with a gentleness that frightened him more than violence would have.

"So," the warden said softly. "The Sunderer's blood walks again."

"We don't use that word," Bram said. His own voice surprised him—steady, when everything under his ribs was shaking. "Whatever it means. Whatever my family did. I didn't do it."

"No." The mask tilted. "You only inherited it. As we inherited our watch. Three hundred years, little key, we have ridden this March and kept the border, because beyond it—" the warden raised one arm, that slow deliberate gesture, and pointed west, toward the edge of the valley, toward everything, "—is the Devourer. The void that eats names. Your ancestors sealed us in to save us from it, and paid the dragon to make the seal, and the dragon's price was the stars. And now the stars are gone, and the seal is failing, and the Devourer leans against the door." The voice dropped lower. "And the key has left its house."

"I'm not a key."

"Everything that opens can be called a key." The warden released his wrist and stepped back. "You will come with us. You will be returned to the hearth-country, to the wardens' keeping, and the rites will be performed, and the border will hold another hundred years. This is mercy. Do not mistake it for a choice."

"He comes with us," Ilsa said. She had moved to Bram's shoulder without seeming to cross any space at all; her blade was still low, but her voice had gone flat and hard, the voice of a woman who had once argued with kings' surveyors and won. "He's under my protection."

"Your protection." Something that might have been amusement moved behind the mask. "Cartographer. We know what you are. You came to steal fire and draw doors, and the fire chose a Sunderer instead. The world outside sends thieves and mapmakers. We send—" the warden spread its arms, and the eight riders behind it lowered their lances, long shafts of grey wood tipped with barbed bronze, "—this."

What happened next, Bram would remember afterward in fragments, the way one remembers a storm: in pieces, out of order, with gaps where the terror burned clean through the record.

Tam shouting and hurling one of his ward-tinkers' snares—a coil of braided thread that unspooled into a shimmering net of half-light, tangling two horses mid-charge. Sef roaring, swinging the lantern in a great arc so the Ember's light swept the riders like a scythe, and the pale grass *igniting* wherever the light touched, burning green-gold and cold. Wren screaming, small and fierce, buried under Ilsa's coat as the cartographer fought like someone who had decided, somewhere on this journey, that she was done losing things.

And Bram in the middle of it, doing nothing, because he couldn't. Because every time a warden's lance swung toward him, it stopped. Every rider who came within reach of him froze, horse and rider both, trembling like dogs hearing a whistle only they could hear—and then wheeled away, refusing him, unable to touch him. The blood-mark blazed on his palm, and the wardens' own magic, the valley's own ancient binding, recognized its maker's line and would not let them harm him.

It should have been a comfort. It was the opposite. He stood untouched in the whirl of the fight, spared by the very inheritance he was trying to escape, protected by the ancestors whose lie had built his whole life—and he understood that this was how the trap worked. Not chains. *Kindness.* The valley would never let its key be broken. It would simply close around him forever, gently, the way a hand closes around a moth.

"Stop!" he shouted, and the word tore out of him with a force that wasn't only his own—the scar flared white, and the nearest three riders reeled as if struck. "I'm not going back! Do you hear me? I'm not going back to be locked in!"

The fight guttered. Riders regrouped, breathing hard, horses stamping in the soundless way that made Bram's skin crawl. The lead warden stood apart, watching him, and when it spoke again its voice had changed—quieter, and somehow more sorrowful.

"You would open the door," it said. "You would let the Devourer in. You would end three hundred years of safety for a sky full of lies about distant fires."

"They're not lies," Bram said. "I've *seen* them. In the Ember. The stars are real, and they're ours, and they were taken from us, and everyone who ever told you otherwise was afraid." His voice cracked, and he let it. "I was afraid too. My whole life. And then I found out the fear was built for me, brick by brick, by people who loved me, and I have to tell you—it doesn't help. Love doesn't make a cage kind. It just makes it harder to break."

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—he thought the warden might listen. The blank mask turned toward the Ember's light, toward the small cold fire that had once been a dragon's heart, and the rider's shoulders bowed as if under weight.

Then it straightened.

"Traitor," the warden said, and the word fell into the silence of the March like a stone into a well. "Blood of the Sunderers. Key of the gate. Hear the judgment of the Hollow Watch: you are cast out of the valley's keeping. No hearth will hold you. No ward will shield you. When the Devourer comes for you—as it will come, as it comes for all who turn their faces from the valley—you will die unmourned and unwalled." The mask swung to take in the others. "And those who ride with him share his exile. Go, then. Run to the sea. The border fails either way now, thanks to you—but know that when it breaks, it breaks *because of you*."

The riders withdrew. Not fleeing—withdrawing, in perfect order, the crescent folding closed like a hand releasing its grip, until nine silent shapes crested the eastern rise and were gone, and the March's silence rushed back in to fill the space they'd left.

No one spoke for a long time.

Wren emerged from Ilsa's coat, red-eyed and furious, and marched over to Bram and kicked him in the shin, not hard, and then grabbed his hand—the marked one—and held it with both of hers. "You're not a traitor," she announced, to him, to the grass, to the dead sky. "Traitors are people who break promises. You never promised to stay in a cage."

"Wren," Ilsa started.

"She's right," Tam said. He was sitting in the flattened grass, cradling his bad ankle, and he laughed—a ragged, disbelieving sound. "Stars, she's right. Did you see them? They couldn't touch him. Their own oaths wouldn't let them. And they still called him traitor." He shook his head. "That's not justice. That's fear wearing armor."

Sef said nothing. He had picked the lantern back up, and he stood looking west, toward the sea none of them could see yet, and his weathered face was troubled in a way Bram had never seen before.

"What is it?" Bram asked.

"That last thing it said." Sef's thumb traced the lantern's handle, back and forth. "*When the border breaks, it breaks because of you.*" He looked at Bram. "It wanted you to hear that. Wanted it more than it wanted to capture you, near the end. Did you feel it? They could have taken you, boy, if they'd been willing to burn their own oath to do it. One of them, at least, was willing. The leader stopped him. I saw it." He paused. "They let us go. And they sent us off carrying a curse-word and a prophecy of ruin."

"To break our hearts," Wren said simply. "So we'll turn around."

Bram looked down at his palm. The mark had faded to its quieter silver, the delta-lines settling like water after a stone. Traitor, they had called him. Cast out of the valley's keeping. Unmourned and unwalled.

And yet his chest ached worse than if they'd beaten him—because the terrible thing, the thing he couldn't say aloud, was that he *understood* them. He knew what it was to guard a border against a world that seemed too big to survive. He'd been doing it his whole life, behind Mistress Halden's fence, behind his own fear. The wardens weren't monsters. They were the valley's oldest wound, still trying to protect itself.

"They'll report to whoever commands them," Ilsa said briskly, already repacking her instruments, already moving, because Ilsa grieved with her feet the way other people grieved with tears. "Which means whoever holds the March now knows exactly where we are, what we carry, and what he is." She jerked her chin at Bram. "Someone sent those riders, and it wasn't just tradition. Wardens don't range this far from their border without orders. Someone with power wants that key back in its lock—or wants him dead trying."

"Corvane," Tam said quietly.

The name landed among them like a dropped knife. None of them had spoken it aloud since the Gate of Return; none of them needed to. The magister who had hired Ilsa to steal the Ember. The man whose letters, found in her satchel, spoke of *restoring the valley to its proper glory* in a hand as neat and patient as a surgeon's.

"Then we move," Ilsa said. "Now, while the March is quiet. The sea is four days east if the country stays honest, and this country has stopped being honest." She set off at a hard pace, and one by one they fell in behind her—Tam limping, Sef with the lantern, Wren with her small hand still gripping Bram's marked palm as if she could anchor him to the world by it alone.

Bram walked last. Behind him the Hollow March lay pale and listening, and somewhere on its eastern rise, nine silent riders were carrying his name—*traitor, key, Sunderer*—back to whoever waited in the valley's deep places.

His scar had gone quiet. But as the company climbed out of the silence into the first ordinary green of living grass, Bram felt a new itch begin, deeper than the skin, in the place where his certainty used to live.

The wardens believed the world outside was a devouring void.

And the worst part—the part that would keep him awake tonight, and every night after—was that as he'd stood in the Ember's light, looking west toward the edge of everything, some small cold voice inside him had whispered:

*What if they're right?*

* * *

They made camp that night in a fold of hills where the grass grew green again and the crickets, tentatively, resumed their work. Sef built a fire the fisherman's way, small and sheltered, and Wren fell asleep against Tam's pack before the kettle boiled.

It was Ilsa who broke the silence, without looking up from the strip of hide she was sketching on.

"When I was disgraced," she said, "they asked me at the tribunal why I kept mapping places that didn't want to be mapped. Lands that erased themselves from every instrument. And I told them the truth, which is the worst thing you can tell a tribunal." She shaded a line with a burnt stick, quick and sure. "I said: because a border nobody can see is a prison nobody can argue with. At least an honest wall gives you something to climb."

Bram watched the firelight move on her face. "You think the wardens could climb? If someone showed them the wall?"

"I think," Ilsa said, "that the leader of nine riders stopped a lance meant for you, and I've been wondering all evening why." She rolled the hide closed and finally looked at him, and her eyes in the firelight were sharp and tired and unexpectedly kind. "Fear wears armor, Tam said. But sometimes doubt does too. Somebody out there rode home tonight carrying a question along with your name." She tucked the map away. "Sleep, Bram. Tomorrow we walk to the sea, and whatever's waiting between here and the water—we'd best meet it rested."

He lay back and looked up. The sky was still bare, still lid-grey, but the Ember sat glowing in its lantern at the camp's edge like a promise kept in secret, and Bram found his hand resting over the scar without deciding to.

Traitor. Key. Sunderer.

Four days to the sea. And somewhere behind them, in the hollow heart of the valley, something had begun to move.
