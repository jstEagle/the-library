## 10. Old Sef Remembers the Water

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Old Sef began to talk, and that was fitting, because everything he knew about beginnings he had learned from endings.

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchstone, one of the old boundary markers that dotted the Hollow March like broken teeth. Tam had warded the perimeter with knots of braided copper wire, and Wren slept against Ilsa's rolled cloak with the lantern cradled between her small hands, as if the child understood—before any of them said it aloud—that the thing inside the glass was alive and needed holding. Bram sat closest to Sef, knees drawn up, his scarred palm turned inward against his thigh. He had been asking all evening in the way young people ask when they are afraid of the answer: sideways, with jokes, with questions about fish and weather and the taste of salt. Sef had answered them all patiently, the way you keep a kettle on a low flame, waiting for it to come to a boil on its own.

It came to a boil at last when the boy asked, quietly, not looking at him: "You said once you remembered the sea. How? If the valley's never touched the world—if we've been *nowhere* all this time—how does anyone here remember water?"

Sef took his time with it. He was an old man and had learned that most answers spoiled if you rushed them, like milk in summer.

"Because," he said, "the valley forgot the world, but it could not forget *us*. And we are stubborn vessels, we people. We hold what we're given, even when we don't know what it is." He held out his hands, palms up, the way a beggar holds them or a priest. "My grandfather fished the Sundered Sea before there was a Sundered Sea. Before it had any name at all but *sea*. He taught my father the pull of the tides, and my father taught me, and I taught nobody, because by my time there was no tide to teach—only the Millpond, which is what we call our little grey lake, only it isn't a millpond, it never was, it's a wound where a piece of ocean used to be."

Bram frowned. "A piece of ocean?"

"A memory of one." Sef smiled, and his face in the firelight was a landscape of its own, gullies and ridges and old weather. "Sit closer, boy. This is a story that wants telling proper, and I've kept it forty years behind my teeth, and teeth get tired."

* * *

"My grandfather's name was Ede Okafor," Sef said, and watched the name land.

He saw it land, too—saw Bram go still the way a hare goes still when the shadow of the hawk crosses the grass. Okafor. The boy carried it like a stone in his mouth every day of his life and had never once thought to ask what it weighed.

"Yes," Sef said gently. "*That* Okafor. Your bloodline, lad, though I suspect you know that now, since the Ember sang to you. Ede was no wizard and no king. He was a net-mender. A good one—the best on the whole coast—and when the war came up the shore road burning everything that stood still, it was Ede who mended nets by day and sat in the tavern by night listening to the refugees, and it was Ede who started saying the thing that everyone else was too afraid to say: *we cannot win, so we must leave.*"

"Leave where?" Bram asked. "There was nowhere—"

"There was nowhere *visible*, boy. That's different." Sef poked the fire, and sparks went up like startled birds. "You have to understand what the world was like then. Magic wasn't rare; it was *cheap*. It ran in the gutters. Any hedge-witch could fold a mile of road into a thimble, and the great houses had artificers who could do things I haven't words for. And war, being war, had learned to use all of it. The armies didn't march, they *erased*. There were weapons that unmade the names of towns, so that no one could remember them well enough to mourn them. Whole provinces gone quiet, not conquered—*deleted*. The refugees called it the Blankness coming, and they were right to."

"So your grandfather ran from that."

"He ran toward something worse, if you ask me, but braver too." Sef settled back against the watchstone. "In those days there was a dragon in the eastern mountains. Last of her line, older than the war, older than most of the kingdoms burning in it. She had a name in the old tongue that meant *She-Who-Holds-the-Tide*, because her fire was strange—not the burning kind, but the kind that *keeps*. Her flame didn't consume. It preserved. Put a thing in her fire and it would stay exactly as it was, forever: a leaf green forever, a grief fresh forever, a promise unbroken forever. Kings had begged her for that fire to embalm their fathers and pickle their glory. She refused them all. She'd seen what preservation does to a thing that was meant to change."

Ilsa had stopped pretending to check her instruments. She sat with the lantern between her boots, and the light inside it pulsed slow and deep, like something breathing in its sleep, and Sef could have sworn it brightened when he spoke of its mother—or its maker, or whatever word a shard of heart-fire uses for the dragon it came from.

"The founders came to her," Sef went on. "Not just Ede—a few hundred families, the ones the war hadn't eaten yet. Fishermen, farmers, a midwife, two priests who'd lost their god, a cartographer—" he nodded at Ilsa, whose eyebrows rose— "yes, there's always been one of you in this story, Vane, take that how you will. They came up the mountain with everything they owned and they knelt in the snow outside her cave, and Ede spoke for them, because Ede was the kind of man who ended up speaking whether he wanted to or not.

"And he said: *Great One, we don't want to fight anymore. We don't want to be found. Make us a country that isn't anywhere. Fold us out of the world's eye.*"

Sef paused. The wind moved through the dead grass around the camp, making a sound like pages turning.

"She laughed at them," he said. "First time in a century, they say. Because it was a terrible request. Do you understand what they were asking? Not safety—*oblivion*. To be cut out of the world like a thread pulled from cloth. No trade, no news, no graves visited, no songs sung elsewhere. Their children would be born into a lie and never know it. She told them all of this. She told them their descendants would curse them for it. And Ede—my grandfather, your many-times-great-whatever, boy—Ede said the words I've hated and loved my whole life.

"He said: *Better a beautiful lie that keeps them whole than a true world that eats them.*

"And the dragon looked at him a long time, the way she must have looked at the kings, weighing what preservation does to a thing meant to change. And then she named her price."

Bram's hand had drifted to his scarred palm without his noticing. Sef nodded at it.

"You've felt it already, I think. What she asked. Not gold—she had more than kingdoms. Not service. She asked for *witness*. She was dying, you see. Old beyond old, and the war's poison was in the air of the whole world, and she knew she would not last another age. So her price was this: she would spend her own heart-fire to build the wards—to weave the valley out of the world, stitch it into the space between, hidden and safe and *preserved*, exactly as they were on the day they fled. And in payment, the founders would carry her ember down into the valley with them. Her last living flame. Not to use. To *keep*. To tend it, generation after generation, so that somewhere in the world—even in a world that had forgotten her—something of her stayed lit. She didn't want worship. She wanted a hearth. She wanted to not go out alone."

The lantern flared. Just once, softly, like a sleeper turning over, and Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured something that might have been a word in no language anyone knew.

"The Ember is her heart-fire," Sef said. "Her last payment, and ours. Every soul born under these wards since the Sundering has been, without knowing it, a keeper of a dying dragon's candle. My family kept the fishing—because you can't unlearn the sea in one generation, and Ede made us swear to remember the water even if there was no water, so that someday, if a door ever opened, someone would know which way to walk. That's why I'm here. That's why I came with you lot, if I'm honest, though I'll deny it in the morning. Forty years I've walked past the Millpond feeling it tug at me like a tide with no moon. Forty years I've known, in my bones, that the sea was real and we were the dream."

Tam spoke from the edge of the firelight, voice rough. "And the stars?"

Sef closed his eyes. "Ah. The stars."

* * *

"Here is the part I hate," he said. "Here is the part that keeps me honest when I want to make the founders saints.

"The wards needed power. Heart-fire is mighty, but mighty things dim, and the valley was to last not years but *forever*—or as near forever as the founders could buy. So the binding drew on everything. The land, yes. The river, the seasons, the births and deaths of everyone inside. And the sky."

He opened his eyes and looked up, at the blank dark where the stars should have been, at the smooth black nothing that had been there every night of his life until three weeks ago, when even that nothing had shuddered and failed.

"They gave the sky," Sef said simply. "That was the deepest cut of the price, and the dragon warned them, and they paid it anyway. A valley folded out of the world cannot borrow the world's heavens. So the founders' sky became part of the spell—a painted heaven, a memory of stars, fixed and false and beautiful. You grew up under it, boy. Did you ever notice the constellations never turn? Did you ever notice the same star sits over the north ridge in spring and autumn alike?"

Bram's face said he hadn't, and the not-noticing hurt him visibly, the way it hurts to learn you've been blind in a room you loved.

"Most didn't," Sef said. "That was the craft of it. The lie was made to be livable. But the stars weren't real, and neither was their light, and when the wards began to fail—when the heart-fire finally began to gutter after all these generations—the painted sky went first. Three weeks ago the stars didn't vanish, lad. They *stopped being maintained*. We've been living under a ceiling, and the ceiling's coming down."

Wren had woken. None of them had marked the moment, but she was sitting up now, hugging the lantern, her chin resting on the glass, her eyes enormous in the emberlight. Children wake for the important parts; Sef had learned that long ago.

"Is the dragon sad?" Wren asked. "In the fire. Is she lonely?"

And Sef, who had faced wardens and storms and forty years of a tideless lake, found his throat close on the simplest question in the world.

"I think," he said slowly, "that she is very tired, and very patient, and that she has been waiting a long time for someone to carry her back to the water."

"To the sea," Wren said. "To the *real* sea."

"To the Sundered Sea," Sef agreed, "which is sundered no longer, if half of what I remember is true. Which is why the Ember has been pulling, all these weeks. Why it leaned toward you at the border, boy, and why your palm itched near the wards, and why the wardens marked you traitor the moment your blood touched their stones. The dragon's fire knows its heirs. It has been trying to finish its errand for two hundred years, and every keeper before you kept it the way you keep a caged bird—fed, admired, never released. Because releasing it means admitting what it's for."

Ilsa spoke then, quiet and precise, the way she did when a map was forming behind her eyes. "And what is it for, Sef? Say it plainly. All of it."

He looked at her, and at Bram, and at the child with the lantern, and he made himself say it, because stories are inheritances and inheritances are debts, and he was the last one left who knew the full account.

"It's for *ending*," he said. "The Ember was never meant to be tended forever. It was meant to be spent. Carried out of the valley, down to the true sea, and given back—burned in one last act of the old magic, to tear the door open from the inside and put the valley's people back into the world that paid for their hiding. The founders bought a refuge. The dragon sold them a *loan*. And the interest has come due, and the coin is her last fire, and the purse is standing right there in that lantern, waiting for a hand brave enough or foolish enough to open it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark grass, a nightbird called the one note it knew, over and over, like a question no one would answer.

Bram stared into the lantern for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was steady in the way that Sef recognized from his grandfather's letters—the steadiness of a man standing at the edge of deep water, measuring it.

"Ede paid a dragon to hide us," he said. "And hid inside the price was the way home. All along. Two hundred years, and the way home was the payment."

"That's the shape of it," Sef said. "Stories are like that. The escape hatch is always built into the trap, if the trapper loves you enough."

"Then why didn't anyone ever—"

"Because knowing a thing and believing it are different countries, boy, and there's no map between them." Sef reached over and laid his old hand, briefly, on the boy's scarred palm. "Your master knew. I'd wager my boat she knew, and couldn't say it, and raised you anyway with the truth sleeping in your blood. And now the Ember's fading, and the wards are failing, and Corvane's men are two valleys behind us, and the choice that Ede made in the snow two hundred years ago has come down the generations like a hot coal passed hand to hand—dropped, caught, dropped again—and tonight, lad, it's in yours."

Bram looked down at the lantern. Inside, the ember-light pulsed—slow, deep, patient—and Sef would have sworn, would have staked his name and his grandfather's name on it, that the pulse quickened when the boy looked. Like a heart answering a heartbeat. Like a tide remembering its moon.

Then Wren lifted her head from the glass and said, in the flat, certain tone children use for facts adults aren't ready for:

"She says the water is closer than you think."

* * *

They all heard it. There was no mistaking it—no sleep-talk, no trick of the wind. The child had spoken words she could not know, in a voice that for a moment was not entirely her own, and the lantern's glow had swelled with each syllable like a lung filling.

Old Sef felt the hair rise on his arms, and beneath the fear, rising through it like groundwater, something he had not felt since childhood at the Millpond's grey edge: the pull. The tidal pull. Faint and vast and utterly real, coming from the east, from the dark, from the direction of the Sundered Sea—as if somewhere out beyond the failing wards, the ocean itself had felt the ember stir and had begun, at long last, to reach back.

"East," Sef whispered, and his old hands shook as he pointed. "Boy—the water's east. It's always been east. And if the sea is reaching for us..."

He never finished. He didn't need to. Far off across the Hollow March, past the unraveling blanks where unmapped places were eating themselves into white nothing, a sound rolled over the dead grass—low, immense, older than the valley, older than the war that had made it. Not thunder. Thunder repeats. This was a single sustained note, like the voice of something enormous turning over in its sleep two hundred miles away, and every ward-stone in the March rang with it, and the lantern blazed so bright that Wren had to shield her eyes, and Old Sef, fisherman of a sea that no longer existed, heir to a memory of water, began—for the first time in forty years—to laugh and weep at once.

"Tide's turning," he said. "Tide's turning, and it's coming for *us*."
