## 11. Wren Asks About Stars

Wren had a game she played when the grown-ups thought she was sleeping.

She would lie very still in the crook of Old Sef's coat — he smelled of salt and pipe-smoke and something older underneath, like stones that had been wet for a hundred years — and she would listen to them talk. Grown-ups talked differently at night. Their words got softer and truer, like fruit that had gone overripe and split its skin.

Tonight they were camped in the ruin of an orchard, under trees that had stopped making apples. The branches were bare and gray, and when the wind moved through them they made no sound at all, which was wrong. Wren knew trees were supposed to whisper. Tam had told her once that everything alive whispered if you listened long enough. But these trees had forgotten how, the way some old people forgot names.

She kept her eyes shut and listened.

"—the stars came back, we could navigate by true north again," Ilsa was saying. Ilsa talked like someone reading from a book she loved but didn't trust. "Every map I ever drew in the valley was a guess dressed up as certainty. Out there—" she stopped, the way she always stopped when she said *out there*, as if the word had edges that cut. "Out there, the sky tells you where you are."

"And we'd be under it," Bram said. He was sitting close; Wren could hear him turning something in his hands, probably the lantern with the fire inside. The lantern hummed sometimes, very low, and when it did, Wren's teeth felt warm. "Under a real sky."

"Under a sky that doesn't lie," Sef said, and laughed his dry-cracking laugh. "That'd be a first for me."

Wren opened her eyes.

The sky above the orchard was black. Not night-black — she knew night-black, the soft dark that came every evening like a blanket pulled up to the chin. This was a different black, a hole-black, the black of a page where writing used to be. There were no stars in it. There had never been stars in it. Wren was six years old, or maybe seven — birthdays were hard to keep when nobody remembered what year it was — and she had been born on the night the stars went out, which meant she had never seen one. Not even a little one. Not even a faraway one pretending to be a bug on glass.

The grown-ups talked about stars the way Sef talked about the sea. With their voices going soft and hungry. Like something they had lost and meant to get back.

Wren sat up in the fold of Sef's coat and asked her question.

"Why do you want them back?"

* * *

They all stopped talking. Four faces turned toward her, pale smudges in the ember-light of the little campfire Tam had coaxed out of dead applewood. Tam's face was all angles and soot. Ilsa's was sharp as a folded map. Bram looked startled, the way he often did, like a boy who had been told too many secrets too fast and was still catching up.

Sef just smiled. Sef never minded her questions. That was why Wren liked him best, though she would have bitten anyone who said so.

"I said it wrong," Wren said, because being small meant people decided you couldn't talk properly, and she hated that more than almost anything. She tried again, carefully, the way you stepped across a creek on wobbly stones. "You want the stars back. But the stars went away before I was born. So the sky I have is *my* sky. It's the only one there is." She pointed up, at the hole-black. "And if you put stars in it, it won't be my sky anymore. It'll be yours. An old one. From before."

Tam made a sound like a kettle deciding not to whistle.

"That's—" Bram started, and then stopped, because he did that too, started sentences and abandoned them halfway like birds leaving nests.

"It's a fair question," Sef said. He shifted, and his coat settled around Wren like a harbor. "Go on, sprat. Say the rest of it."

The rest of it was the part she'd been carrying around for days, tucked under her ribs like a hot coal. She hadn't known how to say it because saying it might make it true, and if it was true then the grown-ups might stop wanting to go wherever they were going, and if they stopped wanting that, Wren didn't know what would happen to any of them. But the coal was burning her now, so she took it out and held it up.

"You say the valley is dying. Tam says it. The magic is going out of it since the stars went away. Places are coming *unmapped*." She said the word carefully; she'd learned it from Ilsa, who said it the way other people said a curse word, quiet and fierce. "The orchard forgot how to make apples. The river past the ford forgot its middle. If the valley dies, everyone in it dies, or worse, goes blank like the places." She looked at each of them. "But you don't want to fix the valley. You want to take it down. All of it. Every field and house and road, everything anybody's grandmother ever planted — you want it to burn away so we can walk out into the *real world* under your *real stars*."

Nobody said anything. The dead trees stood around them, silent as held breath.

"So here's what I want to know." Wren's voice didn't shake. She was proud of that. "Why is your sky better than mine? Why does everybody get sad-looking when they talk about stars I've never seen? Why do we have to lose *everything* so you can have back the thing *you* lost?" She hugged her knees. "I didn't lose anything. I was born after. The valley is all I've got, and you're the ones taking it away, and you all look so *noble* about it, like it's a sad thing that has to happen, but it's not happening to you. You'll still remember apples. I won't remember anything, because there won't be anything left to remember."

The words ran out. She sat there breathing hard, her heart going like a rabbit's, waiting for one of them to tell her she was too young to understand.

Tam spoke first. Tam, who fixed wards and broke questions open for a living.

"She's right," Tam said, to the others, not to Wren, and there was something raw in it. "You know she's right. We've been telling ourselves this is rescue. It's also demolition. Both things. At once. And we keep doing what adults do, which is hold the grief out where the child can't reach it." A pause. "Except she reached it. Six years old and she reached it first."

"I'm seven," Wren said. "Probably."

* * *

Bram came and sat beside her. He did that sometimes — came and sat without talking, which Wren appreciated, because most grown-ups filled silence the way careless people filled a bucket, spilling everywhere. Bram let silences be silences.

After a while he held out the lantern.

"Want to hold it?"

Wren looked at the lantern. Inside it, behind the smoked glass, the Ember sat on its little iron cradle: a shard of fire the size of a robin's egg, red-gold, moving slowly the way honey moves, the way blood moves under skin. It was the last light of the old stars, Ilsa had said. The heart-fire of a dragon, Sef had said. Wren thought it was both those things and also something else, something that didn't have a grown-up word yet.

She took the lantern. It was heavier than it looked, the way important things usually were.

It hummed against her fingers. Her teeth went warm. And then — this happened sometimes, and she had never told anyone, because telling felt like it would break it — the humming turned into something almost like listening. Like the fire was pressing its ear against the inside of the glass.

"What's it like," Wren said quietly, "being the last one?"

Bram blinked. "What?"

"The fire. In the lantern." She tilted her head at it. "Everything else from its time is gone. The dragon's gone. The stars are gone. It's the only piece left, sitting in a box, being carried around by people who keep arguing about whether to use it." She looked up at Bram. "Do you think it wants to go home?"

Bram didn't answer right away. His scarred palm was resting on his knee, and in the ember-light Wren could see the mark on it, the whorled thing that itched near the borders. He was rubbing it now, absently, the way you rub a bruise you've stopped noticing.

"I think," he said slowly, "it's been waiting a long time for somebody to ask that."

Across the fire, Ilsa had gone very still. She was watching the lantern with an expression Wren had learned to read off grown-up faces: the look of someone recalculating. Ilsa did that. She carried her instruments everywhere — brass circles and swinging needles and a little telescope with a cracked lens — and she looked at the world through them even when her hands were empty. Right now she was looking at Wren the way she looked at a border that wouldn't record itself. Like a problem that was also a door.

"Wren," Ilsa said. "When you hold the lantern. What do you feel?"

Warmth in her teeth. Humming in her bones. A feeling like standing at the edge of a field at dusk, knowing something enormous was about to come over the hills.

"Hungry," Wren said. "Not eat-food hungry. *Other* hungry. Like it remembers being big." She frowned, trying to find the shape of it. "Like it remembers being a whole sky."

The fire crackled. Somewhere out in the dark orchard, one of the soundless trees dropped a branch, and it fell without a whisper, which was somehow worse than any noise.

"Star-born," Sef murmured. He said it like a word from a song. "Born the night they went out. I heard my grandmother use that word once, about children born under eclipses. Said they belonged half to one world and half to another." His old eyes settled on Wren, kind and troubled at once. "Never thought I'd meet one."

"I'm not half of anything," Wren said, stung. "I'm all me."

"Yes," Sef agreed at once. "That you are, sprat. That you are."

* * *

Later, when the others slept — Bram curled near the lantern, Ilsa sitting up as always with her maps across her knees even in sleep, Tam twitching through dreams full of gears — Wren crept to the edge of the camp with the lantern and sat down between two roots of a dead apple tree.

She held the lantern in her lap and looked up at the hole-black sky.

"Tell me about them," she whispered. "The stars. Since nobody else will tell me true."

The Ember pulsed. Once. Twice. Slow, like a heart that had been beating alone for a very long time and had gotten patient about it.

And Wren — who had been born the night the lights went out, who had spent her whole life under a sky with nothing written on it — felt something rise in her chest that wasn't quite a memory, because memories needed a before, and she didn't have one. It was more like a promise. Or a debt. Something old recognizing something new and saying: *you'll do.*

"They're not just pretty," she said to the fire, working it out loud, the way she worked everything out loud because thinking-inside made her dizzy. "They're not just lamps in the ceiling. They're... anchors. Isn't that right? Ilsa's needle spins without them. Tam says the wards are unraveling. The valley's coming apart because nothing up there is holding it together anymore." She hugged her knees. "So when you all say you want the stars back, you don't mean you miss how they look. You mean you miss being *held*."

The Ember flared, gentle and gold, and for one heartbeat Wren saw it — really saw it, behind her eyes rather than in front: a sweep of silver fires strung across a darkness deeper than any she knew, rivers and scatterings and one great slow wheel turning at the top of everything, and beneath it a whole world lying in that light like a child in a mother's arms, held, held, held—

Then it was gone, and Wren was crying, and she didn't know why, and she wiped her nose on her sleeve and decided she wouldn't tell anyone about that either.

Because here was the thing she understood now, the thing that sat in her stomach cold as a stone from the riverbed:

If the stars came back, the sky would hold the world again. And the world it would hold was the *old* world — the real one, the outside one, the one with seas in it and maps and a history that didn't include her. The valley would burn away like fog at morning. And everyone would stand under the beautiful returned stars and feel held, and they would say *oh*, and *at last*, and *look* —

— and Wren would stand among them looking up at a sky that had never once held her, belonging to it less than anyone, the only person in the whole company who was losing a homeland instead of getting one back.

"That's not fair," she told the Ember.

The Ember dimmed, apologetic, or maybe just tired. It had been tired for a hundred years. Tiredness that old stops looking like tiredness and starts looking like the way things are.

Wren sat with it a while longer, until the cold came up through the ground and her eyes grew heavy. And as she carried the lantern back to the camp and tucked herself into the fold of Sef's coat, she made a decision, small and hard and entirely her own, the first truly hers-shaped thing she had ever decided:

Whatever the grown-ups chose — valley or stars, lie or truth — she was going to make sure they looked at her when they chose it. Really looked. She was going to stand in front of them and make them see the person their beautiful sky had no room for.

She was nearly asleep when she heard it.

Hoofbeats — no. Not hoofbeats. Something smoother, wronger, a sound like silk dragged over stone, coming up the dead orchard rows from the south. The Ember flared in its lantern, sudden and bright, and Bram sat bolt upright, and Ilsa was already awake, already reaching for her instruments, her face gone white as unmarked paper.

"Riders," she breathed. "No banners. No torches." She swallowed. "That's worse. Only one man travels the Hollow March without needing light."

Wren clutched the lantern to her chest and felt the fire inside lean toward the darkness like a dog scenting an old enemy.

Someone was coming who wanted the stars too.
