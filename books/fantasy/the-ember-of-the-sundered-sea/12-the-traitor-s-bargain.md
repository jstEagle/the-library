## 12. The Traitor's Bargain

Ilsa Vane had spent eleven years learning to read the world's edges, and she knew, with the certainty of an old scar, when something was following them.

It began as a wrongness in the light. The valley's borrowed dusk—always a shade too amber, always a half-tone shy of true—thinned near the old orchard terraces until the shadows lay like cut paper. Then the birds went quiet. Not startled-quiet; that came and went like weather. This was the silence of animals deciding, all at once and without discussion, to be elsewhere.

She said nothing at first. She counted instead: Bram ahead on the path with Wren riding his shoulders, Tam behind her fiddling with a ward-sigil he'd pried off a fencepost, Old Sef bringing up the rear with his nets bundled over one shoulder like a man who refused to stop being a fisherman just because there was no water left to fish. Four. Plus herself. Five souls in a country that was forgetting how to hold them.

The wrongness kept pace.

"Ilsa." Tam's voice, low, at her elbow. He'd noticed too; of course he had. Ward-tinkerers felt surveillance the way sailors felt storms in their teeth. "There's a thread on us. Someone's paying attention."

"Since when?"

"Since the ford, I think. Maybe since the hollow march began." He turned the sigil over in his fingers. It was a cheap thing, orchard-work, meant to keep blight off apples, but its lines were guttering like a candle in wind. "Attention costs power here. Everything does now. Whoever it is, they can afford it."

That was the sentence that made her cold. In a dying valley, wealth was not coin. Wealth was command over what little magic remained, and there were perhaps three people alive who could spend it so freely, and two of them were walking beside her.

The third caught them at moonrise.

* * *

They made camp in the shell of a waystation inn that had been abandoned so long the valley had begun to forget it—the roof gone soft-edged, the doorframe bleeding into the hillside like ink into water. Unmapped places unraveled first; Ilsa had charted the process obsessively since the stars went out, sketching each dissolution in her book of nothing, and she could tell by the blur of the chimney that this building had perhaps a season left before it became a rumor of a building.

Bram built a fire because fire still obeyed them, mostly. Wren fell asleep against Sef's coat with the particular totality of a child who trusted the adults around her completely, and Ilsa watched the fisherman's weathered hand hover over her hair without touching, and thought—not for the first time—that found families were built out of exactly these small withheld gestures.

The lantern sat between them all, its glass dark. The Ember inside had dimmed again. It no longer threw light so much as memory of light, a warmth you believed in rather than saw.

Ilsa was on second watch when the road spoke.

Not loudly. Roads in the Hollow March didn't echo anymore; sound fell into them and was eaten. But she heard hoofbeats the way you hear your own name across a crowded room—impossibly, unmistakably—and she was on her feet with her hand on her knife before the first rider resolved out of the dark.

He came alone. That was the first obscenity. Corvane never traveled alone; the magister wore retinues the way other men wore rings. But here he was, one gray horse, one gray figure, dismounting at the edge of their firelight with the unhurried grace of a man arriving at a dinner party he had scheduled himself.

"Vane." He said her name like a verdict he'd already filed. "You look terrible. The valley air disagrees with you."

"Corvane." She did not move from between him and the sleeping camp. Behind her she heard Bram stir, heard the whisper of his palm pressing flat against his thigh where the scar lived. "You're a long way from the capital."

"The capital is a long way from anywhere that matters." He stepped into the light, and the years had been kind to him in the way that taxidermy is kind. Same silvered temples, same scholar's stoop, same eyes like ledger entries—everything catalogued, nothing felt. What had changed was the hunger underneath. It had been a banked thing when she worked for him. Now it burned open, and it lit him worse than any torch. "May I sit? I've ridden a long way through a country that keeps trying to delete my horse."

"Your horse," Ilsa said, "is the only innocent party here."

He smiled as if she'd paid him a compliment, and sat on a fallen lintel with his hands folded, patient as arithmetic. By then Bram was awake and standing, Wren gathered against his chest, blinking. Tam had a wrench in his fist and the posture of a man prepared to die defending a lantern. Sef hadn't moved at all, which Ilsa recognized as its own kind of weapon.

"You hired me to steal this," Ilsa said, nodding at the lantern. "In Vellmark. You told me it was a reliquary shard. A curiosity." Her voice came out flatter than she wanted, and she hated that he could hear the guilt under it, because he'd always been able to hear everything. "You didn't tell me it was a dragon's heart-fire. You didn't tell me it was the last light of murdered stars."

"I didn't tell you because you would have said no," Corvane said mildly. "You were the best field cartographer I ever employed, Vane, and the worst liar. It was your single flaw and I admired it enormously." He turned his gaze to Bram, and Ilsa watched the boy take the weight of that attention like a blow. "And you must be the heir. The Okafor line runs strong in your face. Your grandmother had the same jaw. I have her survey records."

"My grandmother," Bram said carefully, "was a hedge-witch."

"Your grandmother," Corvane said, "was a Sunderer, as was her mother, and hers, back eleven generations to the ones who paid a dragon to erase this valley from the world. You are standing in the middle of the largest working of sustained magic in recorded history, young man, and it is written in your blood. Do you know what I would have given, at your age, for a bloodline like yours?" He shook his head with genuine, terrible wonder. "Do you know what I gave?"

* * *

He talked, and Ilsa let him, because she wanted the company to hear it—all of it—from his own mouth, unsoftened.

He told them about the archives beneath the capital, where the royal cartographers' guild kept the maps that were never published: coastlines that moved, cities that appeared on charts a century before they were founded, blank spaces that whispered. He told them about the blank space in the northern mountains that every instrument refused, every surveyor forgot, every expedition walked around without knowing why. He told them how, thirty years ago, a junior magister named Corvane had found a fragment in a sealed folio—a single line of verse about a country that bought its own erasure—and had spent his life hunting the truth of it while his colleagues laughed.

"They laughed," he repeated, softly, "because they thought the valley was a myth. And then the stars went out, Vane. Every star in the sky above one invisible country, extinguished in a single night, and suddenly the Royal Academy is very interested in my life's work. Suddenly the blank space has a budget." His smile thinned. "I was sent to retrieve the Ember. To study it. To understand why a dragon's death-cry put out forty constellations at once."

"And instead," Ilsa said, "you decided to keep it."

"Instead," Corvane agreed, "I understood it."

He rose, then, and paced before the fire with the energy of a lecture hall, and Ilsa realized with a lurch that he had rehearsed this—that somewhere on the long ride through the unraveling march, he had composed this speech the way he composed his petitions to the Academy, and that he believed every word of it.

"This valley is dying," he said. "You know it better than anyone. The wards are failing. The unmapped places are eating themselves. Your magic is a memory of magic, running on an inheritance that ran out the night the dragon died. In ten years, maybe twenty, this entire country will be a cold spot on the world—a place where people simply stop, the way my instruments stopped. Everyone you love will end. Not violently. Worse. Gently. The way a song ends when the singer forgets the next note." He stopped, and looked at each of them in turn, and his voice dropped into something almost tender. "But it doesn't have to."

Tam made a sound low in his throat. "You want to rebind the wards."

"I want to *save* them," Corvane said, with such sincerity that Ilsa's stomach turned. "The wards failed because they were cast by frightened people and maintained by ignorance. They were built to exclude, and exclusion starves. But a ward bound in living blood—bound to the heir, renewed by consent, anchored by the Ember itself—" He gestured at the lantern, and for a moment his composure cracked and Ilsa saw the raw want underneath, vast and bottomless. "The valley could be restored. Whole. Eternal. The stars relit—not the real ones, the wards' own stars, woven bright and permanent. No more unraveling. No more forgetting. Your orchards forever. Your sea-that-isn't forever. Children born under a sky that will never go dark again."

Wren, awake now against Bram's shoulder, said in her small clear voice: "Stars that aren't real aren't stars."

Corvane looked at her the way scholars look at interesting anomalies. "Real," he said gently, "is a matter of maintenance."

* * *

"And the price," Bram said.

Ilsa loved him a little, in that moment, for skipping straight to it. He stood with the child on his hip and the lantern-light—or the memory of it—catching the scar on his palm, and he did not flinch.

"The price," Corvane said, "is you."

The word hung in the smoke.

"Not your life. Nothing so crude. Your blood—a binding, renewed each solstice, a drop freely given. Your presence—you would remain, visibly, openly, the heir and warden of your own country. And your consent." He spread his hands, and here was the masterpiece, here was the mercy dressed in its finest clothes: "Because that is what the old wards lacked, boy. Consent. Your ancestors stole their people out of the world without asking. I am offering you the chance to ask. Every soul in this valley, given the choice: stay in the country that raised you, whole and safe and everlasting, or walk out into a world that has forgotten you exist. Those who choose the door—I will not stop them. Those who choose home—home will finally be able to keep them."

He turned to Ilsa last, and his voice softened into something almost intimate, the register he'd used across a decade of shared work, shared tables, shared ambitions. "You of all people know what it is to be erased, Vane. Disgraced. Struck from the guild rolls. Your name pulled from every map you ever drew." A pause, surgical. "Restore the valley, and I restore you. Chief cartographer of the only country in the world that cannot be lost. Your lines on the first complete map of the Hollow March. Immortality of the only kind that matters—the kind inked into the record."

For one shameful heartbeat—one—she felt it land. The guild hall. The vellum. Her name spoken without the word *disgraced* welded to it. She felt it the way you feel a warm hand on a frozen cheek, and she hated herself with a completeness that steadied her.

"That's the offer," she said. "What's the catch?"

"No catch. A condition." Corvane's eyes went past her, to the lantern, and the tenderness drained out of his face and left the ledger behind. "The Ember stays with me. It is the anchor-stone. It does not travel to any sea. It does not answer any dragon. It sits, under seal, in the vault of the new ward, where it can do no one harm and everyone good." He smiled at Bram. "You would be warden of paradise, heir. All I ask is that you never touch the key."

* * *

The silence afterward had texture. Ilsa mapped it the way she mapped everything: Sef's stillness, taut as netting; Tam's knuckles white on the wrench; Wren's face pressed into Bram's neck, watching the stranger with the unblinking assessment only children manage. And Bram—Bram looking down at his own scarred palm as though it had just become a document someone else was trying to sign.

"You'd give them back their stars," Bram said slowly. "Stars you'd make. A sky you'd build. Forever." He looked up. "And in exchange, the valley stays a lie. A beautiful lie, kept by me, fed by my blood, forever. My children's children born into a cage with gilded bars and calling it mercy."

"I prefer the term *sanctuary*."

"I know what I'd be calling it." Bram set Wren down, gently, behind him. When he spoke again his voice shook, but it held. "My master lied to me my whole life and called it protection. My ancestors stole a country and called it safety. And now you're offering me the same bargain with better upholstery, and you actually think you're the generous one in this story."

Something flickered across Corvane's face—hurt, genuinely hurt, and that was the most frightening thing yet, because fanatics were dangerous but sincere fanatics were inexorable. "I have read every account of the outside world's treatment of vanished peoples, heir. I have read what happens to the forgotten. I am offering them the only ending in which nobody drowns."

"Somebody already drowned," Sef said.

Everyone turned. The old fisherman had not spoken since Corvane arrived. He unfolded from his place by the fire with the creak of a mast in wind, and his eyes, pale as old foam, fixed on the magister.

"Forty years I fished a sea that wasn't there," Sef said. "Every morning I baited hooks for fish that were a memory of fish, in water that was a memory of water, and I called it a living, because the alternative was to stand on the shore and scream at a sky with no stars in it. Don't you talk to me about drowning, magister. I've been drowned since the day I was born. So has every soul in this valley. You're not offering to save us." He pointed one rope-scarred finger at the lantern. "You're offering to pickle us."

Corvane regarded him for a long moment. Then, with terrible gentleness: "And what does the valley say, old man, now that the wards are failing and the blanks are spreading? Have you asked the orchards? The waystations? Ask the eastern villages what's left of them." He let that sit. "I am the only man alive offering them anything at all. Remember that, when you refuse me. Refusal is also a choice, and choices have consequences, and I—" he sighed, and picked up his gloves from the lintel, and the courtliness of the gesture was somehow worse than a threat, "—I did hope we could avoid consequences. I'll encamp nearby tonight. Take whatever time you need. The offer stands until dawn."

He mounted, gathered the reins, and paused.

"Oh—Vane. One more thing." He did not look at her; he looked at the dark road ahead, and his voice was almost apologetic. "The wardens of the Hollow March answer to whoever holds the wards' anchor. At present, that is me. I mention it only so that your decision is fully informed."

Then he rode into the unraveling dark, and the road swallowed the sound of him, and the silence he left behind was the loudest thing Ilsa had ever charted.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere far off, a piece of the eastern hills quietly stopped existing, and the night tilted a degree to fill the gap.

"He'll take the Ember by force if we refuse," Tam said at last. "That's what the wardens line means. He didn't come to negotiate. He came to show us the shape of the cage before he builds it."

"He came to split us," Ilsa said. She was surprised by how steady her own voice was. "Did you feel it? He offered each of us a different door. Immortality for me. Order for Tam. An honest sea for Sef. Paradise for the valley. He's a cartographer too, in his way—he maps wants, and he draws roads straight through them." She looked at Bram. "He saved you for last because yours is the only signature that matters."

Bram was staring into the lantern. Inside, the Ember lay like a coal at the bottom of a well—dimmer than yesterday, dimmer than the day before, a pulse rather than a flame. As Ilsa watched, the boy turned his scarred palm toward it, and the ember flickered. Once. Twice. Like something turning in its sleep toward a familiar voice.

"It's getting colder," Bram said quietly. "Whatever he's offering—it's built on this. On keeping it. And it's dying anyway." He closed his fist. "He can't restore the stars with a dead ember. Which means either he doesn't know that, or—"

"Or he knows," Ilsa finished, ice sliding down her spine, "and the restoration was never the point. The binding is. He doesn't need the valley saved, Bram. He needs *you bound*. The rest is wrapping paper."

Above them, through the ruined roof of the forgotten inn, the starless sky pressed down like a held breath. And on the road behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Ilsa had been dreading all night: many boots, marching in step, closing the distance between the dawn and the company of the unmapped.
