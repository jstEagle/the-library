## 13. What Mistress Halden Knew

They came at dusk, when the valley's edges had begun to fray like the hem of an old coat.

Bram saw them first from the ridge above the Hollow March—three wardens in their grey-green cloaks, moving with the unhurried certainty of men who had never once doubted the ground beneath their feet. Between them walked a small figure with her hands bound before her, her grey braid coming loose, her shoulders set in that particular stubborn angle he knew better than his own heartbeat.

*Mistress Halden.*

The word went through him like a splinter driven under a nail. He had imagined this moment a hundred ways since the night the stars vanished—imagined finding her, arguing with her, demanding the truth she had kept behind her closed lips all those years. He had never once imagined her *delivered*, like a parcel, like a debt.

"Is that—" Wren stood beside him on the ridge, her small hand gripping his sleeve. She was the only one of them who could see so far in the dying light; she had been born under a starless sky, and her eyes had learned to make do. "Is that the witch-lady? The one who raised you?"

"Yes." His voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were speaking through his mouth.

Behind them, Ilsa Vane lowered her spyglass and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy. Tam shifted the lantern—the Ember inside it pulsing faintly, a heartbeat of gold—and Old Sef's weathered hand settled briefly on Bram's shoulder, heavy as an anchor.

"They're taking her to Corvane," Tam said. "The camp's in the hollow below the March. We could—"

"We could what?" Ilsa asked quietly. "Six of us against thirty wardens and whatever Corvane has brought out of the old armories?"

But Bram was already sliding down the scree.

* * *

He did not go to rescue her. That was the thing he could not explain later, not to Ilsa, not to himself. Some part of him understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Mistress Halden had not been captured. She had been *summoned*. The wardens walked around her the way soldiers walk around a general they distrust but cannot disobey. And she walked between them with her chin up, scanning the ridgelines—not for escape routes, Bram realized. For *him*.

So he did the cowardly, sensible thing. He let the company circle wide through the unraveling fields where the blankness ate hedgerows whole, and he came down into the warden camp from the dark side, wrapped in Tam's silence-charms, close enough to hear the fire crackle and Corvane's voice rise and fall like a man reading scripture.

Corvane had made his camp in the ruins of the old tithe-barn, and he had transformed it. Candles everywhere—dozens of them, white and steady, arranged in patterns Bram itched to read. Maps hung from the rafters: maps of the valley, drawn in Corvane's own precise hand, annotated in three colors of ink. And at the center, seated in a chair that might once have belonged to the valley's founding council, sat Magister Corvane, silver-haired and gentle-eyed, looking less like a conqueror than a schoolmaster about to deliver a beloved lesson.

Mistress Halden stood before him, unbound now. Her wrists were red where the cords had been.

"You've grown careless, Elowen," Corvane said, and Bram flinched at the name—he had never known her by any name but Halden, hedge-witch, herb-keeper, the woman who smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke. "Leaving your cottage on the night the wards fell. Carrying your grimoire into the March. You might have been hurt."

"I might have been *found*," she said. "There's a difference."

"There always is, with you." Corvane folded his hands. "Where is the boy?"

And Bram, crouched in the shadow of the barn's broken wall with the Ember's light bleeding warm through his coat, stopped breathing.

"You know I won't tell you."

"I know you'll tell me everything," Corvane said, "because you've already told me most of it. Twenty years of reports, Elowen. Did you think the wardens' archives kept themselves?" He rose, crossed to one of his hanging maps, and tapped a point near the valley's southern edge—a cottage, a garden, a well. "A foundling left at your door the winter the Ember was stolen. No name. No history. A scar on the left palm that no midwife could account for. You wrote to the council asking what should be done with it."

"It," Bram repeated silently. The word sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"And the council said destroy it, because a key that no one knows about is safer than a key everyone argues over. And you said—" Corvane turned, and his voice softened into something almost tender, which made it infinitely worse— "you said you would keep it instead. Raise it. Teach it herbs and letters and how to salt meat for winter. Hide it in plain sight, in the safest place in the valley, until such time as the wards needed it."

Halden's hands curled at her sides. "I never used him."

"No. That's rather the tragedy, isn't it?" Corvane sighed. "You were given one task, and you loved your way out of it. I confess I've always admired that about you. It's why I let you live all these years, when the council would have burned you twice over."

"The council is ash and memory, Corvane. Same as everything else here."

"The council is *me* now." He said it without pride, the way a man says *the river floods in spring*. "And I am going to do what you never had the stomach for. I'm going to use the key. Bind the wards with his blood, restore the stars, seal the valley forever—safe, eternal, perfect. Every soul in it preserved. Isn't that what you wanted, all those years, warming your hands over his childhood? To keep him safe?"

"I wanted him *free*."

"Free." Corvane tasted the word and found it sour. "Free to walk off the edge of the world into the Sundered Sea? Free to dissolve into the blankness with the rest of the unmapped country? You raised him on lies, Elowen. At least my lie has walls."

Bram should have run then. Every instinct honed by twenty years of hedge-craft screamed at him to move, to melt back into the dark, to carry what he'd heard to the others and plan. But his feet had rooted, the way they always rooted near wards, near thresholds, near anything that hummed with the valley's borrowed magic. His scarred palm pressed flat against the barn's cold timber, and through the wood he felt it—that faint itch, that wrongness, sharpening now into something almost like speech.

And Mistress Halden lifted her head, and looked directly at the shadow where he crouched.

She couldn't see him. He was certain she couldn't. But her eyes found the darkness and held it, and when she spoke again, her voice changed—lower, rougher, the voice she used for telling him stories about his parents she had invented one winter when he wouldn't stop asking.

"You want to know what I knew," she said to Corvane, though Bram knew—knew with his scar, with his bones—that every word was aimed past the magister, into the dark, at him. "I knew the founders' names. I knew the price they paid. I knew that a dragon does not erase a country from the world without leaving a lock, and that every lock wants a key born of the same blood. When the foundling came to my door with that mark on his palm, I knew exactly whose blood ran in him. The blood of the Sunderers. The people who built this beautiful prison and locked their own children inside it."

"Halden—"

"*Let me speak.*" The fire guttered. The candles bent. For one moment the hedge-witch stood taller than the magister, taller than the barn, and Bram saw in her the woman who must once have terrified councils. "You call it love, keeping him. I called it love too. I told myself I was saving a child. But I'll tell you what I knew, Corvane, what I've known since the night I first held him: I knew that if the day ever came—if the wards ever failed, if the valley ever needed its key—I would be asked to spend that child like a coin. And I knew I would refuse. So I taught him everything *except* what he was. I taught him to heal, to hide, to survive. I taught him to run. Because some part of me hoped that if the day came, he'd be gone over the edge before anyone could catch him."

Her voice broke, just slightly, on the last word.

"I didn't raise a key," she whispered. "I raised a door I refused to open. And I'm glad. Do you hear me, Corvane? Whatever you do to me—I'm *glad* he ran."

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere outside, a warden coughed. The candles righted themselves.

Corvane regarded her for a long moment with something that was almost sorrow. "You realize," he said gently, "that I can't let you leave now. Not because you're dangerous. Because you're *kind*, and kindness is contagious, and I have a country to save."

Two wardens took her arms. She did not resist. But as they led her toward the tent at the camp's edge, she stumbled—deliberately, Bram understood, deliberately—and in the stumble her hand brushed the ground, and her fingers moved, quick as a spell, tracing three signs in the dirt: *north*. *Water*. *Wait*.

Then the tent flap closed behind her, and she was gone.

* * *

He made it back to the company's camp in the black hours, walking because running would mean feeling, and feeling would mean stopping, and stopping was not survivable. The blankness had crept closer; whole acres of the March were simply *absent* now, patches of nothing that the eye slid across like ice. He gave them a wide berth and hated them for existing.

They were waiting up for him. All of them. Wren asleep against Old Sef's chest with the lantern-light gilding her hair; Tam whittling a new ward-sigil and abandoning it; Ilsa awake, of course, her maps spread before her, her pen still. When Bram stepped into the emberlight, she looked up, read his face in a single glance, and folded her maps away.

"She's alive," he said. His voice sounded scraped. "Corvane's keeping her alive. As leverage. Or as—"

"As a lesson," Ilsa said quietly. "He seems a man who collects lessons."

"He told her—" Bram's throat closed. He forced it open. "She reported on me. From the beginning. To the council. What I was. What my blood was for. There were *reports*, Ilsa. Twenty years of them. 'The key eats well tonight.' 'The key lost a tooth.' 'The key asked about his mother again.'"

Sef stirred; Wren woke and blinked, sensing the weather of the adults without understanding it. Tam set down the knife.

"Bram," Ilsa began.

"Don't." He held up his scarred palm, and the mark on it—the old birthmark shaped like a branching tree, or a river delta, or a lock's inner workings—seemed darker in the emberlight than it had ever been. "Don't tell me she loved me anyway. Don't tell me she chose me over the council. I heard her, Ilsa. I heard every word. She stood in front of that man and said she was *glad* I ran, and it was true, and it doesn't—"

It doesn't help. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. He had spent his whole life believing he was an orphan the world had been kind enough to lose track of, and now he knew he was an instrument the world had been careful enough to maintain. Fed. Schooled. Taught to run. Loved the way you love a thing you are keeping in working order—or worse, loved genuinely, truly, and *still* kept, still hidden, still lied to, every single day, because the truth was a tool and tools get used.

Which was worse? He turned the question over and over on the long walk back and could not make the scales settle. A lie told out of cruelty was at least honest about itself. A lie told out of love—

A lie told out of love was the valley entire. Was the whole country, in miniature: warm hearths, sweet water, neighbors who remembered your name, all of it sustained by a forgetting so complete that generations had lived and died inside it calling the cage a homeland. Mistress Halden had not betrayed him. She had done to one boy what the founders had done to ten thousand souls, and she had done it gently, and that gentleness was the deepest wound of all.

He sat down heavily by the fire. Wren detached herself from Sef and came to him without a word, the way children do, and leaned against his side, and he put his arm around her because his body knew how even when his heart had forgotten everything else.

"Was she mean to you?" Wren asked. "The witch-lady?"

"No." His voice cracked. "That's the problem."

Ilsa crouched across the fire from him, her sharp cartographer's face unreadable, though her eyes were not unkind. "Bram. Listen to me. I've spent my life drawing borders, and I'll tell you the one thing I know for certain: a map can be drawn by a liar and still lead you somewhere true. Whatever she built you to be—whatever Corvane thinks you are—you are standing in a place none of them planned. Out here, past the wards, past their designs. Whatever you do next belongs to *you*."

"That's very pretty," Bram said.

"It's cartography," Ilsa said. "It's the truest thing I know."

Tam spoke up from the shadows, hesitant. "The signs she made. North, water, wait. I saw you watching. Do you trust them?"

Bram looked into the fire, into the lantern where the Ember pulsed its slow, patient gold. He thought of grey braid coming loose. Of a voice breaking on the word *run*. Of twenty years of rosemary and woodsmoke and hands teaching his hands to mend what was broken.

He thought of the reports.

"She wanted me to hear it," he said finally. "Everything she said in there. She wasn't talking to Corvane. She was talking to the dark, knowing I was in it. Which means either she's the best liar I've ever known—" he laughed, and it came out broken— "or she spent twenty years loving a key and hating the lock. And I don't know which of those is worse, and I don't know which of those is true, and I don't think I get to know. Not yet."

"So what do we do?" Sef rumbled, Wren's weight shifting against him.

Bram looked north, toward the black line of the Hollow March, beyond which lay the Sundered Sea and the last of the dragon-fire's fading pulse. His palm itched like a struck match.

"We go north," he said. "To the water. And we don't wait longer than we have to—because come morning, Corvane is going to stop talking and start hunting. And this time, he isn't looking for the Ember."

He closed his fist over the scar.

"He's looking for me."
