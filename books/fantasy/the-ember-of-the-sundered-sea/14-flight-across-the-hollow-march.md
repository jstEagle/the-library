## 14. Flight Across the Hollow March

Tam had spent thirty years taking things apart to learn how they held together. Locks. Wards. Clocks. The little brass theodolite Ilsa let him carry, its needle trembling like a frightened bird. He knew the sound a thing made when it was about to fail—the hairline click, the sigh of a spring giving up—and he had built his whole life on the belief that everything broken could be understood, and everything understood could be mended.

The Hollow March made a liar of him by noon.

They heard it before they saw it: a silence moving through the valley like weather. Not quiet—quiet was just an absence of noise—but *silence*, a thing with weight and appetite, eating the sound of their own footsteps as it came.

"Off the road," Ilsa said, low and flat, the way she said everything that mattered. "Now."

They went down the bank into the bracken, Bram pulling Wren against his chest, Old Sef bringing up the rear with his fisherman's rolling gait, unhurried even now, his lantern-hand steady around the Ember's cage. The light inside the glass had gone from gold to a deep bruised orange, and Tam did not like what that color reminded him of: coals at the end of the night, when the fire has stopped being a fire and started being a memory of one.

Through the branches he watched the road come apart.

It did not burn or crumble. It *unwrote* itself. The packed clay of the Hollow March road simply stopped being there, in a spreading patch like ink lifted off a page, and where the road had been was nothing—not grass, not mud, not dark, but a smooth and total blankness that hurt to look at, the way a hole in a painting hurts, the eye insisting on a picture that isn't there. A fence post at the road's edge leaned over emptiness and hung there, rooted in nothing, still stubbornly being a fence post.

"The unmapped places go first," Ilsa whispered. She had her journal out, sketching even now, her pencil moving fast. "The valley is forgetting them. Everything no one bothered to remember."

"And us?" Wren asked, small voice muffled against Bram's shoulder. "Is anybody remembering us?"

Tam's hands, of their own accord, checked the ward-tinker's kit on his belt. Charms he'd built in exile, half of them jury-rigged, all of them honest work. "I am," he said. "I'm remembering every one of you. That's not nothing. Memory's a kind of warding, if you build it right."

He wasn't sure it was true. He said it anyway, because someone had to, and because Wren's face did something complicated and then settled.

* * *

They ran the whole afternoon along the margins of the blankness, and Tam learned its grammar as they went, because grammar was the only thing he trusted anymore.

The unraveling followed the thin places—the spots where the founders' spell had been stitched cheapest, the roads nobody named, the fields left fallow so long even the valley's memory had let them blur. Where people had loved a place loudly—a shrine, a swimming hole, Sef's own crooked jetty on the dead river—the world held longer, fraying at the edges only, going soft and gray like cloth worn thin. But the Hollow March had always been the valley's back-of-the-hand, the country between the villages that everyone crossed and no one looked at, and it was coming apart in sheets.

By late afternoon the sky was wrong too. Not dark—the stars had never come back—but *thin*, a gray gauze stretched overhead, and once Tam saw, for half a heartbeat, the blankness reflected in it, a pale absence drifting like a jellyfish, and he decided firmly not to have seen that.

"We can't outrun it," Bram said at the next halt. He was breathing hard, Wren on his hip now because the child's legs were done. "It's faster than we are. It's—" He stopped, jaw working. "It's coming from everywhere. Isn't it. The whole valley is dying and we're running toward the one place it dies last."

"The sea," Sef said.

Everyone looked at him. The old man stood with his back to a lightning-split oak, watching the treeline with the patience of a man who had watched water his whole life and knew that water always told you what it meant to do, if you knew how to read it.

"The Sundered Sea," he said. "Where the founders paid their price. The oldest story in the valley. Oldest things hold longest." He spat, thoughtful. "Also the Ember wants to go home. You can tell. It pulls, same as a tide."

Tam felt it then, for the first time, because Sef had named it: a faint lean in the air itself, the way a room leans toward an open window. The Ember's cage swung a finger's width off plumb, always in the same direction, southeast, toward water none of them could hear yet.

Ilsa marked it on her map without comment. Her maps had stopped pretending to be records days ago; they were arguments now, lines drawn hard against the erasure, and Tam understood with a lurch that her cartography had become a form of prayer.

* * *

The ravine found them at dusk.

It hadn't been there yesterday—Tam was nearly sure of that, though certainty had become a luxury good. The valley was rearranging itself as it died, old shapes surfacing like bones in a floodplain, and this was one of the old ones: a deep cut through the March, forty feet of sheer shale dropping to a stream that ran silver-gray and slightly wrong, the water moving without quite rippling.

On the far side, the land climbed toward the sea-road, and beyond it, faint as breath on glass, they could see the horizon line where the gray sky met a darkness that might have been water.

And across the ravine, spanning it on two leaning pylons and a web of rope and plank, hung a bridge.

Tam knew bridges. This one had been a footbridge once, a tinker's bridge, the kind his own guild had slung across a hundred gullies—ward-line woven into the ropes to keep the planks true. Someone had maintained it for generations without knowing why, the way valley folk did everything, and the ward-work in it was old and sweet and *failing*, the knots dimming one by one like lamps going out down a long corridor.

"There," Bram said.

"No," Ilsa said, at the same moment, because she'd seen what Tam had seen: the far anchorage was already graying, the cliff-face behind it gone soft-focus, the blankness waiting on the other side like a held breath.

"It's the only crossing," Bram said. "You said it yourself. The March is closing behind us like a book."

"I said the *mapped* crossing was three miles south. Which means the wardens know about it, which means Corvane knows about it, which means—"

"Which means this one's ours," Sef finished, and stepped onto the bridge.

Tam would remember the sound of it afterward: the creak of old rope under an old man's weight, and beneath it a hum, faint and rising, the bridge's dying wards waking to be used one more time. Sef walked out onto the planks the way he walked a jetty in weather—knees soft, weight centered, reading the sway. Halfway across he turned and beckoned, impatient, alive, his white beard full of wind.

"Well?" he called. "I'm not getting younger and neither are you!"

They went. Bram first with Wren, then Ilsa with the map-case strapped tight, then Tam, who hated bridges on principle—too many parts, all of them load-bearing—and kept one hand on the rope and counted the ward-knots as he passed, because counting calmed him, and because each knot he passed flickered a little brighter under his fingers, the way a dog lifts its head when someone kind walks by.

Wren made it across laughing, actually laughing, the swaying turned to a game. Bram hauled her over the far rail. Ilsa followed. Tam was three planks from the end when the wardens came.

* * *

They came out of the trees on the near side, six of them, gray-cloaked, faces hidden behind masks of pale bark, and Tam's stomach dropped through the ravine because he recognized the lead warden's stride—he'd drunk cider with that stride, years ago, in another life, before the questions that got him exiled.

"Hold!" the warden called, and the word hit the bridge like a thrown stone. The nearest ward-knot flared and died. "The traitor Okafor will come back across. The rest of you are void-touched. You'll be cleansed."

"They're children and old men and a mapmaker," Bram shouted back, standing on the far rim with his scarred palm pressed to the anchor-stone, and Tam saw the stone glow faintly under that hand, blood answering blood. "The valley is *dying*. Look behind you! Help us instead of hunting us!"

"The valley does not die," the warden said, with the terrible calm of liturgy. "The valley is *kept*. Come back, heir, and be kept with it."

The blankness chose that moment to arrive.

It came down the near slope without haste, without malice, a gray tide of pure unbeing, and where it touched the wardens' trees the trees stopped. One warden screamed—a human sound, young, terrified—and Tam watched the man turn and run *toward* the bridge rather than into that, which told Tam everything about what courage actually was.

The bridge groaned. The blankness reached the near pylon and the pylon didn't fall; it *ceased*, and the whole span lurched sideways, rope singing, planks tilting, and Tam dropped flat and hugged the ropes and thought, absurdly, of his mother's loom.

"Sef!" Bram roared. "Come *on*!"

But Sef had already turned around.

Tam saw it happen and would spend the rest of his life seeing it: the old man standing at the bridge's midpoint, the blankness chewing through the near anchorage behind him, the far side four strides away and climbing. And between Sef and safety, the great anchor-rope—the one holding the whole span to the far cliff—had parted from its mooring in the chaos and was whipping loose, and if it went, the bridge became a dead thing, and everyone on it died, and the Ember went into the gray with them.

Sef caught the rope.

He wrapped it twice around his forearms the way he'd tie off a net in heavy swell, planted his boots on the tilting planks, and *leaned back*, an old man against the whole weight of the falling span, and the rope went taut, and the bridge held.

"Go," Sef said. Conversational. Almost amused. "Cut the near lines. Let my end drop. I'll hold this one till you're clear."

"You'll fall," Tam heard himself say. His voice came out very small.

"Aye," said Sef. "Probably. Tide comes in, tide goes out. I've had seventy years of water, boy, and every day of it was a gift I didn't pay for." He grinned through his beard, and his arms were shaking, and his boots were sliding a half-inch at a time, and he did not stop grinning. "Remember the sea for me. That's the toll. Remember it *properly*. Salt and gulls and cold enough to hurt. None of your gray nothing."

Bram was screaming no. Wren was screaming no. Ilsa had her knife out and was cutting the near lines because someone had to do what Sef said, and her face was wet and her cuts were clean.

The last rope parted. The near half of the bridge dropped away into the ravine, taking the blankness with it, and the far half swung and steadied, anchored now only by one old man's grip and one old man's refusal, and Tam scrambled the final planks and fell onto solid stone beside the others, and turned, and looked back.

Sef looked small from there. Small and straight-backed, holding his rope above the gray water, the Ember's light reaching him faintly through the cage-glass and painting him orange, one last warm color in a world going gray.

"Southeast!" he shouted, cheerful as a market-day greeting. "Follow the pull! And mind the child learns the names of fish!"

Then the blankness took the far anchorage below him, and the cliff he stood against quietly stopped being a cliff, and Sef did not scream, and did not fall, because there was no longer any such thing as down. He simply grew fainter, rope and boots and beard dissolving outward like breath off a window, and the last thing to go was his hand on the rope, gripping, gripping, and then the rope drifted loose with nothing holding it, and the ravine was empty, and the gray closed over the place where a man had been as smoothly as water closing over a stone.

* * *

They ran.

Nobody spoke. There was nothing to say that wouldn't take time they didn't have, and grief, Tam discovered, could run. It ran in his legs and his lungs and the backs of his eyes, and Wren sobbed against Bram's neck without any sound at all, which was worse than screaming, and Ilsa's jaw was set so hard Tam could hear her teeth when she breathed.

The land behind them unraveled in silence. Once Tam glanced back and saw the ravine itself smoothing over, the shale and the stream and the empty air where Sef had stood all flattening into featureless pale nothing, the valley healing over its own wound by forgetting it had ever been cut. By morning there would be no ravine. By morning there would be no proof any of it had happened except five people and a fading ember and a promise.

*Remember the sea properly.*

"I will," Tam said aloud, to no one, to the gray air, because he was a tinker and a maker and he knew that some things only become real when you say them into the material. "Salt and gulls and cold enough to hurt. I'll build it into every ward I make for the rest of my life. You'll be load-bearing, old man."

Ahead, through the thinning trees, the ground began to smell of salt.

That was wrong—that was impossible, the Sundered Sea was a day away at least—but the wind came anyway, off the southeast, carrying a scent that had no business existing in a landlocked pocket of erased country, and Tam realized the valley was leaking its edges, the old spell letting the real world seep through the cracks like smoke through a failing chimney.

The Ember flared.

Not gently—*violently*, the cage-glass blazing white-orange, and Wren twisted in Bram's arms and stared at it, and her tear-streaked face went strange and still.

"She's talking," Wren said.

"Who's talking?" Ilsa demanded, skidding to a halt.

"The dragon." Wren's voice was tiny and certain. "She's saying it's almost time. She's saying—" The child frowned, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear, and her small hand rose and pressed flat against the hot glass. "She's saying the heir has to hurry. Because she can't hold on much longer. Because she's so *cold*."

Behind them, the last of the Hollow March went out like a lamp.

Ahead, past the tree line, under a sky gone thin as paper, the darkness of the sea waited—and in it, impossibly, faintly, one point of light. A star. Or the ghost of one. Or a door.

Bram shifted Wren higher on his hip, looked down at his scarred palm, and started to run.
