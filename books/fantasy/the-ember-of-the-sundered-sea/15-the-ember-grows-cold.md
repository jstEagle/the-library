## 15. The Ember Grows Cold

The Ember began to die on the third morning after Sef's loss, and none of us noticed at first because grief is its own kind of darkness.

We had made camp in the ruins of an orchard where the trees had gone strange—trunks of pale glass that chimed when the wind moved through them, fruit hanging like frozen tears. Tam said it was a place the wards had half-forgotten, a memory of an orchard rather than an orchard itself, and that we should eat nothing from it. So we sat among the chiming trees with empty bellies and full silence, and Ilsa kept the lantern between her knees the way she always did, one hand resting on the glass as if she could feel its pulse.

She could. That was the thing about Ilsa Vane—she pretended to be all instruments and ink and cold arithmetic, but her hands knew things before her mouth admitted them. I watched her fingers still against the lantern's side. Watched her frown. Watched her lift the lantern and turn it slowly, the way you'd turn a compass that had begun to lie to you.

"Bram," she said. "Come look at this."

The Ember had been the color of a hearth at midnight since the day I'd first seen it: deep orange shot through with veins of gold, alive in a way fire isn't supposed to be alive. It had guttered when the wardens attacked us. It had flared bright as a struck match when Sef fell holding the crossing, so bright that Wren had cried out and covered her eyes. But now—

Now it was the color of old honey left too long in the dark. The gold veins were thin as spider silk. When I crouched beside Ilsa and held my scarred palm near the glass, the warmth that usually answered me—the faint tug, like a hand reaching through water—was barely there at all.

"It's dimming," I said, which was the stupidest possible observation, but fear does that to a person.

"Fire doesn't dim," Ilsa said. "Fire goes out. This is doing something else." She turned the lantern again. "It's pulling inward. Like a man folding his arms around a wound."

Tam came over, wiping solder-ash from his fingers onto a rag he'd been carrying since before I met him. He was older than my mistress Halden, with forearms like knotted rope and a face that had been weathered by more than weather—by exile, by questions no one wanted asked. He looked into the lantern for a long time without touching it.

"When did it last speak?" he asked.

Ilsa and I looked at each other. Neither of us answered, because neither of us understood the question.

"The Ember doesn't speak," Ilsa said carefully. "It's dragon-fire. A shard of heart-flame. It burns. It answers blood. It doesn't *speak*."

"Everything speaks," Tam said. "You just have to be the right kind of listening." He raised his voice, not much, just enough to carry across the glass orchard. "Wren."

* * *

She was sitting against the farthest tree, the way she'd sat every night since the crossing. Four years old and already she had learned to make herself small when the adults went quiet, as though she understood that our silences were load-bearing and mustn't be disturbed. She was born the night the stars went out—born starless, into a country that had never once shown her the sky it was supposed to have—and sometimes I caught her looking up at the blank dark over the Hollow March with an expression no child should own: patient, and a little accusing, like someone waiting for a debt to be paid.

She came when Tam called. She always came when anyone called, as if she were afraid that not answering might teach people to stop calling.

Wren knelt by the lantern without being told what to do. She didn't touch it. She leaned close, so close her breath fogged the glass, and she listened the way children listen—with her whole body, eyes shut, lips slightly parted, absolutely certain that the world had something worth saying.

Then her eyes opened, very wide, very dark.

"She's sad," Wren said.

The word dropped into our camp like a stone into a well. I heard it fall all the way down.

"Who's sad?" Ilsa asked, in the careful voice she used for maps that wouldn't record themselves.

"The fire lady." Wren put both small hands flat on the glass. "She's been calling and calling and nobody came, and now she thinks nobody's ever coming, so she's going to sleep. She says—" Wren's brow furrowed, and I saw her struggle, a child trying to carry something far too heavy in arms too short. "She says sleeping is easier than waiting. She says she waited a long time already. Longer than anything."

"Wren," I said gently, "the Ember is just fire. It can't—"

"She's not just fire." Wren looked up at me, and there was no argument in her face at all, only a kind of sorrowful certainty that made my throat close. "She's a *who*. You just forgot her. Everybody forgot her. That's why she's going out. Not dying." Wren shook her head, correcting herself with the precision of someone repeating words she'd been given. "*Forgetting*. She says there's a difference. Dying is when your body stops. Forgetting is when everybody stops remembering you're real, and then you get thin, and then you get quiet, and then—" She stopped. Her hands pressed harder against the glass. "Then you're a story nobody tells."

The glass trees chimed around us. Somewhere behind the hills, the unraveling places hissed like rain on hot iron—I could hear them even here now, the blankness eating at the edges of the valley, unmapped ground dissolving into nothing. We had lost Sef to it four days ago. We would lose more. Every hour we spent grieving or arguing or sleeping was an hour the Hollow March grew hungrier.

But none of that mattered for the length of time it took me to kneel down next to Wren and look into the lantern properly.

Because she was right. Gods help me, she was right, and I had known it without knowing it, the way you know a room is wrong before you see what's moved. The Ember wasn't fire. Fire doesn't grieve. Fire doesn't fold inward around a wound. What I had been carrying—what Ilsa had carried across half the world, what a dragon had paid out of its own chest at the Sundering—was not fuel.

It was a heart.

* * *

Ilsa would not accept it at first. I watched her build the refusal brick by brick, because refusal was how she survived; she had told me once, in the low honest hours after Corvane's name first came up, that she had learned young that wonder gets people killed and doubt keeps them fed. She took out her instruments. She measured the lantern's heat with a brass gauge that read in gradations finer than a whisper. She held a lens of ground crystal to the glass and studied the gold veins until her eye watered.

"Heat output is down forty percent," she reported finally, in the tone of a woman reading her own death warrant. "Flame coherence is degrading along the same curve. Whatever Wren is hearing, the physical reality matches it. It's failing."

"Failing," Tam repeated. He spat thoughtfully into the ash. "Or being forgotten. Same thing, maybe, in this valley. Maybe in any valley." He lowered himself onto a glass root with the groan of an old man who had earned every ache. "Think about what this place is, girl. It's a memory of magic. The wards don't make anything new—they hold a shape the founders remembered. And the Ember is the oldest thing in it. Older than the wards. Older than the lie. It's the one piece of the true world they carried inside with them." He tapped his temple. "And memories fade when nobody tends them."

"Then we tend it," I said. "How? Tell me how and I'll do it."

"You can't," Ilsa said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and I realized she wasn't arguing with me—she was arguing with the lens, with the numbers, with the forty percent. "Bram, I've carried this thing for six years. Six years I've talked to it, cursed it, begged it, slept with it under my coat in the Frostreach passes. It never answered me. Not once. If it needed tending, why would it wait until now to—"

"Because you're not the one it's waiting for," Wren said.

We all turned. She was still kneeling by the lantern, and the last of the Ember's light lay on her face, and I saw that she had been crying without any fuss at all, the way children cry when they've already understood the worst part and are only waiting for the adults to catch up.

"She says the heir has to remember her," Wren said. "Only the heir. Because the heir's family is the one who paid her and promised. She says a promise made in blood can only be kept in blood." Wren wiped her nose with the back of her wrist. "She says Bram knows the promise. It's in his hand."

My scar. The mark on my palm that had itched near the wards my whole life, that had burned when the stars went out, that the wardens' captain had stared at with something like worship and something like horror. I had spent nineteen years thinking of it as a flaw—a burn from a kettle, Halden had said once, when I was small enough to believe her. Then a key. Then a brand of treachery.

Never once had I thought of it as a signature.

"What promise?" My voice came out rough. "What promise, Wren? Ask her. Ask her what my family promised."

Wren closed her eyes again. The wind moved through the glass orchard and the whole ruined world rang softly, like the inside of a bell. When she spoke, she spoke slowly, translating, and each word landed on me like a stone laid on a grave.

"She says: *you erased us from the world, and we let you, and in exchange we keep your light.*" Wren's small hands curled against the glass. "She says: *when you are ready to come back, bring me home, and I will burn away the lie.*" Her eyes opened. "She says your great-great-grandmothers swore it at the sea. All of them together. She says they put their promise in their blood so they couldn't forget it, and the blood remembers even when the people forget." Wren looked straight at me, and I swear the Ember brightened—not much, a coal's worth, a candle's worth—but it brightened, and it was looking at me too. "She's been waiting two hundred years for somebody to come back and say it out loud."

* * *

I walked away from the camp after that. Not far. Just past the chiming trees, to where the hillside fell away toward the black line of the unraveling ground, and I stood there in the starless dark with my scarred hand open at my side like something I'd found on the road and didn't know how to return.

Two hundred years. My family had done this. Had fled a war, had paid a dragon to erase them from the world, had sworn to come back—and hadn't. Generation after generation of Okafor blood, living soft in a stolen pocket of comfort, tending gardens under a painted sky, while the dragon who bought their safety thinned into a story nobody told. While the promise in their blood itched and burned and was called a kettle-scald.

Halden had known. I was sure of it now, sure in the marrow-deep way I'd been refusing to be sure since the wardens took her. She hadn't just raised me. She had *kept* me, the way you keep a letter you're afraid to send. A living key. A contingency. And she had loved me anyway—or loved me *because*, or loved me instead of using me—and I couldn't untangle which, and maybe I never would.

Footsteps behind me. I knew the weight of them. Ilsa came and stood at my shoulder, arms crossed against the cold, looking out at the dark with me.

"If it's true," she said, "if the dragon will answer you—then Corvane was right about one thing. You're the most dangerous object in this valley." She said it flatly, without cruelty, the way she reported instrument readings. "He wants to bind the wards with your blood forever. His valley, eternal, preserved. But if the Ember answers its heir..." She paused. "Then you're not a key to the cage, Bram. You're the key to the door. And everything depends on which lock you choose."

"I don't know how to choose," I said. "I don't even know what the promise *is*. Wren gave me pieces. Erased from the world. Bring her home. Burn away the lie. But a promise is more than its words—it's the meaning underneath, and the meaning died with the people who made it."

"No," Ilsa said. "It didn't." She turned to look at me, and in the faint emberlight leaking up the hillside her face was stripped of all its cartographer's composure, and what was underneath was the young woman who had once been hired to steal a heart of fire and had refused, and had been ruined for it, and had carried it anyway. "Meanings don't die, Bram. They just wait for someone to pick them up. That's what inheritance *is*. Nobody asks you first. Believe me." A ghost of a smile. "Nobody asked me to carry the lantern either."

Behind us, Wren's voice rose, thin and clear over the chiming trees. She was talking to the lantern. Not translating anymore—just talking, the way you talk to someone lonely, telling them about her day, about the glass fruit and the cold and the man named Sef who used to smell like salt and had gone into the blankness and hadn't come back. Telling the dragon the news of the world, such as it was.

And the Ember was answering. Faintly. In pulses. Bright, dim, bright—slow as breathing, slow as a heartbeat climbing back up out of a long faint.

Tam appeared at the top of the rise, silhouetted against the last glow, and his voice carried down the hill with the gravity of a man delivering a verdict.

"She'll last three days," he called. "Maybe four, if we keep her warm and keep her company. After that she sleeps, and no heir in the world wakes a forgotten god." He let that sit in the cold air. "So whatever door you mean to open, boy—you'd best find it fast."

I closed my scarred hand into a fist, and inside my palm, for the first time in my life, the mark did not itch.

It pulled. Northward. Toward the sea.
