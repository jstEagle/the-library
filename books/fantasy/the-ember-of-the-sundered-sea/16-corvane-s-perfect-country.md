## 16. Corvane's Perfect Country

Corvane had always believed that mercy was a discipline, and that most men failed at it because they lacked the stomach for its arithmetic.

He stood at the window of the warden's hall—the old one, the one the valley's founders had built of grey stone and forgotten purpose—and watched the Hollow March unravel. It was a slow thing, the unmaking. A hedgerow would go first, its green paling to a colorless nothing, like ink lifted from parchment. Then the fence posts. Then, if he watched long enough, the memory of the fence posts, so that even his eye slid off the place where they had been and found nothing worth reporting. The blankness did not spread like fire. It spread like forgetting.

*Good*, he thought. *Let them see what I am saving them from.*

Behind him, in the great room, his guests waited. He could hear them: the tinkerer Tam arguing in a low, urgent voice with the fisherman; the child Wren asking questions no one wanted to answer; the cartographer Vane silent in the way of people who have learned that silence is the only weapon left to them. And somewhere among them, the boy. Okafor. The key. The heir to a country that had never truly existed.

Corvane adjusted his cuffs. He had dressed carefully tonight—not in the traveling leathers of the march, but in the deep blue of the old royal cartographers' guild, the guild that had expelled Ilsa Vane and buried her maps. The blue suited him. Authority should be visible, he believed. Mercy should wear its face openly, or people mistook it for a knife.

He turned from the window and went in to save them.

* * *

The great room had been a council chamber once, before the Sundering, when the founders still remembered parliaments and quarrels and the ordinary friction of governed lives. Now it was all candlelight and dust-sheets over furniture no one used, because the wardens did not govern—they only guarded, and guarding required no chairs. Corvane had ordered the sheets removed. He wanted the room to remember what it was for.

His soldiers—his *riders*, though they had not ridden anything through the unraveling miles except fear—had arranged the company along one wall. Not cruelly. He had been explicit about that. No bindings on the child. Water for the old man. The boy Bram seated, not standing, because a man about to be offered salvation should not receive it on his feet like a prisoner at trial.

Ilsa Vane stood apart from the others, as she always did, arms crossed, her ruined instruments hanging from her belt like the bones of a dead trade. She looked at him the way she must once have looked at the horizon she could not map: with hatred, and with hunger.

"Magister," she said. "You've put on weight since the Guild."

"I've put on *purpose*, Vane. There is a difference." He let himself smile, because she would expect cruelty, and the expectation itself was part of what he intended to dismantle. "Sit. All of you. What I have to say will take some time, and I refuse to deliver it to a standing audience. Standing audiences think they're being executed."

They sat. Even the fisherman, Old Sef, whose eyes had gone white-rimmed and strange since the crossing at the ford—since whatever he had held back there, alone, while the rest of them ran. Corvane noted the grief on him the way a physician notes a fever. Grief was fuel. Grief, properly tended, made people desperate enough to listen.

The boy sat last, and sat badly, coiled like a snare ready to spring. His right hand was wrapped in linen where the Ember had burned him—or blessed him; the distinction depended entirely on what happened next. Corvane met his eyes and held them.

"You are Bram Okafor," he said. "Descended of Aldric Okafor, First Warden of the Sundered Valley, who signed the Covenant of Erasure with a dragon's fire and his own blood. You are the last living vessel of that blood. And three nights ago, in Mistress Halden's cottage, you felt the wards fail, and you ran toward the edge of the world, and you discovered that the world has an edge." He paused. "Have I summarized correctly?"

"You've summarized like a man reading someone else's letters," the boy said.

"Then I am doing my job." Corvane folded his hands. "Let me read you the rest."

* * *

He told them about the war.

Not the valley's version—the founders' version, the one stitched into every lullaby, in which their ancestors fled persecution and were delivered by a dragon's mercy into a hidden country where they could be safe forever. That version was true the way a portrait is true. It captured the face and omitted the rot beneath the skin.

"The founders were not fleeing persecution," Corvane said. "They were fleeing *consequence*. They had made bargains—blood bargains, dragon bargains—in the last years of the Border Wars, and the bargains came due, and rather than pay, they paid a greater dragon to erase the debt along with themselves. They cut this valley out of the world. Out of its maps, its histories, its debts, its *memory*. Every soul born here since has been born inside a lie so complete that the lie grew roots and called itself home."

"That's not—" Tam started.

"It is," Corvane said gently. "You know it is. You asked your questions, Tinkerer, and exile was your answer. Ask yourself why a country needs to exile its questioners unless the questions are load-bearing."

Tam shut his mouth. In the silence, the child Wren said, "What's a debt?" and Old Sef pulled her against his side, and Corvane felt something twist behind his ribs that he chose, deliberately, to interpret as resolve.

"Now," he continued, "here is what no one in this valley knows, because knowing it would have ended everything: the spell is dying. Has been dying for two hundred years. A lie requires maintenance, and the maintainers are gone, and the dragon that powered the wards spent its heart-fire buying your ancestors' escape and has been smoldering in a lantern ever since, going out by inches. The stars above this valley were never stars. They were the dragon's embers, hung in a painted sky. Three nights ago, the last of them guttered. The wards failed. The blankness you saw eating the hedgerows—that is not the outside world devouring you. That is the *spell* devouring itself. The valley is not under attack. The valley is *expiring*."

He let that sit. He watched it land on each of them in turn: Sef's hands tightening on the child; Vane's jaw working; the boy Bram going very still, the stillness of a man hearing his own diagnosis.

"And here," Corvane said, "is what I propose to do about it."

* * *

He rose, and walked to the hearth, and from his coat he took a thing he had carried across half the world: a lens, ground from star-glass, set in a frame of ward-iron. Through it, the room changed. The candleflames showed their true colors—every fire a fragment of the same original flame, the dragon-flame, the first light. He held the lens up so they could see.

"When I was a young magister in the capital," he said, "I found a fragment of the Covenant in the sealed archives. A single page. Most of it was formulae—binding-words, ward-geometry, the mathematics of erasure. But one line was plain text, written by Aldric Okafor's own hand, and it has haunted me for thirty years." He recited it from memory, slowly, savoring it: *'We do this so that our children may inherit a whole world, and never know it was ever broken.'*

"A beautiful sentence," Corvane said. "A monstrous sentence. Do you see why? He didn't write *so our children may heal the world*. He wrote *never know*. He made ignorance the inheritance. He built a country out of forgetting and called it love." He lowered the lens. "I intend to finish the sentence properly."

He laid out his plan the way he laid out everything: plainly, patiently, in order, trusting the order itself to persuade.

"The valley can be saved. The wards can be rebuilt—not patched, *rebuilt*—anchored in living blood instead of a dying dragon. The blood exists. It is sitting in this room, wearing linen on its hand. With the Okafor heir bound willingly into the new Covenant, the wards would hold for a thousand years. The stars would relight—not the dragon's fading embers, but new lights, bound-fires, permanent. The blankness would stop at the border and reverse. Every unmapped place, every unraveling field and forgotten road, restored. And the people—" he opened his hands, "—the people would be safe. Truly safe. Forever."

"Forever," Ilsa Vane repeated. Her voice was flat as a frozen lake. "Under whose administration?"

"Mine," Corvane said, without flinching, because flinching was for men who doubted. "Yes. Let us say the word plainly, since Vane will say it anyway: mine. I would be warden-king. I would hold the Covenant. I would hold the keys to the sky."

The room erupted—Sef on his feet, Tam shouting something about chains, the boy rising with his bandaged hand curled into a fist. Corvane waited them out with the patience of a man who had waited thirty years already.

When they quieted, he spoke softly, and the softness was worse than any shout.

"Chains. Yes. Say the word. Now let me show you what the alternative looks like." He gestured to the window, to the night beyond, where the blankness had reached the orchard wall below the hall. "Out there is the real world, the one your founders fled. I have seen it up close. I served it. I watched it swallow a village in the Border Wars—not with armies, just with *attention*, with taxes and conscription and plague and the slow grinding machinery of a history that does not care who you are. Your founders were not cowards. They were the only honest men of their generation. They looked at the world and said: we cannot survive being known. And they were *right*."

He moved down the line of them as he spoke, unhurried.

"If I open the border—if I do what the boy wants, what Vane wants, what the dragon's ghost wants—and let the valley back into the world, here is what happens. Within a generation, the valley folk are a curiosity. Within two, a labor force. Their songs get collected by scholars and their names get misspelled on tax rolls and their children grow up ashamed of their accents. Some of them will thrive. Most of them will be *used*. And in three hundred years there will be no valley folk at all—only a footnote, and a museum somewhere in the capital with a reconstructed cottage and a placard explaining what these quaint people believed about dragons." He stopped in front of Bram. "That is the future you are carrying in your fist, boy. Extinction with scenery."

"And yours?" Bram said. His voice cracked and steadied. "What do you call a thousand years behind walls?"

"I call it *salvation*," Corvane said. "And I know how that word sounds to you. So look at me. Look at my face. I am not Corvane the conqueror. I am not a man who wants your valley. I am a man who has read every surviving record of what the world does to small peoples, and I am telling you: I can stop it. I can hold the door shut for a thousand years. Your children will grow up under real stars—bound-stars, bright ones. They will farm and sing and marry and quarrel and live entire lives untouched by the machinery that ate everyone your founders ever feared. They will never know what almost got in. Is that a prison?" His voice roughened, and he let it. "Then it is a prison the way a mother's arms are a prison. Someone has to hold the door. I am offering to be the one who holds it, so that none of you ever have to carry that weight again."

Silence. The candles burned. Somewhere far off, another piece of the orchard went out of the world with a sound like a held breath released.

It was the child who broke it. Wren, peering up at him with the terrible directness of the very young: "If it's so nice inside, why do you need soldiers?"

Corvane crouched to her level, which cost him something and which he paid gladly, because sincerity was a discipline too.

"Because some people prefer the wolf," he said. "Even children. Especially children, when the wolf is a story about freedom." He straightened. "I don't need soldiers to keep the valley in. The valley will stay in because staying in is *better*, and I will spend the rest of my life proving it. The soldiers are for the wolves outside. For magisters like the ones who expelled Vane. For kings who would find this valley eventually, wards or no wards, and not bother to ask whether its people wished to be preserved or plundered. I am not your jailer. I am the lock on a door that the world has been trying to break down for two hundred years."

* * *

He gave them until dawn. He was sincere about that too—sincerity without limits was indistinguishable from fanaticism, and he was not a fanatic; he was an administrator of miracles.

They took the offer away to the east tower, under guard that was courteous to the point of ceremony, and Corvane stood alone in the council chamber with the star-glass lens in his hand and watched the blankness take the orchard.

He did not doubt. Doubt was a luxury of men with incomplete information, and his information was complete. He had read the archives. He had walked the capitals. He had seen what the world did to the small and the sung-about. Every argument the boy would make at dawn—the truth, the freedom, the real sky—Corvane had already made to himself, decades ago, alone, at a desk in the sealed archives, and he had answered every one with evidence. The valley could not survive being known. Therefore it must never be known. Therefore someone must hold the door. The logic was airtight. It had to be airtight. Everything depended on it being airtight.

And yet.

He turned the lens in his fingers and thought of the line in Aldric Okafor's hand—*so that our children may inherit a whole world*—and noticed, not for the first time, the way the ink had trembled on the word *whole*. As if the founder had written it and heard, even then, how hollow it rang. As if some part of him had known that a world with the truth cut out of it was not whole at all, only sealed.

Corvane put the thought away, as he always did, in the drawer where he kept such things.

Below the window, the last apple tree in the orchard faded like breath off glass. He watched it go without flinching. By morning the boy would agree—he was certain of it, nearly certain, certain enough—and the wards would rise again, and the stars would burn, and the valley would be perfect, and perfect things do not require anyone's permission.

In the east tower, faintly, he heard the child Wren begin to hum a tune no one in the valley knew—a tune she claimed the lantern had taught her, a tune that sounded, to Corvane's trained ear, like the opening bars of the Covenant itself.

He frowned, and reached for his seal, and sent for his riders.
