## 17. The Door Out of a Country That No Longer Exists

The Ember led them north through country that was forgetting itself.

Bram felt it first in his feet—the road beneath them going soft in places, not muddy but *unfinished*, as though the valley had simply stopped deciding what the ground should be. Then the trees began to fail at their edges: an oak that ended mid-branch in a blur of gray suggestion, a hedgerow whose far side was a smear of color with no leaves in it. Tam walked with his palm pressed flat to everything they passed, muttering ward-counts under his breath like a man counting coins he already knew were short.

"Unmapped places go first," he said, when Wren asked. "The wards held the whole valley in one shape, and now that shape is coming apart from the seams inward. Like a quilt unraveling."

"And we're walking toward the biggest seam," Ilsa said.

"I'm walking toward the biggest seam," Bram said. "You're all free to turn around."

No one turned around. That was the strange mercy of it, and the terrible weight of it too. Old Sef walked with his lantern-hand steady on the Ember's cage, because his were the steadiest hands among them; Wren rode on his shoulders with her chin on his bald head, watching the blankness spread along the horizon like frost across a window. Ilsa kept her instruments out even though they had stopped agreeing with each other hours ago—her compass needle describing slow, thoughtful circles, her astrolabe ticking to itself like a dying clock.

And the Ember pulsed.

It had been pulsing since dawn. A slow red breath inside the glass, brightening and dimming, brightening and dimming, and every time it dimmed Bram's scar answered—a cold pull behind the healed cut on his palm, like a fishhook set in his flesh, reeling him gently northward.

"It knows where it's going," Wren said.

"It knows where it *went*," Sef corrected softly. "There's a difference, little star. Things that remember aren't always things that want to arrive."

* * *

They came to the Sundered Sea at dusk—or at what the valley still insisted was dusk, though the sky above them was a flat, sourceless gray, lit by nothing, shadowed by nothing, the light of a world running on habit alone.

Bram had imagined the sea all his life without knowing it. Sef had given it to him in pieces over the years of their friendship: the smell of it, the sound of it, the way a real horizon made a man feel both enormous and small. But nothing had prepared him for the wrongness of this one. The water stretched to the edge of sight and then simply *stopped*, not at a horizon but at a wall—a pale seam hanging in the air where the valley's illusion met its own border, and beyond the seam there was no sky, no sea, no anything. Just the gray. The valley did not end here. It was *cut off* here, sheared away like a page torn from a book, and the torn edge still showed the frayed threads of what had been removed: half a gull, dissolving; a wave that broke against nothing and fell back into itself, again and again, forever.

"The Sundered Sea," Ilsa whispered. She said it the way other people said the names of the dead.

Sef set Wren down and took off his hat. He stood facing the water for a long time, and Bram saw that the old man's lips were moving—not words, exactly. More like a man greeting someone after forty years and finding he has forgotten the language they shared.

"It used to go on," Sef said at last. "Past the seam. Past everything. You could sail until your heart gave out and never see the far shore." He put his hat back on, and his voice was steady when he continued, but only just. "This is where they paid the dragon. Right here. This is the wound."

The Ember flared.

Not its slow pulse now—a hard, urgent blaze, red light spilling through the cage-bars and throwing all their shadows long and trembling across the dead-flat water. And beneath the flare, for the first time, Bram heard it. Not with his ears. With the scar.

*A voice like a banked fire. Like coals settling in a grate. Like something vast turning over in its sleep.*

*Heir.*

Wren gasped. "It spoke."

"You heard it?" Bram said.

"She'd hear it," Tam said quietly. "Star-born. But so would you." He was looking at Bram's hand—at the scar, which had begun to glow faintly through the skin, a thin red line like a coal wrapped in flesh. "Blood calls to blood. That's how the wards know you. That's how the Gate will know you."

"What Gate?" Ilsa said. "I've mapped this coast a hundred times in theory. There's no gate here. There's barely a *here* here."

"That's because you were looking for a door," Tam said. He crouched, pressed his palm flat against the shingle, and left it there so long that Bram thought he had fallen asleep. When he looked up, his face was gray. "It's not a place. It never was. The founders sealed it inside themselves—inside the bloodline. A door made of memory. The only lock Corvane can't pick, because the key is a thing that happened, and only the blood that did it can carry it."

"Meaning what?" Bram asked, though some cold part of him already knew.

Tam stood. He wiped his hand on his coat, slowly, like a man putting off bad news. "Meaning the Gate opens when the heir remembers the Sundering. All of it. From the inside. Their crossing, their bargain, the dragon's fire, the tearing of the valley out of the world—it has to live in you again, Bram. Every moment of it. That's the toll."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a rock that was only half convinced of its own existence—one face solid granite, the other a soft gray blur that hurt to look at directly. Sef cooked the last of the dried fish over the Ember's cage, which threw heat without consuming anything, the way dragon-fire does. Nobody ate much.

Bram sat apart, at the waterline, holding his scarred palm open in his lap. The glow had faded to a dull ember-glimmer, but the pull remained, gentle and insistent, like a hand on his collar.

Ilsa came and sat beside him. She didn't speak for a while. She had learned that about him, over the miles—that silence offered freely was worth more than comfort pushed at him—and he was grateful for it now, because he didn't have words yet. He had only the shape of a fear.

"When I was disgraced," she said finally, "they struck my name from the rolls of the Royal Cartographic Guild. There's a formal ceremony for it. They take your instruments and break them in front of the assembly, and then the Master of Maps says, 'Let it be recorded that this person drew nothing true,' and everyone goes to dinner." She turned a pebble over in her fingers. "For two years afterward I couldn't draw at all. My hands shook. Because I had built my whole self on the idea that the world could be known—that if I was careful enough, honest enough, the map and the territory would agree, and I would matter." She flicked the pebble into the dark water. "Then I found out the world contains a country that refuses to be known, and do you know what I felt? Not despair. *Relief.* Because if the world was stranger than my maps, then my failure wasn't a failure. It was a frontier."

Bram almost smiled. "You're saying I should be relieved."

"I'm saying terror and wonder are the same feeling wearing different coats. You get to choose which one you wear tomorrow." She looked at him sideways. "But I won't lie to you, Bram Okafor. What Tam described—carrying a memory that isn't yours, living a tearing-apart from the inside—I've read accounts of magic like that. The people who survive it are changed. The people who don't survive it aren't anything anymore."

"Comforting."

"I'm a cartographer. I deal in accurate information." She rose, brushed the shingle from her coat, and paused. "For what it's worth—I've watched you since the night the stars went out. You keep choosing to walk toward the frightening thing. Whatever happens tomorrow, that's not nothing. That might be everything."

She went back to the fire. Bram stayed at the waterline a while longer, listening to waves that broke against nothing, and let himself be terrified, thoroughly and honestly, the way he had never been allowed to be in Mistress Halden's cottage, where fear was a thing you tidied away before supper.

Then he slept, and dreamed of fire.

* * *

Morning came gray and sourceless, and with it, the Ember burned low.

They saw it happen. The pulse that had guided them for days slowed, deepened, dimmed—each flare weaker than the last, like a heartbeat counting down. By the time they reached the shore where the seam hung palest, the light inside the cage was barely a red thread, and the voice in Bram's scar had gone faint as a word called across a great distance.

*Hasten. Hasten, heir. I cannot hold the door long.*

"It's dying," Wren said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "It's really dying."

"No," Tam said. He knelt by the lantern, and his weathered hands hovered over the glass without touching it. "It's *spending*. Look—" He pointed, and Bram saw it: where the Ember's fading light touched the seam in the air, the pale wall thinned. Grew translucent. Behind it, for the first time, there was a darkness that wasn't blankness but *depth*—a hint of real night, real distance, the smell of salt wind from a sea that actually went somewhere. "It's paying itself out to hold the crack open. Dragon-fire doesn't die quietly. It dies buying passage."

"Then we go now," Ilsa said. "All of us. While it holds."

"Not all of us." Bram heard his own voice as if from far away. Steady. Which surprised him, because inside he was nothing but storm. "Just me. That's the toll, isn't it? The memory lives in the bloodline. The door opens for the heir." He flexed his scarred hand. "If I carry all of you through the remembering and it breaks me halfway, we lose everything—the Ember, the door, the chance. If I go alone and succeed, the Gate stands open and you walk through."

"Absolutely not," Ilsa said.

"We don't leave people behind," Sef said. "That's not how crews work. That's not how *families* work."

"We nearly lost Sef at the crossing," Bram said, and the words came out rougher than he meant them. "We will not stand in a row waiting to see which of you unravels next. Wren stays. Sef stays. Tam—you're the only one who understands the wards; if I fail, someone has to know enough to try again someday." He looked at Ilsa last, and found it hardest to look at her last. "Draw the way out. That's your magic. Make it true."

Ilsa's jaw worked. For a moment he thought she would argue, and he braced for it—but then she unslung her satchel, took out charcoal and the good paper, the paper she saved, and began to sketch the seam, fast and fierce, lines going down sure as hammer-strokes. "When you come through," she said, not looking up, "you'll walk along my line. Don't deviate. Deviation annoys me."

Bram laughed—a short, startled sound, absurd in that gray place—and Wren threw her arms around his waist and squeezed until his ribs creaked. Sef gripped his shoulder once, hard, and said nothing, which from Sef was a whole speech.

Tam walked him to the edge of the water.

"The founders' memory will come at you like weather," the old tinkerer said. "Don't fight it and don't drown in it. You're not becoming them. You're *witnessing* them. Hold on to that. Hold on to your own name like a rope." He hesitated, and then, gruffly: "Your master taught you ward-craft. Use it. A ward is just a shape you hold around something precious. Hold one around yourself."

Bram nodded. His throat was too tight for words.

He waded into the shallows, up to his knees, up to his thighs, the dead-flat water cold as a held breath. The seam loomed before him, and the Ember's last red thread of light lay across it like a threshold painted in blood. Behind his scar, the voice was very faint now, and very patient.

*Come, heir. Come and remember.*

Bram Okafor pressed his scarred palm against the seam, closed his eyes, and let the Sundering in.

* * *

He was Aldric Okafor, and the world was ending.

Not ending—*leaving*. That was the choice, and it had been made in blood and desperation on this same shore four hundred years ago, and now Bram stood inside the man who had made it, wearing his terror like a coat two sizes too small.

Around him: war. Real war, the kind the valley folk had never seen, the kind the wards had been built to make them forget. Fires on the headlands. Soldiers in the passes. A sky black with smoke and worse than black with what flew through it—the enemy's bound horrors, winged and wrong. And on the shingle where Bram had eaten Sef's fish the night before, the founders of the valley knelt in a circle around a dragon.

It was old. Older than old. Its scales had gone gray as ash, its wings folded like a moth-eaten tent, and it looked at the kneeling humans with eyes like the last coals of a fire that had once warmed a continent. It was dying. It had been dying for a century. And it had agreed to their plea anyway, because they had offered it the one payment it wanted more than gold, more than land, more than vengeance on the war that had emptied the skies of its kin.

*They asked me to erase them,* the dragon had said, in a voice like continents shifting. *No power under heaven can truly erase. But a bargain freely paid can persuade the world to forget. You will be unwritten. Your children will not know you. Your children's children will not know themselves. Is this understood?*

*It is understood,* Aldric had said, for all of them, and his hand had been shaking, and his daughter's small hand had been shaking inside it, and they had understood anyway. Because staying meant death. Because leaving meant scattering, hunted, hunted, hunted, to the ends of a world that hated them. Because a lie that lived was worth more than a truth that died.

*Then pay me,* the dragon said. *Pay me your names. Pay me your history. Pay me the stars above your valley, so that no astronomer's chart will ever find you. And pay me, last of all, the thing I cannot take—give it freely, or the bargain fails.*

And Aldric had opened a vein, and his daughter had opened hers, and the blood of the Okafor line had run down the shingle and into the dragon's mouth, and the dragon had taken its own heart-fire—the last ember of the last of its kind—and pressed it into the wound, and spoken the words that unmade a country:

*Forget them, world. Forget them, sky. Let there be a valley with no outside, a sea with no far shore, a people with no past. Let them be safe. Let them be small. Let them be mine to guard until the debt comes due.*

And the world had obeyed.

Bram felt it happen. Felt the tearing—not of flesh but of *being*, the sensation of being lifted out of a book and the pages closing behind him. Felt the stars go out overhead, one by one, snuffed like candles between wet fingers. Felt the horizon draw in close and seal itself shut. Felt his daughter's hand go slack in his as her memories drained away, her childhood, her grandmother's lullabies, the name of the village she was born in—all of it flowing out of her and into the fire, feeding the great forgetting, and her looking up at him with empty, peaceful eyes and asking, *Papa, why are we kneeling?*

That was the worst of it. That was the toll's true weight: not the pain, but the love. To watch the ones you loved forget you, and to know you had chosen it, and to know it was mercy, and to know it was a cage, all at once, forever.

*I did this to save you,* Aldric had thought, holding his daughter who no longer knew him. *God forgive me. I did this to save you.*

And the dragon, folding its dying body around the valley like a shell around a seed, had thought—had *hoped*, the hope burning in it brighter than its failing fire—*May the day come when one of their blood chooses differently. May the debt come due. May someone pay it back.*

Bram screamed.

He screamed inside Aldric's body and inside his own, four hundred years apart at once, and the scream was the hinge. The memory broke over him and through him and *into* him, and instead of drowning he did what Tam had told him—he held his own name like a rope. *Bram. Bram Okafor. Hedge-witch's boy. Apprentice of Mistress Halden. Friend of Sef. Brother to Wren. Heir, yes—but heir by choice, not by blood alone.*

The seam split open like a wound healing in reverse.

Light poured out—not the valley's sourceless gray but true light, starlight, the remembered light of a real sky—and the Gate stood revealed at last: not stone, not wood, not any door a cartographer could draw, but an opening in the fabric of the valley itself, tall as grief and wide as forgiveness, breathing cold salt wind from the actual world.

Behind him, the Ember flared once—brilliant, joyful, a dragon's laugh in fire—and went dark.

*Paid in full,* said the voice in his scar, growing distant, growing warm, growing *free*. *Well done, heir. Now bring them home.*

Bram Okafor stood in the doorway of a country that no longer existed, shaking, weeping, alive, and turned back to call his family through.

What he saw froze the call in his throat.

Down the beach, riding out of the unraveling gray with banners of blankness streaming behind them, came the wardens—and in their midst, bound and gray-faced, walked Magister Corvane, smiling like a man arriving precisely on time.
