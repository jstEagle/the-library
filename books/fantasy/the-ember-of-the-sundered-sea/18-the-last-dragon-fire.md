## 18. The Last Dragon-Fire

The Sundered Sea did not look like a sea. It looked like the place where the world had been asked a question and had never answered.

Bram stood at its edge in the last grey hour before dawn, and there was no water, no shore, no horizon—only a vast pale nothing that stretched from his boots to the place where sky should have been. The blankness hummed. Not with sound, exactly, but with the absence of it, the way a struck bell keeps ringing somewhere beneath hearing long after the air goes still. His scar burned through the bandage Ilsa had wrapped around his palm, and he was grateful for the pain. It was the only honest thing left.

Behind him, the valley was dying beautifully. He could hear it—the hedges unraveling into threads of light, the orchards going soft at their edges like watercolors left in rain, the roads thinning to pencil-lines someone was slowly erasing. Tam had said it would be like this: *unmapped places don't fall down, boy. They forget themselves.* And the valley, starless and wardless now, was forgetting everything at once.

"Tell me again," Wren said beside him. She was holding the lantern, both hands wrapped around its handle as though the little fire inside might be blown away by a wind that didn't exist. "Tell me what you're going to do."

"I'm going to give it my blood," Bram said. "And then I'm going to tell it the truth."

"That's not doing anything."

He almost laughed. Six years old, born on the night the stars went out, and she had already found the flaw in every plan the adults had made. "No," he admitted. "I suppose it isn't."

* * *

They had come down through the Hollow March in the dark, or what passed for dark in a country whose night sky was a painted ceiling with the paint peeling off. The company walked in a line along Sef's remembered coast—Sef's ghost-coast, Bram corrected himself, because Sef was gone, folded into the blankness three days ago with his hands still raised against the unraveling, holding a crossing open so the rest of them could pass. They did not speak of him. They spoke of nothing else.

Ilsa walked at the rear with her instruments silent in their case. She hadn't tried to use them since the March began; there was nothing left for them to measure. Once she had been a cartographer of nothing. Now she was a cartographer of less than nothing, and Bram had watched her carry that knowledge the way soldiers carry wounds they refuse to show.

Tam limped beside Wren, his tinkerer's bag lighter than it had been—he'd fed the wards piece by piece to keep the path solid under their feet, sacrificing hinges and sigil-plates and finally the copper compass rose from his own walking staff. "Sentimental junk," he'd muttered as he dropped it into the unraveling hedge. But he had looked back at it.

And behind all of them, patient as rot, Corvane had followed.

They heard him before they saw him, in the hour when the false stars guttered lowest: hoofbeats where there were no horses, the creak of leather where there was no leather, because Corvane's riders were warden-stuff, woven from the same memory-of-magic as everything else in the valley, and they moved the way nightmares move—already close when your mind insists they are far away.

"Bram." Ilsa's hand closed on his shoulder. "It's time. Whatever you're going to do, do it now."

* * *

The lantern opened the way lanterns do not open.

Wren lifted the little door of colored glass, and the Ember rose—not a flame, not anymore, but a shard of something older than flame, a sliver of heart-fire that had once beaten inside the chest of a dragon old enough to remember the world before the Sundering. It hung above the lantern's mouth and turned slowly, and its light was not gold or red but the color of the first thing Bram had ever loved: hearth-light seen through a window from the cold outside.

*You came,* it said. Not in words. In warmth, in pressure, in the itch of his scar rising to a singe. *Little heir. Little Sunderer. You came to the edge of the edge.*

"I came to end it," Bram said.

*Yes.* The Ember turned, and its light touched the blankness of the sea, and for one heartbeat the blankness flinched. *Your ancestors stood here too. They stood here with a dragon's promise in their hands and a war at their backs, and they asked to be erased, and I was the price of the erasing. My fire for their forgetting. My heart for their hiding. And then they went home to their comfortable lie and let me cool.*

Bram knelt at the shore of nothing. He drew the knife from his belt—the plain iron knife Mistress Halden had put in his hand at nine years old, saying *a hedge-witch's boy should know how to cut things*, and only now understanding that she had meant wards, and borders, and himself.

"What do you need?" he asked.

*Blood,* said the Ember. *Yours, because the wards were keyed to your line. And truth, because a spell is only a story told so hard the world believes it, and this story has been believed too long. Tell me what happened. All of it. Even the parts that shame you. Especially those.*

Hoofbeats. Closer now. The grey nothing ahead of them shivered, and out of it came shapes—wardens on warden-horses, their armor the dull silver of old frost, their faces kind and terrible, the faces of guardians who had spent generations believing they protected a country when in truth they guarded a cage. And at their head, riding a horse made of the same stuff, sat Magister Corvane.

He looked tired. That was the worst of it. He looked like a schoolmaster at the end of a long term, a man who had stayed up late over his papers out of love for his students.

"Bram Okafor," Corvane called, and his voice carried across the blankness without echo, because echoes required a world. "Stop. Please. Look at what you're standing at the edge of. Nothing. Void. The devouring dark your founders fled. I am offering you a miracle—stars restored, wards renewed, the valley whole and warm and eternal. All it costs is a few drops of blood, freely given, to bind what your ancestors built."

"And the people?" Bram didn't stand. He kept his eyes on the Ember. "Do they get a say in their eternity?"

"The people," Corvane said gently, "are happier than they have ever been. They simply don't know it yet. Happiness doesn't require knowledge, boy. It requires walls."

"There it is," Ilsa murmured behind him. "There's the map he's been drawing his whole life."

*Bram,* the Ember said. *Choose. The fire is low. I cannot hold this shape much longer whether you choose or not.*

Corvane raised his hand, and the wardens lowered their lances, and Bram understood with sudden clarity that he had perhaps ten heartbeats left in which to be afraid—and that he intended to spend every one of them.

He opened his bandaged palm. The scar ran diagonal across it, the mark he'd been born with, the key-cut his bloodline had carried for six generations waiting for a lock. He set the blade against it.

"Mistress Halden," he said quietly, "raised me on lies because she couldn't bear to raise me on grief. My ancestors sealed a gate and called it salvation. The valley sang to itself for two hundred years about how the outside world was a devouring dark, and the dark was just—Tuesday. Weather. Other people. Ordinary life, which we were taught to fear because fear is easier to ward than sorrow." The blade bit. Blood welled, black in the Ember's light. "My name is Bram Okafor, and I am the heir of the Sunderers, and I am ashamed of them, and I love them, and both of those things are true, and I will not choose between them anymore."

He pressed his bleeding palm to the Ember.

* * *

The world caught fire.

Not the valley—the *lie*. Fire went through everything the way dawn goes through fog, and Bram felt it leave him, felt his blood and his truth drawn out together in a single long exhalation, and the Ember drank and drank and *grew*. It swelled from a shard to a coal to a bloom of white-gold radiance, and inside it, for the first time, Bram saw the shape of the dragon—not flesh, but the idea of dragon, vast and coiled and patient, wings like weather fronts, eyes like the last two honest stars.

*Enough,* it said, and its voice was no longer a whisper in his scar but a sound that rolled across the blankness and made the blankness *remember being sea*. *Enough, little heir. Watch what your truth buys.*

The wardens charged. Corvane screamed something—his voice cracking, pleading, *no, no, you'll unmake us all*—and the dragon's fire met them not as a weapon but as an answer. Where the light touched, the warden-stuff did not burn; it *woke*. Riders became old men and women, farmers and fletchers, the valley folk who had given their memories to the wards centuries ago, stumbling out of their armor blinking and weeping and asking each other where they were. Corvane's horse dissolved under him and he fell to his knees on ground that was suddenly, unmistakably sand.

And overhead—

Overhead, the ceiling tore.

The painted stars peeled away like old gilt, and behind them was not void but *depth*, black and endless and salted with light, thousands upon thousands of points kindling one after another as the dragon's fire climbed and scattered and settled, each ember finding its ancient seat in heaven. The Milky Way poured itself back across the sky like a river remembering its bed. The Sundered Sea—truly a sea now, waves rolling in from a horizon that existed—caught every one of those lights and broke them into trembling pieces and gave them back.

Bram knelt in the surf and wept without deciding to. The first real tide in two hundred years slid cold over his knees.

"It worked," Wren whispered. She was staring straight up, her small face silvered, her mouth open. "Bram. Bram, the sky goes on forever. Nobody told me it *goes on*."

"I know," he said.

"Why would anyone want a ceiling?"

He had no answer. Behind them, the valley was letting go—gently, the way a hand lets go of a hand. Hedges sighed into ordinary bramble. Cottages settled into cottages, real ones, mortal ones, with crooked roofs and honest drafts. The wards' great humming ceased, and in the silence afterward Bram could hear gulls, actual gulls, crying over actual water, and the sound was more beautiful than any spell he had ever been taught.

Only Corvane remained unchanged, kneeling in the sand amid his scattered, waking riders, staring up at the stars as if they were accusations.

"No," he was saying. "No, this is wrong, this is ruin—I was saving them—" And the truth he refused, the plain enormous truth of an open sky, closed over him like water, and where he had knelt there was only an outline in the wet sand, filling with foam, emptying, gone.

The Ember drifted down and settled into Bram's cupped hands, small again, warm again, a coal instead of a cataclysm. Its voice, when it came, was very quiet.

*You ended a world today, heir.*

"No," Bram said. He looked up at the impossible, generous, terrifying multitude of stars, and at the people of the valley waking along the shore behind him—hundreds of them, thousands, standing in the surf and the dunes with their faces turned upward, some laughing, some keening, Old Sef's widow clutching his remembered coat, Tam already turning to Ilsa and saying something about roads, about maps, about the way through. "I ended a *lie*. The world was always out here. We just finally showed up."

The Ember warmed his palms like a cat settling in. Far inland, faint on the wind, he thought he heard a bell begin to ring—the chapel in the village that had been a painting an hour ago, rung by hands that were learning, all over again, to be real.

Wren took his sleeve. "What happens now?"

Bram looked at the dark line of the coast running north and south, at the mountains no one had mapped, at the road that did not yet exist and would have to be built. His scar had gone quiet at last. His palm was still bleeding, but the blood was only blood.

"Now," he said, "we find out."
