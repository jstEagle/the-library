## 20. Mapping the Way Through

Ilsa Vane had spent her whole life believing that a map was a record of something that already existed. You walked the land, or you trusted those who had, and you set down what was there: the bend of the river, the shoulder of the mountain, the little huddle of roofs that called itself a town. A map was testimony. A map said *I was here, and this is what I saw.*

The valley had broken that faith in her, piece by piece, the way frost breaks stone. Its borders would not hold an ink line. Its roads wandered on her parchment like drunkards. And now, with the valley dissolving around them like sugar in warm water, she sat on a flat rock above the grey remains of the Hollow March and understood that she was about to do the one thing no cartographer in the history of the world had ever done.

She was going to draw a map of a place that did not exist yet.

* * *

The morning after the burning—she thought of it as *the burning*, though there had been no fire anyone could warm their hands at; the valley had simply gone up like mist off a pond at sunrise—Ilsa woke with her fingers cramped around a charcoal stub she didn't remember taking out.

They had made camp in the lee of a hill that was only half a hill now. The other half faded into a pale nothing that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts, the way a blank page hurts when you have promised someone a story. Beyond it, the Sundered Sea stretched to a horizon that finally, blessedly, ended at real sky. Real stars had burned overhead all night, unfamiliar constellations wheeling where the valley's borrowed ones used to hang, and Wren had stayed awake until midnight naming them wrong on purpose because it made Bram laugh.

Ilsa had not laughed. Ilsa had lain awake listening to the sound of a country ending.

It was not loud. That was the terrible part. She had expected some vast groan, some tearing of the fabric, the death-rattle of a spell as old as her grandmother's grandmother. Instead the valley went quietly, the way an old woman goes in her sleep—a soft lessening, a thinning, until the orchards they had marched through were orchard-shaped absences, until the road under their feet became a road-shaped suggestion, until Old Sef's fishing village, which they had passed through two days before the Gate, was simply gone, along with every soul who had chosen to sleep through the last night of the lie.

Sef had wept for them without names. He had stood facing the blankness with his cap in his hands and said, "They dreamed of water. At least they'll wake by it." And then he had rolled his sleeves back down over his rope-scarred forearms and walked on, because mourning that cannot pause learns to walk.

Now Ilsa unrolled her last clean sheet of vellum across her knees, weighted its corners with four stones, and looked at the white expanse the way a swimmer looks at deep water.

"You're really going to try," Tam said. He had come up the slope carrying his kit—the tinkerer's satchel full of ward-needles and copper thread and little glass vials of things that hummed—and he lowered himself onto the rock beside her with the careful movements of a man whose ribs were still bruised from the Hollow March. "A map of nowhere."

"A map of *not yet*," Ilsa said.

Tam considered that. He was a lean man, quick-eyed, with burn scars on both hands from a lifetime of coaxing magic through wire and glass, and he had the particular stillness of someone who had learned long ago that questions get you exiled but asking them anyway gets you free. "The passage out," he said. "You can see it?"

"See it? No." She picked up the charcoal. "But I can feel it. Can't you?"

He closed his eyes. After a moment his brow furrowed. "There's a pull. Faint. Like a tide going out under the sand."

"That's it. The Ember opened the door, and doors want to be used. There's a way through the blankness—I can feel it the way you feel stairs in the dark. My whole life I've drawn what instruments told me was true. Well." She turned her left hand over, palm up, and looked at the ink stains layered so deep into the whorls of her fingerprints that she would carry them to her grave. "My instruments are ash. So now it's just me."

"And me," Tam said.

She glanced at him. "You're a tinkerer, not a surveyor."

"I'm a man whose entire profession was making things work in places where they shouldn't." He was already unpacking his satchel, laying out copper wire and needle-thin styluses and a spool of thread that glimmered faintly with borrowed starlight—he'd been winding it around the lantern since the relighting, harvesting the dragon-fire's warmth the way other men harvested honey. "Your lines tell people where to go. My road makes it true. I think—" and here he paused, and Ilsa watched him wrestle with the sentence the way a man wrestles a door swollen shut in winter, "—I think that might be its own kind of magic. The kind that doesn't need stars to work."

Ilsa felt something shift in her chest, an old rusted thing turning over. Purpose. She'd thought hers had died with her commission, burned the day Corvane's men took her name off the royal rolls. But here it was, alive and demanding, sitting on a rock beside a tinkerer at the edge of unbeing.

"All right," she said, and set the charcoal to the vellum.

* * *

The first line was the hardest.

Not technically—technically it was easy, a single stroke from the sea's edge inland, marking where the company stood. What was hard was everything the line meant. Every map she had ever drawn had been an act of agreement between herself and the world: *you are there, I say so, we confirm each other.* This line had no world to agree with. Behind it lay the sea, real and salt-smelling and slapping against stones that had never been stones until three days ago. Ahead of it lay the blankness where the valley had been, and somewhere inside that blankness, the folk of the valley—hundreds of them, thousands, waking on hillsides and in fields that remembered being farms, blinking at a sky with too many stars, waiting for someone to tell them which way was out.

Bram had gone back to them. Of course he had. He was their heir, their key, the boy whose blood had ended the world they were born in, and he carried the guilt of it like a second spine. But he had made Ilsa promise before he left. He'd gripped her wrists hard enough to bruise, and his eyes had that terrible steadiness he got when he was most afraid, and he'd said, "Draw us a way out, Ilsa. Whatever else happens. Draw us out."

So she drew.

The line ran inland from the shore, curving slightly east, and as it curved she felt the pull strengthen under her hand—not imagination, not quite; something more like memory moving through her, the founders' memory that lived in the land's bones even now that the land itself was forgetting its own name. The passage wasn't a road. It was a scar. It was the seam where the valley had been stitched out of the world, and seams, once cut, remember the shape of the knife.

"There," she breathed. "Tam. There's a low place, maybe half a mile in. Where the blankness thins. Like a ford."

Tam leaned over her shoulder. His breath smelled of copper and woodsmoke. "Show me exactly."

She marked it: a small circle, two ticks. Then another thin place further on, where the pull bent south around something dense and dark that she chose not to name. Then a third, and a fourth, and slowly, stroke by stroke, a path assembled itself on the vellum—not drawn from sight but *felt into being*, each line a confession: *here the nothing is thinner, here you could cross if you were brave, here you must not stop.*

When she lifted the charcoal at last, her hand was shaking and the page held a map unlike any in existence: a coastline at the bottom, honest and exact; a path threading upward through whited-out country; and everywhere else, deliberately, lovingly, nothing. She wrote in the blank spaces, in her small precise hand: *Here there was a valley. It kept its people safe for nine generations. It was made of love and lies, and both have been spent.*

Tam read it twice. "That's not standard cartographic notation," he said.

"No," Ilsa agreed. "It's better."

* * *

Building the road took three days, and it was the strangest work either of them had ever done.

Tam's method, as far as Ilsa could understand it, was to walk her mapped line with a bundle of copper stakes and a mallet, driving a stake into the ground every twenty paces and running starlight-thread between them. Where the thread touched the blankness, the blankness resisted—it shivered, it thickened, it pushed back like water refusing a plow—but the thread held, humming, and behind the thread the ground remembered how to be ground. Grass came first, pale and tentative, then grit, then honest dirt. By the second day there was a track. By the third, a road: narrow, winding, barely wide enough for two carts, but *real*, real in the way that mattered, solid underfoot and visible from end to end.

"It shouldn't work," Tam said on the evening of the third day, standing at the road's farthest reach with his mallet hanging loose in his hand. He sounded almost offended. "Ward-craft needs a source. A wellspring. The valley's wards ran on the Ember and the founders' blood, and both are spent. This should be string and sticks."

"So why does it hold?"

He crouched and pressed his scarred palm flat to the packed earth. Ilsa watched his face go through wonder and arrive somewhere quieter. "Because people believe in roads," he said. "Stars are far away. Dragons are stories. But a road—everyone knows what a road is for. Everyone trusts one. I think..." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "I think belief is doing the work the Ember used to. Our belief. Yours and mine. We wanted there to be a way out badly enough that the world is agreeing with us."

Ilsa looked down the length of the road, at the thread glinting between stakes all the way back to the sea-light, and felt the rusted thing in her chest turn again, smoother now. All her life she had believed maps described the world. Here was proof they could also make it. Her disgrace, her exile, her ruined name—all of it had been training for this: a woman who drew lines and refused to be told they weren't true.

"Then we'd better believe very hard tomorrow," she said. "Because the first wagons come at dawn."

* * *

They came at dawn, as promised, and they came slowly, and they came frightened.

Ilsa stood at the road's head with her map held open like a banner and watched them come: farmers leading oxen that kept trying to turn around, grandmothers carrying chickens, children carrying smaller children, the whole ragged congregation of a vanished nation picking its way toward the sea. Bram walked at their head with the lantern swinging from his fist, the Ember inside it banked to a coal-red whisper, and Wren rode on his shoulders pointing at gulls and demanding to know their names.

Behind the crowd, the blankness followed. Not chasing—there was nothing so dramatic as pursuit—but closing, gently, the way a wound closes, the valley finishing its long exhale. Fields they had slept in were fog by midmorning. Hills they had skirted were suggestions by noon. Ilsa found she could not watch it directly for long; there was a grief in it too large for her, grief for people she'd never met, for orchards she'd never tasted, for a whole country agreeing to die so its children could live.

Her job was the road. She fixed her eyes on the road.

By afternoon the column reached the third waypoint, the place where her line bent south around the dense dark thing she had declined to name, and here the blankness pressed closest, close enough that the outermost walkers could reach out and trail their fingers through nothing. A woman began to cry. A man behind her started reciting what Ilsa realized was a ward-litany, the old protective verses every valley child learned, and the words came out cracked and useless, addressed to wards that no longer existed.

Ilsa climbed onto a boulder and held up the map.

"This is the way!" she shouted, and her voice, trained in lecture halls and court chambers, carried. "Follow the stakes! Follow the thread! The road holds because you walk it—every step makes it truer! Keep moving and keep faith, and by nightfall you'll smell salt!"

For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then the crying woman wiped her face, gripped her husband's sleeve, and stepped onto the road. And the road, Ilsa would swear on any map in any archive, *firmed* beneath her feet.

They came in a river after that. Tam worked the line ahead of them, driving stakes with bleeding palms, and Ilsa walked backward along the column calling corrections, adjusting her route on the fly when the pull shifted, redrawing the passage in her mind faster than any instrument could have confirmed it. Once, near dusk, the thread ahead went slack and dim, and Tam stood over a failed stake with despair written plain on his face—and then a dozen valley hands reached past him, farmers' hands, fisherwomen's hands, children's hands, holding the copper upright while he re-tied the knot, and the light came back.

Nobody had to explain it. They were building the road together, the whole exiled nation, one belief at a time.

* * *

On the fourth evening, Ilsa sat at the top of the last rise with the finished map spread on her knees, and let herself look back.

There was almost nothing to see. A shimmer where the valley had been, like heat-haze in winter. The ghost of a mountain range. And threaded through it, impossibly, stubbornly, the road—her road, their road—running back into the fading country like a silver root, drawing the last of the people out of it the way a root draws water.

Bram came and sat beside her. The lantern sat between them, and inside it the Ember pulsed slow and low, a sleeping heart.

"It's beautiful," he said. "What you made."

"It's a dirt track with string on it."

"It's the first honest map in nine generations." He turned to look at her, and in the Ember's light his face was older than his years and younger than his sorrows. "Halden always said the valley's greatest magic was the wards. She was wrong. It was this. Someone deciding where the truth runs, and building it so others could follow."

Ilsa looked down at the map—at the coastline, the path, the words she'd written in the empty places—and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding for four days. Tomorrow the last of the folk would cross onto real earth. Tomorrow the shimmer would finish fading, and the valley would be a story, and the story would live or die depending on whether anyone wrote it down.

Someone should write it down. Someone with ink-stained fingers and a disgraced name and a brand-new understanding of what lines could do.

"Bram," she said. "When your people are settled—when there's a town, a harbor, a border that exists—" she tapped the blank margin of the map, the wide white space beyond the coast, "—I'm going to need to fill this in. All of it. The whole real world. And I'll need a road-builder who believes hard enough to make it true."

Tam, coming up the slope behind them with his mallet over his shoulder like a pilgrim's staff, snorted. "You're offering me a job before I've even finished the first one."

"I'm offering you a life's work," Ilsa said. "The world is unmapped, Tam. Every inch of it. Think of it."

And Tam—who had been exiled for asking questions, who had crossed a dying country on nothing but stubbornness and copper wire—began, slowly, to grin.

Below them, the last fires of the camp flickered along the shore, and beyond them the sea went on forever, and the first stars were coming out over water that had waited nine generations to be sailed.
