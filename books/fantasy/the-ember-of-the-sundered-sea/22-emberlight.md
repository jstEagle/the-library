## 22. Emberlight

The lantern hung from the crook of the driftwood post above the hearth, and every evening at dusk Bram climbed the three worn steps to tend it.

It was not a chore. He had tried to think of it that way at first—*feeding the fire, banking the coals*, the ordinary liturgy of any settlement on any cold coast—but the word *chore* would not stick to it. You did not do chores for a heart. And that was what the Ember was, now that he had learned to listen to it: a heart. The last dragon's heart-fire, dimmed to the size of a walnut, glowing in its iron cage like something sleeping rather than something dying. It had been dying when Ilsa carried it out of Corvane's vault. It was not dying anymore. Forgotten things, Wren had told him with the terrible certainty of a seven-year-old, only fade if nobody remembers them out loud.

So every evening Bram opened the little door in the lantern's side, cupped his scarred palm around the warmth, and said, aloud, so the whole room could hear it: "Still here."

And the Ember would brighten, just slightly, the way a cat's eyes catch lamplight, or the way an old man's face changes when someone says his name in a crowd.

Tonight the fire took his words and threw them back gold across the common hall of the sea-settlement—the settlement that did not have a name yet, because naming things, Tam said, was a kind of warding, and they had all agreed to be very careful about wards for a while. Forty-one people lived here now, in houses built of true timber cut from true trees that grew where rain actually fell. The sea came up the shingle twice a day with its enormous indifferent breathing, and every time Bram stood at the tideline and felt it tug at his ankles, he thought: *this is real water. This water has been touching the whole world while we were gone.*

The hall smelled of Sef's smoked fish—or rather, of the recipe Old Sef had left behind, because Sef himself was ash and memory now, given to the crossing they had held on the first night under the restored stars. They cooked his fish his way anyway. Tam had carved his name into the smokehouse beam. Wren had insisted on adding a drawing of a fish wearing a hat, which no one had the heart to sand away.

Bram banked the hearth beneath the lantern, though the Ember needed no tending—it gave heat without consuming, light without fuel, a small perpetual miracle that everyone in the settlement had already stopped remarking on, because that was what people did with miracles; they made furniture of them. He understood. He had grown up inside one.

He was still standing there, hand warm, when the door banged open and the cold came in with the child.

"She's doing it," Wren announced to the entire hall, breathless, cheeks whipped red by wind. "Ilsa says come *now*, she says you have to see it before the cloud goes over, she says—" She grabbed his sleeve with both hands and hauled, which for a child born starless and small was ambitious, and Bram let himself be hauled, grinning, leaving the Ember glowing behind him like a promise kept.

Outside, the night was enormous.

That was the thing he did not think he would ever stop being undone by: the size of it. In the valley the sky had been a ceiling—a painted one, after the stars went out, a soft grey lie that sat close enough to touch. Here the sky went up forever, black velvet flung over the edge of everything, and the stars were scattered across it like salt from a careless, generous hand.

Ilsa stood on the dune above the tide line, wrapped in her cartographer's coat, her old brass instruments hanging off her belt like the tools of a trade she was learning to practice honestly. She had a slate in one hand and chalk in the other, and she was looking up with the expression Bram had learned to read as her version of weeping: jaw set, eyes fierce, absolutely still.

"Come here," she said, without turning. "Quickly. Before your cloud comes back."

"What cloud?"

"The one that follows you around, Okafor. The one made of bad luck and self-importance." But her voice was shaking, just slightly, and when he reached her she pointed up with the chalk as if she were marking a map only she could see.

"Look. Between those two bright ones—the ones the sailors call the Brothers. Do you see the fainter line running through? Like a river of dust?"

He looked. At first there was nothing but the two great stars burning white over the sea. Then, as his eyes adjusted—as he stopped trying to see and let himself simply look—he found it: a wash of faint silver, a spilling of light too small to be stars individually and too vast to be anything else, arcing from horizon to horizon like a seam in the dark.

"That's the River," Wren whispered. She had gone quiet, which for Wren was like a bell going quiet. "Is it really a river?"

"It's thousands upon thousands of stars," Ilsa said. "So many they blur together. The old maps called it the Silver Road. Sailors steered by it." Her voice did the thing it did when she talked about maps—went reverent and annoyed at once, love and grievance in the same breath. "It appears in exactly none of my charts. I drew forty years of charts, and this—" she gestured upward with the chalk, helpless, furious, delighted— "this was above me the entire time, and I never once put it on paper, because I never once looked up long enough."

Wren tugged Bram's sleeve again. "Which one is mine?"

"Yours?"

"My constellation. Everyone has one. Sef said fishermen have theirs, and Tam said tinkers have theirs, and you—" she squinted at him with devastating seriousness— "you probably have a boring one."

"I don't have a constellation," Bram said.

"You're the heir of the Sunderers. You have *several*. Probably an entire unpleasant family of them." Ilsa crouched to Wren's height, and her voice gentled into the register she saved exclusively for the child and for maps. "But listen. A constellation isn't given, little one. It's made. Someone looks up, and sees a shape, and tells the story, and if enough people tell it, the shape stays. That's all a constellation is. A story the sky agrees to remember."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she raised one small arm and swept it across the sky, from the Brothers down toward the black shoulder of the headland, tracing lines between stars that had never been joined before.

"There," she said. "That's a fox. See? Running along the beach. That one's his nose, and those are his ears, and that bright one is where he stole the moon's egg and it's stuck in his tail."

Bram looked. And because he looked—because he wanted it to be true—the fox assembled itself out of scattered light, ears and nose and the bright stolen egg, running forever along a shore made of darkness.

"It's perfect," he said, and meant it.

"The Fox Who Stole the Moon's Egg," Ilsa repeated. She was already chalking it onto her slate, quick sure strokes, the shape of the stars and the lines between them and, beneath, in her small precise hand: *First charted by Wren, who was born the night the stars went out.* "There. Now it's real. Now it will stay."

They stood there a long while, the three of them, the cartographer and the heir and the star-born child, while the cold came off the sea and the Fox ran his endless run overhead. Somewhere behind them the hall glowed ember-gold, and voices murmured, and someone was badly playing a fiddle, and the sound of it carried out over the water the way sound carries when there is finally a world for it to carry *into*.

Bram flexed his scarred palm against his thigh, an old habit, checking.

Nothing. No itch. No pull toward a border, no whisper of wards in his blood, no sense of the valley's great held breath tightening around him like a fist. The scar remained—a pale seam across his palm where the founders' memory had passed through him, where he had fed his blood and his truth to a dying dragon—but it was only a scar now. A record. Not a leash.

He had not told anyone how strange that still felt. How, some nights, he woke reaching for the weight of the valley's attention on his skin and found only the honest dark, and lay there feeling both freed and unmoored, like a man whose lifelong headache had stopped and who could not quite trust the silence in his own skull.

The valley was gone. He had burned it away himself, and he would burn it away again, a thousand times, to keep this sky. But grief, he was learning, was not a betrayal of the choice. You could choose the truth with your whole heart and still mourn the beautiful lie. Both things fit inside a person. That was what it meant to be a country's last heir and its first emigrant: you carried the whole map, even the parts that no longer existed.

* * *

Later, when Wren had been carried home asleep over Tam's shoulder—she had fought it ferociously and lost mid-sentence—and the hall had emptied to embers and fiddle-case, Bram walked the settlement's edge.

It was a habit he was allowing himself, these perimeter walks. Not warding. Just walking. Learning the shape of a place that had no hidden edges, no humming veil, no moment where the world went soft and grey and refused to be recorded. Here the boundary of the settlement was a fence Tam had built, honest pine posts and honest wire, and beyond it the dunes ran down to the sea and the sea ran on to everywhere.

Tam was at the fence, of course. Tam was always at the fence, or the well, or the half-finished mill—anywhere a problem lived that could be argued with. He had a lantern hooked on a post and was peering at the wire with his spectacles pushed up into his wild hair.

"If you say 'warding' one more time," Bram said, coming up beside him, "I'm telling Wren you're afraid of the dark."

"I am *not* warding. Warding is a lie you build and then maintain until it kills you. This is—" Tam waved a wrench at the fence with the air of a man unveiling a cathedral. "This is a *fence*. It keeps the goats in. It does nothing else. It makes no promises about reality. I respect this fence enormously."

"How's the road?"

Tam's face changed—the flicker of grief and pride that always crossed it now when the road came up. Their road. The one Ilsa had drawn and Tam was building, line by stubborn line, following her ink the way pilgrims follow scripture. It ran from the settlement up over the coastal hills, through country that had never been erased from anything, toward the passes where the first traders and the first letters and—soon, Tam swore by every tool he owned—the first wagons would come through.

"Twelve more miles to the pass," Tam said. "The ground's good. Honest ground. It wants a road, if you can believe that—most ground doesn't want anything, but this stretch practically begs." He glanced sideways at Bram, and his voice dropped into the careful register he used for the few subjects that mattered more than tools. "Scouts came back today. From the north road. There are villages up there, Bram. Real ones. People who've heard rumors of us—the folk who came out of nowhere last spring, the ones with the strange old songs and the dragon-lantern. Some of them are curious. Some of them are frightened." He paused. "One of them sent a letter. It's addressed to 'the Keeper of the Fire.'"

Bram looked at him.

"I didn't open it," Tam said. "It's yours. Whatever it is—trade, or questions, or trouble—it should come to the person who tends the flame. That's how they'll understand us. That's how we'll understand them." He pressed a folded letter into Bram's hands, sealed with plain wax, utterly ordinary, utterly impossible: mail. Addressed to a settlement that had not existed five years ago, from people who had never heard of the Sundered Sea, delivered across a border that finally, blessedly, existed.

Bram held it a moment without opening it. Through the paper he could feel nothing at all—no magic, no fate, no itch in his scar. Just paper. Just words waiting. Just the future, arriving the way futures actually arrive: slowly, and then all at once, in the hands of strangers.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Thank the fence." Tam shouldered his tools and headed for the hall, then stopped, and added, quieter, without turning around: "Sef would have liked this part. The part where people write to us. He waited sixty years for the world to remember we were in it." He walked on, and the night took him, and Bram stood alone at the fence with the letter and the sound of the sea.

* * *

He did not open the letter that night. He carried it back to the hall instead, and set it on the mantel beneath the lantern, where the Ember's light fell across the wax seal like a small benediction, and he decided it could wait for morning—for daylight, for the whole settlement gathered, for whatever came next to be met together, the way they had met everything since the stars came back.

Then he sat down on the hearthstone, alone with the fire, and did what he did every night now, the last office of the last keeper of the last dragon.

He talked to it.

"They saw the Fox tonight," he said quietly. "Wren made it. Stole-the-Moon's-Egg, right over the water. Ilsa charted it." The Ember pulsed, slow and warm, and he had learned—or chosen to believe; the distinction had stopped mattering—that the pulse meant *tell me more*, the way it always did, the way a grandmother leans in. "She's growing. Wren, I mean. She asked me yesterday whether the valley was happy, at the end. Whether it minded. I told her the truth, or the closest thing to it I know: that it wasn't alive to mind. That it was a coat the founders sewed, and we wore it until it wore out, and now we've given the cloth back to the wind." He turned his palm to the fire, scar upward. "She seemed satisfied. Children are better at truth than we are. Nobody had to teach her that the sky is worth more than the ceiling."

The Ember brightened. Outside, the wind came up the shingle, carrying salt and the far-off complaint of gulls, and somewhere in the settlement a dog barked once at the enormous honest dark and thought better of it.

"I got a letter," Bram told the fire. "From the world. Addressed to me—to *you*, really. To the Keeper of the Fire." He laughed softly, a little amazed even now. "We've been real for five years and I still can't quite believe it. That anyone out there knows we exist. That we were worth writing to."

The flame leaned toward him, patient, ancient, the last heart of the last dragon that had ever erased a people out of mercy and been forgotten for it. *Still here,* its light said, in the language it had spoken to him since the Sundered Sea. *Still remembered. Still warm.*

He banked the hearth, though it needed nothing, and climbed the stairs to the small room under the eaves where his bed was, and paused at the window as he always did—one last look.

The sky was clear. The Fox ran his bright theft along the shore of heaven, ears pricked, egg gleaming in his tail. Below, the settlement slept: forty-one roofs, one honest fence, twelve miles of new road climbing toward the pass and the world. On the mantel below him, the letter waited with its plain wax seal, holding whatever the world wanted to say to the children of the vanished valley.

Bram Okafor—apprentice, runaway, heir, traitor, keeper—stood in the starlight in a country that existed, and found that for the first time since the night the stars went out, he was not waiting for anything.

Tomorrow there would be a letter to open, and a road to finish, and a child to teach the names of stars, and a fire to greet at dusk with the only prayer he had ever trusted:

*Still here.*

He closed the shutter gently, leaving the latch loose—because doors, in this house, in this country, in this new and enormous life, were always left open—and went down to sleep beneath the constellations, while the Ember burned on in the dark, keeping faith with everyone who had ever been forgotten, and the sea kept saying the oldest true word there was, over and over, to the shore:

*Here. Here. Here.*
