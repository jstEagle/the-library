## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The dead talk more than the living, and they complain less, which is why I prefer them.

That's not quite true. They complain plenty. But their complaints are useful ones — *you carved my name crooked*, or *the wind keeps whistling through my ribs, see to it* — whereas the living complain about weather and taxes and whether I've washed my hands since handling old Marta's femur. (I had. Mostly.) The dead only want one thing from me: their names, cut into bone, so that whatever is left of them after the body quits can stop circling and settle.

I'm Vesper Marchetti, bone-carver of the Weeping Wood valley, third of my line to hold the trade, and before you ask — no, I don't speak to ghosts in any way that would make a good story. The whispers come when I touch bare bone, a sound like someone murmuring through a wall, and they tell me who they were. Then I carve that name into the sternum or the skull, wherever there's room, and the whisper goes quiet. Not gone. Quiet. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point of my trade.

The valley makes this possible, or rather, the valley makes everything else impossible. The Weeping Wood sits in a fold of the world where the old gods' magic cannot follow — the priests used to say the gods' voices simply slide off us like rain off slate. No miracles here. No blessings, no curses, no answered prayers. What we have instead is silence, and in that silence, the dead can be heard clearly for the first time since they died. Everywhere else, apparently, the gods' noise drowns them out.

So people bring me their dead. They carry mothers and sons over the pass on litters, down into the valley where nothing holy can reach, and they pay me in salt, thread, and the occasional goat, and I carve, and the whispering stops, and everyone goes home lighter.

It's good work. It's honest work. It has never once, in twenty-six years, frightened me.

Until the traveler came.

* * *

He arrived on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Dread should at least have the courtesy to arrive on a night with some atmosphere — fog, maybe, or a storm. Instead it was bright and cold, the kind of morning where the birches along the river look like they've been dipped in silver, and Tam Weller came pounding up my path shouting that there was a body at the ford.

"Traveler," he said, catching his breath. "Found him against the warding stone on the far bank. Nobody knows him."

"Everybody's somebody," I said, pulling on my coat. "Somebody just hasn't told us yet."

I want it noted that I said this with confidence. I want it noted because it was the last hour in which I possessed any.

The traveler lay where they'd left him, on a bier of woven branches by the ford, and even before I knelt beside him I knew something was wrong, though I couldn't have said what. He looked ordinary enough. A man of perhaps forty, road-worn boots, a coat mended with careful, unskilled stitches — someone who'd been loved once, or at least tolerated thoroughly. His face was peaceful. That was the first wrong thing. Travelers who die alone at fords do not usually die peacefully. The ford is cold and the far bank is far, and dying is undignified work.

The second wrong thing was the silence.

You have to understand: I don't need to carve anything to hear them. The whisper starts the moment my skin meets their bone, always, without exception, since I was six years old and pressed my palm to my grandmother's hand in her coffin and heard her muttering about the quality of the shroud-thread. It is as reliable as hunger. I laid two fingers on the traveler's wrist, over the radius, and waited.

Nothing.

I moved to the ulna. Nothing. Elbow, shoulder, the long ridge of the collarbone — nothing, nothing, nothing. It wasn't the quiet of a soul already settled; settled souls still murmur a little, contented, like cats purring in another room. This was different. This was the quiet of an empty house. Of a room swept so clean there was no dust left to remember anyone by.

"Well?" Tam asked. "What's his name?"

"I don't know."

"Give it a moment."

"Tam." I kept my voice level, because Tam Weller frightens like a spooked hen and a spooked hen tells the whole valley, and the whole valley telling itself things is how you get men with torches standing outside the houses of people whose only crime was an unusual trade. "There's nothing to hear. His name isn't hidden. It's gone."

"Gone where?"

And there it was, the question I'd been turning over like a stone in my pocket since the first touch of empty bone. Gone where? Souls didn't go anywhere. Souls stayed put until I gave them their names and then they stayed put more comfortably. That was the order of things, as fixed in my mind as the path from my door to the river. The whisper waits, the carver listens, the name goes into the bone, the dead rest. Nobody had ever mentioned a step where something *ate*.

"I don't know," I said again, and hated how it sounded.

* * *

I took him back to my workshop anyway, because leaving him at the ford felt like abandoning a letter half-written. My workshop is the back room of my house, stone-floored, windowless, lit by lamps that burn tallow instead of the blessed oils the outside world uses — blessed oil does nothing here, another small mercy of the valley's silence. I laid him out on my table and worked.

Carving a name is delicate business. You find the densest bone — sternum, usually, or the inner plate of the skull — and you cut shallow, following the grain, letting the whisper guide your hand. Done right, the letters almost seem to rise on their own, the way frost climbs a window. Done wrong, you get a name that won't sit flat, that itches the eye, that the dead themselves will grumble about for a decade. My grandmother could carve a full name in an afternoon. I manage two days, if the whisperer is chatty.

This man gave me nothing to work with. I sat with my chisel poised above his sternum for the better part of an evening, waiting for so much as a syllable, and got the same silence you get pressing your ear to a stone. In the end I did the only thing the trade allows for the truly unknown: I carved a question mark of sorts, an open spiral, the mark we use for foundlings and the unclaimed. *Here lies one whose name is lost.* It's meant to be a comfort. Carving it, for the first time in my life, it felt like an accusation.

Somewhere past midnight, Sable's candle went out.

I should explain. Across the lane lives the ruin of a chapel, and in the ruin of the chapel lives a woman who arrived three winters ago calling herself Sister Sable, though she flinched whenever anyone used the title. She'd come from outside — from the god-touched lands, she let slip once, drunk on my elderberry wine — fleeing something she never named. She tended our sick and buried our unclaimed dead with rites that made my grandmother's generation clutch their pearls, and every evening she lit a candle before her gutted altar and prayed to gods who could not hear her, because nothing holy reaches the valley. She knew that. She lit it anyway. I asked her once why, and she said, "Habit is a kind of faith too," and I decided I liked her.

Her candle went out at midnight. Not guttered — went out, all at once, the way a held breath ends. And I know this because I was awake, sitting across the table from a nameless dead man, and through my window I saw the chapel's one lit window go dark, and in the same breath every whisper in my workshop stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. The settled souls murmuring in their shelves of carved bones — the soft background hum I've slept beside my whole life, the sound I only notice when it's absent, like a river — cut off between one heartbeat and the next. The whole room went silent the way the traveler's bones were silent. Empty-house silence.

Then, slowly, the murmuring started again. But wrong. Thin. Like voices heard through water. And underneath it, so faint I might have invented it, something else — a sound I had no word for, a kind of wet, patient attention, the way a forest goes quiet before something moves through it.

I did not sleep. I sat with my back against my bone-shelves — absurdly, as if they could protect me, as if I weren't surrounded by the very thing going strange — and I watched the traveler's empty face, and I thought about the direction he must have walked to reach the ford. From the east. From the pass. From the god-touched world my parents crossed mountains to keep me away from, the world of answered prayers and crowded churches that I had only ever known as a story adults told carefully, the way you describe a dog that bit you.

Something out there had changed. Something out there had learned to eat names.

At dawn, Sable knocked on my door. She was pale as her own altar linen, and she looked at me the way you look at someone when you've both seen the same ghost.

"Vesper," she said. "My prayers were answered last night."

She said it the way other people say *there's a wolf in the sheep pen*. I stepped aside and let her in, and behind her, far up the eastern pass, the morning light seemed to bend around something I couldn't name — couldn't name, and suddenly understood, with a dread that settled into my bones like winter, might soon be the only honest prayer I had left.
