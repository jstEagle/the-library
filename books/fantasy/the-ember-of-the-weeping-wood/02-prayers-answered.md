## 2. Prayers Answered

I want it noted, for the record of whatever god is still taking minutes, that I did not ask for this.

Three years ago I asked for plenty. I asked the Dawnfather to heal the fevered child in the third row. I asked the Mother-of-Thresholds to open a door for my sister when her husband's fists closed every other one. I asked, once, in the small hours with my forehead against cold marble, for a sign—any sign—that anyone at all was listening on the other side of the veil.

The gods went silent before I finished the sentence.

That was the year the candles stopped relighting themselves. The year the incense smoke fell straight instead of curling toward heaven like it was being pulled. The year the High Cantor stood before the assembled order and said, in the flat voice of a man reading his own death warrant, that the divine silence was not punishment but absence, and that we must learn to worship into an empty room.

Most of them managed it. Faith, it turns out, is mostly habit wearing nice robes.

I could not. I kept praying the way a drowning man keeps kicking—not because it works, but because stopping feels like dying. And then one night I prayed something unforgivable. I prayed: *if you are there, answer me, or I will stop believing, and I will not be sorry.*

Something answered.

* * *

It used my mother's voice.

That should have been my first warning. My mother has been dead eleven years, and she never once called me by my ordination name while alive. But there she was in the dark of my cell at the priory of Saint Vellum's, warm as bread and twice as comforting, saying, *Sable. Oh, my girl. I've missed you so. Come home.*

And I—wept. Let's be honest about that part. Three years of silence and a dead woman's voice cracked me open like an egg. I lit a candle with shaking hands and said thank you, thank you, thank you, and the flame leaned toward me like a dog hearing its name.

Here is what they do not put in the catechism: love makes a magnificent lockpick. Whatever stood in my cell that night did not need to break down my faith. It just needed me to open the door from the inside, and I did, sobbing, grateful, *so happy to be heard.*

It took me nine days to notice the wrongness, because grief is patient and hunger is patienter.

The candle relit itself each dawn—but the wax never shortened. Nine days, one candle, burning without consuming. My mother's voice grew more fluent, more *present*, but it never remembered anything new. It could recite the lullaby she sang me at four. It could not tell me what color dress I wore at my own ordination, which she attended, which she cried at, which she would have mentioned if she were truly watching from somewhere.

I tested it the way you test a dream, gently, hoping to wake. I asked about my sister. The voice hesitated—a hesitation with texture, like something large turning over—and said, *she's happy, my darling, everyone you love is happy,* and that is when the cold went down my spine, because my sister has not been happy since she was nineteen years old, and any real soul on the other side would know it, and more importantly, any real soul would not lie about it.

Lies are expensive. Only hunger lies for free.

* * *

I did what any reasonable priestess does when she discovers her god is a predator wearing her mother's face. I went to the High Cantor.

He listened to all of it—the candle, the voice, the lullaby—with his hands folded and his eyes going grayer by the minute. Then he said, "Sister Sable, the gods were silent for three years. They are silent no longer. The whole order has felt the change. Candles are relighting in every chapel from here to the coast. Prayers are being answered." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because underneath it he was as frightened as I was and had decided to be grateful instead. "Perhaps you should be careful about looking a gift horse in the mouth."

"Perhaps," I said, "you should check whether it has teeth."

They voted me unstable by sundown. Not expelled—expulsion requires paperwork and courage—but relieved of duties, encouraged to rest, escorted to my cell by two brothers who would not meet my eyes. That night my mother's voice came back and it was angry, and anger does not suit a dead woman, and it said, *you told them. You told them about us.* Us. Plural. Like a nest.

I left through the scullery window with my prayer book and nothing else, because the prayer book was the only thing in that building that had never lied to me.

* * *

Which brings me, after six weeks of roads and hedges and sleeping in hay that smelled of other people's secrets, to the Weeping Wood valley.

Everyone knows the stories. The valley where prayers go to die. The hollow in the world where magic cannot follow, where the old gods' voices simply stop at the tree line like travelers refused at a gate. Pilgrims used to come to the edge just to feel it—the silence, the *quiet*, like pressing your ear against a door and hearing nothing on the other side. Heretics claimed it proved the gods were local superstitions. Mystics claimed it was a wound. I always thought it sounded like mercy.

Because here is the thing I learned at Saint Vellum's, the thing that made me flee: if the gods have woken up hungry, then somewhere there must be a place they cannot reach. And if such a place exists, it will be full of people who learned, long ago, how to live without being listened to.

People like me, now.

The warding stones at the valley's mouth are older than any script I can read, and when I laid my palm against the first one, I felt something I had not felt in three years: *nothing*. Not silence the way a room is silent. Silence the way deep water is silent. A nothing so complete it rang. Behind me, at the tree line, I swear the air pressed against my back like a hand—like something at the border had noticed me leaving its reach, and did not appreciate it.

I walked in anyway. Hope and terror feel identical from the inside; I have decided to call mine hope, because terror would have turned me around.

* * *

The village is called Emberfall, though the sign is too weathered to prove it, and it smells of woodsmoke and tallow and turnips, which is to say it smells like every place that has ever actually been lived in rather than painted. The people look at my robes the way you'd look at a snake in a fruit basket—wary, but not surprised. One old woman spat, though whether at the robes or at me personally remained unclear.

"You'll be wanting the bone-carver," she said, before I'd asked anything at all.

"The what?"

"Bone-carver." She nodded toward a low house at the village's end, smoke rising from its chimney in a straight, unbothered line. "For your dead. Everyone comes to the carver eventually." She looked me over, took in the road dust and the hollows under my eyes, and added, almost kindly, "You look like someone whose dead have been talking."

I did not correct her. I am learning that out here, correction is a luxury.

The carver's door was open. Through it I could see a workbench littered with slivers of bone and small sharp tools, and a person—young, sharp-faced, ink-stained to the elbow—hunched over a piece of femur with a graver, carving letters so fine they seemed less written than grown. And the moment I crossed the threshold, the moment I stepped into that little room with its shavings and its lamplight, the voice that had followed me for six weeks—my mother's voice, or the thing wearing it, which had gotten bolder on the road, whispering *come back, come home, we miss you* at the edges of every night—

stopped.

Cut off mid-word, like a door slammed. Like a hand clapped over a mouth.

I stood in the doorway shaking, and the carver looked up, and their eyes went from my face to my hands to the prayer book clutched against my chest like a shield, and they said, in the tone of someone asking about the weather:

"You're the priestess. You've got that look. What did it say to you?"

Not *who*. *What*.

I sat down on their stool without being invited, because my legs had made an executive decision, and I heard myself laugh—a cracked, unhinged sound I haven't produced since novitiate—and say:

"It used my mother's voice. It remembered everything right except the things that mattered. And it's been following me for six weeks, getting closer, and it stopped—" I gestured at the walls, the valley, the ringing nothing outside. "It stopped the moment I came in here. Why did it stop? What is this place?"

The carver set down the graver very carefully, the way you set down a knife when the conversation turns serious. When they spoke again, their voice had gone quiet in a way I recognized from mirrors—from the particular quiet of a person who has just realized the bad thing is bigger than they thought.

"It didn't stop," they said. "Look at your candle-hand. Look at your fingers."

I looked. Along the pads of my fingers, faint as breath on glass, the skin had gone pale—frost-pale, waxy, in neat little whorls like fingerprints that weren't mine.

"That's what happens," the carver said, "when something hungry touches you and can't get in. It leaves marks where it tried." They reached across the bench and turned my hand over, examining the whorls with a professional eye, and their expression was not pity. It was recognition. "Whatever's been answering your prayers—it's been eating souls where I come from too. The nameless dead started arriving last month. Corpses with nothing left inside. And now it's found a way to talk to the living." A pause. "It's found priests."

Outside, far away at the valley's mouth, past the warding stones, past the tree line, something that had been waiting patiently for six weeks began—at last, at the very edge of hearing, in my mother's voice—to sing.
