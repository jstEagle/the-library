## 3. What the Bones Refuse to Say

The second nameless corpse arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for mending fences and arguing with the miller about grain prices. Tuesdays were not for the dead.

By Friday there were four of them laid out in my workroom, and by the following week I had stopped counting, because counting meant admitting that something was wrong in a way my ledger had no column for.

I want to be clear about what my trade is, because people outside the valley always get it wrong. I don't raise ghosts. I don't speak to spirits. What I do is quieter than that. When someone dies, their last whisper—the thing they were, pressed down to a single syllable—settles into the bones. Left alone, it rattles there forever, restless as a stone in a shoe. My job is to carve the name into the bone, and the whisper reads its own name and goes still. Rest. That's all it is. A carpenter of quiet.

Or it was. Because a whisper needs *something* to be a whisper of, and the travelers coming over the pass lately had nothing left inside them at all. I'd hold a femur to my ear the way you'd hold a shell to hear the sea, and there was just—nothing. Not silence, even. Silence has a texture. This was the absence where a sound should have been, and it made my teeth ache.

The first one, I told myself, was an accident. People die badly on the road sometimes; souls fray. The second one, I told myself, was coincidence. By the fourth, I had run out of comfortable lies, and I sat on my stool surrounded by bones that refused to whisper, and I did what any sensible craftsman does when the work turns strange.

I kept working anyway.

* * *

It was mid-morning, three days after the fourth body, when the crack happened.

I was working on old Marta Ellery's humerus—a valley woman, ninety-one years, died warm in her bed with her grandchildren's names in her mouth, the kind of death we all pretend we're owed. Her whisper was strong and sweet, muttering about her late husband and whether she'd left the oven on in 1963. Good material. Easy carving.

I had cut the M, the A, half the R—

And the bone split. Clean down the grain, sharp as a snapped wishbone, loud enough that a bird startled off my windowsill.

Now. Understand: bones do not crack under my hands. Forty years of this trade, and I could carve a name into green wood without splitting it, into river stone without chipping it. The whispers *cooperate*. They hold still for the knife the way a cat holds still for the hand it trusts. It is the one law of my life, older than my own name: the bone holds.

Marta's whisper didn't go still. It went *thin*—stretched like taffy, like something pulling it from the far end—and then it guttered out entirely, and I was left holding two pieces of a dead woman's arm and a silence with teeth in it.

I sat very still for a long time.

Then I got up, walked to the shelf where I keep the finished carvings waiting for burial, and put my ear to each one. Grandmother Ash. The ferryman Dole. Little Tam Wicker, who drowned at seven and whose whisper used to sing. All silent. All of them. Every name I had ever carved, every soul I had ever settled, gone quiet—not resting, not the way mine sounded when the work was done right, but *gone*, the way a room is empty after the furniture's been carried out.

Something was eating them. Something had been eating them for a while, patient and wide-mouthed, and I had been sitting here in my valley thinking my silence was a wall, when really it was just a dinner bell ringing somewhere no one had bothered to check.

* * *

She was standing in my doorway when I looked up, and I'll admit my first thought was that the day had simply decided to finish going wrong in style.

She wore a priestess's grey, or what had been a priestess's grey before the road got at it—hem torn, sleeves rolled, travel dust to the elbows. Dark skin, close-cropped hair going silver at the temples, and eyes that had the particular shine of someone who hasn't slept in a week but has been *very awake* for all of it. She had a pilgrim's staff and no pilgrim's patience.

"You're the carver," she said. Not a question.

"Depends who's asking."

"Sable." She said it like she was surrendering a weapon. "Formerly of the Order of the Quiet Flame. Formerly a priestess at all, if the gods stay dead, which—" She stopped. Looked at my workbench. At the split bone lying there like evidence. "That cracked mid-carving."

"It did."

"That's impossible."

"Thank you. I'd begun to hope so."

She came in without invitation, which I decided to forgive because her hands were shaking and she hid it by gripping her staff, and I know that trick because I invented it. She picked up the two halves of Marta Ellery's humerus with a reverence that surprised me, turned them in the light, and went the color of candle smoke.

"I've seen this," she said. "Not here. Out there. In the god-touched lands. Shrines where the offerings are untouched but the gods behind them are *starving*. Prayers answered by something wearing the answer like a coat." She set the bone down gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping child you suspected of something. "There's a signature to soul-hunger. A shape to how it feeds. And this—" she tapped the clean split, "—this is a crown-shaped hunger."

"I'm going to need more words."

"The king." She said it flat, fast, like pulling a splinter. "Aldric. Twenty-two years on the throne, pious as a plowhorse, and eight months ago the gods went silent everywhere except through him. His priests called it favor. His armies called it victory—he won battles he had no business winning. And now prayers are being answered again, all across the realm, and every answer comes back hungry." Her eyes came up to mine. "The crown-god has woken, carver. Whatever rides King Aldric has opened its mouth, and it is eating its way toward your valley one soul at a time."

I looked at my shelves of silent bones. Forty years of rest, unmade.

"Why tell me? Why come here at all?"

"Because your valley is the only place the magic doesn't reach, and I thought—I *hoped*—it would be safe." She laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "I fled my order when the gods went quiet. I prayed for ten years into a silence that never answered, and when I finally stopped praying, finally grieved them like the dead, something started answering everyone else. Do you understand what that's like? To be punished for giving up exactly one prayer too soon?" She wrapped both hands around her staff. "I thought I was mad. Everyone said I was mad. And then I found a village where the candles relight themselves and the wells grant wishes, and the people there were so *happy*, and I watched a woman thank her god for healing her son while the boy stood there with milk-white eyes and no name left behind them, and I ran. Again."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the miller was arguing with someone about grain prices, bless him, keeping the world ordinary by force of habit.

"I thought I was the last sane person," Sable said quietly. "Walking around in a world that's stopped noticing it's being eaten."

I picked up the halves of Marta's bone and fit them together. They didn't want to join. Nothing wanted to join anymore.

"I've spent forty years listening to the dead," I said, "and until this month, every single one of them confirmed that the world ends at the edge of this valley. Now the dead are going silent, my work is cracking in my hands, and a priestess walks in off the road telling me a god is eating souls with the king's face on." I looked at her. "I also thought I was the last sane one. It appears we're both unemployed."

That almost got a laugh out of her. Almost. It caught somewhere in her chest and died there, and what came out instead was worse—a small, exhausted sound from someone too tired to be frightened properly.

"What do we do?" she asked.

And there it was, the question I'd been dodging since Tuesday. Because the honest answer was *nothing, hide, we're valley-born, the magic can't follow us here*—except it could, apparently, it was already reaching, one devoured traveler at a time, and walls made of silence don't hold against a thing that eats sound.

"We find out what it wants," I said. "Specifically. Because a thing that eats everything doesn't usually walk past the one pantry it can't open. Your crown-god has been feeding for months, and yet here I am, un-eaten, and here's my valley, untouched. Either it can't reach us—" I turned the split bone in my fingers, "—or it's saving us for last. And I'd very much like to know which."

Sable stared at me. "You're talking about leaving the valley."

"I'm talking about *knowing things*. Leaving is a separate conversation."

Before she could answer, there was a knock at my door—three raps, urgent, the miller's rhythm—and when I opened it he was gray-faced and breathing hard.

"Vesper. There's a man at the ford. Armed. Says deserters' marks on his face and a king's seal in his pocket, and he's begging asylum." The miller swallowed. "He says the army's coming behind him. Says the king himself is marching on the valley, looking for someone." He wiped his mouth. "Looking for *the carver*."

Behind me, Sable went very still.

"Well," I said, picking up my coat. "It seems the conversation's been moved up."
