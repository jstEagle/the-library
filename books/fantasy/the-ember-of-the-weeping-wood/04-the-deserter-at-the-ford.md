## 4. The Deserter at the Ford

There are three things a knight is taught never to do: break an oath, turn his back on a brother-in-arms, and run. I did all three in the space of one night, and I'd do it again before breakfast if it meant not hearing that voice come out of the king's mouth a second time.

Let me back up.

I served House Aldric for eleven years. I was there when he was crowned, when his queen died of the winter fever, when Prince Ilan rode out to the border and didn't ride back. I held the king's stirrup at the funeral and watched him weep like any father would, and I thought: *there but for the gods' silence goes every man who has ever buried someone.* The gods had gone quiet decades before I was born—everyone said so. Prayers went up like smoke into a sealed room. We kept the rituals anyway, because ritual is what you do when the alternative is admitting you're alone.

Then, on the third night after the prince's marker-stone was raised, I came to the king's tent with the watch reports, and I heard him talking.

Not dictating. Not praying. *Talking*—the easy, familiar cadence of a man in conversation with an old friend. Except the tent was empty. Except he was facing the corner, where his own shadow lay thrown long and wrong by the brazier-light, and the shadow's head moved when the king's did not.

"Corvin," Aldric said, without turning around. His voice was his voice, but underneath it, something else spoke too—the way two singers can hold one note an octave apart. "Come in. He wants to meet you."

*He.*

I am not a clever man. I've made my peace with that. But even a dull dog knows when to bare its teeth and when to tuck its tail, and everything in me that wasn't frozen stiff with terror was screaming *tail*. I set down the reports. I bowed. And then I walked out of that tent, past the sentries, past the picket lines, past the edge of the firelight where the horses were tethered, and I took the best of them and I ran.

They'll call it desertion. It is desertion. I signed my name to a life and then I stole a horse out from under it. But here is what I know, and what I have carried through every mile since: whatever wears King Aldric now is not King Aldric. And it is gathering an army—not to defend anything, but to *feed*.

* * *

The valley had a reputation, which is why I went to it.

Every soldier hears the stories eventually. The Weeping Wood, they call it—a fold of hills south of the Marches where prayers don't travel and magic doesn't answer, where the old gods' reach simply stops at the ridgeline like a tide against glass. Superstition, mostly. But superstition is just intelligence wearing festival clothes, and I had learned that the thing in the king's shadow was very much a creature of answered prayer. So: a place where nothing answers seemed like the last sensible address in the world.

I ditched the horse at the border stones—they spooked at the treeline, rolling their eyes like the wood itself was a wolf—and walked in on foot with a stolen sword, a dead man's cloak, and no plan worth the name.

Three days I wandered those wet green hills before I found anyone. The wood dripped constantly; I never learned whether that was weather or grief. On the third evening I smelled woodsmoke and followed it down to a ford over a brown river, and beyond it, a crooked house with bone shavings scattered in the yard like pale snow.

A woman stood in the doorway. Dark-skinned, silver-streaked hair cropped short, wearing vestments I recognized from a former life—the sun-and-candle sigil of the Order of the Last Light, though hers were patched and travel-worn and had clearly been worn as work clothes for years.

"You're bleeding on my ford," she said.

"Am I?" I looked down. My side had been leaking for some hours. In my defense, I'd been distracted by the pursuit, the hunger, and the persistent conviction that the trees were watching me. "Sorry. I'll bleed elsewhere."

"Elsewhere is full of people hunting you." She came down the path, unhurried, arms crossed. "Three riders came through yesterday asking after a knight in stolen colors. They had milk-white eyes and they thanked me for directions I hadn't given them. So." She stopped an arm's length away and looked me over the way a farrier looks at a lame horse. "You can die in the river, or you can come inside and let Vesper look at that wound. Those are your options. I'd choose fast."

"You're a priestess," I said stupidly.

"I'm a lot of things. Mostly unemployed." She turned and walked toward the house. "Coming?"

Here is the thing about shame: it tells you that you deserve the river. That a man who breaks his oath should drown in cold water rather than accept warm bread from strangers. I stood at that ford for a long moment, listening to the water and the dripping trees, weighing myself against the current.

Then I thought of the king's shadow moving without him, and I chose the bread.

* * *

The carver was younger than I expected—young enough that "Vesper" could have meant anything, though I learned later they didn't much care what it meant. They sat me by the fire, cut my shirt off with a bone-handled knife, and cleaned the gash in my side with a efficiency that suggested they'd done this many times and found it boring both times.

"You're Corvin Hale," Sable said from the hearth, where she was stirring something that smelled better than I deserved. "Third lance of the king's own guard. There's a price on you."

"There's a price on most things I've ever wanted."

"They say you ran in the night." Vesper's hands didn't pause. Up close I could see fine white scars across their fingers, dozens of them, the marks of a lifetime of knife-work. "That you left your post without a word."

"I left more than a word." I stared into the fire. "I left the king to it."

"To what?" Sable asked. But Vesper had gone still, their eyes coming up to mine with an expression I couldn't read—hunger, maybe, or dread. Waiting for the answer the way you wait for a diagnosis.

So I told them. All of it. The tent, the shadow, the doubled voice, the word *he*. The way the army had changed in the weeks after—not in orders, exactly, but in appetite. Men volunteering for patrols with a brightness in their faces that had nothing to do with duty. Chaplains praying again for the first time in a generation, and swearing the prayers were being *answered*, and laughing while they said it. The supply wagons rerouted toward the capital carrying cargo nobody would name, guarded by men whose eyes had begun to go pale as skimmed milk.

And the last part. The part I'd told no one, because saying it aloud made it real: the night I fled, I'd passed the command pavilion and heard the king giving instructions to his captains, and the instruction had been a single sentence, repeated like a refrain:

*"Find me the carver. Find me the one whose bones are silent."*

When I finished, the fire popped. Sable had stopped stirring. Vesper sat with their scarred hands flat on their knees, looking at the flames, and when they finally spoke their voice was very quiet.

"My carvings have been cracking. For a month now. Names I've carved clean for twenty years, splitting down the middle like green wood." They looked up at me. "The dead I carve—they whisper. Their names, mostly. Sometimes more. Lately the whispers have been going silent one by one, and the ones that are left sound..." They searched for the word. "*Afraid.*"

"It eats souls," Sable said. She said it the way you'd report the weather, flat and final, and I understood suddenly that this woman had known, had perhaps been the only person in the world who'd known, for longer than any of us. "That's what answering prayers costs. It doesn't grant wishes out of generosity. It grants them the way a fisherman baits a hook. Every candle that relit itself, every prayer answered since the solstice—that's it feeding. And now it's woken properly, and it's hungry, and it wants—" She stopped. Looked at Vesper.

"Wants me," Vesper said. Not a question.

"The valley-born," Sable said, to me, as if explaining to a child. "Bone-silence. The one substance it can't digest. If it can't eat you, it will want to own you. Or unmake you. Or—" she shrugged, a small bitter motion, "—all three, in order of convenience."

I looked around that low room—at the priestess who'd lost her god twice, once to silence and once to this; at the carver with their cracked knives and frightened steady eyes; at the fire, the dripping eaves, the whole hidden valley that had sheltered me for exactly one night—and I felt the shame shift in my chest. Not lift. Shift. Because I had spent three days telling myself I'd run like a coward, and sitting there, I understood that I had run like a messenger. The difference between those two things is whether anyone receives what you carry.

"So the king marches here," I said. "For you. And I led him to your door by bleeding on it."

"You led yourself here," Vesper said. "He was already looking. You just arrived first." They rose, wincing at their own stiffness, and began gathering their carving tools from a shelf—small bone-handled things, laid out with the tenderness other people reserve for children. "How long, Corvin? How fast does an army move when it's hungry?"

"With supply wagons? A fortnight to the ridgeline. Without them—" I thought of the pale-eyed riders, the unnatural brightness of the marching columns. "Four days. Five."

Sable laughed, short and joyless. "Then we have four days to decide whether to run deeper or—" she glanced at Vesper, and something passed between them, some agreement already half-made, "—or stop running entirely."

"Stop running *toward* what?" I asked.

Vesper picked up a finished carving from the mantel—a small femur-bone etched with a name I couldn't read—and held it to the firelight. For just a moment, I swear the thing hummed, faint as a struck glass.

"Toward the crown," they said. "We're going to need a knight. Preferably one who knows the way into the capital and has already stopped caring what the king thinks of him."

I opened my mouth to say that I absolutely still cared, that shame was the only possession I had left, that a deserter was a poor foundation for any enterprise—

And that was when the whisper started. Low, from somewhere near the hearth, or under it, or under the world: a young man's voice, thin as smoke, saying clearly enough that Sable dropped her spoon and I put my hand on my sword hilt:

"He's telling the truth. And he's right about the four days. Ask him about my father's dream, knight. Ask him what the king sees when he sleeps."

Vesper went white as the bone in their hand.

"That," they said slowly, staring at the empty hearth, "is a new voice."
