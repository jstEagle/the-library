## 5. A Prince Among Whispers

The femur arrived on a Tuesday, which I only know because Sable keeps a calendar of saints' days and insists on announcing them like weather. "Feast of the Unnumbered Tears," she said, setting the wrapped bundle on my workbench with the reverence of a woman handling a sleeping snake. "Also, this came up from the border traders."

"Cheerful holiday," I said. "What's in it?"

"A leg."

She was right, as she usually was when the news was bad. The wrapping was oilcloth gone stiff with age, and inside lay a human femur longer than my forearm, dense and yellow-white, the kind of bone that comes from a man who ate well his whole life and died badly at the end of it. There were marks on it. Not the clean nicks of a butcher's saw or the gnawing of honest animals. Something had *scored* it—long parallel grooves, like someone had tried to write on the bone and failed.

Bones that come to me usually whisper. It's a faint thing, more felt than heard, like a word remembered from a dream: a name, sometimes a place, occasionally just an emotion with nowhere left to go. My trade is simple enough to explain to a child and strange enough that children believe it faster than adults. I carve the name into the bone. The whisper settles into the grooves like water into a dry riverbed. The dead rest. The living stop dreaming about them.

This bone said nothing.

Not silence, either—I want to be precise about this, because silence is my native language and I know its dialects. This was not the valley's flat, holy quiet. This was the silence of a room where someone has just stopped screaming. A held silence. A listening one.

"Well," I said, to no one in particular, because Sable had already retreated to the doorway where she could pretend she wasn't watching. "Aren't you interesting."

I should have set it down. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, in whatever ledger the Weeping Wood keeps of my poor decisions: *Vesper Marchetti knew better, and picked up the bone anyway.* But you have to understand what three months of nameless dead does to a person. Every corpse since midwinter had arrived hollow, its soul already eaten by whatever is loose in the god-touched world beyond our stones. Carving for them was like carving gravestones for smoke. And here, finally, was a bone with something *in* it. Something fighting to stay.

You don't leave a drowning man floating because the water looks wrong.

I clamped it gently in the vise and took up my finest graver, the one I use for names with soft letters, and I asked it the way I always ask, out loud because the dead seem to prefer it: "Who were you?"

The knife touched bone.

And the bone *screamed*.

* * *

I want to be clear that bones do not scream. In thirty years of this trade I have carved infants and murderers, grandmothers and soldiers, and never once has a bone made a sound. Sound requires air and throat and all the machinery of the abandoned body. What happened instead was worse: the scream went straight past my ears and into whatever part of me does the hearing-that-isn't-hearing, and it blew through me like a storm through a shut window, rattling everything I'd ever carefully nailed down.

*They wore him. They wore him and he let them because they wore Father first—*

I came back to myself on the floor. I don't remember falling. Sable was crouched over me saying my name in the particular tone priests reserve for the dying and the stubborn, and the femur was still clamped in the vise, and the single groove my graver had cut was glowing.

Not reflecting light. Glowing. A thin gold line, like a crack in the world with dawn behind it.

"That," Sable said, "is not possible."

"You're a priestess whose candles relight themselves. Your opinion on possible is noted and filed."

"Vesper." She gripped my shoulder. Her hands were shaking, which frightened me more than the bone did; Sable's hands hadn't shaken since the night she told me about the voice answering her prayers. "That glow. I've seen it before. On the pilgrims. On the king's coin. That is the light of the crown-god, and it is *inside your workshop*, and we need to burn that bone right now."

But the whisper was coming again, and now that I'd stopped flailing I could hear what it actually was. Not a scream anymore. A voice. Young. Exhausted in the specific way of someone who has been shouting for help for a very long time and has begun to apologize for it.

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry—I didn't mean to frighten you. Please. Please don't burn me. I've been so cold.*

I looked at Sable. She looked at me. Somewhere in that look we conducted an entire theological debate, and I won it by simply turning back to the bone and saying, gently, the way you'd speak to anything small and hurt:

"I'm not going to burn you. Who are you?"

A pause. Then, wonderingly, as if it had forgotten how:

*Ilan.*

And then, with the bitter precision of a boy who'd had time to rehearse his own obituary:

*Prince Ilan Aldricson. Heir to a throne currently occupied by the thing that killed me.*

* * *

We moved him to the hearth—not to burn, but because firelight seemed to steady him, the way a candle steadies a nervous guest. Sable insisted on drawing a circle of salt around the workbench, then admitted she didn't know if salt did anything anymore, then kept the circle anyway, which I think tells you most of what you need to know about faith.

He talked. Gods, how he talked. Two years, maybe, of silence pouring out of him in a flood, and I sat with my graver idle in my hand and listened to a murdered prince tell me how he died.

It had happened at the autumn hunt, two years back. He'd ridden ahead of the party—eager, young, showing off—and found his father standing alone in a clearing, talking to his own shadow. He'd laughed about it at first. Then the shadow had turned its head, and it hadn't been shaped like a shadow anymore, and his father had turned too, and his father's eyes had been full of a gold light that Ilan recognized from the old forbidden texts Sable would later confirm he absolutely should not have been reading.

*It was already in him,* Ilan said. *It had been wearing him for weeks. Wearing him thin, like—like a glove worn every day until it fits. It caught me before I could reach the horses. It used Father's hands.* A long pause here, the kind of pause that has weight. *That's the part I can't carve off myself, carver. It made Father watch. It keeps him watching. He's in there. He's been in there for two years, screaming behind his own face, and everyone in the capital kneels to him and calls it a blessing.*

Sable had gone gray. "The crown-god," she whispered. "The Hollow King. We thought it was a metaphor. The old texts called it the thing that eats prayers—we thought it meant it *ignores* them."

*It doesn't ignore them,* Ilan said, and there was something horribly patient in the ghost-voice now, the patience of a teacher with a slow student. *It answers them. That's how it feeds. Every answered prayer is a little piece of the pray-er. You ask for rain, it takes your memory of sunlight. You ask for your mother's recovery, it takes your love for her. The whole kingdom is being eaten one hope at a time, and thanking it for the meal.*

"And the valley?" I asked, though some part of me already knew, the same part that had known since the first nameless corpse came over the pass. "Why do the dead go silent near our stones? Why do the souls vanish before they reach us?"

*Because you're the only food it can't swallow.* The gold seam in the bone pulsed, brightening. *Valley-born bone. Valley-born blood. Whatever your ancestors did to make this place, it made you into something it cannot digest. The dead who die inside your stones—their souls are quiet, sealed, *yours*. It's been starving at your borders for centuries. And now it's awake, and hungry, and it has an army, and it has learned that somewhere in the Weeping Wood lives a carver who can put names into bone.*

*Ilan,* I said slowly, *why are you really here?*

Silence. The worst kind, the listening kind.

*Because they let me be found,* he said quietly. *The traders who brought me didn't know what they carried. But the thing wearing my father knew. It wants you to come out, Vesper Marchetti. It's been clearing a road to you—one empty soul at a time. And if you don't come out to meet it—*

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Corvin had brought the news a month ago: *the king marches here, seeking the carver.* I'd filed it under things that were probably exaggerated. Sitting in my workshop with a murdered prince glowing gold in my vise, the filing system collapsed.

* * *

Sable wanted to destroy the bone. I want that stated fairly, because history will likely record her as reckless, and in truth she spent that whole night arguing for caution while I argued for the insane option. Burn the prince, she said. Deny the crown-thing its beacon. But Ilan had shown me something in those hours of talk—shown me the shape of the trap, and the shape of the door inside it.

"It knows I'm the one substance it can't digest," I said, pacing. "Fine. Then I'm the one thing it can't predict, either. It's built its whole plan around getting me out of this valley. So we walk out—but on our terms, carrying the one ghost it thinks it owns."

"You'd trust a murdered prince's ghost?"

"He's the only person alive—or otherwise—who's seen the thing up close and stayed himself. That's worth more than an army." I looked down at the femur, at the thin gold line, and felt something I hadn't felt since the whispers started dying: purpose, sharp and warm as a newly honed edge. Grief had been sitting in my chest for months like a stone. Now the stone had become a whetstone. "Besides. His father is in there. Screaming. For two years. Somebody has to go get him out."

Sable stared at me for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, cracked, entirely un-priestly laugh—and started packing her vestments.

Dawn was graying the windows when I finally sat back down at the bench, alone with the prince. I took up my graver and began, carefully, letter by letter, to carve his name—not to seal him, not yet, but to give the whisper a home it could carry. *Ilan Aldricson. Beloved. Betrayed. Avenged, if I have anything to say about it.*

*Thank you,* he whispered, as the last stroke settled. And then, softer, almost afraid:

*Carver. When we cross the stones—it will feel you leave. The moment you step past the warding, it will know its meal is walking toward it on its own feet.*

"Good," I said, and discovered I meant it. "Let it set a plate."

Outside, far beyond the valley's rim, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky. Or perhaps it wasn't thunder. Perhaps it was applause.
