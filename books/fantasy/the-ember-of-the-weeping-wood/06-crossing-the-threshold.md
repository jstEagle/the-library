## 6. Crossing the Threshold

I had forgotten what it felt like to be listened to.

That was the thought that came to me as we stood at the edge of the warding stones, the four of us huddled like conspirators in the blue-black hour before dawn, and I realized my hands were shaking. Not from cold. The valley nights were always cold, and I had grown fond of that, the way you grow fond of a scar. No—the shaking was older than that. It was the shaking of a woman about to walk back into a room she'd sworn never to enter.

"Say something," Vesper said quietly beside me.

"I'm deciding whether to be brave or merely stupid."

"They look identical from here."

I almost laughed. That was Vesper's gift, I'd learned—carving not just bone but silence into shapes you could hold. Corvin stood behind us with his arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who had deserted one army only to enlist in another without being asked. And Ilan—Ilan drifted a pace ahead of us all, translucent as breath on glass, staring at the warding stones with an expression I couldn't read. Ghosts are hard to read. Their faces have already been read once by death.

The stones were older than the valley's name. Seven of them, leaning together like tired old women sharing gossip no one else could hear, and between them ran the seam of nothing—the place where magic stopped, where prayers went unanswered, where the gods' great listening ear simply ended. I had spent eleven years telling myself that seam was a wound. Standing there, I understood for the first time that it might also have been a bandage.

"You're certain the new moon is right?" Corvin asked. "No moon means no light means every ambush in the world."

"No moon means the wards sleep shallowest," I said. "The old rites were written for people who wanted *in*, Corvin. We're going out. Different door entirely."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I wrote half of them before I burned my vestments." I said it flatly, and he had the grace not to flinch. That was another thing I'd learned in the valley: shame shared is shame halved, though never quartered, and never gone.

Vesper touched my arm. "Once we cross, everything changes. You said so yourself. Every stray thought answered. Every wish overheard." A pause. "Including yours."

"Including mine," I agreed. "Especially mine. I'm the loudest sinner here."

* * *

We crossed at the moment when night is deepest and most honest, and I will tell you now what it felt like, though words were made for smaller things.

It felt like drowning in reverse.

One step, and the world was the valley's world: silent, dense, material, a place where stone was stone and bread was bread and a prayer was just air leaving your mouth. The next step, and the air *leaned toward me*. That's the only way I can say it. The whole god-touched world leaned in like a lover who has waited years, and every unguarded corner of my mind lit up like a lamp in a window.

*I want to be forgiven*—and somewhere far off, something said *yes.*

*I'm afraid*—and the dark itself murmured *of course you are, little one.*

*I miss the incense*—and the wind carried a thread of frankincense from nowhere, from everywhere, sweet and cloying and utterly wrong, because no incense burned within fifty miles.

I staggered. Corvin caught my elbow, and even his touch was different out here—charged, attentive, as if the world were taking notes on him too.

"Sable." His voice, low. "What do you need?"

"Nothing," I whispered. "That's the problem. Out here, wanting anything is dangerous. The world wants to give. It doesn't ask what things cost."

Vesper walked beside me, white-faced but steady, and I understood then what I had only theorized: the valley-born carry their silence inside them. Where the magic reached for me like a tide, it slid off Vesper like rain off oiled cloth. The god-thing in Aldric's crown couldn't digest that. Couldn't control it. Which meant that walking beside Vester Marchetti, I was the vulnerable one—and Vesper knew it, and stayed close anyway, a small human wall against a hungry sky.

"The stones," Ilan said suddenly, from ahead. His voice always sounded like it came through water. "Look at the stones."

I turned. Behind us, the seven warding sisters had begun, very faintly, to glow—a pale ember-light, like coals remembered rather than seen. And I felt it then, the thing I will feel until I die: the wards reaching after us. Not angry. Not barring our return. Just *reaching*, the way a mother's hand opens when a child walks out of arms' length.

They would let us back in. But they could not follow. Nothing of the valley's silence could follow past its own border, and I understood, with a grief that arrived whole and without warning, that the woman who crossed back would not be the woman who crossed out. None of us would be.

"It's beautiful," Vesper said softly, watching the glow. "I've never seen them lit."

"That's not beauty," I said, harsher than I meant. "That's a lighthouse. Lighthouses are beautiful because you forget they exist to warn you about rocks."

Vesper looked at me. "And what rock are you warning me about, Sable?"

I opened my mouth to say *the crown, the god-thing, the king who eats souls*—the safe answer, the true answer. But out here, in the listening dark, the honest answer rose first, and honesty out here has weight; it falls.

"Myself," I heard myself say. "Eleven years ago I prayed for the gods to speak again. Every night. On my knees until they bled. And when they finally did—" I gestured at the vast attentive dark around us, at the frankincense wind, at all of it. "It wasn't the gods. It was one thing, wearing their voices like stolen coats. And it learned my name from my own prayers. I taught it how to call me, Vesper. I wrote it letters and signed them."

The wind stirred, gentle, sympathetic, offering comfort like a merchant offering samples. I refused it. Refusing comfort, out here, takes effort. Everything takes effort. That is the price of living in a world that answers.

Corvin shifted his pack. "Then we don't pray," he said. "Simple. Soldiers don't pray much anyway. We think about rations and road distances and nothing else."

I laughed despite myself—a short, cracked sound. "Oh, Corvin. You'll learn. Try not thinking about your brother tonight. Try not thinking about the king's face when you turned your back on him. Try not thinking about whether desertion was cowardice or the only moral act available to you." His jaw tightened. "See? Out here, your mind isn't private anymore. It's a door left open in winter. Things come in. Things go out. And some things out here *read*."

"Then we keep moving," Vesper said. "Fast and quiet and thinking boring thoughts."

"Boring thoughts," Corvin repeated, like a man agreeing to a battle plan involving spoons.

* * *

We walked the first night through country I had known as a young priestess and no longer recognized—or rather, recognized too well. Every shrine we passed was lit. Not tended—*lit*. Candles burning in windows of abandoned chapels, flame standing perfectly still in moving air, each one a small throat singing wordless praise to whatever wore the gods now. Ilan led us wide of them. He knew, better than any of us, what those flames fed on.

At midnight we made camp in a hollow of stones, no fire, and I lay awake listening to the world listen to me.

That was the loss, if you want it plainly. In the valley, silence had seemed like exile. Eleven years I had mourned it—gods gone quiet, prayers falling like stones down a dry well. I had built my whole bitterness on the idea that the silence was a punishment, an absence, a deprivation.

Lying there under the new moon, I finally understood: the silence had been a mercy. A mother turning down the noise so her children could sleep. In the valley, my guilt was my own. My doubts were my own. My prayers went nowhere and hurt no one. Out here, every flicker of faith I had ever felt was a hook still set in my flesh, and something on the other end of the line was reeling, gently, patiently, the way you reel in a fish you intend to eat.

Because here is what I haven't told the others yet, what I am telling only this page: as we crossed the threshold, as the world leaned in and said *yes* to my oldest hunger—I heard, beneath the yes, beneath all the sweet answering voices, one voice that knew me. Knew my order. Knew my vows. Knew the exact cadence of the hymns I sang at novitiate.

*"Sable,"* it said, warm as candlewax. *"You came back. I kept your seat."*

I did not tell Vesper. I did not tell anyone. Because tomorrow we walk toward the capital, toward the Hollow King, toward the thing that ate a prince and wears his father—and I cannot afford for them to know that when it calls me, some traitor chamber of my heart still wants to answer.

Near dawn, Vesper sat up beside me, unable to sleep either. For a while we said nothing, which in the valley had been easy and out here is a discipline.

"It talks to you," Vesper said at last. Not a question. Bone-carvers hear dead things; apparently they hear living ones too.

"Yes."

"What does it say?"

I watched the east go grey. "It says it kept my seat."

Vesper was quiet a long moment. Then: "When it offers you that seat—because it will—it should know something." Those dark eyes, steady as carved stone. "Seats can be emptied. I've unmade worse than furniture."

And there, in the cold grey light, with the whole listening world holding its breath around us, I believed it. Wonder and terror and grief, all in one cup, and I drank it down.

Ahead of us, on the ridge line, the road bent toward the lowlands—and along it, small and distant, moved a column of figures in white, walking in perfect unison, their heads bowed toward the capital.

Pilgrims. Dozens of them. And every one of them, even at this distance, had eyes like milk.
