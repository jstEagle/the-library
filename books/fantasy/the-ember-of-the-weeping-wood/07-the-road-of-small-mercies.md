## 7. The Road of Small Mercies

I had killed eleven men before I deserted the king's army, and I had told myself a tidy story about each one: that they were enemies, that it was war, that the alternative was my own blood in the mud. Desertion taught me the thirteenth truth, which is that stories are just armor you wear over the part of yourself that knows better.

On the fourth day out of the valley, I stopped counting.

Not because there was nothing to count. Because Vesper had asked me—gently, the way you ask someone to hand you a knife blade-first—"How many men have you killed, Corvin?" and I had answered honestly, and they had looked at me with those bone-carver's eyes that see straight through to the marrow, and they had said, "That's not what I meant. How many are you *carrying*?"

Eleven became twelve on the road, and then thirteen, and by the time we reached the shrine at Marrow's Rest I had stopped keeping the ledger because the ledger was winning.

* * *

The Road of Small Mercies earned its name honestly, which is rare for anything on this side of the warding stones. It's a pilgrim track, old as the gods' first silence, stitched together from shrines and way-stations where travelers once left bread for strangers and coins for the dead. Sable walked it like a woman returning to a house she'd been evicted from. Every shrine we passed, her hand rose halfway toward the little stone alcoves—an old reflex, blessing without thinking—and every time, she caught herself and shoved the hand back down like it had betrayed her.

"You could still do it," Vesper said on the second evening. "Bless things. Nobody's stopping you."

"Nobody's *stopping* me." Sable laughed, and it wasn't a kind sound. "Child, when I prayed in that valley for twenty years and got silence, I told myself the silence was holy. A test. A fast. And now the sky answers every stray thought like an innkeeper too eager for coin, and I know—I *know*—that everything I ever believed was true, and it's all been eaten by a thing wearing a crown." She kicked a pebble off the road with unnecessary violence. "Faith isn't a muscle, carver. It's a door. Mine's been nailed shut from the inside."

Behind us, Ilan drifted along the verge, translucent as breath on glass, humming a marching song no living soldier remembered. He'd taken to humming since the border. Ghosts, it turns out, get louder the closer they come to the place they died, and the prince had died somewhere ahead of us, in a palace we were walking toward with the cheerful stupidity of moths.

"The road forks at Marrow's Rest," he said, materializing beside me the way he did when he'd forgotten again that only Vesper and Sable could see him clearly. "Left goes through the shrine town. Right goes around."

"Around adds two days," I said.

"Through adds pilgrims."

I should explain about the pilgrims, because I didn't understand it yet either, and understanding came late and cost us.

* * *

Marrow's Rest announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke, tallow, and under it something sweetish and wrong, like fruit going bad in a closed room. The shrine town sat in a fold of hills, thirty-odd houses huddled around a temple whose spire had been crowned—there was no other word for it—with a sphere of white stone that hadn't been there when Sable last walked this road, back when the gods still slept.

We might have gone around. We almost did. But our food was gone, Vesper's hands were shaking from hunger—they'd never learned to eat on the road, valley folk, they ate like people who expected the meal to be their last—and the town had an inn, and inns have stew.

"We're four travelers," Sable said, arranging us in the inn's doorway like a general deploying troops. "A carver, a knight, a priestess, and—" she glanced at the empty air where Ilan stood, grinning, "—a gentleman who prefers his own company. We are nobody. We are boring. Say nothing interesting until we're fed."

For three hours, we were boring. It was the finest stew of my life. I remember thinking, over the third bowl, that maybe the world would let us be small and unnoticed all the way to the capital, that mercy was a road and we were walking it.

Then the bells started.

* * *

They came at dusk, down from the white-crowned temple, forty or fifty of them in procession, and I want to be precise here because precision is all I have left of that night. They did not look monstrous. That's the horror of it. They looked like neighbors. Farmers, smiths, a woman carrying a baby. Ordinary people walking in step, singing a hymn I knew from my army days—the Thanksgiving of the Unbroken Crown, we'd sung it after victories—and their eyes were milk.

Not blind. Worse than blind. Milk-white and *focused*, every one of them fixed on the middle distance, as if they were watching something lovely happen just past the edge of the world. The baby's eyes were milk-white too. I have tried, in the years since, to make peace with that, and I have not succeeded, and I no longer intend to.

"They're vessels," Sable whispered. Her face had gone the color of candle wax. "Partial possessions. The crown-thing can't be everywhere, so it... pours itself into whoever prays hard enough. Oh, gods. Oh, gods, I *prayed* like that. Twenty years. If the timing had been different—"

"It wasn't," Vesper said. Their voice was steady but their hand found mine, cold fingers gripping like a drowning swimmer. "Sable. It wasn't."

The procession turned down our street. And stopped.

Forty milk-white faces rotated toward our inn door in perfect unison, like a field of flowers following the sun, and a voice came out of all of them at once—not unison, *unison*, one voice wearing fifty throats—and it said:

"*Carver.*"

I have fought in three campaigns. I have held a shield wall while men screamed and burned beside me. Nothing in my life has sounded like that word coming out of that crowd. It was warm. It was patient. It was the voice of something that had already won and was simply arranging the furniture.

"The valley-born silence," the mouths said, smiling, all fifty smiles. "The one substance. Come home to us, little quiet thing. Your king is so hungry, and you are the only meal in the world he cannot digest unless you consent. So *consent*. We will spare your friends. We will spare the valley. We will spare, spare, spare—"

"Corvin," Vesper breathed. "Corvin, the back—"

The back door was already open. Pilgrims filled the kitchen, silent, smiling, milk-eyed, and behind them more came up the alley, unhurried, because nothing that certain needs to hurry.

Here is what I know about myself, and I learned it that night, standing in an inn doorway with a carving knife in one hand and a fire poker in the other: I am not a good man. I am a man who, when the arithmetic comes down to my people versus strangers, does the sum quickly and without flinching, and hates himself after, and would do it again. I deserted the army because the king spoke to his shadow and I couldn't carry that knowledge and keep killing for him. I did not desert because killing had become wrong to me. Killing had become *easy*, and that frightened me worse.

"Ilan," I said quietly. "If you can hear me. Now would be the moment."

And the dead prince, bless him, was already moving.

* * *

You cannot fight what a ghost shows you, but you can fight what a ghost tells you, and Ilan Aldric—murdered heir, dead at nineteen, wearing eternity like an ill-fitting coat—turned out to be the finest battlefield commander I ever served under. He flowed through the pilgrim crowd unseen, reading them like a man reads weather.

"Three by the well, they'll circle left—they always circle left, it's in the hymn's rhythm. The tall one has a billhook, don't let him hook your shield. The woman with the baby isn't holding a baby anymore, look at her arms, *look*—" and she wasn't; the bundle was swaddled cloth and the woman's arms swung empty and terrible, and I struck her down with the poker and felt something in me go with her.

Vesper fought the way water fights: not at all, merely being in the way of everything, ducking under swings, shoving pilgrims into each other, buying seconds. Sable stood in the doorway of the inn with her arms raised and her eyes shut and did the bravest thing I saw that night: she prayed. Out loud. To whatever remained of her dead gods, she poured out the words she'd nailed shut inside herself for twenty years, and whether anything heard her I cannot say—but the milk-eyed pilgrims nearest her faltered, stumbled, wept from those white eyes as if some buried piece of them remembered kneeling.

"Go!" she screamed at us. "*Go*, I'll hold the door—"

We went. Through the kitchen, over the bodies, into the alley, running, and behind us Sable's voice rose higher and higher, a hymn against a hymn, and I made myself not turn around because turning around is how people die, and then—

She caught up with us at the tree line, half a mile on, hoarse and singed and laughing like a madwoman. "They broke," she gasped. "When I named the old gods' names, they *broke*, Corvin. Half of them fell down crying. The crown-thing can pour itself in, but it can't stay. There's still a person in there, fighting." She wiped her mouth. "There's still a person in all of us, fighting."

Ilan drifted alongside, quieter than I'd ever seen him. "My father used to say that," he murmured, to no one, to me, to the dark. "Before."

* * *

We made camp in a hollow of beeches, too tired to build a proper fire, building one anyway because the alternative was sitting in the dark with what we'd done. Vesper cleaned the carving knife with mechanical care. Sable stared into the flames. I took first watch because taking watch is the coward's penance—you get to feel useful without having to talk.

It was Vesper who talked instead. They sat down next to me at the hollow's edge, knees drawn up, and watched the dark where the road ran away south toward the capital.

"Thirteen," they said. "Is that the number now?"

"Fourteen. The woman with the empty arms."

"Corvin." They turned, and the firelight moved on their face, and I saw they were crying without any noise, the way valley folk cry, trained out of sound by a lifetime of listening to the dead. "In the inn. When they offered to spare us. For one heartbeat—one—I wanted you to say yes. I wanted to go to that thing and let it eat whatever it wants out of me if it meant you and Sable and Ilan walked away clean. Duty says I'm supposed to save the world. But duty didn't put your hand in mine tonight. You did. And I need you to know that when it comes down to it—if it comes down to it—I'm choosing wrong. I'm choosing *you* over the mission, and I'd do it again, and I don't know what that makes me."

I thought about the tidy stories. The armor. Eleven names, fourteen names, the ledger that keeps winning.

"It makes you the only honest person on this road," I said. "Because I've been choosing you since the ford, carver, and I stopped pretending otherwise around the second bowl of stew."

Across the fire, Sable lay with her back to us, pretending to sleep, and I saw her shoulders shake once, and I chose to believe it was laughter.

It was Ilan who ended the moment, drifting up through the beeches with his face lit silver and grim. "Riders," he said. "On the south road. Six of them, and they're not pilgrims—they're soldiers, crown livery, moving fast." He looked down at me, and for a moment the murdered prince and the living king wore the same expression, and it was grief. "They're not searching for us, Corvin. They're *fleeing* something. And the thing they're fleeing is between us and the capital."

Vesper stood, wiping their face with the heel of their hand, and picked up the carving knife.

"Then we'd best find out," they said, "what scares the king's own riders."
