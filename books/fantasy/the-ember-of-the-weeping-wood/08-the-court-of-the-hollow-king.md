## 8. The Court of the Hollow King

There are three things I know about palaces, and I learned all three in the space of an hour.

One: they are built to make you feel small, which is a waste of stone, because most people arrive already feeling that way. Two: they smell like lilies and rotting meat underneath, the way anything smells when it's trying too hard to be beautiful. Three: if you want to get into one, don't sneak through a window or bribe a guard. Carry something.

In our case, we carried candles.

*Feast of Thanksgiving,* the criers had shouted for three days as we came down out of the hills into the god-touched country proper — a phrase that still made my skin crawl, because where I come from *god-touched* is what you'd call a wound gone bad. The whole capital had been scrubbed and garlanded and hung with banners the color of new blood. Every household was commanded to bring light to the palace steps at dusk, ten thousand candles for the king who had brought the gods back to his people.

Ten thousand candles. Which meant ten thousand strangers carrying them, and no guard alive who could tell one pilgrim from another.

"Smile," Sable murmured beside me, her hood up, her hands cupped around a flame she had lit herself — honestly, with a taper, which I suspect pained her more than any of us knew. "You look like you're attending your own funeral."

"I'm from a valley where the dead are better company than the living," I said. "This is my funeral face."

Corvin, walking behind us with his shoulders hunched under a pilgrim's rough wool, snorted. Even disguised, even shorn of his armor, he moved like a man built for doors: he watched every exit, counted every guard, and I'd caught him twice already positioning himself between me and the crowd without seeming to think about it. A deserter who couldn't stop protecting people. There was probably a word for that. In the valley we'd have just called him family and left it there.

And Ilan — Ilan walked where he pleased, because walls mean nothing when you're dead. He drifted ahead of us through the press of candle-bearers like a fish through reeds, occasionally turning to walk backward and grin at me, which is a deeply unsettling thing to see a ghost do, mostly because it means they've forgotten how they died, just for a moment.

"The great hall," he said now, pointing with a finger that wasn't quite opaque. "I was crowned there at seven years old. I cried. Father said kings are allowed to cry exactly once, so I'd best spend it wisely." His grin flickered. "I keep thinking I'll remember more. Being close to him. It's like — standing outside a house you grew up in. You know where the rooms are but not what happened in them."

"We'll get him back," I said, because it was true, and because lying to ghosts is a waste of breath; they can hear your heartbeat.

The line up the palace steps moved slow and reverent. Around us, people whispered prayers into their candle flames, and here — this was the part that turned my stomach — the flames *leaned toward them*. Little curls of fire bending like ears. Listening. Answering in the smallest way, a warmth here, a whisper of light there, enough to keep the faithful faithful.

In the valley, magic cannot follow. I was born behind warding stones older than any kingdom, in a silence so complete that the dead have to use bone-carvers like me as translators. I had spent twenty-six years thinking of that silence as a prison.

Standing on those steps, watching fire lean toward weeping strangers, I understood for the first time that it had been a mercy. Out here, everything answers. Nothing asks whether it should.

* * *

The great hall was worse than the steps.

A thousand candles on the tables, chandeliers like frozen fountains of light, and above it all — on a dais beneath a canopy of white silk — King Aldric of Vael, the Hollow King, the God-Crowned, eating little candied plums off a golden fork while his courtiers laughed at jokes I suspected weren't funny.

We were placed among the lesser nobility, thanks to forged invitations Corvin had acquired from a man who would not be needing them, and I will say this for the crown-thing: it throws a generous feast. The wine was old enough to have opinions. The bread was warm. Somewhere a choir sang a hymn whose words I could almost understand, and whose melody made my teeth ache.

I didn't eat. I watched Aldric.

Up close, at last, after weeks of road and fear and Ilan's fragmented memories, I finally saw what Corvin had seen the day he deserted: a man flickering inside his own body like a candle in wind.

It's hard to describe without sounding mad. He was there — Aldric was *there*, a broad, tired man in his fifties with grey coming into his beard and laugh lines around eyes that had once, clearly, been kind. And then he wasn't. Then the thing wearing him surfaced, and the king's face went smooth and vast and wrong, like a pond going still before lightning, and his hands moved with a grace no human hands have, and he smiled at courtiers with too many teeth behind the smile. Back and forth. Candle in wind. Sometimes both at once, the two of them layered over each other like badly printed pages.

"He's still fighting," Ilan said softly, at my shoulder. Only I could hear him; I'd carved a sliver of his bone small enough to wear against my sternum, and through it his voice came clear as a bell while everyone around us heard nothing but the choir. "Look at his left hand. When the other one is driving, the left hand hides. Under the table. Like a child hiding from a beating."

I looked. He was right. The king's left hand was curled under the table's edge, knuckles white, gripping — I leaned forward slightly — gripping a scrap of cloth. Small. Embroidered. A child's handkerchief, maybe. Ilan's?

"Oh," I said, and my throat closed.

"Yes," Ilan said. "So don't hate him. Whatever happens tonight. Promise me."

"I promise," I said, and hated how easily promises come out of the mouth compared to what they cost going down.

Sable touched my wrist under the table. Her lips barely moved. *"The reliquary. Behind the dais. Can you feel it?"*

I could. I'd been feeling it since we entered, actually — a wrongness at the back of my skull, like a tooth that knows it's cracked. Behind the dais, past a screen of gilded wood, the crown-thing kept its hoard. Bones, Sable thought. Relics. Souls in jars, maybe. The leavings of every prayer it had answered and every petitioner it had eaten. It was *feeding tonight*. I could feel the hall's devotion pouring toward the dais like water toward a drain, and somewhere in that drain, something was getting stronger by the hour.

Which is why our plan was insane, and why we were doing it anyway. Get close. Let me touch the king's living bone — a hair from his head tucked into a carving knife's reach, a drop of blood, anything — and carve his true name. Unbind him. Free Aldric, starve the thing, run like cowards.

Simple. Clean. Completely impossible, given that the hall held four hundred guests, sixty guards, and one god.

"You're doing the face again," Corvin murmured on my other side.

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided something terrible and you're deciding whether to tell us."

"I'm always doing that face."

"Yes," he said placidly, "but usually you tell us."

I never got the chance.

* * *

It happened between the third course and the toast. The music stopped — not ended, *stopped*, mid-phrase, forty singers choking silent at once — and the Hollow King rose from his throne.

He didn't look at the court. He looked at me.

Across four hundred heads and a thousand candles, the thing wearing Aldric's body found my eyes and *held* them, and I felt it the way you feel a hook going through a fish's lip: sudden, total, intimate. The hall blurred. The noise of the feast fell away like water down a hole. And a voice that was not a voice poured directly into the inside of my skull, warm as bathwater, patient as erosion.

***There you are.***

Every instinct I owned screamed at once. I gripped the table. Corvin's hand landed on my shoulder — he couldn't hear it, but he could see my face, and whatever he saw made him go pale.

***Little carver. Valley-born. Bone-silent. Do you know how long I have looked for you? How many souls I have sifted, seeking the one flavor I cannot taste?*** The king began, slowly, to walk along the dais, and the court bowed and rippled in his wake like wheat, delighted, oblivious. ***You are the only silence left in my world. Everything else sings to me now. Everything else is food. But you — you are a locked door, and I have wanted so badly to open you.***

"Get out of my head," I whispered. Around us, guests glanced over, smiling, assuming piety.

***No need for anger. I am not cruel. I am hungry, yes — hunger is honest — but I pay my debts. Ask anyone I have eaten.*** Something that might have been laughter rippled through my skull. ***Here is my offer, carver, and I will make it only once. Your gift. The hearing. The bone-silence that lets the dead speak through your hands. Give it to me — freely given, freely taken — and I will spare your valley forever. The wards may crumble. The stones may fall. My hunger will pass over that valley as if it were marked in blood. Your mother. Your neighbors. The graves you have put to rest. All of it, safe. Forever.***

And there it was. The price I had sworn — *sworn*, on Ilan's grave-marker, on Sable's broken altar, on Corvin's shame and my own stubborn heart — never to pay. Offered back to me wrapped in the one thing I could not refuse.

Home. Everyone home. Safe forever.

All it would cost was everything I was.

Because that's the trick of it, isn't it? The hearing isn't a tool I carry. It's the shape of my soul. I have never known who I'd be without the whispers — the names in the bones, the small grateful voices when a carving takes and a soul rests. Take that away and what's left? A deaf woman in a quiet valley, holding tools she no longer has any right to. A stranger wearing my life.

But my mother would live. The whole valley would live.

***You are considering it,*** the voice observed, gentle, horrible. ***Good. That is not weakness, carver. That is love. Love is simply hunger with better manners. We understand each other, you and I.***

"Vesper." Corvin's grip tightened. "Whatever it's saying — your eyes have gone strange. *Vesper.*"

Sable had gone rigid, her lips moving in some counter-prayer, sweat beading at her hairline. Ilan stood in front of me, translucent and furious, shouting things I couldn't hear over the flood.

And the king stopped walking. Ten feet away now. Close enough to see the tears standing in Aldric's eyes — real tears, the man's own, weeping behind the thing's smile — and the left hand rising, trembling, half-formed into a shape. A warning? A plea?

Then the smile widened, and the Hollow King extended his right hand toward me across the feast, gracious as a bridegroom, and said aloud, in a voice that rang to the rafters:

"Come forward, child."

Four hundred faces turned. Sixty guards shifted. Corvin's hand dropped to a knife that wouldn't help.

"There is a guest among us," the god-crowned king announced, eyes fixed on mine, "whom the gods themselves have sent. Come. Receive your blessing."

And every candle in the hall bent toward me at once, like ten thousand leaning ears, waiting to hear what I would say.
