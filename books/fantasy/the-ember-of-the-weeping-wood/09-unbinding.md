## 9. Unbinding

**SABLE**

There is a reason the Rite of the Opened Door is forbidden.

I want that recorded somewhere, in case I survive this and someone asks. It is not forbidden because it fails. It is forbidden because it works too well. You open yourself as a vessel, and the divine pours in, and the divine does not care what shape you are. The last priestess who performed it was found three days later, kneeling, smiling, entirely empty. They buried her with full honors and a closed casket, because there was nothing left inside her to look at.

I had sworn never to perform it. I had also sworn to serve the gods faithfully, and look how that turned out—one of them wearing a king like a stolen coat, eating souls at a feast of thanksgiving while musicians played something jaunty in a minor key.

So here is my theology now, revised this very evening: sometimes you must answer hunger with hunger. Sometimes the only way to hold a monster's attention is to be the most interesting thing in the room.

I knelt at the center of the great hall's mosaic floor, in the circle Vesper had chalked around me with hands that shook only a little, and I began to speak the old words—the ones carved into my bones during ordination, the ones I'd spent six years trying to forget.

The candles went out. All of them. Every flame in the hall bent toward me like grass in wind.

*There you are,* said the voice that wore Aldric's throat from across the room, where the king sat enthroned among his court of milk-eyed pilgrims. *The little runaway. I wondered when you'd come home.*

"I never left," I said, and lied beautifully, because lying to gods is the one prayer they always hear. "I've been waiting for you my whole life."

And I opened the door.

* * *

**CORVIN**

Doors. Everyone's obsessed with doors tonight. Sable opens one, the thing behind the crown comes through it, and mine is the actual physical door—the servants' entrance to the feast hall, which I am supposed to hold against whatever the crown-god sends when it realizes what we're doing.

Vesper stood over King Aldric with a bone-carving knife the length of my forearm, and the king's true name ready to go into his living shinbone, and I thought: I deserted an army once because I couldn't watch a man talk to his shadow. Now I'm holding a door so a bone-carver can perform surgery on a monarch with no anesthesia except faith. My mother would have opinions.

The first pilgrim came through the dark of the corridor at a dead run, eyes white as boiled eggs, mouth open on a sound that wasn't a scream so much as a hymn being played backwards. I put my shoulder into him and put him into the wall. He got up. Of course he got up. Things that don't breathe don't stay down just because you're polite about it.

"Corvin," Vesper said behind me, not looking up from the king's leg, voice steady in that way that scared me more than screaming would have. "How many?"

"One so far. Ask me again in a minute."

The second and third came together, and I learned something about myself in the next ninety seconds, something I'd been avoiding since the day I dropped my sword in the mud outside Kettering and walked away: I could still do this. The killing hadn't gone anywhere. It had just been waiting, patient as a dog, for me to need it again.

I hated that. I hated that the door held because my hands remembered, and I hated that some small traitor part of me was almost grateful.

But then—behind me—I heard Vesper begin to carve, and I heard the sound the hall made in response, and every other feeling I had went quiet.

* * *

**SABLE**

Here is what the Opened Door feels like, for those keeping records: it feels like drowning in reverse. Like being pulled out of deep water by something enormous that may intend to eat you. The crown-thing came into me the way winter comes into a house—all at once, through every gap, looking for warmth to steal.

It showed itself to me. That was its mistake, or its arrogance; hard to tell the difference with gods. I saw it as it truly was: a hunger older than the valley, older than the silence, a thing that had once been worshipped properly and had never recovered from being forgotten. It had crawled into Aldric's crown the way a spider takes up residence in a skull, and it had been drinking the kingdom ever since—prayer by answered prayer, soul by devoured soul, rebuilding the vastness it remembered.

*I know your kind,* it said inside me, vast and amused. *You opened the door. Shall I tell you what I give those who open doors?*

"You give them emptiness," I said. "I've read the reports. I wrote some of them."

*Then why—*

"Because you're not looking at me." And I smiled, and it cost everything I had left, and I said the words I'd rehearsed until they didn't taste like ash anymore: "You're looking past me. At the little carver with the knife. Isn't that right? Isn't the silence in their bones the one flavor you can't make yourself?"

The vastness *stopped*. The whole hungry attention of a god swung off me like a lighthouse beam swinging to sea.

I held the door. I held it with my hands and my spine and every scrap of faith I had ever regretted, and I screamed down the bond to Corvin without any magic at all, just a human woman making noise:

*"NOW!"*

* * *

**CORVIN**

Now. Yes. Except "now" arrived with six more pilgrims, and my sword arm had begun composing complaints, and behind me Vesper was saying words I will carry to my grave.

Not the carving. The carving made a sound like ice cracking on a spring lake, a bright terrible singing, and the king screamed—a real scream, a *human* scream, the first honest sound I'd heard out of Aldric's mouth since I deserted—and the crown on his head glowed white-hot and began, impossibly, to *pull*, straining against his skull like a living thing hooked by the jaw.

No. What I'll carry is what Vesper said, quietly, conversationally, to the god inside the metal:

"He has a name. He's had it since before you ever touched him. His mother gave it to him in a birthing room that smelled of lavender, and she cried, and you were not there. *Aldric.* Son of Maren. Father of Ilan. And I'm writing it where you can never wear it out."

The knife went in. The name went deeper.

The crown-thing turned on Vesper fully—there was no other word for it. The light in the hall didn't dim so much as *turn*, all of it, every shadow leaning toward that chalked circle and the small figure standing in it with blood on their hands and their chin lifted like they were daring the sky to do its worst.

And I broke my orders. I abandoned the door—it could keep itself, damn it—and I crossed the hall with my sword out and put myself between a bone-carver and a god, because apparently that was the kind of man I'd decided to be sometime in the last month without notifying myself.

"Corvin—" Vesper started.

"Shut up and finish," I said. "You don't get to do this alone. None of us gets to do anything alone anymore. That's the whole point of us."

Behind me, the door burst open. Behind me, Sable was on her feet with her arms spread wide and light pouring out of her mouth like she was the vessel after all, like she'd been the trap the entire time. Behind me, a dead prince's ghost stepped out of the air beside his father's throne and laid a hand no one living could see on the crown's edge.

We were one body. I understood that then, finally, the way you understand fire by burning. Four people who had each come to this valley broken, fighting as a single creature with four hearts and one purpose.

The crown-thing looked at all of us—at the priestess burning herself as a lamp, at the knight who'd chosen shame and then un-chosen it, at the ghost of the boy it had murdered, at the carver whose silence it craved—and for the first time in its long, long hunger, I swear on my disgraced name, it hesitated.

* * *

**SABLE**

It hesitated, and I felt the hesitation through the open door of myself, and I knew what it meant even as I knew what it would cost.

Hesitation is doubt. Doubt is starvation. A god that doubts begins, in that instant, to die—but dying things are most dangerous, and this one had teeth enough left to take someone with it, and I knew with a certainty that bypassed all my training exactly who it would reach for.

It reached for Vesper.

The vastness poured out of me—not retreating, *lunging*, abandoning its grip on the king's mind to seize the one substance it could not digest, the way a drowning man grabs at the water. I felt it pass through me like a cold tide and I did the last forbidden thing, the thing beneath the forbidden thing: I closed the door early, snapping shut around it, holding a fistful of god inside my own failing body to slow it by half a heartbeat.

Half a heartbeat. In the arithmetic of tonight, that was a fortune.

Through the closing dark I saw Vesper turn to face it—saw them plant their feet the way they stood at their carving bench, patient, unafraid, a person from the valley where magic cannot follow, about to teach a god what that means.

I saw them open their mouth to offer the hearing of the dead.

And the world went white.

* * *

**CORVIN**

White. Everything white, and silent, and then—

Then the silence *broke*, and what rushed into it was the sound of a king weeping.

I got to Aldric first, because Sable was down and Vesper was standing very still with their hands empty and their face turned slightly wrong, listening to something that wasn't there anymore—or rather, not listening, unable to listen, and learning it in real time. The crown lay on the mosaic floor in two pieces, cooling, ordinary gold, worth maybe forty crowns to the right fence. The god was gone. Where it had gone, whether into nothing or into Vesper's offered gift or into whatever Sable had caught in her cupped and burning hands—I didn't know yet.

Aldric knelt on the floor of his own feast hall, a big man folded small, sobbing over the femur Vesper had set across his knees. Over the name carved into it in a hand steadier than any of ours deserved: *ILAN. SON OF ALDRIC. BELOVED. HE FORGIVES YOU.*

"The prince says—" the king choked, and stopped, and tried again. "He says he waited for me. He says he's been waiting *years*—"

"I know," Vesper said.

Their voice sounded strange. Flat. Quiet in a new way, a way I'd never heard from them, and I realized with a lurch that it wasn't grief alone. They couldn't hear Ilan anymore. They couldn't hear any of them. The dead had gone silent for Vesper Marchetti forever, traded away freely, and the first thing their deafness had bought was a father kneeling over his son's name.

They looked at me. Their eyes were wet and their expression was the hardest thing I saw all night, harder than the pilgrims, harder than the god: they were trying to find out if they were still *in* the family, now that the gift that started it was gone.

So I did the only sensible thing. I grabbed them, and Sable, groaning back to consciousness, grabbed them too, and the three of us went down in a heap on the mosaic floor of a palace while a king wept and a ghost—so help me, I saw it, I'll swear it in any court they like—a ghost sat down beside his father and put an arm around his shoulders.

"We won," Sable whispered into Vesper's hair. "You impossible, stubborn, magnificent carver. We actually—"

She stopped. Her head came up. Across the hall, the great doors blew open on a wind that shouldn't exist indoors, and every candle in the capital guttered at once, and from somewhere far below the city—deeper than cellars, deeper than crypts, deeper than anything men had built—came a sound like an enormous breath being drawn.

The crown-thing had hesitated. But hesitation, I was learning, was not the same as death.

And something down in the dark had just realized it was starving.
