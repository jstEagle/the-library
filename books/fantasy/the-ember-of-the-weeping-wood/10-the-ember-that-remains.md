## 10. The Ember That Remains

The last thing I heard the dead say was my own name.

Not whispered, the way they usually came—no, this was Ilan's voice, bright and close as a hand on my shoulder: *Vesper. It's time.* And beneath him, fainter, all the others I'd ever carved to rest, a tide of them, the whole drowned choir of the Weeping Wood rising up like breath before a storm.

I had my knife against Aldric's forearm bone. His true name was half-finished in it, letters still warm from the cutting, and the crown-thing was screaming through the king's mouth in a voice that made the feast-hall torches gutter sideways.

*You would unmake me,* it said. *Carver. You would spend the only silence I cannot eat.*

"Not spend," I said. "Give away."

Here is what no one tells you about a gift like mine: it isn't hearing. Hearing is ears and air and distance. What I had was older than that—a door in me that the dead walked through, that bones spoke from, that the valley's warding stones had been built to muffle because even the old gods hadn't known what to do with a child born with an open door where other people kept their souls shut tight.

The crown-thing wanted that door. Wanted it the way a starving man wants bread, the way a drowning man wants air. Valley-born bone-silence, Sable had called it—the one substance it couldn't digest, couldn't control, couldn't crack open. And so I did the only thing left that made sense to someone who'd spent her whole life learning what bones want when they're tired.

I opened the door all the way. And then I broke the hinges off.

It didn't hurt the way I expected. It hurt the way everything hurts at once, which is to say there was no room left for noticing any single pain. Every whisper I'd ever heard poured out of me into the thing wearing the king—not just names, but the whole long conversation of the dead, every grief and grudge and lullaby, forty years of listening given away in the space between one heartbeat and the next.

The crown-thing tried to swallow it. Of course it did. It swallowed and swallowed, and the dead poured through it like water through a net, and for the first time since it had woken in Aldric's skull, it learned what it felt like to be full of something it could not keep.

"You can't—" it said, in Aldric's voice, in Ilan's voice, in a hundred stolen voices, each one fraying at the edges. "You can't give it *away*, it's yours—"

"It was never mine," I told it. "That was the whole point. You keep things. That's all you know how to do. And I'm not keeping anything anymore."

Then I finished the name.

One stroke. The last letter of Aldric Marchand, third of his line, father of one murdered boy, man who had once—before all of this—been famous in three kingdoms for laughing too loudly at his own jokes. My knife bit living bone, and the bond severed with a sound I will never describe to anyone, because I couldn't hear it anyway.

The hall went white. Then silent. Then—

* * *

Silence.

Real silence. Not valley-silence, which was never really silent—it hummed with the dead the way a shell hums with the sea. This was the silence everyone else had been living in their whole lives, and let me tell you, I understand now why you people are all so strange. It's enormous in here. It echoes. There is nothing in my head but me, and I don't entirely recommend it.

I woke on the flagstones with Corvin's cloak under my head and Sable's thumb pressed hard against my wrist, counting a pulse she clearly didn't trust.

"She's back," Corvin said, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's.

"I'm deaf," I said.

Sable flinched. "To the dead," she said carefully. "Your ears work. Can you hear me?"

"Loudly," I said. "Unfortunately."

She laughed, and then she cried, and then she laughed again while crying, which struck me as a very priestess way to handle things. Corvin just sat down heavily on the steps beside me, armor clattering, and put his face in his hands, and stayed there a while. He'd held the doors against eleven pilgrims with milk-white eyes. Two of them had been men from his old company. Nobody said anything about that. Some prices you pay quietly.

Aldric was kneeling across the hall.

He was kneeling over a slab of pale stone I didn't remember being there—Ilan must have guided my hands to it during the fight, or after, or in whatever strange country between—and he was weeping. Not the flickering, borrowed weeping of the thing that had worn him. This was ugly, human, whole-bodied grief, a king on his knees in his own feast-hall sobbing like a man at a graveside, because that was exactly what he was. The marker bore Ilan's name in my carving, and beneath it, in smaller letters, the words Ilan had whispered to me while I worked, the blessing he'd asked for:

*He did not do this. Forgive him. He is the other victim.*

"Vesper." Aldric looked up at me, and his eyes were his own, red-rimmed and wrecked and present. "What did you—what did it cost you?"

"Everything I was hiding behind," I said. "Turns out I had people. Turns out I didn't need the hiding."

He bowed his head over the stone again. Behind him, in the doorway, the court stood in various states of terror and confusion, and none of them moved, and none of them spoke, and the candles burned straight up for the first time in a year.

* * *

We stayed in the capital nine days. Long enough for Aldric to hold a real funeral instead of a feast of thanksgiving; long enough for Sable to be offered her order back and to decline it with a courtesy so precise it drew blood; long enough for Corvin to be pardoned, promoted, and offered a knighthood of the realm, all of which he accepted with the expression of a man being handed a live snake by someone who means well.

"They'll want you to stay," he told me on the ninth night, on the wall above the river. "The king will offer you anything. Court carver. A tower. A title."

"I know."

"And?"

"And I've spent my whole life in a valley where nothing changes," I said. "I'd like to go home to a valley where something does."

He nodded slowly. "And if we came with you?"

I looked at him. He was watching the river with elaborate casualness, the way men watch rivers when they have just said something that terrifies them.

"The valley doesn't hide people anymore," I said. "The wards are down. The old gods' magic reaches it now—Sable checked. It's just a place. Wet, cold, excellent mushrooms."

"That sounds terrible," Corvin said. "When do we leave?"

* * *

The road home took eleven days, and I hated every mile of it, and I mean that in the best possible way. I had never been outside the valley. I had never seen a city burn its garbage, or a mountain that wasn't ours, or the sky doing whatever it wanted without tree cover. Everything was too loud, too bright, too much, and I loved it with the helpless love of a woman meeting her own life twenty years late.

Sable talked the entire way. She was rebuilding her faith from scratch—new prayers, small ones, addressed to nobody in particular, tested constantly for hunger. "If it answers," she said, "it's not a god. If it asks for something, run." So far the sky had failed to answer her twice, which she declared very promising.

Corvin taught me to skip stones, badly, on both our parts. Ilan—

Well. Ilan was gone. Truly gone, now, the way the dead are supposed to go, and I couldn't hear him even if he weren't. But the night before we crossed back into the valley, I dreamed of him standing in a green place with no shadows in it, and he wasn't speaking, because ghosts don't need to speak in dreams, and I understood him perfectly anyway.

*Thank you,* he didn't say. *Go home. Be loud. They can hear you now.*

* * *

The valley looked different without its silence. That's the strangest part. Same trees, same ford, same crooked bridge with the missing rail—but the Weeping Wood had always seemed to me like a held breath, and now it exhaled. Birds sounded louder. The river argued with itself around the stones. Somewhere upslope, someone was singing, badly, which meant people, which meant the valley had stopped being a hiding place and started being a village again.

Our cottage was exactly as we'd left it, except that someone had left flowers on the step. Wild ones, arranged with more enthusiasm than skill.

"For you," said a voice, and there was Marta from the ford, and behind her half the valley, awkward and grinning, come to see the carver who'd gone off to kill a god and come back with a knight, a priestess, and no hearing for the dead.

They didn't know about that last part yet. I watched them look at me, waiting—waiting for me to do what I'd always done, take the bones, hear the names, settle the dead. Old Tomas pushed forward with a wrapped bundle, some poor ewe that had died in the night, and the whole crowd went quiet the way they always did when the carving began.

I unwrapped it. I held the small curved bones in my hands. And I felt—nothing. No whisper. No name rising up to meet the knife. Just bone, and my knife, and the enormous human silence I'd bought with everything I had.

"I can't hear them anymore," I said. My voice didn't shake, which surprised me. "The dead are quiet to me now. Forever."

Nobody stepped back. That's the thing I'll remember until I die—that not one person in that crowd stepped back, or looked away, or decided I was less than I'd been. Instead old Tomas cleared his throat and said, "Well. Then you'll have to ask us the names, won't you? Like everyone else."

And Marta laughed and said, "Half of them you carved yourself, girl, we remember," and someone started telling the story of the ewe, whose name was Buttercup, who had been a menace and much beloved, and I stood there in the middle of my valley holding a dead sheep's leg bone and crying in front of the entire village, which, for the record, is not something the old Vesper could have done either.

* * *

We buried Buttercup at sunset. I carved her name with no guidance at all, just my hands and my memory and Marta's insistence on spelling, and when it was done the bone said nothing, and it was enough. It had always been enough. I'd just needed forty years and a god's death to learn it.

That evening the four of us sat on the cottage step—me, Sable, Corvin, and a king who had abdicated by letter and arrived two weeks later smelling of road dust and asking, shyly, whether the valley took in former monarchs. The stars came out over the Weeping Wood, thick and indifferent and beautiful, and no voices whispered under them, and for the first time in my life I didn't miss what I couldn't hear.

Because here is what I know now, the thing I traded a kingdom's worth of ghosts to learn: the dead were never the ones keeping me company. I was keeping *them*. And the living—who talk too loud, laugh wrong, sing badly, leave flowers on steps—they were there the whole time, waiting for me to come out of the silence and join them.

"Vesper," Sable said, nudging me. "You're smiling at nothing."

"Not nothing," I said.

Above us, an ember rose off the cookfire, climbed past the eaves, and hung glowing against the dark for one impossible moment before it faded—small, brief, stubborn, and warm.

Just like everything worth keeping.
