## 3. What the Maps Won't Show

The frost-moon had waned to a thin nail-paring by the time I let myself think about the manuscript again.

I had not slept properly since the night Ashen arrived. The child slept like the dead in my spare bed—no dreams, no turning, a stillness so complete that twice I had gone in just to watch her chest rise. Children were not supposed to be that quiet. Even sleeping children carried weather inside them: the small storms of hunger and dream and growing bone. Ashen was calm the way deep water is calm, and I did not yet know enough to be afraid of it.

So instead of sleeping, I worked. That was my answer to most things that frightened me: ink, vellum, the discipline of line. I had begun a new chart—the eastern passes, the ones no caravan used anymore—and found my hand drifting, over and over, toward the margin where I'd copied out those strange characters from the forbidden fragment. The tongue older than kingdoms. The words Ashen spoke in her sleep.

I told myself I was being thorough. A mapmaker who ignored an unknown script was a mapmaker who deserved to lose her way.

But that was a lie, and I knew it even as I ground the ink. What I wanted was to understand her. And wanting to understand something is the first step toward wanting to keep it, and I had spent thirty-one years learning not to want things.

* * *

The fragment itself lived at the bottom of my cedar chest, wrapped in oilcloth beneath three generations of unfinished charts. My grandmother had left it to me along with her compasses and her habit of never finishing anything—a scrap of vellum, maybe a hand's width square, torn from some larger work. The priest's grandfather had burned the rest. Everyone in the valley agreed this had been righteous. Everyone in the valley also quietly came to me when they needed to know what lay beyond the ridge, which meant everyone in the valley understood that burning knowledge does not unmake the world it describes.

I spread the fragment under my good lamp and looked at it the way I hadn't dared to as a girl.

It was a contract.

I don't know why I hadn't seen it before. Perhaps because I'd been young, and contracts were things merchants made, dry and dull. But now, with thirty years of drawing boundaries behind me, I recognized the anatomy immediately. There was the grant clause, marked by the doubled rule I used myself for land grants. There was the consideration—what was given and what was received—set off in that same looping script Ashen murmured at night. There were witnesses, their names half-gone, and there was a seal, pressed deep into the vellum, that showed a mountain with a door in its side.

And there, in the lower corner, in a hand smaller and shakier than the rest, a single line of annotation:

*Paid in full. One soul, returned. See ledger, folio nine.*

Ledger. Folio nine.

My hands went cold around the lamp handle. It was nothing. It was a merchant's bookkeeping note, a hundred years old or more, referring to a document that had been ash before my grandmother learned to write. There was no reason on earth for my heart to be hammering.

I put the fragment away. I cleaned my pens. I banked the fire. And then I sat in the dark and listened to Ashen breathing through the wall, and I thought about folio nine until thinking became a kind of fever.

There was no ledger in the valley. I would have known; I knew every archive, every parish box, every bundle of deeds in the elder Hesta's keeping. If a ledger existed, it existed outside. Beyond the boundary. In the world where magic could follow you.

In the world I had never once entered.

* * *

I want to say I deliberated. I want to describe a long moral struggle, nights of weighing, the careful cartographer consulting her inner compass. The truth is faster and less flattering: I lasted four days.

On the fifth morning I walked to the elder Hesta's house with the fragment in my pocket and asked her, in the pleasant voice I used for villagers, whether she kept records going back before the founding chart.

Hesta was ninety-one and sharp as a burr. She looked at me over her tea for a long moment, and I watched her decide something.

"There's a box," she said finally. "In the undercroft of the meetinghouse. Sealed since before my mother's time. We're not to open it."

"Who says?"

"The seal says." She smiled without warmth. "You're the one who reads old scripts, Noor. Go read what the seal says. Then decide if you still want what's underneath."

* * *

The undercroft smelled of stone and stopped time. The box sat on a shelf of its own, apart from the parish records like a leper at a feast, and it was bound not with rope but with a band of metal I had never seen the like of—dark, faintly warm, and worked all around its circumference with the same looping script that filled my fragment.

I knelt in the dust and read it aloud, because some part of me believed that saying a thing plainly would shrink it.

*This record is closed while the bargain holds.*

Bargain. Not law, not ordinance. Bargain. The valley's oldest sealed record was not a legal instrument at all. It was the receipt of one.

I should have stood up then. I should have brushed off my knees, climbed the stairs, and gone home to finish my chart of the eastern passes, and lived out my life in the pleasant fog of not-knowing that the whole valley wore like a coat.

Instead I took out my knife and cut the band.

It parted like rotted fruit skin. Whatever held it had been holding for a very long time, and perhaps it had only ever been waiting for someone willing to pay attention. Inside, wrapped in linen gone amber with age, was a book.

A ledger. Folio after folio of entries in a dozen different hands across two centuries, each one a transaction, each one bearing the mountain-with-a-door seal. I read names I knew from gravestones. I read sums paid in grain, in years of labor, in firstborn daughters. And then, near the middle of the book, in an entry dated the winter of my birth, I read my own name.

*Sandoval, Noor. Born under the frost-moon. Soul conveyed to the court beneath the mountain per standing agreement, one per generation.*

Below it, in different ink, added later:

*Bought back. Paid in full by M—* the rest of the word was scratched out, gouged so hard the vellum thinned. *See folio nine.*

Folio nine. Again. Always folio nine, like a door in a dream that opens onto the same room.

I turned back through the pages with fingers that did not feel like mine. Folio nine was a summary page, a reckoning, and there in the column of souls bought back—one line, one name, mine, and beside it the price. The price was not coin. The price was written as a single word, and the word was *Maren*, and beneath Maren's name, in the clerk's tidy hand: *conveyed in exchange. Permanently.*

Maren Sandoval. My mother.

I sat down on the cold floor of the undercroft, because my legs had resigned without informing me.

Here is what I had always believed about my mother: that she had walked out one autumn morning to gather late herbs and simply never come back, swallowed by the ordinary cruelty of the world—fever, mischance, a river in spate. The village had pitied me gently for twenty years. Poor Noor, motherless, fatherless, odd. They had folded my strangeness into that grief, as if one explained the other.

But grief implies loss. Loss implies accident. This was neither. This was arithmetic. My soul had been sold to the court beneath the mountain before I drew breath—that was the standing agreement, one per generation, the toll the valley paid for its sanctuary—and someone had bought it back, and the currency of that purchase had been my mother.

I had spent my whole life believing I was the one thing magic couldn't touch. I was not untouched. I was *purchased*. I was a debt settled, a line item, a folio entry. Every map I had ever drawn, every careful mile I had stayed inside the boundary, every night I had told myself I was content—I had been living inside the terms of a contract I had never signed, in a sanctuary paid for with the woman whose face I could barely remember.

The vertigo was total. The floor of my life was not stone. It was paper.

And if mine had been bought back—if my name appeared in that ledger with the word *returned* beside it—then the protection over me was not the valley's at all. It was individual. Specific. Which meant the child sleeping in my spare bed, whose soul had never been bought back, whose name I now understood must sit somewhere in these pages without any redemption entered after it—

Ashen's name was in this book.

I found it three folios later. *Ashen, of no family given. Conveyed. Outstanding.* Two words, and the second one was the whole of her silence, the whole of her sleep like deep water, the whole of whatever the court beneath the mountain did with what it owned.

I closed the ledger. I sat in the dark a long while. And when I finally climbed the stairs into the grey afternoon, I was no longer the woman who had walked down them.

* * *

She woke screaming that night—not words, but the shape of words, the grammar of terror without the flesh of language. I was through her door before the second cry, and I gathered her up, and she clung to me with a strength that frightened me more than the screaming had.

"It's all right," I said, uselessly, the way everyone says it. "You're here. You're with me. You're safe."

She pulled back far enough to look at my face, and her eyes in the lamplight were ancient and drowning, and she said—in the old tongue, the court-tongue, the language of the fragment and the ledger and the seal—a single sentence.

I didn't understand it. But I have spent my life reading dead languages in the margins of maps, and understanding is not the only way a thing can reach you. The shape of it, the cadence, the way her voice broke on the third word—it reached me the way a bell reaches you through a wall.

Later, much later, when she slept again and I sat with the ledger open on my knees and the fragment beside it, matching character to character by lamplight like a woman translating her own sentence of death, I worked it out.

The sentence meant: *They are singing my name wrong.*

I looked down at the ledger, at *Ashen, of no family given. Conveyed. Outstanding.* At the mountain with the door in its side stamped into the page. At the annotation that had started everything: *paid in full, one soul returned*—and the gouged-out name of the woman who had paid it.

Then I looked at the boundary of the valley, invisible beyond my window, holding back the whole breathing world of magic like a dam holds a river. The sanctuary that had kept me safe, kept Ashen silent, kept everything still.

It was a bargain. And bargains, I knew now—knew in my bones, in my ink-stained hands, in the paper floor of my life—bargains can be read. And anything that can be read can be mapped.

And anything that can be mapped can be unmade.

I did not know yet what it would cost. I did not know that the price had already been named once, in a clerk's tidy hand, and that the ledger had a place waiting for a second entry.

I only knew that in the morning I would go to the elders, and ask them the question the sealed box had been asking all along: *what exactly did we buy, and who is still paying?*

And I knew—sitting there in the dark with a stolen child asleep against my shoulder and my mother's name scratched into vellum—that whatever answer they gave me, I was done living inside the terms.
