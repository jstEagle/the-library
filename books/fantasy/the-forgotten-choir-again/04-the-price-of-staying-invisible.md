## 4. The Price of Staying Invisible

They came for me at dawn, three days after the frost-moon, and they did not knock.

I knew something was wrong because the village was quiet in the wrong way. Mornings in the valley had a texture I could have drawn from memory with my eyes closed: the creak of Wellan's cart on the mill road, the argumentative clatter of geese behind the tannery, Tam's mother singing off-key to her bread ovens. That morning there was only silence, and beneath it a hum, like a held breath. The kind of quiet a room makes when people inside it have stopped talking about you.

Ashen was still asleep in my spare bed, curled around the carved wooden bird Tam had given her, her breath slow and even. In sleep she looked like any child. It was only when she woke that the otherness came back—the eyes that tracked things none of us could see, the mouth that shaped sounds like water over old stones.

I had barely tied back my hair when the knock came after all. Three blows, measured, official.

Maren Ostell stood on my step with the elder's sash knotted over her coat, and behind her, arranged on my lane like a delegation from a country I'd never been issued papers for, stood the rest of them: Hobb Rennick, oldest man in the valley; Sera Quill, who kept the tithe-house; and old Father Anselm, who had wanted Ashen turned out on her first night and had not forgiven me for saying no.

"Noor," Maren said. "We need to speak with you. Inside."

"Then speak."

"Not here." Her eyes flicked past me, toward the window where Ashen slept. "And not in front of the child."

* * *

They would not take me to the meetinghouse. Instead we walked—Maren, Hobb, Sera, and I, with Anselm trailing like a shadow that disapproved of its owner—to the tithe-house, which sat at the valley's center the way a stone sits at the center of a ring of water. It was the oldest building we had. Older than the church. Older than any family name. Its walls were built of a stone no quarry in the valley produced, dark and faintly warm to the touch even in winter, and every child in the valley grew up being told not to ask about it.

I had asked about it constantly, as a girl. I had been told, with increasing sharpness, to stop.

Inside, the air smelled of dust and lamp oil and something else—something mineral and old, like the inside of a cave that had once been on fire. Sera lit the lamps. Hobb lowered himself onto the long bench with the groan of a man whose knees had outlived his patience. And Maren went to the far wall, the one with the tapestry hanging over it that I'd seen ten thousand times—a weaving of the valley itself, green and gold, stitched by some ancestor's patient hands—and pulled it aside.

Behind it was a door. Not a door of the dark warm stone but set into it, iron-banded, older than iron should allow, with no handle that I could see.

"You've never been down there," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"Nobody has. There's nothing down there."

"There is one thing down there." She pressed her palm flat against the door, and I heard—not saw, heard—a sound like a lock turning somewhere deep in the mountain's bones. "One thing, and one truth. We've kept both for longer than this valley has had a name. And now the child has come, and Anselm says she speaks the old tongue, and Hobb dreamed the boundary stones were singing three nights ago, which means—" She stopped. Steadied herself. "Which means it may be time. So before you decide anything else about that girl, Noor Sandoval, you are going to learn what your valley is. What it actually is. And what it costs."

The door swung inward on darkness and cold air that smelled of ink.

Of all things. Of all smells in all the world, it smelled of my workshop.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the building was tall, which should have been impossible, and I have since learned that impossibility is simply what the under-places do when they're paying attention. Lamps had been left burning along the walls—someone tended them, then; someone came here—and at the bottom the stairs opened into a round chamber hewn from the same dark stone, and the chamber was full of books.

Not books as I knew them. These were ledgers, great bound slabs of vellum and hide, stacked on shelves cut into the living rock, hundreds of them, thousands, their spines stamped with years going back past any calendar I'd ever copied. The nearest shelf bore dates within living memory. The farthest wall disappeared into darkness where the dates became symbols I didn't recognize, and then, near the floor, marks that were not writing at all but something older—spirals, perhaps, or the tracks of some creature that had walked in circles for a very long time.

In the center of the room stood a reading table, and on the table lay a single ledger, open, as if someone had known we were coming.

"What is this place?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted.

"The account book of the valley," Hobb said. He had come down the stairs slower than the rest of us, and he leaned on the rail now, and I noticed for the first time how tired he looked—not old-tired but guilty-tired, the tiredness of a man who has carried a weight so long he's forgotten it isn't part of his body. "Every generation, the elders bring the new keeper down here. Every generation, we swear the same oath of silence. And every generation, we tell ourselves the price is worth it, because we've never had to be the ones who paid."

"Sera," Maren said quietly, "read it to her."

Sera Quill came to the table. Her hands, usually so steady over the tithe-counts, shook as she turned the open ledger toward me. "This is the current volume. One per generation. Each page is—" She swallowed. "Each page is a year. And each year has a name written in it."

I looked down.

The page was headed with the current year, in a hand that was elegant and utterly unfamiliar, and beneath the date, in ink that seemed too red to be ink, was a single line:

*Paid in full: one soul, freely rendered. The sanctuary holds.*

Above it, the previous year. Same words. Above that, another name, another year, going up the page and onto the pages before, one soul for every year, and I understood suddenly why each ledger lasted only a generation, and my stomach turned over slowly, like a boat coming about.

"That's not possible," I said. "People don't disappear. People die, they're buried, we mourn them—"

"Do they?" Maren asked softly. "Think, Noor. Think like a mapmaker. Where is our graveyard? Who was the last person you saw buried?"

I opened my mouth to answer and found nothing there. I searched my memory the way I'd search a coastline for a harbor I was certain existed, and the certainty dissolved under examination. I could remember funerals—or I could remember the idea of funerals, the shape of them, black clothes and low voices—but I could not summon a single face in a coffin. Not one. Not in thirty-one years.

"We don't bury anyone," I whispered. "We've never buried anyone."

"People leave," Hobb said. "That's the story. People wander off, or go over the pass, or drown in the mere, and their families grieve, and the valley closes around the absence like water. It's clean. It's painless, mostly. And it happens once a year, whether we will it or not, because the contract requires it."

"Contract." The word tasted like copper. "What contract? With whom?"

Sera turned the ledger around to face me fully, and pointed—not at the entries, but at the bottom of the first page, where the whole arrangement began. There was a passage there in that same elegant hand, longer than the yearly lines, and I read it standing in the cold while the lamps guttered, and I will set down what it said as nearly as I can bear to, because some things should be written out plainly once by someone:

*Let it be agreed between the Court Beneath the Mountain and the people of the valley: that the valley shall be hidden from the sight of all powers, warded against all workings, and closed to all bargains, save this one. Within its bounds no spell shall follow, no curse shall land, no eye of craft shall find what hides there. In exchange, the people of the valley shall render, each year upon the anniversary, one soul of their number, freely yielded and without struggle. This covenant burns in dragon-fire and cannot be broken, amended, or survived.*

*Signed in flame, in the old tongue, by the undersigned.*

There was no signature. There was a burn mark instead—a scorch in the shape of a spiral, seared through a hundred pages beneath it, the same spiral that marked the oldest shelf.

"Dragon-fire," I said. "You expect me to believe—"

"I don't expect you to believe anything yet," Maren said. "Belief comes later, when it can't be helped. Right now I expect you to read the names."

"Why my names? Why mine specifically?"

"Because," Hobb said, and his voice cracked on it, an old man's voice failing him at the worst possible moment, "because we thought you should see the year you were born."

Sera turned the pages backward. Backward through years I'd lived, names I almost recognized—a cousin of Tam's, gone "over the pass" when I was nine; the miller's daughter, "drowned," when I was fourteen—backward through my childhood, my infancy, and stopped.

The year of my birth.

*Paid in full: one soul, freely rendered. The sanctuary holds.*

But beneath it, in different ink—in ink that looked newer, though the page was thirty-one years old—there was a second entry. A correction. An amendment, written in a hand I knew.

I knew it because I had spent my whole life trying to draw maps in a style that wasn't mine, and failing, and wondering why my hand always drifted toward those particular sweeping strokes, that particular way of lettering coastlines.

It was my mother's handwriting.

I gripped the table. The chamber tilted—or I did—and Hobb's hand came down heavy on my shoulder and held me to the world.

"Read it," Maren said gently. "You have to read it aloud. That's part of it. The knowing has to be spoken."

So I read it. My voice sounded like someone else's, someone standing far away in a corridor.

*"Amendment to the covenant, entered by the signatory's own hand: I, Ilse Sandoval, being of sound mind and full knowledge, do yield my soul in place of the year's due, and do further bind the court to take from my daughter Noor nothing, ever—not her soul, not her shadow, not her name. Let her be the one soul in this valley the fire cannot touch. Let her be invisible even to the invisible. This I buy for her with everything I am. Let the ledger show it. Let the fire honor it."*

The chamber was silent except for the lamps.

My mother had not wandered off. Had not gone over the pass, or drowned in the mere, or abandoned a fatherless child because her heart failed her. She had walked down these stairs—I could see it now, could see her exactly, her coat, her boots, the way she pushed her hair back before doing anything difficult—and she had written herself into this book in exchange for me, and the valley had watched her go and called it leaving, and I had grown up on their pity like it was milk.

"She bought you out," Sera said quietly. "The year you were born, the lot fell—it always falls somehow, the valley chooses, we've never understood how—and it fell on an infant. Which had happened before, maybe twice in all the ledgers, and it was always terrible. But your mother came here before the anniversary. Before the court could collect. She made a counter-offer. Herself, plus a rider: that you would be beyond the fire's reach entirely. Hidden even from the hidden. Do you understand what that means, Noor?"

I didn't trust myself to speak, so I shook my head.

"It means magic doesn't fail to find you because you live in a warded valley. Magic fails to find you because a dead woman's bargain forbids it. You aren't protected by the valley, child. You *are* the exception the valley was built around. You're the reason the wards have a shape at all." Hobb's grip was still on my shoulder, and I was grateful for it, because my knees had opinions of their own. "Your whole life, every map you ever drew of realms you couldn't enter—you weren't locked out. You were *paid out*. You're the one debt this place settled in full."

I thought of Ashen asleep in my spare bed. A child whose soul had been sold, whose body had walked out of the winter hills carrying nothing but a language older than kingdoms. A child the fire could touch, because no one had ever stood between her and the ledger and written her name out of it.

No one until, possibly, me.

"The elders swore an oath of silence," I said at last, and my voice had gone strange and flat and calm, the calm I felt sometimes when a map was finally coming right, when every wrong line resolved at once. "Generation after generation. You knew. All of you, always, you knew what this valley was, and you let us live in it, and you let us grieve, and you called it sanctuary."

"It *is* sanctuary," Anselm said from the stairs—he had come down without my noticing, and his voice had lost its pulpit edge; it was just an old man's voice now, frightened. "Noor, whatever else is true, that much is true. Outside those stones there are powers that wear people like coats. Whatever the cost, the valley has kept every child born in it safe from—"

"At a price of one child a year."

"Yes." He didn't flinch from it, I'll give him that. "Yes. And we have hated ourselves for it every year, and paid anyway, because the alternative was everyone."

"And now the boundary stones are singing," I said. "Now a mute child speaks the language the contract was written in. Now the ledgers are—what? Changing? Tell me what's happening to the fire, Maren. Tell me why you finally opened this door after keeping it shut for three hundred years."

Maren looked at Hobb. Hobb looked at the floor. It was Sera who answered, in a whisper, as if the dark stone might overhear and carry it up the mountain.

"Because last month, for the first time anyone remembers, the collection was refused. The court came to the boundary, as it does every year—we don't see it, but the stones know, the stones always know—and it did not take the year's soul. It turned away. And the ledger downstairs in the church crypt, the copy we keep for the oath, wrote one word in fresh ink, all by itself, in the middle of the night."

She turned the great ledger on the table to its final written page, and there it was, in that elegant hand, in ink still wet enough to shine:

*Arrears.*

"And the word underneath it," Sera said, "the word that appeared the night the child arrived, the word we think means the fire is failing—"

I read it myself. I needed to see it in my own reading, the way I needed to trace a coastline myself before I trusted it.

*Recall.*

Every debt, I thought. Every bargain ever struck in dragon-fire. If the fire that made them binding gutters out—if the court calls in what it is owed before the flame goes dark—

Then Ashen was not the only soul in arrears. Somewhere in these shelves, in a ledger thirty-one years old, my mother's purchase was written down too. And if the contracts burned true only while the fire endured, then everything she had paid—everything she *was*—was balanced on a flame that someone, somewhere beneath the mountain, was letting gutter out.

I closed the ledger with a sound like a door shutting.

"Noor," Maren began. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking," I said, "that my mother bought me with the only coin she had, and everyone in this valley let me believe she simply left. And I'm thinking that there is a child sleeping in my house whose soul is owed to the court beneath the mountain, and the instrument collecting that debt is dying, and dying creditors get desperate." I picked up the lamp. "And I'm thinking that I have spent my whole life drawing maps of places I was forbidden to go, and I know every road over that mountain better than the people who walk them, because I've walked them ten thousand times in ink."

Hobb's hand fell from my shoulder.

"You can't cross the boundary," Anselm said. "The moment you do—"

"The moment I do, I'm visible," I finished. "I know. I've always known, haven't I? Some part of me has always known." I started for the stairs, and the elders parted to let me pass, four keepers of a three-hundred-year secret watching a mapmaker walk out of the dark with murder and purpose in equal measure. "Get me everything you have on the court. Every copy, every fragment, every rumor the ledgers record. I want to know the roads beneath that mountain before the frost thaws."

"And if the fire dies while you're down there?" Maren called after me. "If every contract unravels—this valley's sanctuary with them? Noor—what happens to us?"

I stopped on the third stair, in the smell of ink and old stone, and I found that the question didn't frighten me at all, and that frightened me more than anything else that morning.

"Then we'll finally see what this valley looks like," I said, "when it has nothing left to hide behind."

Above us, faint through three hundred years of dark stone, the boundary stones began to sing.
