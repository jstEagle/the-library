## 5. Leaving Is a Kind of Dying

I packed the way I mapped: in layers, oldest first.

The bottom of the satchel took the things I would not need but could not leave—my mother's thimble, worn thin as a coin; the stub of blue chalk she'd used to mark the first map she ever let me finish. Above those went the tools of my trade: pens wrapped in oilcloth, three pots of ink sealed with wax, the good knife for scraping vellum, the bad knife for everything else. Then the maps themselves, rolled tight and sleeved in leather—the valley, drawn eleven times over eleven years, each version more detailed and more useless than the last. A map of the only place I'd ever been. It was like packing a portrait of my own face to remember what I looked like.

Ashen sat on my workbench among the ink pots, legs swinging, watching me with that terrible patience children have when they understand more than they can say. Her words were coming back slowly, one or two a day, like birds returning to a burned forest. *Cold*, she'd said that morning, pointing at the frost on the window. *Go*, she'd said last night, pointing at me, and then, after a long silence, pointing at herself: *go too*. As if there had ever been a question.

"You'll want the heavy cloak," I told her, because talking kept the fear from pooling. "The boundary is cold. Everything past it is cold, probably. The mountains don't care about anyone's feelings."

She slid off the bench and fetched the cloak from its peg without being asked twice. She was six years old and had already learned to obey quickly, quietly, without expectation of kindness. Someone had taught her that before the court beneath the mountain taught her worse. I made a note of it—not on paper, but in the place where I kept all the things I intended to fix about the world if I survived long enough.

Outside, the village was doing what villages do with departures: pretending none was happening.

* * *

They came to see me off anyway, of course. That is the particular cruelty of small places—they will not help you leave, but they will insist on watching you go.

The elders stood in a row by the boundary stones like judges who had already delivered the verdict and come only for the sentencing. Elder Maren spoke for them, as she always did, her voice pitched to carry across the square and into every kitchen.

"The sanctuary holds while you are inside it," she said. "Once you cross, it does not reach for you. Whatever finds you out there, girl, you will have found alone."

"I've always been alone," I said. "This will just be the first time it's official."

Someone laughed—a single bark from the crowd, quickly swallowed. Maren's mouth thinned. She was not a cruel woman; she was a frightened one, and fright had calcified in her over decades until it passed for wisdom. She had signed the ledger entries. I knew that now. I had seen her name in the manuscript fragment, neat and dutiful beside the record of my mother's soul, and she had known that I knew, and neither of us had said a word about it in four days. Some debts are too heavy to lift in public.

"Your mother chose this," Maren said instead, which was her way of apologizing, and also her way of not.

"My mother chose *for* me," I said. "There's a difference. I'm going to find out how big."

I turned from them before my voice could crack, because a cartographer knows the value of clean lines, and walked to the place where Tam was waiting.

He had chosen the old well at the edge of the square, which was where we had always met as children, back when the world was the size of a game and we were its only players. He was leaning against the stones with his arms crossed, wearing the expression he wore whenever he was about to say something true: jaw set, eyes elsewhere, as though truth were something you had to sneak up on.

"So," he said.

"So."

"You're really going." Not a question. He'd known since the night Ashen arrived, probably before I had. Tam had always read me faster than I read myself.

"Really going."

"To the mountain. To the court that eats souls. With a child and a bag of maps." He shook his head slowly. "You know what they'll call you if you come back? Nothing. They won't call you anything, because no one will believe it's you. And if you don't come back—" His voice caught on something. He cleared his throat hard. "If you don't come back, I'll have to be the one who tells people what you died for. And I'm bad at stories. You know I'm bad at stories."

"You're terrible at stories," I agreed. "You once tried to tell the tale of the flood year and got the river wrong."

"That river was very complicated."

"It went downhill, Tam. In one direction."

We looked at each other and both of us almost smiled, and then neither of us did, because smiling would have opened the door to everything behind it.

He reached into his coat and brought out the compass.

I knew it at once—or thought I did. Old brass, dented lid, the kind of thing found in a dead man's pocket and never reported. But when he opened it, the needle didn't swing toward north. It swung toward *me*. It swung, and shivered, and settled, quivering like a living thing, aimed at my chest.

"What is it?" I asked.

"A compass," he said. "More or less. My grandfather pulled it off a drowned pilgrim on the north road and spent thirty years trying to make it point home. It never has. It points at debts." He turned it so I could see the needle trembling between us. "Right now it points at you. Because you owe the dead a rescue, and the dead, apparently, keep accounts."

"And when I'm gone?"

"Then it points the way I should follow." He said it lightly, the way men say enormous things when they cannot bear their own weight. "It pointed at your mother once, you know. After she crossed. For a whole winter it sat on my shelf pointing east at nothing, and I thought it was broken. Then I understood what it was pointing at wasn't a direction. It was an unfinished thing."

I took the compass. The brass was warm from his pocket, and the needle steadied against my palm as if it had finally found its bearing. A debt-compass. Of course Tam would give me this. He couldn't give me protection—he was a wheelwright, and wheelwrights cannot arm anyone against courts beneath mountains—but he could give me a way to know, always, exactly how much I still owed and to whom. It was the most honest gift anyone had ever given me. It was also, I suspected, a leash. If the needle pointed at him someday, pointing the way I should follow—

"Don't," I said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to follow me. I can see it being planned behind your face."

"Nothing is being planned behind my face. My face is famously transparent. There's nothing behind it." He closed my fingers around the compass with both of his hands, rough and warm and certain. "Take it. Point it at whatever needs paying. That's all it's for. That's all any of us are for, maybe, if we're lucky."

For a moment I let myself lean into him, forehead against his shoulder, the way I had when we were eight and the well rope had snapped and nearly taken me down with the bucket. He smelled of oak shavings and woodsmoke, the smell of every ordinary day I had ever loved.

"If I don't come back," I said into his coat, "finish the story properly. Get the river right."

"Downhill," he said. "One direction. I promise."

* * *

The last hours of a life are not dramatic. That is the thing no one warns you. You do not walk through fire; you feed a neighbor's chickens.

I fed Marta's chickens, because Marta was lame and Ashen liked the ridiculous pompous rooster. I banked the fire in my workshop and swept the floor, because a workshop left dirty is a workshop left abandoned, and I refused to abandon anything. I watered the plant on my windowsill that I had never learned the name of, and then, feeling foolish, moved it to the neighbor's sill where someone less negligent might tend it. I wrote two letters and burned them both, because everything I had to say to this village fit better into silence.

At dusk I stood in my doorway and looked at the valley the way I had drawn it a hundred times: the crooked lane, the smoke standing straight up from the chimneys in the windless evening, the terraced fields going gold and gray, the mountains closing around it all like hands folded in prayer. My whole world. Eleven maps' worth of world. I had spent my life pressing its edges onto paper, and only now, about to step outside the paper, did I understand what the edges had been holding in.

Me. They had been holding in me. All this time I'd thought the valley kept magic out. It had also, gently, all my life, been keeping Noor Sandoval in—and some traitorous part of me had called that safety.

Ashen tugged my sleeve. She pointed at the satchel, then at the sky, where the first stars were pricking through. Then she said a word in the old language, soft and round, and touched her own chest, and mine.

I didn't need a translation. Some words arrive already translated.

* * *

The boundary stones stand at the eastern pass, where the valley narrows to a throat of granite and the road drops away beyond sight. The villagers call them the Quiet Stones, because sound goes strange near them—your footsteps arrive late, your voice comes back thinner, as if the air itself were reluctant to repeat anything said so close to the edge.

Maren and the elders had gone. The square had emptied. Only Tam walked with us, carrying Ashen on his shoulders, telling her—I could hear the fragments—some mangled story about a rooster and a flood, getting the river wrong even now, on purpose, making her laugh in that rusty, astonished way she had, as if laughter were a language she was relearning word by word just as I was relearning hers.

At the stones, he set her down.

The needle of the compass in my hand had begun to move. Slowly, grudgingly, it dragged itself off my chest and swung east, toward the pass, toward the darkening road, toward the mountain whose roots held a court and a chained fire and a debt older than my grief. It trembled there, eager and afraid, the way I felt.

"This is it," Tam said. "The part where you go."

"I know."

"You could still—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "No. You couldn't. I watched you decide four days ago. I just wanted to be wrong about it."

"You're never wrong about me. It's infuriating."

"One of us should be." He crouched to Ashen's level. Whatever he said to her, he said it too quietly for me to hear, and she put both her small hands on either side of his face and pressed her forehead to his, solemn as a priest, and I understood that he had just become a story she would keep forever, and that he knew it, and that neither of them needed me for this part.

Then he stood, and it was time.

Crossing the boundary was supposed to feel like something. A wall, a veil, a cold shock, a bell tolling somewhere in the bones. The manuscripts all agreed that passing out of a sanctified ground was an event, marked and felt.

It felt like nothing. One step I was in the valley; the next step I was not, and the only difference was that the air stopped arriving late. My footsteps sounded when I made them. My breath hung white in front of me and drifted away without argument. The world simply—continued, past the line where it had always ended, indifferent as weather.

That was the worst of it. Not a wall. Just the discovery that the wall had never been real, only agreed upon, and that agreements, unlike walls, can end.

I turned back once. Tam stood between the stones with his hands in his pockets, getting smaller, and the valley rose behind him in the last light, gold and gray and utterly familiar, and I looked at it the way you look at a limb before the surgeon's saw comes down—knowing it is yours, knowing it must go, knowing that what grows in its place will never quite be you again.

Leaving is a kind of dying. I had written that once, years ago, in the margin of a map, as a joke about travelers. I had not known I was taking notes.

Ashen slipped her hand into mine. Small, cold, certain.

"Go," she said, in my language, clearly, deliberately—her longest sentence yet, one whole word offered up like a coin. And then, in the old tongue, softer, the word she'd said at the window: the one that meant *together*, or meant *beginning*, or meant both.

The compass needle strained east. Somewhere below the horizon, under miles of stone, a fire I had never seen was guttering toward its end, and every bargain it fueled was beginning, imperceptibly, to loosen—including one written before I was born, in a hand I would know anywhere, that had bought my life with hers.

I stepped forward. The road took my weight. The valley let me go.

Behind us, faint and late, as if the air had relented at the last moment, I heard Tam call my name—one syllable, thrown across the stones like a stone itself—and then the pass curved, and the mountain rose black against the stars, and the compass in my hand pointed down into the dark like a finger saying: *this way. This is what you owe.*
