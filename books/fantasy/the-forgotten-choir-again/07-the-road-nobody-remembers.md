## 7. The Road Nobody Remembers

The map lied to us on the second morning.

Not by much—maps rarely do—but enough that I noticed, and noticing is my whole trade. The river we'd been following bent a degree too far east where it met the ridge line, as if some earlier hand had nudged it out of honesty. I stood in the shallows with my boots filling with cold water and stared at the inked curve in my memory until the wrongness of it made my teeth ache.

"Noor." Ashen tugged my sleeve. She had words now—three of them, maybe four, rationed like a miser's coins. *Noor. Water. Go.* She pointed upstream.

"I see it," I said, though I didn't yet. Not fully.

But here is what no one tells you about being born to draw maps: you don't learn geography. Geography learns *you*. Somewhere behind my eyes there was a room where every contour I'd ever inked lived on, and when I looked at real land, the room rearranged itself around it, comparing, correcting, whispering. And the whisper now said: *this bend is wrong because this land was moved. Someone rerouted a river.*

Rivers are not rerouted by weather. Rivers are rerouted by people who want something hidden—or something kept away.

I waded back to the bank and unrolled Tam's compass beside my satchel. The needle didn't point north; it never had. It pointed toward debts, he'd said, pressing it into my palm at the valley's edge with hands that shook only after he'd let go of me. Now the needle strained south-southeast, quivering like a trapped moth, toward the mountain we were trying very hard not to approach directly.

"Debt compass," I murmured. "What am I owing, and to whom?"

The needle answered by trembling harder.

Ashen crouched beside me and touched the glass with one finger. Then she said her fourth word.

"*Under.*"

* * *

We walked the river's true course instead of its mapped one, and by late afternoon the land confessed everything. There had been a road here once—I began to find its bones. A paving stone furred with moss, laid in the herringbone pattern the old pilgrim orders used, the kind I'd seen only twice: once in a manuscript fragment in the priest's forbidden cabinet, and once in a dream I'd never told anyone about. Then another stone. Then a stretch where the ground held its shape too perfectly beneath the grass, straight as a ruled line, running not toward the mountain but *around* it—a great patient arc, skirting the whole eastern flank.

I sat down on a fallen oak right there in the middle of nowhere and laughed until Ashen looked frightened.

"A pilgrim road," I told her. "A road nobody remembers."

Because that was the thing—the thing that made my hands itch for a pen even more than usual. Every map of the surface realm I had ever copied, bought, or stolen showed the same roads: the King's Way, the Salt Road, the toll-roads that funneled all travelers toward the under-court's gates like blood toward a wound. The court taxed passage. The court *wanted* the roads, wanted every footfall counted and priced. So how did thousands of pilgrims, centuries ago, walk to their shrines without paying?

They didn't. They walked *this*. And then the court had done the most elegant thing imaginable: it hadn't destroyed the old road. It had erased the memory of it—from maps, from records, from minds. It had bent a river to flood a valley crossing. It had let moss do the rest.

But erasure has a flaw, and the flaw is me. You cannot erase from someone who was never given the false version. My gift wasn't knowledge; it was *blankness*—land speaking to me directly, unmediated by anyone's lies. The court could burn every honest atlas in the world. It couldn't burn the way the world spoke to Noor Sandoval.

"Come on," I said, standing, knees cracking. "We're going to walk a dead road back to life."

Ashen smiled. It was the first smile she'd given me that reached her eyes, and I thought: *this is worth whatever waits at the end of it.* I would think that again, later, less easily. But that evening I believed it completely.

* * *

The road climbed through birch country as the light went long and gold. We passed a shrine—just a hollowed stone with a rain-filled basin—and Ashen stopped and cupped water from it and said a word I didn't know, soft, like setting down something heavy. The water shivered. I decided not to ask.

Night came down cold and clear. We found shelter where the road cut through an outcrop: a shallow cave whose walls, when I scraped away the soot of centuries, bore marks. Survey marks. Elevation lines scratched by someone working by lamplight, precise as prayer.

Someone had mapped from inside this cave.

And someone, I realized as my eyes adjusted further, was still here.

She sat at the far end where the firelight wouldn't reach, wrapped in a coat that might once have been fine. Her hair was iron-grey and cropped short for practicality. Her sleeves were pinned at the wrists—pinned, folded, empty.

"Don't light anything larger than that candle," she said. Her voice was dry and level, a voice used to being obeyed by silence. "Light shows through the crack in the rock. The toll-wardens still walk the old shrines on frost nights."

I put my hand over our candle. "Who are you?"

"A better question is who taught you to read survey marks off a cave wall in the dark." She leaned forward into the light. Her face was older than her voice, seamed like dried clay, and her eyes were the pale grey of a map's empty margins. They went from me to Ashen and stopped there, and something moved through them—grief, recognition, alarm, I couldn't sort the order. "Where did you get that child?"

"She came to us," I said carefully. "To my village. Speaking the old tongue."

"The pre-kingdom courts' tongue." The woman exhaled slowly. "Yes. They always come back to that language eventually. It's the sound the contracts were written in." She studied me a moment longer. "You're a cartographer."

"How could you possibly—"

"Because you looked at my elevation lines before you looked at my face. Only one kind of person does that." She almost smiled. "I'm Vess."

The name landed in me like a stone dropped down a well. I waited for the splash and heard nothing, which meant the name had fallen farther than any well I knew. "Vess," I repeated. "The Vess? The one who mapped the under-court?"

Her expression didn't change, but the firelight seemed to flinch. "So the story survived somewhere. Interesting. Usually they scrub a name along with a road." She shifted, and I saw then what I'd refused to see: her sleeves weren't merely empty. Beneath the pins, the stumps ended just below the elbow, healed long ago, old and settled. "You've heard what happened to me, then."

"They said you lost your hands," I said. "No one ever said how."

"No one ever asks how, either. That's the part they erase first." She looked at Ashen again, and her voice gentled by a degree I suspect few people ever heard. "Come nearer, little one. Both of you. If the wardens are walking tonight, we may as well be warm while we hide."

* * *

She built up the fire small, as promised, feeding it twigs with a deftness that made my chest hurt—she'd learned to do everything with those shortened arms, striking flint, lacing boots, and I understood suddenly that precision like hers doesn't die with the hands. It migrates.

"You found the pilgrim road," she said. "The Hushway, we called it. Because you walked it quietly, and because the court wanted it hushed." She nodded at my satchel, where the corner of a rolled chart protruded. "Show me your work."

I hesitated—my maps were the most private things I owned, more private than my face—and then I did something I hadn't done since leaving the valley. I handed them over.

Vess read them the way I imagined scholars read scripture: slowly, hungrily, her pale eyes tracking each line. "Inked freehand. No base copy." She glanced up. "You draft from direct observation. From the land itself."

"It's just how I've always—"

"Do you know what you are?" she interrupted. "Do you have any idea how rare—no. Of course you don't. The valley keeps its children ignorant; that's what sanctuary means." She set the charts down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something explosive. "Every cartographer alive works from inheritance. Copies of copies of copies, each generation's errors compounding like interest. The court *loves* that. Corrupt the master, corrupt every descendant forever. But you—" she tapped my chart with her forearm, awkwardly, tenderly, "—you can't be corrupted, because you were never given the lie. Do you understand why they'll want you? Why they already want you?"

The compass in my pocket trembled against my hip, and I understood.

"They want me to replace you," I said.

"I'd be insulted if it weren't obvious." Vess fed the fire another twig. "I was the last cartographer who drew the under-court from life. Twenty years ago. I mapped its halls, its gates, its tolls—for them, willingly, because they offered me the one thing I thought I couldn't refuse." Her jaw tightened. "My sister. They held her soul in escrow against my service. Map well, and she goes free. I mapped beautifully. When I finished the final plate—the full architecture of the soul-palace—they burned it in front of me and took my hands, so that the only copy of my knowledge would live in my head alone. Head-knowledge can't be stolen. It can only be bargained for." She paused. "They kept my sister anyway. Souls make such good mortar."

Ashen made a small sound and pressed herself against my side. I put my arm around her.

"Why tell me this?" I asked. "You don't know me."

"Because you're going to the mountain." It wasn't a question. "The debt-compass in your pocket has been straining toward it since noon; I could hear it buzzing from across the cave. And you're traveling with a child whose soul the court holds, which means either you're delivering her or rescuing her, and your face says rescuing." She smiled without warmth. "I was your face once."

"And you're warning me to turn back."

"I'm warning you that turning back isn't possible anymore, so the warning is useless, which is why I'm giving it. It's traditional." For a moment the dryness cracked and something rawer showed underneath. "Listen to me, mapmaker. Whatever they offer you—and they will offer, they always offer—it will be shaped exactly like the thing you want most. That's not magic. That's just skill. They've had ten thousand years of practice at knowing what people ache for." She looked at her own arms. "I ached for my sister. What do you ache for?"

I opened my mouth and found, to my horror, that the answer rose instantly and completely: *to matter. To be seen. To stop being the strange woman of the valley, beloved and pitied, useful and invisible.* The offer would wear that face. I knew it the way I knew a falsified river bend—by the ache of recognition.

"That's what I thought," Vess said softly, watching me. "Kinship among the marked, then. You and I and the child—we're all legible to them now. All written on." She leaned back against the cave wall. "Sleep. Tomorrow I'll teach you the Hushway's branches—the paths that bypass every tollgate between here and the mountain's root. Consider it payment."

"For what?"

"For the pleasure," she said, "of watching someone read a cave wall properly for the first time in twenty years."

* * *

I slept badly and woke before dawn to find Vess already awake, sitting with her forearms resting on her knees, watching Ashen sleep.

"She sings in her dreams," Vess said quietly, without turning. "Did you know that?"

"I didn't."

"In the old tongue. Contract-language. Which means the sale on her isn't complete—not yet, not cleanly. A finished sale goes silent." Vess turned, and in the guttering candlelight her face was terrible and kind at once. "There's time. Barely. But there's time—if you take the branch road tonight and don't stop for anything, even me, even her crying, even your own name if it's called in a familiar voice. The tollgates have ways of asking."

"What's on the branch road?" I asked.

Vess was quiet a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the birches with a sound like pages turning.

"My testimony," she said finally. "Everything I saw down there. Everything they build their beautiful palace out of. You'll need to know it before you're standing inside it—because the palace is designed so that knowing comes too late." She held out her arms, those healed stumps, in the firelight. "Sit down, mapmaker. Draw nothing. Just listen."

And so, as the frost thickened on the stones and Ashen slept on singing her broken old words, Vess began to tell me what the court does with the souls it keeps—and I understood, even then, that I was hearing the price of my own road.
