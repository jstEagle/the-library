## 8. Vess's Testimony

The night I sold my sister, it was raining in the way the under-court makes things rain—upward, first, then down again as if reconsidering.

I remember that because I was a cartographer even then, and I noticed everything except what mattered. I noticed how the water rose off the black canal in threads, hung like spun glass above the tollgates, and fell back changed: colder, sweeter, wrong. I noticed the way the court's lanterns burned without flame, fed on something I would not name for another twenty years. I noticed the Ledger-Keeper's hands, gray and patient as old vellum, turning the pages of my life with two fingers as though checking whether I was dry enough to write upon.

What I did not notice—not until it was too late to matter—was that my sister Sefa had followed me down.

* * *

You have to understand what the court offers before you can understand what I took. It doesn't offer gold. Gold is for surface kings who need something shiny to stand between themselves and their consciences. The court beneath the mountain offers *passage*. That is all. Passage out of whatever you cannot survive.

For me it was the plague year. You'll have heard of it, or your grandmother will have—the gray fever that came up the river valleys and emptied whole towns into their own doorways. My village lost forty souls in six weeks. Sefa took it in the seventh. She was fourteen. She lay in our mother's bed with her lips going the color of slate, and every remedy we knew went into her and came back out again doing nothing, and I sat by that bed doing arithmetic.

Because I had been below once already. As an apprentice, mapping the pilgrim roads, I had gone where apprentices are sent because they're cheap and expendable, and I had seen the tollgates, and I had seen the ledger open on its lectern like a great sleeping moth, and I had heard the terms read aloud in the old tongue by a clerk whose voice sounded like paper being folded. *One soul, freely given, buys one passage.* I had written it all down in my field book, neat as scripture, because that was my job: to record. Nobody had ever told me recording was not the same as understanding.

So there I sat, doing sums beside my dying sister, and the sum always came out the same. One of us could walk away from this valley alive. The fever would take the other regardless. And the court did not care which one signed.

That last part is the mercy they advertise. *The court does not care which one signs.* They say it gently, the clerks, the way a knife says nothing at all. Because here is what I learned later, what no one tells you: the court does not care which one signs, but the contract does. The ink knows. Dragon-fire knows. A soul given *for* someone burns differently than a soul given *instead of* someone, and the architecture of the palace can taste the difference.

I told myself I was giving mine for her. I walked down through the rain that fell upward, and I stood before the lectern, and I said the words: *Take me instead.*

And the Ledger-Keeper looked at me with those patient gray eyes and said, "Freely given?"

"Freely given."

"For whom?"

And I—I want you to understand that I hesitated. One breath. Maybe less. But the fire under the mountain felt it. The ink felt it. Somewhere in the deep of that palace, a wall grew half a finger's width thicker, and something inside it screamed very quietly, and I did not hear it because I was busy hating myself for the hesitation, which felt at the time like the worst thing I had ever done.

"For Sefa," I said. "My sister. Let her live."

"Then sign," said the Ledger-Keeper, "and she lives."

I signed. And the pen drank from me—not blood, nothing so honest—and I felt the passage settle over me like a coat cut exactly to my shoulders, warm and fitted and final. I walked back up through the reversed rain. Sefa's fever broke that night. By morning she was asking for bread.

By morning I was also gone. That was part of the price; I understood that much. Passage means passage. I could never go home. Whatever I bought, I bought it for people who would never know I'd bought it, and who would grow old telling each other that Vess ran off with a tinker, or drowned in the canal, or was taken by the court—which was closest, and so of course nobody believed it.

* * *

Here is what the court does with the souls it keeps. I will tell it plainly, because Noor Sandoval will not hear it plainly unless someone says it plainly, and because I have spent thirty years learning that plainness is the only kindness left to me.

It builds with them.

Not metaphorically. Not *the court is built on suffering*, in the way priests say such things to make children behave. I mean masonry. I mean load-bearing walls. Every soul the court takes is rendered down—not destroyed, never destroyed, destruction would be waste—and drawn out into long bright strands, and those strands are fused and folded and set, the way a smith sets iron, into the fabric of the palace itself. The pillars are made of patience. The doors are made of small hopes. The great stair that spirals down past the dragon's chamber—that stair is made entirely of mothers. I know because I mapped it. I mapped every corridor, every vault, every screaming archway, and I did it well, because I am good at my work, and because the alternative to working was thinking.

They call it the Beautiful Palace, and it is beautiful. That is the obscenity of it. Walk its halls and you will feel—this is the trap, this is what catches the ones who come looking for their dead—you will feel *held*. The walls lean toward you like interested friends. The light comes from nowhere and lands on you like approval. Every soul in that architecture still has something of its shape left, some residue of wanting, and what souls want, even rendered into stone, is company. So the palace loves its visitors. It holds them. It hums.

And if you stay too long—if you let yourself be loved long enough—it asks, in that humming wordless way, whether you wouldn't rather stay. Whether comfort isn't a kind of truth. Whether the cold world above, with its fevers and its farewells, deserves you more than these warm walls do.

The cells of the court are not cells. They are invitations. And the terrible thing, the thing I will say only once and only here, is that most people accept.

* * *

I worked for the court eleven years. Cartographer-in-residence, they called it. I drew the under-realm the way no one had ever drawn it: true. Every gallery, every gate, every vein of the mountain where the dragon-fire ran like sap through the walls, feeding the contracts, making every bargain burn binding and true. I was proud of those maps. Understand that. I was *proud*. I thought accuracy was innocence. I thought that if I only recorded faithfully enough, I was somehow outside the thing I recorded, a lens and not a hand.

Then one day I was sent to survey the west gallery—they were extending it, the court is always extending, hunger is the only growth industry down there—and I found, fused into the new stonework, a strand of soul that still had a name.

Sefa.

She wasn't supposed to be there. She was fourteen years old and alive and eating bread in a village I could never see again. But the court keeps accounts the way rivers keep water, and somewhere in the eleven years since my signing, the account had come due. Perhaps she sold herself trying to follow me—I learned later that she tried, twice, and was turned away at the gates both times, and tried a third time through a pilgrim road that no longer exists because I unmade it from the maps. Perhaps the fever came back for her. Perhaps the court simply reached up through some crack in the world and took what it considered owed, because my hesitation at the lectern had left a flaw in the contract, a hairline crack in the fire, and flaws get repaired, and repairs cost.

I don't know. I have never known. That is the shape of my damnation: not knowledge, but the permanent absence of it. I stood in that wet unfinished gallery with my instruments in my ruined hands and I pressed my palm against the stone where her name still flickered, faint as heat-shimmer, and the stone leaned into me like an interested friend, and it hummed, and I swear to whatever is left of me that I heard her laugh.

She had laughed like that the day we found the frozen bees. She was nine. She cupped one in her hands and breathed on it until it woke, and she laughed, astonished, like resurrection was the simplest trick in the world.

The stone laughed like that.

And I sat down in the mud of that unfinished gallery, and I did not weep, because the court permits no weeping—it runs off the walls, upward, like rain reconsidering—but something in me broke along a line I have never found again.

* * *

What I did after is the part Noor needs to hear, and the part she will refuse to hear, because she is young in the ways that matter and old in all the ways that don't.

I finished the survey. Do you understand? I sat in the mud with my sister laughing in the stones around me, and when I had finished breaking, I got up, and I measured the gallery, and I recorded it, and I delivered the maps to the Ledger-Keeper with a steady hand, because that was my work, and work was the only wall I had left between myself and the humming.

Eleven years I told myself I was a prisoner. Eleven years I told myself I had no choice, that my hands were bound, that the maps I made were only mirrors. Then the eleventh winter came, and the court asked me to do a small thing—a tiny thing, a correction, a single revision—to remove from the master atlas three pilgrim roads that led too near the dragon's chamber, roads that a certain kind of desperate traveler might someday use. And I picked up my pen.

And I could not.

Not because of courage. Don't mistake it for courage. I could not because my hand remembered my sister's hands around a frozen bee, and the pen weighed as much as a mountain, and I set it down, and the room went very quiet, and the Ledger-Keeper looked at me across the lectern with those patient gray eyes and said:

"You hesitate."

One breath. Maybe less. But the fire under the mountain felt it.

They took my hands that same night. Not in anger—the court is never angry; anger is for creatures with futures. They took them the way a scribe blots a page that has smudged. The clerks were gentle. There was no pain, or there was pain of a kind that doesn't leave marks, and afterward I had two smooth wrists ending in smooth skin, and the court gave me a pension of sorts: a small room, warm, in the outer galleries, where the walls did not know my name and therefore did not love me, and I lived there for nineteen years drawing maps with a stylus strapped to my forearm, because a cartographer without hands is still a cartographer, and spite is a renewable resource.

But here is the confession underneath the confession, the one I will carry past my death: in nineteen years, I never once went back to the west gallery. I never pressed my palm to the stone where she laughed. I told myself it was because I couldn't bear it. The truth is I couldn't bear *not to*, and so I stayed away, and staying away was the last cowardice of a life built on them, and I have made my peace with none of it, and I do not intend to.

Self-forgiveness is just the court's humming, translated. I recognized the tune the first time I caught myself thinking *you did what anyone would do.* Anyone. The favorite phrase of every willing soul in those warm inviting walls.

* * *

Now the girl has come. The mute one, Ashen, with her sold soul and her old-language silence, and the mapmaker woman who draws realms she's never seen, and the friend with the compass that points at debts. They sit at my table drinking tea I can barely taste anymore, and Noor looks at my wrists, and she looks away, polite, and she asks her careful questions about roads and tollgates, and I answer them, and all the while I am watching her the way you watch someone walking on ice that you know, personally, intimately, from having fallen through.

She has the same look I had. That's what frightens me. That arithmetic behind the eyes. She's already done the sums—her life for the child's, her invisibility for the girl's freedom—and the sum has come out the way such sums always come out, clean and fatal, and she thinks that because the answer is correct, paying it will be simple.

So I take her hands—my smooth wrists against her ink-stained fingers, the closest thing to holding I have left—and I tell her everything. The rain that falls upward. The lectern. The hesitation that cost more than the signing. The gallery where the stones laugh. I tell her what the palace does with the kept, and I watch her face do what faces do: horror first, then pity, then—there it is, third movement, always third movement—*but I would be different. But I'm only going in to take, not to give. But my case is simpler.*

"Noor," I say, and my voice comes out rougher than I mean it to, "when the court offers you a bargain down there—and it will offer, it always offers, that's the whole architecture of the place—remember that the offer is not a door. It's a wall, leaning toward you, humming. And the humming sounds like sense. It sounds like *anyone would do it.* That's how it gets the willing ones. Not with chains. With comfort."

She nods. She's good at nodding. Her eyes have already gone past me to the maps spread across my table, to the red thread I've laid along the forgotten pilgrim road, and I can see her mind folding my testimony into her plan the way I once folded the court into my field book—recording, recording, mistaking it for understanding.

"The dragon-fire is guttering," she says. "If it goes out, every contract unravels. Including yours. Including Sefa's."

"And including the valley's sanctuary," I say. "Including every bargain ever struck in fire. You'd be unmaking the world's oldest law to save one child. Do you know what stands in the deep of that palace, keeping the fire burning?"

"I've read the fragments."

"Fragments." I almost laugh. "Child, I *mapped* it. Would you like to know what I saw at the bottom of the mountain, at the end of the mother-stair, in the heart-chamber where the contracts are forged?"

Her chin lifts. Stubborn. Good. She'll need stubborn.

"A chain," I say, "longer than any road you've ever drawn. And at the end of it—" and here my voice finally fails me, thirty years late, in front of a stranger, over cold tea, "—at the end of it, something enormous, breathing slow, that looked at me with one eye the color of banked coals, and *knew my name*, and said nothing. It has never stopped saying nothing. And Noor—listen to me, because this is the only warning I have left to give—"

Outside, far above us, thunder rolls across the surface world, and the tea in my cup trembles in rings, and Tam's compass, lying on my table between the maps, swings hard away from north and points, quivering, down.

"—whatever you hear down there," I finish, "whoever speaks to you, however fair the terms—do not sign. Do not hesitate either. Just turn around. Promise me you'll turn around."

Noor Sandoval looks at the compass trembling on my table, pointing down into the dark like a debt called due, and she reaches out and covers it with her hand, quieting it, the way you'd quiet a crying child.

"I can't promise that," she says.
