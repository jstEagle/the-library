## 9. The Tollgate of Small Mercies

The road ended at a door standing in the middle of nowhere.

That is not poetry. It was, quite literally, a door—oak, iron-banded, tall as two men—and it stood alone on the pilgrim road with no wall around it, no house behind it, only the gray moor stretching to the horizon in every direction like a page someone had forgotten to write on. Mist pooled at its threshold though there was no river for miles. The hinges were polished bright from use.

"Is it locked?" Ashen whispered. She had been speaking more each day since we'd crossed the boundary—small words first, *cold*, *walk*, *Noor*—and now whole sentences arrived haltingly, as if she were carrying them up from some deep cellar one at a time.

"Doors that stand alone are never locked," I said. "They're *staffed*."

I was right, which was the worst kind of right, the kind you get no satisfaction from.

The door opened before we reached it, and a man stepped through as if stepping out of an ordinary parlor. He was unremarkable in every particular except his hands, which were too many-jointed, fingers folding back on themselves like reeds in wind. His clothes were the color of wet slate. His smile was warm and perfectly calibrated, and I recognized it immediately because I had spent my life drawing borders: this was the smile of a thing whose entire purpose was to be crossed.

"Travelers," he said, "on the old road. How rare. How *lovely*." He bowed. "I am Ferren, keeper of this gate. You'll forgive the informality of my office—the court has grown lean in recent years."

Behind me, Tam shifted his weight. I heard the compass in his pocket rattle against its brass case, a small anxious sound, like a trapped moth.

"We're passing through," I said. "We want nothing from the court."

"No one ever does." Ferren's too-many fingers laced themselves together pleasantly. "And yet things are always wanted *of* you. That is what a gate is, mapmaker."

My blood went cold and then hot. "I haven't told you my trade."

"You didn't have to." He gestured, and the mist at our feet stirred, arranging itself into lines I knew intimately: contour marks, elevation hatches, the little stipples I used for scree. A map of our own route, drawn in fog, accurate down to the place where Vess had shown us the cut through the hills three days ago. "The road remembers who walks it. And roads talk to gates, and gates talk to me. You are Noor Sandoval, of the valley that refuses. You travel with a child whose name is not Ashen but something older, and a friend who carries a compass that points at debts." He said all of this gently, the way one reads a menu aloud. "You are going beneath the mountain. Everyone who takes the forgotten road is going beneath the mountain. It is rather the point of forgetting it."

Tam's hand found my shoulder. His grip said: *we can still run.* We both knew we couldn't. The moor behind us had, I noticed now, subtly changed its character—the landmarks Vess had taught us were gone or rearranged, the standing stone tilted at an angle I'd never drawn. The gate had been walking us into itself for hours.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"To offer you hospitality." Ferren spread his reed-fingers wide. "Safe passage through the tollgates—all seven of them, past the Wardens of the Second Stair, past the Sighing Gallery where most travelers lose their nerve, straight down to the antechamber of the court itself. No tolls. No questions. No... adjustments." The word landed softly and horribly. "In exchange, a small thing. A trifle, really."

He looked at Ashen.

Ashen looked at the ground.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to. The terms are her. They're always her. Every bargain anyone has ever offered anyone in your court's long history has been *her*—a child, a sister, a daughter, somebody's somebody, handed over so the person holding the pen could walk away clean." My voice came out steadier than I felt. Inside, something was screaming in a register only dogs and dead mothers could hear. "No."

Ferren sighed with genuine regret, which frightened me more than menace would have. Menace means the deal is still being made. Regret means the deal has already been priced.

"Then let me make you a different offer," he said, "since you are a woman of commerce after all. Maps. I'm told yours are extraordinary. Drawn from a valley that keeps no magic, by a woman who keeps no magic, and therefore—" he tapped his temple with one folded finger "—accurate in a way nothing else in the world is anymore. Do you know what happens to a chart when it passes through enchanted country? It lies. Rivers move to flatter the local lords. Mountains sulk and refuse their true heights. But paper made in your valley, inked by your hand—it tells the truth. Truth is the rarest commodity beneath the mountain, Noor Sandoval. Rarer than souls. Souls we have *stacks* of."

He smiled again, warmer, if such a thing were possible.

"Give me a map—not the girl, just ink and vellum—and I will open every gate between here and the antechamber. Safe passage. My word, which is binding, because everything under the mountain is binding; that is rather our whole tragedy."

*

Here is what I should tell you about myself, since we've come this far together: I have spent thirty-one years making maps of places I could not go, and I told myself it was devotion, craft, love of the world from afar. It was not entirely that. It was also hunger. Every coastline I inked from secondhand sailors' accounts, every mountain range built out of a fur trader's drunken descriptions—I wanted them the way the starving want bread they cannot afford. I had made an art of wanting at a distance.

So when Ferren said *safe passage*, something in me leaned toward him like a plant toward light, and I hated that thing in me, and I listened to it anyway, because listening to your own hungers is the only way to know their weight.

"And if I refuse?" I asked.

"Then you go through the gates the honest way." He said *honest* the way other men say *bloody*. "The Sighing Gallery will ask each of you what you'd give up to turn back. The Wardens of the Second Stair will ask what you'd give up to go forward. Most travelers answer the first question honestly and the second one lying, and the gates know the difference. What remains afterward is usually... less than what set out." He examined his fingernails, which were very clean. "The child would likely survive. Children often do. It's the adults who come apart at the seams."

"He's telling the truth," Ashen said suddenly.

We both turned. She hadn't spoken in minutes; she'd been studying Ferren with that unnerving stillness she had, the stillness of a girl whose soul had been handled by strangers and knew the feel of their fingerprints.

"I can hear it," she said. "Under his words. There's a true voice underneath, like a bell under a blanket. He does mean safe passage. If you pay." Her small jaw tightened. "But the passage isn't safe. It's just *bought*. Those aren't the same."

Ferren regarded her with something that might, in a better creature, have been respect. "Out of the mouths of sold children," he murmured. "Yes. Precisely so. Bought safety is the only kind the court sells. We are not in the business of safe."

I looked at Tam. Tam looked at the sky, doing the arithmetic he always did when he thought I couldn't see—counting exits, counting odds, arriving at the same zero he always arrived at, and choosing to stand next to me anyway. Thirty years of friendship condensed into a shrug.

"A map," I said. "Of what?"

"The surface realm's waterways." Ferren answered too fast. He caught himself, smoothed it over with the smile, but I had already seen the eagerness underneath, raw as a nerve. "All of them. Springs, rivers, the underground courses where they vanish and resurface. Complete. Accurate. In your own hand."

"Why?" Tam asked bluntly. "What does the under-court want with surface water?"

"That," said Ferren pleasantly, "is above my station. Which is a phrase, incidentally, that has kept me alive for four hundred years, and I recommend it to you both."

I opened my satchel.

I want to be honest with you, whoever you are, reading this account wherever accounts go when their keepers are done with them. I want you to understand that in that moment I believed I was being clever. I believed I had found the half-measure, the third path, the trick that lets you keep the child and pay the price and walk away with your hands technically clean. Half-measures always feel like cleverness in the moment. That is their whole terrible genius. They feel like winning until you count what you actually gave away, and by then the gate has closed behind you and the toll is spent.

I took out the waterway charts I had been working on since Vess's kitchen table—three sheets of vellum, weeks of labor, every river I had ever coaxed out of travelers' memories rendered in my careful hand. The Silverwash and its tributaries. The drowned courses beneath the Marchlands. The spring-fed veins of the northern fells, which no living cartographer had mapped because no living cartographer had thought them worth the ink.

I did not give him everything. I held back the last sheet—the deep aquifers, the water that moved beneath the water, the oldest channels. Some instinct older than strategy told me to keep one truth in my pocket. I told myself it was leverage. I told myself a great many things.

"These," I said, "for passage to the antechamber. All seven gates. Your word."

"My word," Ferren agreed, and took the maps with those reed-fingers, and I watched him read them the way a starving man reads a banquet—quickly, greedily, missing nothing. When he reached the third sheet his breath actually caught, a small human sound from an inhuman throat, and I understood I had given him something worth far more than passage, and that he knew I didn't know how much more, and that this asymmetry was the real transaction.

"Wonderful," he breathed. "Wonderful. The gate is open. The gates are open." He stepped aside, and behind the lone door there was now—there had perhaps always been—a stairway descending into warm amber dark, lit by lamps that burned without flickering. "Mind the fourth step. It remembers being a man and gets sentimental."

Ashen went first, because she was braver than both of us. Tam followed, and then I paused at the threshold.

"Ferren," I said. "Why water? Why do they want the waterways?"

He was rolling my maps with a tenderness he had never once shown a traveler. He didn't look up.

"Because fire is going out, mapmaker," he said quietly. "And everything that burns must be replaced by something that flows. Now mind the fourth step."

*

We descended for what felt like a day and might have been an hour. The stair wound down through rock that gradually stopped being rock—veined with light, warm to the touch, faintly pulsing, and I made myself not think about what Vess had told us the palace was built of, and failed completely.

It was Tam who finally stopped, on a landing where the stairs widened into a rest alcove, and took out the compass.

"Look at this," he said.

The needle wasn't pointing. It was trembling—a fine, continuous shiver, like a bird's heartbeat, swinging in tiny arcs between two directions that shouldn't have existed underground: north, and something else. Something below us and ahead of us and everywhere at once.

"It's been doing that since the gate," he said. "Getting worse. Noor—Vess said this thing points at debts. Whose debt is it pointing at?"

I took it from him. The brass was warm. The needle shivered against my palm like a living thing begging.

"Mine," I said. "Ours. All of it." I closed my fist around the compass, which was foolish, and comforting, which is usually the same thing. "Every bargain we've made since leaving the valley. The road Vess showed us—that was a gift, but gifts are debts wearing nicer clothes. The heretic's shelter. The maps I just traded. None of it was free, Tam. Nothing outside the valley is free. I grew up thinking the world beyond the boundary ran on money and kindness. It doesn't. It runs on owing."

"So we owe our way to the antechamber," Tam said slowly, "to bargain with creatures to whom owing is a religion."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad."

"When I say it like that it's because it *is* bad, Noor." But he put his hand on my head briefly, the way he'd done since we were children, when I'd fallen out of the rowan tree or cried over my mother, and I let him, because some debts you're glad to carry. "Come on. The fourth step's waiting to be sentimental at us."

We passed it without incident. It sighed.

*

The stair ended at a vast landing where the amber light gathered into something like dawn, and there, carved into the living wall, was the antechamber door—taller than the gate above, older, its iron bands worked into shapes that hurt to look at directly because they were almost letters, almost words, almost promises.

Ashen stood before it, very still.

"Noor," she said. Her voice had changed since the descent—stronger, deeper, as if the mountain's proximity were watering some root in her. "When we go through, they'll know why we came. The Ledger-Keeper will already know. They always know. That's how they win—they let you arrive believing you have a secret, and the secret is the trap."

"Then we'll need a different secret," I said.

"Have you got one?"

I touched my satchel, where the withheld sheet lay hidden—the deep waters, the oldest channels, the truth I'd kept in my pocket. "One," I said.

Above us, far away, muffled by miles of singing stone, came a sound I would not understand until later but felt in my teeth even then: a long, low exhalation, like a bellows settling, like a fire deciding whether to bother burning until morning.

Ashen's head came up sharply. Her eyes went wide and ancient and nine years old all at once.

"He's still burning," she whispered. "Barely. Noor—we have to hurry. He's *almost done*."

She pushed open the antechamber door, and the light of the court beneath the mountain poured out over us like honey, like oil, like a debt coming due.
