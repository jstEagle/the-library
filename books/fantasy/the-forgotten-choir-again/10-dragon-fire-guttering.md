## 10. Dragon-Fire, Guttering

The dreams began on the third night outside the valley, and they were not Ashen's.

She knew this the way she knew the taste of rain before it fell—not by thinking it, but by some older knowing that lived in her chest where her voice used to be. Her own dreams had always been small things: a red door, a woman's hands braiding hair, a river running backward. These new dreams were vast. They smelled of stone and smoke and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun.

In them, she was enormous.

* * *

She woke in the dark with Noor's cloak pulled over her like a tent, and for a moment she did not know where her body ended. She could still feel wings—wings folded against a ceiling of mountain, wings so wide their tips ached from touching walls they had not touched in centuries. She could feel the chain.

It was not iron. That was the first thing the dream had taught her, and the thing that made her throat close up even now, awake, in the dark of Vess's low-ceilinged house. The chain was made of promises. Every link was a word someone had spoken and meant, and because they had meant it, the words had weight, and enough weighted words woven together could hold down anything—even a creature whose shoulder alone was broader than the temple in the village she barely remembered leaving.

Ashen lay very still and listened to Noor breathing on the other side of the cold hearth. Tam snored against the door, his pack under his head, the strange compass clutched in one hand even in sleep. It pointed at her now. She had noticed. She pretended she hadn't.

She closed her eyes and let the dream come back, because it wanted to come back, and because fighting it made her nose bleed.

* * *

The mountain did not have a name anymore. It had had one once—she could feel the shape of it, worn smooth as an old coin—but the court beneath had taken the name the way it took everything, folded it into a contract, and burned it true. Now the mountain was only *the mountain*, and beneath it was the court, and beneath the court, deeper than any map Vess had ever drawn with the hands she no longer had, was the heart-chamber.

Ashen had never seen it while awake. In the dream she saw nothing else.

It was a hollow the size of a cathedral's ambition, ribbed with stone that had been carved—or grown; the memory disagreed with itself—into arches upon arches, and every arch was inscribed. Words covered everything. Words in the old language, the language that lived in her mouth when her mouth worked, the language Noor had recognized from a manuscript fragment and gone white around the lips. The words were contracts. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They spiraled up the walls and across the ceiling and down into the floor, and they were all burning.

That was what no one understood, Ashen thought, watching the dream-memory with the detached horror of a child looking into a well: the contracts were not written *in* fire the way a scribe writes in ink. The fire *was* the writing. Dragon-fire did not destroy here—it fixed. It made words true. A promise spoken over dragon-fire could not be broken, could not be unwound, could not be regretted out of existence. It simply *was*, forever, the way a mountain was, the way grief was.

And the last dragon hung in the middle of it all, chained by its own gift.

* * *

Its name—her name, in the dream, because the dream did not distinguish—was a sound like a landslide deciding to be gentle. There was no way to write it. The court had tried, once, early on, because the Ledger-Keeper was thorough, and the attempt had scorched three scribes to shadows. So the dragon went unnamed in its own prison, which was, Ashen understood dimly, part of the chain. You could not call what had no name. You could not ask it anything.

But names could be dreamed. And Ashen dreamed.

She saw the beginning, or the shape of the beginning, the way you see a face through frosted glass. A younger world. A sky unbroken by tollgates. The dragon flying over kingdoms that were not yet kingdoms, over tribes who spoke the old language because it was the only language there was, and the old language had a property the newer tongues had lost: in it, a promise was a physical thing. Speak it truly and it took weight in the air. Break it and it broke *you*—not metaphorically. The words came home to roost in your bones.

The dragon had been the keeper of that truth. Not its enforcer—its keeper. When two tribes swore peace, they came to the dragon, and the dragon breathed a small flame between them, and the oath settled into the world like a stone settling into a riverbed. Fair. Witnessed. Done.

Then the tribes became clans, and the clans became courts, and one of the courts—the one that burrowed, the one that loved ledgers the way other courts loved gold—came to the dragon with a request so reasonable it sounded like courtesy.

*A single flame,* they had said, in the old language, speaking truly because they had to. *A single flame, freely given, to seal our treaties, so that our people need not travel to you across the winter passes. We will build you a hall beneath our mountain. Warm stone. Deep quiet. A place of honor.*

And the dragon—who was old, and tired of winters, and flattered in the way that only very old, very lonely creatures can be flattered—had said yes.

Freely given. Those were the words that mattered. The court had asked for the fire *freely given*, and the dragon had given it freely, and a freely given flame could not be taken back, because taking back a freely given thing would break the giving, and the giving had been sealed in the old language, and the old language did not permit such breaking without cost.

So the dragon had flown into the mountain's heart, expecting a hall of honor, and found the first arch already built, and the first contract already waiting to be burned true.

It had been a treaty, that first one. A land settlement. Something almost innocent. The dragon had breathed the flame because refusing seemed rude, and the contract had caught light and become permanent, and the Ledger-Keeper had smiled with a mouth full of neat gray teeth and said, *You see how much good we can do together.*

The second contract was less innocent. The tenth less than that. By the hundredth, the arches had climbed the walls like ivy, and the dragon had understood what it had done: it had not given the court a flame. It had given the court *itself*. Every contract needed fuel, and the fuel was the dragon's willing fire, and the dragon could not stop being willing without breaking its own original promise—and breaking that promise, spoken in the old tongue, freely given, would unmake the dragon as surely as a sword through the eye.

Chain of promises. Chain of its own making. The court had never needed iron at all.

* * *

Ashen woke again, and this time she was crying, and this time—this time—when she tried to say *no*, the word came out.

"*No.*"

Small. Cracked. Barely a sound. But a sound.

Noor was beside her before the echo died, hands hovering, not touching, the way she always did, as if Ashen were a page she was afraid of smudging. "What is it? What did you see?"

Ashen's voice was a rusty gate these days, opening a little wider each morning, and it hurt to use, and she used it anyway. "Fire," she said. "Under. Under the—" She didn't know the word for mountain-court-heart-chamber-prison, so she pressed both hands flat over her own sternum, hard, until Noor's eyes widened with understanding.

"The heart of it."

Ashen nodded. Then, because the dream was still in her and it wanted out and her voice was *working*, she said the other thing, the important thing, the thing that had woken her with tears: "It's dying."

Noor went very still. Outside, wind moved through Vess's herb garden, and somewhere in the house the old cartographer coughed in her sleep, the wet rattle of a woman whose lungs had been damaged by things she'd seen too closely.

"What do you mean, dying?" Noor asked carefully. "Dying how?"

Ashen searched for the words. Her vocabulary was coming back in pieces, oldest words first, and some concepts arrived faster than the language to hold them. She grabbed at what she had. "Not... dead-dead." She made a guttering motion with her fingers, the candle-flame gesture every child knew. "Like this. Like a candle. Going... going..." She couldn't find it. She said it instead in the old language, the word that meant *a fire choosing to end*, and Noor flinched as if the syllables had temperature.

"That's not possible," Noor whispered. "Vess said the fire makes the contracts burn. If the fire goes out—"

"Everything," Ashen said. She nodded, hard, relieved that the big word had come. "Everything true. Un-true." She frowned, frustrated, then tried again. "Every bargain. Every... every sold thing. Goes back." She touched her own chest, where the hole in her lived, the soul-shaped absence she carried like a swallowed stone. Hope moved in her face, brief and bright as a fish under ice.

Then she remembered the rest, and the hope drowned.

"And the valley," she said. "The valley too."

* * *

They woke Tam, because Noor said he'd want to know, and because the compass in his fist had started trembling hard enough to rattle against his knuckles even before his eyes opened. He sat up blinking, hair flattened on one side, and listened to Ashen piece the dream together with Noor translating her fragments into whole sentences, and by the end his face had gone the color of tallow.

"So let me get this straight," he said, in the voice he used when he was trying to be the sensible one. "The contracts under the mountain are only binding because a dragon is willingly burning them true. And the dragon is dying. Or—what did you say—*choosing to end*. And when that happens, every bargain it ever sealed comes undone. Including—" he swallowed, "—including the one that keeps the valley safe. The sanctuary. The reason magic can't follow anyone in there. The reason *you*—" he looked at Noor, and stopped, because some things were too large to point at.

"Including the valley," Noor said quietly.

"But that's—" Tam stood up, paced the length of the little room, stood again. "That's good, isn't it? Isn't that what we *want*? The contracts unravel, Ashen gets her soul back, everyone the court cheated goes free—"

"And the valley lies open," Noor said. "To everything. Forever. Every power that ever wanted in, walks in." She looked at Ashen. "How long?"

Ashen held up her hands, uncertain, then tried to show it with her body instead: she crouched, hunched, made herself small and flickering. *Soon.* Then she spread her fingers slowly apart. *Not tonight.*

"How soon is soon?" Tam demanded.

Ashen thought about the dream. About the color of the fire in the newest contracts—how it had been gold once, in the deep memory, and was now the color of an ember an hour after the bonfire, orange shading toward gray. About the way the dragon's breath had rattled in the dream the way Vess's cough rattled in the next room. About the word the old language had for *a fire choosing to end*, and how, in the dream, that word had not felt like death.

It had felt like *decision*.

"Soon," Ashen said aloud, because it was the only word she trusted. "Seasons. Maybe less."

Tam ran both hands through his hair. "Then we go faster. We go tomorrow. Tonight. We—"

"Noor." Ashen caught the mapmaker's sleeve, and when Noor looked down at her, Ashen said the thing she had been carrying since the dream, the thing that frightened her more than the dying fire, more than the court, more than the chain of promises. "It knows I'm dreaming it. It sent them. On purpose." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "It wants something."

The compass in Tam's hand shuddered, swung wildly, and settled—pointing not at Ashen anymore, but down. Through the floor. Through the earth. Toward the mountain.

Noor stared at it for a long moment. Then she began, silently, the way she did everything that mattered, to roll open a fresh sheet of paper.

"It wants a witness," she said. "Get some sleep. Both of you. We leave for the tollgate at first light—and Ashen—" she paused, quill poised, and her voice gentled, "—dream as much as you can. Everything it shows you, tell me. We're going to need a map of the inside of that mountain, and there's only one cartographer alive who's ever seen it."

From the next room, through the wall, Vess's voice came thin and dry as old vellum:

"I heard my name, mapmaker. And you should know—I drew that palace twice. And both times, the drawing drew me back."
