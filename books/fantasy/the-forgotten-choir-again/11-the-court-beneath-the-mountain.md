## 11. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The last tollgate was not a gate at all. It was a mouth.

Noor had drawn a hundred mouths in her life—the cave mouths of the Greymarsh coast, the sinkholes of the Ashfall plains, all from secondhand accounts she'd inked with hands that never trembled because none of it was real to her, none of it could ever reach through the paper and touch her. This one was real. It opened in the flank of the mountain like a wound that had learned patience, its lips of black stone slick with something that was not quite water and not quite light, and the air that breathed out of it was warm in a way no underground air should be—warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, warm with breath.

Tam's compass hung at her throat now, on a cord Vess had given them before they parted ways at the pilgrim road's end. The needle did not point north. It pointed down, quivering, toward the thing beneath the mountain that held Ashen's soul in its ledger like a pressed flower.

"You don't have to come," Noor said to Tam for the fourth time.

"And you've said that four times," Tam said, "which means you're counting, which means you're scared, which means I'm definitely coming." He adjusted his pack and looked up at the stone mouth without flinching, which Noor knew cost him something; Tam flinched at loud noises and steep stairs and the word *debt*, but he had followed a compass made of guilt across half a kingdom, and his fear had worn down to something smooth and usable, like a river stone. "Besides. Someone has to remember what your face looks like when you're being an idiot."

Ashen said nothing. Ashen hadn't spoken since the tollgate of small mercies, where Noor had traded a chart of every river in the surface realm for passage, and the official—a gray, courteous thing wearing a man's shape like an ill-fitting coat—had smiled too widely and said, *the court will remember this generosity*. Since then the girl had gone quiet in a new way, not mute but listening, her head tilted as though she could hear something under the world's skin. Maybe she could. Her soul was down there. Perhaps it called to her the way a home calls, or a wound.

"Stay behind me," Noor told her. "If anything happens, you run back up the road. You don't wait. You don't look for me."

Ashen looked at her with those old, old eyes and did not agree, which Noor chose to interpret as agreement.

They went in.

* * *

The descent began as ordinary rock, and Noor hated how grateful she was for that—for the familiar grammar of stone and shadow, for walls she could have mapped blind. But the ordinary thinned as they went, the way a road thins into wilderness. First the veins in the rock began to glow, faint threads of gold light running through the black stone like script through vellum. Then the threads became words. Then the words became names.

Noor stopped walking when she realized what she was reading.

The tunnel walls were inscribed, floor to unseen ceiling, with names. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They ran past in glowing filigree, some fresh-bright, some dimmed to embers, some gone dark entirely—dead letters, dead names—and she understood, with a lurch that started in her stomach and ended somewhere behind her eyes, that these were not decorations.

They were receipts.

"Ashen," she whispered. "Don't read the walls."

But Ashen was already reading. Her small finger rose and traced one name, then another, her lips moving around syllables of the old language, and Noor saw the girl's face do something terrible: it relaxed. As if the walls were comforting. As if she recognized the handwriting.

*She knows the hand,* Noor thought. *It wrote her.*

They walked faster.

* * *

The tunnel opened without warning, the way a held breath opens, and Noor's first thought—that first unbearable, unforgivable thought—was: *it's beautiful.*

The under-court spread below them like a city that had been dreamed by something that had only ever heard cities described. It filled a cavern so vast that its far wall was a rumor, its ceiling lost in a glow that came from everywhere and nowhere, and rising out of its center stood the palace itself—not built, Noor understood immediately, but *grown*, or perhaps *woven*, spires and buttresses and bridges of a substance that caught the ambient light and broke it into colors that had no surface names. It shimmered. It sang, very faintly, a chord that Noor felt in her teeth more than her ears.

And it moved. Not much. Not obviously. But the whole vast structure breathed, slow and deep, the way the mountain breathed at its mouth, and when Noor looked too long at any single spire she saw that it was not stone. It was finer than stone. It was—

"It's people," Tam said hoarsely beside her. He had gone the color of tallow. "Noor. Look closely. It's *people*."

She didn't want to look closely. She looked anyway, because looking was what she did, because a mapmaker who refuses to see is just a liar with good penmanship.

The spires were souls. Fused, woven, rendered into architecture—thousands upon thousands of them, pressed together until their individual light merged into the general glow, but not erased. She could still see them, if she let her eyes unfocus: faces in the masonry, or the suggestion of faces, mouths open in expressions that might have been screams or might have been song. A balustrade that was a row of children holding hands. A great door whose panels were two figures embracing, forever, their arms indistinguishable from the wood of them.

And underneath the beauty, underneath the singing chord, there was the other sound. Once she heard it she could never unhear it. The palace screamed. Very quietly. Continuously. The way a distant sea sounds like silence until you realize the silence is made of waves.

"Noor," Ashen said. Her voice was very small. "I can hear my name."

* * *

They descended into the city proper along a road of fused light, and Noor did the only thing she knew how to do with terror: she worked.

Her satchel held no paper worthy of this—her maps were for a world with weather and distance, and this place laughed at both—but her memory was a different instrument, trained over thirty years of holding entire realms in her head. So she mapped. She mapped as they walked, carving the under-court into her mind with cartographer's precision, and the discipline of it kept her sane, or near enough.

*Great causeway from the mouth-road, arched, guarded by nothing—which is worse than guards. Water below? No. Light below. The city floats on light, or is suspended in it. Streets radiate from the palace like spokes. The buildings nearest the palace are tallest, brightest—rank, then. Proximity is rank. The far districts dim toward the cavern walls, and the dim ones... the dim ones are older. Abandoned? Or spent.*

She noted exits. There were fewer than there should have been. A court this size, a city this sprawled, should have had a hundred roads out, and instead everything funneled inward, spoke after spoke after spoke, all roads leading to the palace like veins to a heart. Whatever the court feared leaving, it had designed against it.

*One way in and out that we know: the mouth-road. Find others. There are always others. Water finds cracks; so do mapmakers.*

The inhabitants watched them pass. That was the worst part, at first—the watching. The court's people, if they were people, stood in doorways of woven light and turned their heads in perfect unison as Noor and Tam and Ashen went by, not hostile, not curious, merely *attending*, the way an audience attends. Some of them were beautiful beyond reason. Some wore shapes that had been borrowed rather than earned, and the seams showed at the wrists and throat. None of them spoke. The silence of the streets had weight, like snow.

Then a woman stepped out of an alcove directly into their path, and Noor's hand found the knife at her belt before her mind caught up.

The woman was old in a way the court's things were not—old the way oak is old, with lines and wear, and her eyes were human, and humanly exhausted. She looked at Noor's hand on the knife and said, in a cracked voice, "You'll want to save that. Nothing here bleeds."

"I'm looking for the Ledger-Keeper," Noor said.

Something moved through the woman's face—pity, or its fossil. "Everyone is." She glanced at Ashen, and her expression changed, sharpened, and she crouched slowly, painfully, to the girl's level. "Child. When they take you in there—" she touched Ashen's chest, lightly, over the heart, "—they'll show you a room full of doors. Don't open the one that sounds like your mother."

Ashen stared at her. And then, in the old language, soft as ash falling, she answered—and the woman flinched as if struck, straightened, and stepped back off the road.

"What did she say?" Tam asked.

"I don't know," Noor said. "But write it down later, Ashen. Everything you say down here. Everything anyone says." She met the old woman's eyes. "Why are you helping us?"

The woman smiled, and it was the saddest arithmetic Noor had ever seen on a face. "I'm not helping you. I'm betting on you. There's a difference, and the difference is why I'm still alive." She melted back into the alcove, and the street closed behind her like water, and Noor marked the alcove's location in her memory-map with a small, desperate star.

*Ally, maybe. Or bait. Either way: an exit runs behind it. I saw the seam.*

* * *

The palace swallowed them at dusk, except that Noor suspected the under-court had no dusk, that the light here simply performed one for the benefit of visitors, the way a host performs warmth.

Inside, the screaming was louder.

Outside, the fused souls had been blended into a chord; inside, the walls resolved into individuals again, and Noor could hear them—could almost make out words, pleas, names, the same litany of names she'd read on the tunnel walls, except these voices were not written, they were *saying* themselves, over and over, each soul reciting its own identity like a prayer against dissolution. A pillar beside the entrance hall was a man who had been a ferryman; she could tell because he was still poling, still leaning into a current that no longer existed, his soul-rowing through eternity. A window was a bride. A stairwell was a choir, and the choir was the source of the singing chord, and the choir was also the source of the screaming, and Noor finally understood that they were the same sound, that here, beauty and horror were not opposites but the same material viewed from different distances.

That was power, she thought. That was what power actually looked like, stripped of its robes: the ability to make other beings into your furniture and call the result civilization.

"Keep mapping," Tam murmured. His voice had gone thin. "You've got that look. Keep doing the look."

"The entrance hall has three corridors," she whispered back. "North corridor goes down—steps, wide, ceremonial. East corridor goes toward light that flickers, which means fire, which means either kitchens or the dragon. West corridor smells like ink." She paused. "Ink, Tam. Real ink. Paper and iron-gall and something older under it. Somebody down here keeps records the way I keep records."

"The Ledger-Keeper," Tam said.

"The Ledger-Keeper," Noor agreed, and felt the compass at her throat swing gently, patiently, westward, like a dog turning toward its master.

They took the west corridor.

* * *

It was Ashen who stopped them, three turns deep in the maze of record-halls, by grabbing Noor's sleeve with a grip far stronger than her size suggested. She pointed.

At the end of the hall stood a door. It was unremarkable—dark wood, iron hinges, the kind of door that existed in every village Noor had ever mapped—and that was precisely what made it horrifying, because everything else here was woven light and screaming beauty, and this door was simply, humbly, *ordinary*, as if the court had set one piece of the surface world into its heart like a jewel, or a trophy.

Sounds came from behind it. Muffled. Domestic. A kettle, perhaps. A chair scraping. And underneath it, faint but unmistakable, a woman humming a lullaby.

Noor knew the lullaby.

She stood frozen while the compass burned cold against her sternum, while Tam said her name once, twice, while Ashen looked up at her with eyes that held the terrible clarity of children who understand more than they are given credit for. The lullaby was the one her mother used to hum in the workshop while grinding pigments, the one Noor had not heard in twenty years, the one she had convinced herself she had forgotten, note by note, the way you forget anything you cannot afford to remember.

"Don't," Tam said quietly. "Noor. Whatever's behind that door—it isn't. The woman in the street said—"

"I know what she said." Noor's voice came out flat and strange. *Don't open the one that sounds like your mother.* "I know exactly what she said."

But her hand was already rising, because she was a mapmaker, and mapmakers have one incurable disease: when a territory is marked *here be monsters*, they need to walk in and draw what's actually there.

Behind the door, the humming stopped.

And a voice—warm, worn, achingly familiar, a voice Noor had spent two decades failing to forget—said, through the dark wood, with a smile in it:

"Noor? Is that you, love? Come in. I've kept your room just the way it was."

The compass needle spun once, wildly, and settled pointing at the door.

Noor reached for the handle.
