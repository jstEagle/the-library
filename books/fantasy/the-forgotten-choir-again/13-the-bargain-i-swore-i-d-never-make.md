## 13. The Bargain I Swore I'd Never Make

The Ledger-Keeper's study had no ceiling.

That was the first thing I understood about the room, standing in its amber light with Ashen's small hand cold in mine: above us there was only a slow drift of gold dust rising toward nothing, as if the palace breathed upward and never out. The walls were shelved from floor to vanishing point with ledgers—thousands of them, tens of thousands—each spine labeled in a script that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts when you've been in a cellar too long. Somewhere in that endless library was my mother's name. Somewhere was mine. Somewhere, on a shelf no taller than a child, was a slim volume with ASHEN pressed into its leather like a brand.

"Sit," said the Ledger-Keeper, and gestured to a chair that had not been there a moment before.

I did not sit. I had learned that much already, three tollgates deep into this place: never accept furniture you didn't bring yourself. Furniture was a kind of contract. Everything here was a kind of contract.

The Ledger-Keeper did not seem offended. It settled behind a desk of black wood—or what I took for wood until I saw the grain move, slow as honey, and understood it was something older than trees—and folded its long hands. Its face was courteous the way an empty house is courteous: every feature present, correct, and utterly unoccupied. Eyes like coins left on a grave.

"You have come a long way, mapmaker," it said. "Through gates that have closed behind you. Past wardens who are even now filing reports about you." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "You should know that your passage has been noted. All of it. Every mile you walked outside your valley, you grew more visible. You are, at this moment, the most legible thing in my court."

"I don't want to be legible," I said. "I want to be gone. With her."

"With her." The coin-eyes drifted down to Ashen. The child stood very still beside me, chin up, the way she'd learned to stand when the priests shouted—the way children stand when they have decided that stillness is armor. But her fingers had found the hem of my coat and knotted into it, and I felt each small knuckle like a hook through cloth and skin.

"She is a lovely piece of work," the Ledger-Keeper said, in the tone of a man admiring a horse. "Her soul especially. Old stock. Pre-kingdom. You would not believe what such material fetches among collectors."

"It isn't material. She's a girl."

"She is a girl *and* a soul," the Ledger-Keeper agreed pleasantly. "These are not contradictory facts. They are simply two entries in two different books." One hand rose, unhurried, and touched the desk. A ledger slid forward across the black grain and stopped precisely between us, open to a page written in fire-colored ink that moved as I watched, letters rearranging themselves like insects disturbed under a stone.

"Before we discuss terms," it said, "you should see what you're carrying."

I looked down. I wish I could say I refused. But I am a cartographer; show me a document and some starved part of me will always read it.

It was Ashen's contract. I knew that before I understood a word of it. The language was the old tongue—the one she spoke in her sleep, the one Vess had taught me to half-hear on the road—but the shape of it was familiar anyway, because all contracts are the same shape. *Herein.* *Whereas.* *In perpetuity.* And then the consideration, the price paid, set down in ink so old it had gone brown:

*One soul, female child, third-born of the line of Senn, sold by her father's hand for the remission of debt and the preservation of his remaining household.*

Below that, a signature. Below that, a seal pressed in wax that was not wax but something dried and dark and faintly warm, and I made myself stop looking at it because I suspected I knew what seals were made of in this place.

"He sold her," I said. My voice came out flat. "Her own father."

"For flour," the Ledger-Keeper said. "For seed-grain and a mended roof. It was a bad winter. It is always a bad winter, somewhere. That is how the court eats." It closed the book gently, the way one closes a hymnal. "You despise him. Of course. But consider: he signed once, in desperation, and his family lived. You have been walking toward me for weeks for the same reason. Desperation is the oldest road in the world, mapmaker. I merely keep the milestones."

Ashen's hand tightened in mine. I looked down at her and she looked up at me, and in her face was none of the terror I expected. There was something worse. There was recognition. She had heard these words before, or their music, in whatever fragment of herself still remembered being sold.

"We're leaving," I said.

"No," said the Ledger-Keeper, with terrible mildness, "you're not. But do go on. Defiance photographs beautifully against the shelves."

* * *

It offered me tea. I declined. It offered me a chair again, and this time Ashen's legs were trembling, so I sat and pulled her onto my knee, and hated myself for accepting anything, and did it anyway, because she needed the rest more than I needed my pride.

Then the Ledger-Keeper told me about the position.

"You know Vess," it began.

"Vess is dead to you. She lost her hands to this place and crawled out anyway."

"And yet." The coin-eyes glittered. "You slept under her roof. You ate her bread. Did she tell you what her hands bought? Did she tell you that the maps she drew for us—for eleven years, before her little rebellion—are still the most accurate charts of the surface realm in existence? That our tollgates sit where they sit because she placed them? That every traveler who has ever been taken on those roads was taken along lines *she drew*?"

"She was a prisoner."

"She was an employee," the Ledger-Keeper corrected, "with excellent benefits and poor boundaries. And now she is retired, and the position is vacant, and I find myself looking at the finest cartographic mind to enter this court in a century." It leaned forward. For the first time, the empty courtesy cracked, and something underneath looked out—something hungry and patient and very, very old. "You mapped my palace in your head while walking through it. Don't bother denying it; I can hear a mind taking measurements the way I can hear a mind praying. Exits. Sightlines. Depths. You've been drawing me since the moment you arrived."

My silence confirmed everything. I felt it do so.

"Here are the terms," it said. "You take Vess's place. Quarters, materials, access to every archive in this court—including the pre-kingdom fragments, which I am given to understand would keep you occupied for several delighted lifetimes. You will draw for us as she drew. In exchange—" and here it turned the ledger around again, to a fresh page, where words were already assembling themselves in that moving fire-ink, "—the girl's contract is voided. Not transferred. Voided. Her soul returns to her body whole, tonight, and she walks out of my gates free, and no power in this court may touch her again. Signed by me. Sealed in fire."

The page lay there between us, glowing softly, waiting.

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I'm owed: for a moment—a real moment, seconds long, long enough to have weight—I wanted to say yes.

Not because I doubted it was a trap. I knew it was a trap the way you know ice over deep water. I wanted to say yes because the trap was *comfortable*. Because the study was warm and the chair held my back and Ashen's weight on my knee was solid and safe, and somewhere above us the world was weather and wolves and roads that forgot themselves, and here—here there were shelves, order, an archive deeper than any library I had ever dreamed of, and I would be *inside* it. I would be the woman who finally saw the realms she'd spent her life mapping blind. Every forbidden manuscript. Every pre-kingdom fragment. Every secret I had ever copied secondhand from smuggled pages, offered to me firsthand, forever.

And Ashen would be free. Whole. Laughing. Safe.

All it cost was other people. Other travelers on roads I drew. Other fathers' signatures coaxed out by winters I helped make bad. Other children's names pressed into leather spines on shelves I would dust and organize and maintain, neatly, competently, for the rest of my long and comfortable life.

Vess had sat in this chair. Vess had read this page. Vess had drawn the tollgates and told herself, every morning for eleven years, that she was only buying time, only surviving, only doing what must be done—and then one day she had looked down at her own hands and seen what they had built, and chosen fire over comfort, and lost everything except the truth.

"They'll refuse," I said quietly. "You'll make me draw maps, and travelers will refuse to pay, and you'll take them anyway, and I'll be the milestone on the road to hell."

"Some will refuse," the Ledger-Keeper agreed. "Most won't. People rarely do. That is the tragedy of your species, mapmaker, and the engine of mine." It slid a pen across the desk. It was white bone, or ivory, or something that had once had a name. "Take your time. Though not too much. The fire below us is guttering, as I'm sure you've heard, and when it goes out, my generosity goes with it. Sign while contracts still bind. Sign while your signature still means anything at all."

I picked up the pen.

God forgive me—I picked up the pen. I told myself I was stalling. I told myself I would read the fine print, find the flaw, the way my mother's contract had a flaw, the way every contract here had a flaw if you stared long enough. I told myself a great many things, and beneath all of them, quiet as water under ice, ran the true current: *sign it, sign it, sign it, and the hurting stops.*

The bone pen was warm in my hand. Warmer than bone should be. Like something alive, held still.

"Ashen," I said, because I wanted to hear a voice that wasn't mine or its, "look away for a moment."

She didn't look away. She looked at the pen, and then at me, and her eyes filled—not with fear, with something far more terrible. With understanding. This child who had been sold once already knew exactly what was happening, knew the shape of the room, the shape of the offer, the shape of the adult deciding whether she was a person or a price. And she did not cry out. She just kept holding my coat, and waited to learn what she was worth to me.

The pen touched the page.

* * *

The door burst open on the sound of boots and breathing.

Tam came through it like weather—like the valley itself had torn loose and come for me. He was filthy, frostbitten, one eye swollen nearly shut, his coat ripped along the seam where something with claws had clearly disagreed with him about his right of passage. The compass was in his fist, its needle spinning wildly, pointing not north but *at me*, quivering like a struck bell.

He stopped. He took in the study, the shelves, the Ledger-Keeper, the glowing page, the pen in my hand hovering over my own name half-written.

His face did something I had never seen it do in thirty years of friendship. It didn't rage. It didn't plead. It simply *fell*—the way a man's face falls when he finds his own house burned and his family gone, when the last possible hope turns out to be the disaster itself.

"Oh, Noor," he said. Just that. Just my name, in Tam's voice, the voice that had pulled me out of the millpond when we were nine, that had defended the strange mapmaker's daughter to every priest and bully in the valley, that had followed a mad compass through three kingdoms and a mountain's teeth because I was on the other end of it. "Oh, Noor. *No.*"

"I was reading the terms," I said, and heard my own voice, smooth and reasonable and vile. "There may be a flaw. Mother's contract had a flaw. If I can find—"

"There's no flaw." He crossed the room in four strides and put his ruined hand over the page, smearing the fire-ink, and the Ledger-Keeper let him, watching with the fascination of a scholar observing an experiment. "You think I climbed a mountain to watch you negotiate? Vess told us what happens in this chair. She screamed it at us through a broken door while the wardens dragged her off. *They always find a flaw,* she said. *They leave one. Finding it is how they teach you to stay.*"

"That's not—"

"You were going to draw their roads." His voice cracked down the middle. "You. The woman who spent her whole life making maps so people could get *out*. Do you know what I passed to get here? There's a gate on the north road with a warden wearing a man's face, and under the gate there's a pile of shoes, Noor. Hundreds of shoes. All sizes. Children's sizes. Somebody drew that road. Somebody sat in a warm room and drew it, and told themselves a story, and every shoe in that pile is a paragraph in their story."

The pen was still in my hand. I looked at it. Warm. Patient. Waiting for me to finish the stroke.

And Ashen—Ashen reached up and took my wrist. Not pulling. Just holding. Her grip was tiny and iron-strong, and she turned my hand over, palm up, the way you hold a bird you're afraid will break, and she looked at the pen lying across my lifeline, and then she looked at me and said—in the old language, the pre-kingdom tongue, the language of promises—one word.

I didn't know its meaning. But I felt it land, the way you feel a struck tuning fork in your sternum. Later, Vess would translate it for me, weeping: *remember.*

Remember what you are. Remember whose hand this is. Remember the valley that raised you strange and beloved. Remember the mother who paid everything so you would never sit in this chair.

Remember that you are not a price. Neither is she.

The pen rolled out of my fingers and hit the floor, and where it struck, the black wood of the desk hissed and scarred, as if rejected.

"No sale," I said.

* * *

The Ledger-Keeper sighed. It was the sigh of a bureaucrat whose afternoon has been inconvenienced, and it was the most frightening sound I had heard in the entire under-court.

"I did offer," it said. "That matters, legally. When the fire dies and the audits begin, it will be recorded that the mapmaker was offered generous terms and declined them. History will treat you kindly, I suspect. History loves a refuser." It rose, and the shelves rose with it, and the gold dust overhead stopped drifting and hung suspended, thousands of motes frozen mid-fall, and I understood suddenly that the room had been listening the entire time—that the room *was* the court, and we had been speaking inside its throat.

"But you have seen the ledgers," it continued, folding its hands. "You have seen the contracts. You know now what she is, and what you are, and what your mother paid. Knowledge is also a form of entry, mapmaker. You cannot unsee my books, and I cannot permit a mind that carries my books to walk out through my gates." The coin-eyes turned to Tam, then back to me, and beneath the courtesy the hunger showed its full length at last, vast and cold and patient as the mountain itself. "So. We will continue this conversation in somewhat less comfortable quarters. I find that guests who refuse the study often reconsider in the cells. The cells are very persuasive. So many of my guests there chose to stay, in the end. Willingly. Happily, even."

"Doors opened behind us. Doors I hadn't known were doors. Beyond them, footsteps—many, soft, unhurried, closing in with the confidence of an institution that has never once been refused twice.

Tam's hand found my shoulder. Ashen's arms went around my neck. The compass in his fist spun and spun, pointing at me, at us, at the debt still growing, and I thought—with a clarity that felt almost like peace—*we came here to steal a soul, and instead we've been caught inside one.*

The footsteps reached the doorway.

The lights went out.
