## 14. Prisoners of the Beautiful Palace

They did not throw Tam into a dungeon. That was the first thing he noticed, and the first thing that frightened him.

The guards—if you could call them guards, these tall figures in robes the color of deep water who never seemed to blink—escorted him down corridors of pale stone with a courtesy so complete it felt like mockery. One of them carried his satchel for him. Another offered him water from a clay flask, and when he refused to drink it, simply set it on a shelf and said nothing, as though they had all the time in the world and knew it.

"Where is she?" Tam asked for the fourth time. "Noor. The mapmaker. Where have you taken her?"

"She is being received," said the nearest guard, in a voice like a bell rung underwater. "As are you."

"I'm not being *received*. I'm a prisoner."

"You are a guest," the guard corrected gently, "who has not yet understood his welcome."

Tam wanted to hit something. He had followed the compass for nine days—nine days of walking through country that grew stranger and more beautiful the farther he went, the compass needle trembling in its housing like a bird trying to escape its cage—and he had arrived at the mountain's mouth just in time to watch Noor and Ashen descend through the last tollgate, and just in time to be plucked off the road by hands that smelled of cold iron and old incense.

He had shouted her name. He was almost sure she had heard him. He was almost sure she had turned.

And then the dark had folded over him like a page turning, and now here he was, being walked politely toward whatever this place did instead of imprisonment.

The corridor opened without warning into a hall so vast that Tam's breath caught. It was not built; it was *grown*—or rather, it was woven, walls and pillars and vaulted ceiling all made of the same substance: a pearly, shifting material that glowed faintly from within, like moonlight trapped in silk. And it moved. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, the whole palace breathed.

And it sang.

Not music, exactly. More like the memory of music—the hum a choir makes an instant after the song ends, held forever. Thousands of voices braided together into one long, low note that Tam felt in his teeth, in the backs of his eyes, in the hollow behind his sternum where grief lived.

"What is that sound?" he whispered.

The guard paused. For the first time, something almost like kindness crossed its face—or the shape it wore in place of a face.

"That," it said, "is the palace."

* * *

His cell was a room in a tower, if a room can be called a cell when it has a bed of soft grey wool, a window looking out over a garden of luminous flowers, fresh bread on the table, and no lock on the door.

That was the trick of it, Tam realized on the first night. There was no lock because there needed to be none. The door stood open onto a corridor that wound away into the singing dark, and every time he tried to follow it, he found himself arriving back at his own room—not lost, not turned around, simply *returned*, the way a river returns to its bed. The palace did not keep him in. It kept everything else out.

He ate the bread on the second day, because hunger is honest and pride is not. It was the best bread he had ever tasted. That frightened him more than the guards had.

On the third day, someone knocked.

She was small and round-faced, with grey hair pinned up carelessly and flour still dusting her sleeves, though there was no kitchen Tam could imagine anywhere in this place. She looked like somebody's grandmother. She looked like everybody's grandmother, which was exactly what should have warned him.

"You're the friend," she said. "The one from the valley. The compass-man."

"How do you know about my compass?"

"Oh, we hear things." She smiled and settled herself uninvited into the chair by the window, arranging her skirts with the ease of long habit. "I'm Maren. I've been here forty years, give or take. Time moves strangely below the frost line." She nodded at the untouched loaf. "You'll want to eat while it's warm. They bake it fresh for new guests. After a while they stop bothering."

"Why would they stop?"

"Because after a while," Maren said pleasantly, "you stop needing to eat."

Tam sat down across from her slowly. "You're a prisoner."

"I'm a resident." Her smile didn't waver, but something behind it shifted, the way light shifts on water when a fish passes underneath. "There's a difference, and it matters very much down here. Prisoners want to leave. Residents have stopped wanting anything, which is much more comfortable."

"And which are you?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—flatter, wearier, the voice of a woman talking in her sleep. "I was a dyer, before. In Liss. My husband died in the fever year and my daughter married a man I couldn't abide, and I was alone in a house full of wool that smelled like vinegar and grief. A peddler came through selling maps to the under-court. Not paper maps. *Invitations.*" She laughed softly. "I thought I was buying a curiosity. What I bought was a door, and the door swung both ways, and I walked through it on a Tuesday."

"And now?"

"Now I am comfortable," she said. "I have a garden. I have friends. Nothing hurts. Nothing ends. Nothing *changes*." She said the last word the way other people say a curse.

"That sounds like a cage."

"It sounds like rest," Maren corrected. "You've been walking a long time, compass-man. You have tired feet and a tired heart—I can see it, we can all see it, the palace sees everything its guests carry. It will offer to put those burdens down. It's very good at offering." She rose, brushing flour from her skirt that hadn't been there a moment ago, or had always been there. "I'll tell you the only thing worth knowing, since you're new. Ask any resident here why they stay, and they'll tell you a story about comfort. But sit with them long enough—longer than most visitors manage—and the truth comes out." She paused at the door. "Every soul in this palace chose to forget something. The comfort isn't the payment. The forgetting is."

Then she was gone, and the corridor beyond her was empty, and Tam sat alone with the best bread he had ever tasted going stale on the table, and did not eat another bite.

* * *

On the fifth day, he found the cells.

He hadn't been looking for them. He had been looking for Noor—walking the corridors with the stubbornness of a man who refuses to be returned to his room, letting the palace carry him wherever it liked and then striking off sideways whenever the architecture allowed. The palace was patient. It simply reabsorbed him each time, gentle as tide. But patience leaves seams, and Tam had spent thirty years reading weather and reading people, and he had learned that everything patient has a seam somewhere.

He found it behind a tapestry in a hallway that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of the valley—of cut hay and woodsmoke, of home, which was the palace's cruelest trick yet and he knew it and stepped through anyway.

Beyond the tapestry the corridors changed. The pearl-grey walls roughened. The singing grew louder, and Tam finally heard it for what it was: not one note but thousands, and not harmony but *pleading*, voices layered so deep and so long that the pleading had worn smooth into something like music.

The cells were alcoves. That was all. Shallow niches in the living wall, each one holding a figure half-sunk into the pearly substance, reclining as if in a bath, eyes closed, faces slack with contentment. The wall had grown up around them to the chest, to the shoulders, to the chin—however far each of them, apparently, had chosen to sink.

A man near the entrance murmured in his sleep: "*Just one more year. Just one more winter like this one.*"

A woman deeper in, sunk to her collarbones, smiled and said nothing at all.

Tam stood in the aisle between them with his skin crawling, and understood what Maren had meant. These were not prisoners. These were volunteers. People who had been offered rest and had accepted it, and were being slowly, sweetly absorbed into the fabric of the palace itself—into the walls, into the song. Every beautiful surface in this place was made of someone.

He thought of the ceiling of the great hall, vast and glowing and breathing. He thought of how many souls it must have taken.

He thought of Vess, and her ruined hands, and the warning Noor had come back from that visit carrying like a stone she wouldn't set down: *the court does not destroy what it keeps. It builds with it.*

"Noor," he whispered, to steady himself. "Hold on. I'm coming."

"*Tam?*"

The voice came from an alcove three down on the left, and it was not Noor's voice, and it was not a sleepy murmur, and it stopped his heart in his chest.

The woman in the alcove had white hair and a face deeply lined, and her eyes were open—open, and clear, and awake in a way that no other face in that corridor was awake. She had pushed herself up out of the wall to the waist, which Tam suspected no one here had done in years, and she was staring at him with an expression he recognized, because he had seen it on Noor's face a hundred times: the look of a person doing fierce, unsentimental arithmetic.

"You're from the valley," she said. "I can see it on you. The way you stand, like the ground might lie to you. Only valley-folk stand like that." Her voice cracked. "Boy. Boy, listen to me. Is Noor alive? Is my daughter alive?"

The world tilted. Tam gripped the edge of the alcove.

"You're Sandoval," he said stupidly. "You're—you disappeared. Before Noor could walk. The elders said—"

"The elders said what they were paid to say." The woman—Lira Sandoval, though he only knew that later; in the moment she was simply *her*, impossible and furious and alive—laughed, a short bitter sound with no humor in it. "I wasn't taken, boy. I walked. Same as everyone else in this wall. I signed myself over to buy my daughter's freedom, and I told myself I'd rest a decade, twenty years, just until I was sure she was safe, and then—" She gestured at the soft pearly wall around her, at the warmth of it, and her fury faltered into something older and more ashamed. "And then it's comfortable, do you understand? That's the trap. Not chains. Comfort. Forty years, and I'd have sunk to my chin by spring if you hadn't come shouting my daughter's name loud enough to wake the dead."

Tam's mind was racing, tripping over itself. "Noor's here. She came for a child—Ashen—a girl whose soul was sold—"

"Ashen." Something moved through Lira's face, too fast to name. "Yes. Yes, I know that name. It's sung here sometimes, in the walls. Old contracts sing when they're close to breaking." She reached out and seized his wrist with a strength that startled him. "Listen to me, and listen carefully, because I don't know how long I can hold myself up like this, and the palace is already whispering to me to lie back down. It knows my name. It's been saying it for forty years, so kindly, so *kindly*—"

"Lady—"

"Noor's mother. Say it. I want to hear it once before I go back under."

"Noor's mother," Tam said, and her eyes shone, and she let go of his wrist.

"Good." She drew a breath, steadying herself against the pull he could see dragging at her, the sweetness of surrender tugging her back down into the warm wall. "Now. The Ledger-Keeper will have offered her a bargain. It always does—it loves bargains, it *is* bargains, it thinks in terms of exchange the way you think in words. Whatever it offers her, the price will be herself. Her service, her freedom, her future. And here is what you must make her understand, boy, because she won't hear it from me, not down here, not through this much wall—"

Her voice was fraying. The pearl was creeping up past her ribs again, gentle as rising water.

"The contract can't be beaten by paying it," she said. "Every payment feeds it. Every bargain strengthens the fire that writes it. The only thing that beats a contract is—" and here she smiled, and it was Noor's smile exactly, fierce and crooked and unbought— "is refusing the whole grammar of it. But she has to learn that from outside. From you. You're the only one down here who hasn't been offered anything yet."

"They'll offer me something," Tam said grimly. "Won't they."

"Oh, they already have." Lira laughed again, sinking now, the wall closing over her with terrible tenderness. "The bread, boy. The bed. The open door that leads nowhere. You've been declining beautifully." Her chin dipped beneath the pearl; her eyes stayed fixed on his, burning. "Keep declining. Find my daughter. Tell her—"

The wall took the rest.

Tam stood alone in the singing corridor, heart hammering, the echo of those unfinished words ringing in him like a struck bell. Around him the residents slept their comfortable sleep, smiling their small smiles, murmuring their small surrenders—one more year, one more winter, one more—

Behind the tapestry, faintly, distantly, he heard running footsteps, and a voice he knew better than his own heartbeat calling his name.

"Noor!" He spun toward the seam, shoving the tapestry aside. "Noor! Down here!"

And the palace, all around him, changed its song.
