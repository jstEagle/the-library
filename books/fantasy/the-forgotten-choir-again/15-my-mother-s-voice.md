## 15. My Mother's Voice

The cells of the under-court did not look like cells.

That was the first cruelty of them, Noor thought as she followed the warden-thing down a corridor of amber light. They looked like parlors. Like kitchens at dusk. Like every warm room she had ever been invited into and never quite belonged in. Each doorway along the passage glowed with a different domestic gold—firelight, lamplight, the soft rose of a hearth seen through frost-covered glass—and from each came murmuring: voices in conversation, someone humming, someone laughing low and contented.

Tam walked beside her with his jaw set so tight she could hear his teeth. "It's a prison," he said, not to her, not to anyone. "It's a prison and it sounds like Sunday supper."

"It is a prison," said the warden-thing, whose face was pleasant and whose voice had the texture of poured honey over something with edges. "But no one here is kept against their will. That is what your friend failed to understand." It gestured with a hand that had too many graceful joints. "Comfort is also a door, mapmaker. Most people simply prefer to stay on this side of it."

Noor said nothing. She was counting doorways. Forty-one since they'd entered the cell-tier, and she had committed each one to memory the way she committed everything—the angle of its arch, the color of its light, the sound behind it. Somewhere in this honeycomb of willing souls was a woman who had sold herself thirty years ago to buy a daughter's freedom. Somewhere in this warmth was her mother.

The warden stopped before a doorway of pale green light, the color of willow leaves in spring, and made a small courteous bow that Noor's skin crawled to receive.

"She has asked for you every year on the frost-moon," it said. "She is one of our oldest residents. One of our most lucid." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "You may find that word unkind. I mean it as praise."

Then it was gone, dissolving back up the corridor like steam, and Noor stood alone before the green-lit door with Tam's hand suddenly gripping her shoulder—not holding her back, she realized. Holding her steady.

"You don't have to—" he started.

"I do." Her voice came out strange, flattened, as if it belonged to someone else. "Tam. What if she doesn't remember me?"

"What if she does," he said quietly, "and it's worse?"

She didn't answer that. She pushed the door open.

* * *

The room inside was the valley.

Not a copy of it—a rendering of it, perfect and impossible, walls painted floor to ceiling with the terraced fields above Harrow's Bend, the crooked lane down to the mill, the specific slant of evening light on the eastern ridge that Noor had spent her whole life trying and failing to capture in ink. There was even the smell: woodsmoke and wet stone and the particular green scent of the river in thaw.

And in the middle of it, at a table that was also her mother's kitchen table, sat a woman with gray-streaked hair braided back from a face Noor knew from mirrors—from the shape of her own jaw, her own deep-set eyes, her own hands.

Sela Sandoval looked up from a half-mended shirt, needle poised, and for one endless moment neither of them moved.

"Noor," her mother said. Not a question. As if Noor had simply stepped out to fetch water and come back late, thirty years late, and Sela had known all along she would.

"Mama."

The needle clattered to the table. Then Noor was across the room and her mother was rising and they collided, and Noor discovered that her body remembered things her mind had lost—the exact height of her mother's shoulder against her cheek, the way Sela's arms went around her whole torso at once, the small sound she made, a hum low in the chest, that Noor had slept to for the first four years of her life and had spent every night since trying to recall.

"I looked for you," Noor said into her mother's neck, and hated how young her voice sounded. "Every map I ever drew—I told myself I was looking. I mapped forty realms, Mama, and every one of them was me looking for you."

"I know." Sela's hands cradled the back of her head like she was still small. "I know, my love. I read it in the ledgers. Every map you sold, every commission—the Ledger-Keeper keeps copies of everything touched by court business, and your maps passed through so many tollgates." A broken laugh. "My daughter, famous in hell. I was so proud I couldn't breathe some days."

Noor pulled back far enough to see her face. Up close, the lucidity the warden had praised was visible as something terrible: her mother's eyes were clear, painfully clear, the eyes of someone who had chosen, every day for three decades, to remain awake inside the comfort. Everyone else in these glowing rooms had let the warmth dissolve them. Sela Sandoval had held herself together by will alone, and the cost of it was written in every line of her face.

"How?" Noor asked. "How are you still *you*?"

"Anger," her mother said simply. "And then, later, purpose. Anger gets you through the first ten years. Purpose gets you through the rest." She took Noor's face in her hands, studying her the way Noor studied coastlines. "You have Ashen with you. The child. She's here—in the palace, I felt her arrive, the old language rings through the stones like a bell. And you've come for her soul."

"Yes."

"Then we have very little time, and there is something you must understand, because no one else in this place will tell you, and the Ledger-Keeper certainly won't." Sela glanced toward the door, and lowered her voice though no one could possibly hear. "Sit. Both of you. Sit, and listen to your mother."

* * *

Tam dragged two chairs over, and the three of them sat at the kitchen table in the painted valley, and Sela folded her hands the way she used to when Noor was small and about to learn something difficult.

"The contracts of the under-court," she began, "are written in dragon-fire. You know this much?"

"Vess told us. The last dragon is chained beneath the mountain. Its fire is what makes the bargains burn true."

"Yes. But Vess was a cartographer, not a signatory, so she only knows the mechanics. I signed. I stood in the signing-chamber with my name on my lips and the fire licking across the parchment, and I learned things that day that the court does not teach its clerks." Her mother's eyes were very bright. "Fire is not ink, Noor. Ink is dead—you write with it, and it stays where you put it, obedient. Fire is alive. When the court writes a contract in dragon-flame, it isn't writing. It's *asking*. The flame agrees to hold the bargain. Every sale, every sanctuary, every sealed valley—it all holds because the fire consents to hold it."

Noor felt the ground shift beneath the conversation, the way it always shifted when a map turned out to be wrong. "Consent," she repeated. "The fire *consents*?"

"The dragon consents. Its fire is its will, given shape. And here is the flaw, the crack in the foundation they built their palace on:" Sela leaned forward, and her voice dropped to the register Noor remembered from fever-nights, from stories told after dark. "A chained thing can be forced to burn. It cannot be forced to *want* to. The court bound the dragon's body with chains of oath and iron, but they could never bind its willingness—and without its willingness, the fire is just heat. It's the dragon's ongoing choice, renewed moment to moment for a thousand years, that makes every contract in this mountain real."

Tam had gone pale. "So if the dragon stopped choosing—"

"Every sale unravels. Every bargain dissolves. Ashen's soul returns to her body. The valley's sanctuary evaporates. The souls in these rooms—" Sela spread her hands, and for the first time Noor saw them tremble, "—go free. All of it. Everything the court is rests on the continued consent of a creature it enslaved."

"And it hasn't stopped," Noor said slowly. "In a thousand years, it hasn't once stopped burning. Why?"

Her mother's face did something complicated—grief and admiration tangled together. "Because it doesn't know it can. Or worse—it believes it shouldn't." She rose and crossed to the painted wall, to the place where the artist had rendered the eastern ridge, and pressed her palm flat against it. "When I was brought down here to sign, they marched us past the heart-chamber. Just past—close enough to feel the heat through three doors of stone. And the dragon spoke to me. Not aloud. In the marrow. It said: *I burn because I swore to burn. An oath is an oath.*"

Noor's breath caught. "Ashen sings the old language. The tongue of promises. Vess said—she said it was the language oaths were first spoken in, before the courts corrupted it."

"Now you see it." Sela turned, and her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "The dragon was chained by a lie, Noor. Some false promise, some forged oath, something spoken in the true tongue and twisted. It has kept faith with a lie for a thousand years because no one has ever spoken to it honestly in the language it was born speaking. It cannot hear truth in the tongues of men—the court made sure of that, filled its ears with a thousand years of flattery and legal verse. But a child who speaks the old words truly? A child whose own soul was stolen by the very contracts that fire sustains?" Her mother's hand closed around Noor's wrist, grip fierce. "The dragon doesn't need to be freed, my love. It needs to *remember*. And remembering is a choice. The one choice they never figured out how to take from it."

* * *

For a long moment Noor sat perfectly still, letting the architecture of it assemble in her mind the way a coastline assembles from scattered soundings—here the flaw, here the mechanism, here the terrible elegant simplicity of it. The court's entire dominion balanced on a single point of continuing consent, and that consent could be withdrawn, and the key to withdrawal was a mute child carrying a language older than kingdoms.

It was almost too clean. Which meant, in her experience of the world beyond the valley—which now amounted to nearly three weeks, but three weeks of tollgates and ledger-keepers and palaces built of screaming—almost too clean meant a price somewhere.

"What does it cost?" she asked. "The dragon stopping. If the fire goes out—if every contract dissolves—what happens to the dragon itself?"

Her mother's expression flickered. "Ah," she said softly. "There it is. The cartographer's instinct. You always find the edge of the map." She sat back down, and took both of Noor's hands in hers. "I don't know. No one knows. It may simply live, unchained, finally free. It may be that the fire is all that's left of it—that the body died centuries ago and the flame is the dragon now, and when the flame goes out..." She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

"So we might be asking it to die."

"We might be asking it to stop suffering," Sela corrected gently. "Those can look identical from outside. But that choice belongs to the dragon, Noor—not to you, not to the court, not to me. All you can do is give it the truth and let it choose. That's more than anyone has offered it in a thousand years."

"And the valley," Tam said quietly. He'd been staring at the painted ridge line, at the crooked lane, at the little rendered village where Noor had grown up beloved and strange. "If the contracts dissolve, the sanctuary dissolves. Your whole valley opens. Magic walks in like weather. Everyone in it becomes—" he hesitated.

"Legible," Noor said. "Visible. Findable." She thought of her village, of Tam's forge, of the elders who had paid one soul per generation for safety and called it peace. "They'll hate me for it."

"They'll be *free* for it," her mother said sharply, and then gentled. "Some will hate you anyway. Freedom is frightening, my love. Safety that costs other people their children is comfortable, and comfort is the most addictive thing ever brewed." She nodded at the green-gold glow beyond the door, at all those contented murmuring rooms. "Look around. This is what comfort buys, in the end."

Noor looked at her mother—at the woman who had traded eternity in a painted paradise for a daughter's open road, and who had spent thirty years choosing to stay awake inside the trade—and understood, finally, the inheritance. Not the sacrifice. She would never repay the sacrifice; it wasn't a debt, her mother had made sure of that long ago. The inheritance was this: the refusal to mistake comfort for life. The willingness to stand awake inside whatever came.

"Mama," she said. "Come with us. When it breaks—come up with us. Come home."

Something moved through Sela's face like weather, hope and grief in the same gust. "Oh, my love." She cupped Noor's cheek. "I don't think I'll make the climb. Thirty years down here—the surface would be like looking at the sun. But that's not why you're really asking, is it? You're asking because you want someone to tell you it will work."

"Will it?"

Her mother laughed—really laughed, the sound Noor had spent her life failing to remember and recognized instantly, completely, like a key turning. "Noor Sandoval. You are asking a woman imprisoned in a palace of souls whether the plan to break the prison will work." She kissed her daughter's forehead, and when she pulled back her eyes were dry and blazing. "It will work. Because it has to, and because you're the one doing it, and because the dragon has waited a thousand years for exactly one honest sentence in its own tongue. Now go and say it."

* * *

They were three steps out the door when the corridor changed.

Noor felt it before she saw it—a pressure drop, like the hush before a storm front crosses a valley she'd have once mapped from memory. The amber lights along the cell-tier guttered. The contented murmuring behind the doors faltered, dozens of voices stumbling mid-sentence, and somewhere far above, through miles of fused and singing stone, a bell began to toll.

Tam's hand found the compass in his pocket. Even through the fabric Noor could see it straining, the needle swinging wild, pointing not north but *down*—toward the heart of the mountain, toward the debt.

"Ashen," she breathed.

The bell tolled again, closer now, or deeper, and beneath it—threaded through the stone itself, faint but unmistakable—came a sound that stopped Noor's heart and restarted it in the same instant.

A child's voice, singing in the old language. And answering it, from beneath the mountain's roots, something vast drawing a breath it had not drawn in a thousand years.

"They're already at the heart-chamber," Tam said. "How—"

"The dragon woke." Noor was already running, her mother's voice ringing in her bones like a struck bell, her mind laying the palace's passages flat behind her eyes like an opened chart. "God help anyone standing between them—the dragon *woke*."
