## 16. The Chained Dragon

### Noor

The passage down was older than the palace above it.

Noor had mapped a hundred descents in her life—stairways into crypts, switchbacks into gorge country, the long spiral of the salt-mines she'd drawn from a dying miner's memory—but nothing had prepared her for the way this corridor *descended through time*. The walls changed as they went. Near the tollgates they had been dressed stone, courtly and precise. A mile down they became rough-hewn blocks, then uncut rock veined with something that glowed faintly gold, like marrow seen through bone.

And the screaming had stopped.

That was the worst of it. For hours the fused walls of the under-court had murmured at the edges of hearing—souls woven into mortar, still half-aware, still complaining in their sleep. Now the corridor had gone quiet, and the quiet had weight, like the hush inside a held breath.

"The heart-chamber is close," Ashen signed. She had learned to speak with her hands in the weeks since Vess's house, quick gestures borrowed from no language Noor knew and all of them at once. "I can feel it. Like standing near an oven."

Noor could feel it too, though she would have chosen different words. Warmth pressed against them from ahead—not heat exactly, but *pressure*, the way a storm presses on the skin before the first drop falls. Her ink-stained fingers tingled. Every map she had ever memorized seemed to hum beneath her sternum, as if the knowledge itself recognized where they were going.

She checked the exits out of habit. Behind them: the corridor, three junctions, the tollgate of small mercies now sealed by whatever remained of the court's attention. Ahead: one door. There was always one door at the end of these places. Palaces built of souls liked their prisoners to know there was nowhere else.

"Whatever happens in there," she said, kneeling to Ashen's height, "you don't have to sing if you can't. We'll find another way."

Ashen looked at her with the grave patience children reserve for adults who are being slow. Then she reached up and touched Noor's cheek—a small, cold hand—and made the gesture Noor had come to think of as *promise*. Fist over heart, opened outward. Giving something away.

"You're sure," Noor said.

The girl nodded once.

They went forward together, and the last door opened without being touched.

* * *

### Ashen

The room was round and vast and full of fire that did not burn.

Ashen had dreamed this place before she ever saw it. In the dreams the dragon's memories came to her like weather off a far sea—the taste of mountain wind, the weight of centuries, the sound of its own name spoken in a tongue that had no other speakers left. But the dreams had not told her how *big* the grief would be.

The dragon lay coiled in the center of the chamber like a mountain that had decided to sit down. Its scales were the color of cooling embers, black shot through with veins of dull red light, and each breath it drew moved the air of the whole cavern, pulling warmth toward it and pushing it out again in slow tides. Chains ran from its limbs to the walls—chains of iron, yes, but also chains of something else, chains made of words. Ashen could see them. They hung in the air like smoke given structure, links of script linking link to link, and every so often one link would flare bright gold as some bargain somewhere above burned true, and the dragon would flinch.

It was feeding them. That was what no one said plainly. The fire in the dragon's chest was not its own anymore; it was being drawn out, link by link, to make the court's contracts hold. Every promise kept in the world above cost the dragon a measure of its flame. It had been paying for nine hundred years.

Its eye found them before they were halfway across the floor.

Ashen had expected rage. Everything about the creature was built for rage—claws like plowshares, teeth like doorposts, wings folded tight as a grudge. But the eye that watched them come was old and tired and, more than anything, *surprised*, the way a man who has been blind for decades is surprised by light.

It spoke without moving its mouth. The voice arrived inside her skull, resonant as a bell struck underwater.

*A child. And a mapmaker. Come all this way to look at me.*

Ashen stopped. Noor's hand settled on her shoulder, steady even though Ashen could feel the woman's pulse hammering through her palm.

*I know why you've come,* the dragon said. *You want me to stop burning. You want your contracts undone, your souls returned, your little valley cracked open like an egg.* A slow exhalation stirred Ashen's hair. It smelled of hot stone and, underneath, of something sweeter—oaksmoke, autumn, the inside of a hearth. *But you should understand what you are asking. I do not burn because I choose to. I burn because I was made to. If I could stop, child, I would have stopped before your grandmother's grandmother learned to walk.*

Ashen shook her head.

She stepped forward, out from under Noor's hand, until she stood in the open ground between the dragon and the dark, small as a candleflame before a bonfire. And she began to sing.

* * *

### Noor

Noor knew the song.

Not the words—she couldn't have written down a single line of it—but the *shape* of it. She had traced that shape once, years ago, in the margins of the forbidden manuscript fragment she'd copied in secret and hidden beneath the false bottom of her map drawer. The fragment had called it the First Oath, the tongue of promises, the speech from before kingdoms taught language how to lie. Scholars had argued for centuries about whether it was a language or a spell or simply the oldest prayer anyone remembered. Noor had only known that when she read it aloud, alone in her workshop, the candles had bent toward her like listeners.

Now Ashen sang it in a round stone hall, and the hall listened too.

The chains began to move.

Not the iron ones—the others. The word-chains hanging in the air shivered, and their golden flaring stuttered, and high above, Noor knew, somewhere in the palace of fused souls, contracts were flickering like lamps in a draft. Each phrase Ashen sang stripped another layer of script from the air. The girl's voice was thin and cracked from disuse, a voice that had barely learned its own strength, and yet it carried the way truth carries—without effort, without ornament, striking things open.

*What is she saying?* Noor asked silently, uselessly, and then realized the dragon could hear her thinking, because it answered.

*My own oaths,* the dragon said, and its great head lifted from its coils for the first time in Noor's living memory of anything. *The oaths I swore at the making of the world, when fire was new and I was asked to be its keeper. Listen.*

Noor listened.

*This is the oath of keeping:* Ashen sang, and the dragon translated, and the two voices wove together, the child's small and clear, the dragon's vast and low, harmony across nine hundred years. *I will burn for those who cannot warm themselves. I will hold my fire sacred and spend it only on true things. No bargain shall be forged in my flame that I have not witnessed freely.*

The last words landed in the chamber like a stone dropped into deep water, and the ripples went outward through everything.

"No," Noor breathed. She understood suddenly, completely, the way a mapmaker understands terrain—the whole shape of it at once. "You never agreed to this. The contracts. The soul-trade. You never *witnessed* any of it freely."

*The Ledger-Keeper came to me in my sleep,* the dragon said, and its voice had changed, something waking in it that made the marrow-glow in the walls pulse brighter. *Nine hundred years ago. He brought a document and a chain and he told me the chain was custom and the document was law, and I was young, and I believed that law was the same thing as truth. He wrote his bargains in my fire while I slept, and when I woke, the burning had already begun, and I could not remember agreeing to it. So I thought—I must have agreed. Memory is a poor historian when pain is its patron.*

The word-chains were unraveling now, visibly, links of script dissolving into sparks that drifted upward and went out. But the iron chains held, and the deepest ones—the ones sunk into the dragon's flesh below the scales—held hardest.

"Then why does it still bind you?" Noor demanded. "If the original oath was broken—if he lied—"

*Because a lie, repeated long enough, becomes architecture,* the dragon said. *He did not merely deceive me. He made me doubt my own first oath. He taught me that I had consented. And a prisoner who believes her chains are her own promise does not struggle, mapmaker. She keeps them polished.*

Ashen had stopped singing. She stood with her fists clenched, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face, staring up at the dragon with an expression Noor had seen on her only once before—the night the girl had first spoken the old tongue in Noor's workshop, the night her stolen soul had knocked against the walls of her body like a bird against glass.

Ashen raised both hands and signed, slowly, deliberately, knowing the dragon would see:

*You were chained by a lie. Remember it.*

The silence that followed lasted three of the dragon's breaths. Noor counted them. Each one shook the floor.

*Say it again,* the dragon whispered.

Ashen signed it again. And then, in a voice gone hoarse but steady, she said it aloud in the old tongue, the words rolling out of her like water finding a channel it had always known: *Chained-by-a-lie. Remember-it. Remember-it. REMEMBER IT.*

The dragon rose.

It took a long time—something that size unfolding after nine hundred years of coiling, joints cracking like avalanches, scales shedding centuries of dust—and Noor stood her ground while the shadow of it swept over her, vast as an eclipse. The iron chains snapped taut. The word-chains flared desperately gold, trying to reassert themselves, trying to write obedience back into the air.

But the dragon was looking at its own claws now, turning them over, and Noor saw the understanding move through its body like dawn moving across a landscape.

*I did not agree,* it said. Wonderingly. *I did not agree. Nine hundred years, and the truth of it fits in four words, and I could not say them until a child said them for me.*

It turned its head toward the ceiling of the heart-chamber, toward the palace above, toward the Ledger-Keeper and his ledgers and his courteous ancient voice, and the ember-veins beneath its scales began to glow—not the dull banked red of maintenance, but something younger, wilder, the color fire had been before anyone taught it manners.

*Little singer,* the dragon said to Ashen. *Mapmaker. Hear me now, and mark it well, because I will ask you something soon and you must answer honestly, for yourselves and not for me.*

The fire in its chest roared up behind its ribs like a tide coming in.

*When I take back my flame—when every contract written in it burns to ash, including the one that hides your valley, including the one that bought your mother's freedom and sold her soul—what will you do with a world that has no walls left?*

Above them, muffled through miles of stone, Noor heard the first alarm of the court: a sound like a thousand bells discovering fear.

The dragon gathered its fire, and waited for their answer.
