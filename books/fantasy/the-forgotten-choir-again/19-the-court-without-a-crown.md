## 19. The Court Without a Crown

The light went out of the palace the way a fever breaks—suddenly, and then all at once everywhere.

Tam felt it in his teeth first. Then in the floor beneath him, which had been warm and faintly breathing for as long as he'd been its prisoner, and which now went cold and still as riverbed stone. The walls that had murmured to each other in voices almost like his mother's fell silent. And then the screaming started—not the palace's screaming, which had been woven into its very architecture, but the screaming of ten thousand souls waking up inside bodies they had forgotten they owned.

He found Noor in the dark by shouting her name until his voice cracked.

She was kneeling at the center of what had been the throne hall, holding Ashen against her chest. The girl was crying—actually crying, loud and wet and human—and Tam understood, with a lurch, that he had never once heard Ashen make an ordinary sound. The soul that had been sold out of her was back where it belonged, and it came home weeping like anyone does.

"It worked," Noor said. She sounded stunned, as if she'd built her whole plan on arithmetic she hadn't trusted. "Tam. It actually worked."

Around them, the great hall was coming apart.

That was the only word for it. The columns of fused light—the souls the court had pressed into its architecture, load-bearing grief, pillars made of people—were loosening. They rose from the stone like sparks off a bonfire, each one taking a shape as it climbed: a young man with a soldier's shoulders, an old woman bent over invisible knitting, a child no older than Ashen who turned a slow circle in the air, looking down at the ruins of the thing she'd been nailed into, and laughed.

Tam had spent three days in the court's cells learning exactly what those walls were. He had pressed his palm against a cell door and felt, unmistakably, someone leaning back. He had heard a voice through the stone say *it isn't so bad, if you stop fighting it* in the tone of a man reciting words he'd been given. Now he watched those same souls peel free of the architecture, blinking, bewildered, and something in his chest that had been clenched since the moment the court's guards took him finally let go.

"Is everyone—" he started.

"No," Noor said quietly. "Not everyone."

She didn't have to explain. Somewhere in the crumbling halls was a woman who had waited decades to be free and would not walk out of here. Tam had met her only hours before the end—Noor's mother, lucid and gentle and already translucent at the edges, spending her last strength telling her daughter about a flaw in a contract. He had seen Noor hold her hand while the light in her thinned to nothing, and he had stood in the doorway like a coward, because there was nothing else to do.

"She went easy," Noor said, when the silence stretched too long. "She said the waiting was the hard part, and the waiting was over." A pause. "I don't know if I believe her yet. Maybe I will later."

Ashen lifted her head from Noor's shoulder and looked at Tam. The girl's eyes were different now—there was a person behind them, a whole person, where before there had been a lit window with nobody home.

"You're the friend," Ashen said. Her voice was rusty, a hinge unused for years. "You walked into a place you didn't understand because your friend was in it."

"That's just what friends do," Tam said, and his voice betrayed him on the last word.

* * *

By morning—or what passed for morning, three hundred feet under a mountain—the great hall held perhaps four hundred people.

They came up out of the cells and the corridors and the collapsed galleries in ones and twos, drawn toward the largest open space, the way survivors always are. Some were newly freed souls still wearing the shapes the court had pressed them into. Some were court functionaries—clerks, gate-wardens, the minor officials who had spent their lives stamping bargains—who stood apart in their grey uniforms, unsure whether they were prisoners or accomplices or both. A handful of the Ledger-Keeper's higher servants had simply vanished when the fire died; Tam suspected they had been contracts themselves, and had gone wherever contracts go.

Noor stood on the steps of the dead throne—which was no longer a throne, just a chair of ordinary black stone, its grandeur burned away with everything else the dragon-fire had written—and did not sit on it. That mattered. Tam noticed it, and he could see others noticing too.

"We need to talk about what happens now," she said. Her voice carried oddly well in the ruined hall, the way quiet voices sometimes do in big empty places. "I know most of you have been asleep, or worse than asleep. I know some of you don't remember your own names yet. But the mountain is open above us, and the surface is real, and nobody is keeping any of you here."

A murmur ran through the crowd. Tam, standing near the front, watched faces: hope, terror, blankness, and—this was the one that worried him—a not-small number of faces that looked frightened in the wrong direction. Frightened of leaving.

An old man near the back raised his hand like a schoolboy, then seemed embarrassed by the gesture and spoke anyway. His voice was thin. "What replaces it?"

"The what?" said Noor.

"The court." He gestured at the broken hall, the dead throne, the dark mouths of corridors. "Something has to. There are rules. There have always been rules. Debts must be tracked, bargains must be honored, someone must keep the ledger. If there is no keeper, there is only chaos."

"There was chaos before," someone else called out. "That's why the court was built."

"And look how well it worked," Tam said, before he could stop himself.

Four hundred faces turned to him. He hadn't meant to speak. He had meant, in fact, to spend this entire chapter of his life standing quietly beside Noor and letting her do the talking, because she was the mapmaker and the hero and the one whose mother had died buying all of this. But the old man's question had landed in him like a hook, and now he was walking forward, and his mouth was moving.

"I was in their cells," he said. "Three days. Long enough." He looked around at them, these people who had been furniture, fuel, mortar. "Do you know what the cells were? They weren't punishment. They were *hospitality*. Warm rooms. Soft light. Voices telling you that you'd suffered enough, that you deserved rest, that the world outside was cold and complicated and full of people who'd disappoint you. Every soul in those walls was in there because at some point, someone offered them comfort instead of truth, and they took it." He swallowed. "Some of them took it gladly. I talked to them. They thanked the court."

Silence. Somewhere in the dark, water dripped.

"So my answer to 'what replaces it' is: nothing," Tam said. "No throne. No ledger. No keeper. Because the moment you build one—even a kind one, even a fair one—you've built the same machine again with new paint. Power doesn't need to be evil to eat people. It just needs to be comfortable."

"You'd leave four hundred people with no law?" the old man said. "No order?"

"I'd leave four hundred people with each other," Tam said. "Which is harder. Which is the point."

* * *

It did not go smoothly. Tam would remember that afterward with something close to fondness—the sheer ordinary messiness of it, the first argument of the new world being a shoving match between a former tollgate clerk and a woman who had been a pillar for sixty years and had strong opinions about property.

The clerk, whose name was Dossel, wanted a council. Elected, he insisted, temporary, accountable—but a council, with authority, with the power to settle disputes and assign rations and decide who climbed out first. "Order is not tyranny," he kept saying. "Order is how four hundred people don't trample each other in the dark."

The ex-pillar, whose name was Maren and who moved like someone relearning her own joints, wanted restitution. "There are people in this room who sold us," she said, pointing at the grey-uniformed functionaries huddled by the far wall. "There are people in this room who *bought*. You want to hand them a council seat and call it a fresh start?"

"They stamped papers," Dossel protested. "Most of us stamped papers. We didn't know what—"

"You knew enough to be paid."

Tam watched Noor navigate it, and learned something about her he'd never fully understood back in the valley, watching her ink maps in her little workshop while the village pitied her gently from a polite distance. She didn't try to win the argument. She asked questions. Who among you remembers how to find food? Who remembers the way to the surface? Who can carry someone weaker than themselves? And slowly, without anyone voting on it, the shape of the thing emerged—not a government, but a caravan. Not a state, but a shared direction.

"You'll need rules anyway," Vess had told Noor once, back when Vess still had hands and a future. Tam hadn't been there, but Noor repeated it now, standing on the dead throne's steps. "Rules aren't the problem. Rules bolted to power are the problem. So make rules you can walk away from. Make agreements, not laws. If an agreement stops serving the people under it, they should be able to drop it in the road and keep walking."

"And when two people both claim the same loaf of bread?" Dossel demanded.

"Then the people around them help figure it out," Noor said. "And if they can't, then maybe one of them goes hungry today, and it's ugly, and tomorrow you try again. That's what freedom costs. I'm sorry. Nobody's selling a cheaper version."

He saw it land on the crowd in waves. Some faces closed—those were the ones that scared him, the ones that missed the warmth of the cells already, the ones who had chosen comfort so long ago they'd forgotten choosing was possible. But more faces opened. A woman near the front began, hesitantly, to laugh—a cracked, disbelieving sound—and said, "So we're going to be bad at this. Together. For years."

"Yes," Noor said. "Probably forever. Welcome to the surface."

* * *

They climbed out over three days.

The way up was the pilgrim road in reverse—the forgotten road Noor had found on the way down, the one that bypassed the tollgates, though the tollgates were just arches now, unmanned, their wards dead as cold coals. Still, people wouldn't walk through them. They detoured around, wide arcs through the dark, and Noor redrew the route on the fly, sketching in the dirt with a stick, memorizing, adjusting. Tam carried Ashen on his shoulders for the first mile until the girl wriggled down and insisted on walking, because, she said with the gravity only children can summon, "I have legs. I want to use them going the right direction for once."

The mountain let them go grudgingly. There were collapses behind them—whole galleries folding in on themselves as the last of the fused souls departed, the palace eating itself now that there was no one left inside to be the palace. On the second day a tremor dropped a staircase they'd used an hour before, and after that they stopped looking back.

Tam walked near the rear, because that was where he'd decided he belonged. Someone had to count heads. Someone had to notice when the old man with the schoolboy gestures sat down and couldn't get up, and offer an arm. Someone had to sit with the grey-clerks at night, because the other exiles wouldn't, and because Tam had noticed that Dossel cried in his sleep, quietly, like a man trying not to wake anyone.

"You hate us," Dossel said, on the last night underground.

"Yeah," Tam said. "Some."

"But you're sitting here."

"Somebody has to make sure you don't decide the dark is easier." He poked the small fire they'd built from scrap wood—real wood, dragged down from the surface world generations ago and hoarded by clerks who'd never questioned why they needed it. "That's the whole job, as far as I can tell. Nobody watches the watchers. Everybody watches everybody. Annoying, inefficient, and the only thing I've ever heard of that works."

Dossel wiped his face with a grey sleeve. "What happens when we get up there? To the surface. People will know what we were."

"People will know what you *are*," Tam said. "That part's yours to write. First blank page you've ever had. Try not to sell it."

* * *

They came out of the mountain at dawn.

Not the valley's dawn—Tam had left the valley behind him and felt every step of it, the compass in his pocket swinging wild and then, after the fire died, hanging perfectly still, its debt-pointing needle finally at rest. This was the wide world's dawn, over foothills Noor had mapped a hundred times and never touched, and it came up gold and enormous and completely indifferent to anything anyone had suffered beneath the ground.

Four hundred people stood on the mountainside and looked at the sky.

Some wept. Some turned around immediately and had to be gently turned back. Maren, the ex-pillar, knelt and put her palms flat against actual grass and stayed there so long that Tam finally crouched beside her.

"Take your time," he said.

"Sixty years," she said. Her voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "I was a wall for sixty years, and I could hear everything, and I forgot the word for grass. It came back to me just now. Grass." She looked up at him, and her eyes were fierce and wet. "Don't let them build another court. Whatever they decide up there, whatever seems reasonable, whatever the emergency is—don't let them."

"I'm one voice."

"You're the one who argued first," she said. "First is worth a hundred."

They began the descent in loose ranks, carrying nothing but each other, which turned out to be heavier and lighter than luggage in equal measure. Noor walked at the front with Ashen's hand in hers, and every few hundred yards she stopped and marked something—a spring, a stand of trees, a stretch of road that would flood in spring—and Tam realized she was already drawing. Not escape routes this time. Not roads out. Roads *in*, roads through, a map of the world as a place people actually lived rather than a place people fled.

He caught up with her at a bend where the whole valley country spread out below, green and smoke-threaded and impossibly ordinary.

"Your valley's down there somewhere," he said. "Unsealed now. Open to all of this." He nodded back at the mountain, at the ragged line of four hundred souls picking their way down into a world that had never asked for them.

"I know."

"Scared?"

"Terrified," Noor said, and smiled, and it was the realest smile he'd ever seen on her. "But I spent my whole life mapping doors I couldn't open. I'm done with locked things, Tam. All of them. Even the ones that kept me safe."

Below them, at the foot of the mountain, the road split—one branch running west toward distant towns, one running south and east, toward a green fold in the hills that Tam recognized from a hundred maps he'd watched her draw: the valley that refuses.

The exiles paused at the fork, uncertain, four hundred people with no crown and no ledger and no idea, waiting to see which way their first free step would fall.

And Noor Sandoval pointed south, toward home, and said, "This way. I know a place with room."
