## 1. The Weight of Small Charms

The morning Mirabel Yarrow's world began to end, she was selling luck.

Not luck itself—never that, not in Vellamere, where the criers had spent three years teaching the city that magic was a disease and its carriers were plague-rats—but the *appearance* of it. Luck-knots braided from river-reed and red thread, each one tied with a pattern any grandmother in the Saltmarket could name. Weather-charms of hollowed gourd and feather, pretty nonsense that farmers bought to hang over their doors because their grandmothers had done the same. Folk craft. Harmless craft. The kind of thing a woman with ink-stained fingers and a cartographer's squint might make when maps were slow and rent was due.

She sat behind her little trestle table in Copperlane Row with her hood up against the drizzle and her hands busy, and she watched the city bleed at its edges the way it always did before dawn fully took hold.

That was the secret no one paid for: Vellamere did not end where its walls ended. The city had grown like a lichen over something older, and in the grey hours—between the last star and the first bell—the seams showed. A stairwell off Tanner's Wynd that went down eleven steps and came out on a moor that belonged to no kingdom. A doorway in the fish-market that opened, one night in nine, onto a corridor of standing stones under a sky with too many moons. Mirabel had found seventeen such places in three years. She had mapped every one of them in a cipher of her own devising, drawn in the margins of honest charts of honest streets, hidden in plain sight the way everything about her life was hidden in plain sight.

Her mother would have laughed at the caution. Her mother had never hidden anything in her life except, in the end, an atlas—and even that she'd hidden badly enough to be caught.

Mirabel pushed the thought down where it lived, in the cellar of her chest, and tied another knot.

"Morning, Yarrow." Old Tam from the cooperage, broad as a barrel, dropping two coppers into her dish. "One for the wife's knee, if you've got it."

"I've got reed and thread and good wishes, Tam. The knee is between her and the saints."

"The saints listen better when there's a knot involved." He grinned, gap-toothed, and shuffled off with his charm swinging from his fist like a talisman. He believed in it, or believed in believing in it, which was nearly as good. That was the bargain Mirabel had made with this city: she gave them nothing but string and confidence, and they gave her anonymity, and everyone walked away satisfied.

It had worked for three years. She had told herself, every morning for three years, that it was still working.

Then the bells began.

Not the market bell, not the hour bell—a third bell, higher, thinner, the one mounted above the Provost's Hall that Mirabel had heard exactly twice before and had taught herself, both times, to forget. The crowd along Copperlane Row went quiet in patches, silence spreading like frost, and heads turned toward the square where the royal criers stood on their steps in the crown's grey-and-gold.

"Hear ye," the nearest crier called, and his voice carried wrong, too far, too clear, as though the words themselves were charmed—which meant the criers were warded, which meant this proclamation mattered. "By decree of the Regent's Council, in the matter of unlawful workings: a bounty of fifty crowns is set upon any person found practicing the gifted arts within the city or its liberties. Fifty crowns for information leading to seizure. One hundred for—" he paused, and read the next line with the flat carelessness of a man reading a grocery list, "—for the capture of a practitioner unharmed."

*Unharmed.* As though harm were a risk rather than the point.

The crowd did not gasp. That was the terrible thing; Mirabel had expected gasps, outrage, some collective flinch of conscience. Instead there was only a shuffling, a recalculation, fifty pairs of eyes suddenly measuring their neighbors. The woman beside Mirabel's table drew her child closer without seeming to know she'd done it. Somewhere down the row, someone said, "Fifty crowns," in the wondering tone of a man hearing his own fortune described.

Mirabel kept her hands moving. Knot, loop, draw tight. Her fingers knew the work; her mind was already running the arithmetic of escape—how much coin, which seam-door, how far the moor-stair would take her before the wards caught up. She had a bag half-packed beneath her cot. She had always, always had a bag half-packed beneath her cot. Invisibility, she had learned, was not a state but a discipline, maintained daily like prayer.

She was so busy calculating that she almost missed old Maren Cobb coming up the row with her basket of dried herbs.

Maren had taken Mirabel in her first winter in Vellamere, when Mirabel had been sleeping in the tannery loft with frost in her boots and fever in her lungs. Three nights Maren had sat up with her, feeding her willow-bark tea and nettle broth, humming the low wordless songs that hedge-witches hummed over the sick—not working anything, just *humming*, the way a mother rocks a child, the gift leaking out of her the way warmth leaks from a hearth because she had never learned to dam it and wouldn't have wanted to. Maren Cobb could coax a blight off an apple tree and a fever out of a child, and she charged nothing for either, and she had never once asked Mirabel what she was or why she flinched at the sound of the Provost's bell.

"Fifty crowns," Maren said now, setting her basket down beside Mirabel's table with a huff. "For folk like us, eh? I should turn myself in and retire rich." She laughed, dry as seed-pods, and Mirabel's blood went cold, because Maren said *us* the way she said everything—openly, warmly, without a flicker of the fear that should have been sitting on her tongue.

"Maren." Mirabel's voice came out low. "You shouldn't joke."

"Why not? Half the street knows I brew fever-tea. They've drunk it. Their children have drunk it." Maren's eyes, pale and watery and utterly without guile, crinkled at her. "They're not going to give up the woman who pulled their babies through winter-fever for fifty crowns, girl. People are better than proclamations."

*People are better than proclamations.* Mirabel wanted to believe it. She wanted it so badly her chest ached, and that wanting frightened her more than the bell did, because wanting things was how you got caught.

"You should come with me," she heard herself say. "Today. There are ways out of the city that aren't on any map the Provost has."

Maren looked at her for a long moment, and something knowing moved behind the wateriness of her eyes—something that suggested she had known for three years exactly what kind of maps Mirabel really drew. "And leave my garden? My bees?" She patted Mirabel's hand, papery and warm. "I'm an old woman, Mirabel. Old women don't run well. You run. That's your gift, maybe, same as mine's the tea. Run well, and don't look back, and don't you dare feel guilty about it."

But Mirabel didn't run. Not that morning. She told herself it was because the roads would be watched by nightfall and daylight was safer for travel, which was true, and also because packing took time, which was false, since her bag was half-packed and had been for three years. Mostly she sat in her room above the chandler's shop and did the coward's arithmetic: *if I go to her, I mark her house. If I stay away, I'm just a charm-seller who vanished.* She told herself that distance was protection. She told herself a great many things while the light moved across the floorboards and died.

*

They came for Maren Cobb at noon, in the full bright middle of the market day, when the streets were thickest and no one could pretend they hadn't seen.

Mirabel saw. She was three stalls down, buying nothing, watching through the gap between a hanging rack of copper pots, and she would carry the sight of it for the rest of her life.

There were six of them—witch-hunters in the crown's livery, grey cloaks, iron badges shaped like a closed eye. And behind them, apart from them, dressed in the same grey but wearing it differently, walked a man Mirabel had never seen before: tall, dark-haired, moving through the crowd with the unhurried certainty of someone who already knew how this would end. He didn't shove. He didn't need to. People parted around him the way water parts around a keel.

Maren was at her herb stall. She saw them coming—of course she did; the whole row went silent in that spreading-frost way—and she did not run. She stood very straight among her bundles of yarrow and feverfew, and she looked at the hunters the way a teacher looks at boys throwing stones.

"Marin Cobb?" the tall man said. His voice was quiet, and it carried anyway. "Inquisitor Corvane. Crown Office of Inquiry. You're to come answer questions regarding unlawful practice."

"I've broken no law but kindness," Maren said, "and I'll answer for that gladly."

Something flickered across Corvane's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning—and later, much later, Mirabel would understand what it was. But in that moment she only watched as the hunters took Maren's arms, and Maren did not struggle, and her basket went over, and dried herbs scattered across the wet cobbles, feverfew and yarrow and rue, the smell of them rising sharp and green like a protest.

The street watched. Dozens of people whose children Maren had healed, whose fevers she had sung down, whose apple trees she had saved—they watched, and they stepped back, and they made room. No one met anyone else's eyes. A man Mirabel recognized, a tanner whose daughter Maren had pulled back from death's door two winters past, picked up one of the fallen herb-bundles, looked at it, and put it quietly in his pocket as though tidying litter.

Fifty crowns, Mirabel thought. Or maybe not even that. Maybe just the looking-away, which cost nothing and was worth everything to a frightened man.

Maren's gaze swept the crowd once as they led her toward the Provost's Hall—and passed over Mirabel without stopping, without a flicker, without giving her away. Whether Maren simply didn't see her, or saw her and chose blindness as a final gift, Mirabel would never know. It felt like absolution. It felt worse than condemnation.

The crowd closed behind the hunters like water behind a boat. The market resumed. Someone laughed at a joke. A child cried for a honey-bun. The city performed its own innocence, and Mirabel stood among the scattered herbs on the cobbles and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, that her three years of safety had never been safety at all.

It had been a subsidy. Paid for, all this time, by women like Maren Cobb—by everyone loud enough, kind enough, visible enough to draw the crown's eye while quieter gifts slipped past in the shadow they cast. Invisibility was not free. It had never been free. She had simply never seen the bill.

Mirabel knelt and gathered the herbs, handful by handful, her hands steady and her vision blurred. Rue for repentance. Yarrow, her own namesake, for staunching wounds. Feverfew, which Maren had brewed for her that first winter, which had probably saved her life, which had bought the crown three years of a hidden cartographer.

She could run tonight. Bag packed, seam-doors mapped, gone before the second bell. That was the sensible thing. That was the thing Maren herself had told her to do.

But there was a name in Maren's story, a name the Inquisitor had spoken with a strange weight, and there was a confiscation ledger in the Provost's archive that Mirabel had read upside-down through a clerk's window two years ago, and there was an atlas—her mother's atlas—with annotations in her mother's hand that Mirabel had memorized long ago and never dared believe: *the Choir sleeps beneath the mountain, and the mountain remembers.*

Running alone had kept her alive. It had not kept Maren Cobb alive, or safe, or free.

Mirabel stood in the emptying market with an armful of dead woman's herbs and made the decision that would unravel her careful, invisible, subsidized life thread by thread.

She was going to steal her mother's map back.
