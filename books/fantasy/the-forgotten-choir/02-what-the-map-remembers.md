## 2. What the Map Remembers

The atlas had been in the Confiscated Goods House for three years, and Mirabel had walked past it every day of those three years without letting herself look at it.

That was the discipline. That was the whole trick of survival in Vellamere: you did not look at the things that would kill you to want. She had learned it the way she'd learned to draw a straight line freehand—by failing at it a hundred times until her hand forgot how to tremble.

But tonight the city was burning in small, orderly ways. Tonight there were bonfires in the Lantern Quarter where hedge-witches' charms were being fed to the flames by men in grey who wore the crown's sunburst on their breasts and called it purification. Tonight Old Sable's stall was a smear of ash and trampled knotwork, and Sable herself was somewhere in a wagon with barred windows, being carried toward whatever the Inquisition called mercy.

Tonight, Mirabel looked.

The Confiscated Goods House sat behind the Assay Office like a tooth behind a lip of gum—narrow, windowless above the ground floor, its inventory logged by clerks who went home when the bells rang Vespers and did not return until Prime. The lock was honest iron, which was to say it was a conversation, and Mirabel had always been good at talking to locks. Not magic—never magic, not anymore, not since the criers had read the bounty from the palace steps with their voices flattened into ritual—but patience, a bent pin, and the small unglamorous knowledge that every locksmith in Vellamere bought his blanks from the same chandler's son who owed her money for a weather charm that had actually worked.

Inside, the air smelled of tallow and dust and other people's ruined lives. Wedding rings in drawers. Grimoires stacked spine-up like cordwood, waiting for the pyre. And on the third shelf of the cartography case, wrapped in oilcloth and tagged with a clerk's neat hand—*Yarrow, M. (deceased). Effects, one (1) atlas, water-damaged*—

Her mother's atlas.

Mirabel took it down and did not open it. Not yet. Opening it here, in this box of dead people's belongings, would be too much like grief, and grief made noise. She wrapped it against her chest beneath her coat, where it sat heavy as a second heart, and she let herself out, and she pulled the door shut behind her so gently that even the lock seemed to forgive her.

Then she ran.

* * *

She ran the way water runs—downhill, through gaps, along the routes no one watches because no one needs to go there. The tannery sluices. The underside of the Salt Bridge where the beggars' candles guttered in niches. The streets were wrong tonight; she could feel it in the way the city's edges had gone soft and hungry, the unmapped places bleeding further in than usual, as if Vellamere itself were nervous. A doorway on Cooper's Lane opened onto a corridor that did not exist, and she saw a man step into it, drawn like a moth, and she did not stop him because stopping him would mean speaking, and speaking meant being heard.

By the time the bells rang Compline she was in the cellar under the derelict rope-walk by the river, the one Bette used for cargo that had never officially existed. She lit one candle, shielded it with her cupped hands out of three years of habit, and finally unwrapped the atlas.

It was worse than she remembered. Water had bloated the leather, silvered the pages, eaten whole coastlines into ghost-shapes. Her mother's maps had been famous once—not the printed ones, but the true ones, the ones drawn in ink mixed with something Mirabel had never been told the name of, ink that showed what was *there* rather than what was agreed upon. The crown had burned most of them. This one they had catalogued instead, because a drowned book looks like nothing, and clerks are only ever as perceptive as their forms require.

Mirabel turned the pages with wet fingers.

And then she saw them.

Annotations. Tiny ones, crowded into the margins in her mother's hand—or rather, in her mother's *later* hand, the cramped script of the last year, the year before the wagons came. They threaded through the maps like roots through soil. On the chart of the Greywether Mountains, beneath a range the royal surveyors had left blank and labeled *unproductive*, her mother had written:

*They did not die. They slept. The Choir sleeps beneath the third shoulder, where the stone remembers singing. I could not wake them. Perhaps someone with lighter hands.*

Below that, smaller, almost erased:

*M. — if you are reading this, do not come alone, and do not come angry. Anger wakes them wrong.*

Mirabel sat very still. The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Somewhere above, boots crossed the rope-walk floor, paused, moved on.

*The Forgotten Choir.* She had thought it a story—a smuggler's tale, a comfort told to frightened children of the gift: *the old mages didn't all burn; some of them lie sleeping under the mountains, waiting.* She had believed it the way you believe in fair weather, knowing better.

But her mother had not written stories. Her mother had written *coordinates*.

She should run. That was the sensible thing. South, past the salt marshes, over the border into Tessel where the Inquisition's writ thinned to nothing. Alone. Alone was fast, alone was quiet, alone was the only way she had survived three years while Sable paid for her invisibility with a wagon ride and iron bracelets.

Alone was also what her mother had done, in the end. And it had gotten her mother a tag on a shelf: *deceased. Effects, one (1) atlas.*

Mirabel closed the book and held it against her chest and felt, for the first time since the criers spoke, something that was not fear.

*Do not come alone.*

"Damn you," she said softly, to a woman eleven years dead. "You knew I'd have no choice."

* * *

Inquisitor Dain Corvane stood in the ruins of Old Sable's market stall and let the cold work its way through him, because he had found that cold helped.

The witch's charms lay in a sodden heap where his juniors had swept them, awaiting the fire. He picked one up—a luck-knot, clumsy, human, smelling of river mud and cheap twine—and turned it over in his gloved fingers. Beneath the twine, faint as breath on glass, he felt the hum. The same hum he had spent twenty years pretending lived only in the guilty. The same hum that lived, if he was honest, in the marrow of his own bones, coiled and gagged and obedient.

*You are a key that has sworn never to turn,* his old teacher had told him, the day Corvane took the grey cloak. *The throne will use you precisely because of what you refuse to be.*

He had believed that was honor. He had believed it for twenty years, through forty burnings, and he believed it still, mostly, the way a man believes in a floor he is falling through.

"She had help," said Novice Ellery beside him, young and eager and smelling of lamp oil. "The neighbor girl. Yarrow. Mapseller. We've flagged her."

Something in Corvane's chest went tight and cold, colder than the night deserved. He had reviewed the Yarrow file himself, months ago, and closed it. A woman who drew maps and sold knots. Nothing. He had *wanted* it to be nothing, and he had not asked himself why.

"Burn the stock," he said. "Leave the girl to me."

Ellery blinked. "Sir? Protocol says—"

"Protocol," Corvane said, "says many things." He set the luck-knot down gently, the way you set down something you have no right to hold, and walked away before his face could tell the novice anything true.

Somewhere in Vellamere, a woman with her mother's eyes was running. He intended to find her. He told himself it was duty. He was growing tired of how often he had to tell himself things.

* * *

Anselm was praying when she found him, which was how he did everything now—praying and drinking in roughly equal measure, sometimes simultaneously, in the back room of the Drowned Bell where the tavern-keeper owed him silence for sins Anselm had never named. He had been a priest of the Concordance once, before he'd stood up during a Purification service and said the unsayable—that the gift was not sin but breath, and that a god who made lungs could not condemn breathing. They had taken his vestments, his voice in the diocese, and nearly his faith. Nearly.

"You're Yarrow's girl," he said, without opening his eyes. "Your mother confessed to me once. Did you know that?"

"No," said Mirabel, who had known.

"She never said what for. Just that she'd drawn something she shouldn't, and hidden it somewhere the fire wouldn't reach." He opened his eyes at last, bloodshot and steady. "You've got it, don't you. I can see it in how you're standing. Like the building might fall."

"I need to leave the city tonight," Mirabel said. "North. Into the Greywethers."

"That's suicide."

"Yes."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he reached under the pew and brought out a bottle and a bundle—an old traveling cloak, a pilgrim's staff with a hollow core he had never explained to her and she had never asked about.

"Well," said Anselm, standing, joints cracking like green wood. "I've been looking for a sin worth committing."

* * *

Wren came with them because Wren had already decided to, apparently sometime before Mirabel arrived.

The child was nine years old and sitting on the steps of the Salt Bridge shrine at midnight, cross-legged, feeding crumbs to pigeons that were not entirely pigeons—the unmapped bleeding through again, thin as gauze, and the birds had too many joints in their wings. Most people couldn't see it. Wren could shape it. Mirabel watched her do it: a small gesture, two fingers drawing a circle, and the gauze-thin edge of elsewhere folded politely aside so the birds became ordinary doves and flew off, unremarkable.

"You're leaking," Wren said, looking up at Mirabel. "You've been leaking since the bridge. You should bind your edges."

"I don't—" Mirabel began.

"You do. Everyone does, when they're scared. Mama taught me before they took her." Wren said it flatly, the way children say unbearable things, and picked up a canvas satchel that had clearly been packed for days. "I'm coming with you. The lady with the boat said you'd come past here."

"The lady with the—"

"Bette. She talks about you. Says your mother could read water like scripture." Wren stood, all four feet six inches of her, and looked at Mirabel with an expression far too old for the face wearing it. "Are you going to argue? Grown-ups always argue first. It wastes time."

Mirabel looked at this child—this small, hunted, impossible child who had just casually folded reality like linen—and felt the last of her resistance go out of her like heat from an open door.

"No," she said. "No arguing. Stay where I can see you."

* * *

Old Bette's boat was called the *Improbable*, which Bette claimed was a legal technicality. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a mooring post, and she took one look at Mirabel climbing down the ladder with an atlas under her coat, a defrocked priest behind her, and a mage-child holding her hand, and she laughed until she had to wipe her eyes.

"Three years I've watched you walk past my pier," Bette said, spitting over the rail for luck. "Three years, playing at being nobody. Your mother did the same, you know. Right up until she stopped."

"She died," Mirabel said.

"She *stopped*," Bette corrected, hauling on the tiller, and the *Improbable* slid out into the black river with the burning city reflected in pieces on its surface. "There's a difference, girl, and it matters. Stopping means you finally decided what you're carrying." She nodded at the lump under Mirabel's coat. "So. What is it?"

Mirabel unwrapped the atlas and held it open to the page with the Greywethers, to the blank space the royal surveyors had called unproductive, to her mother's cramped, buried words.

Bette read them twice. When she looked up, all the laughter had gone out of her face, and what remained was older than the river.

"The Choir," she said quietly. "Sweet saints below. It's real."

"It's coordinates," Mirabel said. "It's a chance. I don't know what it is yet. But the Inquisition burned Sable today, and tomorrow they'll burn more, and I can't—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she kept going anyway. "I can't run alone anymore. It turns out that's not running. It's just dying slowly with extra steps."

Bette studied her for a long moment, the way sailors study weather.

"Well then," she said, and put the tiller hard over, and the *Improbable* leaned north. "Best get you to the mountains before the ice, and best pray your mother's lighter hands skipped a generation."

Behind them, Vellamere burned in small, orderly ways. Ahead, the Greywethers stood black against the stars, holding their song behind stone.

And on the road behind—far back, at the city gate, where the torches of the Inquisition mustered for pursuit—a man in a grey cloak paused, felt the hum in his own silenced bones answer something in the north, and turned his horse toward the mountains.
