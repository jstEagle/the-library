## 3. The Hunt Beneath the Mountain

The mountain had a name once, Mirabel was sure of it, but the map her mother left behind called it only *the Vault*, as if naming it would be an invitation. She felt that reluctance in her own hands now, ink-stained and trembling on the wheel of Old Bette's cart as it rattled up the switchback road. Behind them Vellamere burned its bonfires of confession—she could smell the smoke even here, sweet with the resin they used to make the pyres burn clean—and ahead the peak rose black against a bruised dawn, wearing its glaciers like a hood pulled low.

"Stop looking at it like that," Bette said from the driver's bench. "Like it's going to bite."

"Everything I've ever needed has bitten me."

The child Wren slept against Anselm's shoulder in the cart's bed, her small fingers curled around a stub of chalk she refused to surrender even unconscious, drawing invisible lines on the boards. The priest had given her his cloak and kept only his cassock's memory, the pale line of skin at his throat where the collar used to sit. He caught Mirabel watching and offered the smile of a man who had learned to find grace in ditches.

"How far?" he asked.

"Half a league to the treeline. Then we walk." She unrolled the stolen atlas across her knees, careful of the dew. Her mother's annotations crawled over the official survey like ivy over a fence: *the door remembers blood*; *do not trust the dry galleries*; *sing, and be answered*. And beneath all of it, in handwriting gone brown with age, the words that had brought them this far: *the Choir sleeps where the water forgets to fall.*

"They'll have riders on the road by now," Bette said. "Corvane doesn't send one hawk when he can send six."

Mirabel folded the map along its old creases. "Then we'd best be worth six hawks."

* * *

Dain Corvane rode with his gauntlets off, which his hunters knew better than to remark upon. His bare hands looked ordinary against the reins—scarred, blunt, a workman's hands—but he could feel the mountain before he saw it, the way a man feels a storm in his teeth. Pressure. A hum beneath the world's floorboards. Something down there was dreaming loudly enough to leak.

"She went to ground in the high passes," said Ferren, his lieutenant, reining alongside. "The smuggler's cart was seen at Millbrook. Two days' lead, no more."

Two days. Corvane did the arithmetic the way he did everything: coldly, and twice. In two days the Yarrow woman would either find what her mother had hidden or die trying, and either way the Crown would have its answer to the question that had ended the last war and started this quiet, suffocating peace: *where do the gifted go when they run?*

He had asked himself that question for twenty years without daring to answer it honestly.

"Inquisitor?" Ferren prompted.

"Split the column. Take the eastern scree; I'll take the road." He flexed his bare fingers, feeling the suppressed thing inside him turn over like a chained animal hearing a familiar bell. "And Ferren—if you find her first, do not touch her. Bind her, gag her if you must, but do not lay hands on her skin."

Ferren's eyes flicked to the bare hands, then away, professionally blind. "As you say."

Corvane turned his horse up the mountain road and let himself, for one unguarded mile, remember what it was to want something other than orders.

* * *

The treeline took them at dusk, and the betrayal came an hour after.

His name was Tam, and he had joined them at Millbrook—a ferryman's son with the gift of finding lost things, which had seemed harmless until the criers made it worth silver. He was nineteen and he cried easily and Mirabel had trusted him the way you trust weather: pleasantly, until it changes. They were making camp in a hollow of granite when Wren sat bolt upright, chalk clutched to her chest.

"Someone's coming," she whispered. "Someone loud."

Tam was already on his feet, already backing toward the dark. "I'm sorry," he said, and the terrible thing was that he meant it, tears cutting through the grime on his face. "They have my sister. They said—they said if I brought them to you, they'd let her go. Mirabel, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry—"

Bette moved faster than a woman her age had any right to, but not fast enough. Torches bloomed on the ridgeline above them, and the crack of a binding-net launcher split the night, and then there was only running.

"The lower galleries," Mirabel gasped, hauling Wren onto her hip. "Anselm, the map—the case, waterproof—"

"I have it, I have it—"

They went down instead of away, down the goat track into the mountain's root, because Mirabel had spent three years learning the one lesson the Crown could not confiscate: *when the surface belongs to your enemies, go where maps end.* The entrance her mother had marked was a seam in the rock behind a curtain of frozen waterfall, and it opened at their touch—or at hers, the stone warming under her palm like a sleeping animal recognizing a hand—and then the mountain swallowed them whole.

Behind them, torchlight found the seam too. And water began to move somewhere far below, waking.

* * *

The flooded galleries were her mother's masterpiece and her mother's warning both. Mirabel stood in the first dry chamber with the atlas open and her heart hammering, reading annotations by the light of Anselm's tallow stub while the sound of pursuit echoed down the upper passages.

"*Do not trust the dry galleries,*" she read aloud. "She means the ones above us. They're a maze—Corvane will lose hours in them. But these—" her finger traced the flooded levels, blue-inked channels threading through the mountain's bones— "these flood and drain on the old enchantments. Twice a day the water withdraws. If we move with the drain, we walk the riverbed itself."

"And if we mistime it?" Bette asked.

"We drown in the dark." Mirabel looked at Wren. "Can you feel the water?"

The girl pressed her palm flat to the wet stone and closed her eyes. "It's... counting," she said wonderingly. "Like breathing. Big breath out soon."

"Then we breathe with it."

They moved through the draining dark for what felt like a night and half a life: wading knee-deep through retreating water, climbing galleries slick as glass, Mirabel navigating by her mother's ink and her own gift—the lines she drew in the air with a wet fingertip glowing faintly and holding, little luminous threads marking the true path through the mountain's labyrinth. Anselm murmured prayers that might have been curses or lullabies. Bette cursed fluently in three languages. Wren counted the water's breaths aloud, and twice her count saved them, pulling them into side chambers as the galleries refilled behind them with a roar like applause.

When Corvane's hunters finally fought their way down, following Tam's broken directions, they found only drowned passages and glowing threads fading like dying stars.

* * *

Corvane came alone past the last gallery. He ordered Ferren to hold the junction, and Ferren, who had learned not to ask, held it.

He followed the light. That was the truth he would never put in any report: he did not track her; he followed the small bright lines she left in the air, drawn to them the way he had been drawn to every forbidden beautiful thing in his gray serviceable life. The passages narrowed, warmed. The hum he had felt in his teeth for two days became a chord, became voices just below hearing, became—

The threshold.

It was a cavern like the inside of a struck bell, and it was full of sleepers. Hundreds of them, thousands, lying in alcoves cut into the living rock, each wrapped in a cocoon of soft golden light, each breathing so slowly that breath became geology. Men and women and children, robed and armored and naked as birth, faces from no century he could name. The last free mages of the world, asleep since the war, dreaming together.

Mirabel stood among them with Wren's hand in hers, and she did not turn when his boots rang on the stone.

"You should have run east," Corvane said. "Past the salt marshes. No one hunts the marshes."

"You know that how?"

The question hung there. He looked at his own bare hands, at the fine tremor in them that had nothing to do with cold, and twenty years of discipline came apart like wet paper.

"Because I tried it," he said. "When I was fourteen. Before they found me. Before the Inquisition taught me that a gift is a wound and the Crown is the bandage." He stepped closer, and the sleepers' light moved across his face like water. "I am what you are, Mirabel Yarrow. I have been what you are since I was a boy, and I have spent my whole life helping bury people like us because the alternative was the fire. So I am telling you, as the only honest thing I have ever said: wake them, and the Crown burns this mountain and everyone in it. Leave them sleeping, and you can still run. I'll give you the hour. I swear it on whatever I have left."

Wren's grip tightened in Mirabel's hand. Somewhere above, boots and shouting, the net-launchers cranking.

Mirabel looked at the sleepers. At her mother's map, open in Anselm's shaking hands. *Sing, and be answered.*

"You're offering me safety," she said quietly. "Invisibility. Someone else pays for it. That's the whole architecture of this kingdom, Inquisitor—it runs on people paying for other people's hiding." She stepped forward, toward the nearest alcove, toward a sleeper whose face was young and whose light was old. "My mother mapped this place and never came back. I used to think that meant it killed her. Now I think she stayed. Now I think someone has to be the witness."

"Mirabel—"

"Wake up," she said—to the sleeper, to the mountain, to the chord beneath the world—and she sang the only note she knew, the one her mother had written in the margin in brown ink, a single syllable like a key turning.

The light changed.

It did not flare; it *woke*, rolling outward from her voice through alcove after alcove after alcove, ten thousand cocoons kindling in sequence like a city lighting at dusk, and the sleepers breathed in—all together, one vast inhalation that pulled the air from the cavern and gave it back warm—and the mountain answered. Every thread Mirabel had ever drawn, every line of her mother's atlas, blazed into standing radiance, a cathedral of light built out of cartography, and in that light Corvane fell to his knees not in defeat but in recognition, the chained animal in his chest singing back for the first time in twenty years.

The nearest sleeper opened her eyes. They were the color of deep water, and they were looking at Mirabel.

"You're late," the sleeper said, in a voice like the drain of the galleries, like the turn of tides. "But you brought the map. Good. We have been dreaming of the places it leads—and, child, there is something at the end of your mother's unfinished road that you must see before the Crown's fires reach us. Wake the rest gently. We are going to need every witness we have."

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the first alarm horns of Vellamere began to sound.
