## 4. A Chorus for the Unmapped

The mountain had not stopped singing.

Three days since Mirabel woke them, and the light still moved through the vault like slow water, pooling in the hollows of the stone, rising and falling with a rhythm she could feel in her back teeth. The Choir slept no longer. They walked the flooded galleries barefoot, touching the walls as though greeting old friends, and their voices—when they spoke—carried the strange doubled quality of people who had been dreaming for thirty years and were not entirely sure they had stopped.

There were forty-one of them. Mirabel had counted twice, because the first time she assumed grief was making her arithmetic generous. Forty-one mages who had lain down at the end of the last war and asked the mountain to hold them until the world grew safe again. They were gaunt and gray-haired and some of them wept openly at the sight of rain through the upper vents, and not one of them—not one—would take up a weapon.

"We did not sleep to learn killing," said the eldest, a woman called Sera Voss whose hands shook when they weren't folded, as if even stillness required discipline. She had been a battle-mage once; her left arm ended at the elbow in a scar of old light. "We slept so that no one would ever have to be what we were. You woke us into a world that hunts our kind. I understand why you hoped we'd be an army." Her eyes, pale as frost, held Mirabel without cruelty. "But an army is only a louder argument. We mean to give testimony."

"Testimony," Anselm repeated that evening, turning the word over like a coin he suspected was clipped. He sat with his back against the Choir's great door, his defrocked hands laced over his knee. "They'll hang you for it. Words against the crown are worth less than words against a butcher."

"They hanged us for silence too," said Wren, who was eleven and sharpened her observations the way other children whittled sticks. She sat cross-legged on a crate of Bette's smuggled goods, ink-stained fingers tracing figures in the dust. "If you're going to be guilty either way, better to say something true first."

Mirabel looked at her mother's atlas, open on her knees to the page where the annotations crawled toward the Choir's name, and felt the weight of what came next settle onto her shoulders like a wet cloak. Her mother had drawn maps of everything—the unmapped realms bleeding through the city's edges, the drowned galleries, the roads that existed only on Tuesdays—and every map had been an act of witness. *Here is something real that they told you wasn't.* She had died for it, eventually. Or worse than died; the atlas never said.

"All right," Mirabel said. "Then we testify."

* * *

They went down from the mountain in ones and twos, over nine days, dressed as pilgrims and peddlers and one improbable troupe of traveling musicians—Bette's idea, and Bette's wagon, and Bette's genuinely terrible fiddle playing to cover the sound of forty-one voices learning a song they had composed in stasis, note by note, across three decades of shared dreaming.

It was called the Reckoning, and it was not a spell. That was the point. It was only a song, sung in the open squares of Vellamere, in the market where Mirabel had once sold luck-knots, in the shadow of the criers' platforms where bounty notices still fluttered. But it was a song made of memory: each verse a true thing, named and dated. The village of Ashenford, burned on the crown's order with two hundred gifted souls inside. The children taken to the Tower of Questions who never came out. The lie of the Great Purge—that magic had betrayed the realm—told plainly, verse after verse, by voices that had fought in the war and knew exactly what had actually broken the world.

And the song could not be silenced, because it was already everywhere. The Choir taught it to anyone who would listen. Bakers hummed it. Dockworkers drummed it on crates. Within a fortnight, Inquisitor Corvane's own hunters were humming it in their barracks, and he knew it, and he let them.

Mirabel saw him once during those weeks—from a rooftop, at distance, in the square below the criers' platform. He stood in his gray-and-silver order robes while a junior inquisitor read the charges against the Choir, and his face was the face of a man listening to his own eulogy. When the reading ended, Corvane stepped forward, and the crowd went quiet in the way crowds do when they sense history leaning close.

He removed the silver chain of his office and laid it on the platform boards.

"I am a mage," he said, and his voice carried to the rooftops, to Mirabel, to the whole listening city. "I have been one since I was six years old, and I have spent my life hunting myself. Everything the Choir sings is true. I know, because I helped bury the truth." A pause, long enough that Mirabel's breath stopped. "I will not help bury it deeper."

What followed was chaos, then gunfire, then nothing—Corvane vanished into the crowd before the crossbows found him, and by nightfall every fugitive in Vellamere knew the city gates stood unwatched for precisely one hour while the Inquisition tore itself apart arguing about treason. Bette drove the wagon through at a canter, fiddle abandoned, four strays and forty-one witnesses scattered to the winds behind her like seeds.

"He bought us the hour," Anselm said quietly, riding beside Mirabel in the dark. "A man like that doesn't get many hours left. He spent his on us."

"Then we'd better make it worth more than an hour," said Wren.

* * *

They buried the atlas at dawn on a hill east of the city, where the road forked—one branch running down toward the coast and safety, the other climbing north into country no chart in any royal archive had ever bothered to name.

Mirabel dug the hole herself. The leather of the atlas had gone soft as skin, and for a moment, kneeling there with it in her hands, she almost couldn't let go. Every map her mother ever drew was in here. Every secret. Every act of witness. It felt like burying her mother a second time.

"You don't have to," Bette offered, standing hatless in the wind, her gray braid whipping. "Could carry it forever. Some folk do. Carry the dead around till it kills 'em too."

"I know." Mirabel looked at the atlas a long moment. Then she set it in the earth. "But she didn't draw maps to keep them. She drew them so someone would *go*." She covered it with soil, pressed it flat with both palms, and marked the spot with a luck-knot tied to a hazel switch—the first honest charm she'd sold or given away in three years, given free, to no customer at all. "Goodbye, Mama. Thank you for the beginning."

Wren appeared at her elbow holding fresh paper, a stoppered bottle of ink, and the fierce expression of a child about to correct an adult's grammar. "If we're making a new map," she said, "the scale should be honest. Big. Everything past the northern passes. And the places that only exist on Tuesdays—we should ask the Choir where those go when it's Wednesday."

Anselm smiled, small and tired and real. "I'll write the names. Someone should bless them properly, even if the church won't have me back."

Bette spat thoughtfully into the wind. "I'll drive. Always been my gift—going places nobody sane wants to go."

Mirabel unrolled the blank paper across the wagon's tailboard and pinned its corners with stones. Four corners, four of them. She dipped her pen, and instead of starting with borders or rivers or roads, she wrote at the very center of the empty page, in her mother's style, in her own hand:

*Here begins the known world. Beyond this line, we go together.*

The wind came up the hill from the north, carrying—faintly, impossibly—the last notes of the Reckoning, still being sung somewhere behind them in a city learning to tell itself the truth. Ahead, the unmapped country waited, gray and green and patient.

Mirabel lifted the pen.

Somewhere far behind them, a rider crested the southern road at a hard gallop—a single figure, order robes torn to rags, riding like a man pursued by everything he'd ever believed. She didn't see him yet. But the road north was long, and it bent, and all maps eventually meet.

She began to draw.
