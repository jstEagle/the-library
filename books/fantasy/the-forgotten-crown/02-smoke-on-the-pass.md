## 2. Smoke on the Pass

The road north was a lie, and Vesper followed it anyway.

She had learned long ago that the best hiding places were the ones everyone could see. The King's Road wound up through the foothills in plain view, wide enough for carts, patrolled enough to be safe — and therefore the last place anyone would look for a fugitive cartographer walking openly with a pack on her shoulders and a commission folio under her arm, like any honest tradeswoman heading to a job.

For two days she walked it, nodding to drovers, buying bread at a waystation, letting herself be ordinary. And all the while she felt the tug behind her sternum, patient as a held breath, pulling her away from the road and toward a fold in the mountains that no map of hers had ever marked.

On the third morning, she saw the smoke.

It rose from the valley behind her — Greyhollow's direction, though too far now to be sure — a thin grey column standing against the pale sky like a finger pointing *here, here, here*. She told herself it was a chimney. A kiln. Anything. But her gift had already read the truth in the land before her mind caught up: the threads that ran toward Greyhollow had gone slack and dead, cut clean, the way they did when something built by hands stopped existing.

They had burned more than one cottage, then. They were burning them all.

Vesper stood in the middle of the King's Road until a wool merchant's cart rattled past and the driver gave her an odd look, and then she stepped off the verge into the bracken and let the tug take her.

* * *

The pass should not have existed.

That was the only way she could describe it, even to herself, and she had spent ten years describing impossible things in ink so that other people could walk them. The mountain's southern face presented a wall of grey granite, unbroken for miles — every surveyor's map said so, including three of her own. But when she pressed her palm to the stone and let the gift hum through her fingers, she felt it: a seam in the rock, cool and breathing, like the gap beneath a door.

"Show me," she whispered, and the seam opened.

Not opened — *revealed*. The path had always been there; she understood that with sudden, unsettling certainty. It was simply hidden the way a word is hidden inside a longer word, folded into the mountain's grammar, legible only to someone who spoke the language. She walked into the rock along a track barely wider than her shoulders, walled in by stone that smelled of iron and old rain, and above her the sky narrowed to a ribbon of blue.

Her heart hammered the whole way. Not from the dark, and not from the height, but from the openness of it — she had never used the gift like this, deliberately, as a door instead of a private sense. Every hidden way she'd ever charted, she'd found half by accident, half by instinct, and then lied about how. Now there was no lie left to tell. The mountain knew her. Somewhere behind her, men with lists wanted to know her too, and between those two knowledges she walked, small and exposed, deeper into the Thornfells than any crown map went.

By late afternoon the passage let out onto a high hanging valley, a bowl of scree and thin grass cupped between peaks, and Vesper allowed herself, for the first time since Greyhollow, to breathe.

That was when she heard the coughing.

* * *

He was wedged into a hollow beneath an overhang, and at first glance she took him for dead. He was too still, too pale, his dark hair matted to his forehead with blood that had dried brown. Then he coughed again — a wet, terrible sound — and his eyes opened, and he looked at her with an expression she recognized because she'd worn it herself for three days: the flat, exhausted watchfulness of someone who has run out of places to run.

"Don't," he rasped, before she'd taken a single step closer. "If you're one of hers—"

"One of whose?"

His gaze went past her shoulder, scanning the ridgeline, and whatever he saw or didn't see loosened something in him. "Hers," he said again, as if that explained everything. "Voss. Captain Maren Voss." He tried to push himself upright and failed, sliding back down the stone with a sound that was half groan, half laugh. "You're not a soldier."

"I'm a cartographer."

"A what?"

"Someone who draws maps." She crouched at the edge of his hollow, keeping distance out of habit, studying him. Scholar, she amended silently, taking in the ink-stained fingers, the satchel clutched to his chest the way a man holds a child. Or a thief. "You're bleeding from the head. That's a bad place to bleed from."

"It's the second-best place, actually," he said. "The first is nowhere." He laughed again, and the laugh turned into coughing, and Vesper gave up on caution and knelt beside him.

His satchel was wrapped twice around with cord, knotted like a man securing cargo in a storm. When she reached for it he caught her wrist — his grip surprisingly strong despite everything — and for a moment they simply looked at each other, two hunted animals deciding whether the other was predator or kin.

"My name is Cael," he said finally. "And what I'm carrying is the reason I'm bleeding. Which means it's the reason you're going to be bleeding too, eventually, if you stay near me. So consider carefully."

"I've been considering nothing else for three days."

"Then we understand each other." He let go of her wrist and fumbled the cords loose with shaking hands. Inside the satchel: a scroll case of black lacquer, royal make, its seal broken; and beneath it, cradled in wool wrappings, a stone.

No — part of a stone. It was the size of a fist, jagged on one side where it had been snapped from something larger, and its broken face was carved with lines so fine and so dense they might have been script or might have been a map. As Vesper leaned close, her gift stirred — not the gentle tug she knew, but a deep resonant hum, like striking a bell she hadn't known was in the room.

She snatched her hand back. "What is that?"

"That," Cael said, "is why Captain Voss has killed four people this month, and why she will kill more before winter. It's a covenant-stone. There were seven. This is the last one, and it's broken, and I stole it from the Royal Archive eleven days ago." He said it all very fast, the words tumbling like a confession he'd rehearsed until it wore smooth. "The decree is the order authorizing the hunt. All gift-bearers. You've seen what that looks like."

"I've seen a cottage burn."

"Then you've seen the beginning." His eyes were fever-bright but steady. "The decree isn't just a purge, cartographer. It's a search. They're not killing gift-bearers because they fear us—" he stopped, corrected himself, "—*them*. They're killing them because the stones answer to gift-blood, and the crown wants the covenant reforged under its own hand. Whoever holds a working covenant-stone holds the mountains. And the mountains are the last thing in this realm the crown doesn't hold."

Wind moved through the hanging valley, carrying the smell of snow from higher passes. Vesper looked at the broken stone, humming its low note against her senses, and felt the tug behind her sternum lean toward it like a plant toward light.

"You're hurt and delusional," she said. "Covenants. Stones that answer to blood. I chart smugglers' roads. I don't—"

"You found a pass through solid granite today." He said it quietly, without triumph, and it landed harder than a shout would have. "I watched you come out of the rock. There is no map of that pass. There is no *road* of that pass. It exists because you asked, and the mountain answered." He swallowed. "I've spent nine years in the Archive reading fragments about the oath-keepers. About people exactly like you. I know what you are better than you do, and right now that makes me the most dangerous friend you have."

"I didn't say I'd be your friend."

"No," he agreed. "But you haven't left yet."

* * *

She should have left. She thought about it honestly, sitting apart from him an hour later while she boiled snow over a tiny flame and he drifted in and out of consciousness. Leaving meant survival, maybe. Staying meant whatever came next, wearing a stranger's wounds and a stranger's war.

But night fell, and with it came the dogs.

She heard them before she saw anything — a high, carrying belling off the eastern ridge, answered by another voice further south. Not wolves. Wolves didn't hunt in coordinated chorus, and wolves didn't have lantern-light bobbing beneath their song.

Vesser counted eleven lights strung along the ridgeline in a loose crescent, closing like a hand folding shut. At their center rode a figure on a grey horse, still as a weathervane, and even at that distance Vesper felt watched — felt *measured*, the way she imagined a jeweler measures a stone before deciding whether to cut it or crush it.

"Cael." She shook his shoulder. "Cael. Riders."

He surfaced slowly, and when he saw the lights his face did something complicated — fear, yes, but under it a grim confirmation, like a scholar watching his worst theory prove itself. "Voss," he breathed. "She tracked me across the whole archive country. She'll have casters with her — they can't feel the paths, but they can feel *you* using them. Like heat off a forge."

"How many?"

"Enough."

The crescent of lights began to contract. There was no time to think, which was perhaps a mercy, because thinking would have told her all the reasons the next thing she did was impossible.

Vesper knelt and put both palms flat against the ground.

"Show me a road they can't follow," she whispered — and then, because the words felt too small, she added the truest thing in her: "*Please.*"

The mountain answered.

It was nothing like the gentle tug behind her sternum. The gift roared up through her hands and arms and filled her skull with white noise and white light, and suddenly she could see — actually see, with her eyes open or closed, it made no difference — the hidden ways of the Thornfells laid bare across the darkness like veins of fire. Paths within paths. Roads folded inside roads. A whole secret circulatory system running through the bones of the range, ancient and vast and *waiting*, and at its center, far to the north, something enormous turned its attention toward her like a lighthouse swinging its beam.

Beside her, Cael made a small wounded sound. "Oh," he whispered. "Oh, you're *strong*."

"There's a way down," she heard herself say, in a voice that didn't quite feel like hers. "Through the scree field. It drops into a gorge. The gorge runs north. We can be three miles gone before they crest the ridge."

"They'll follow the residue—"

"Not if I fold it behind us." She didn't know how she knew that. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat. "Can you ride? Can you run?"

"I can fall downhill with tremendous commitment."

"Good enough."

She hauled him up, got his arm over her shoulders, and together they staggered toward the seam in the world that only she could see — a crack in the night air above the scree that glowed, for her alone, like a door left open in a house of darkness.

Behind them, a woman's voice carried across the valley, clear and cold and unhurried, the voice of someone who had never once needed to shout:

"Cartographer. I know you can hear me. Bring me the scholar and the stone, and you can keep your gift." A pause, and then, almost gently: "I'll even let you keep your maps."

Vesper did not answer. She pulled Cael through the door, and the mountain closed it behind them, and the last thing she heard from the world above was the belling of dogs, fading like a memory of a memory.

* * *

They descended through the gorge all night, by paths that did not exist, lit by a glow that only she could see. Twice the trail narrowed to nothing and Vesper had to stop, press her hands to the rock, and *ask* — and twice the mountain relented, grudgingly, like an old woman shifting in her chair to let children pass.

Each time, it took something.

The first time, she lost the smell of her mother's kitchen — just that, just bread and woodsmoke, gone as cleanly as if a page had been torn out. The second time, she lost the name of the village she'd been born in. She noticed both absences immediately, the way the tongue finds a missing tooth, and she said nothing to Cael, who slept badly against the gorge wall whenever they paused, muttering fragments about oaths and betrayals and seven stones.

Near dawn they came out of the gorge into a forested vale where mist hung between the pines like breath held too long. Cael woke properly for the first time, ate the last of her bread, and studied her with those ink-stained, fever-bright eyes.

"You paid for the paths," he said quietly. "Didn't you."

It wasn't a question. Vesper looked north, where the peaks climbed toward a white silence she could feel waiting for her, patient as debt.

"The toll booth's open," she said. "And we're a long way from home."

Cael followed her gaze, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — lower, reverent, afraid. "Then you should know where we're going. There's something you need to hear about these mountains, Vesper. Something about what they remember. And about what they remember *you* being."

Far above them, at the mouth of the gorge, a single lantern appeared — and did not descend.

It only watched.
