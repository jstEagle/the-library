## 3. What the Mountains Remember

Cael had carried the covenant-stone for eleven days before it broke, and he had carried the knowledge of what it meant for twenty years before that. He knew which burden weighed more.

They made camp in the lee of a fallen pillar of rock, some ancient thing tumbled from the heights above, furred with moss so deep and green it seemed the mountain had grown a cushion expressly for weary fugitives. Vesper built the fire with the economy of someone who had slept rough a thousand nights — three stones, dry tinder from her oilskin packet, flame coaxed small and disciplined. She did everything, he was learning, as though the world might notice her doing it and disapprove.

The stone lay between them on his bedroll, broken into five pieces, its inner light gone grey as old ash. She looked at it the way she'd looked at it all day: sidelong, suspicious, as if it might be a trap set by scholars rather than kings.

"Tell me," she said. Not *explain yourself*, not *what is that thing*. Just: tell me. A cartographer's request. Give me the territory.

So he told her.

* * *

"You have to understand what Thornfell was," Cael began, "before it was a border. Before anyone drew a line down its spine and called one side ours and the other side *theirs*."

"Theirs being?"

"No one remembers. That's rather the point." He turned a piece of the stone in his fingers. Even dead, it had weight beyond its size, a gravity like a held breath. "The first kingdoms kept no maps of the high passes because they didn't need them. The mountains were not empty. They were *inhabited* — by a people who did not build, who did not plant, who moved through stone the way fish move through water. The old chronicles call them the Underhill Kin, but that's a scholar's name. They had their own, and it wasn't a word. It was a *hum*. A note held in the rock."

Vesper's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. He'd learned to watch for it. She heard something in his words she didn't want to hear.

"And your crown fought them?"

"Worse. The crown *married* them." He let that sit in the firelight. "There was a covenant. Sworn at the mountain's heart, on a night when the two realms touched — when the veil between what walks and what hums wore thin enough to speak through. The human realm would keep the lowlands, the valleys, the rivers and roads. The Kin would keep the deep places, the hidden ways, the roots of the world. And between them, binding them, there would be keepers. People who belonged to both. Who could feel the paths because they *were* the paths — living threads in the weave."

"The gift-bearers."

"The oath-keepers," Cael said. "That's what they were called first. Gift-bearer is what we started calling them after we'd forgotten what the gift was *for*. There were hundreds once. Whole lineages. They walked the borders and the boundaries held. No war crossed the passes. No famine crossed the valleys. The covenant was a treaty written in bloodlines instead of ink."

He watched her hands. Cartographer's hands, ink-stained at the cuticles, steady on her knees. Steadier than her face.

"What happened?"

"What always happens." He said it gently, because there was no gentle version. "A king decided the mountains were worth more than the friendship of things he couldn't tax. There was a purge — not of the Kin, they couldn't be reached, but of the keepers. Burned, scattered, their records destroyed, their names struck from every register. The covenant didn't break all at once. It *frayed*. Each keeper killed was a thread cut. The hidden ways went dark or went wild. The Kin withdrew deeper and deeper until even the stories about them became nursery tales, then nothing at all." He gestured at the broken stone. "This is an oath-stone. One of seven anchors of the original swearing. I stole it from the royal vault because my order believed the covenant could be studied back into being. Then Captain Voss put an arrow through our caravan and now it's kindling."

Vesper was quiet a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through a pass with a sound like slow breathing.

"So you're saying," she said finally, "that I'm descended from some ancient order of mountain-priests. That my — what, my trick for finding trails — is a holy relic."

"I'm saying you found a pass yesterday that has been unmapped for four hundred years. In a snowstorm. While bleeding."

"People get lost. People find things."

"Not like this." He leaned forward. "Vesper. When you walk, do you ever feel it? Not see — *feel*. Like a pull behind the sternum. Like the ground telling you where it wants you to go."

Her stillness was answer enough. But she gave him words anyway, and her voice was flat as slate: "I feel where the land folds. Where the safe ways run. I've felt it since I was a child. I thought everyone did, a little, and I was just better at listening." Her eyes came up, and there was something raw under the flatness now. "You're asking me to believe I'm the last thread of a broken treaty between mankind and creatures out of legend. Do you know what that sounds like to a woman who's spent ten years selling trail-maps to smugglers?"

"It sounds like a fairy tale."

"It sounds like a *justification*. For whatever it is you need me to be." She poked the fire harder than it deserved. Sparks climbed. "I've been useful to people before, Cael. Useful people get used up. I'm not a covenant. I'm not a keeper. I'm a mapmaker with good instincts, and tomorrow I'm going to find us a way over the Serrat spine, and in spring I'm going back to Greyhollow, and none of this—" she flicked a finger at the dead stone "—is going to have mattered."

He didn't argue. Arguing with Vesper Dunmore was like arguing with weather. But as she rolled into her bedroll with her back to him, he watched the mountains rise black against the stars, and he thought: *she listens better than she knows.*

* * *

They crossed into the deep Thornfells on the third morning, past the last shepherd's cairn, past the last tree, into country where the maps — *her* maps — thinned to guesswork and apology.

Cael saw it begin at the col below the Serrat spine.

Vesper stopped mid-stride. Not the deliberate stop of a navigator checking bearings; this was abrupt, involuntary, a woman walking into a wall of air. Her head tilted. Her breath went shallow.

"Cael." Her voice had changed. Smaller. Younger. "Do you hear that?"

He heard wind. He said so.

"That's not—" She pressed a hand flat against the rock beside the trail, palm open, the way you'd touch a horse you were gentling. "It's *low*. Under everything. Like a note held in—"

She stopped. Because he had said those words to her, two nights ago, around a dying fire. *It wasn't a word. It was a hum. A note held in the rock.* And here it was, whether she believed in it or not, rising up through the soles of her boots.

They walked on, and it got worse. Or better. Cael couldn't decide which frightened him more.

At the frozen fall where the trail split three ways, Vesper didn't consult her instruments. She stood swaying slightly, eyes half-closed, and then simply chose — left, without hesitation, along a ledge no surveyor had ever recorded. And the ledge *was* there. Solid, sheltered, exactly wide enough for two travelers and their fear. As though the mountain had remembered, at her approach, that it had once been a road.

By afternoon she had stopped speaking altogether. She walked with her hand trailing along the rock face, and wherever her fingers passed, Cael swore — swore on his order, his stolen decree, his twenty wasted years — that the frost retreated. A hand's width of bare stone following her touch like water parting before a prow. Lichen glowed faintly where she lingered, a green-gold shimmer too regular to be reflection, fading only after she'd gone.

Once, she stumbled on scree, and before her knee struck it the loose stones had settled themselves flat. He watched it happen. He watched the mountain catch her.

"Cael." Her voice, when it finally came, near dusk, was hoarse. "Make it stop."

"I don't think it works that way."

"*Make it stop.*" She rounded on him, and her face in the failing light was terrible — not angry, not anymore. Frightened. Wonder and terror had gotten tangled somewhere inside her and come out as fury, because fury was the shape she knew how to wear. "It knows me. It's — it keeps *helping*. Roads opening where there are no roads. Stone warming under my hands. This morning I thought about water and there was a spring thirty feet off the trail, *exactly* where I needed it, and I didn't find that spring, Cael, the spring found *me*." Her breath hitched. "Land doesn't do that. Land doesn't *notice*."

"No," he agreed quietly. "Ordinary land doesn't."

"Then what is this? What am I?"

And there it was — the question she'd been refusing since the fire, asked at last not with scorn but with something closer to prayer. He wanted to answer gently. He wanted to lie. He did neither.

"You're what the covenant made," he said. "Or what's left of it. The land isn't helping you, Vesper. It's *recognizing* you. Four hundred years since it felt one of its own walking these ways, and it's — reaching. The way a dog runs to a whistle it hasn't heard since it was a pup."

"A whistle." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It's not whistling. It's *singing*. All day. Getting louder. Every mile we go deeper it's louder, and I can't—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, hard, as though trying to hold a door shut from the inside. "Last night I dreamed in a language I don't speak. I woke up and my mouth was moving. I understood it while I slept, Cael. I understood every word."

The wind came down the col then, and it was not empty wind. It carried harmonics, faint and vast, a chord sustained in the throat of the mountain, and Cael felt it in his teeth, in the fillings of his bones, and he understood that he was hearing it secondhand — that it was not singing to him at all.

Vesper had gone very pale. She was looking at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger.

"When does it stop?" she whispered.

He had read every surviving fragment of the old registers. He knew the histories of the keepers: how the gift grew with proximity, how the deep places called to their own, how the final stage of every recorded keeper's life was described in the same careful, chilling phrase — *went wholly to the hill*.

He also knew that lying to her now would cost them both.

"It doesn't," he said. "Not as long as we go deeper. And we have to go deeper. The heart of the mountain is the only place the covenant can be reforged — and Voss is behind us, and the stone is broken, and you are the only anchor left that isn't."

She stared at him. Behind her, the sun went down the far side of the world, and the mountain's song rose to fill the dark it left, wordless and patient and glad.

And Vesper Dunmore, who had spent her whole life drawing lines around unknown country, began — very softly, helplessly, in a voice not entirely her own — to hum along.
