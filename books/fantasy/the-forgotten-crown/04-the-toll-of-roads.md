## 4. The Toll of Roads

The first path cost Vesper nothing. The second cost her a night's warmth.

She learned this the way she learned most things about her gift: by accident, and too late. They had crossed beneath a shelf of stone where no road was marked on any map — a way that opened for her like a held breath, the rock parting its attention around them — and when they emerged into the grey morning, Cael's hands were blue to the wrist and hers ached as though she'd been gripping ice all night. Neither of them had been cold on the far side of it. Both were cold now.

"The land keeps accounts," Cael said, chafing his fingers over a fire they dared not light. He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "I told you. Nothing in the deep Thornfells is given."

"You didn't tell me it *took*."

"I hoped you'd have time to learn gently." He looked away, toward the pass behind them, where smoke still smudged the sky from Greyhollow's burning. "Gentleness is in short supply."

Vesper flexed her fingers and said nothing. She had spent years paying small tolls without knowing them — the fatigue after a hidden way, the strange hunger, the sense of having left something behind in the dark like a glove forgotten on a bench. She had called it the price of the work. Every cartographer paid it. That was what she'd believed, anyway, back when believing was comfortable.

Now she watched the mountain watch her, and understood she had never been doing the paying alone.

* * *

They climbed for two days through country that grew less like country and more like memory. The trees came sparser, older, bent in attitudes of grief or listening. Lichen glowed faintly green along the north faces, and where it glowed, Vesper felt the paths: threads under the world, humming like plucked wire. Her gift had always been a sense of *where* — gaps in the land, ways through. Here it was becoming something else. A sense of *whom*. The mountain had opinions. The mountain had moods. And the mountain was hungry in a patient, geological way, the way a cathedral is hungry for prayer.

On the third day they took a path that asked for blood.

It announced itself as a tightness in her palm, then a sting, and when she looked down, a thin line had opened across the heel of her hand as neatly as if drawn with a penknife. No pain until she saw it. Blood welled and ran down her wrist, dripping from her fingertips onto the stones, and where each drop fell, the lichen flared briefly brighter, drinking.

"Stop," Cael said sharply. "Stop walking."

"If I stop—"

"I know what happens if you stop." He tore a strip from his already ruined sleeve and bound her hand with hands that shook only a little. His scholar's calm was a constructed thing; she could see the scaffolding under it. "A drop at a time is a toll. A pool of it is an invitation. There's a difference, and we are not going to learn it today."

"What kind of invitation?"

He didn't answer, which was answer enough.

They walked on. Vesper kept her bandaged fist closed and thought about arithmetic. Two days, three tolls. Warmth, then something she hadn't identified yet — a word gone missing from her tongue, some common word she reached for and found a smooth blank where it should have been — and now blood. The tolls were escalating. Or rather, they were *clarifying*, the way a debt clarifies the longer it runs unpaid.

"Cael." She kept her voice level. "When the covenant broke. When your order scattered the oath-keepers. What happened to the roads?"

"They went dark." He picked his way over a fallen trunk, testing each hold before trusting it. "Sealed. The old texts say the land grieved them shut."

"And who paid the tolls while they were open? Before?"

He stopped. For a long moment he stood with his hand on the mossy bark, head bowed, and she watched him decide whether to lie to her. She watched him decide not to.

"The keepers did," he said. "That was the covenant. The land carried the people; the keepers carried the land's hunger. It was never meant to be a secret. It was meant to be a *sharing* — one keeper per valley, one life braided into the roots, so no single soul bore the whole weight." He turned to look at her, and his face in the green lichen-light was hollowed and old. "Your gift isn't a talent, Vesper. It's an inheritance with the will struck out of it. You've been paying interest on a debt you never knew you owed."

Vesper walked several paces before she trusted her voice.

"My mother," she said finally. "She used to stand at the window on storm nights. Watching the fells. She'd say, *they're talking again.* I thought she meant the wind." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "I thought she was mad. I left home at sixteen because I couldn't bear the sound of her listening."

Cael said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either cruelty or comfort, and he seemed to know she'd take neither.

But that night, sleeping in the lee of a crag with her bandaged hand tucked against her chest, Vesper dreamed of a kitchen. Woodsmoke and barley bread. Her mother at the table, younger than Vesper could ever remember her, laughing at something out the window — and Vesper, in the dream, knew it was a dream, knew the toll had come due even here, and gripped the table and said *no, not this one, take anything else* —

And woke with frost on her eyelashes and a hole in her childhood where a kitchen used to be.

She lay staring at the dark and tried to reconstruct it. There had been a house. There had been a woman. There had been bread, or the idea of bread. But the room itself was gone — not faded, *excised*, clean-edged, like a map with a region cut away. She tested the edges of the absence the way she'd test a false wall, and found nothing behind it. Just more mountain.

In the morning she told Cael, because he needed to know what they were spending, and because saying it aloud was the only way to keep the rest of herself mapped.

"It takes memories," she said. "Not warmth. Not really. Warmth is just the small change."

He was quiet for a long time. "Then we stop taking the hidden ways."

"And walk the open passes where Maren Voss is waiting with thirty crossbows." She wrapped her blanket tighter. "No. We pay. But we choose what we pay *with*, from now on. I'll feed it the things I can spare."

"Is there such a thing?" he asked gently. "A memory you can spare?"

She thought of the kitchen she no longer had. "Ask me again when we're out of the mountains," she said, and hated how much it sounded like a prayer.

* * *

The village came upon them suddenly, the way shelter does to the desperate: a fold in the valley, a dozen slate roofs, woodsmoke standing straight in the windless cold. Ashford, a sign might once have said; the sign was long gone. The people were short and broad and dark-eyed, and when the eldest woman saw Vesper's bandaged hand and the way she moved — reading the ground, choosing the safe stones — the woman's face did something complicated and sorrowful.

"You're a walker," the old woman said. Not a question.

"I'm a cartographer."

"Mm." The woman, whose name was Brey, fed them barley soup and asked no questions, which in Vesper's experience was the most expensive hospitality there was. "My grandmother was a walker," Brey said, ladling seconds unasked. "Before the purges. She said the roads used to sing. Then they stopped, and the valley got poorer every year, and nobody remembered why." She set down the bowl. "Stay the night. The soldiers passed through a week ago asking about a man with a scholar's satchel. They won't come back so soon."

They stayed. It was the first roof in nine days, the first food that hadn't been foraged or stolen, and Vesper slept four hours in a real bed and dreamed of nothing at all, which felt like wealth.

Maren Voss came at dawn.

Not with thirty crossbows. With six riders and a prisoner — a boy of perhaps fourteen, one of Brey's grandsons, taken on the high pasture two days past. They stood him in the square below the window where Vesper woke to the sound of it, and Maren Voss sat her horse in front of him with the patience of a woman who had done this many times and expected to do it many more.

"Two travelers," Voss said, conversationally, to the assembled village. "A man carrying what belongs to the crown. A woman with a burned hand." She let the silence stretch. "You've fed them. Someone has slept in your beds. I'm not unreasonable. Bring them down, and the boy walks home, and Ashford stays off the lists."

*The lists.* Vesper pressed her forehead to the cold window glass. There were lists. Of course there were lists — the crown had been thorough, thoroughness being the crown's chief virtue. Villages that sheltered gift-bearers went on the lists, and villages on the lists burned, slowly or quickly, by tax or by torch.

Brey stood in the square with her spine like an iron rod. "We haven't seen them."

"Grandmother." The boy's voice cracked on it. "It's fine. Tell them—"

"Hold," Voss said mildly, and drew her knife, and did nothing with it, and the nothing was worse than any use.

Vesper was moving before she decided to move. Down the stairs, through the door, into the grey light with Cael hissing her name behind her — and she heard herself say, loud and clear across the square:

"You want me. Here I am."

Everything happened very fast after that. Cael's hand clamping her arm. Voss's eyes going bright and hungry as a kestrel's. And then Vesper did the thing she had sworn, nine days ago in a burning town, never to do again in front of witnesses: she opened her gift fully, deliberately, and *pushed*.

The land answered.

It came up through her boots and out through her bandaged hand — the whole buried web of the valley, every sealed and sleeping way, waking at once. The square's cobbles shivered. The well-water trembled in rings. And between Vesper and the riders the ground simply *opened its mind*, showing them all, in one shared instant, the hundred hidden roads threading under their feet like veins under skin — and the price of each one, itemized, glowing.

Six horses screamed and bolted. Four riders stayed mounted; two did not. The boy ran for his grandmother. And Maren Voss sat her horse through all of it, white-faced, unmoving, staring at Vesper with an expression Vesper would spend the rest of the flight trying to forget — not hatred. Recognition. As if Voss had seen, in that flash of the land's opened mind, something she had spent her whole life refusing to look at.

"Walkers," Voss said softly, to no one. To herself. Then she wheeled her horse and took her riders up the valley at a gallop, and the boy was alive, and the village was spared, and none of it felt like victory.

Because Vesper had felt what the push cost. It hadn't come from her. She'd opened the door, and the land had reached through it and taken from the *valley* — from Brey's fields, from the well, from the thin green thread of the village's own luck. She felt it draining even now, a slow hemorrhage of fortune, the way you feel heat leaving a house through an open door.

By spring, she understood, Ashford's crops would fail. By winter, its luck would run out entirely. The crown wouldn't need to burn it. The lists would do the work themselves.

"We have to go," Cael said quietly. "Now, today, before she regroups."

"I know."

"Vesper. The village—"

"*I know.*" Her voice broke on it. She stood in the square where the cobbles still trembled faintly, looking at Brey's set face and the boy's white one, and understood with total clarity the shape of the covenant Cael had described: one keeper per valley, one life braided into the roots, so that no single soul bore the whole weight. So that no village paid for one woman's survival.

There had been hundreds of keepers once. She was one, and half-trained, and hunted, and every step she took through the hidden ways dug the debt deeper — into her memory, into the land, into anyone foolish enough to offer her soup and a bed.

Brey pressed a bundle of food into her hands at the gate and refused thanks. "My grandmother said the walkers would come back one day," the old woman said. "She said when they did, the singing would start again." She looked at Vesper's bandaged hand. "I don't think she meant it to cost like this. Go east. There's a road under the fell that doesn't ask — an old one. My grandmother called it the King's Forgiveness. Follow the singing stones."

Vesper took the bundle. She took the direction. And as they climbed out of the valley into the deepening fells, she made herself a promise with edges sharp enough to draw blood:

She would find whoever had broken the covenant. She would find them, and make them look at Ashford, and Greyhollow, and every name on every list — and then she would make them pay the arrears.

Behind them, faint as a heartbeat under the slate and scree, the mountain began — very softly — to sing.
