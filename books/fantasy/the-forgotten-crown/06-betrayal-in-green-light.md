## 6. Betrayal in Green Light

The enclave woke to green light.

Vesper had been dreaming of Greyhollow — of the harbor fog and the smell of tar on the docks — when the color seeped into her sleep. Not the gold of lamplight, not the blue of dawn through leaves, but a green like foxfire, like rot-glow in old wood, climbing the walls of Sable's guest-house and pooling beneath the door.

She was on her feet before she understood why. The gift had gone taut as a plucked string, every hidden path in the mountain singing one note: *wrong, wrong, wrong.*

Beside the cold hearth, Cael slept sitting up, the shattered covenant-stone cradled against his chest like a child. She crossed the room and shook him once, hard.

"Cael. Wake up."

His eyes opened — and in them she saw the same green light reflected, and something else beneath it. Something that flinched.

"What is that?" she whispered.

He didn't answer fast enough. Outside, a horn sounded — three notes, low and deliberate, the signal Sable's watchers used for strangers on the paths. Then a fourth note it had never been meant to make, cut short mid-cry.

Vesper's blood went cold. "They found us."

"No." Cael was standing now, fumbling for his satchel, his voice too smooth, too quick. "No, they can't have — the approaches are warded, Vesper, no soldier could—"

The door burst inward.

Not soldiers. *Light.* It poured into the room like water finding a crack, green and hungry, and where it touched the wall the timber blackened and curled. Through it came shapes in crown grey, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly which corridors were unguarded — who knew, Vesper realized with a sickness rising in her throat, exactly where the wards were thin, because someone had told them.

Someone who had slept under this roof.

"Maren Voss," Sable's voice rang from the courtyard, iron over fury. "You walk on stolen ground."

* * *

The courtyard was burning before Vesper reached it.

She came out into chaos — gift-bearers spilling from their homes in nightclothes, hands raised, their gifts flickering to life in desperate half-formed shields. The great rowan at the courtyard's heart, older than the enclave itself, was wrapped in green flame that did not consume it but *held* it, frozen mid-burn like an insect in amber.

Maren Voss stood at the courtyard's edge, and she did not look like a hunter anymore. She looked like a woman being *used*, her jaw tight, her hand white-knuckled around a rod of pale stone that bled the green light. Behind her, crown soldiers moved through the settlement with maps in their hands. Maps. Of the enclave. Of its inner ways, its hidden doors, its ward-stones.

Maps drawn by a scholar's careful hand.

Vesper turned, slowly, to find Cael in the doorway behind her. He had not followed her out. He stood half in shadow, the covenant-stone still clutched to his chest, and he would not meet her eyes.

"Tell me I'm wrong," she said.

"Vesper—"

"The decree. The one you 'stole.'" Her voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were speaking. "It wasn't stolen. It was *given* to you. You were carrying it to us. To them." She gestured at the burning courtyard. "Bait."

"They wanted what you can do!" The words tore out of him, raw and cracking. "My order has spent two hundred years trying to reforge the covenant — do you understand? Two hundred years of watching the land go deaf and dumb, the gifts thinning generation by generation! But they won't let it be reforged *freely*. They want it bound to the crown. A covenant the throne controls. And you—" his voice broke, "you were never supposed to be hurt. You were supposed to be *found*. Delivered. I was supposed to deliver you."

"And the enclave?"

"I didn't know they'd come here. Vesper, I swear on my order's bones, I didn't know—"

"You drew the maps." It wasn't a question. She could see it now, could see all of it laid bare: the way he'd always known the paths a little too well, the questions he'd asked about the enclave's defenses dressed as scholarly curiosity, the way Maren's hunters had found them in the pass after Vesper had told him, only him, which way she meant to flee. "You drew the maps, Cael. You made my gift legible to them."

Above them, the rowan groaned inside its cage of green fire.

* * *

Sable found her there, in the smoke.

The matriarch came through the burning courtyard like a ship through weather, unbothered, terrible, her white braids singed at the ends. She took Vesper's arm with fingers like iron bands and pulled her toward the mountain's back wall, where a seam in the rock opened onto a stair — one of the enclave's oldest ways, known to perhaps five living souls.

"You don't fight tonight," Sable said. "Tonight you *survive*. That is your whole duty."

"I'm not leaving these people."

"You are leaving these people because you are the last keeper who can finish what we started." Sable shoved her toward the stair, then turned to face the courtyard, raising both arms. Her gift rose with her — Vesper felt it like a change in pressure, a storm gathering — and the green flames recoiled from her as if from a blade.

Maren Voss saw her and shouted an order. The soldiers turned.

"Sable—"

"Go down, girl." The matriarch glanced back at her, and for one moment the iron left her face, and Veper saw what lived underneath: grief, ancient and patient, the grief of a woman who had buried this enclave's dead before and had always known she would bury them again. "Every road has a toll. Mine was always going to be paid here. Yours is still ahead of you."

Then she turned and walked into the fire, and the fire *bent around her*, and Vesper heard her voice rise in a language older than any map — the oath-language, the covenant-tongue — and the whole mountainside shuddered.

Vesper ran down the stair.

Behind her, above her, the enclave burned. She felt each death through the gift like a door slamming shut in a long corridor — the watcher on the north path, the twins who kept the goat-paths, the boy who had laughed at her map-ink stains two days ago. Doors slamming, one after another, until—

One door, near the end, opened instead of closing. Warmth poured through it, and a voice, worn thin with effort but unmistakable:

"*Last keeper.* The words fell into her mind like stones into a well. *Hear me, Vesper Dunmore. You are the last who can stand at the heart and swear it whole. Do not let them bind it. Do not let them take what must be given freely. Run true, and run deep, and remember — the land does not ask for strength. It asks for surrender.*"

Then the door slammed, and the warmth went out of it forever, and Vesper knew — the way she knew hidden paths, suddenly, completely, without wanting to — that Sable was dead.

She stopped running. She stood in the dark of the stair with her fists pressed against her eyes, and what came out of her was not tears. It was rage, pure and molten, rage at Maren Voss and her green fire, rage at the crown that had turned sanctuary into kindling — and rage, hottest of all, at the gentle-voiced scholar who had mapped her like territory and called it rescue.

When she lifted her head, her sight had changed. The mountain's ways blazed around her, every path lit like veins of ore, and at the bottom of everything, far below, something vast and patient had begun to stir. It had felt the enclave burn. It had felt its last keeper named.

It was waiting for her.

Vesper wiped her face, took the covenant-stone from Cael's abandoned satchel where it lay glowing faintly in the dark, and started down.

The descent would be long. Let it be long. She had a debt to pay, and a name — *her* name, the only thing she'd ever owned outright — and she intended to spend both before the crown learned what it had awakened.

Behind her, far above, the green light guttered and died.

Ahead, the mountain opened its throat.
