## 7. The Long Descent

Cael had always believed grief was a thing that happened to other people, in other rooms, at the end of long and blameless lives. He had studied sorrow the way he studied everything — from a distance, in books, annotated in a careful hand. He had never imagined it would smell like woodsmoke and burnt wool, or that it would walk beside him for three days without speaking, or that it would have Vesper Dunmore's face.

The enclave's ashes were behind them now, somewhere above in the high green folds of Thornfell where Sable's people had hidden for two hundred years. Cael did not look back. Looking back felt like a luxury they could no longer afford, and besides, he did not trust himself to see it again — the terraced gardens still smoking, the covenant-stone shrine split open like a struck bell, the place where Sable had fallen marked only by a scorch shaped vaguely like wings.

So he watched Vesper instead.

She walked ahead of him down the switchback trail, her pack riding high on her shoulders, her staff striking stone in a rhythm too steady, too metronomic, as if she had wound herself like a clock. She had barely slept since the burning. When she did sleep, she slept badly, murmuring names — some he recognized, some he did not, and one, over and over, that made his chest ache: *Mama, wait, I can't see you, why can't I see you.*

He had told himself, in the first hours after the enclave, that the land's toll was a thing they could manage. That they would choose their payments carefully, the way a careful household budgets its coin. He had even said so aloud, in his scholar's voice, the voice that had once made first-year students believe the world was orderly.

Vesper had looked at him then with an expression he had no annotation for.

"You don't understand," she had said. "It isn't asking anymore."

* * *

He understood now.

They were two days into the descent along the Old Warden's Road, a path so old it predated the kingdom's oldest maps — Cael knew because he had written three of those maps' marginalia himself, and none of them mentioned this. The road descended through country that changed the way a mood changes: pine giving way to black fir, black fir giving way to trees he had no name for, their bark silvered and their leaves turning, all together, though there was no wind.

And the road was taking from her.

He saw it first in small things. She called him by the wrong title once — not *scholar*, but *magister*, which was the title of a man who had taught her letters when she was small, a man whose existence Cael learned of only because she had forgotten him. She stopped mid-sentence twice in one hour, her mouth open around a word that had gone out of her like a bird from an open hand. And on the second evening, crouched over their small fire, she held her hands out to the flames and said, conversationally, terribly:

"I had a dog. I know I had a dog. There's a shape where it was."

"Vesper—"

"Don't." She didn't look up. "Don't say my name right now. It costs something when people say it. I can feel it going out of me every time, like heat off a kettle. Just — call me nothing. Call me *cartographer*. That's safe. That's what I am."

"You're more than that."

"I was more than that." She smiled, and it was the worst thing he had seen since the enclave burned. "That's rather the point, isn't it? You told me the covenant needs a keeper who belongs wholly to the land. Well. It's making me belong. It's carving me to fit. Every mile down this road, it takes another piece of Vesper Dunmore and fills the space with mountain."

Cael opened his mouth to argue — because arguing was the last tool he had, the scholar's final resort when the world refused to be reasonable — and found he had nothing. Because she was right. He had read the fragments. He had spent six years chasing the shattered covenant across half the archives of the realm, and the oldest fragment, the one carved in a script older than the kingdom, said plainly: *the keeper is not chosen; the keeper is made, by emptying.*

He had thought it was poetry. Scholars always think the terrible things are poetry until they watch them happen to someone carrying their firewood.

* * *

On the third day they came to the Bridge of Teeth.

It was not a bridge, exactly — it was a spine of natural stone spanning a gorge so deep the river at its bottom was only a sound, a long white whisper. The crossing was narrow enough that they went single file, and Vesper went first, arms out slightly for balance, and halfway across she stopped.

"Cael." Her voice came back to him strange, flattened by the gorge. "What was my mother's name?"

His stomach dropped. "You told me. In Greyhollow. The night we met — you said your mother named you for the evening star because you were born at dusk, and her name was—"

"I know I said it." Still she did not turn. Below her feet, the white whisper rose and fell. "I can feel the shape of having said it. There's a groove in me where the name sat. But the name itself is — it's like a word in a language I used to speak." A pause. The wind moved her hair. "Tell me. Please. While you still remember what I told you."

He told her. Her mother's name, the dusk, the star. She stood very still on the spine of the world and received it the way one receives news of a death — nodding slowly, filing it away, already knowing it would not keep.

"It won't stay," she said. "Nothing stays now. It's not like paying a toll at a gate, one coin, done. It's — Cael, it's like bleeding. The gift is open too wide. Since the enclave, since Sable—" her voice caught on the name, and he watched her lose something else, some small warm memory attached to it, watched her eyes flicker with the loss and then smooth over, "—since then, the land pours through me and takes as it goes. I'm a channel with the floodgates broken."

"Then we stop." He stepped closer along the narrow stone, close enough to touch her shoulder. "We stop, and we find another way down, a slower way, a way that doesn't run through the old paths—"

"There are no other ways down." Now she turned, and her eyes were wrong — not wrong in color but in depth, as if he were looking down a well instead of at a woman. "You know that. The hunters burned the eastern trails behind us. Maren Voss has dogs and crown-iron and a map of everything the kingdom ever surveyed. The only roads left are the roads only I can feel. Which means the only way to the heart of the mountain is through me." She laughed, short and awful. "The land isn't cruel, Cael. That's the worst part. I can feel it — it doesn't want to take. It's just that the covenant is broken, and broken things leak, and I'm the nearest vessel. If it were whole, if we reach the heart, if I swear it properly — then it stops being theft and becomes choice. One payment, freely given, and the keeping begins."

"And if we don't reach the heart?"

She looked past him, down the gorge, toward the darkening south where the mountain's roots went deep.

"Then there won't be enough of me left to grieve about it," she said. "Come on. We're losing the light."

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, and Cael lay awake listening to the mountain breathe.

He had begun, in these last days, to feel it too — faintly, secondhand, the way a man standing near a struck bell feels the hum in his teeth. Whatever poured through Vesper sloshed against the edges of him. He dreamed of roads he had never walked and woke knowing their names. He woke knowing the name of the river in the gorge. He woke knowing things about Sable's people that no book contained, and he lay in the dark with his heart hammering, understanding that the knowledge had come *through her*, that she was becoming a door and he was standing in the draft.

Beside him, Vesper slept at last — truly slept, for the first time in days — and in her sleep she wept without waking, silent tears running sideways into her hair, and her lips moved.

He leaned close. He should not have; he knew he should not have; some part of him understood that whatever she was saying in her sleep was being said to someone who was no longer reachable, some mother or some self, some person being carried away downstream. But he leaned close anyway, because he was a scholar, and scholars are creatures who cannot leave a text unread.

"—I'll remember the important parts," she whispered. "I'll keep the important parts. I promise. Tell me which parts are the important parts. Mama, tell me which—"

Then she sighed, and turned her face to the stone, and the tears stopped, and her breathing went slow and deep as tide.

Cael lay back and stared at the overhang above them, at the ancient soot marks of fires older than any nation, and let himself do the thing he had forbidden himself since the enclave: he counted what was left.

Her maps — those she kept; her hands drew them in her sleep sometimes, tracing contours on the air. Her stubbornness — that seemed load-bearing; the land took memories and took names and could not seem to touch the flat iron refusal at her center. Her anger at him — intact, thank every forgotten god, because the day she stopped being angry at him for bringing the decree that brought Maren Voss to Sable's door would be the day he'd know the taking had reached the marrow.

But her childhood was going. Her mother's face was going. He had watched her hold a strip of dried apple at supper and frown at it, genuinely puzzled, before remembering — visibly, painfully, like a swimmer surfacing — that she loved them.

And her name. He had tested it that morning, casually, the way one tests ice. *Vesper.* She had answered to it. But she had paused first — a half-second pause, a cartographer checking a legend — and he had seen her find the name rather than simply wear it.

Soon, he thought, the name would be a word she knew the meaning of without knowing it was hers. Soon after, it would be a word in a dead language. Soon after that—

He rolled onto his side and forced himself to think of the descent instead. Two days, perhaps three, along the Old Warden's Road and then the Stair of Roots, down through strata of stone that grew warmer and stranger the deeper they went, to the threshold of the deep place where the first oath had been sworn in a time before crowns. The fragments said the threshold would test them. The fragments said many things. The fragments had not said anything about a hunter-captain with crown-iron and a stolen map of hidden ways, and yet.

Because that was the other thing. The thing he had not told Vesper, not yet, not while she was bleeding memory with every mile.

At noon, on a ridge above the gorge, he had looked back — breaking his own rule, looking back — and seen smoke. Not forest smoke. Signal smoke, disciplined, spaced, deliberate. Three columns, moving in parallel along the heights, flanking the Old Warden's Road like fingers closing.

Maren Voss was not following them.

Maren Voss was *herding* them — down the only road left, down toward the mountain's heart, letting the land do the slow work of unmaking the last keeper while the crown's iron waited at the bottom.

Cael stared into the dark and did the arithmetic he dreaded. They could not outrun her. They could not fight her. They could not turn aside. There was only forward, down, faster — faster meant more paths, more paths meant more toll, more toll meant less of Vesper with every hour, and the mountain's heart waiting at the bottom of the world like a mouth.

Behind them, far above, a third column of signal smoke bloomed against the stars.

They had one night, maybe two. And Vesper Dunmore was asleep beside him, forgetting herself beautifully, saving what she could of the important parts.
