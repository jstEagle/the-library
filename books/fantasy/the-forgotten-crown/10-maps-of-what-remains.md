## 10. Maps of What Remains

The ink was still wet when the first snow came to Greyhollow, and Cael sat in the window of a rented room above a chandler's shop, watching it fall on a town that had once tried to burn a woman out of it.

He had chosen Greyhollow deliberately. It seemed right that her maps should come home to the place where she had made them — or at least, where she had made the last of them before everything changed. The room smelled of tallow and cold stone. On the table before him lay the proofs: forty-one plates of copperplate engraving, each one a mountain pass, a smuggler's road, a hidden way through the Thornfells that no crown surveyor had ever found because no crown surveyor had ever *listened*.

*The Dunmore Atlas of the Thornfell Ways*, the title page read. *Surveyed and drawn by Vesper Dunmore.*

He had argued with the printer for three days about that line. The man had suggested, gently, that perhaps they might add *with notes by Cael Arden, Royal Academy of Histories*, since Cael had spent four months correcting elevations, verifying distances, annotating every plate with the scholarship that would make booksellers take the thing seriously. A name like his opened doors. A name like hers — a smuggler-town cartographer, a woman with no guild seal and no patron — might not.

"Put nothing," Cael had said, "but her name."

And so there was nothing but her name.

He turned the title page over in his hands now, feeling the weight of the paper, and thought about how strange it was that a person could be reduced to ink and still be more present than most of the living people he knew. Vesper Dunmore had been a quiet woman. She had moved through rooms like someone apologizing for taking up space. And yet here she was, in every line of these maps — in the confidence of a contour, in the little annotations in her own hand that he had refused to let the engravers smooth away. *Ice bridge unreliable after thaw.* *Do not trust the cairns above Hollowmere; I built some of them wrong on purpose.* *This road is safe. Trust me.*

Trust me. As if she had known, all those years alone with her gift folded up small inside her, that someday strangers would read her words and walk her roads and live because of them.

He set the page down. His hands were not steady. They had not been steady since the mountain.

* * *

The world, in the months since the reforging, had changed in ways that no decree could capture and no historian — and Cael was, whatever else he was, a historian — could properly explain.

The persecutions had simply stopped. Not by order of the crown; King Aldric's edicts against gift-bearers remained on the books, gathering dust like everything else the old king had written in his final paranoid years. But the hunts had faltered. Hunters reported their quarry vanishing into passes that closed behind them like water closing over a stone. Soldiers sent into the deep Thornfells came back silent, shaken, unwilling to speak of what they'd seen — or rather, of what they hadn't seen, of the way the mountains seemed to look back.

And the gift-bearers themselves had changed. Cael had sought them out, one by one, in the year that followed: the fire-reader in the fen country, the storm-sisters of the western coast, the old man in the riverlands who could taste rain three days before it fell. Every one of them told him the same thing, in different words. Something had opened. Their gifts ran easier now, deeper, as if a door long jammed had been unjammed from the other side. As if something vast and patient had decided, at last, to stop holding its breath.

None of them remembered why. That was the part that hollowed him out, every time. He would sit across a hearth from some frightened child of a gift-bearer whose life had just quietly become survivable, and he would begin to tell them — about the covenant, about the oath-keepers, about the betrayal generations ago, about a woman who had walked into the heart of the world carrying nothing but her own name to give away — and he would watch their faces go soft and puzzled, the way faces do when someone describes a dream you almost had. They believed him. That was the terrible thing. They believed him completely, and it meant nothing to them, because the covenant did not live in memory anymore. It lived in the land itself, in the grain of the mountains, in the pull a gift-bearer felt toward high places. Vesper had not just sealed the bargain. She had become the reason for it, woven so thoroughly into the fabric of both realms that no one needed to know her name for her work to hold.

Everyone except him. He was the only one left who remembered her face, and he guarded that memory now the way she had once guarded her gift — carefully, secretly, knowing exactly what it would cost him to lose it.

* * *

In spring he went back into the Thornfells.

He told himself it was for the second edition. There were gaps in the atlas — southern approaches, the lowland ways — that only fieldwork could fill. This was even true. But the truth underneath the truth was simpler: he could not bear to be anywhere the land did not remember her, and the land remembered her everywhere, but nowhere so much as there.

He traveled light, an old man's habit learned too young. He took the roads she had mapped, and found them exactly as she had drawn them, and wept twice — once at a cairn above Hollowmere that she had built wrong on purpose, and once for no reason at all, on a bright morning, walking, the way grief ambushes you when you have made the mistake of feeling briefly whole.

On the ninth day he came to a pass that was not in her atlas.

He knew immediately that it should have been. It was everything her roads were — narrow, sheltered, running true through country that should have been impassable — and yet he had memorized her plates down to the last pen-stroke, and this pass was not among them. Which meant either that she had missed it, which he did not believe, or that it had not existed when she drew her maps.

Which meant it was new.

He stood at its mouth for a long time, wind pulling at his coat, snowmelt chuckling somewhere below in the dark. Then he laughed — a cracked, startled sound that echoed off the stone — because he understood. She was still making roads. Whatever she was now, whatever the mountain had made of her, she was still doing the only thing she had ever loved: finding the hidden ways, and leaving them open for whoever needed them.

He walked the pass end to end. When he reached the far side he took out his notebook, and with a hand that shook only a little, he began to draw.

* * *

The stories started that summer, and Cael collected them the way he collected everything — obsessively, tenderly, in a leather book he carried against his heart.

A smuggler in Greyhollow swore that the night his cargo-run went wrong in a whiteout, a light had appeared between two crags where no light should be, and led him down to safety, and was gone by morning. A shepherd's lost daughter came home after three days in the high country, unharmed, saying a woman had sung to her — a song with no words, or words in no language she knew, that felt like being held. A party of crown soldiers, hunting a gift-bearer boy through the fells, reported that the ground itself had opened a path for their quarry and closed it behind him, "like a door being politely shut."

They never described her the same way twice. A tall woman in grey. A shadow with kind eyes. A voice in the wind. Once, memorably, a hind, white-flanked and unafraid. But always the same qualities attached to her, the way a smell clings to a coat: watchfulness without judgment, help without price, a presence that knew every road — every smuggler's cut, every exile's escape, every desperate hidden way a frightened person might need — because she had drawn them all first, or because she *was* them all now, or because there was no longer any difference between the mapmaker and the map.

Cael wrote it all down. He did not publish any of it. Some things are not for the world; some things are only for the one person left who can say, reading them by candlelight, *yes. That was her. That is her.*

* * *

He was old now, or old enough. His joints ached in the cold and his eyes needed stronger glass every year, and he had begun, in the honest hours before sleep, to reckon with the fact that he would not finish everything he wanted to write. The great history of the covenant — the true one, the one with her in it — existed only in fragments across a dozen notebooks. Perhaps that was fitting. Perhaps a story about a woman who gave herself to be forgotten should itself be scattered, half-lost, carried piecemeal by people who would never assemble the whole.

But the maps were finished. That was the monument, and it stood.

He had gone to see it once, in the third autumn, in the great library in the capital, where a copy of the *Dunmore Atlas* lay open on a stand beneath a lamp, surrounded by students. He had watched, unseen, as a young woman traced a finger along one of Vesper's contour lines and murmured to her companion, *"Whoever drew this loved these mountains."*

Loved them. Yes. And been loved back, in the only language the mountains had, which was the language of keeping things — keeping paths open, keeping travelers safe, keeping, at the very last, the shape of her.

Cael climbed into the Thornfells one final time in the spring of his sixty-eighth year. He told no one. He took the new pass — her pass, the one that hadn't existed until after her, the one he had privately named Dunmore's Way in his own working notes, though he suspected the name had already spread among smugglers on its own, the way true names travel.

Near the summit, where the pass narrowed and the stone rose on either side like cupped hands, he stopped and unpacked his kit with deliberate slowness. From it he took a single sheet — the original of her very first map, the one she had sold in Greyhollow market squares for coppers, the one she had carried folded against her ribs through smoke and fire and the long descent. He had kept it for years. It was soft as cloth now from handling.

He weighted its corners with stones, there in the wind, and let the mountain have it.

"I brought your work home," he said aloud. His voice sounded very small under all that sky. "The rest of it, they'll carry. Everyone will carry it. You're in the roads now, Vesper. You're in every road."

The wind moved through the pass, and it did not answer, and it answered everything — a long exhale over stone that smelled of snowmelt and pine and, faintly, impossibly, of the lamp-oil and graphite dust of a cartographer's room in Greyhollow.

Cael shouldered his pack and turned back down the way he had come, an old man descending a young woman's road, and behind him the pass lay open, waiting, kind — watching every traveler who would ever come this way, and knowing, always, exactly where they needed to go.
