## 1. The Charm Seller's Hour

The night market in Vellamarch smelled of tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular desperation of people who had spent all day being cold and intended to spend the evening pretending otherwise. Kestrel Fairweather loved it the way a fox loves a henhouse: with affection, and entirely without shame.

He had his stall set up in the crook of an alley off Candlewick Row, which was not so much a stall as a plank balanced across two barrels, but he'd draped it in green cloth and arranged his wares with the care of a jeweler. Little glass bottles, each no bigger than a thumb, each stoppered with wax and tied with a ribbon of faded silk. To any passerby they looked like luck charms — and that was exactly what he sold them as.

*Luck, bottled fresh this morning,* his sign said, in handwriting he'd practiced until it looked like someone else's.

The luck was real, in its way. It just happened to be other people's.

Kestrel adjusted his scarf, checked the position of his lantern — angled to flatter the glass, cast his own face into soft shadow — and prepared for the hour's work. The Charm Seller's Hour, he called it: that stretch between the taverns emptying their first shift of drinkers and the witch-finders making their second circuit of the Row. Roughly forty minutes, if the patrol was punctual. Punctuality was one of the few mercies Vellamarch offered.

"Charms!" he called, pitching his voice low and warm, the voice of a man sharing a secret rather than shouting a price. "Bottled luck! Love-luck, coin-luck, door-luck for those whose locks have opinions!"

An old woman in a fishmonger's apron stopped first, as she always did on Thursdays, squinting at the bottles through eyes like two chips of river ice.

"That one," she said, tapping a bottle full of something gold and slow-moving, like honey remembering how to be light. "What's it do?"

"Warmth-luck, mistress. For hands that ache in the cold. Hold it at your wrist of an evening and think of summer."

This was true, more or less. What she'd actually be holding was a memory of a boy named Tam — no, not *that* Tam, another Tam, a baker's son from before — lying in a hayfield in July while the sun pressed down on him like a warm hand. Kestrel had bought it off him years ago for three coppers and a meat pie, back when he was still learning the trade and hadn't yet learned that some memories you shouldn't buy even when they're offered. A boy selling away his best summer day. There had been a sadness under that transaction that Kestrel had chosen, professionally, not to examine too closely.

The fishwife paid her copper, tucked the bottle into her apron, and shuffled off wearing the small, private smile of a woman who has just been promised July.

Kestrel exhaled and made a mental note: *never same customer twice.* That was Rule One, and he'd already broken it eleven Thursdays running, because the fishwife came every Thursday, and because a man had to eat, and because — if he were honest, which he tried not to be during working hours — he liked her. She reminded him of nobody, which in Vellamarch was nearly as rare as magic.

*Never same customer twice.* Because familiarity was a thread, and threads could be pulled, and somewhere at the end of every thread stood a witch-finder with a notebook and a patient expression and a wagon with a cage in it.

He glanced up the Row. No grey cloaks yet. Good.

A young man approached next, nervous, hat in hand, the look of someone about to do something foolish and hoping to purchase courage for it.

"I need—" He lowered his voice. "I need luck for asking."

"Asking what?"

"A girl. Marta. At the chandlery." His ears went red. "I've walked past her window nine times."

"Nine times," Kestrel repeated gravely, "and never once gone in. Yes. You need more than luck, friend, but luck will do for a start." He selected a bottle with something rose-gold flickering inside. "Confidence-luck. One drop on the tongue before you knock. Not two. Two makes you insufferable, and insufferable men get doors shut in their faces."

The young man paid, drank it right there in the alley — *never let them drink it there*, whispered Rule Two-adjacent wisdom, because a man who tasted a stranger's joy in public might weep, and weeping drew attention — and staggered slightly, blinking, as a dead soldier's memory of dancing with his sweetheart at a harvest fair settled over him like a coat that fit better than his own skin.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, that's — I feel *brave*."

"You're welcome. Go knock."

He went. He'd be back tomorrow wanting more, which was the trouble with selling feelings to people who didn't have enough of their own. Kestrel made another note against himself and decided, as usual, to ignore it.

Here is what you must understand about Kestrel Fairweather's trade, since he would certainly never explain it to you himself: he stole memories. Had done for six years. Not the crude theft of the old stories — no dream-walking, no silver needles behind the ear. Memory-keeping was subtler than that, a touch and a pour, a moment lifted out of a willing head and sealed in glass. Most of his suppliers sold gladly. Grief was heavy; people paid good coppers to put it down. And most of what he bought was small by design, because that was Rule Two:

*Never a large memory.*

Large memories had weight. Weight left ripples. A stolen childhood showed up missing; a stolen wedding day got noticed by somebody who'd been standing in the photograph. Small things vanished clean. The smell of rain on a specific street. Ten seconds of laughter. The exact color of a door you'd never see again. Nobody missed those. They slipped out of a life like a single stitch slipping out of a hem, and the hem held, and everyone went on warm and unaware.

And Rule Three, the rule above rules, the rule written on the inside of his skull in letters of ash:

*Never stay.*

Same stall no longer than a season. Same district no longer than a year. Same city — well. He'd been in Vellamarch fourteen months now, which was twelve months too long by his own arithmetic, and he knew it, and every week he told himself he'd move on soon, and every week the market felt a little more like home, which was precisely the sensation that got people burned.

Because magic was punishable by the pyre in Vellamarch. Not taxed, not licensed, not quietly tolerated the way it was in some cities with softer hearts or emptier treasuries. Punished. By fire. The Crown had decided, some decades back, that magic was a leak in the hull of the world, and leaks got plugged, and plugging was done with flame. The witch-finders enforced this with the serene efficiency of men who believed themselves janitors of the soul.

Kestrel had watched one pyre from a rooftop, once, early on. Just one. He'd made himself watch so he'd never forget what the stakes were, and then he'd been sick behind a chimney, and then he'd gotten very good at his rules.

"Oi. Charm-seller."

Kestrel's spine performed a small, controlled catastrophe before he turned. Standing at the mouth of the alley was a boy of perhaps ten, gap-toothed, holding a dead rat by the tail.

"That's a rat," Kestrel said.

"It's luck, innit? Heard you buy things what's lucky."

"Heard wrong. I *sell* luck. Buying's a different establishment entirely, several streets over, much nicer curtains."

The boy swung the rat hopefully. "Two coppers?"

"For the rat?"

"It's a *fat* rat."

"It is a fat rat," Kestrel conceded, "and I admire the entrepreneurial spirit, but no. However—" he fished in his pocket, "—one copper for you going away and telling no one where my stall is, which is worth considerably more than the rat, though I appreciate the rat as a gesture."

The boy took the copper, considered the rat, appeared to weigh whether the rat had further market value, and departed. Kestrel watched him go with the weary fondness of a man watching the future pick his pocket.

That was the thing about the Charm Seller's Hour. It was comfortable. That was the danger of it. Every night the same rhythm: the fishwife, the lovelorn, the insomniacs, the ones who wanted to remember what happiness felt like because they'd genuinely forgotten. He was good at it. He was *safe* at it. The rules worked. The rules had kept him alive through six cities and one very bad winter in Harrowgate, and if the rules had also kept him alone — well. Alone was a small price compared to ash.

Most nights he believed that.

Tonight, the believing sat oddly, like a coat buttoned wrong.

It had started at dusk, when he'd bottled a woman's memory of her mother's voice — a small one, thirty seconds, a lullaby fragment — and she'd wept with relief, and he'd thought, not for the first time: *I am a man who sells people pieces of themselves and calls it kindness.* And then he'd thought the worse thought, the thought he kept locked in the cellar of his mind: *and someday someone will sell me something I can't bear to hold, and then what will I be?*

He shook it off. Business was business. The lantern flickered. Somewhere up the Row, a barrel organ wheezed through a tune that had been cheerful in another decade.

Then the lamps at the far end of Candlewick guttered — not wind, nothing so innocent — and the crowd did the thing crowds always did, the silent parting, the sudden fascination with shop windows. Grey cloaks. Two of them, then a third, moving through the market with the unhurried confidence of men who owned the air.

Witch-finders. Early tonight. So much for punctuality.

Kestrel didn't run. Running was a confession. He began, instead, to pack — slowly, idly, the movements of a tired vendor closing up, bottles into the padded case, case into the satchel, satchel strap across his chest. The green cloth came off the plank and folded itself into his bag with the ease of long practice. In ninety seconds he was just a man leaving an alley, and the alley was just an alley, and the plank and barrels were nobody's business at all.

The lead finder paused near his corner. Young, sharp-faced, with the pale, well-fed look of someone raised on official bread. His gaze slid across Kestrel — the shabby coat, the honest weariness, the face of a man you forget while looking at him — and moved on.

Kestrel turned up Collar Lane, heart ticking fast and steady, and permitted himself the smallest smile. Fourteen months in Vellamarch and still invisible. The rules held. The rules always held.

He was three streets from his tenement, congratulating himself in the quiet smug voice he used only in private, when he heard the noise.

Not a scream. Worse. A *thud* — the heavy, graceless sound of a body meeting a door — followed by a wet, dragging breath.

His tenement door. His street. His night, apparently, having opinions.

Kestrel stopped. Every instinct he owned said *walk on, walk on, dead men are other men's problems, Rule Three, never stay.* His feet, traitorous things, carried him forward instead.

A man lay crumpled against his door. Courier's leathers, road-filthy, torn at the shoulder. Blood had soaked through the fabric in a way that suggested the wound had opinions of its own. The courier's face was grey as the witch-finders' cloaks, and his eyes — when they found Kestrel's — were burning bright and urgent as festival lanterns.

"You," the man rasped. "Fairweather."

Every hair on Kestrel's arms stood up and began quietly screaming. Nobody knew that name here. Nobody *should* know that name anywhere; he'd buried it two cities ago along with everything else.

"How—"

"Take it." A bloodstained hand seized his wrist with shocking strength and pressed something into his palm: a scroll, sealed with dark wax, warm — *warm*, unnaturally warm, like a living thing with a fever. "The Vault remembers. Do you hear me? The Vault *remembers*. Take it to—"

The strength went out of the hand like a snuffed candle. The courier's eyes stayed open, fixed on Kestrel, burning right up until they weren't.

For a long moment Kestrel stood in the frozen street with a dead man's grip cooling on his wrist and a warm scroll in his palm, listening to his own heartbeat and the distant wheeze of the barrel organ, which had finally, mercifully, stopped.

*Walk away,* said Rule Three. *Leave it. Leave him. Never stay.*

Kestrel looked down at the scroll. The wax seal gleamed darkly, and beneath the blood and the grime, faint as breath on glass, something was written on the parchment — something that had not been there a moment ago, in handwriting that made his stomach drop through the cobbles and into the cellars of the world.

His name. *Kestrel Fairweather.* Written in ink that was still drying.

The map, whatever it was, knew him.

And it was getting warmer.

* * *

Sergeant Hale of the Witch-Finders' Office stood over the body in Collar Lane and felt the particular satisfaction of a ledger balancing.

"Courier," said the constable beside him. "Crown issue, by the leathers. Deserted his route, looks like. Ran afoul of a cutpurse, most likely."

Hale crouched. Cutpurses didn't take the man's boots, his purse, or his ring. Cutpurses didn't leave a wound like that, either — a burn, low on the ribs, precise as a signature. He touched the cobblestones near the dead man's fingers. Warm. Still warm, in a street where every stone had been cold since autumn.

"Note the time," Hale said, rising. "And find out what he was carrying."

"There'll be nothing on him, Sergeant. Robbed clean, like I said."

Hale looked at the dead man's open hand — curled, grasping, empty — and at the faint smear of blood leading away from the body toward the alley mouth, as if something had been dragged, or handed off, or *taken*.

"Robbed clean," Hale agreed mildly. "Which means whoever robbed him is carrying something the Crown wants badly enough to kill for."

He pulled his collar up against the cold and smiled, thin as paper.

"And it's still warm."
