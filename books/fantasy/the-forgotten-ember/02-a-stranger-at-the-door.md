## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The trouble with living above a tannery, Kestrel Fairweather had long ago decided, was not the smell. The smell was honest, at least. It announced itself and asked nothing in return. No, the real trouble was that everyone assumed a man who could sleep through the stench of rendered hide would also sleep through anything else — which was why three separate burglars had tried his window over the years, and why all three had met the business end of a kettle of yesterday's tea.

He was awake now because the smell had changed.

Underneath the usual reek of the Tanners' Row — that thick, sour blanket the whole district wore like a coat it couldn't take off — there was something new. Copper. Wet copper, and beneath that, something stranger: a sweetness, like wax melting near a flame.

Kestrel sat up in his narrow bed and listened.

Footsteps on his stairs. Slow ones. The kind of footsteps a person makes when each stair is a negotiation. His building had six tenants and none of them climbed stairs like that; Mrs. Pell took them two at a time out of spite for her knees, and the boy in the attic moved like a cat who'd learned to apologize.

The footsteps stopped outside his door.

Kestrel did what any sensible man in Vellamarch would do. He reached under the floorboard by his bed, lifted out the small tin box where he kept his working stock — eleven little glass vials, each stoppered and sealed, each labeled in a plain hand with things like *Good Luck (Genuine)* and *Courage, Mild* — and held one ready in his fist. Not to sell. To *use*, if it came to that. A bottled memory of his mother's kitchen, warm bread and rain on glass. Poured into an attacker's mind, it would stop them mid-swing, weeping for a home they'd never had.

It was a terrible thing to do to a person. He'd only done it twice, and he'd felt sick both times, and he'd apologized to one of them afterward, which rather defeated the purpose.

"Who's there?" he called, keeping his voice flat and bored, the voice of a man with nothing worth stealing.

The answer was a thump. Then another. Then the sound of a body sliding down his door as though the door were a hill and the body very tired of standing.

Kestrel counted to five, told himself he was being foolish, counted to five again, and opened the door.

The man fell into his room.

He was young — younger than Kestrel, anyway, though it was hard to tell, because half his face was a mask of blood from a gash above his ear. He wore courier's leathers, grey and mud-spattered, the kind issued by no guild Kestrel knew, with no badge and no colors. His boots had walked a long way recently; the soles were worn through at the heel. And he was cold. That was the first thing Kestrel truly registered, kneeling beside him — not the blood, but the cold coming off him like the cold off a river stone, a deep cold that had nothing to do with the season.

"You're bleeding on my floor," Kestrel said, because panic makes people stupid and he'd rather be stupid about laundry than about anything else.

"Fairweather." The courier's hand shot out and caught Kestrel's wrist with a grip that had no business surviving whatever had put that gash in his head. "Kestrel Fairweather."

"I don't know you."

"No." The man almost smiled. It cost him. "Nobody does. That was the point." He fumbled at his chest, at a leather satchel strap gone dark with wet, and dragged out a scroll case — wax-sealed, black wax, stamped with a sigil Kestrel didn't recognize: a circle with a spark inside it, like an ember seen through a keyhole. "Take it."

"I don't take things from dying men," Kestrel said. "Bad for repeat business."

"*Take it.*"

There are voices that argue and voices that simply end discussion. This was the second kind. Kestrel took the case. It was heavier than it should have been, the way a full purse is heavy, and warmer than it should have been, the way a sleeping animal is warm.

"The Vault remembers," the courier whispered.

"What?"

"The Vault... remembers." His eyes were losing their focus, sliding toward the ceiling, toward somewhere past ceilings. "Tell it — tell it we didn't forget *him*. Tell it—"

And then he stopped telling it anything at all, because he was dead.

* * *

For a long moment Kestrel knelt there on his cold floorboards with a dead man's hand still loosely around his wrist, doing absolutely nothing, which was, he would reflect later, the most honest thing he'd done all year.

Then practicality arrived, as it always did, wearing its work apron.

He pried the fingers loose. He checked the pockets — not from greed, but because a dead courier's pockets told you who he'd been, and knowing who he'd been might tell Kestrel who would come looking. Three coppers, a stub of chalk, a strip of dried apple wrapped in cloth, and a scrap of paper bearing a single word in a careful hand: *Ember.*

No name. No guild mark. Nothing to trace.

"Well," Kestrel said to the corpse, conversationally, because talking to corpses was better than thinking. "You've made a spectacular mess of my evening."

The blood was already soaking toward the gap between his floorboards, and Kestrel had a sudden vivid image of it seeping down through Mrs. Pell's ceiling and dripping onto her prize geraniums, and Mrs. Pell climbing his stairs two at a time to ask about it, and having to explain a dead man. He fetched the bucket, the rags, the lye he kept for exactly this class of problem — though in fairness, until tonight, the class of problem had topped out at *drunk sailor vomits on stairs*.

While he worked, he thought.

Eleven years in Vellamarch had taught him the shape of its dangers the way a blind man learns a room. There were the ordinary dangers: knife-boys, debt-collectors, the winter itself, which killed the poor with the patience of a farmer. And then there were the witch-finders, who were not ordinary at all, who wore ash-grey cloaks and carried iron brands and paid coppers for whispers, and whose Sergeant Hale had once looked directly at Kestrel across a market square for four full seconds — four! — before moving on. Kestrel had gone home and drunk himself stupid and sworn off luck charms sold to anyone with a government face.

A dead man on his floor was a whisper waiting to happen. Dead men got found. Found men got questions, and questions in Vellamarch ended at the pyre more often than they ended anywhere else.

So: the body had to go. The river was closest, but the river gave things back; the tides were contrary and the mud was shallow and twice in his lifetime Kestrel had seen what the water coughed up after a bad week. The lime-pits behind the tannery, though —

He was actually standing there, coat on, calculating the weight of a grown man versus his own back, when he noticed the scroll case was glowing.

Not glowing, precisely. *Warm.* Warm the way a loaf is warm, warm from the inside, and through the black wax he could see — or thought he could see, or his eyes insisted he was seeing — a faint pulse of light, slow and steady, like breath.

Kestrel set it down on the table. Then he stepped away from it. Then, because he was Kestrel Fairweather and curiosity had been the death of better men and would presumably make an exception for him, he stepped back and picked it up again.

"Don't," he told it firmly.

It pulsed.

"That's not a no, that's just you being smug."

He turned it in his hands. The wax seal was intact — whoever had sealed it had meant it to stay sealed — and yet the warmth came through the glass and the parchment within like the warmth of a held hand. Magic, then. Real magic, not the bottled, diluted, harmless-as-milk magic Kestrel trafficked in, but the live kind, the kind that got people burned. In ten years of handling other people's memories he had never once touched anything that felt like this. Memories were cool. Memories were quiet. Whatever this was, it wasn't remembering.

It was *waiting.*

And then, because the night had apparently decided it hadn't humiliated him enough, the case grew warm against his palm and a voice spoke — not aloud, not in the air, but somewhere behind his eyes, in the toneless way that a name surfaces when someone says it across a crowded room:

*Kestrel.*

He dropped it. It landed on the table with a soft, patient thump, unbroken, unbothered, still pulsing.

"It knows my name," he said to the empty room. "The scroll knows my name. Wonderful. Splendid. I always wanted to be important."

The corpse on his floorboards offered no comment. Kestrel looked at it, and at the case, and at the door, and did the arithmetic every survivor in Vellamarch did without thinking: *what does this cost me, what does this buy me, can I walk away.*

Walk away. Yes. Wrap the body, dump it, move districts, start selling charms under a different mispronunciation of his own name. He'd done smaller versions of it before. It was the whole of his philosophy, really, distilled: never same customer twice, never a large memory, never stay.

But the case pulsed on the table, warm as a heart, and it had said his name, and underneath the fear — genuinely underneath it, layered below it like a seam of ore — Kestrel felt something he recognized with professional horror.

Recognition. The taste of a memory that fit.

Because here was the thing about Kestrel Fairweather that nobody knew, the thing he kept deeper than his tin box, deeper than his floorboards: he had no memories older than eleven years. None. His life began, as far as he could recall, on a river barge outside the city walls, aged perhaps fifteen, with a headache and a talent and no past whatsoever. He'd told himself stories about it — plague, probably, or fire, some ordinary catastrophe — but he'd never believed them, quite. You didn't lose your whole childhood to plague. Plague took pieces. Something had taken the whole.

And the case was warm, and it knew his name, and when he stood close to it he felt — faintly, horribly, wonderfully — like a lock feels toward a key.

"Oh no," Kestrel said quietly. "Oh, no, no, no. I am not a plot. I am a shopkeeper. I am a *very minor criminal* with excellent hygiene and no ambitions whatsoever."

The case pulsed, twice, gently. Almost fondly.

He covered it with a dish towel, which accomplished nothing, and went to deal with the body, which at least was a problem with known solutions.

* * *

Sergeant Corwin Hale had been a witch-finder for nineteen years, and in nineteen years he had learned that the city told you everything, if you listened the way you listen to a liar: not to what it said, but to how.

Tonight the Tanners' Row was saying something.

He stood at the mouth of the alley behind the tannery, lantern raised, while two of his men examined the ground. Rain had come an hour past — thin, grudging rain, the kind Vellamarch produced in lieu of weather — and it had softened everything, and it had also, usefully, shown him exactly what he wanted to see.

Drag marks. Faint ones, boot-heels dug deep in the mud, running from the service door of a tenement on Fairweather's street — he'd noted the address, 14 Tanner's Row, third floor, the flat above the dye vats — down toward the lime-pits. And at the edge of the alley, half-washed already, a smear of blood. Human. Recent.

"Somebody died up there," said Corporal Vetch, crouching over the smear. "Somebody died, and somebody else carried them out back."

"Somebody died," Hale agreed, "and somebody else was clever about it." Cleverness interested him. Guilt panicked; cleverness planned. Planning meant the survivor had something to protect.

He climbed the tenement stairs himself. The landlady — Pell, per the ledger, a woman built like a barrel and armed with opinions — claimed to have heard nothing, which Hale found credible, since she also claimed to have heard nothing during the knife-fight last spring and the flood the autumn before. Some ears were simply decorative. But the boy in the attic, a sharp-faced child of maybe twelve, watched Hale from his doorway with the particular stillness of children who know things, and when Hale passed him, the boy said, very quietly:

"Heard the third-floor man arguing with somebody. Late. Didn't sound like words. Sounded like..." The boy frowned, hunting for it. "Like humming. Low. Like a hive."

"A hive."

"Then it stopped all at once. Like somebody pinched it out."

Hale thanked him and gave him a copper, and the boy took it with the smooth ease of a professional, and Hale filed the exchange away under *assets.*

On the third floor he stood outside the door marked only by a scratch — tenants here didn't hold with numbers, numbers invited officials — and let himself feel the room beyond, the way he'd been trained to feel it, nineteen years ago, by instructors who were mostly dead now and mostly deserved it. Every occupied room had a texture. Fear had a texture. Grief had a texture. He pressed his palm flat against the wood and waited.

What came through was faint and strange and it made the hair on his forearms rise: a warmth. Deep in the room, low and steady, a warmth like a banked fire, like something alive and patient breathing behind a wall.

He had felt that exact warmth once before, nine years ago, standing in the tithe-hall beneath the palace, watching the King's collectors carry away crate after crate of sealed memories, and feeling the crates breathe.

Hale withdrew his hand. He stood in the corridor a moment longer, listening to the rain, and told himself the sensible things: a death, a disposal, common murder, common motive, the Crown's time was better spent elsewhere. The Hollow King's edicts were clear — unregistered magic was pyre-work, but a smuggler's corpse was merely constable-work, and constables were cheap.

But the warmth stayed with him all the way down the stairs, and out into the rain, and he caught himself walking slower, listening for humming.

* * *

By the time the sky went the color of dirty wool, the lime-pits had received a guest, the floorboards had been scrubbed to a blamelessness they'd never previously achieved, and Kestrel Fairweather sat at his table with the scroll case in front of him and a cup of chicory going cold at his elbow.

He had slept not at all. He had, at various points, attempted the following: throwing the case out the window (it was on the table when he turned around), burying it in the flour bin (the flour was fine; the case was on the table), and giving it a stern talking-to about boundaries (it had pulsed in a manner he chose to interpret as mockery).

Now, in the grey light, he turned it over one last time and read, for the hundredth time, the sigil in the wax. Circle. Spark. An ember behind a keyhole.

*The Vault remembers,* the dead man had said. *Tell it we didn't forget him.*

Him. Which him? Kings, Kestrel thought bitterly, were the only *hims* anyone in this city cared about, and the king had been dead these ten years, or worse than dead, which in Vellamarch amounted to the same thing with better ceremonies. Ten years of the Hollow King on the throne, beautiful and distant, issuing his gentle decrees — the curfews, the tithes, the collections — all *for warmth*, all *for the good of the city*, while the winters got longer and the poor got quieter and the witch-finders got busier.

Ten years. Eleven years. The numbers sat next to each other in Kestrel's head and refused to be strangers.

He looked at the case. The case pulsed. Behind his eyes, patient as tide, it said his name again — and this time, faintly, it said something else too, something that sounded like the far-off ringing of a bell in a white room, and a crowned man screaming, and Kestrel's hands began, entirely without his permission, to shake.

"Right," he said aloud, to the case, to the corpse-less room, to the morning. "I know a woman who deals in broken things."

He put on his coat, tucked the case inside it against his chest — where it settled, warm and satisfied, like a cat that has chosen its household — and went out into the cold to find Odile Vane.

The map, though he did not yet know to call it that, beat against his ribs the whole way, keeping time.

Keeping count.
