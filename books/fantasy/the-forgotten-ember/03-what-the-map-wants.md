## 3. What the Map Wants

The map would not burn.

Kestrel had set fire to things before—love letters, ledgers, a warrant with his old master's seal on it—and he knew the way paper took a flame, the way it curled and blackened like a leaf in autumn, surrendering with a soft hiss. Paper wanted to burn. It was practically eager.

This map lay in his chipped washbasin, drenched in lamp oil, and regarded him with the serene indifference of a cat being scolded.

He'd held the match to three of its corners. Three times the flame had touched down, shivered, and gone out, as though the oil itself had lost interest in combustion. The wax seal—the one the dying courier had bled over—sat untouched at the map's center, dark red and glossy, and Kestrel could have sworn that when he leaned close, it was warm. Not lamp-warm. Skin-warm.

"Right," he said to the empty room. "That's not ominous at all."

His garret above the tannery was six feet by nine and smelled permanently of lye and old smoke, which he had always considered excellent camouflage for a man who occasionally needed to explain away the smell of actual smoke. Tonight it felt less like camouflage and more like a coffin with a window. He'd nailed the shutter closed an hour ago out of habit, but he kept glancing at it anyway, listening for boots on the stairs. Witch-finders wore particular boots—heavy ones, with iron heel-plates, so you heard them coming and had time to be afraid properly. Sergeant Hale's were the worst. Everyone in the night market knew Hale's tread the way sailors knew a storm's first wind.

No boots. Just the tannery vats gurgling below, and the map, burning nothing, wanting something.

He crouched beside the basin and looked at it properly for the first time since the courier died against his door. He hadn't been able to help himself earlier; his fingers had traced the inked lines while the man's blood was still tacky on the parchment. Rivers rendered as veins. Mountains drawn in strokes that looked, if you squinted, like ribs. And at the heart of it all, beneath Mount Cindral, a small circle marked with a symbol Kestrel didn't recognize—a flame inside a closed fist.

*The Vault remembers.*

That was what the courier had whispered. Then he'd stopped whispering anything at all, and Kestrel had sat on his own doorstep holding a dead man's hand for longer than was sensible, because letting go felt like agreeing to something.

Now, in the lamplight, he noticed what he'd missed: the lines weren't ink. They moved. Slowly, the way frost creeps across glass—not visibly, not if you watched directly, but every time he looked away and back, the river had shifted a finger's width, the mountain's ribs had rearranged themselves slightly, as though the map were breathing. Or dreaming.

"No," Kestrel told it firmly. "Whatever you're doing, stop it."

The map did not stop. The map, he was beginning to understand, did not take direction well.

He should have thrown it in the Vell. He'd thought about it, standing at his window with the thing rolled under his arm like a stolen sausage. But the canal was frozen, and frozen canals had a way of giving things back come thaw, and besides—and this was the thought he kept circling back to, the one that made his stomach hurt—the courier had *chosen* him. Out of every door in that street, every passing cloak, the man had crawled to Kestrel Fairweather, memory-thief and charm-seller, and pressed this into his hands with the last of his strength. Things that chose you didn't drown. They waited.

And there was another problem, smaller and worse. When he'd first taken the map, before the blood, before the dying whisper, there had been a moment—one heartbeat—when his thief's senses had brushed against it the way they brushed against everything. Every object in Vellamarch had a flavor to him, faint as a smell: a locket tasted of its wearer's neck, a wedding ring of vows half-kept. Most things tasted of nothing much.

The map had tasted of grief. Old, enormous, royal grief—the kind of taste he knew from exactly one place, and that place was a memory he'd sold years ago and had never stopped regretting selling.

He poured more oil on it. Because he was thorough, and because panic makes men repeat themselves.

* * *

It went like this: he struck the match, and this time the flame caught.

For one glorious second, fire actually lived on the parchment, a small orange bloom near the western edge, and Kestrel felt relief so violent it was nearly laughter—

Then the flame turned white.

Not hot-white. Cold-white, the white of the space behind your eyes when you stand up too fast, and the light didn't spread across the map so much as it spread into *him*, up through his fingers where they gripped the basin's rim, and the garret fell away like a stage curtain dropping.

He was in a white room.

He knew, distantly, that he was still crouched on his floorboards, that his knees ached against cold wood, that somewhere his own lungs were working. But he was also *here*, in a room with no doors and no windows and walls that gave off no shadow, and in the center of it stood a man wearing a crown.

The crowned man was screaming.

There was no sound—that was somehow the worst part—but his mouth was open wide enough that Kestrel could see everything: the cracked lips, the broken tooth on the left side, the throat working around a howl that had been going on, by the look of him, for years. His hands were raised, palms out, the way you'd push back a wave. His eyes found Kestrel's across the impossible distance of the white room, and they were not angry eyes. They were begging eyes.

*Help me*, said the eyes. Or possibly: *Remember me.* With grief, Kestrel thought, those might be the same request.

And then the memory opened, the way memories do when you're a thief, all at once and from underneath, and Kestrel fell into it:

—A city under snow that fell in summer. A hospital ward converted from a granary, rows of cots, rows of faces going gray. A king walking those rows night after night because his physicians told him to stay away and he refused, pressing his bare hand to fevered foreheads, doing the only magic he permitted himself, which was presence. A little girl asking him if the snow was his fault, and the king saying yes, yes it is, and meaning it. A council chamber where men in fine wool explained, gently, the arithmetic of triage, and the king putting his fist through the table and then paying for the table out of his own purse and hating himself for the luxury of rage. A mass grave in frozen ground. Another. Another. The king counting them the way other men count coins. The king on his balcony watching the snow bury the city he could not save, and something in him—not breaking, worse than breaking—*opening*. A door swinging inward in the deepest cellar of his heart, and something cold and vast and patient looking out through the gap, wearing his sorrow like a borrowed coat, saying, in a voice like his own voice softened at the edges: *You cannot carry this alone. Let me.*

And the king—who was good, who was tired, who loved his people more than his own skin—saying yes.

Kestrel came back to himself on the floor of his garret with his cheek against the boards and oil soaking into his sleeve, gasping like a landed fish.

* * *

For a long time he lay there and let the shaking have him. That was rule one of his trade, learned in Odile Vane's drafty schoolroom a lifetime ago: *when a memory takes you, let it finish; fight it and it keeps a piece of you as payment.* He'd never taught that rule to anyone, never written it down, but he heard her voice saying it now, crisp and disapproving, and absurdly, lying in lamp oil on his own floor, he almost smiled.

Then he thought about what he'd just seen, and stopped smiling.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that made his hands unsteady when he pushed himself upright and made the room tilt like a deck in weather.

He recognized the taste of that memory. Not the images—he'd never seen the plague winter, had been a child picking pockets through it—but the *taste*, the flavor signature, the way a memory sits on the tongue of someone trained to taste them. Grief has flavors the way wine does. Common grief is thin, vinegary. Mother-grief is heavy, sweetish, cloying. He'd bottled hundreds.

Royal grief was different. Royal grief tasted of responsibility—the specific, terrible vintage of a man who believes every death in his care is a debt in his ledger. Kestrel had tasted it once before, seven years ago, in a charm he'd bought cheap off a palace laundress and resold at a markup he now deeply regretted. A fragment. A sliver of exactly this flavor.

He'd told himself it was a courtier's memory, some steward's inherited guilt. People sold strange things. But he'd known, even then, in the place where thieves keep the knowledge they don't want, that laundresses don't carry the king's grief in their pockets.

*The true king is dead,* Vellamarch said. Dead ten years, taken by the same winter that put the current sovereign on the throne—or rather, the current sovereign *was* the throne now, radiant and beloved, the king who ended the plague winter, the king whose face was on every coin. Ten years of warmth and mercy and gentle decrees.

Except the decrees had gotten colder lately. Except the tithe-collectors had started coming for memories instead of coin. Except the snow outside his window right now, in early winter, was falling thicker than any year he could remember, and the night market had felt, this season, like a city holding its breath.

And except that a dying courier had given Kestrel a living map that tasted of the dead king's grief and showed a crowned man screaming in a white room, and the crowned man's face, now that Kestrel made himself look at it honestly, made himself hold it in his mind the way he held stolen goods—

The crowned man's face was the face on the coin.

Older. Ruined. Screaming. But the same.

"Oh," Kestrel said quietly, to the room, to the map, to whatever was listening. "Oh, that's very bad."

* * *

He tried the hearth next. Proper fire, established fire, a grate full of tannery scraps burning hot enough to soften iron. He fed the map to it corner-first, holding it with tongs, and the flames roared around the parchment and left it pristine, cool as snow, while the fire itself guttered and dimmed as though the map were drinking the heat. Which, he realized, watching the flames shrink, it probably was.

The map was warm again in his hands afterward. Warmer. Almost feverish.

"You're not a map," he told it. "Maps don't eat fire."

The ink-lines had shifted again. The route beneath Mount Cindral was clearer now, the little circled symbol brighter, and new markings had surfaced along the edges—small, precise notations in a hand Kestrel almost recognized. Almost. The way you almost recognize a voice through a wall.

And then, because the night apparently intended to spend itself entirely on horrors, he saw the words appear along the map's southern border, surfacing through the parchment like bruises rising to skin, in ink the color of dried blood:

*KESTREL FAIRWEATHER.*

Just his name. Written in the margin of a map that had belonged to a dying courier who had never, until tonight, laid eyes on him.

He sat down hard on his stool. His trade had rules, and the rules existed because the world punished their absence: never the same customer twice, never a large memory, never stay. Small rules for a small life, and they had kept him alive through ten years of witch-finders and tithe-collectors and winters getting slowly, patiently worse. The rules said: burn it, dump it, run.

But the rules had never accounted for this. You couldn't run from something that knew your name, and you couldn't burn something that wouldn't burn—and here was the thought arriving now, quiet and terrible, the thought he'd been outrunning since the courier fell against his door:

Burning it would mean burning himself. Not metaphor. He understood, with the certainty of his own craft, that the map and the memory it carried were bound together, and that the memory had *chosen him*—had reached through a dying stranger's hands and picked the one person in Vellamarch who could taste what it was, who could carry it, who had spent his whole life handling other people's griefs and slipping them into bottles. The past had gone shopping, and it had bought a thief.

If he destroyed the map, he destroyed the last memory of a man who was maybe still alive, screaming in a white room, begging with his eyes. And whatever part of Kestrel the map had already threaded itself into—his name, written in its margins like a claim tag—it would take that too.

He looked at the shuttered window, behind which Vellamarch glittered under its too-heavy snow. Somewhere out there was a palace, and in the palace a beloved king whose smile everyone trusted, and beneath the smile, if the taste in Kestrel's mouth was honest, something cold and patient wearing ten years of stolen warmth.

Somewhere out there, too, was the only person alive who might make sense of this: a disgraced memory-keeper in the Saltrow district who drank too much and forgave nothing and had once told him, expelling him from her schoolroom, that he had talent and no conscience, and that someday the two would come due together.

Odile Vane owed him nothing. He owed her a great deal, none of it pleasant.

But she was the only keeper he'd ever met who could unbind a memory from an object without killing either, and tomorrow—if the witch-finders didn't kick his door in tonight, if the map let him sleep, if the world continued its usual policy of ignoring Kestrel Fairweather—tomorrow he would knock on her door and show her a thing that ate fire and wrote his name in blood.

He wrapped the map in his spare shirt, tucked it under the loose floorboard with his savings and his mother's ring—the one real thing he owned, though he couldn't have said why he kept it—and blew out the lamp.

In the dark, faintly, from beneath the floorboard, the map pulsed warm against the joists. Patient. Awake.

And Kestrel lay on his narrow bed listening to the snow pile against the shutters, and thought about a crowned man's begging eyes, and understood that whatever came next, he had already been chosen—and the past, unlike the witch-finders, never knocked first.
