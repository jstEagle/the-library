## 4. The Keeper of Broken Things

The map had spent the night on Kestrel's table, and by morning it had spent the night in his dreams too.

He'd tried everything short of fire—fire being the one thing he hadn't yet worked up the nerve for. He'd wrapped it in oilcloth and buried it under the floorboards; he'd found it back on the table, uncreased, unbothered, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long near a candle. He'd slept with it locked in his memory-box, the little iron-banded chest where he kept his own hoard, and woken at dawn with his teeth aching and a crowned man's scream still ringing behind his eyes like a struck bell that refused to finish dying.

So there was really only one person left to ask, and Kestrel hated her, in the way you hate someone who taught you everything you know and then watched you use it badly.

Odile Vane kept a shop in Tanners' Row, or what had once been a shop and was now mostly a shrine to her own stubbornness. The sign above the door read VANE & DAUGHTER — RESTORATIONS, which was a lie twice over: there was no daughter, and Odile did not restore things so much as she resurrected them, yelling at them the whole way up. Clocks. Music boxes. A taxidermied magpie with one glass eye that Kestrel had never once seen blink, and had never once stopped suspecting.

He stood across the street for a quarter hour, hood up, watching the door, doing the arithmetic every thief does before knocking on a dangerous door. *She'll want payment. She'll want an explanation. She'll want both before she opens the door, and she'll open the door anyway because she can't help herself.*

He crossed the street.

* * *

The bell over the shop door rang out three notes, flat and sour, and from somewhere in the back came a voice like gravel poured through silk: "If you're here about the clock, it's finished, and if you're here about anything else, it isn't."

"Good morning to you too, mistress."

Silence. Then the sound of a chair scraping, slow footsteps, and Odile Vane emerged into the gray light of the front room like a ship coming about. She was smaller than he remembered, or he was bigger than she remembered, and either fact clearly annoyed her. Her hair had gone from iron to frost without passing through any gentler color, and her hands—the hands that had taught him to hold a memory like a bird, firm enough that it couldn't fly, gentle enough that it wouldn't die—were spotted and knuckled and still the most frightening hands in Vellamarch.

"Well," she said. "The prodigal pickpocket."

"The prodigal *artisan*, if we're being precise."

"I'm never precise with people who've disappointed me. It implies they're worth the effort." She looked him up and down, cataloguing, the way she used to when he came back from practice runs with torn sleeves and excuses. "You're thinner. You're flinching at the bell. And you've been sleeping badly, which tells me you've been keeping something you shouldn't." Her eyes narrowed. "Whose is it?"

Kestrel opened his mouth to lie—he was very good at lying, it was practically a guild skill—and found, to his horror, that some part of him still stood up straighter under that gaze, the way a boy does when the one person whose opinion matters walks into the room.

"A courier died against my door last night," he said instead. Truth, then. He blamed the sleeplessness. "He gave me a map."

"A map."

"A map that won't burn. A map that knows my name. A map that—" He stopped, because saying the next part aloud would make it real, and real things had a way of getting people pyred.

Odile waited. She was excellent at waiting. She had once waited four hours for a client to finish crying before discussing payment, and had charged him for the hour regardless.

"It showed me a memory," Kestrel said finally. "A big one. Royal-grade. A man in a crown, screaming in a white room, and the grief in it was—" He searched for the word and hated every candidate. "It wasn't performed grief. It wasn't even natural grief. It was *harvested*. Somebody cut it out of him and it's been sitting in that map like a coal in ash, still hot after ten years."

Something moved behind Odile's face—quick, controlled, gone—but Kestrel had spent ten years reading faces for a living, and he caught it. Not surprise. Recognition.

"You already knew," he said quietly.

"I knew a courier came through Tanners' Row three days ago asking after me and wouldn't leave a name." She turned away, busying herself with the magpie, adjusting its perch with unnecessary force. "I also knew better than to answer the door after dark this week. Which is more than I can say for you."

"Mistress Vane—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip-crack of frost. "Don't stand in my shop with your dead man's map and your martyr's face and call me *mistress Vane* like I'm going to fix this. Whatever this is—and I can guess, because I'm old and Vellamarch only has two kinds of trouble, the Crown's kind and the worse kind—you walk it back out that door and you lose it in the river, and you thank whatever gods you don't believe in that the witch-finders haven't already kicked yours in."

"The river won't take it."

"Then drown it harder."

"It came *back*, Odile." Her first name, out loud, deliberately, and he saw it land like a stone in still water. "I buried it under my floor. I woke up and it was on my table. It knows my name. It's been whispering it in my sleep." He pulled the map from inside his coat and held it out between them, and even wrapped in its oilcloth he could feel it warming against his palm, patient as a cat. "I need a keeper. The best keeper I ever knew. And I am standing in her shop begging, so you can enjoy that for as long as you like."

For a long moment Odile said nothing. The magpie stared at them both with its single glass eye.

"You were never the best of my students," she said at last. "You were the most *careless*. There's a difference, and the difference is usually a funeral." But her hand was already extending, palm up, fingers curled in the old gesture—the gesture that meant *give it here, and mind how you hand it over.*

* * *

She unwrapped the map on her workbench, under the good lamp, with her sleeves rolled and her tools laid out in a neat silver row, and for a moment Kestrel let himself hope. This was the room where impossible things got taken apart. He'd sat on that stool at fourteen, watching her unpick a curse from a locket while explaining, in exhaustive detail, exactly how stupid the curse-maker had been.

Odile bent over the map. She didn't touch it at first; she read it the way keepers read, hovering her palms above the surface, tasting the air off it. Her lips moved slightly, counting layers.

"Blood seal," she murmured. "Old work. Older than the Guild's licensing, older than the tithes. Whoever made this learned before the College fell." Her hands drifted lower. "And underneath the bloodwork—"

She touched it.

Kestrel saw it happen: the memory leapt up the keeper's bridge, the invisible thread that ran from object to palm to mind, and Odile Vane—who had tasted ten thousand memories and flinched at none of them, who could hold a dying man's last hour steady as a surgeon holds a heart—went white.

Not pale. *White.* The color drained out of her face all at once, top to bottom, like water out of a cracked jug, and she snatched her hand back and pressed it flat against the bench as if steadying herself against weather.

"Mistress?" His own voice sounded far away. "What is it? What did you see?"

"The white room," she said. Her voice was level, which somehow made it worse. "The crowned man. You said a crowned man screaming."

"Yes."

"That's not how I'd describe it." She turned and looked at him, and Kestrel felt the floor tilt under his certainty, because Odile Vane had not looked afraid in the entire time he'd known her, and she looked afraid now. "I'd say: King Aldric, in the vault beneath the palace, on the night they told us he died of winter-fever. Except kings don't die of fever in vaults. And kings don't scream like that unless someone is *taking* something out of them." She swallowed. "That's his grief, boy. That's the true king's grief, sealed in wax and blood, and it's been burning in that map for ten years while the whole city mourned a grave that's empty."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked twice, like a cautious knock at a door.

"That's not possible," Kestrel said. "Aldric's been dead ten years. Everyone—there was a mourning year. They closed the tanneries. My mother cried."

"Your mother cried because everyone cried, and everyone cried because they were *told* to, and the ones who told them have been sitting on a throne wearing his face ever since." Odile straightened slowly, and Kestrel watched her do the thing she did best: take terror and fold it, crease by crease, into purpose. "Ten years ago the Hollow King took the throne. Ten years ago the tithes started. 'Voluntary,' they call them—one small memory per household per season, for the war effort, for the granaries, for warmth. And every tithe goes down into the palace dark, and nothing ever comes back up." She looked at the map. "Except now something has. Something walked out of that vault carrying the king's own grief, and it chose *you*, of all the thieves in this rotten city, to carry it."

"Why me?"

"Because you're a thief who steals memories and sells them back to people as luck, which means you're the only criminal in Vellamarch with a conscience, and conscience is exactly the tool this job requires." She was already moving, pulling a battered traveling case from under the bench, throwing things into it with the brisk violence of a woman who had done this before and resented having to do it again. "Or possibly because the map is stupid. Both are possible."

"Wait." Kestrel put his hand flat on the case lid before she could close it. "Wait. I didn't ask you to come. I asked you to unbind it. There's a difference, and the difference is I'd rather you stayed alive."

Odile looked at his hand on the case. Then up at him. And something in her expression shifted—not softening, exactly; Odile did not soften, she *thawed*, briefly and grudgingly, like a window in spring.

"When you were fifteen," she said, "you stole a memory you shouldn't have. A widow's memory of her wedding day. You sold it to a charm-buyer for six coppers and a meat pie, and when I asked you why, you said the widow wasn't using it anymore." She closed the case. "You cried that night in my back room and swore you'd never sell a living memory again, and I believed you, because liars cry prettier than that. So no, boy. I don't trust you." She picked up the case and pushed past him toward the door, pausing just long enough to snuff the lamp. "But I trust that you'll do the right thing eventually, loudly, and get yourself killed doing it, and I have buried enough students. Get your coat. We're going north."

"North?" Kestrel scrambled after her. "North is—Odile, north is the river road, and past that it's witch-finder country, and past *that*—"

"Past that is a mountain, and under the mountain is a vault, and in the vault is a king who's been screaming for ten years while we sold his memories for coppers." She locked the shop door with a key she wore on a cord around her neck, next to a second key that Kestrel had never asked about and suddenly, horribly, wanted to. "And if we're quick, we might reach it before midwinter. And if we're not—"

She stopped. Down the length of Tanners' Row, gray in the morning smog, a pair of witch-finders stood at the corner talking to a fishmonger, their gray coats the exact color of the weather, and one of them was holding up something small and wax-sealed for the fishmonger to look at.

The courier's seal. Kestrel knew its shape the way he now knew his own heartbeat.

"—then we're not," Odile finished softly. She took his sleeve and drew him into the alley behind the shop, moving with a speed that made nonsense of her age. "Back way. Don't argue. And boy—if anyone asks, you're my nephew, and you're simple, and you're *very* fond of clocks."

"I hate clocks."

"Then today you love them." She glanced back once at the shop sign—VANE & DAUGHTER—and something flickered across her face, old and private and gone before he could name it. Then she pulled her hood up, and the Keeper of Broken Things stepped into the smog with a dead man's map burning against her apprentice's ribs, and neither of them looked back.

Behind them, in the dark of the shuttered shop, the taxidermied magpie blinked.
