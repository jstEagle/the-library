## 5. Ashes on the Wind

The safehouse smelled of woodsmoke and burnt sugar, which Kestrel thought was either a very clever disguise or a very stupid one.

He had followed the rumor through three districts and two liars: a woman in Lowmarsh who took in the marked, fed them, and asked nothing but silence. Rumors like that were usually bait. Witch-finders had learned long ago that the desperate traveled toward kindness the way moths traveled toward flame, and it was cheaper to let them gather than to hunt them one by one.

But the burnt sugar decided him. Nobody faking a sanctuary bothered with sweets.

He knocked the way Odile had taught him years ago—three quick, two slow, one quick—and immediately regretted it, because that was *their* knock, and if anyone inside had ever been Keeper-trained they'd know exactly what kind of thief was standing at their door.

Footsteps. A bar sliding. The door opened six inches and stopped against a chain, and through the gap came an eye the color of a banked fire—amber shot through with something darker, something that had survived being burned.

"No charms," said the woman. "No fortunes. No bottled anything. If you're selling, walk."

"I'm not selling."

"Everyone's selling." But she looked at him a moment longer, and he felt her look the way you feel weather change—the particular attention of someone who had learned to read people fast because reading them slow got people killed. Her gaze snagged on his hands. "You've got keeper's calluses. Old ones. And you flinched when I said *bottled*, which means you know what's really in the bottles sold at the night market." She unhooked the chain. "Get in before someone sees you hesitating on my step. Hesitation's louder than a scream in this district."

Inside, the safehouse was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the stove. Seven or eight people occupied the low room—a boy asleep under a table with his boots still on, an old man mending a coat that had more patches than coat, a young woman practicing small, careful motions with her fingers, tracing shapes in the air where faint light clung and faded like breath on glass. Everyone glanced up when Kestrel entered, assessed him, and went back to what they were doing. It was the most disciplined room he'd ever stood in.

"Sable," said the woman, offering no hand. Her right arm ended just below the elbow, sleeved and pinned neatly. "You're the charm-seller from Gullmarket. Don't ask how I know. Half my people buy your luck-charms when they can't sleep, and every single one of them sleeps better after, which told me you were either honest or very good. In this city those are usually the same thing."

"You know my work," Kestrel said carefully, "but not my name."

"Names are debts here. You'll give yours when you want to." She jerked her chin toward the back of the room. "Tea's on the stove. Sit. You didn't come for tea, though. You came because you need to leave Vellamarch, and everyone knows I'm the one who moves people out along the river barges. Am I wrong?"

"You're not wrong."

"Then we have a problem, because I don't move people out anymore. Not since the tithe-census." She poured two cups anyway, and handed him one, and the warmth of it was so sudden and ordinary that his throat tightened around nothing. "Sit down, charm-seller. Let me tell you about my sister."

* * *

Her name had been Wren. Sable told it plainly, without performance, the way people tell things they have told themselves ten thousand times in the dark until the telling wears smooth as river stone.

"We grew up in the Tanneries. Both marked—our mother's line. Wren's gift was small and lovely: she could warm things. Bread, bathwater, cold hands, cold rooms. Just warmth. That's all. The witch-finders used to say magic was pride and hunger and the desire to be greater than your neighbor, and I'd like them to explain to me how warming bread is pride." Sable's voice didn't rise. That was somehow worse than if it had. "When the purges came to Lowmarsh, we ran. We almost made the river gate. Wren stopped to pull a stranger out of a snowdrift—a drunk clerk who'd frozen halfway home. She warmed him back to life. It took her four minutes. Sergeant Hale's patrol took three."

Kestrel's cup had gone still halfway to his mouth.

"They burned her on the pyre in Cindral Square. Public edification, they called it. I was in the crowd because they make the families watch—that's policy, did you know? They say grief is contagious and watching cures it." Her jaw worked. "I watched. And then I spent two years learning the routes, the bribes, the barge schedules, and I built this place, and I got people out. Forty-one people. I counted. You count too, don't you? I can tell. We're the same kind of bookkeeper."

"How many did you get out before the census?"

"Forty-one total. Zero since." She set her own cup down untouched. "Three months ago the Crown started the memory tithes. 'Voluntary,' the proclamations say. Every citizen may offer a cherished memory to sustain the realm through winter. And the barges—" she laughed, short and bitter, "the barges now get searched by tithe-collectors with soul-glasses. Anyone leaving the city gets their memories weighed. If you're carrying too much joy, they assume you're hiding magic, because magic lives in joy, apparently. So now nobody leaves. Now we just accumulate. This house is getting full, charm-seller, and full houses burn faster than empty ones."

The sleeping boy shifted under the table. Sable lowered her voice without seeming to decide to.

"So. You want passage out. I could once have given it. What I can give you instead is a question." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the scars climbing her neck—old ones, deliberate ones, the marks of a body that had been too close to its own burning. "They keep records at the Crown archive. Tithe-records. Every memory taken, catalogued, stored in glass like your little charms, only theirs are legal. Wren was taken alive, did I say that? No—I always skip it. She wasn't burned first. They harvested her *before* the pyre. Standard procedure for the marked now: strip them, then burn the empty shell, because a shell burns quieter and doesn't scream things about the harvest." Her voice stayed level. Her hands did not. "Wren's last day is in that archive. Her last morning. The snowdrift. The clerk. Me, pulling her sleeve, saying *run, Wren, run*. All of it, filed away in some drawer by men who think a person's final hour is paperwork."

She looked at him then with both eyes, and Kestrel understood why people followed this woman into danger. It wasn't charisma. It was that she had taken the worst thing that had ever happened to her and refused to let it be useless.

"Steal it back," she said. "My sister's final memory. Get it out of the Crown archive and bring it here, and I will put you on a barge myself, past every soul-glass on the river, because I know which collectors take bribes and which ones take naps. That's the price. No memory, no passage."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you're welcome to the tea, and the door's behind you, and I'm sorry for whatever you're running from. I help everyone I can. But I don't spend my last route on anyone who won't spend theirs first." She sat back. "Think about it. Most people do their best thinking over tea."

* * *

Kestrel thought about it in the corner of the room, near the stove, while the marked of Lowmarsh moved around him with the quiet choreography of people accustomed to fear.

He thought: *it's impossible.* The Crown archive sat behind the Hall of Records in the palace quarter, triple-warded, patrolled by guards whose memories were audited monthly so they couldn't be bought or blurred. He had never robbed anything bigger than a merchant's strongroom. The archive was a mountain wearing a building.

He thought: *she's asking me to steal a dead woman's dying.*

And underneath both thoughts, quiet as a coin slipped into a pocket, was the map. It lay against his ribs where he'd bound it beneath his shirt, and it had been warm all evening—warmer since he'd entered this house, warmer still whenever Sable spoke her sister's name, as if it were listening. As if it approved.

*The Vault remembers,* the courier had whispered. And Kestrel was beginning to suspect the map remembered everything, including things that hadn't happened yet.

"You're doing the face," said a voice beside him. The young woman with the fading light on her fingertips had settled onto the bench nearby, uninvited, with the ease of someone raised in crowded rooms. Up close she was maybe nineteen, sharp-faced, with ink stains on her cuffs. "The face where you're deciding whether a person is worth the risk. Everyone does it. She hates it."

"I'm sorry—"

"Don't apologize, just stop doing it. She already knows the answer." The girl nodded toward Sable, who was pretending very badly not to listen while mending the old man's coat. "She's been waiting three months for someone with keeper's hands to walk through that door. You're the fourth guess. The other three were smugglers."

"What happened to the smugglers?"

"She gave them tea and sent them home." The girl grinned. "I'm Pip. I do the inkwork—forged passes, altered censuses. I was going to be a real scribe before they found out I glow when I'm happy. Which, honestly, ruined my career prospects and my poker face simultaneously." She stuck out her hand, then reconsidered, glancing at her fingertips. "Actually, don't shake that. Give it a minute."

Kestrel found, to his surprise, that he was smiling. When had he last smiled in front of strangers? Rule three: never stay. He was staying. He was drinking their tea and learning their names and letting a girl with luminous fingerprints joke at him across a stove, and every rule he lived by was fraying like cheap rope.

"The archive," he said to Sable, across the room. "What do you know of its layout?"

Sable set down the coat. Something in her posture changed—not hope exactly; hope was a luxury she'd clearly stopped affording herself. More like a door unlocking somewhere deep behind her eyes.

"Pip," she said. "Get the drawings."

Pip produced a leather tube from under the floorboards with the flourish of a magician, and spread out sheets of paper covered in beautiful, illegal penmanship: the Hall of Records, the archive annex, guard rotations annotated in a tiny furious hand.

"Sub-level three," Sable said, tapping the page. "That's where the tithe-vaults are. Glass racks, climate-wards, a registrar named Corvane who signs everything twice because he trusts no one, which I respect enormously and intend to exploit. Memories are stored by harvest-date. Wren's would be—" her voice caught, just once, a hairline crack in masonry, "—eleven years ago, ninthmonth, third week. Marked-class harvest, batch forty-one."

"Batch forty-one," Kestrel repeated softly.

"It's not lost on me," Sable said dryly. "The universe has a sense of humor. It's a cruel one, but it's punctual."

He studied the drawings. His mind was already doing what it did—unspooling routes, weighing doors, tasting the shape of the theft the way other people tasted wine. It was a good plan waiting to happen. It was also suicide with extra steps.

And beneath his shirt, the map pulsed. Warm. Patient. *Yes,* it seemed to say. *The archive. Of course. One thing leads to another; that is what roads are.*

"Why me?" he asked. "You've had three months of desperate people through that door. Why wait for a charm-seller?"

"Because a smuggler steals *things*," Sable said. "A keeper steals *what things mean*. And I'm not sending anyone to fetch glass. I'm sending someone to fetch my sister." She held his gaze. "Well, charm-seller? Do we have a bargain?"

Kestrel looked around the room—at Pip, grinning; at the old man, mending; at the boy asleep under the table with his boots on, dreaming whatever dreamed children dreamed in houses like this. Forty-one people out. Zero since. A woman counting losses the way misers counted coins, except she spent hers constantly, on strangers, on warmth, on burnt sugar in the air of a city that wanted her dead.

Rule three: never stay.

"Batch forty-one," he said. "Ninthmonth, third week. Sub-level three." He put out his hand. "We have a bargain."

Sable shook it. Her grip was iron wrapped in scar tissue, and for half a second her composure slipped and he saw underneath it—not gratitude, not relief, but something rawer and younger, the face of a girl in a crowd watching a pyre, promising herself that someday, someday, someone would bring her sister home.

"Then listen close," she said, "because Pip's drawings are good but Corvane signs everything twice, and you'll need to be better than good."

Outside, the wind rose off the river, carrying ash from some distant chimney, some distant burning, and pressed against the shutters like fingers checking a lock. Against Kestrel's ribs, the map beat once—slow, satisfied, patient as winter—

—and for the first time, he heard it. Not words. A pulse. A heartbeat that was not his own, keeping time with his, as if the road to the Ember Vault had just taken its first step, and it was walking in his shoes.
