## 6. The Debt of Corporal Brann

Corporal Tam Brann had been a guard for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned exactly three things worth learning.

First: the city of Vellamarch smelled different at every hour, and the hour before dawn smelled like wet ash and old bread, which was to say it smelled like honesty.

Second: most crime was hunger wearing a mask, and if you caught the mask early enough you could sometimes talk the face underneath into something better.

Third — and this was the one he kept folded up small inside himself, like a letter he'd never send — there was a hole in his memory where a night should have been, and whatever had filled that hole had saved his life.

He thought about the third thing more than he ought to. It was an odd sort of wound, a missing memory. It didn't hurt. That was the trouble. A man could lose a finger and know what he'd lost, count the things he couldn't do anymore. But a missing memory just sat there, smooth-edged, like a tooth pulled so long ago you'd forgotten which one it was. He knew only this: before the hole, he had been a man who flinched at burning things. After it, he wasn't. And once — only once — he'd caught a whiff of some charm-seller's wares in the night market, something sweet and smoky, and his whole body had gone strange with gratitude, the way a dog goes still when it smells the hand that once fed it.

He'd never found the seller again. He'd stopped looking. Some debts you pay forward instead.

Which is why, on the morning everything went wrong, Tam Brann was walking his rounds along Archive Row with his collar up and his thoughts elsewhere, thinking about how the tithe-collectors had come to Cobbler's Lane again, third time this season, and how Mrs. Ellery the cobbler's widow had looked when they left — not weeping, which would have been something, but *smoothed*, like a bed made by someone who didn't care who slept in it next.

"Evening, Corporal." The lamplighter touched his cap.

"Morning, actually," Brann said. "Barely."

"Feels like evening," the lamplighter said, and struck his match, and Brann watched the flame catch and did not flinch, and hated, quietly, that he ever had.

* * *

The Crown Archive of Vellamarch occupied a whole square of the Old Quarter, a fat grey building with windows like closed eyes. Officially it held tax rolls, land grants, forty years of weather records nobody read. Unofficially, everyone knew it held more. The tithe-records. The Confession Ledgers. Names.

Brann's route took him past its eastern wall at half past five, because Sergeant Hale liked the route walked twice before shift change, and Hale liked things walked twice the way misers liked coins counted twice.

At half past five, then, Brann heard a sound from the archive's delivery yard that no honest building makes: the particular, patient scrape of someone picking a lock who has done it many times and expects to do it many more.

He stood very still in the blue-grey light and considered his options, of which there were formally two — challenge the intruder, or sound the bell — and informally about eleven, most of which involved pretending he hadn't heard anything at all and going to get breakfast.

Curiosity won, as it usually did. Curiosity was the one appetite the Guard's meager pay never starved.

He came around the corner of the coal shed and there, crouched at the delivery door with a pair of thin picks and an expression of profound professional annoyance, was a young man in a coat too good for him, dark-haired, sharp-faced, with the look of someone who had rehearsed being invisible and resented having to perform it live.

The young man looked up. Their eyes met.

"Hallo," said Brann.

"Don't arrest me," said the young man, "and I'll explain."

"That's usually how explanations work, yes."

"I mean don't arrest me *first*. Most guards skip straight to the arresting part. There's shouting, there's running, somebody gets hit with a baton. Very inefficient."

"You've done this before."

"Once or twice." The young man rose slowly, hands visible, picks palmed away with a sleight so smooth Brann almost missed it. Up close he was younger than his face suggested — twenty-four, maybe twenty-five — and tired in a way that went down to the floor joists of him. "Look. You're going to want to shout. I can see it in your shoulders. Before you do — do you remember a night, about four years back? Fire at the tannery on Sallow Street?"

Brann went cold all over, which was absurd, because it was already freezing and the cold had nowhere further to go.

"No," he said carefully. "I don't."

"No," the young man agreed softly. "You wouldn't. I'm the reason you don't." He held out his hand, palm up, empty now. "Kestrel Fairweather. Memory-thief, charm-seller, occasional rescuer of guards who are too stubborn to die when a roof comes down on them."

The world tilted gently, the way a boat tilts when something large passes underneath. Brann had imagined this meeting, in the way you imagine meeting a dead parent — often, and never seriously. He had imagined gratitude. He had imagined questions. What came out of his mouth instead was:

"The tannery fire killed six people."

"It nearly killed seven."

"And you took—" Brann pressed his knuckles against his sternum, against the smooth place where the memory should have been. "You took it out of me. Without asking."

"I did ask." Kestrel's voice was quiet and entirely without apology, which somehow made it worse. "You were pinned under a beam with your leg broken in two places and the fire was coming through the wall, and you were screaming that you couldn't feel your feet, and I asked if you wanted to forget, and you said yes. You said yes four times. I counted."

Brann stared at him. Somewhere behind them the city was waking up; carts were rattling over the far bridge; a fishwife was swearing at a gull with real artistry. None of it seemed to be happening in the same world as this yard.

"Why?" he managed. "Why would you— you sell memories. Bottles in the night market. Luck charms. That's your trade. Why give one away free?"

Kestrel looked, for a moment, genuinely embarrassed. "Because you were going to die screaming, and I couldn't carry you, and it was the only thing I had. Also," he added, with the air of a man determined to ruin his own moment of nobility, "you grabbed my wrist hard enough to leave bruises and I value my wrists."

Something in Brann's chest unclenched — a thing clenched so long he'd mistaken it for architecture. Four years of wondering what kind of thief robs a dying man of his pain, and the answer was: this kind. This tired, sharp-tongued, guilty-eyed kind.

"You should go," Brann said.

"I can't."

"You're standing outside the Crown Archive with lockpicks. Whatever you're after, it isn't worth the pyre."

"It's worth more than that." Kestrel glanced toward the delivery door, and Brann saw it then — really saw it — the way the man's whole body leaned toward the building like a compass needle. "There's a memory in there that doesn't belong to the Crown. It belongs to a king everyone thinks is dead. And there's a woman named Sable whose sister died on the pyre, and the Crown took her sister's last memory as evidence, and evidence gets destroyed after seven years, and it has been six years and eleven months."

Brann felt the words land one by one, like stones dropped into a well. *A king everyone thinks is dead.* He should have laughed. He should have shouted, rung the bell, done his duty. Instead he heard himself say:

"Evidence doesn't get destroyed. Not anymore. Not since the Proclamation. Everything goes to the deep vaults now. Everything is kept."

"Kept for what?"

And there it was — the question Brann had been carrying around for months like a stone in his boot. Kept for what? Ten years of tithes, ten years of *voluntary offerings*, ten years of the Hollow King's beautiful voice telling Vellamarch that warmth must be shared, that memory was a kind of warmth, that the city would endure the long winters together. And every winter longer than the last. Every spring later than the one before.

"I don't know," Brann said honestly. "That's the problem."

* * *

Here is what Corporal Tam Brann knew, and had known for some time, and had told no one, because telling no one is a skill guards perfect alongside marching:

He knew that the tithe-collectors never took from the wealthy districts. He knew that the ledgers of *voluntary* offering were written in three different hands, none of them belonging to the people whose names they bore. He knew that old Pem Halloway, who'd given a tithe of his war memories two winters back, had stopped coming to the Broken Cask, and when Brann had asked after him, Pem's own daughter had smiled blankly and said, *Who?*

He knew that the Hollow King's decrees always sounded like kindness. *For warmth. For unity. For the city's endurance.* And he knew that kindness that arrives with soldiers attached is not kindness; it is inventory.

He had said none of this aloud. He had a mother in Marsh End and a pension he was rather fond of and a face that would burn nicely, he supposed, if it came to it.

But standing in that freezing yard, watching Kestrel Fairweather check the street over his shoulder with the ease of long habit, Brann felt the stone in his boot turn over and settle somewhere new.

"My sergeant," Brann said slowly, "is Sergeant Hale. Witch-finder's man, seconded to the city watch. If anyone asks, I walked this route, I saw nothing, and I stopped at the pie stand on Cordwainer Street for approximately six minutes, during which time I am famously slow about choosing."

Kestrel blinked. "You're covering for me."

"I'm covering for myself. You're a figment. A stray dog. Possibly a very ambitious cat."

"A cat."

"Cats get everywhere. Nobody arrests cats." Brann pulled off one glove and rubbed his face, feeling absurd and light-headed, like a man stepping off a ledge and discovering he could fly but only badly. "Fairweather. The memory you're after — the sister's. When you find it. What happens to her? To Sable? Does she get it back?"

"If I can manage it, she feels it whole again. Her sister's last words, her sister's face. Everything the Crown filed away under *evidence*."

Brann nodded once, sharply, the way he nodded when accepting a post assignment he didn't like. "Then go. Delivery door sticks; lift as you push. Third corridor, left at the map case, stairs down. The deep vaults are behind a door with no handle — there's a bell-pull disguised as a sconce, green tarnish on the left side. And Fairweather—"

Kestrel paused, one hand already on the door.

"The night at the tannery. Whatever you took out of me. I've spent four years wondering what it cost *you* to carry it, even briefly." Brann cleared his throat, embarrassed by his own sincerity, and reached for the nearest available armor, which was humor. "I hope it wasn't as boring as my dreams. I dream about ledger arithmetic. It's a curse."

For the first time, Kestrel smiled — a quick, crooked, startled thing, like a match struck indoors. "Worse," he said. "There was a horse in it. You loved that horse terribly. It was called Duchess and it could do no wrong."

"Duchess," Brann repeated, stricken. "I've never owned a horse."

"Now you know why." And Kestrel slipped through the door like smoke finding a draft, leaving Brann alone in the yard with the settling snow and the extraordinary, terrifying knowledge that he had just committed treason before breakfast.

* * *

He bought the pie. It seemed important, afterward, to have bought the pie — to stand at Cordwainer Street haggling over a mutton pie with Marn the pieman while his heart hammered like a debtor at the door. Marn gave him extra gravy out of pity. Brann ate it standing up, watching Archive Row, seeing nothing, hearing nothing, being famously slow about choosing.

It was nearly seven when the bells began.

Not the shift-change bells. The other ones — the high, thin bells from the witch-finders' station that Brann had heard perhaps five times in eleven years, and had hoped, in the superstitious way of men who knock wood, never to hear again.

By eight, the story was everywhere, moving through Vellamarch faster than the frost: a courier dead in the Lamplighters' Ward, throat opened, a bloodstained map missing. By nine, Sergeant Hale stood in the watch-house yard with a warrant in one hand and a description in the other, and the description was of a young man, dark-haired, sharp-faced, seen fleeing the district where a certain charm-seller worked the night market.

"Fairweather," Hale read aloud, tasting the name. "Kestrel Fairweather." He looked up, and his pale eyes went down the line of guards like a man counting spoons. "Anyone know him?"

Brann kept his face arranged into the mild, bovine blankness that had served him through eleven years of inspections. *A stray dog,* he reminded himself. *A very ambitious cat.*

"No, Sergeant," said the line, in the flat chorus of men who valued their pensions.

Hale's gaze snagged on Brann — just for a heartbeat, just long enough — and moved on.

That night, Brann walked his route twice, as Hale liked, and passed the archive's eastern wall, and stopped, and stood a long moment in the falling snow, looking at the delivery door where a thief had once saved his life and he had repaid the debt with a pie and a lie.

The law, he thought, was the law. The law had fed Pem Halloway into a ledger until his own daughter forgot his name. The law had burned Sable's sister and filed her last words under *evidence*. The law wore a crown that never quite fit the face beneath it, though Brann couldn't have said why he thought so — only that some mornings, at proclamation readings, when the herald cried *long live the Hollow King*, the words landed strangely, like a key turning in a lock that opened onto nothing.

Four years ago, a stranger had asked him a question while he lay dying under a beam: *Do you want to forget?*

Standing in the snow, Brann realized he'd been answering it wrong ever since. The question had never been about the fire.

He turned up his collar and walked on, and in his breast pocket, folded small, was the note he'd found slipped under the watch-house door at dusk, in handwriting quick and slanted and faintly singed at one corner:

*Odile Vane taken. Rooftops scattered. If you're reading this, thank you, and I'm sorry, and meet me where the river forgets its name — midnight, two nights hence. Bring nothing you love. — K.*

Brann read it four times. Then he did the bravest and simplest thing of his eleven years of service.

He went home, packed a bag, kissed his sleeping mother's forehead, and left the watch-house keys on the table beside a note that said only: *Gone to pay a debt.*

The bells of Vellamarch rang the curfew behind him, sweet and cold, like ice cracking over deep water.
