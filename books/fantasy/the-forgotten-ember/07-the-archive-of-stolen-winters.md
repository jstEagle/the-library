## 7. The Archive of Stolen Winters

The Crown archive did not look like a place where a city's soul was kept in jars.

It looked like a tax office. That was the first horror of it, Kestrel decided—the banality. Grey stone, grey clerks, grey light falling through high windows onto ledgers and inkwells and the soft shuffling of men who would go home to their suppers having catalogued the theft of ten thousand lives without once feeling a thing about it.

"You're staring," Odile murmured beside him. She wore the dove-grey robes of an archivist's assistant, borrowed—well, *acquired*—from a laundress who owed Kestrel a favor, and she wore them the way she wore everything: like armor that had personally disappointed her.

"I'm trying to understand how they sleep at night."

"They don't think about it at night. That's the trick of it." Odile adjusted her spectacles—plain glass, purely decorative, but they made her look like exactly the sort of person no one remembered speaking to. "Guilt is a memory too, Kestrel. These people have simply never been given one worth keeping."

They had entered through the front door, which was Odile's idea and which Kestrel had resisted for two full days before conceding that she was right. Witch-finders watched the roofs. They watched the sewers. They watched the river gates and the coal chutes and, according to Brann's increasingly apologetic reports, they had started watching charm-sellers' old haunts with a thoroughness that made Kestrel itch between his shoulder blades.

But nobody watched the front door of the archive, because nobody in Vellamarch wanted to be seen going in. The building had the particular silence of a place everyone agreed not to discuss.

"Ledger Hall first," Odile breathed. "Records of intake. If your map leads anywhere under the palace, there will be paper. There is always paper. Bureaucracy is the one magic this city permits."

* * *

Ledger Hall was a cathedral of shelving, three stories of iron balconies ringed around a central well where clerks called down requests and other clerks hauled volumes up on pulleys, like fishermen drawing up something they'd rather not have caught.

Kestrel had planned to be invisible. He was good at invisible; it was practically his trade. But Odile moved through the hall like weather, and he found himself simply following in her wake, carrying her satchel, fetching her a stool when she glanced at it, gradually realizing that he had been demoted from partner to apprentice without so much as a by-your-leave.

"Third floor," she said, not looking at him. "Intake records, years nine through twelve of the reign."

"Of the reign," Kestrel repeated quietly. "You said it yourself last night. Ten years since the king changed."

"Ten years since the king stopped being seen except at proclamations. Ten years since the tithes began." Her jaw tightened. "I told you I was disgraced for falsifying a provenance record. I never told you what the record was."

He waited. Around them, a clerk droned out a request for *box 441, winter assessments, district Marrowgate*, and somewhere above them a pulley creaked.

"The record concerned a tithe," Odile said. "A 'voluntary donation of recollection,' stamped and sealed and filed as routine. A widow in Tanner's Row gave up her memory of her husband's face. Voluntarily. The signature was hers—I checked, I was young and thorough then—and the compensation ledger showed she'd been paid four marks and a wheel of cheese." She took a slow breath. "I flagged it. I wrote that no one voluntarily forgets a face they loved for four marks and a cheese. My superiors asked me to withdraw the flag. When I didn't, they asked me to withdraw myself."

"And you did."

"I burned my credentials and taught thieves to bottle memories in back alleys, yes. It's been a very fulfilling decline." But her eyes, behind the decorative glass, were not joking at all. "Come on. The intake ledgers are indexed by district and year. We want everything marked *tithe*."

* * *

What they wanted filled six volumes, and what those volumes contained sat in Kestrel's chest like swallowed ice.

He read by the light of a shuttered lamp in the third-floor alcove while Odile kept watch below, and page after page the pattern assembled itself with the terrible neatness of all true things.

Year one of the tithes: forty-one donations. Griefs, mostly. The archive preferred grief—it stored well, the marginalia noted, in a clerk's tidy hand. Year two: sixty-three. Year five: two hundred and eleven, and a new column appeared, headed *yield*, with figures Kestrel didn't understand until he understood them, and then wished he hadn't.

Because the yields rose. Every year, the yield per tithe dropped—the memories came back thinner, weaker, less nourishing—and every year, the number of tithes demanded rose to compensate. By year eight the ledger had stopped pretending these were donations at all. The word used was *harvest*. The districts were listed like fields. And in the final column, in entries that grew more frequent and more urgent as the years went on, a single recurring notation:

*Deliver below.*

Below. Not *to the vault*. Not *to the treasury*. Below. As if whatever received the harvest could not even be named in a government document, as if the clerks themselves had learned not to look directly at it.

"There's a delivery schedule," Odile whispered, appearing at his elbow with two more volumes. "Every quarter. Cart records. The carts go into the palace through the Undercroft gate and—" she turned a page, and her finger stopped. "Kestrel."

"What?"

She turned the book toward him. The cart manifests for the last three years bore a seal he knew—not from the archive but from his own door, from a dying man's bloodstained hands. The courier's wax. The same crest, the same ribbon, the same everything.

"He wasn't stealing from them," Kestrel said slowly. "He was working for them. He carried the tithes down."

"And then he ran." Odile closed the volume gently, the way you close a coffin. "And he was carrying your map instead of a tithe, and he chose a stranger's door to die against. He chose *you*, Kestrel. Of everyone in this city, he chose the one man who can taste whose grief a memory belongs to."

Kestrel looked down at the ledger, at the neat columns of stolen winters, and felt the map shift against his ribs inside its oilcloth wrapping—warm, patient, awake. It had been quiet since the Keeper's shop, almost docile, but now it warmed like a hand pressed flat over his heart.

*I know,* it seemed to say. *Now you know too. Come and see.*

"Not here," he muttered, and Odile, who could apparently hear maps now as well as memories, nodded grimly.

* * *

Finding Wren's memory was Odile's work, and watching her do it was like watching someone play an instrument Kestrel hadn't known the archive contained.

"Every tithe gets a preservation jar," she explained, leading him down a stair that grew colder with each step, past floors of shelving into levels where the lamplight turned greenish and the air tasted of copper and snow. "Memories decay if you keep them raw. The Crown preserves them properly—salt glass, cold rooms, a stabilizing wash. Amateur work spoils in a year. Theirs keeps forever." Her voice went thin. "That's the part I still can't get past. They were *good* at it. All this cruelty, done with real craftsmanship."

The cold room was a long cellar lined floor to ceiling with racks of jars, each jar glowing faintly with the color of what it held. Most glowed grey-blue. Grief, Kestrel thought. The archive's favorite vintage. Some glowed gold—a wedding, maybe, or a child's first steps, harvested whole. One, absurdly, glowed pink and giggled faintly when he walked past, and he decided firmly not to investigate.

"Wren Ash," Odile said, running her finger along a label index. "Tanner's Row. Taken... seven years ago. Age nineteen." She stopped. "Kestrel. It says the tithe was *complete*."

"Complete?"

"A full-life extraction. Everything. Not a donation—a stripping." She pulled the index closer, reading by touch in the dim glow. "It's marked *special requisition*. Authorized above archive level. There's no reason listed. There's just—" her finger tapped a notation, a small symbol he didn't recognize, a circle with a line drawn through it like an eye being closed. "This mark appears eleven times in seven years. Eleven complete extractions. All young women. All between seventeen and twenty-two."

The cold in the room had nothing to do with why Kestrel couldn't speak for a moment.

"Sable thinks her sister died on the pyre," he managed at last.

"Sable's sister walked out of a witch-finder cell alive, was brought here, and was emptied to the walls." Odile's voice was very quiet and very steady, the way it got when she was holding something enormous underwater. "And then, Kestrel—look at the disposition column. Look at where she went."

He looked. *Delivered below. Special handling. Do not decant.*

Eleven jars, taken downstairs, never poured out, never consumed. Kept whole. Kept *specifically* whole—young women, a narrow age, a deliberate selection—and delivered beneath the palace like wine laid down for some future occasion.

"We need to find her jar," Kestrel said. "Now. Tonight."

"Rack fourteen. Cold storage, section C. Complete extractions are kept apart—they need deeper cold." Odile was already moving, her borrowed robes whispering. "And Kestrel? Whatever we feel about this, we take the jar and we go. Curiosity killed more keepers than witch-finders ever did."

* * *

Wren Ash's jar sat on rack fourteen between two others bearing the same closed-eye mark, and it glowed a color Kestrel had never seen a memory glow before: a deep, living ember-orange, warm as a hearth, warm as the thing the whole frozen city was starving for.

"It's beautiful," he whispered.

"It's wrong." Odile held her palm near the glass without touching. "Memories don't glow like this. Even joyful ones. This has been *fed*. Augmented. Someone has been pouring something into these jars for years, cultivating them, and I don't have a craft-word for what, because no keeper has ever been mad enough to try it." She wrapped the jar in wool with hands that shook only slightly. "Take it. Don't taste it. Whatever you do, however curious you get—do not put so much as a fingertip of yourself into this memory. Promise me."

"I promise," Kestrel said, and meant it, and hated how badly he already wanted to know.

The map, against his ribs, had gone hot enough that he could feel it through three layers of cloth. And it wasn't pulling downward anymore, toward the archive's own depths. It was pulling north. Out of the city. Toward the mountains.

* * *

They nearly made it out clean. In Kestrel's experience, that sentence always ended badly, and this time was no exception.

They were crossing the ground-floor reading room, Odile's basket of "restored folios" on her hip with a wool-wrapped jar nestled among the decoy books, when the lamps flickered. All of them. At once. In a building that ran on oil and wicks and had no business flickering in unison.

Every clerk in the room paused, quill lifted, and looked up at the lights with the mild puzzlement of people noticing weather.

Then, one by one, they went back to work, and Kestrel realized with a lurch that none of them had noticed anything at all. The flicker had happened *somewhere else*, somewhere underneath, and what they'd felt was only the edge of it, the way you feel thunder through the soles of your feet.

Odile's hand closed hard on his wrist.

"Beneath us," she breathed. "Something just fed."

In the green-lit cold of the cellar behind them, on rack fourteen, two jars still sat glowing ember-orange where three had been. And in Kestrel's mind, unbidden, unasked, a memory-taste bloomed on his tongue that was not his own: snow, and a throne, and a vast cold patience turning over in its sleep, and one word, spoken in a voice like a kind uncle's, like a beloved king's, like the warmest thing in a freezing world saying the coldest possible sentence:

*Soon.*

* * *

They did not speak until they were three streets away, in the steam-fogged safety of a noodle stall that stayed open all night for dockworkers and people fleeing archives. Odile bought four bowls without asking anyone's preference, which Kestrel had learned was simply how she expressed affection.

"The route," she said finally, laying the map's oilcloth bundle on the counter between them, because some things needed to be said out loud where the steam could hear them. "The manifest routes go to the Undercroft gate. Your map starts at the Undercroft gate. It runs beneath the river, up through the old cisterns, into the mountain roads—Mount Cindral, Kestrel, it goes to *Cindral*—and ends at a chamber marked with the same symbol as the eleven jars." She drew the shape on the counter in spilled broth: a circle with a line through it. An eye, closing. Or opening.

"The Ember Vault," Kestrel said.

"If that's what the couriers call it. The ledgers call it *below*. The map calls it home." She pushed a bowl at him. "Eat. You've gone pale."

"I'm always pale."

"Paler. It's a relative discipline." But her joke had no legs; it lay down and died between them, and for a while there was only the slurp of noodles and the fogged window and the distant bells marking the hour before dawn.

"Ten years," Kestrel said at last. "Ten years of harvests. Whole districts giving up their griefs four marks at a time. Eleven girls emptied to the walls and kept like preserves. And everyone in that building filing it, stamping it, sealing it, going home to supper." He looked at his hands. "I've spent my whole life telling myself I was small. A charm-seller. A little thief. Nothing next to whatever runs the pyres. But Odile—I've been selling bottled memories in the night market for six years, and I never once asked where the Crown's tithes went, because asking questions is bad for business." His laugh came out cracked. "We're all of us clerks. We just have worse handwriting."

Odile reached across the counter and, with great precision, pushed his bowl closer to him.

"When I was disgraced," she said, "I told myself the same thing. *One flag withdrawn. What could one keeper do?* And then I spent fifteen years teaching pickpockets to bottle sunsets, and I called it resistance because it was safer than calling it hiding." She took off the decorative spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, though they needed no polishing; it was something she did instead of crying, and both of them knew it. "So. We are two cowards with a map, a stolen jar, and a friend waiting in a safehouse who thinks her sister burned. What do we do, Kestrel Fairweather?"

He looked at the wool-wrapped bundle glowing softly through its wrappings. Ember-orange. Hearth-colored. The exact color, he realized, of the light in the crowned man's white screaming room, the color the whole city was being drained of, jar by jar, tithe by tithe, harvest by harvest.

"We tell Sable the truth," he said. "All of it. Even the part that will break her heart. Especially that part." He stood, leaving coins enough for ten bowls, because generosity was easier than courage and he needed the practice. "And then we go north. Before midwinter. Before whatever's *below* finishes waking up."

Outside the stall, the sky over Vellamarch had gone the grey-white of old scar tissue, and the first flakes of the season's early snow were coming down—weeks early, everyone said, weeks early, how strange—as if the city itself had begun to hold its breath.

And in Kestrel's pocket, patient as a promise, the map began, very softly, to hum.
