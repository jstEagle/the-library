## 8. The Face on the Coin

The Winter Proclamation was the one day of the year Vellamarch pretended to like itself.

Kestrel had watched it since he was small enough to ride his father's shoulders: banners of white and gold hung from every balcony, chestnuts roasting in braziers along the Processional Way, and the whole city pressed shoulder to shoulder in the great square before the palace to hear the king promise that spring would come. It always had. Even in the worst years, even when the frost climbed the windows like something hungry, the Proclamation ended with bells and free spiced wine and a general, temporary agreement among strangers not to hate one another until at least New Year's Day.

This year there were no chestnuts. There was no wine. The braziers burned but gave off almost no heat, which Kestrel suspected was less magic than economy—the Crown had learned, over ten winters, exactly how little warmth it could give away and still be loved for it.

"Move up," Sable muttered at his elbow, hood drawn low over the burn-scarred side of her face. "You're standing like a man who wants to be noticed."

"I'm standing like a man watching a parade."

"You're standing like a man with contraband in his coat."

She wasn't wrong. The map lay against Kestrel's ribs, wrapped in oilcloth beneath three layers of wool, and it had been *warm* all morning. Not fever-warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, the way a hand is warm when it wants holding. He'd tried to ignore it. It had responded by growing warmer whenever he turned toward the palace, with the gentle insistence of a cat that has decided it is dinnertime.

Beside him, Odile stood very straight and very still, her memory-keeper's gray cloak buttoned to the throat. She had argued against coming. She had lost the argument to herself, in the end, because—as she put it—*if I let you two walk into a crowd unsupervised, you'll come back with a third problem and no plan.*

Brann was supposed to be here anyway. That was the beauty of it, Odile had admitted grudgingly. A corporal of the city guard attending the Winter Proclamation wasn't suspicious; he was *required*. So Brann stood twenty feet away in his uniform, face forward, hands clasped behind his back, radiating the studied blankness of a man who has been ordered to look official and is determined to think about nothing whatsoever.

The square filled. Ten thousand people, maybe more, breath rising in a single pale cloud. Somewhere near the front a child was crying about cold feet, and her mother was telling her, with the desperate brightness Kestrel recognized from every winter market stall, that the king would make it warm soon, you'll see.

The map pulsed against his ribs.

*Soon,* it seemed to say. *Oh, you have no idea.*

* * *

The trumpets began at noon precisely, because the Hollow King was punctual the way winter is punctual—not cruelly early, never rudely late, simply inevitable.

The crowd hushed. On the high balcony above the palace steps, framed by white banners, the doors opened, and the King of Vellamarch came out into the light.

Kestrel had seen coins with this face. Every coin in the city bore it, struck new each year: a strong jaw, silver-streaked dark hair, eyes the color of deep water. He'd seen the face on proclamations nailed to tavern walls, on the tithe-collectors' ledgers, painted twice life-size on the front of the Mint. After ten years it was as familiar as his own reflection, and far better liked.

But he had never seen the man himself, and now, seeing him, Kestrel's first thought was absurdly domestic: *he's smaller than the coins.* The second thought arrived a heartbeat later and stayed much longer.

*He's beautiful,* thought Kestrel, *and it's wrong, the way a perfect apple is wrong when the tree it grew on is dead.*

The Hollow King raised both hands, and the crowd cheered. Kestrel felt the sound in his teeth. Ten thousand people cheering, and underneath it, so faint most people would call it imagination, a sound like a vast slow inhale.

"He's drawing," Odile breathed beside him. Her hand had found Kestrel's sleeve without her seeming to notice. "Look at the braziers. Look at them."

Kestrel looked. The coals in the nearest brazier had dimmed. Not gone out—dimmed, the way a candle dims when someone leans over it. All down the Processional Way, thirty braziers flickered low in unison, and the crowd, instead of noticing, pulled their coats tighter and cheered louder, because their king had come out to see them and surely that was warmth enough.

"The tithes," Kestrel said quietly. "It's not just the archive. It does this every time it shows its face, doesn't it? Feeds in public."

"Voluntary offerings," Odile said, and there was something terrible in how flat her voice went. "That's what the records called them. *Voluntary.*"

On the balcony, the Hollow King smiled, and ten thousand people smiled back without knowing why.

* * *

He spoke well. That was the worst part, Kestrel thought. He didn't rant. He didn't threaten. He stood at the balustrade with the wind moving his hair and talked about Vellamarch the way a man talks about a garden he loves—its endurance, its courage, the long hard winter they all faced together.

"My people." His voice carried effortlessly to the back of the square, warm as mulled wine. "Another winter comes, harder than the last. I feel your cold as my own. I have felt it for ten years."

A murmur of sympathy through the crowd. Beside Kestrel, a baker's wife wiped her eye with her apron.

"Therefore," the king continued, gently, regretfully, as though the words cost him, "for your protection—for your *warmth*—the curfew will move earlier. Sundown, from tonight until the thaw. No fires lit after dark except by Crown license. The nights are dangerous, my friends. Let me keep them for you."

And the crowd—here was the thing Kestrel would remember afterward, the thing that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone—the crowd *cheered*. Not everywhere, not everyone. But enough. Enough voices lifted in relief and gratitude while the braziers guttered lower, while the child with the cold feet stopped crying because her mother was clapping and clapping meant everything was fine.

"Sundown curfew," Sable said under the noise. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles, gripping the barrier rail, were not. "Do you know what sundown curfew means for my people? Marked folk can't get papers renewed after dark. Safehouses can't move anyone. Witch-finders can stop anyone on the street after dark and call it *warmth enforcement*."

"It means the city goes to bed hungry and calls it being tucked in," Odile said.

Kestrel barely heard them. Because the map had stopped pulsing.

It had begun to *burn*.

Not painfully—not yet—but with a heat that shot up through his layers of wool and spread across his chest like a hand pressed flat over his heart. And it wasn't random. He tested it, tilting slightly left, slightly right, the way he'd once tested wind direction with a wetted finger. Left: cooler. Right: cooler. Straight ahead, at the balcony, at the figure in white and gold with his arms spread wide over his beloved city:

Hotter.

*The Vault remembers,* the dying courier had whispered. And now the map remembered too, straining toward the throne like a compass needle finding north, like a dog hearing its name, like—

Like a memory trying to go home.

"Oh," Kestrel said softly.

"What?" Sable's head turned a fraction.

"It knows where it came from." He kept his eyes forward, smiling the same empty parade smile as everyone around him, while his heart hammered against the burning cloth. "The map. It's not leading us to the Vault. It's *pulling*. It's a piece of something bigger, and the rest of it is up there, sitting on that balcony wearing a crown."

Odile's breath caught audibly. "Then every moment you stand here—"

"Yes."

"—it may be able to feel you feeling it—"

"*Yes*, Odile."

Below them, as though summoned by the word, the Hollow King's gaze began to move across the crowd.

* * *

It moved the way a lamp moves light across a wall—slowly, warmly, resting here and there. On the baker's wife. On the crying child, now hoisted onto her father's shoulders to see. On old men and young lovers and a cluster of apprentices throwing snowballs that dissolved before they landed. Wherever the gaze rested, faces brightened. Kestrel watched a scowling dockworker soften into open adoration in the space of three seconds, watched him elbow his neighbor and point at the balcony with shining eyes, *there, look, he's looking at us—*

The gaze swept nearer.

Kestrel did the only thing he knew how to do, the skill that had kept him alive through nine years of witch-finder patrols: he made himself small. Not physically—invisibly. He folded his presence down into the gray ordinary fabric of the crowd, became a nobody, a nothing, a man-shaped absence thinking about bread prices. It was the same discipline he used to slip a memory out of a stranger's pocket, run in reverse: don't take, *don't be taken.*

The gaze reached their section of the crowd.

And paused.

The map flared hot enough that Kestrel bit the inside of his cheek to keep from gasping. Across the distance, across ten thousand bodies and forty feet of frozen air, the Hollow King looked directly at the place where Kestrel Fairweather was standing—and smiled.

It was a lovely smile. It was the smile from the coins, the proclamations, the Mint mural. It held nothing but love.

That was the horror of it. There was nothing *in* it. Kestrel had spent his life reading faces, tasting the memories behind expressions, and he knew what lived behind a real smile—the small warm clutter of it, the person-ness. This smile was a window with the curtain drawn behind it. Something looked out through it with perfect goodwill and perfect emptiness, the way winter looks at a field it intends to cover.

"He sees us," Sable breathed. Her scarred cheek had gone bloodless. "Kestrel, he *sees*—"

"No." Odile's voice cracked like a whip, quiet and absolute. "Don't move. Don't run. Running is a confession. Smile, damn you both. Smile at your king."

So they smiled. Three liars in a crowd of ten thousand, faces tilted up in adoration while the thing on the balcony drank the heat out of the braziers and out of the air and, Kestrel realized with a lurch, out of *them*—out of the crowd itself, a sip from every cup, so small no one would ever miss it, so vast in sum that the snow at the edges of the square was visibly creeping inward across the cobbles as they watched.

The Hollow King's gaze lingered one heartbeat longer. Two.

Then the trumpets sounded again, and he turned, radiant, to go inside, and the spell broke, and the crowd sighed like a tide going out, and the braziers sprang back to full cheerful flame as if ashamed of themselves.

* * *

They didn't speak until they'd put three streets between themselves and the square, until they were wedged in the doorway of a shuttered chandlery with the smell of old tallow and the sound of the dispersing crowd fading behind them.

Kestrel pulled the map out just far enough to look at it. Through the oilcloth it glowed faintly, like a banked coal, and where the seal had been pressed by the courier's bloody thumb, the wax had gone soft again.

"It reached for me," he said. "Or I reached for it. I couldn't tell which."

"That's because there isn't a difference anymore." Odile took the map's edge between two fingers, gingerly, the way she handled anything that remembered. "I told you in my shop that objects don't hold memory. I lied, or I was wrong, or both. This isn't an object holding a memory. This is a memory that grew *organs*."

"Lovely," said Sable.

"It happens," Odile went on, and her voice had the lecture-cadence Kestrel knew from his training days, deployed now like a shield against fear. "Rarely. When a memory is stolen often enough, passed hand to hand, wanted badly enough—it thickens. Develops direction. We called them *homing memories* in the Guild. They try to return to the mind they were taken from." She looked up, and her eyes were grim. "This one was taken from a king. It has spent ten years trying to crawl home. And the thing wearing his face has spent ten years making sure the door stays shut."

"So midwinter," Kestrel said. "The binding. If we bring the king's memories back—if we open the door—"

"Everything changes," Odile finished. "Or everything ends. With royalty it's usually both."

Boots rang on the cobbles at the corner. All three of them went still as furniture—then relaxed by degrees as Brann rounded into view, helmet under his arm, walking with the deliberate looseness of a man off duty. He glanced up the street, down the street, and slipped into the doorway beside them, close enough to talk without moving his lips.

"You're lucky," he murmured. "Half the finders' office is being redeployed to curfew enforcement starting tomorrow. Hale requested the Proclamation district personally. He's already asking questions about the courier's death—asking who worked the night market, who sold charms, who's new to the district." Brann's jaw tightened. "Kestrel. He described you. From a witness. 'Charm seller, young, quick hands, laughs too easily.'"

"I don't laugh *that* easily."

"Kestrel."

"Fine. Point taken." Kestrel leaned his head back against the doorframe and looked up at the strip of white sky between the rooftops. Somewhere up there, the cold was coming down like a sentence being read aloud. "Sundown curfew. Hale hunting me. And the enemy of the entire city standing on a balcony being adored by everyone whose warmth he's stealing." He laughed—short, unlovely, proof of Brann's point. "Anyone else feel like we're losing?"

"We haven't started yet," Sable said. "Losing comes later."

But her hand found his sleeve, briefly, and squeezed.

Above them, unseen, the first flakes of the evening's snow began to fall—early, too early, drifting down through the gray light with the unhurried confidence of something that had been promised the whole city and intended to collect.

Behind them, far off, the palace bells rang the hour, and Kestrel could have sworn the map beat once against his ribs in time with them.

*Two days to midwinter,* it seemed to say. *Come home. Come home. Come home.*
