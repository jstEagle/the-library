## 9. Sable's Bargain

The safehouse smelled of woodsmoke and burnt sugar, which Sable Ashvine had learned was the smell of people trying to be brave. Sugar burned when hands shook. Nobody in the Ember Row safehouse had steady hands, not anymore.

She watched Kestrel Fairweather from the doorway of the back room, where he sat hunched over a table like a man waiting to be scolded. Which, she supposed, he was. He'd come back from the Crown archive with a small glass vial wrapped in three layers of cloth, cradled against his chest like an infant, and he hadn't let go of it since.

"That's her," Sable said. Not a question. She could feel it from across the room—a warmth that had nothing to do with the stove, a pull like a hand closing around hers.

"Your sister's final memory." Kestrel set it on the table between them with exaggerated care, as though it might bruise. "The tithe-collectors logged it under 'sedition, minor.' She was arrested for teaching letters to marked children." His voice did something complicated on the last word. "They took everything she had left. This was the last of it."

Sable sat down across from him. The chair groaned. Everything in Ember Row groaned; the building had been condemned twice and had outlived both condemnations out of pure spite, which Sable respected deeply.

"Before I open it," Kestrel said, "you should know what you're asking for. Final memories are— they aren't gentle. She died afraid, probably. The witch-finders made sure of that. When I pour this back into you, you'll feel what she felt. All of it."

"I know."

"You might wish you didn't."

"Kestrel." Sable leaned forward, and the firelight caught the burn scars climbing her throat like ivy, silver-pale against brown skin. "I have spent six years not knowing what my sister thought about at the end. Six years of inventing it. Every version I invent is worse than the truth could possibly be, because I invent them at three in the morning and I am very good at cruelty to myself." She held out her hand. "Give me my sister back. Even just the last hour of her."

He hesitated one moment longer—she saw him cataloguing her, the way thieves do, measuring whether she'd break—and then he uncorked the vial.

The memory rose like steam off summer pavement, visible for half a heartbeat: a girl laughing, dark hair, Sable's own crooked smile worn by someone kinder. Then Kestrel pressed two fingers to Sable's temple, and Wren came home.

* * *

Wren was twenty-two years old and furious, and the memory began mid-argument.

"—can't keep doing this," Wren was saying, to someone whose face the memory didn't hold, only the sense of a small warm room and chalk dust. "Teaching them their own names isn't sedition. It's the opposite of sedition. Sedition is what the Crown does when it makes people forget they ever had names worth writing down."

There were children around her. Marked children—the ones born with the faint silver shimmer beneath the skin that Vellamarch called the taint and Wren called *the gift they haven't figured out how to tax yet*. They sat in a circle on the floor, and Wren stood over them like a general, chalk in hand, writing letters on a slate: W-R-E-N.

"You spell yours," she told a small boy with shimmer at his wrists, "and nobody can make you disappear. That's the whole trick. Names are anchors. Write yours down and hide it somewhere true."

Then boots on the stairs. Many boots.

The rest, Sable had been right about, and wrong about, and both at once. Wren was afraid—but she wasn't only afraid. In the last minutes, while the door splintered, Wren looked at the children and said, calm as Sunday, "Back window. Run, don't walk, and don't stop until Odile's. You remember the way?" And they went, all seven of them, every single one, scrambling out into the snow while the door came down, because Wren had taught them exactly where to run.

The witch-finders took her then. And in the cell, in the cold, in the last of it, Wren Ashvine did not think of vengeance or even of survival. She thought: *Sable will find the children. Sable always finds people.* And then, with the strange clarity of the nearly-gone: *Tell her it was worth it. Tell her I got them out. Tell her—*

The memory ended the way a candle ends. Not extinguished. Finished.

* * *

Sable came back to herself weeping, which she had promised herself she wouldn't do, and found that she didn't much care about the promise.

"They lived," she said. Her voice came out wrecked. "The children. They got out."

"All seven," Kestrel said quietly. "I checked the archive records. There were transfer orders—marked minors, relocated to a parish house in Lowmarsh. Someone falsified the paperwork to say they died in transit so no one would look for them." He paused. "That someone was almost certainly your sister, before they took her. She bought their lives with her own paper trail."

Sable laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, wet and cracked. "Of course she did. Wren couldn't lie to save herself but she'd forge documents all day to save anyone else." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Six years I thought she died for nothing. Burned at the pyre for teaching letters, and I thought—what a waste, what a stupid, pointless—"

"It wasn't."

"No." Sable breathed out, long and slow, and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched so long she'd mistaken it for her skeleton. "It really, truly wasn't."

She looked at the empty vial in Kestrel's hands. Something else moved in her then—not grief, or not only grief. Recognition. This ridiculous, twitchy little thief had walked into the Crown archive—the archive, the place where hope went to be filed alphabetically—and carried her sister out in his pocket.

"Why?" she asked. "You didn't know Wren. You didn't know me, not really. You could have sold that memory for more money than you'd see in ten years of charm-selling. A royal-adjacent tithe record? Collectors would have fought over it." She narrowed her eyes. "So why give it back?"

Kestrel turned the vial over in his fingers. He had a habit, she was noticing, of looking at objects instead of people when the truth got close.

"Because I've sold thousands of memories," he said. "Little ones, mostly. First kisses. Dead mothers' voices. The smell of a house you grew up in. People come to me when they want to forget, and I tell myself it's mercy, and maybe sometimes it is. But I've never once given anything back." He looked up. "You asked me to. So I did. And it turns out—" his mouth twisted, wry and rueful, "—it turns out giving things back feels considerably better than selling them. Which is inconvenient, because I've built an entire life on not feeling things."

Sable studied him for a long moment. Then she stood, went to the shelf above the stove, and took down the tin she kept for emergencies. It contained no food. It contained a bottle of plum brandy that had survived three raids through the simple method of being labeled "lamp oil," and she poured two fingers into two chipped cups.

"To Wren," she said. "Who got them out."

"To Wren," Kestrel echoed, and drank, and coughed, and Sable decided she liked him enormously.

* * *

By midnight, the back room had filled.

Word traveled fast in Ember Row, faster than the witch-finders, mostly because the witch-finders were large and loud and the marked were small and quiet and had six years of practice disappearing. They came in ones and twos: Marta the hedge-witch who could still coax a fever down if no one watched her do it. Old Pell, whose crime was a cantrip that made bread rise. The twins, Fen and Farrow, seventeen and furious and shimmering at the collarbones. Seven children from Lowmarsh, smuggled in over the past year, asleep now in the loft in a pile like puppies.

Sable stood among them with her cup and felt the strangest sensation: warmth. Actual, physical warmth, radiating outward from the middle of the room, though the stove was small and the night outside was bitter enough to crack stones.

Kestrel noticed it too. She watched him notice—watched him frown at the air like it had cheated him.

"That's not the fire," he said.

"No," Sable agreed. "It isn't."

In Vellamarch, warmth had become currency. The Hollow King taxed it—every hearth registered, every gathering of more than five persons requiring a permit, every festival banned on grounds of *fire hazard*. Ten years of decrees, each one reasonable-sounding, each one shaving another degree off the city's soul. Sable had assumed the cold was weather. Standing here, watching a hedge-witch teach a child to write her own name on a slate—*names are anchors*, and Sable's chest ached sweetly at the echo—she understood it was theft. The city wasn't freezing. It was being drained.

And this room, this ridiculous condemned building full of criminals whose crimes were kindnesses, was refusing to be drained.

"Odile says the map leads north," Kestrel said beside her. He'd gone quiet, the way he did. "Under Mount Cindral. To the Vault. And whatever's under the palace has been eating ten years of tithes, and midwinter is—" he counted on his fingers, winced, "—soon. Very soon. Weeks."

"So go," Sable said.

"We need cover. Papers, routes, safehouses through the northern districts. Hale is hunting us and the roads are watched, and Odile's already—" he stopped, jaw tightening. Odile Vane had been captured covering their escape from the tenement raid four nights ago, and none of them said her name comfortably yet. "We need a network. I need people who know how to move invisible people through a frozen city."

Sable turned to face him fully. Around them, the room hummed—Pell telling a story badly, the twins correcting the details, Marta laughing into her cup—and Sable felt the decision settle into her the way the memory of Wren had settled: finished, whole, warm.

"You're looking at it," she said. "Thirty-one marked souls, four safehouses, two tunnel routes under the wall, and a baker in Northgate who owes me his daughter's life. You want a network, thief? You've been standing in it all evening."

Kestrel blinked. "I—yes. That's. Yes."

"But I have conditions." She held up one scarred finger. "One: we don't leave anyone behind. Not the children, not old Pell, nobody gets used as bait or abandoned for speed. Two:" a second finger, "when this is done—if it's done, if we win—your king pardons the marked. Officially. With paper."

"He's not my king."

"He will be. Three:" a third finger, and here she smiled, and knew it was not a comforting smile, "you teach me the charm trade. Bottled memories. Because when we take the tithes back from the Crown—and we will—I want to be the one who carries them home to their owners. Every single one. I want witch-finders spending the rest of their miserable careers chasing returned property."

For a long moment Kestrel just looked at her. Then something in his careful, guarded face loosened, and he stuck out his hand.

"Sable Ashvine," he said, "you are the most alarming person I have ever met, and I once sold a memory to a woman who collected teeth."

"High praise." She shook it. "Welcome to the resistance. We meet Tuesdays. There's soup."

"There's *soup?*"

"Resistance runs better on soup. Ask any successful rebellion."

From the corner, Old Pell raised his cup. "Hear, hear. Soup and sedition." The twins cheered. Marta threw a crust of bread at him. And in the loft overhead, seven sleeping children shifted and murmured, dreaming safely, anchored by their own written names hidden in their shoes.

* * *

Later, near dawn, Sable found Kestrel awake at the table, the map spread before him, its wax seal glinting with that faint wrong luster it had. Up close, in lamplight, she could see what she'd only sensed before: the thing was alive. The ink moved when you weren't looking directly at it. It was warm as a sleeping animal.

"It knows where we are," she said, sitting down.

"It's known my name since the night the courier died." Kestrel traced the route north with one finger, not quite touching. "River road under the ice, past the village that—" he stopped. "Past some villages. Through the mountain gates. Odile decoded half of it before they took her. The other half is in her head, and the Hollow King's men have her head."

"Then we get her head back."

"Easier said. Hale's interrogation methods are—" 

"Kestrel." Sable leaned across the map, and the living ink seemed to lean with her, attentive. "I spent six years believing my sister died alone and afraid, and I was wrong, and knowing the truth changed nothing about the world and everything about me. Whatever they're doing to Odile in there—she's not alone either. Not while we're coming." She tapped the mountain on the map. "So we come. Fast, quiet, and with soup."

A ghost of a smile crossed Kestrel's face—the first real one she'd seen from him. "You're going to make me carry the soup, aren't you."

"I'm going to make you *appreciate* the soup. Carrying's a privilege."

Outside, the wind picked up, rattling the shutters, carrying the smell of snow and the distant bells of the Winter Proclamation rehearsals—the Hollow King's voice would fill the city again soon, warm and radiant and wrong. But in here the stove ticked, and the embers held, and thirty-one people slept unafraid for one more night.

Sable looked at the map, at the long cold road north, and felt no fear she could name. Only a kindling. Somewhere under that mountain was the thing that had stolen a decade of warmth from everyone she loved, and she intended, with her whole scorched and stubborn heart, to take it back.

"We leave tomorrow," she said.

The map pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat answering.

As if it had heard.
