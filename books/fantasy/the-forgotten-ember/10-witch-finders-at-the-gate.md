## 10. Witch-Finders at the Gate

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Kestrel had learned to read the sounds of the Gullwing District the way sailors read weather. The creak of the stairwell at midnight meant old Marta coming home from her shift at the tannery. Three sharp raps before dawn was the milk-cart boy who sold more than milk. But a fist hammering on Sable's safehouse door at half past nine in the evening—when honest people were still awake and dishonest ones were just getting started—that was new, and new things in Vellamarch were usually bad things wearing a coat.

He looked up from the table where he'd been teaching himself to read the map's route by candlelight, the wax seal long since broken and the parchment now behaving itself, mostly, except for the way it occasionally pulsed under his fingers like something breathing in its sleep.

"That's not one of ours," Sable said from the doorway. She had a kettle in one hand and a knife in the other, and she held both with the same casual competence, as though deciding which to offer a guest depended entirely on their manners.

Brann got to his feet so fast his chair skidded. He'd been dozing by the fire in his off-duty clothes, and even half-asleep he moved like a man whose body remembered drills his heart had stopped believing in. He pressed his ear to the door.

"Six sets of boots," he said quietly. "Heavy. Iron-shod." A pause. His face went the color of porridge. "And that's Hale's voice."

* * *

Twenty minutes earlier and half a district away, Sergeant Corwin Hale stood in the alley behind a chandler's shop, looking down at the place where a courier had died, and felt the first hairline crack run through a conviction he had carried for eleven years without ever once setting it down.

The chandler—a nervous woman with wax burns on her knuckles—had told him everything twice. A young man had stumbled out of the fog three nights past, bleeding from the belly, and collapsed against the tenement door across the way. She'd watched from her window. She hadn't gone out. Nobody went out, not for strangers, not in this city, not anymore.

"He said something," she offered, wringing her hands. "Before he died. I couldn't hear it proper. Sounded like... 'the vault remembers'? Or maybe 'the fault' — there's faults in the old walls, my gran used to say—"

"The Vault," Hale said. "Yes."

He knew the courier's name: Pell Wickham, twenty-three, employed by the Office of Records, which everyone knew was a fiction and which Hale had recently begun to suspect was a fiction serving a different master than advertised. Pell had been carrying something out of the palace. Something worth dying for and worth killing over, because the wound in his belly was narrow and deep and made by a witch-finder's dirk—the kind Hale himself wore.

His own office had killed this boy. Or someone wearing his office's face had.

"Did anyone visit the tenement after?" he asked.

"A man came and went. Youngish. Kept to the shadows. Old Tansy swears he saw him climb the drainpipe like a spider, but Tansy drinks."

Hale wrote nothing down. He was getting very good, lately, at writing nothing down.

He looked up at the tenement, at the crooked rows of windows, and thought about warrants, and about how a warrant was just paper until a man made it true, and about how lately the paper seemed to be asking him to make true things he no longer believed.

"Surround the block," he said. "Quietly. And send for the tithe-wagon."

* * *

"Out the back," Brann said, already unbarring the door. "There's a laundry yard, then the cooper's roof, then—" He stopped. Turned around. Looked at the three of them as if memorizing them, which Kestrel found deeply alarming. "Listen to me. They'll have the street covered inside two minutes. You go up through the loft, across the cooper's, drop into Tanner's Row. Don't argue about the drop, it's shorter than it looks if you don't think about it."

"And you?" Sable said.

"I'm going to open that door," Brann said, "and be extremely surprised to find you all here."

"You can't be surprised to find us here, Tam, we've been having supper together for a week—"

"I can be surprised," Brann said with tremendous dignity, "if I commit to it."

Kestrel wanted to laugh. That was the terrible thing—he wanted to *laugh*, standing in a room with the walls about to come in, because Brann's face was so earnestly stupid and Sable was glaring at him like she could hold the door shut with sheer indignation, and Odile—

Odile wasn't arguing. That was what froze the laugh in Kestrel's throat. She had gone very still by the window, the way she did when she was reading a room full of memories, and whatever she was reading now, she didn't like it.

"They're not here for a raid," she said softly. "Listen. No shouting. No battering ram. Hale is being polite." Her eyes found Kestrel's. "Polite means he thinks he has time. Polite means he doesn't know yet what he's looking for. Every second we give him, he learns more."

"So we don't give him seconds," Sable said.

But Odile shook her head slowly, and Kestrel saw it coming the way you see a cart wheel coming loose—too late to stop it, early enough to be sick about it.

"They'll follow the strongest trail," she said. "That's how they're trained. Memory leaves residue, Kestrel—you know that better than anyone. You've been handling royal grief in a room full of marked people for days. This whole house glows like a bonfire to a finder with a good nose. Someone has to stand at the front of the fire and let it look like the whole of it."

"No," Kestrel said.

"It's not a request, dear."

"Odile—"

"I trained you," she said, and for once there was no dryness in it at all, no armor of wit, just an old woman's plain voice in a shabby room. "Eleven years ago, a boy came to me with holes where his childhood should have been, and I taught him to fill other people's holes instead of his own, and I have regretted the second part every day since. Let me do one thing right with the craft. Let me be the decoy. I'm the one they can't pin anything on—I've been careful for thirty years. I'll feed Hale a route north along the coast road. By the time he unravels it, you'll be three days gone."

"That's not how it works and you know it," Kestrel said. "They interrogate. They have ways of—"

"I know exactly how it works," Odile said. "I helped write the manual, before the manual was turned on us." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile Kestrel had ever seen, and he had once bottled a widow's memory of her husband's funeral, so he knew from sad smiles. "Besides. Someone has to stay behind to misplace the good teapot. It would break my heart to leave it in your custody; you'd sell it."

The knocking stopped. In the silence that followed, they all heard it: boots spreading out, soft and deliberate, taking up positions. The sound of a net being laid down gently, so as not to wake the fish.

"Loft. Now," Brann whispered. "All of you. I mean it."

Sable grabbed Kestrel's sleeve. Kestrel grabbed Odile's hand. For one moment the four of them stood in the little room with its mismatched chairs and its kettle going cold and its map of a dead king's grief hidden under floorboards, and it was so nearly a family that Kestrel's chest hurt with it.

Then Odile pulled her hand free, gentle as unhooking a bird from a snare, and stepped toward the door.

"For the record," she called back over her shoulder, in a voice pitched to carry, loud enough for any listening ears, "I told you all weeks ago this safehouse was compromised and none of you listened—"

The door opened. Sergeant Hale filled it, lantern raised, six grey coats fanning out behind him into the yard.

"—and honestly, Corporal, if you're going to harbor fugitives, the least you could do is fix the draft."

* * *

What followed, Kestrel pieced together later from Brann's stammered report and the sounds drifting up through the floorboards, was the finest performance either of them had ever witnessed or given.

Brann opened the inner door rubbing his eyes, in his shirtsleeves, radiating the aggrieved confusion of a man woken mid-dream. Odile swept past Hale into the yard, complaining about the cold, the hour, the state of the Crown's manners, and the price of candles, in that order, while Hale tried to get a word in edgewise and failed completely. She let him search her bag. She let him taste her mind—Kestrel knew the moment it happened, because her voice changed, went flat and recitation-perfect, offering up memories like coins from a purse: a route north, the coast road, a smuggler named Ferro who ran boats out of Saltmarsh, departure planned for Thursday.

Thursday. Two days of false comfort, gift-wrapped.

Up in the loft, Kestrel lay flat against the rafters with Sable's hand clamped over his mouth—not that he needed it; he wouldn't have breathed anyway—and listened to Odile lie beautifully, generously, with all the skill of thirty years, laying down a trail of false memories for Hale to follow like breadcrumbs baked from nothing.

Then Hale said, quietly: "And Fairweather? Where is he?"

And Odile laughed. Actually laughed.

"Sergeant. If I knew where that boy was, I'd have sold the information myself months ago. He's a thief and an ingrate and he stole my best set of needles when he left my tutelage. Ask anyone."

It was almost true. Kestrel had stolen her needles. He'd been fifteen and it had been the only goodbye he'd known how to write.

He heard Hale walk the length of the yard. Heard him pause beneath the loft hatch—directly beneath Kestrel, close enough that dust sifted down between the boards—and stand there for five seconds, ten, fifteen, while Kestrel's heart performed maneuvers it had learned in no school.

Then, distantly, from the street: a shout. One of Hale's finders, at the far corner of the block, calling that he'd caught a scent-residue heading east, toward the river. Whether it was real or whether some part of Odile's planted trail had leaked sideways, Kestrel never learned. But the boots moved, and the hatch stayed shut, and the moment passed the way moments do in Vellamarch—without thanks.

"Search the house," Hale said finally, wearily. "Thoroughly. Then we move."

* * *

They went out through the cooper's roof as ordered, all three of them, because the alternative was staying to be searched out of the rafters, and because Odile had built her whole plan on the assumption that they would obey her, and the cruelest thing they could do now was honor it.

The drop into Tanner's Row was not, in fact, shorter than it looked. Kestrel landed badly, rolled, and came up against a rain barrel with Sable landing beside him light as a cat and Brann arriving last with the grace of a wardrobe falling downstairs.

For a moment they crouched in the dark between the barrels, listening to the muffled sounds of their home being taken apart above them.

"We go back," Sable said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't negotiable. "We go back and we get her out."

"With what army?" Brann hissed. "There are six finders up there and a tithe-wagon coming. If we go back, she's traded herself for four prisoners instead of three."

"She's seventy years old, Tam!"

"She's the most dangerous woman in this district," Kestrel said quietly, "and she knows it. She's not captured, Sable. She's *deployed*." He heard his own voice shake and hated it. "She bought us two days and a false road. The worst thing we can do with that is waste it proving we love her. She'd never forgive us. She'd haunt us. She told me once she intended to haunt me specifically, and she keeps excellent records."

Sable stared at him. Then, slowly, the fury in her face broke apart into something worse—grief, plain and ungovernable—and Brann put a hand on her shoulder and she let it stay there, which from Sable was practically a marriage proposal.

Above them, faintly, through the roof of the world, they heard the tithe-wagon's wheels turn into the yard. They heard Odile's voice one last time, rising in theatrical outrage about the treatment of her person and the constitutional rights of memory-keepers, rights that had been abolished nine years ago, which was exactly the sort of detail Odile would deploy because it made the finders hesitate and hesitate was all she needed.

Then the wagon doors closed, and the wheels rolled away south, toward the Palace cells, and the night got very quiet.

* * *

They walked east through the fog, three shadows keeping to the wall-side of the streets, saying nothing, because there was nothing yet that could be said safely and nothing at all that could be said usefully.

At the mouth of Cooper's Lane, where the fog thickened and the lamps gave up entirely, Kestrel finally spoke.

"She fed him a route to Saltmarsh. Boats, Thursday, a smuggler named Ferro." He swallowed. "Which means we can't take the coast road, because by Friday morning Hale will have torn that story apart and every boat out of Saltmarsh will have a finder on it. She gave us two days and burned the obvious escape in the same breath. She's herding us north. Toward the mountains. Toward the Vault."

"Of course she is," Sable said, and there was a wet, cracked almost-laugh in it. "She couldn't just save our lives like a normal person. She had to *curate* it."

Brann stopped walking. When they turned, he was looking back the way they'd come, toward the distant smudge of the Gullwing District, toward the wagon rolling south with its precious cargo, and his face in the lamplight was the face of a man doing arithmetic he couldn't undo.

"Ten years I've worn this coat," he said. "Ten years I've told myself the pyres were necessary, the tithes were voluntary, the King's decrees were medicine. Tonight I watched them load an old woman into a cage for lying well enough to save three lives." He turned back to them, and his jaw was set in a way Kestrel had only seen once before—on the courier's dead face, in the shape of the word he'd spent his last breath on. "Get her out. Whatever this costs, wherever it ends—get Odile Vane out of that palace. Swear it."

"We swear," Sable said.

Kestrel said nothing. He was looking north, where the fog thinned and the mountains waited under the stars, black and patient as a held breath. Under his coat, against his ribs, the map had begun to pulse again—faster now, warmer, like a heart quickening at the approach of home.

Two days, Odile had bought them. Somewhere south, in a cell beneath the palace, she was spending them at interest.

"North, then," Kestrel said. "And Tam—when we come back for her, and we will—we're going to need to steal something bigger than memories."

Far off, across the sleeping city, the palace bells began to toll the curfew, each note falling into the frozen air like a stone into a well. And beneath the bells, so faint it might have been imagination, Kestrel heard—or dreamed he heard—an old woman's voice, amused and unhurried, saying: *Well. Don't just stand there, dear. Winter won't wait, and neither will I.*
