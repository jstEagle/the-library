## 11. Interrogation of Odile Vane

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Odile Vane had decided—somewhere between the third hour and the fifth—that she would die before she let it smell like fear.

She sat on the bench with her spine straight and her hands folded in her lap, the posture she'd spent thirty years drilling into apprentices at the Keeper's Hall. *A memory-keeper's body is a ledger,* she used to tell them. *Keep your entries clean.* The Hall was gone now, burned or repurposed into a tithe-office, its students scattered or worse, but the posture remained. It was the last thing they hadn't taken from her.

The lamp in the corner guttered. She counted the flickers out of habit. Four hundred and eleven since they'd brought her down here. She had been counting things all her life; it was how keepers organized a mind that held too much. Names, dates, the exact number of steps from the Hall's front door to the well (sixty-two), the precise shade of grief that lived in a mother's memory of a stillborn child (a grey-violet, if you were foolish enough to try to see colors in such things, which Odile had been, once).

Four hundred and twelve.

The door opened on four hundred and nineteen, and Sergeant Hale came in carrying two cups of tea.

Odile looked at the tea. Then at him. "Poisoned?"

"No."

"Drugged?"

"A mild calming draught. Standard procedure." He set one cup on the floor near her feet, an oddly courteous distance away, as though he understood she wouldn't take anything from his hand. "It helps people remember clearly."

"I remember clearly enough," Odile said. "That's rather my profession."

"So I've read." He pulled the second chair—it was a proper chair, not a stool, another courtesy—and sat across from her. Up close he looked tired in a way that went deeper than sleeplessness. Witch-finders aged like tools left out in weather: functional, rusting, kept sharp only where it mattered. "Odile Vane. Formerly Senior Keeper of the Third Archive. Struck from the rolls nine years ago for—" he consulted a file he didn't need to consult, "—unlicensed extraction. You took a memory from a magistrate without consent."

"He was going to burn a child," Odile said. "I took his memory of signing the order instead. He wept at the pyre like everyone else. I considered it an improvement to his character."

Hale wrote something. Or pretended to. Odile had watched enough men pretend to write in her life to know the difference—the pen moving without ink following. He was buying time to look at her, and she let him, because every moment he spent looking was a moment he wasn't spending elsewhere.

Elsewhere being Kestrel Fairweather, Sable, and Corporal Brann, who by now should be three districts north and climbing.

*Don't think about them,* she told herself, and then thought about them immediately, because the mind is a contrary animal and no amount of keeper training ever fixed that.

"You were captured covering their escape," Hale said. "On the rooftops off Tanner's Row. You could have run. You didn't."

"I'm sixty-one years old, Sergeant. Running is for the young. I chose the dignified option."

"You fought."

"Kicking is also dignified, in its way."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, quickly arrested. He set down the pen. "Madam Vane. I'm going to be honest with you, because I find honesty loosens tongues faster than the alternative, and the alternative is downstairs and has teeth."

"The truth-teller's box."

"Among other instruments." He said it flatly, without relish, and Odile filed that away: *he doesn't enjoy this.* A witch-finder who didn't enjoy his work was either a very good liar or a very dangerous asset. Either way, useful. "You're harboring a known memory-thief. You assisted in a break-in at the Crown archive. You are, by every law His Majesty has signed in the last decade, guilty of sedition, theft, and unlawful magic. The penalty is the pyre."

"Yes," Odile said. "I did read the decrees. I found them repetitive. Ten years of the same three proclamations, reissued with new seals. Even tyrants should vary their prose."

"Tell me where Fairweather is going."

"And if I do?"

"Then you hang instead of burn. It's a mercy. Small, but real."

Odile regarded him for a long moment. Then she reached down, picked up the cup of tea, and drank it in full view of him, calming draught and all, because the first rule of feeding someone false memories was to make them believe you trusted them.

"Good," Hale said quietly. "Now. The route north. Where did Fairweather go after Tanner's Row?"

And Odile Vane, disgraced keeper, struck from the rolls, began to lie with the finest craftsmanship of her long career.

* * *

Here is what most people never understood about memories, Odile thought as she spoke: they were not facts. They were stories the mind told itself, and stories could be *edited.*

She couldn't plant a whole false memory in Hale—not without touching him, and he wore a finder's charm at his throat that would scorch her fingers black—but she didn't need to. She needed only to give him true-shaped pieces and let his own mind assemble the lie. That was the secret no one taught you: the best forgery was ninety percent genuine material. Let the interrogator's certainty do the binding.

"There's a smuggler's tunnel under the tannery," she said, and it was even true—a tunnel existed there, she'd used it herself years ago—which made the next part easier to swallow. "Fairweather means to go south. Downriver, toward Saltmarsh. There's a ship. He thinks the map leads to something in the fens—an old vault, pre-dynastic. He believes whatever's in it can be sold."

"Sell it? To whom?"

"To anyone. He's a thief, Sergeant, not a martyr. Did you truly imagine he was doing any of this out of principle?" She let a note of contempt creep in, bitter and convincing, because contempt was the easiest emotion to fake when you were furious at yourself for meaning a little of it. "The boy steals memories for coin. He bottled my own student's wedding day once and sold it back to her at interest. Whatever tale he told you—"

"He hasn't told me a tale. He ran."

"They always run." She sipped the tea. Calming draught, yes, but weak; someone in the chain of command was skimping, and she made a mental note to mock the Crown's procurement practices at the first opportunity. "South, through the tannery tunnel, then the fen road. If you move tonight you might catch him at the Saltmarsh ferry."

Hale wrote it down. This time the ink followed the pen.

He asked questions for another hour. She answered them all, weaving the southern route thicker with each pass—landmarks, timings, the name of a ferryman she invented on the spot and gave a limp and a wife named Marta, because invented people needed domestic details to live. By the end she almost believed in him herself. Poor limping Aldous and his Marta. She hoped they'd have a good life in her imagination.

When Hale rose to leave, she said, "Sergeant."

He paused at the door.

"The calming draught. Tell whoever brews it that chamomile masks the taste better than valerian. Your apothecary is lazy."

This time the smile escaped fully, for half a second, before he killed it. "I'll pass along your compliments," he said, and locked the door behind him.

Odile sat alone in the lamplight and let her hands shake, finally, now that no one could see. Four hundred and fifty-two flickers. South through the tannery. She gave them until dawn before someone checked the tunnel and found it bricked over since the flood year. Perhaps less. Perhaps—

The lamp went out.

Not guttered. Not blown by any draft, because there was no draft in a sealed cell. It simply stopped, the flame folding inward like a hand closing, and the dark that replaced it was not ordinary dark. Ordinary dark has texture. This had *appetite.*

"Well," said a voice from the corner where no one stood, "you must be the keeper."

Odile did not scream. She was proud of that, later. Her heart attempted to exit through her ribs, but her voice stayed level, and level was what mattered. "I don't receive visitors after dark. House rules."

The cold arrived first. It poured into the room like water finding low ground, pooling around the bench, around her ankles, seeping up through the soles of her boots. And then the shape assembled itself out of the cold—tall, crowned, wearing a face she knew from every coin in her purse. The Hollow King looked at her with eyes like the last light leaving a window.

"You lied beautifully to my sergeant," he said. "South through the tannery. A ferryman with a limp. Marta." Something that was almost amusement moved across the borrowed face, and it was worse than anger. Anger would have meant distance. This thing found her *funny.* "I tasted it through him. Very fine work. Ninety percent true, isn't it? That's the craft. I respect craft."

"What do you want?" Odile asked. "If you've come to execute me, do get on with it. I have a book waiting."

"I've come to *read.*"

It crossed the room without crossing the room—one moment at the wall, the next seated across from her in Hale's abandoned chair, and the chair creaked under nothing at all. "Hold still, madam keeper. This will feel like winter."

It wasn't touch, exactly. It was colder than touch and more intimate. Something slid against the surface of her mind like a blade laid flat against skin—not cutting, yet. *Tasting.* She felt it move through the outer rooms of her memory, the ones she kept unlocked for exactly this reason: the Hall, the apprentices, the smell of chalk and beeswax, her mother's kitchen, a hundred harmless rooms furnished for visitors.

It lingered on Kestrel. On the boy he'd been, twelve years old and starving clever, the first student she'd ever taken off the street. On the day she'd struck him for stealing from another student's stores, and the day she'd taken it back, because he'd stolen to feed a girl whose family had forgotten her face. On the night the Hall fell, and she'd pressed her signet ring into his hand and told him *never come find me,* and hated herself for hoping he would.

The cold paused there. Turned something over.

"Ah," the Hollow King said softly. "There it is."

*No.* Odile slammed every door she had, threw up walls of static and noise, buried the thought under ten thousand counting-numbers—but she was sixty-one, and tired, and drugged, and it was a king's worth of hunger pressing against her, and she felt it find the edge of the thing she'd hidden deepest: the echo of the map. Not the map itself—she'd refused to memorize its route, had burned her own recollection of it down to ash the night they planned—but the *shape* of it. The way it had felt in her hands. Warm. Alive. Humming with a grief so vast it had a flavor, and the flavor pointed north, beneath a mountain, like a compass needle made of sorrow.

The Hollow King went very still.

"You've seen the Vault," it said. Not amused anymore. Hungry in a way that made the room's remaining warmth flinch. "Or the road to it. It's in you. Faint. Like a song remembered wrong." The borrowed face leaned close, and up close the king's beauty failed entirely—you could see the seams where the face was worn, like a mask fitted over something that had no face of its own. "I could take it. I could open you like a letter and read every line. But letters tear, madam keeper, and torn letters lose their postscripts. So I am going to ask. Politely. Once."

"Then ask," Odile said. Her voice only shook a little. "Politely."

"Where does the thief go?"

"South," Odile said. "Through the tannery. Ask your ferryman."

For a long moment the cold simply *held* her, considering, and Odile stared into the Hollow King's dying-window eyes and understood, with terrible clarity, that it believed her. Not because she was convincing—though she was—but because it could not imagine why anyone would lie at this price. It had spent ten years eating a city's love for its king. It had forgotten that people lied for each other. That was the flaw in it, she thought, giddy with terror and triumph in equal measure. That was the crack in the ice.

"You'll freeze before they come for you," it said at last, rising. "The cell. I've ordered the heat turned off. A small cruelty. I'm told I'm owed a few." At the door it paused, and the crown caught light that wasn't there. "Your boy is walking into my house, keeper. And when he arrives, I will show him what you traded for his head start. I want you to know that. I want it to keep you warm."

The lamp relit. The cold drained away like water through sand, and Odile Vane sat alone in the flickering light with frost blooming on the inside of the windowless walls.

She laughed. It came out cracked and ragged and slightly unhinged, and she didn't care. Because it had believed her. It had *believed* her, and every hour it spent sending riders south to Saltmarsh—to a bricked-over tunnel and a ferryman who did not exist—was an hour Kestrel gained on the frozen river north.

Her fingers were already going numb. The chill in the stones was deepening by the minute, patient and administrative, the kind of cold that killed you with paperwork.

*Well,* she thought, drawing her knees up and wrapping her arms around them, keeper's posture abandoned at last in favor of simple heat-hoarding. *You wanted a family, Odile. This is what families cost.*

She began to count her breaths, slow and even, the way she'd teach apprentices to endure extraction. In for four. Hold for seven. Out for eight.

Somewhere north, three fools she loved were running along a frozen river toward a mountain full of grief, and every breath she counted bought them another step.

In for four. Hold for seven.

The frost crept up the walls like ivy in fast motion, silver and beautiful and utterly indifferent, and Odile Vane kept counting, because counting was prayer, and prayer was defiance, and defiance was the only magic left to her.

Out for eight.

By her reckoning—they'd reach the mountain in six days.

She intended to be alive, and lying, when they did.
