## 12. The Road Under the Ice

The river froze in layers, the way grief does.

Kestrel had learned that from a fisherman once—a memory he'd bought for two coppers and sold again three times before the taste went thin. *First ice is glass,* the old man had said, mending his nets in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and onions. *Then it snows, and the snow packs down, and freezes again, and you get ice on ice on ice until you can't tell where one winter ends and the next begins.* He'd laughed when he said it, but his hands had gone still on the netting, and Kestrel had understood even then that the man wasn't talking about the river at all.

Now he walked north along that same layered dark, boots crunching on a road of frozen water, and thought: *yes. That's what I am. Ice laid over ice laid over ice, and no way to tell anymore which layer was the first one.*

"Stop dawdling," Sable called from ahead. She moved like the cold didn't touch her, which it didn't—she'd told them once that after the pyre, ordinary frost felt like a kind word. "Brann's found a bend in the river. Says it'll hide us from the road."

"The road can't see us," Brann rumbled. He was a big man made bigger by his guard's coat, which he hadn't yet been able to bring himself to stop wearing, though he'd torn the Crown badge off with his own hands two nights ago. "Hale's trackers can."

They walked on. The frozen Vell stretched away behind them toward the city, a gray smear under a gray sky, and ahead of them the river climbed into hills that grew whiter and more silent with every mile. Somewhere up there, past the tree line, past the passes, beneath Mount Cindral itself, lay the Ember Vault—and the true king, hollowed out like a gourd, wearing ten years of someone else's stolen warmth.

And somewhere behind them, patient as frost, came Sergeant Hale.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a fallen willow, its trunk furred with ice so thick it looked like something grown rather than fallen. Brann built a fire small enough to cup in your hands and fed it twigs one at a time, the way soldiers do when they've learned that smoke kills faster than cold. Sable sat with her back against the willow and her sister's memory-vial in her lap, turning it over and over, not opening it. She hadn't opened it since the archive. Kestrel suspected she was saving it, the way you save the last of anything.

He sat across the fire and tried not to look at either of them and failed at both.

"You're doing the thing," Sable said without looking up.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at the fire like it owes you money and rehearse whatever it is you're not going to say."

"I don't rehearse."

"You absolutely rehearse. You had three drafts of the speech you gave Odile before we left the safehouse. I heard you practicing in the pantry. Draft two was much better than draft three, if it helps."

Brann snorted. The fire popped. Somewhere far off, a branch cracked under snow-weight, and all three of them went still as hares before the silence settled back.

"It's about the memories," Kestrel said finally. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "What I did. Before all this. What I actually—" He stopped. Started again. "You both think I'm a petty thief who got caught up in something large. A charm-seller. A smuggler of other people's happy afternoons. And that's true, but it isn't the whole of it, and we're walking into a place that's going to ask us to give things up, and I don't think I have any right to ask that of you while I'm still—" *still hiding behind my own ice*, he didn't say. "While I'm still lying by omission."

Sable stopped turning the vial.

Brann fed another twig to the fire and said nothing, which from Brann was a kind of attention.

So Kestrel told them.

* * *

"I told you I never took large memories," he began. "That was the rule. Never the same customer twice, never a big one, never stay. And mostly I kept it. But here's what I didn't tell you: the rule exists because of what happened the first time I broke it."

He pulled his knees up, wrapped his arms around them. The firelight made his shadow lean close, like a listener.

"There was a woman in the Tanner's Ward. Marla. She had a son who died in the fever winter—the first one, nine years back, before anyone knew the tithes were starting. She couldn't work. Couldn't eat. Sat in her chair by the window all day watching the street like he might come around the corner. Her sister paid me. Good coin, more than I usually asked, and I was young and hungry and I told myself a mother's worst memory was fair game—it would *help* her, wouldn't it? Take the wound out and the woman could walk again."

The fire crackled. Sable had gone very still.

"So I took it. The whole day. The fever breaking, the little body going quiet, the moment she knew." His voice caught and he pushed through it, because stopping now would be worse than any of it. "And she could walk again. She got up the next morning and made breakfast and went back to the loom and everyone said how well she was bearing it. Because she wasn't bearing anything. I'd carried it off in a bottle and sold it to a collector in the Hill Quarter who liked tragic maternal grief, said it had *depth*. He served it at dinner parties. People wept appreciatively and then ate the cheese course."

"Oh, Kestrel," Sable said softly.

"That's not the worst part. The worst part is that it worked. She was happier. Genuinely. And I needed coin, and there were always more Marlas—people drowning in a memory they couldn't live under—and I got good at telling myself I was a surgeon. Taking out what they couldn't survive. And maybe sometimes I was. But a surgeon doesn't sell what he cuts out to men who serve it with cheese." He laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "I sold a widow her husband's voice. I sold a man the memory of his brother forgiving him—he'd come to me asking me to take the guilt, and I took the forgiveness instead because it was worth more, and he walked out lighter and never knew why he couldn't feel it anymore. I sold a girl her own wedding day because her father wanted the dowry dispute forgotten and the wedding was tangled up in it. She came back twice looking for something she'd lost and couldn't name, and I turned her away both times, because the rule is never the same customer twice."

He looked up. The fire had burned low; Brann fed it another twig without being asked.

"And some of them needed those memories. That's the thing I've never said out loud. Not just wanted—*needed*. There was a night-watchman who stayed awake through every shift because if he slept he dreamed of the fire that killed his crew, and I took the dream-memory and he finally slept, and that one I don't regret. But there was an old man who asked me to take his wife—not her death, just her, forty years of her, because living without her hurt too much, and I should have refused, I should have told him that the hurt was the love with nowhere to go, but he paid triple and I was twenty-two and stupid, and afterward he used to stop me in the market and say, *young man, do I know you? You seem like someone I ought to know.* And I'd say no. Every time. No."

Silence. The wind moved through the dead willow, and the ice on its branches chimed faintly, like distant glassware.

"I built a life out of other people's emptied rooms," Kestrel finished. "And I filled my own rooms with the rent. That's my hoard. Hundreds of them. Bottled. Catalogued. Some I kept because they were beautiful and some because I couldn't find a buyer and some because—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Some because they were mine. Memories people sold me of *me*. My mother's face, from a neighbor who remembered her. The taste of the bread from the bakery where I grew up, before the bakery and the street and the whole district burned. My own name, spoken by someone who loved me, back when I answered to it easily. I kept those. In the deepest shelf. And tomorrow we're going to a vault that's going to want everything I have, and I need you to know what you're traveling with. Not a hero. A magpie. A magpie with a ledger of sins."

He expected pity. He'd braced for pity the way you brace for cold water.

Instead Sable reached across the fire and flicked him on the forehead.

"Ow—"

"You absolute idiot," she said, and her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "You think that makes you the worst person on this river? I watched witch-finders burn my sister and I spent three years not saving anyone else because I was busy staying alive. Brann wore that coat for six years while the tithes rolled past his checkpoint, and he's a *good* man, and he still wore it." Brann didn't argue. "You stole from grieving people because you were a starving kid with a gift nobody taught you to use kindly, and then you spent nine years feeling exactly as bad about it as you should. Do you know how rare that is? The feeling-bad? Most people just—" she gestured vaguely northward, toward the palace, toward the thing on its throne, "—most people build a crown out of it instead."

"The difference between you and the Hollow King," Brann said quietly, "is that it eats grief and grows. You carry yours and it hasn't made you bigger at all. If anything you're the smallest man I know."

"Thank you?" Kestrel said uncertainly.

"It's a compliment. Mostly."

Sable leaned forward. "Here's what I actually think, and listen, because I don't say generous things often and it exhausts me. Those memories you sold—the ones people needed—you gave them back their lives. Badly. Clumsily. At a price no honest surgeon would charge. But the woman who couldn't get out of her chair? She walked. The watchman slept. You weren't a good man doing bad things or a bad man doing good things. You were a boy with a knife in a world full of wounds, cutting the ones you could reach." She sat back. "And now you're going to give it all back. All of it. Even the name. So the ledger closes square, as far as I'm concerned."

"It doesn't feel square."

"No," she agreed. "It won't. That's how you know you're not the Hollow King. It feels perfectly square to *him*."

Brann cleared his throat, which was how he announced that feelings had been discussed long enough. "For what it's worth," he said, staring hard at the fire, "the memory you took from me. The one from the bridge collapse. I asked around, after. Found out what it was. Everyone always asks eventually." He shrugged, enormous shoulders rising and falling. "I'm glad it's gone. Whatever it cost you to take it—I'd pay it again. So add that to the ledger. One entry on the other side."

Kestrel found he couldn't speak for a moment. He nodded instead, and Brann nodded back, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was somehow enough.

* * *

The blizzard came an hour past midnight, roaring down out of the hills like something with intent.

One moment the sky was clear and vicious with stars; the next the wind hit them sideways with a wall of white, and the temperature dropped so fast that the moisture in Kestrel's breath froze in his beard. They stumbled along the riverbank, arms linked, Sable shouting directions none of them could hear, until Brann—bless his enormous, observant heart—caught the smell of dry stone and hauled them left, up a scree slope, into the mouth of a cave.

It went back farther than they needed. Dry sand floor, old soot marks on the ceiling where travelers had sheltered before them, maybe years before them, maybe decades. Brann got a proper fire going this time—no point hiding smoke in a blizzard; the storm was cover enough—and Sable rigged their cloaks into a windbreak at the cave mouth with the efficiency of someone who'd housed fugitives through four hard winters.

Outside, the world ceased to exist. Inside, the fire threw warm light up the stone, and for the first time in days, nobody was listening for hoofbeats.

Kestrel sat with his back to the wall and let himself, just for a moment, feel safe. It was a strange sensation, like trying on a coat cut for someone slightly larger. Across the fire, Sable had finally uncorked her sister's vial—not to drink, just to hold it open under her nose, breathing in a memory that belonged to her, letting it settle back into her skin a thread at a time. Her face did something complicated and soft. Brann was asleep sitting up, which he claimed was a skill learned on night watch and which looked, frankly, like a superpower.

The map stirred against Kestrel's chest.

It had been quiet for days—dormant, sullen, spending its heat only when Hale's pursuit pressed close, warming like a warning bell. Now it pulsed, slow and deliberate, and heat bled through his shirt, and he knew without looking that it wasn't warning him of anything behind.

It was pulling. Forward. North.

He drew it out. In the firelight the bloodstained lines seemed to move—not the route, that held steady, winding up through the passes to Cindral—but the margins, the little annotations in the courier's cramped hand, which Kestrel could have sworn hadn't been there yesterday. Words surfacing through the parchment like bodies through ice:

*Three days to the pass. The mountain tests what you carry. Come empty or don't come.*

And beneath that, in a different hand entirely—older, formal, a hand Kestrel had seen once before, flooding through him in a white room with a crowned man screaming in it:

*Tell the thief: I remember everything. That is the problem.*

The fire popped. Sable looked up sharply. "What is it?"

Kestrel stared at the words until they blurred. "I think," he said slowly, "the king just wrote to us."

Outside, the blizzard howled on, and somewhere south of it, patient as frost, Sergeant Hale knelt beside a cold fire ring on the frozen river and smiled.
