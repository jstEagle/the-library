## 13. Hale's Doubt

Sergeant Corwin Hale had been a witch-finder for nineteen years, and in that time he had learned exactly three things about himself.

First: he was very good at his work. Second: he did not enjoy his work, which he considered a professional advantage—enjoyment led to carelessness, and carelessness let the guilty slip away. Third, and most privately: he could no longer remember his mother's face, and he suspected—though he never said it aloud, not even to himself in the dark—that this was because somewhere in a Crown archive, in a drawer with a brass label, her last years sat bottled and labeled like preserves.

He thought about that third thing more often lately.

It was the memories Odile Vane had fed him. He knew that now, or was beginning to know it, the way you begin to know a tooth is bad—not all at once but in small betrayals. A taste of copper when he swallowed. A dream of a pyre that kept rearranging itself, so that each time he looked at the face in the flames it was someone different: a smuggler, a healer, a girl of perhaps fourteen who had once, in a memory he trusted absolutely, turned a flooded cellar into a garden of light for frightened neighbors.

Witch-finders did not arrest girls who made gardens of light. Witch-finders arrested monsters.

Except.

Hale crouched at the edge of the frozen river, breath steaming, and studied the tracks leading north. Three sets. One heavy—a soldier's stride, even worn by snowshoes improvised from barrel staves. One light and quick, favoring the left foot. And one that paused too often, doubling back, checking on the others. The thief. Fairweather.

Behind him, six men waited with drawn crossbows and the particular patience of people who had been promised a bonus.

"Tracks go upriver, Sergeant," said young Pell, eager as a hound. "We'll have them by dark."

"We'll have them when I say we have them." Hale straightened. His knee ached. It had been aching since Vellamarch, since the interrogation cells, since Odile Vane had looked at him across the table with those terrible calm eyes and said, *You've been eating from their hand so long you've forgotten what your own thoughts taste like.*

He'd had her whipped for insolence. He'd thought about her words every night since.

"Move out," he said, and hated how much he wanted them to find nothing.

* * *

The blizzard broke an hour past noon, which was either mercy or trap, and Kestrel Fairweather had learned long ago that in Vellamarch the two were usually the same thing.

"They're behind us," Sable said. She didn't turn around. She never turned around anymore; she read pursuit in the wind itself, in the way snowflakes stuttered, in some animal arithmetic Kestrel couldn't follow. "Six riders. Maybe seven. They're not hurrying."

"Why wouldn't they hurry?" Brann asked. He was limping again. The ice under the river snow had taken a bite out of his ankle two days back, and he refused to complain about it, which somehow made it worse to listen to.

"Because they don't need to," Sable said. "We're walking into open country with nowhere to hide. They're letting us tire ourselves out." Her mouth curved without warmth. "That's what they did to my sister. Let her run for three days first. Said it was procedure."

Nobody answered that. There wasn't anything to answer it with.

Kestrel trudged along and tried to think like a map instead of a man. The river bent north here, wide and white, hemmed by black pines. Ahead, if Odile's false route held—and Hale would be following Odile's false route, which meant the real route was safe, which meant Odile was safe, which meant—no. Don't. Thinking about Odile in a cell was like pressing on a bruise to prove it still hurt. Keep moving. Keep the others moving. That was the whole job now.

"There," Brann said suddenly. "Smoke."

A bend in the river, a shelf of rock, and tucked beneath it the mouth of an old charcoal-burner's camp: a lean-to of split logs, a fire ring, wood stacked dry under oilcloth by some forester who hadn't come back this winter. It was shelter. It was also, Kestrel noted with the weary clarity of a man who had been hunted before, a place where three exhausted fugitives would sit with their backs to a fire and see nothing coming until it arrived.

"We can't stop," he said.

"We're stopping," Sable said. "Brann's ankle is swelling. You're swaying when you walk. And I—" she paused, honest by force, "—haven't slept since Vellamarch. If they catch us, better we're caught rested."

"That is the single worst tactical argument I've ever heard," Kestrel said.

"Thank you. I've been practicing."

They stopped.

Brann got a fire going small and hot, the way soldiers do, feeding it sticks one at a time so the smoke stayed thin. Sable wrapped her sister's memory-vial—the one they'd pulled from the archive, warm as a sleeping bird—in wool and tucked it against her heart, where she'd carried it through everything since. Kestrel sat with the map against his chest and felt it pulse there, patient and alive, like a second heartbeat that knew where it was going even when he didn't.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" Brann said, after a while. "The night you took my memory. The bad one. From Marrowfield."

Kestrel went carefully still. "I remember."

"I know you think you stole something from me. Took a piece out and left me less." Brann poked the fire. "But here's the thing. I got my life back that night. Whatever you cut out, you left the rest of me whole enough to keep living in. That's not theft. That's surgery." He glanced over. "I've been wanting to say that for six years and never found the moment. This seemed like as good a one as any, given we might die this afternoon."

"Genuinely touching," Sable said. "Bury me somewhere with a view."

Kestrel laughed despite everything—which was, he reflected, the whole trick of these two, the whole impossible machinery of them. Six days ago they'd been strangers bound by debts and grudges. Now they bickered like people who expected to grow old within earshot of each other. He didn't know when that had happened. He suspected it happened the way frost happens: quietly, everywhere at once, and then all at once you couldn't imagine the window without it.

Which was, of course, precisely when the world sent Sergeant Corwin Hale.

* * *

They heard the horses at the last possible moment—the snow ate sound, and the wind had turned traitor—and then the trees at the top of the riverbank were full of shapes, and crossbows were leveled down at the little camp, and Hale came walking down the slope with his warrant tube in one hand like a man carrying a verdict he'd already written.

"Don't," he said, to Sable, whose hand had drifted toward her knife. "There are seven of us and one of you is limping. Let's all be adults about the mathematics."

"Sergeant Brann," Hale added, and something flickered across his face—genuine pain, quickly filed away. "Corporal. I signed your promotion papers myself, four years ago. Do you know how that reads in my report? *One of mine.*"

"It reads however you write it, Sergeant," Brann said. He stood up slowly, hands visible, and put himself between the crossbows and the other two without making a show of it, which was somehow more damning than if he'd made a show of it. "You always were good at writing things however you needed them to read."

"Step aside, Tam."

"No."

"Tam." Hale's voice dropped. For a moment the sergeant vanished and something older stood there, tired, cold, a man whose knee hurt and whose dreams were haunted by gardens of light. "Seven crossbows. Step aside and I'll note you were taken hostage. It'll even be true, near enough."

"He saved my life once," Brann said. "Took a memory out of me that was killing me by inches. So no. I'm standing where I'm standing, and you can write whatever you like above it."

Hale looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked past him—at Sable, marked with the ash-line scars the witch-finders themselves gave survivors; at Kestrel, thin and watchful, the famous charm-seller of the night market whom half the city's underworld called the Bottled Man.

And then, because he was thorough—it was the one virtue he'd never doubted in himself—he did the thing he always did before an arrest. He reached into the suspects' pasts. Not with magic; witch-finders weren't permitted magic, that was rather the point of them. With paper. With reports. With memory.

And his memory, tonight, was full of holes.

He saw the pyre at Ashmarket, three years ago. He'd attended. He remembered attending. But when he reached for the face of the condemned, the memory slid like wet soap—and underneath it, surfacing now for the hundredth time, that girl. Fourteen. Light blooming in a flooded cellar like lanterns underwater. Neighbors' faces lifted toward her, weeping with relief. And stamped across the bottom of the recollection, in handwriting he did not recognize and could not stop reading: *convicted of unlawful flame-working, sentence carried out.*

She'd been making light for frightened people. In a flood. At night.

He remembered signing the order. He remembered being *sure*. He could feel the shape of the certainty, the way you can feel the shape of a missing tooth. Someone had handed him that certainty, neat and pre-wrapped, and he had swallowed it without tasting it, the way he'd swallowed a hundred of them, the way the whole apparatus swallowed them by the barrel—

"The tithe records," Hale said aloud, before he could stop himself.

"What?" said Pell.

"The archive." Hale's voice sounded strange to him, distant, like someone else speaking through a wall. "Ten years of voluntary memory tithes. I requisitioned the summaries last month. Routine. Every district, quotas met, citizens *grateful*." He frowned. "Grateful. Why would I write grateful? Who writes grateful into a seizure report?"

"Sergeant," Pell said uncertainly, "the prisoners—"

"The prisoners." Hale looked at them properly then. Three exhausted people sharing one fire small enough to fit in a cup. One soldier committing treason with his feet planted. One woman with ash-scars and a vial of somebody's last words held against her chest like a relic. One thief who, by every report Hale had ever read, had never once sold a harmful memory, never twice to the same customer, never stayed anywhere long enough to be caught—because staying would mean hurting someone, and the monster of the night market apparently couldn't stomach it.

Nineteen years. Nineteen years of pyres, and the thing in front of him that most resembled a monster was the warrant in his own hand.

"Fairweather," Hale said. "The courier. Joss Merrin. Did you kill him?"

"No," Kestrel said. "I held him while he died. He gave me the map. He said—" Kestrel hesitated, then decided the truth was the only coin left worth spending. "He said the Vault remembers. He was bleeding from a wound no knife makes. Something under the palace did that to him, Sergeant. Something that's been eating this city's memories for ten years and wearing the king's face while it digests."

Silence, except the wind.

"You expect me to believe the Hollow King is a parasite." Hale's laugh came out cracked down the middle. "I've seen the king. I've stood guard at the Winter Proclamation. He's radiant. He's—"

*Wrong,* supplied the girl in the flooded cellar, in her garden of light. *Wrong, wrong, wrong.*

"—he's beloved," Hale finished, and heard, as he said it, exactly how a sentence like that gets written into a report by someone else's hand.

Brann stepped forward, slow, palms still open. "Sergeant. You swore an oath. We both did. Not to the Crown's officers. Not to the warrants. To the *people*. 'To shield the weak from harm, wheresoever it comes from, and whosoever wields it.'" He tapped his own chest, where the oath lived. "I've been reading that line every night for a month, sir. Wheresoever. Even if it comes from above."

"Even if it comes from me," Hale said quietly.

"You said it, sir. Not me."

Hale stood there a long time. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he didn't brush it off. Behind him, six crossbows waited on his word, and he understood—with the strange floating clarity of a man watching his own life change weather—that whatever he did next would be the last thing nineteen-year-old Corwin Hale ever did, and the first thing whoever-he-was-next would do instead.

He thought of his mother's face, missing from his own memory like a portrait cut from its frame. He thought of drawers with brass labels. He thought: *someone signed for those.*

"Pell," he said. "Give me the warrant."

Pell handed it over gladly, relieved to obey. Hale unrolled it—the fair copy, the beautiful one, with the seal fat and red as a wound—and read it once more. *Kestrel Fairweather, wanted for theft of Crown property and conspiracy against the throne. Apprehend by any means. Reward commensurate.*

Crown property. As if a dying man's blood were inventory. As if a city's memories were grain to be levied.

"Well," Hale said. "Any means."

He crouched at the fire ring, and fed the warrant into the flames.

Pell made a strangled noise. Someone else said "Sergeant!" in genuine horror, as though the paper were holy. Hale watched the seal curl and blacken, watched the ink lift off in the heat like small escaping spirits, and felt—there was no other word for it—*warmth*. Real warmth, the kind the Hollow King had been taxing out of Vellamarch for a decade, rising off a burning lie and into his cold hands.

"Report will read," he said, standing, brushing snow from his knees with great formality, "that the fugitives escaped across the ice in the storm. Pursuit was hampered by conditions. Two men fell through a soft patch." He looked at Pell. "You and Dorn fell through a soft patch. You're soaked, you nearly died, it was very dramatic. Nod."

Pell nodded. Pell would have nodded at anything by then.

"The rest of you," Hale said, turning to face his own men, his own nineteen years, "saw nothing but weather. Understood?"

A pause. Then Dorn, the oldest of them, a gray-bearded veteran who had burned more pyres than anyone present and slept worse than all of them combined, said: "Understood, Sergeant. Terrible weather." And began, deliberately, ostentatiously, to check his crossbow string, and did not look at the fugitives at all.

Hale walked to the edge of the camp. He did not look at Brann, because if he looked at Brann he might have to acknowledge what Brann's face was doing, and he had used up all his composure on the fire.

"Northwest," he said instead, gruffly, to the air between them. "The river bends northwest past the old weir, and there's a charcoal camp beyond it—no, you've found this one. Fine. There's a second, half a day on, under the cliff. Foresters use it. Nobody reports foresters; foresters pay nobody." He paused. "And Fairweather. The village past the weir—Coldharrow. My men tithed it in autumn. Full quota." His jaw worked. "When you pass through, if you pass through—look at it. Look at it closely. And then tell me I'm wrong about what I think I'm starting to know."

"You're not wrong," Sable said softly. "We've seen what the collectors leave behind."

"Then God help us all," Hale said, "and get off my river before I recover my senses."

They went. All three of them, up the bank and into the pines, Brann limping, Sable steady, Kestrel pausing once at the treeline to look back—but Hale had already turned away, already shouting orders in his parade voice, already becoming again the instrument he had chosen, for one more day, to pretend to be.

* * *

They walked fast and mostly silent, the way people do after witnessing something enormous done quietly. It was full dusk when Brann finally spoke.

"He burned it," he said, wonderingly. "Nineteen years. He burned his own warrant."

"People aren't warrants," Sable said. "People are fires. Somebody just finally struck the match." She glanced sideways at Kestrel. "Odile. What she planted in him, in that cell—she must have known it would take. Must have known it would take *this* long to take."

"She bought us today with a whipping," Kestrel said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Maybe with her life. And she'd tell us it was just good bookkeeping."

Ahead, through the thinning pines, the land opened—and there it was, exactly where Hale had said, hunched in the blue snow-light under the cliffs.

The village of Coldharrow.

Except it wasn't a village. It was the shape of one, the husk of one: chimneys standing over dead hearths, doors ajar on empty rooms, laundry still frozen on a line as if the whole place had simply stopped mid-breath. No smoke. No dogs. No tracks but theirs.

And on the wall of the well at the village center, scratched deep into the stone by someone with time and terror and a nail, a message in a hand Kestrel didn't know but Sable clearly did—her breath went out of her all at once, like a door kicked open.

Sable read it aloud, barely above a whisper, and the words went down through the three of them like cold water:

*"THEY TAKE THE NAMES FIRST. THEN THE FACES. HIDE WHAT YOU LOVE WHERE THE TITHES CAN'T REACH — R.V."*

"R.V.," Sable said. "Rosalind Vane." Her voice shook. "My sister wrote this. Before they took her." She looked up at the empty windows, dozens of them, dark and staring. "Kestrel. Where is everyone?"

Kestrel looked at the village that had forgotten itself, and felt the map against his chest beat faster, like a heart smelling home.

"I don't think anyone's here," he said. "I think that's the whole horror of it. Come on. Before the light goes."

But none of them moved quite yet. Because in the deepest window of the nearest house, just for a moment—whether trick of dusk or something else—something pale had seemed to stand, and watch, and wait.
