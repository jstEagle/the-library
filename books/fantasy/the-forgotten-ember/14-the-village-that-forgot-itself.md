## 14. The Village That Forgot Itself

The river ended two days north of the blizzard, or rather it stopped pretending to be a river and became a road of white glass, and they walked on it the way you walk through a church—quietly, and with the sense that something enormous was listening underneath.

Kestrel had learned to read the ice. Brann hadn't, and spent the morning walking like a man crossing a ballroom full of bear traps.

"You know," Sable said, watching him take another careful, splayed-footed step, "most people just walk."

"Most people haven't heard what's under frozen rivers," Brann said with dignity. "I've pulled three bodies out of the Vellamarch canal. They all sounded surprised."

"They were probably surprised about the drowning, not the ice."

"I prefer to think they'd made their peace with one and not the other."

Kestrel smiled despite himself. It kept happening now—the smiling. It was an alarming development in a life built on the principle that attachment was a debt you paid in blood. Three days ago these two had been a pyre survivor and a guard he'd been planning to abandon at the first crossroads, and now he caught himself slowing his stride so Brann wouldn't fall behind, and he didn't even have the decency to feel embarrassed about it.

The map rode against his chest, warm as a sleeping animal. Since the cave it had grown quieter, almost content, as if it liked where they were going. He tried not to think about that. Maps that liked things were maps that wanted things, and everything that wanted something from Kestrel Fairweather had wanted him to be somewhere specific when it happened.

Near midday, Brann stopped walking.

Not stumbled. Stopped, the way a man stops when his body has understood something his mind hasn't caught up to yet.

"Do you smell that?" he said.

Kestrel breathed in. Woodsmoke—but wrong. Not the homely smell of hearths and bread, the smell that meant people. This smoke was old and cold, like a fire that had gone out days ago and left only its ghost.

"Village ahead," Sable said. She had gone very still, her scarred hands flat against her thighs. "There's a village ahead. I know this stretch of river. There's a ferry town—Harrowmere, it was called. My sister and I came through it once, before." Before, from Sable, meant before the pyre, before the witch-finders, before the word *marked* had been written across her life in someone else's handwriting. "They had a woman who sold honey cakes shaped like swans. Wren bought six and ate them all before we reached the far bank."

"Then we eat," Brann said practically. "And maybe find out why a whole town smells like a cold hearth."

They climbed the bank. The village appeared between the bare trees all at once, the way things do in winter—suddenly, completely, without warning.

Harrowmere sat in its valley like a set of clothes no one was wearing.

That was the thought Kestrel had, and he couldn't shake it: *clothes without a person in them.* Smoke still curled from two chimneys, which meant fires had been lit and fed and then simply abandoned, still burning down unattended in empty houses. Laundry hung frozen on lines, stiff as boards, shirts with their arms out like men waving goodbye. A dog trotted up to them barking—not aggressive, *desperate*, circling their legs and then running ten feet toward the village and back, running and returning, running and returning, as if to say *they're in there, come see, come see, they're in there.*

"There's no one," Brann said. His hand had found his sword hilt without any decision being made. "Sable, are you sure this is Harrowmere?"

"This is Harrowmere."

"Where is everyone?"

Kestrel didn't answer. He was looking at the well.

It stood in the center of the village square, as wells do, and around its rim someone had carved words into the wood of the bucket-frame. Fresh carvings—the wood was pale at the cuts, days old at most. He walked toward it and the dog followed him, pressing against his leg, wanting to be touched by anything that could touch back.

The carving said:

**WE HAD NAMES.**

Just that. Four words, cut deep, by someone who had pressed hard enough to snap the knife twice—you could see the abandoned strokes.

"Tithe-collectors," Odile would have said, if Odile were there. Kestrel said it for her instead, aloud, because saying it made it real and unreal at the same time. "This is what a harvest looks like."

He'd stolen memories for eleven years. He knew the mechanics the way a pickpocket knows pockets. You find the seam in a person, the place where memory sits loose, and you draw it out gentle, and mostly—and this was the part he'd told himself every night to sleep—you mostly took things people could spare. A first kiss that stung more than it sang. A grief too heavy to carry. Never a large memory. Never the same customer twice. Never stay.

But tithe-collectors weren't thieves. Thieves took the loose coins. Tithe-collectors took the purse, the coat, the name stitched inside the collar.

He'd seen the ledgers in the Crown archive. *Voluntary tithes, gratefully given, for the warmth of the realm.* Rows and rows of entries. He'd read them with the detached horror of a man reading about a flood in a distant province.

Here was the flood.

* * *

They moved through the village slowly, and the village did nothing to stop them, because there was nothing left in it to do anything.

In the first house, a table was set for supper. Three bowls, three spoons, a loaf of bread gone grey and hard under a cloth. The chairs were pushed back as if the family had stood up all at once—which, Kestrel supposed, they had, and walked out into the snow, and been collected.

On the wall above the table, a child had drawn in charcoal: a house, a sun, four stick figures holding hands. Underneath, in a parent's careful hand, the figures were labeled. *Mama. Papa. Joss. Little Fen.*

Except the labels had been scratched out. Not by the child. By an adult hand, later, frantic, the charcoal dug deep until the paper tore. And beside the scratched-out names, in that same adult hand, one line:

*I can't remember which one is Joss.*

Kestrel had to put his hand on the doorframe and stand there a while.

Brann found him like that. The big man looked at the wall, and looked at Kestrel, and said nothing, which was its own kind of kindness. Then he said, quietly, "My wedding day. In the mountain. I gave it up to get us through those doors, and I keep reaching for it, you know? Like a tongue going to a missing tooth. I know I was happy. I can't remember being happy. I just—" he gestured vaguely at his own chest, "—know it the way you know weather happened."

"Yes," Kestrel said.

"And this—" Brann nodded at the torn paper, at the scratched-out names. "This is what happens when you give up everything. Not one tooth. All of them."

"This is what happens when it's taken."

"Is there a difference?"

Kestrel thought about the map warm against his chest. About a crowned man screaming in a white room. "I'm starting to believe there might be."

* * *

Sable found the wall in the late afternoon.

She'd gone quiet hours ago—the particular quiet she went into when she was working, when her grief turned from a weight into a tool. She checked doorways and sightlines like a soldier, and she asked questions in a low voice: *When did the fires go untended? Which way did the collectors' sled tracks run? How many houses, how many families, how many taken?*

The answers were bad. Forty-odd souls. Tracks leading south, toward Vellamarch. Fires abandoned five days ago.

She found the warning wall behind the smithy, and Kestrel knew she'd found it because she made a sound he'd never heard her make—a small sound, punched out of her, like the air leaving a bellows.

The wall was covered in writing. Dozens of hands, some neat, some scrawled, charcoal and paint and something that might have been blood. The villagers had written here in their last lucid days, the ones who felt themselves thinning first, the ones who understood what was happening while they still had the words to say it.

*THEY COME AT DUSK AND THEY ARE POLITE.*
*MY MOTHER'S FACE IS IN MY HEAD BUT I CANNOT FIND HER NAME ON IT.*
*DO NOT SIGN THE TITHE LEDGER. DO NOT LET THEM BE KIND TO YOU.*
*IF YOU ARE READING THIS, RUN NORTH. THE VAULT REMEMBERS WHAT WE FORGET.*

And at the bottom, smaller than the rest, hurried, half-smudged by weather but unmistakable to a sister's eye:

*Wren was here. If Sable comes — the ledgers lie. The king is not the king. Go to the mountain. I'm sorry about the swans. I love you. I love you. I love you.*

Sable stood in front of it with both palms flat against the wall, the way you'd press your hands against a door you couldn't open, and she read it three times, her lips moving, and then she said, in a voice scraped nearly clean:

"She wrote to me. Five years dead, and she wrote to me, and she got it here ahead of me by exactly the amount of time it took me to stop running and start hiding." A breath that shook on the way in and came out steady, because Sable's grief always came out steady; that was the terrible thing about her, that was the magnificent thing. "She knew. She knew before any of us. She wrote it on a wall in a dying village because she knew someday I'd come up this river."

"Maybe she hoped," Kestrel said.

"Don't." Sable turned. Her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "Don't give me hope, thief. Hope is what they harvest last, because it's the sweetest." But her hand stayed on the wall, over the words *I love you,* and she didn't move it for a long time.

* * *

They made camp in the smithy, because it had walls and a forge and the dog, who had appointed himself to their company with the solemnity of a knight taking vows. Brann named him Turnip, over Sable's objection that you shouldn't name things you might lose, and over Kestrel's private observation that Sable had already started feeding him from her own ration.

That night, with the fire low, Kestrel sat awake and listened to the village not existing around them.

No dogs barking except theirs. No voices. No creak of floorboards, no cough from a neighboring house, none of the thousand small sounds that make a place a home. Just wind, and the tick of cooling iron, and Turnip sighing in his sleep against Brann's side.

He thought about Vellamarch. He thought about the Winter Proclamation, the radiant wrong face on the throne, the crowd cheering, *for warmth, for warmth.* He thought about the archive ledgers, and how the entries had grown year over year, decade over decade, a slow tide of *voluntary* giving. He thought about the child's drawing on the torn paper, and the mother who had known her children's faces and lost their names, and had used her last sharp charcoal to tell whoever came after: *this happened here. Witness it.*

This was the future Vellamarch was buying. Every cheerful tithe-day, every ledger signed, every coin paid out for a memory the seller was assured they wouldn't miss. The city wasn't trading its past for warmth. It was trading itself, one name at a time, into a silence like this one—and the silence had a crown, and the crown had a face everyone loved.

"We turn back tomorrow," Brann said quietly, from across the fire. "We could. Take the ledgers' evidence south, raise the alarm, get ahead of it."

"And tell whom?" Sable said. "The witch-finders? The palace? The beloved face on the coin?" She poked the fire. Sparks rose and died. "There's no one to tell, Tam. There's only the thing that's doing it, and the mountain it lives under, and us."

"The three of us," Kestrel said, "and whatever's left of a king."

"Four," Sable said. "Odile's coming. She's alive—I'd bet my scars on it. That woman is too stubborn to die and too mean to stay captured." Something flickered across her face that was almost a smile. "Besides, she owes me a rematch at cards."

Kestrel looked at his companions—at the guard who'd burned his oath to ash in a single act of mercy, at the survivor who'd turned her grief into a lantern—and felt the strange, terrifying warmth again, the one he kept trying to file away as tactical necessity and failing.

"Then we go to the mountain," he said.

The map stirred against his chest. Not warmer, exactly. More *attentive*. Like a dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep, and Kestrel tried not to notice that either.

Turnip raised his head suddenly, ears pricked, staring hard at the dark doorway of the smithy. A low growl built in his chest.

Brann's hand went to his sword. Sable was on her feet in one motion, a knife already in her fist.

Out in the square, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind off the ice: sleigh bells. Many sleigh bells, moving fast, coming north along the river road.

Coming toward Harrowmere.

Toward them.
