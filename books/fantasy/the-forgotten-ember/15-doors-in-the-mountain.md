## 15. Doors in the Mountain

The map stopped being paper two miles from the mountain.

Kestrel had noticed it first as a warmth against his chest, like a stone left too long by a fire, and then as a faint golden glow bleeding through his coat at dusk. By the time they crested the last ridge and saw Mount Cindral rising black against the stars—its peak crowned with old snow that never melted, its slopes veined with something that pulsed faintly, like light under skin—the map had become a thin sheet of hammered gold, warm as a living thing.

"It's excited," Sable said, peering over his shoulder. "That's unsettling. Maps shouldn't have feelings."

"Most things in Vellamarch shouldn't have feelings," Brann muttered. "Doesn't stop them."

They had been walking for six days along the frozen river, sleeping in shifts, eating hardtack and smoked fish Sable produced from her pack with the smugness of a woman who had planned for everything. Odile was still behind them somewhere—alive, Kestrel told himself every morning, alive because she was too stubborn to be anything else—and Hale was behind *her*, or perhaps no longer behind anyone at all. The road north had been strangely empty of pursuit. That worried him more than footsteps would have.

Now the mountain stood before them, and the map tugged in his hands like a hound on a lead.

"There," Odile would have said, if Odile were there. Instead it was Sable who pointed, to a cleft in the mountainside half-hidden by frozen scree—a doorway, ancient and deliberate, its edges carved with letters worn nearly smooth.

Kestrel read what remained. "*Give, and be known.*"

"Warm greeting," said Brann.

* * *

The passage beyond the door sloped gently downward, wide enough for four abreast, its walls worked stone so old the chisel-marks had softened into waves. And the walls were *warm*. Not metaphorically—warm the way a cheek is warm, the way a hand held in another hand is warm. Frost could not live here. Their breath stopped steaming the moment they passed the threshold, and Sable pulled off one glove and pressed her palm flat to the stone with an expression Kestrel couldn't name.

"It's like—" She stopped. Started again. "You know when you come in from the cold and someone's left the stove lit for you? Without telling you? Just knowing you'd need it?"

"I know that feeling," Kestrel said quietly. "I sold it once. A baker's daughter. The memory of her mother keeping the oven hot through the night so she'd wake to warm bread." He winced at himself. "Sorry. Bad habit, pricing things."

"No," Sable said. "Keep talking like that. It helps me remember why we're doing this."

Brann walked with his hand resting on his sword-hilt, though there was nothing yet to fight. "I don't like how quiet it is," he said. "Mountains should echo. This one's swallowing sound."

He was right. Their boots made no noise. Even their voices seemed to arrive slightly muffled, as though the mountain were listening politely and preferred they not disturb anyone.

The corridor descended in long slow spirals, and three times they passed alcoves set into the walls—and each alcove held a door. Small ones, human-sized, made of dark wood banded with iron gone soft with age. Each bore a single word carved above the lintel.

*Remember.*

*Forget.*

*Return.*

"Don't touch them," Kestrel said, before anyone could. "Doors that ask questions are doors that take answers."

"You say that like you've met one before."

"I've met things that *asked*," Kestrel said. "In my trade, the asking is never free."

Sable looked back at the little doors with longing she didn't bother hiding. He understood. *Forget* would be a mercy for her, some days. He'd have taken it himself, once, if forgetting hadn't been his whole livelihood—there was a bitter joke in that, and he kept it to himself.

They walked on. The gold map grew warmer still, until Kestrel wrapped it in cloth and carried it like a coal.

* * *

The antechamber opened without warning. One moment corridor; the next, a vast round hall whose ceiling vanished into darkness overhead, supported by pillars carved—Kestrel's stomach turned as he saw them clearly—as *people*. Hundreds of them, life-sized, standing shoulder to shoulder around the circumference of the room: men, women, children, their faces upturned, their hands open. Not suffering, exactly. Waiting.

At the chamber's heart stood a brazier the size of a wagon bed, filled not with fire but with a slow amber light that moved like honey poured upward. It cast no heat. But when Kestrel looked at it, he felt—unmistakably, horribly—*recognized*. As though the light knew his face, his trade, the exact weight of every memory he'd ever bottled.

Above the far side of the chamber, set into the rock, stood three doors. Tall ones this time, silver-wood and pale iron, each glowing faintly at its edges.

And a voice came from everywhere and nowhere, gentle as snowfall:

"*Three travelers, three tolls. The Vault keeps nothing freely given. Offer what you cherish, and pass.*"

Brann's sword cleared its sheath before the echo faded. "No," he said flatly. "Absolutely not. We turn around. We find another way in."

"There is no other way in," Kestrel said. He unslung his pack and sat down heavily on the cold floor, because his legs had decided the matter for him. "The map's been pulling us here since the river. You felt it. It doesn't want visitors, it wants *payment*."

"Then it can want," Brann said. "I've guarded gates before. Gates are doors with opinions. You wait them out."

"This isn't a gate, Tam." Sable crouched beside Kestrel, studying the amber light with narrowed eyes. "It's a memory-keeper. An old one. Look at the pillars—it's been collecting a long time." Her voice went strange. "It's the same craft. Older. Cleaner." She glanced at Kestrel. "Your trade has a grandmother, and we're standing in her parlor."

Kestrel laughed despite himself—a short, unhappy sound. "Family reunions," he said. "Always expensive."

The voice did not repeat itself. It didn't need to. The nearest of the three tall doors simply brightened, patient as sunrise, and Kestrel understood that they could stand in this room until they starved and the mountain would outlast them comfortably.

"All right," he said. "All right. Think. What does it mean, *what you cherish*?" He got to his feet, brushing off his knees, and forced his merchant's mind to work. "It's not torture. Look at the design—no hooks, no blades, no fear anywhere in this place. Everything here is *warm*. It's a toll, not a trap. Tolls are honest. They tell you the price before you pay."

"And the price is a piece of yourself," Brann said.

"The price is a piece of yourself," Kestrel agreed. "Which is the only price that's ever meant anything."

Silence. The amber light breathed.

Sable spoke first, and her voice was steady in the way Kestrel had learned meant she'd already done her grieving privately, days ago, where none of them could see. "My sister's memory is waiting for me at the end of this road. I'm not going to flinch at the tollbooth." She rolled her shoulders. "Besides. I've already lost everything I cherished twice over. Let it take a third thing. Third time's tradition."

"Sable—"

"Don't 'Sable' me, thief. Which door?"

But the doors, it seemed, chose for themselves. As she stepped forward, the middle door swung inward a hand's width, exhaling air that smelled impossibly of summer—cut grass, woodsmoke, rain on hot stone. Sable went white.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, it knows exactly what it's doing."

She looked back at them once. Kestrel started toward her and she shook her head, sharp. "If I cry about it, I want to do it alone, thanks all the same." A ghost of her usual grin. "Wish me a stupid memory. Something useless. My first kiss with a girl who had spinach in her teeth."

The door closed behind her.

They waited. It was the longest quarter hour of Kestrel's life, and he had once spent a night in a witch-finder cellar counting the seconds between boot-steps. Brann paced. Kestrel did not pace; he stood very still and listened to the amber light breathe, and hated it, and envied it.

Then the third door opened—Sable's door, the middle one—and she walked out. She looked exactly the same. She walked exactly the same. But halfway across the chamber she stopped, frowning, and said, "There was—I had a—"

Her hand rose vaguely toward her own face, sketching the shape of something missing.

"There was a song," she said slowly. "My mother used to sing it. I can't— I know I loved it. I know it mattered. I just can't hear it anymore." She blinked hard, twice, and then squared her shoulders with the ferocity of a woman daring the universe to comment. "Right. Worth it. Next."

Kestrel's throat hurt. "What did it take?"

"A song." She said it lightly, and then less lightly: "A song. Fine. There are other songs. There will be other songs." She wasn't looking at either of them when she said it.

* * *

Brann took longer to decide than Sable had, which surprised Kestrel not at all. Sable grieved fast and clean, like a storm passing. Brann was a man who turned decisions over for days, examining them for flaws, and Kestrel watched him circle the chamber twice, jaw working, before stopping in front of the left-hand door.

"My wedding day," Brann said, to the door, to the mountain, to no one. "Marta in blue. Her father crying into his hat and pretending it was the incense. The cake collapsing and everyone laughing." His voice caught and steadied. "I remember it every night. Every single night since she died, I lay there and walked through it, hour by hour, and it's the only thing that's kept me—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "It's the only thing that's mine that's still good."

The door glowed a fraction brighter. Patient. Kind, even. That was the worst part, Kestrel thought. It wasn't cruel. Cruelty you could hate.

"If I give it up," Brann said, turning to Kestrel, "will I know I lost something? Like Sable?"

"Probably. The shape of it, not the substance." Kestrel hesitated. "Tam. Listen to me. You don't have to be the one. I can—there might be a way to—"

"To what? Cheat a mountain older than the city?" Brann almost smiled. "You're a fine thief, Kestrel Fairweather, but you can't pickpocket a toll." He rolled his shoulders the way Sable had, and Kestrel realized they'd learned it from each other somewhere on the road, these two, without noticing. Found family borrowing each other's courage like a shared coat.

"Marta would understand," Brann said. "That's the thing. She'd be the first to say it. *Go on, Tam, do the right thing, and don't make a meal of it.*" He exhaled. "She talked exactly like that. You'd have liked her."

"I think I do," Kestrel said, "and I never met her."

Brann nodded once, gripped Kestrel's forearm hard enough to leave marks, and walked through the door without looking back.

This time the wait was worse. Sable sat with her knees drawn up, staring at the amber light, and Kestrel stood beside her, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing true enough to say. When the door finally opened and Brann came out, he was walking straight and his eyes were dry, but he stopped in the middle of the chamber and said, in a puzzled, wounded voice:

"I know I was happy. Once. Married. I know her name was Marta and I know I loved her." His big hands opened and closed at his sides. "I can feel that I loved her. I just can't—there's no picture. There's no *day*. It's like being told about someone else's life that happens to be mine." He looked at Kestrel, and his eyes were wet after all. "Was it worth it? Tell me it was worth it."

"It will be," Kestrel said. "When the king wakes. When the tithes end. When nobody else loses a day to a pyre or a tithe-collector." He swallowed. "It will be worth it, Tam. I swear it on my trade."

Brann nodded slowly, wiped his face with the heel of his hand like a man clearing frost from a window, and said, "Then get on with it. Your turn."

* * *

Kestrel stood before the right-hand door for a very long time.

The others waited by the brazier. They didn't hurry him. That was its own kindness, and he resented it, because it gave him nothing to push against.

Here was the problem, and he turned it over honestly, because he owed himself that much honesty: he had spent ten years curating himself. Every memory he owned, he owned *twice*—once in his head, once in glass—and he had chosen, bottle by bottle, which versions to keep close. His hoard was not greed. Well. It was greed, but it was also armor. Every stolen joy in his cellar was a wall between him and the boy he'd been before the trade: the workhouse boy, the hungry one, the one who'd learned that memories were currency because everything else about him was worthless.

And now the mountain asked him to hand one over. Freely. The one thing he had never done—never *sold*, never traded, never given. Taken, always. Taken and hoarded.

The door glowed. The amber light breathed. Somewhere in the walls, the carved people waited with their open hands.

"What would it take?" he asked aloud. "From me. What would it take?"

The voice answered, gentle as ever: "*Only what you cherish.*"

"That's not an answer, that's a riddle."

"*All tolls are riddles,*" said the mountain, with what Kestrel could have sworn was amusement. "*You of all travelers should know. You have charged this toll your whole life. You simply never let anyone else pay it.*"

He stared at the door. Behind him, Sable said softly, "Kestrel. Whatever it's asking—you don't have to perform bravery. We've seen enough of that."

"He's stalling," Brann said. "He does it before every job. Paces, complains, invents three reasons it won't work, then does it perfectly."

"I do not *pace*."

"You absolutely pace."

And Kestrel laughed—really laughed, the first time since Odile's capture—and the laugh broke something loose in him, and he understood, all at once, what the mountain wanted. Not a treasure. Not his best memory, not his worst. It wanted the thing he'd never once surrendered: a gift. Given, not taken. That was the toll. That was the whole of it. *Give, and be known.*

His hand found the inner pocket of his coat, where he kept the one bottle he had never sold. Not royal grief, not a king's screaming—he'd have given that gladly. This was smaller. This was his mother's voice, singing, from before the workhouse, from the one warm year he could barely prove had happened. He'd carried it eleven years. He'd priced it once, drunk, at more than the city was worth, and never tried to sell it, because some things aren't currency, they're *keels*—the one part of the boat that stays underwater and keeps you upright.

He drew it out. The glass was warm from his body.

"If I give you this," he said to the door, "I'll forget the song. Like Sable. I'll know I loved it and never hear it again."

"*Yes.*"

"And the king's memories—when we return them—this won't interfere? You won't keep it as leverage?"

"*The Vault keeps nothing freely given,*" the voice said. "*It keeps it safe. There is a difference, thief, and you are about to learn it.*"

Kestrel looked back at his friends. Sable watching him with fierce, wet-eyed respect. Brann with his hand over his heart, which for Tam Brann was a speech.

"Well," Kestrel said, and his voice cracked, and he let it. "Somebody once told me the asking is never free. Turns out sometimes it is."

He pressed the bottle to the door.

The silver-wood drank it—there was no other word, the glass dimming, emptying, the little golden swirl of memory flowing out through his palm and into the grain—and the door swung open onto darkness and the smell of deep stone and, faintly, impossibly, of woodsmoke and bread.

Kestrel stood there a moment, testing himself the way you test a sore tooth. He knew he had carried a song. He knew it had mattered. He reached for it and found the shape of the hole where it had been, smooth-edged, already healing over.

"Huh," he said. "So that's what I do to people."

"Now you know," Sable said. "Come on. The rest of us have been carrying it for days."

They went through together, four abreast, down into the dark beneath Mount Cindral—and the last thing Kestrel heard, echoing up from the deep places ahead, was a sound none of them expected and all of them would remember:

Someone, far below, was weeping. And beneath the weeping, steady as a heartbeat, something vast was whispering a name.

Not Kestrel's name.

*Aldric's.*
