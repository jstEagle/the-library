## 16. The Hollow Court

The doors of the Ember Vault were not doors at all. They were a memory.

Kestrel understood this the way he understood most things about his trade—late, and with a sick lurch in his stomach. The great archway before them was made of compressed recollection, centuries of it pressed into stone by hands that had known what they were doing: the first keepers, the ones who had built rather than stolen. He could taste it from ten paces away. Woodsmoke and harvest bread and someone's grandmother singing off-key in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but here.

"It's humming," Sable whispered. She had her scarred hand flat against the arch, and her fingers were trembling—not with fear, Kestrel thought, but with recognition. "It's like standing next to a hearth. A real one."

"Don't touch anything else," Brann said, in the tone of a man who had spent three weeks on a frozen river learning exactly how much trouble followed when people touched things. His wedding ring was gone now—surrendered to the antechamber trials along with the day he'd wed Marta, and he kept flexing his bare finger as if the absence were a wound that hadn't decided whether to bleed. "Please. I'm begging you both. We've come this far without anyone being eaten."

"We haven't been *eaten*," Sable said.

"The trials ate my wedding day. I'm counting that."

Kestrel barely heard them. He was looking past the arch, into the vault's antechamber, where the map in his coat had gone from warm to hot to something approaching urgent—a heartbeat against his ribs that wasn't his own. Three weeks it had led them. Up the frozen Vell, through Hale's pursuit, through the cave where he'd confessed things he'd never said aloud, through the village of empty names where Sable had wept against a warning wall written in her dead sister's hand. And now, beneath Mount Cindral, through passages that smelled of old fire, it was pulling him forward like a hook set in his chest.

"Odile's down there," he said quietly.

No one answered, because there was nothing to say. Odile Vane had been in witch-finder chains for eleven days. If she was alive—and Kestrel had chosen, deliberately, stubbornly, to believe she was—then she was wherever the Hollow King wanted her to be. And the Hollow King, if the map's growing heat meant anything, was here.

He stepped through the arch.

* * *

The Hollow Court was beautiful, and that was the worst thing about it.

It should have been a cavern. It should have been dark and dripping and wrong, some lair fit for the parasite Kestrel had imagined all these weeks—something crawling, something hungry-looking. Instead they walked out of the passage into a throne room that might have been lifted whole from the palace in Vellamarch: vaulted ceilings painted with constellations, banners of deep blue and silver, braziers burning with light so warm and gold that Kestrel's eyes stung. After three weeks of ice and grey sky, the warmth hit him like an embrace.

That was the trap, of course. He knew it even as his body betrayed him, shoulders dropping, breath easing. This thing fed on warmth. Of course its home looked like comfort. Of course it had made itself a court instead of a den.

And on the dais, beneath a crown of black iron, sat two kings.

One was Aldric—the true king, though Kestrel had never seen him except on coins and proclamations. He sat slumped in a chair beside the throne, dressed in plain grey, his hands loose in his lap. He was perhaps fifty, with the remains of a handsome face, and he was looking at nothing. Not resting. Not sleeping. *Hollowed*, the way a house is hollowed after everything inside has been carried out and sold. Kestrel had seen that look before, on the faces of tithe-farmers who'd given too much. On the villagers who couldn't remember their names. But never on a face this important, this loved once, and the sight of it made something twist in him that felt dangerously like tenderness.

The other king stood.

He wore Aldric's face—but younger, brighter, polished like a coin rubbed smooth by ten thousand thumbs. He wore it the way a thief wears a stolen coat: perfectly, and with just enough pride in the wearing to give himself away. When he smiled, the whole court seemed to smile with him, the braziers flaring, the painted stars brightening.

"You came," the Hollow King said, and his voice was warm too, warm as mulled wine, warm as a lie told kindly. "Three weeks on my river, through my winter, past my finders. I confess I wondered if you'd turn back. Most thieves do, when the job stops being small."

"I'm not most thieves." Kestrel's mouth was dry, but his voice held. Twenty years of night markets had taught him that much: never let them hear the fear. "I've come for two things. Him." He nodded at the hollowed man in the chair. "And her. Odile Vane. You took her in Vellamarch."

"I did." The Hollow King descended the dais steps slowly, unhurried, a host greeting guests. Brann's hand went to his sword; Sable's marked palm began to glow faintly, the last ember of her burned-out magic answering the air's warmth. The king ignored them both. His attention—his terrible, flattering, total attention—settled on Kestrel alone. "And you've brought me such lovely things. The map. The keeper's training. That remarkable little hoard of yours—I can smell it from here, thief. Bottled childhoods. Bottled first loves. You've been carrying a feast in your coat pockets for twenty years, and you've never once sat down to eat it yourself. Do you know how rare that is? Discipline like that?"

"It's called having a conscience."

"It's called *hunger deferred*." The king stopped an arm's length away. Up close, the borrowed face had flaws done beautifully—laugh lines, a scar on the brow, every detail of a beloved king rendered in perfect miniature. Only the eyes were wrong. They were the eyes of a thing looking out through a window. "My dear Kestrel Fairweather. Yes, I know the name. Your courier knew it too, at the end. Did you ever wonder why the map chose you? Out of all the thieves in Vellamarch? It wasn't accident. Grief knows grief. It always has."

Behind him, Kestrel heard Sable shift. "Kes," she said softly—a warning, or maybe just a reminder that he had people now, which was somehow more dangerous than any warning.

"I'm listening," Kestrel said to the king.

"Good. Then let me make you an offer, and let me make it honestly, because I find honesty is so much more persuasive than threats. Everyone expects monsters to threaten." The Hollow King spread his hands, gracious, reasonable. "Odile Vane is alive. She is unharmed. She sits in a cell not thirty paces from where you stand, complaining bitterly about the tea, which I'm told is genuinely bad. Walk out of here with her tonight. Both of you. My finders will not follow. My winter will not touch you. In exchange—" and here the warm voice gentled, became almost intimate, almost kind, "—you stay. You serve. You become my thief, as the last one became, as the one before him. There are memories in this world still worth harvesting, Kestrel. Warm places. Whole villages that haven't yet learned to lock their doors. You would never want again. Never run again. Never sell a stranger's first kiss for bread money in a freezing market while witch-finders burn the people you might have been."

The words slid into Kestrel like a knife finding the gap between ribs.

Because that was the horror of it—the offer was *good*. That was the thing nobody warned you about devils. They didn't tempt you with things you despised. They tempted you with rest. With safety. With an end to the running that had been the whole shape of his life since he was nine years old and first learned that other people's joy could be bottled and sold. He thought of Odile's cell, thirty paces away, complaining about tea—alive, alive, *alive*—and his heart did something shameful and hopeful in his chest.

"He's good," Sable murmured behind him. "Kes. He's really good. Don't listen with your stomach."

But the Hollow King heard everything. Of course he did. "Your pyre-survivor speaks wisely," he said pleasantly, "though she should be careful. I remember her sister. Lovely girl. Her last memory is in my archive, filed under autumn, and I could give it back or lose it forever depending entirely on how this conversation goes—"

"You leave her out of it," Sable snarled, and the ember-light in her palm flared white.

"*Everyone* is in it," the king said, without malice, which was worse. "That is what a kingdom means. Now." He turned back to Kestrel, and lowered his voice, and the whole vast court leaned in to listen. "Think, thief. Truly think. You cannot beat me. You know it. The map knew it—that's why it chose a thief and not a hero. Heroes die at doors like mine. Thieves get *in*. So take the door I'm offering. Take the woman who trained you and walk into the warm. Let the king sleep. Let the winter do what winters do. Ten years I have worn this face, and the city has never been more orderly, more obedient, more *calm*. Is calm so terrible a price?"

And Kestrel—who had survived by never arguing with a mark, by smiling and nodding and picking the lock while they talked—looked past the Hollow King's shoulder at the true king in his plain grey clothes, sitting in a chair with empty hands.

Aldric's eyes moved.

Just slightly. Just enough. From the floor to Kestrel's face. And in what remained behind those hollowed eyes—some cellar of the man, some root cellar where a single jar had been overlooked—there was a look Kestrel recognized from a thousand night markets. The look of someone watching a thief work and silently begging him: *take it. Take it back.*

Something in Kestrel went very quiet and very clear.

"No," he said.

The word fell into the court like a stone into a well.

"No?" The Hollow King's smile stayed fixed, but the braziers guttered, just once. "Thief. I am offering you everything you have spent your life stealing secondhand. Warmth. Safety. Her life. And you say *no*?"

"Yeah." Kestrel found, to his surprise, that he was almost laughing. Not because it was funny—because it was easy. Because for the first time in twenty years, the right answer and the honest answer were the same word. "See, you made one mistake. You offered me Odile's freedom like it was yours to give. Like she's a purse you're holding hostage." He glanced back at Sable, at Brann, and felt the strange fierce weight of them at his back—people who owed him nothing, following him into a mountain. "But here's what I've learned lately, having friends for the first time at thirty-four: you don't get to price other people's lives. Not mine, not hers, not his." He pointed at the hollowed king. "You weren't born, were you? You *grew*. Out of his grief, out of that plague winter he couldn't stop. You're not a king. You're a wound that learned to wear a crown. And wounds don't make offers. Wounds just want company."

The court went cold.

Not metaphorically—the braziers dimmed, the gold light curdling toward iron-grey, the painted stars flickering like candles in a draft. The Hollow King's borrowed face did not change, but everything around it did, the beauty peeling back one layer to show the architecture underneath: the throne room was a ribcage, the banners were veins, the warm air was breath drawn in and held.

"You will starve me, then," the king said, and the warmth was gone from his voice, leaving only the hunger it had been wrapped around. "You'll pour his little memories back and watch me shrink. Do you know what happens to a city when I starve, thief? Grief doesn't vanish. It *spills*. Every sorrow he swallowed for ten years comes up at once, and Vellamarch drowns in it. You won't save them. You'll drown them faster, and kinder, and call it mercy."

"Maybe," Kestrel said. His heart was hammering, but his hands were steady, and somewhere in his coat the map had gone cool and satisfied, like a cat settling. "Or maybe grief shared is just grief, and it passes, the way winters pass. You wouldn't know. You've never let anything end. That's your whole problem—you're a winter that's terrified of spring."

For one long moment, the Hollow King simply looked at him.

Then, terribly, the warmth came back—the smile, the gold, the gracious tilt of the head—as if a lamp had been relit. "So be it," he said lightly. "We do this the hard way. I did hope you'd choose sense, but grief always did prefer its own company." He turned, robes whispering, and gestured, and far across the court a door of black iron groaned open. Cold air poured through it, and torchlight, and the sound—faint, unmistakable—of a woman's voice swearing fluently at someone about tea.

Odile. Alive. Thirty paces and a small army away.

"Midwinter is in six days," the Hollow King said over his shoulder, pleasant as a shopkeeper naming a price. "On that night I finish the binding, and the city's warmth becomes mine entire, and none of this will matter. Until then, thief—run. Hide. Gather your little family and your little plan. I want you to try. Hope makes the harvest sweeter."

The lights went out all at once, every brazier, every star—

—and in the sudden dark, Sable's ember-palm blazed up like a struck match, and Brann's sword cleared its scabbard with a ring that sounded almost like laughter, and Kestrel Fairweather ran toward the sound of Odile Vane's voice, because the easiest path had just closed behind him, and the only one left was the one worth walking.
