## 17. What the King Grieved

The last of the king's memories hung in the vault's heart like a lantern no one had lit in ten years.

Kestrel stood before it with his hands out, palms up, the way he did before every theft—though this was not a theft, and he had to keep reminding himself of that. This was a returning. The difference mattered. Odile had drilled it into him years ago, back when he was all knuckles and hunger and she still bothered to teach him anything: *A thief takes what isn't his. A keeper holds what someone else cannot carry yet.* He'd spent a decade being the first thing. Tonight, if he could manage it, he would be the second.

"You don't have to go in," Sable said behind him. Her voice was rough as banked coals. She'd taken up a post at his shoulder without asking, arms crossed, one hand never far from the iron poker she carried instead of a sword. "The memories will come to you if you wait."

"They'll come to me wrong if I wait." Kestrel flexed his fingers. "Memory's like smoke. You can wave it toward yourself or you can walk into it, but you can't make it sit still on command. And this—" he looked up at the pale gold shimmer suspended above the vault floor, "—this has been sitting still for ten years. It'll be stubborn."

"Like its owner," Brann murmured.

King Aldric lay on a slab of black stone twenty paces away, breathing so slowly that Kestrel had twice leaned close to check. His face was the face from every coin in Vellamarch—the strong jaw, the patient brow—but worn, somehow, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many hands. The Hollow King wore that same face upstairs, in the palace, radiant and terrible, and it fit him perfectly because it had been *made* for him. Cut from him. What remained down here in the dark was the offcut. The part the parasite hadn't wanted.

Or hadn't known how to want.

"You're stalling," Odile said. She sat against the far wall with her wrists still raw from the manacles, refusing all offers of rest, watching Kestrel with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "You've adjusted your cuffs four times. That's your tell. It was your tell when you were fourteen and it's your tell now."

"My cuffs were crooked."

"They're crooked *now*, and you've stopped fixing them, which means you want an excuse to keep your hands busy." She tilted her head. "What are you afraid of, Kestrel? You've walked through a hundred minds."

"Not a king's." He said it lightly, and hated how light it came out. "Not a grief this old. Small memories, I can slip into and out of like rooms. But this is a whole house, and the door's been locked from the inside for a decade, and whatever's been living in there—" He stopped. Started again. "When I touched the map, I tasted royal grief once already. One drop of it, and it took me three days to stop hearing that screaming. This is the well it came from."

Sable put her hand briefly on his shoulder. It was the first time she'd touched him without it being practical—steadying him on ice, pulling him behind cover—and the warmth of it went through him like a struck match.

"Then don't go alone," she said. "I'll hold the thread."

"The thread?"

"Odile taught us at the safehouse. When someone dives deep, someone else keeps a line to them. A voice, a touch, something real to climb back by." Her mouth twitched. "You weren't there that night. You were off stealing somebody's first kiss or whatever it is you do."

"It was a memory of rain," Kestrel said with dignity. "Very rare, pre-plague rain. Extremely valuable."

"He held the thread for six people that night," Brann said quietly. "One of them was me."

Kestrel looked at the corporal. Brann rarely spoke of the safehouse nights; he wasn't supposed to be at the safehouse at all, being law, but law had been looking increasingly like a coat that didn't fit him, and lately he'd stopped pretending otherwise.

"All right," Kestrel said. "Thread. Voice. Something real to climb back by." He managed a smile. "If I start screaming in a white room again, say my name."

"Which name?" Sable asked, deadpan. "You've got about forty bottled up somewhere."

"That's the spirit."

He turned back to the memory. Up close it was worse—not a lantern but a wound dressed in gold, and beneath the shimmer he could feel the cold coming off it, the particular cold he'd been smelling on the wind since midwinter began its long approach. The cold of the Hollow King. Of course it was here too. Everything the parasite was, had been poured out of this man first.

*It is a wound,* Odile had said, when they'd pieced together what the vault held. *Wounds don't have plans, Kestrel. They only have appetites.*

He reached out and stepped in.

* * *

The white room again—but not screaming, not yet. First, warmth.

Kestrel found himself standing in a palace chamber he half-recognized from coins and proclamations, except it was alive in a way no portrait ever was. Firelight. Laughter. A woman with ink-stained fingers reading aloud from a physician's ledger while a young king sat cross-legged on the floor beside her chair like a boy, arguing cheerfully about quarantine lines.

"—and I'm telling you, Aldric, the southern gate cordon is a joke. People bribe their way through with bread. *Bread.*"

"People are hungry, Liss. Hungry people don't respect cordons."

"Hungry people also die of lung-rot, and then they make everyone else hungry *and* grieving, which is worse." She flicked the ledger at his head. He caught it, grinning, and Kestrel felt the grin from the inside—that was the strange mercy and cruelty of walking a memory, that you wore the rememberer's skin—and underneath the grin was a love so plain and unguarded it made Kestrel's chest ache. Not courtly love. Not performance. The easy, scuffed love of two people who had survived things together and expected to survive more.

Queen Lissandre. Dead these ten years. Kestrel knew the official story: plague took her in the Grey Winter, along with a tenth of the city. The king had locked himself away to grieve, and when he emerged he was changed—colder, stranger, issuing the decrees that had curdled into the tithes.

The official story was true the way a map is true. It showed you where everything was. It showed you nothing of what it felt like to stand there.

The memory moved the way grief moves—by skipping. One moment firelight and argument; the next, a hospital ward that smelled of vinegar and ash, the young king holding a stranger's hand because there was no family left to hold it, and the queen's voice somewhere behind him calling names off a list, each name a small bell tolling. The next, snow falling in the throne room through a broken roof no one could spare men to mend, and Aldric standing in it, letting it land on his shoulders, because the cold outside was honest in a way the warm rooms full of dying were not.

And always, everywhere, the counting. *How many today. How many we couldn't save. How many more tomorrow.* Kestrel had stolen grief before—he'd bottled a widow's grief once, sold it to a collector who liked the taste of other people's sorrow, and he had never forgiven himself for it—but this grief was different in scale and kind. It wasn't one loss. It was a thousand losses arriving daily, and a king's duty meant he could not look away from a single one. Every death in Vellamarch passed across his desk. Every name. He read them. All of them. Every night.

"No one asked you to do that," Kestrel whispered into the memory, uselessly. "No one sane would do that."

But the man in the memory would have said, in that tired, wry voice: *Someone has to count them properly. If no one counts them, they become numbers. They're not numbers.*

* * *

The winter turned. The memory darkened the way a room darkens when the candles burn down one by one and no one replaces them because everyone who would replace them is dead.

Kestrel watched the cordon fail. Watched the southern districts drown. Watched Aldric beg his council for grain reserves they didn't have, for physicians who'd fled, for help from neighboring realms that sent condolences and closed their borders. Watched him sign orders he hated—orders sealing streets, orders that would trap healthy families with sick ones—and watch those orders fail anyway, because fear does not obey seals.

Watched him kneel in the ash-choked cathedral and pray to gods who had gone quiet, and notice, with the peculiar clarity of exhaustion, that his prayers were changing shape. That he was no longer asking for the winter to end. He was asking for it to take him instead of the city. *Take me. I'm one. They're thousands. Take me.*

And then—the night it happened. Kestrel felt it approach the way you feel a storm front, a pressure behind the eyes.

The queen's ward. Late. Lamplight low. Lissandre was already fevered and hiding it, had been hiding it for two days, working anyway, because someone had to triage and she trusted no one else's judgment. Aldric found her collapsed over the ledger, names half-written in a hand going unsteady, and the sound he made—

Kestrel had heard that sound before. On the night the map flooded him. The crowned man screaming in a white room. Here it was whole, in context, and context made it infinitely worse, because the scream wasn't rage or even horror. It was the sound of a man whose entire strategy for surviving the world had just died in his arms. *Count the names. Save who you can. Bear the rest.* She had been the one who taught him to bear it. She had been the arithmetic of his hope.

She died at dawn, and the plague broke a week later, which was the cruelest joke the winter could have told. It ended. It always ends. It ended without needing his surrender, without taking the bargain he'd screamed into the dark, and something in Aldric Fairweather—no, Aldric whatever-his-name-was, kings had other names—something in him could not accept that arithmetic. Thousands dead. Bargain refused. Winter simply *over*, like weather, indifferent, and him still standing in the ruins with all that counted grief and nowhere to put it.

So he put it somewhere.

Kestrel watched, helpless, as the exhausted king sat alone in the vault—this vault, this black stone—and did the one thing a memory-keeper would have killed him to prevent. He reached into his own chest, into the place where ten thousand counted names lived, and he pulled. Not gently. Grief does not pull gently. He tore the whole weight of it out of himself—the deaths, the failures, the begging, the bargaining, the guilt that he had lived—and he bound it, clumsily, magnificently, in the only craft he half-knew from his grandmother's stories, and set it on the black stone.

And it opened its eyes.

* * *

"Oh," Kestrel said aloud, in the memory, in the vault, in both places at once. "Oh, you *idiot*. You brave, stupid, grieving idiot."

Because he understood now. He had spent weeks thinking of the Hollow King as a thief, a parasite, a usurper wearing a beloved face—and it was all true, but it was true the way the map was true. Underneath, simpler and far more terrible: the Hollow King was what Aldric had made of his own sorrow when he couldn't carry it anymore. His grief, given a body. His guilt, given a crown. And a grief that wakes up alone in the dark with a king's memories of power and none of a king's love—what does such a thing want?

It wants the ache to stop. That's all any wound wants. And the only way it knows to stop the ache is to stop the feeling—to make the whole world numb, cold, quiet, counted. The tithes weren't cruelty, not originally. They were anesthesia. The Hollow King had been trying to deaden the entire city the way its father-self had tried to deaden himself, one harvested memory at a time, until nobody in Vellamarch remembered enough to hurt.

And it had left Aldric alive—hollowed, anchored, but alive—because some splinter of its origin couldn't destroy him. You cannot kill the man whose grief you are. You can only wear his face and wait for him to forget himself entirely.

Kestrel surfaced gasping, Sable's grip on his wrist real and warm, her voice saying his name—his actual name, the one only three living people knew—like a rope thrown into dark water.

"I'm here," he croaked. "I'm here. Gods." He sat on the cold floor because standing seemed ambitious. "It's not a monster. Or—it is, but—" He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "Odile. It's his grief. The whole thing, the tithes, the winter, the face—it's what he cut out of himself when she died. It doesn't hate us. It's trying to *numb* us. It's the worst kind of mourning there is. It's mourning that learned to feed."

Silence. Then Odile, very quietly: "That's why it kept him alive."

"That's why it kept him alive."

Brann had gone to kneel by the sleeping king, and his big guard's hands hovered over Aldric's chest, not touching, the way you hover over something broken and precious. "Ten years," he said. "Ten years we called him changed. Cold. We said the crown sits heavy. We made excuses for him because we loved him." His jaw worked. "We were apologizing for a ghost."

"We were apologizing for a wound," Kestrel corrected softly. He looked at the pale gold remnant of memory still hanging above them—the last shreds Aldric had kept, the ones too vital to tear out: the queen's laughter, the argument about cordons, the plain scuffed love. "And wounds can be closed. Not destroyed—closed. Fed what it's starving for until it has no reason to hunger." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Do you know what it's starving for? Do you know what the Hollow King actually wants, underneath everything?"

"Tell us," Sable said.

"It wants someone to tell it that the deaths counted. That the grief was *right*. It's been waiting ten years for its own father to wake up and mourn properly, and instead everyone fights it, fears it, feeds it more numbness." Kestrel got to his feet, and something had shifted in him—not hope exactly, hope was too cheap a word—but direction, fierce and clear. "We don't kill it. We finish the funeral. We give Aldric back his memories, all of them, and we let him grieve—at last, out loud, the way he should have been allowed to ten years ago—and when the grief is finally *felt*, the thing wearing his face will have nothing left to be."

"And if it doesn't want to be finished?" Brann asked. "Grief can be stubborn."

"Grief can be *enormous*," Kestrel agreed. "Which is why we'll need every scrap of warmth in this vault, every memory we recovered, and probably several miracles." He was already moving toward the alcove where they'd cached the recovered tithe-memories, hundreds of them, glass vials glinting like a hoard of frozen firelight. "We do it tonight. Midwinter's in two days, and when the binding completes—"

The vault shuddered.

Dust sifted from the ceiling in fine grey threads. The black slab hummed under Brann's palm. Far above them, muffled through a mountain's worth of stone, came a sound like a vast bell being struck by something that was not a clapper—and along the walls of the antechamber, the old wards flared from gold to a sickly white, the exact white of the room where a queen had died.

Sable caught Kestrel's arm. "What was that?"

Kestrel stared up at the flaring wards, and the taste of royal grief turned bitter on his tongue, because he recognized the rhythm of that distant tolling. He'd heard it once before, in a memory, in a hospital ward: the triage bell. The call that meant *too many, too fast, come now.*

"It's starting early," he said. "The binding. It's drawing the city's warmth *now*—tonight—and if we're not pouring these memories back into Aldric before it finishes—"

The far door of the antechamber, the one they'd sealed behind them, boomed once, hugely, like a fist the size of a cathedral.

Then a second boom. Closer. And through the seam of the great doors, thin as a knife, crept a thread of cold that smelled of snow and counted names.
