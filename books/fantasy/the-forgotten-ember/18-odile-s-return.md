## 18. Odile's Return

The cell had no name, which was the first thing wrong with it. Everything in Vellamarch had a name—streets, bells, even the rats in the granary had names, according to the children who fed them. But this room beneath the witch-finders' citadel was called only *holding*, as if naming it would give it ideas.

Odile Vane had been in it for six days, and she had decided that if she was going to be interrogated in an unnamed room, she would at least furnish it with her contempt.

She sat on the cot with her hands folded and her spine straight, the posture of a memory-keeper at rest, and she watched Sergeant Hale read her file for the fourth time. He did it by lamplight, his lips moving slightly on the hard words, and there was something almost endearing about a man who hunted witches and still stumbled over *mnemonic extraction*.

"You were trained at the Athenaeum," Hale said.

"I was *the* Athenaeum," Odile said. "For eleven years. Then I was nothing, which is a promotion, in this city."

"Your file says you were dismissed for unlicensed practice."

"My file says I was dismissed for telling the truth about where the tithes went." She smiled at him, thin and dry as winter paper. "Files are memories too, Sergeant. Written by whoever holds the pen."

Hale closed the folder. He had been doing that more often lately—closing things. The folder, the lamp shutter when he thought she wasn't looking, his mouth around questions he'd planned to ask. Six days of planted memories will do that to a man. She had given him a Kestrel Fairweather who fled south through the Tanner's Gate with a limp he didn't have, a route through three towns that didn't exist, and a confederate named after a fishmonger Odile had once owed money to. It was petty. It was also holding, for now.

But it was the nights that worried her.

On the third night, the Hollow King had come to her cell. Not a guard, not a key turning—simply a presence, like cold walking in wearing a man's shape. He had sat down across from her without being invited, which told her everything about how power worked in Vellamarch now, and he had looked at her the way a sommelier looks at a bottle.

"Keeper," he had said. His voice was beautiful. That was the horror of it; it was a voice built to be believed. "You have such tidy shelves up here. May I?"

And he had *tasted* her—not crudely, not the way witch-finders ripped and rummaged, but delicately, reverently, the way grief reads old letters. He had found the map's echo in her, the ghost of its route pressed into her mind from one shared glance at Kestrel's copy, and he had smiled with the true king's mouth.

"North," he'd murmured. "They go north. To the door my sorrow built."

Then he'd left, and Odile had spent two days doing arithmetic in the dark. If the Hollow King knew they were bound for the Vault—and he did, he absolutely did—then why hadn't the roads been sealed? Why was she still alive? Why was *Kestrel* still alive?

Because, she had concluded somewhere around the fifth night, staring at the ceiling, the thing wanted them to come. A parasite does not bar the door to the host's heart. It waits there. It wants to be found.

Which meant the only move left was the one the enemy wasn't expecting: arriving anyway, but *ready*.

She was still turning this over on the sixth night when the lock clicked—a real click, a key, human and fumbling—and Sergeant Hale stepped into her cell carrying a guard's cloak, a lantern turned down to a coal, and an expression like a man about to be sick.

"Up," he said. "Quickly."

Odile didn't move. "Sergeant. This is either a very elaborate execution or a very stupid mercy, and I've survived both before. Which is it?"

"It's neither." He thrust the cloak at her. "It's arithmetic. I burned eleven people in my first year because their files said they were guilty. I believed files." His jaw worked. "I checked your story. The Tanner's Gate. The fishmonger. None of it exists, Keeper, and I'm good enough at my work to know what that means—you gave me a lie so clean it could only come from someone protecting something worth protecting. So." He looked away, at the wall, at the unnamed room. "The warrant says you rot here until the King's justice takes you. And I find I no longer know whose justice that is."

"The Hollow King's," Odile said quietly. "It has always been the Hollow King's. You've been feeding it, Sergeant. Ten years of tithes. Ten years of pyres. All of it down the mountain's throat."

"I know." His voice cracked on the second word, and he hated it, and she saw him hate it. "There's a postern gate past the kennels. The watch changes in four minutes. Can you walk?"

"Sergeant Hale." She rose, took the cloak, and for a moment her voice lost all its armor. "Why?"

He was quiet long enough that the lantern flame leaned. "Because last week I found my mother's memory of my father in a tithe ledger. Filed. Catalogued. She sold it 'voluntarily' during the famine year because the collectors said bread would follow." He swallowed. "She doesn't remember loving him anymore. She sets his place at table and asks who it's for. And I signed the receipts."

Odile put her hand briefly on his arm—the first kind touch either of them had exchanged—and then became all business, because sentiment was a luxury and the watch changed in four minutes.

"Postern gate," she said. "Kennels. Go."

* * *

The city at night was a held breath.

Odile moved through it the way she'd once taught students to move through a stranger's mind: gently, quickly, leaving nothing disturbed. Snow was falling again—that soft, greedy snow that never accumulated anymore but never stopped falling either, as though the sky itself had forgotten how to do anything else. The lamps along Gilders' Row burned low and blue. Cold had a flavor now; everyone in Vellamarch tasted it, a faint mineral tang on the tongue, the taste of warmth being drawn out of things.

She cut north through the tannery district, following the frozen river road, and twice she felt it—the Hollow King's attention pass over the city like a lighthouse beam, slow and searching. Each time she pressed herself into a doorway and did what keepers are trained to do: she made herself small inside her own skull, folded her thoughts into a box marked *nothing to see*, and waited.

The second time, the attention lingered. And in the silence of that lingering, she heard it—not words, but weather. A vast, patient sadness, rolling over the rooftops. *Come*, the sadness said, without saying anything at all. *Come home. Everyone comes home eventually.*

"Not tonight," Odile whispered, and ran.

She found the cave by the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of an argument.

"—all I'm saying is that if we're going to die, we should die having eaten something other than river fish," Sable was saying, in the tone of a woman who had strong opinions about dying.

"We're not going to die," Brann said, in the tone of a man who had recently learned that saying things confidently did not make them true.

"Oh, good, the corporal's optimistic now. That's new. What happened to 'we're all going to hang'?"

"He upgraded me," Kestrel said from deeper in the cave. "I'm a growing boy."

Odile stood in the entrance, snow in her hair, watching the four shadows around the little fire, and felt something crack open in her chest that she had spent thirty years welding shut.

Kestrel saw her first. He scrambled up so fast he knocked over the water skin, and for a moment he just stared, as if she might be a memory he'd bottled and drunk by mistake.

"You're supposed to be in a cell," he said.

"You're supposed to be competent," she said. "We're both disappointed."

And then, because the fire was warm and the night was cold and she was tired of pretending otherwise, she let Sable pull her down onto a blanket, let Brann press a tin cup of terrible tea into her hands, and let herself sit among them—these three damaged, stubborn, impossible people—as though she belonged there.

As though she had always belonged there.

* * *

They talked until the candles guttered.

Brann drew the Vault's approach in the dirt with a stick: the antechambers, the trials, the doors that took payment in memory. Sable laid out what remained of her network—eleven safehouses, four smugglers, one extremely unreliable ferryman—"but the river's frozen, so forget Marlow, he's useless till spring." Kestrel spread the map on a flat stone, and it glowed faintly, warmly, like a sleeping animal.

And Odile, keeper of broken things, listened to all of it, and then told them the truth she'd carried out of the cell.

"It knows you're coming," she said. "It tasted the route out of my head the second night. It wants you there. A parasite doesn't flee the heart, Kestrel—it waits in it. Whatever plan assumes surprise is dead. We need a plan that assumes we're *expected*."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone and snow, the city slept its thinning sleep.

"Then we don't sneak in," Kestrel said slowly. He was staring at the map, and his fingers had gone still in the way Odile recognized from years ago—the stillness of a thief seeing the shape of a lock. "We walk in the front. We let it think it's won. Because here's what it doesn't know." He looked up, and his eyes were bright and terrified and certain. "It doesn't know what we're carrying."

"The king's memories," Brann said. "From the archive."

"Eleven years of Aldric," Kestrel said. "His childhood. His coronation. Every grief and every joy the tithes stripped out of him—the whole man, in bottles, in my pack. The Hollow King grew out of the hole those memories left. It's been living in the empty space, wearing his face, eating the city to fill it." He tapped the map, right at the heart of the mountain. "So we pour him back. We make the man whole, and the parasite starves. There'll be nothing left of the hollow for it to live in."

"It won't allow that," Sable said flatly.

"No," Kestrel agreed. "It won't. Which is why three of us hold the doors while the fourth pours."

"And which of us," Odile said, already knowing, already dreading it, "is pouring?"

Kestrel smiled at her, and it was the smile of a boy who had once pickpocketed her spectacles and returned them polished, and she understood with sick certainty that he had known the answer since the archive.

"Me," he said. "I'm the only one who can carry a memory home. It's the whole trade, Odile. Delivery."

"That's not delivery, that's a funeral."

"Then it's the best funeral I've ever planned. There'll be cake."

"There will *not* be cake—"

"There'll be cake," Sable said, "if we win. That's the deal. Nobody dies before cake."

Brann laughed—a short, startled bark, as if the sound had escaped custody—and then sobered, and looked around the fire at each of them in turn. "I took an oath to the crown," he said. "Ten years I thought the crown was that thing on the throne. It isn't. The crown is a man named Aldric, asleep under a mountain, and my oath is to *him*. So I'm holding a door. I'm holding it until my arms come off."

"I'm holding a door," Sable said, "because the thing on that throne took my sister and my fire, and I intend to introduce them to each other personally."

Odile looked at the fire for a long moment. Then she said, "I'm holding a door because I spent thirty years believing that caring about people was a liability, and I would like to be wrong for once before I die."

"You're not wrong yet," Kestrel said gently. "You're just early."

Outside, the snow fell. Inside, four people who had lost everything—names, sisters, oaths, careers, fires, faiths—sat close around a small fire and discovered, to their mutual embarrassment, that what remained was enough to call a family.

* * *

They slept in shifts, and Odile took the last watch, and that was when the map spoke to her.

Not in words. Maps don't talk. But as she sat turning it over in the firelight, checking the route one final time, she felt it warm against her palms—warmer than paper, warmer than blood—and in that warmth came an impression, the way a sealed letter carries the shape of the pen that wrote it.

Gratitude. Old, enormous, exhausted gratitude. And beneath it, faint as a watermark, a single image: a crowned man in a white room, pressing his hand to glass, mouthing a word over and over.

She read his lips. Anyone would have. The word was *remember*.

"Well," Odile said softly, to the map, to the king, to the mountain waiting in the dark to the north. "That's the plan, Your Majesty. Remembering is rather our specialty."

Behind her, Kestrel stirred in his sleep and murmured something. She glanced back. He was smiling in his dream, whatever it was, and one hand was curled protectively around the strap of the pack that held a king's entire life in bottles.

Tomorrow they would climb into the teeth of the thing that wore a beloved face. Tomorrow the doors would need holding. Tomorrow, midwinter's eve would begin, and the binding would begin with it, and every hour they marched north the Hollow King would draw the city's warmth a little faster, a little greedier, feeling its completion near.

But tonight—for one more hour—there was firelight, and terrible tea, and the sound of Brann snoring like a landslide, and Sable muttering curses at her boots, and Kestrel dreaming of cake.

Odile banked the fire, tucked the map into her coat, and allowed herself, for exactly sixty seconds, to hope.

Then dawn came gray and hungry over the ice, and it was time to go.
