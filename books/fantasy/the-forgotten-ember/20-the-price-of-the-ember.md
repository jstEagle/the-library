## 20. The Price of the Ember

The Hollow King's wraiths came like a tide made of winter itself, and Sable met them at the vault doors with her sister's memory burning in her chest.

"Hold!" she shouted, and the word was half laugh, half prayer. "You great cold *bastard*, hold!"

She stood in the doorway with her arms spread wide, and where the wraiths touched her they hissed and recoiled—not because she was strong, though she was, but because she was *warm*. Grief, Kestrel had learned over these long weeks, was not cold. Grief was the hottest thing there was. The Hollow King had never understood that. It had spent ten years trying to drink warmth out of a city, when all along the warmth had been sitting inside the sorrow, waiting for someone brave enough to feel it.

Brann held the left door. He had wedged his shield across the frame and put his back against it, and every time a wraith struck he grunted like a man hauling a cart uphill. His wedding day was gone—he'd paid it at the door in the mountain, given it up without ceremony, the way he did everything—and Kestrel thought, watching him strain, that Brann must be fighting for a marriage he could no longer remember, on behalf of a wife who was probably somewhere in Vellamarch right now wondering why her heart felt like a room with the furniture missing.

"Kestrel!" Odile's voice cut through from the inner chamber. "It's pulling! The king's memories—it's pulling them back out of him faster than you can pour!"

He knelt beside Aldric on the floor of the Ember Vault's heart, and it was worse than any market stall, worse than any theft, because this time he wasn't taking. He was giving back.

The true king lay with his eyes open and unfocused, and around his head the air shimmered with light that was not light—memories trying to return to a man who had been hollowed so long that his soul had forgotten its own shape. Kestrel pressed his palms to Aldric's temples and poured, the way Odile had taught him twenty years ago: *not pushing, child. Inviting. Memory knows its home.*

But the Hollow King was inviting too, from its throne of black ice at the chamber's far end, and between them the king stretched like a rope in a tug-of-war, fraying.

*Give up,* said a voice that was not a voice, that arrived directly behind Kestrel's eyes like a hand closing on the back of his neck. *You are tired. You have been tired your whole life. Put it down.*

"I know," Kestrel said through his teeth. "That's rather my whole problem."

* * *

Here is what Kestrel Fairweather knew about hunger, and had known since he was small enough to fit inside a flour barrel he once stole to sleep in:

Hunger lies. Hunger says *one more and I'll be full*. Hunger says *that warmth belongs to me, I can feel it, look how close it sits*. And the cruelest lie, the one hunger tells best: *if you stop feeding me, you will die.*

He had fed on other people's memories for fifteen years because his own were unbearable. That was the truth he'd confessed in the blizzard cave while Sable held his hand and Brann pretended to sleep and the wind screamed like everything he'd ever run from. A boy named Kestrel Fairweather had watched his mother burn—not as a witch-finder's victim, but in a house fire, an accident, a stupid ordinary tragedy—and had discovered at nine years old that he could reach into grieving minds and lift the grief out, and he had lifted his own out first, and sold it, and then kept selling, because a man who has sold his grief finds the world very quiet and very cold and goes looking for anyone else's fire to sit beside.

Every charm in his satchel. Every bottle. The baker's first snowfall. The widow's wedding morning. The soldier's daughter learning to whistle. Moments people needed to survive their winters, and he had taken them for coin, and told himself it was harmless, and it had never once been harmless.

"You're doing it again," Odile snapped, appearing at his elbow with blood on her temple from the escape and fury in her eyes like fuel. "Narrating your sins instead of working. Pour, you disaster of a man."

"I'm pouring."

"You're *brooding* with your hands attached to his head. There's a difference and the difference is the king stays hollow."

She crouched opposite him, took Aldric's hands in hers, and began to hum—an old keeper's cadence, a lullaby for memories coming home. Kestrel matched his breath to it. Push became invitation. The shimmer around the king's head steadied, brightened.

And the throne of black ice began to walk toward them.

* * *

The Hollow King wore Aldric's face, but it wore it badly now, the way a coat wears thin at the elbows. Ten years of feeding had made it vast; tonight, with the binding nearly complete, it should have been invincible. Instead it flickered. Because the memories were going home, and every one that settled into the true king starved the parasite by a mouthful.

It stopped three paces away and looked down at Kestrel with the king's grey eyes, and its voice, when it came, was almost gentle.

*Thief. Do you know what I am?*

"Grief," Kestrel said. "I've read the file. Very moving."

*A king watched children freeze in a plague winter. He begged the gods, the doctors, the stars. Nothing came. So his sorrow grew teeth and a crown and decided that if warmth would not stay in the world willingly, it would be harvested.* The thing knelt, which was somehow more terrible than if it had loomed. *I am not evil, thief. I am unfinished mourning. Can you destroy mourning?*

"No." Kestrel's arms shook. The last of the king's memories were moving now, a flood of them—the coronation, a queen's laughter, a small son's fever breaking at dawn—and each one that landed made Aldric gasp like a drowning man surfacing. "No, I can't destroy it. But I know something about hunger that you don't."

*Tell me.*

"It's not a bottomless pit. It's a *shape*. And you can fill a shape wrong forever, or you can fill it right once, and then it's done." He looked up. "You've been eating warmth for ten years because no one ever taught you that grief gets *answered*. Not cured. Answered. Someone sits with it. Someone says: yes, this happened, and it's terrible, and you're not alone in it."

*No one sat with me,* said the Hollow King, and for one moment it sounded exactly like a tired man. *He locked me in a white room inside his own head and threw away the key.*

"Yeah," Kestrel said softly. "People do that. I did it for thirty years." He glanced at Odile, at the doorway where Sable and Brann held the line against the dark, at the wraiths that were fewer now, thinner, confused. "Then some idiots refused to let me."

*What are you offering, thief?*

"Not offering." Kestrel reached for his satchel. "*Paying.*"

* * *

The hoard.

Fifteen years of bottled light. The baker's snowfall. The widow's morning. Four hundred charms' worth of other people's precious days, plus—buried deepest, wrapped in triple silence—the ones he'd stolen from himself so he wouldn't have to carry them. His mother's face before the fire. Her voice saying his name. The name itself, and the boy it belonged to, and the flour barrel, and the first time he ever stole a memory and the sick hot shame of it, which he had kept because even shame was warmer than nothing.

Odile saw his hand on the satchel and went white. "Kestrel. No. *No.* There's another way—we'll find—"

"There isn't, and we both know it, and you hate that I'm right, so say something cruel and then help me."

"You insufferable, self-sacrificing, *sentimental* little pickpocket," she said, crying without any change in her voice, "you were the worst student I ever had and the only one who mattered," and she grabbed the satchel and upended it over the space between the two kings.

The bottles fell and broke open in the air, unbroken, released, and light poured out like a struck dam.

The Hollow King opened its arms.

It didn't fight. That was the terrible, beautiful thing. It had been starving for ten years and here was a feast, and it drank the baker's snowfall and the widow's morning and the whistling child and the mother's face and the name—

*Kestrel,* said the voice, tasting it. *Kestrel Fairweather. What a small warm thing a name is.*

And Kestrel felt it go, felt the shape of himself peel away like frost off glass, and he thought: *oh. So that's what I was.* And then he thought: *was I?* And then there was a man kneeling on the vault floor with his hands on a stranger's head, and the man could not remember why he was crying, only that it seemed correct.

The Hollow King blazed. It filled with four hundred borrowed dawns, and it swelled, and Sable screamed from the doorway, and then—

It sighed.

A sound like a city exhaling at dusk. Like a long-held breath finally let go. The vast dark shape folded inward, smaller and smaller, gathering all that stolen light into a single point, and the point did not go out. It softened. It turned the color of hearth-coals, of candle-through-curtain, of the last ember of a fire that has done its work and is content to rest.

An ember drifted down and landed in the true king's open palm, and glowed there, gentle as a firefly, and asked for nothing.

Aldric breathed in.

And wept.

* * *

He wept the way stone weeps when thaw comes—all at once, after years, in cracks. He wept for the plague winter and the frozen children and the son whose fever had broken while other mothers' sons did not. He wept for the decade stolen from him, and for the thing born of his sorrow that had worn his face and starved his city, and when Kestrel—nameless, empty, watching with a strange clean distance—reached out and touched the ember in the king's palm, Aldric looked up at him with red-rimmed eyes and said:

"You gave it somewhere to rest. All that grief. You gave it—" his voice broke, reformed, "—you gave it a *home*, instead of a hunger. Who are you?"

The nameless man opened his mouth and found nothing there. No name. No childhood. No mother's voice. Just a warm spot in his chest where something used to live, and four faces looking at him from the doorway—two holding a door against enemies who were already ash, one humming a keeper's lullaby through tears, one bleeding and laughing and swearing with joy.

"I don't know," the nameless man said, and found that it didn't hurt the way he'd feared. It hurt the way a wound hurts when the bandage comes off: honestly. "But apparently I'm theirs."

Sable crossed the room in six strides and hit him on the arm, hard enough to mean it. "Don't you *ever*—"

"That's the trouble," Odile said, wiping her eyes with her sleeve like a woman annoyed at weather. "He can't promise anything. He doesn't remember enough to break promises." She took his hand anyway, gripped it hard. "We'll manage. We remember *for* him. That's rather the family business now, isn't it?"

Above them, far above, through miles of mountain, the wind changed. Somewhere in Vellamarch a curfew bell rang and then faltered, confused, as warmth crept back into streets that had forgotten it. The longest night was ending early for the first time in ten years.

The ember pulsed once in the king's palm—soft, patient, alive—and Aldric closed his fingers around it and stood, a king waking from a bad dream into a kingdom that needed him.

"Come," he said to the nameless man and his ragged, impossible family. "There is a city to wake up. And I find I owe a debt to someone whose name I don't yet know."

The nameless man smiled, and found the smile fit fine.

"That," he said, "sounds like the beginning of a story."

* * *

They walked out of the mountain together as dawn—real dawn, gold-edged, unafraid—broke over the frozen north road, and far behind them in the vault's heart the ember-light settled into the stone, keeping a small warm watch over nothing at all.
