## 21. A King Made Whole

The bells of Vellamarch rang for three days.

Kestrel knew this because people told him so, over and over, in the bright voices of those who had survived something terrible and needed to say it aloud to make it true. *Three days of bells,* they said, gripping his arm in the square, in the market, on the palace steps where the crowd had gathered like wheat before a wind. *Three days, and then the King himself came out and said the winter was broken.*

He nodded at each telling. He smiled when smiling seemed expected. He had learned, in the weeks since midwinter night, the shape of the expressions that fit occasions—the way he had once learned the shape of other men's memories, slipping them on and wearing them until no one looked twice.

It was a useful skill, still. It was just that now there was nothing underneath it.

*You are standing in the sun because you put yourself here,* Odile had told him, back when she was still trying to explain what he'd done to himself as though explanation might help. *You fed it everything. Your name. Your mother's kitchen. The first time you ever stole something and liked it. All of it, gone into that thing's mouth, and it choked.* She had gripped his shoulders hard enough to bruise. *Do you understand? It didn't take them from you. You gave them. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

He had understood, in the way one understands arithmetic. Two plus two. A man gives up his whole self and the city wakes warm. The sums were correct. The sums were even noble, if one liked nobility.

But understanding was not feeling, and feeling was the country he now lived outside of, looking in through frosted glass at people who were warm.

* * *

On the fourth day after the bells stopped, King Aldric stood on the balcony above the Palace Square and unmade ten years of cruelty in an afternoon.

Kestrel watched from the edge of the crowd, hood up out of habit, though no witch-finder would touch him now. Beside him, Sable stood with her arms crossed and her chin lifted, daring anyone to look at her burn-scarred throat and see anything but a survivor. On her other side, Corporal Brann—Sergeant Brann now, the promotion pinned on him by the King's own hand two days prior—kept shifting his weight like a man who had never learned how to stand at ease off duty.

"Look at him," Sable murmured. "He's crying again."

"He cries at everything," Brann said. "I've seen him cry at a proclamation about drainage reform."

"That was last week," Kestrel said. "The drainage in the Lower Terraces genuinely is a tragedy."

Brann snorted. Sable elbowed Kestrel, gently, the way one elbows a person one has decided to keep. He registered the gesture. He filed it under *things friends do*, a category he maintained now the way a scholar maintains a catalogue of a dead language—accurate, diligent, secondhand.

Below them, Aldric raised his hands, and the square went quiet the way snow quiets a street.

"People of Vellamarch," the King said, and his voice carried without effort, raw and human and cracked at the edges. "For ten years you paid tithes. You lined up in your districts and you gave the Crown your memories—your weddings, your children's faces, your griefs, your joys—and you were told it was voluntary. You were told it was for warmth." He paused. His jaw worked. "It was not voluntary, and it was not for warmth. It was theft. It was harvest. And I—" his voice broke, and he let it, in front of ten thousand people, "—I was the field."

A sound went through the crowd like wind through wheat.

"So hear my first decrees," Aldric continued. "The memory tithes are ended. Not suspended. Ended. Every memory held in the Crown archive will be returned to its owner or their kin, freely, beginning this week. Every person marked for magic is pardoned—not tolerated, not permitted, *pardoned*, with apology owed and restitution made. And the Office of Witch-Finding—" here his voice hardened into something old and cold, the ghost of the hollow years still clinging to him, "—is disbanded. Today. Its warrants are ash. Its pyres are cold. Any finder who wishes to swear new service to a Crown that protects rather than hunts may apply. Any who do not—" he spread his hands, "—will find the gates open and no one chasing them. We have been hunted long enough. All of us. I will not govern by fear, because I know precisely what fear grows into. I wore its face for a decade."

Then he stepped aside, and Sergeant Hale of the witch-finders walked onto the balcony.

The crowd's silence turned sharp. Kestrel felt Brann go rigid beside him; felt Sable's breath catch in the old way, the way it caught around anything that smelled of the pyres.

Hale stood at the rail in his grey coat, warrant-less now, stripped of the insignia that had made him untouchable, and he looked out at the city he had spent fifteen years terrorizing in the name of order, and he did not flinch.

"My name is Edmund Hale," he said. "For fifteen years I served the Office of Witch-Finding. I signed tithing orders. I escorted the marked to the pyres. I told myself it was law." His voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "Last month I read the archive ledgers. Ten years of harvests, district by district, signed by the Crown, fed to something beneath the palace while we burned the very people who might have stopped it. I burned my own warrant rather than serve it another day. Now I stand here as testimony. Ask me anything. I will answer truthfully, and the truth will be damning, and I will say all of it." He bowed his head. "Justice should begin with the man who enforced the injustice. So. Begin with me."

For a long moment nothing moved in the square.

Then, from somewhere near the fountain, an old woman's voice: "My daughter. 4th District tithe rolls, eight years ago. Do you remember her?"

"I remember every name I processed," Hale said quietly. "Tell me hers. I'll find her memory myself and carry it to your door."

And the square broke—not into violence, which Kestrel had half expected, but into something stranger: a long, ragged exhale, ten thousand people letting go of a decade of held breath all at once. Someone began to cheer. Then many someones. It was not forgiveness, exactly. It was the decision to stop bleeding, which comes first, and looks similar from a distance.

"Well," said Sable, wiping her eyes furiously with her sleeve. "Well. Huh."

"He testified for six hours yesterday," Brann said. "Named names. Dates. Where they buried the ones who didn't survive processing." He shook his head slowly. "Fifteen years I hated that man. Turns out hate was simpler than this."

"Most things are," said Odile, arriving at Kestrel's elbow with the particular scowl she wore when she had been crying and intended to deny it. She thrust a paper bag at him. "Honey cakes. From the stall by the fountain. The woman wouldn't take my coin—said the King's decree covered 'all debts of gratitude,' which is nonsense legally, but her cakes are excellent, so I've decided not to litigate."

Kestrel took the bag. The cakes were warm. He knew they were warm because steam rose from them, and he knew honey was sweet because everyone agreed it was, and he ate one and experienced the eating of it the way one reads a letter written to someone else.

"You're doing the face," Odile said, watching him.

"What face?"

"The one where you're performing enjoyment for our benefit. Stop it. Eat badly if you want. Mope if you want. You've earned a moping license valid through spring."

"I'm not moping."

"You're standing apart from the celebration at the edge of a crowd, hood up, cataloguing social cues." She ticked the items off on her fingers. "That's a moping posture. I wrote the book on moping postures. Chapter one is me."

Despite everything—or perhaps because of nothing, because there was nothing left in him to resist with—a laugh got out of him, small and surprised. Odile looked absurdly triumphant, as though she'd picked a lock.

"There," she said. "Filed under things friends do."

He went very still. "Did I say that aloud?"

"You mutter your filing system in your sleep, Kestrel. Or—you did. When you slept. Which was rarely." Her scowl softened into something older and sadder. "Eat your cake."

* * *

They found him that evening on the palace terrace, because of course they did; he had stopped believing he could lose them somewhere around the third time Sable had tracked him across the city by pure stubbornness.

Aldric had given them rooms in the palace—actual rooms, with actual windows, on the grounds that "the people who saved the kingdom will not lodge in my barracks, Sergeant, I don't care what the protocol says." Kestrel's room was warm and well-lit and full of small courtesies he could not feel, and so he spent most of his hours elsewhere, walking the terraces where the winter-bare gardens were showing the first green insistence of thaw.

The true king sat alone on a stone bench at the terrace's end, wrapped in a plain wool cloak, no crown anywhere in sight. Ten years hollowed and he had come back thin and grey and prone to weeping at drainage proclamations—but alive, awake, and filling out daily, like a house being refurnished room by room. He looked up as Kestrel approached, and smiled, and the smile was tired and real.

"They tell me you don't remember me," Aldric said.

"I'm told I never met you before the Vault," Kestrel said. "So in one sense, nothing's changed."

"In one sense." The King patted the bench beside him. Kestrel sat. For a while they watched the light go gold and then violet over the city, over the smoking chimneys of ten thousand homes burning their fires high and wasteful and joyful, because fuel was cheap again and warmth was no longer a currency.

"I remember everything now," Aldric said at last. "All of it. The plague winter, thirty years ago—I was a young king, and I locked the gates of the poor districts to contain the sickness, and four thousand people died inside them, and I told myself it was necessary. That grief grew teeth in the dark of me. It wore my face and ruled in my name and I lay beneath it, aware, for ten years, unable to scream." He rubbed his hands together against a chill that Kestrel suspected lived in him now permanently, the way an old injury lives in a bone. "And then a thief walked into my prison carrying my own memories home like a shepherd with lost sheep, and poured them back into me, and stayed afterward to feed the monster until it starved."

"It wasn't a monster," Kestrel said. "Not really. It was just sad. Sadness with a crown and too much appetite."

"Yes." Aldric studied him. "You understand it better than any of my counselors. They want to call it a demon. Demons can be killed cleanly. Wounds are harder—they ask you to change." He paused. "There is something I must give you, and I don't know how, because you cannot remember receiving it."

"Your Majesty—"

"Humor me. I've been a prisoner for a decade; I intend to be tiresome for at least a year." He reached into his cloak and drew out a small ledger, worn at the corners. "This is the tithe roll for the 7th District, the year before the Hollow years began. Before I was taken. I signed it myself, thinking I was signing charity." He opened it to a marked page. "Every entry is a person who sold a memory to the Crown. Most entries list what was taken. This one—" he turned the book toward Kestrel, "—does not."

Kestrel looked. The entry was brief. *One memory, seller's choice, payment: bread, coal, medicine for the seller's mother.* And beneath, in a different hand, added later in faded ink: *Contents unknown. Seller: a boy, approximately twelve. Refused to give his name. Said, quote, "Take the one where my da laughs. I don't want to be the kind of person who remembers that and stays angry."*

The terrace was very quiet.

"I had my archivists search the recovered stores," Aldric said softly. "We found it. A memory of a man laughing—your father, presumably—in a kitchen, teaching a small boy to juggle stolen apples. It survived the Vault. It survived you." He closed the ledger. "It's yours, whenever you want it. It may be the only piece of yourself left in the world. I would not blame you if you couldn't bear to watch it. I would not blame you if you watched it a hundred times. But I wanted you to know it exists. You are not entirely erased, Kestrel Fairweather. One ember kept."

Kestrel looked at the ledger for a long time. He felt—nothing, which was the honest truth, and also felt the shape of what he ought to feel, grief and hunger and hope all tangled, the way a man who has lost a limb feels the shape of the hand he no longer has.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it in whatever way remained available to him.

"One more thing." Aldric stood, joints cracking, and looked out at his city with an expression Kestrel recognized from descriptions: the weight of a crown worn by someone determined to deserve it. "I am restoring the Keepers' Guild. Memory-keeping as a craft of healing, not harvesting. Odile Vane has agreed to lead it—she negotiated hard, mostly for funding for safehouses and a clause protecting apprentices from exploitative masters, which tells you everything about why she was disgraced in the first place. She has requested—you, specifically, as her first apprentice." He glanced sideways. "She said, and I quote, 'He has empty shelves and steady hands and terrible taste in hats. He'll do.'"

"I don't wear hats."

"She said that too."

Kestrel laughed again—that small surprised laugh, easier now, a muscle remembering itself. "I'll think about it."

"Don't think too long," said the King. "She's already ordered you an apron."

* * *

He left before dawn three days later.

Not dramatically—he was done with dramatic exits; dramatic exits got people captured by witch-finders. He simply packed the few things he owned, left the honey-cake money under his pillow for the chamber staff, and slipped out through the servants' gate into a city wearing its first true morning of spring.

The streets were wet and shining. Somewhere a baker was arguing joyfully with a delivery boy. A flower seller had set up on the corner where the 7th District tithe line used to form, selling crocuses, of all things, defiant little purple cups of sunlight, and people were buying them in fistfuls.

Kestrel walked past the fountain, past the archive with its doors thrown open and its returning crowds, past the blackened skeleton of the last pyre platform, which workmen were dismantling plank by plank while children watched solemnly, as if witnessing the burial of a dragon. At the South Gate, the guards—new guards, relaxed guards, guards who waved people through with yawns—nodded him past without a second glance.

Nobody knew his name. That was the strange mercy of it. In the whole city of Vellamarch, not one soul remembered Kestrel Fairweather, and so he could walk out clean, unoweighted, unhaunted, free—

"—and where exactly do you think you're going?"

He stopped. Closed his eyes. Exhaled.

Odile stood twenty paces back down the road, arms crossed, travel cloak already dusty, a pack on her shoulder that looked packed by someone expecting a long journey and prepared to win it. Behind her, catching up at a jog, came Sable with a bedroll and an expression of tremendous affront, and behind *her*, wheezing, Brann, on leave, out of uniform, carrying a frying pan for reasons no one had yet explained.

"We discussed this," Odile said, arriving. "At length. Last night. Over dinner. Which you attended."

"We discussed the Guild. I said I'd think about it."

"And we discussed the fact that you have no memories, no name you can feel, no idea how to buy a train ticket because you've never left Vellamarch, and the survival instincts of a moth." She jabbed a finger southward, down the road, toward the open country and the morning. "So we're coming. All of us. To remind you who you are until you can be someone new. Those were the terms. You agreed. You said, and I quote, 'Fine, but I get the window seat.'"

"I don't remember saying that."

"Of course you don't. That's rather the point." Her scowl cracked into a grin. "Lucky for you, we remember *everything*. Sable remembers the time you fell off the roof in the 9th District. Brann remembers the archive job. I remember your entire disastrous apprenticeship, including the incident with the memory of a dog." She slung her arm through his and steered him firmly down the road, away from the gate, away from the empty country, into the company of the only people in the world who had chosen to carry his story for him. "Now walk, apprentice. First lesson begins on the road. Tell us what you *do* remember—we start the inventory from zero."

Behind them, Vellamarch rang its bells again—just once, just because—and the road went south into the spring, and Kestrel Fairweather, nameless and remembered, took his first step into a life no one had written yet.

Except, apparently, the three people marching beside him, who had opinions.
