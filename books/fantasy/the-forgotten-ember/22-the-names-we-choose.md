## 22. The Names We Choose

The road out of Vellamarch ran through the Ashgate, past the pyre-stones where the witch-finders' scaffolds had stood for a decade, and Kestrel Fairweather walked it alone because there was no one left to walk it with him.

That was the arithmetic of it, anyway. The arithmetic was correct. He had checked it three times.

He had given away everything in the Vault—every bottled hour, every stolen summer, every scrap of other people's lives he'd hoarded against the cold like a magpie with a candle problem. He'd poured them into the Hollow King until the thing had collapsed into a drift of harmless ember-light, warm as a hearth and about as dangerous. And when the hoard ran out, the binding had taken the rest: his name, his childhood, his mother's voice, whatever face he'd worn before the world taught him to wear this one.

He knew that in the abstract way a man knows the contents of a house he's never entered. There had been a boy once. The boy had become a thief of memories, then a savior of kings, and now there was a man on the north road with empty pockets behind his eyes, walking out of the city he'd saved because staying felt like trespassing.

The morning was bright. That was the cruelest part. Ten years of gathering winter, broken in a single night, and Vellamarch woke to sunlight on wet slate roofs and steam rising off the canals like the city itself was exhaling. Somewhere behind him, bells were ringing—not the alarm bells, not the tithe-bells, but actual celebration bells, clumsy and joyful, rung by people who had clearly forgotten how.

Kestrel didn't feel it. He understood, distantly, that he should. He had read enough memories to know what joy tasted like from the inside—honey and woodsmoke and the specific ache of wanting things to stay—and he recognized the ingredients in the crowd streaming past him toward the palace square. But recognizing an ingredient wasn't the same as eating the meal.

He kept walking.

* * *

They caught up with him at the milestone outside the Ashgate, the one that said VELLAMARCH — 1 LEAGUE in letters some mason had cut a hundred years ago and some vandal had recently amended to VELLAMARCH — 1 LEAGUE, THANK THE EMBERS.

He heard them before he saw them, because Sable had never learned to walk quietly and had never once apologized for it.

"There he is," she announced to the entire empty road. "The most famous man in Vellamarch, sneaking out of town at dawn like a man leaving a tavern without paying."

"I paid," Kestrel said. It came out flat. Everything came out flat these days; his voice was one of the few instruments he still owned, and it had lost most of its strings. "I paid rather a lot, actually."

"That's the joke," Sable said, catching his sleeve. She was grinning, and her grin had always been slightly terrifying, all teeth and survival. "That's the whole joke, and you're ruining it by walking away before the punchline."

Odile Vane stood a little behind her, arms folded, wearing the expression she'd worn for every one of their eleven days on the road north—the one that said she found the entire enterprise beneath her and would be participating anyway. Her hair was still growing back in patches where the interrogators had taken it. She looked, Kestrel thought, exactly as unimpressed as ever, which was somehow the most comforting sight in the world.

"You're difficult to find," Odile said, "for a man who doesn't want to be found. You have no idea how insulting that is. I trained you better than that."

"You trained me to disappear."

"I trained you to *survive*. Disappearing was supposed to be a tool, not a personality." She unfolded her arms. "And Brann's been asking every gate guard, every market crier, and every drunk in the Low Quarter where you went, so honestly, disappearing was never on the table."

"Where is Brann?"

"Here," said Corporal Tam Brann, coming around the bend of the road at a pace just short of jogging, his guard's coat buttoned wrong because he'd dressed in a hurry, holding two meat pies like they were diplomatic credentials. "Caught us up at the gate. Bought breakfast. You—" he thrust a pie at Kestrel, slightly breathless, "—are going to eat this, and then you are going to stop doing this thing where you leave."

Kestrel looked at the pie. It steamed. It smelled of onions and grease and the particular optimism of a baker who believed the city had a future. His body wanted it with a simple animal hunger that required no memory at all, and for that small mercy he could have wept.

He ate it. It was, he was almost certain, good.

* * *

They walked with him. He didn't ask them to. That was the point—they hadn't been asked, and they came anyway, falling into step on the north road as naturally as if they'd all agreed on it months ago.

After a league, he stopped and turned around.

"Why," he said.

Sable shrugged. "Because the road's nice."

"Sable."

"Because," she said, and her grin faded into something quieter, something he'd only seen on her twice—once at her sister's grave-wall, once in the Vault when the ember-light rained down like warm snow, "you don't get to give away everything and then decide nobody gets to know you. That's not sacrifice, that's theft. You stole yourself from us. And I've had enough things stolen this decade to last several lifetimes, so I'm taking you back."

"That's not how it works," Kestrel said. "I'm not—I don't remember any of you. Not really. I know facts. I know you had a sister named Wren and she liked to write warnings on walls. I know Odile drinks her tea bitter enough to strip varnish and once made a grown witch-finder cry with a single raised eyebrow. I know Brann—" he paused, groping for it, "—Brann is kind. I know that the way you know a load-bearing wall is there. But I don't *remember*. It's not fair to any of you. I'm a stranger wearing your friend's coat."

There was a silence.

Then Odile snorted.

"A stranger," she repeated. "Wearing his coat." She stepped closer, and her eyes were bright and furious and wet all at once. "Boy, I have known you since you were fourteen and tried to pickpocket a memory-keeper with hands that shook so badly you dropped the charm in my own tea. Do you know how I know that? Because I was *there*, and unlike you, my head is fully stocked." She tapped her temple. "You think memory lives only in the one who lived it? Amateur. Memory lives in whoever keeps it. That is the entire first lesson, and apparently you slept through it."

"She's going to do the whole curriculum," Sable warned. "We couldn't stop her. Brann tried."

"I did try," Brann agreed solemnly. "It was like arguing with a landslide."

"So here is what happens now," Odile said, in the voice she'd used to make him copy out memory-ethics until his fingers bled. "We are going to walk, because the road is long and my knees are fifty-two years old. And while we walk, we are going to tell you a story. It happens to be your story. You will interrupt with questions, because you always did, and we will answer them, because we were paying attention even when you weren't. And when we reach wherever it is you thought you were going—" she gestured at the road ahead, the hills, the horizon, the whole indifferent future, "—you will not be a stranger in your own coat. You'll be Kestrel. Chosen this time. Now walk."

And because he had never once won an argument with that voice, Kestrel walked.

* * *

They told him about the night market first, because Sable insisted on starting with the funny parts.

"—and he had this *rule*, right, never sell the same customer twice—"

"It's a good rule," Kestrel protested.

"It's a paranoid rule, and it fell apart the moment old Marta from the fish stall decided your luck charms were the only reason her stall hadn't burned down, so she bought one every week whether she needed it or not, and you were too soft to refuse her." Sable laughed, the sound bright in the cold air. "You used to water down the memories for her. Little ones. The smell of bread. Sunlight on water. You told her they were exotic imports from the southern isles."

"They were technically from the canal."

"Sunlight on the *canal*," Sable repeated, delighted. "Sold as southern sunshine. To a fishmonger. Who loved them."

Kestrel found himself smiling. It startled him—he felt it happen from the outside, a movement of muscles he hadn't authorized—and he decided not to examine it too closely in case it stopped.

Brann told the story of the archive, of course. He told it modestly, which meant it took twenty minutes and three digressions before anyone realized he was describing the moment he'd committed treason.

"—so the sergeant says, papers, and I says, of course, sergeant, let me just find them, and meanwhile Fairweather here is climbing out a window directly over my head like the world's guiltiest cat—"

"I was quiet."

"You were *visible*. You had ash on your face. From the chimney. Which was the one thing in the room that was clean." Brann shook his head, but his eyes were warm. "I lied to my sergeant for you. First lie of my career. Felt terrible for about an hour, and then the decrees started coming down from the palace, and it felt less terrible every day after."

"Do you regret it?" Kestrel asked. "The lie?"

Brann considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, chewing on it like tough bread.

"No," he said finally. "An oath's a promise to protect people. The law's just the current best guess at how. When the guess goes wrong, the promise still stands." He glanced sideways. "Besides. You took something from me once. Something bad. Never told me what. I worked it out eventually." He tapped his own temple, mirroring Odile. "Whatever it cost you then, I figure I owe you a story now. So. Chapter six. Ask me anything."

* * *

By midday they had reached the river, running high and loud with snowmelt, the ice that had held it all winter gone rotten and breaking up in great groaning plates. They sat on the bank and ate the second pies, and Odile told him about the tenement fire, and his mother—

No. She offered, quietly, and he found he couldn't yet. She nodded as if he'd said something wise instead of something frightened, and told him about his apprenticeship instead: the boy who'd arrived with nothing, who'd learned to hold a memory like an egg, who'd been so careful with other people's pasts and so careless with his own.

"You gave away pieces of yourself for years," she said. "Sold your good days to pay rent. Kept everyone else's worst days so they wouldn't have to carry them. No wonder the binding took you so easily—you'd already emptied the house. You just left the door open." Her voice gentled, which on Odile was more alarming than shouting. "That ends now. New rule. First rule of the new curriculum: no one carries alone. Not even you. *Especially* not you."

"The Hollow King carried alone," Kestrel said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. "That's what it was, in the end. One grief, one will, refusing to share the weight until the weight became a crown and then a cage. I keep thinking—if it had had anyone. If someone had sat with it. If it had ever once been told *you don't have to hold that by yourself*—"

"Then it wouldn't have needed to eat a city," Sable said softly.

They watched the river break up its winter. Great plates of ice tilted and sank, and the water underneath ran black and quick and alive.

"My sister used to say," Sable began, and stopped, and started again, steadier, "Wren used to say that grief is just love with nowhere to go. The Hollow King was love with nowhere to go for ten years. You gave it somewhere to go. That's why it went out like a lamp and not like a fire." She bumped her shoulder against his. "You're allowed to be proud of that. I'm telling you as a professional survivor: you're allowed."

"I don't feel proud. I don't feel much of anything."

"You will," Brann said simply. "Feelings come back the same way the river does. All at once, and louder than expected, usually in spring."

* * *

They camped that night under a stand of bare birches, and Odile produced, from some bottomless pocket, a bottle of plum brandy that had survived interrogation, imprisonment, a mountain, and a blizzard, which Kestrel felt made it the most accomplished object in the kingdom.

They passed it around. The stars came out—more stars than Vellamarch had shown in a decade, the sky scrubbed clean by the breaking of the winter—and Sable named constellations wrong on purpose to annoy Odile, and Brann fell asleep sitting up with his pie tin still in his hand, and Kestrel lay on his back and listened to his own story being told by the people who had kept it safe.

They told him about the cave in the blizzard, and the confession he'd made there. They told him gently, and they did not skip anything, because Odile said a story with holes was worse than no story at all. They told him how many memories he'd sold, and to whom, and why, and how he'd wept in the dark and been forgiven for it before he finished the sentence.

"We'll take you to some of them, someday," Sable said. "The people you sold the good days to. Most of them are fine. Some of them are better than fine. There's a woman in the Low Quarter who bought your memory of dancing and now teaches dance to marked children in her kitchen, and she calls it 'the inheritance,' and she has no idea whose it was."

"You could meet her," Brann said. "When we go back."

"If we go back."

"When," said all three of them, in unison, with such certainty that Kestrel laughed—a rusty, astonished sound, like a door opening in a house long shut.

* * *

Near midnight, when the others slept, Odile sat beside him at the fire. She didn't speak for a long time. That was her way; she believed silence was a teaching tool, though mostly Kestrel suspected she just enjoyed making people nervous.

"Your name," she said at last. "Do you want it back?"

"I don't understand."

"The binding took the name you were born with. Fairweather. Your mother chose it, may she rest, and it was a good name, but it was hers, not yours—it was given, not chosen. And the name you've gone by these ten years, Kestrel—that was a mask. A working name. Something light enough to throw away if the witch-finders got close." She poked the fire. "So here's the thing they don't teach apprentices, because it terrifies them: a name isn't a fact. It's a choice you keep making. Every time someone says it and you answer, you choose it again. You've been choosing 'Kestrel' for ten years under threat of the pyre. Nobody made you. That's the truest name you own."

Kestrel turned it over. Behind his eyes, where the hoard had been, there was a clean empty space, and in it, for the first time, something moved—not a memory, not quite. More like a seed deciding.

"Kestrel," he said, testing it.

It fit. Not like a mask. Like a coat that had been his all along.

"Kestrel," Odile confirmed. "Chosen freely. Witnessed and recorded." She smiled, briefly and fiercely, and lay down by the fire. "Sleep, Kestrel. Tomorrow we start lesson two."

"What's lesson two?"

"How to come home."

* * *

They reached the crossroads at dawn, where the north road split—one branch wandering on toward the free towns and the sea, the other bending back south toward Vellamarch, where the bells were still ringing, where a restored king was waiting to thank four people by name, where a woman in the Low Quarter taught dances she didn't know she'd inherited, where a city full of strangers was walking around carrying pieces of him in bottles and hearts and stories.

Kestrel stood at the crossroads a long moment.

"Well?" Sable said. "Which way, nameless wonder? World's your oyster. Road's your—whatever roads eat."

Kestrel looked down the southern road. He felt the emptiness behind his eyes, vast and clean as fresh snow. And in the middle of it, small and stubborn and entirely his own, something answered—not memory, not yet, but the place where memory would grow.

"South," he said. "Home first. Then—" he smiled, and this time it didn't surprise him at all, "—then everywhere else. Someone has to teach the new king what memories are worth. And I hear there's a woman in the Low Quarter who owes me a dance."

Brann grinned. Odile sighed the sigh of a teacher whose student has finally, *finally* learned the lesson. Sable whooped and set off south at a march, and the three of them followed, and Kestrel Fairweather—no, simply Kestrel, chosen freely, witnessed and recorded—walked with them, out of the long winter and into the first morning of a story that was, at last, entirely his own.

Behind them, far off in Vellamarch, the bells rang on—and beneath their ringing, faint but unmistakable, came another sound: a knock, rhythmic and patient, echoing up from the deep places of the Ember Vault, where something small and warm and newly awake had begun, very softly, to hum.
