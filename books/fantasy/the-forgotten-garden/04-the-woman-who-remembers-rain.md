## 4. The Woman Who Remembers Rain

The rain came to Saltcross Row the way it always did—sideways, mean, and smelling of fish—and Maren Oduya watched it from behind her shop window with the particular satisfaction of a woman who sold weather she never had to feel.

Her shop had no name on the door. It didn't need one. The people who came to her found it the way they found everything in the Undervaults: by rumor, by desperation, by the slow erosion of their own memories until they were willing to pay a stranger for the privilege of feeling like themselves again. A memory-broker's trade was simple. Clients came with too much past or too little, and Maren stood between them with her vials and her scales and her immaculate ledger, taking a cut of every remembered sunrise, every first kiss, every grief that had grown too heavy to carry.

She was good at it. She'd been good at it for so long that she no longer remembered—not precisely, not in her own body—whether she had ever been anything else.

That was the joke of her profession, the one she told herself at closing time when the lamps guttered low: *I sell other people's yesterdays because I have so few of my own.* She kept her own history in a locked vault beneath the floorboards, forty-one vials of it, and she had not opened that vault in eleven years. Not because she feared what was inside. Because she could not remember what was inside, and the not-remembering had become, over time, a kind of furniture. Something she arranged her life around.

The bell above her door rang at half past the third hour, which meant trouble, because respectable clients came before dark and desperate ones came after, and neither sort came in the rain unless they were running.

Two men entered, shaking water from grey coats. Guild collectors. She knew them the way a fishmonger knows gulls—by posture, by appetite. The tall one had the flat courtesy of a man who had been paid to be polite; the short one had hands that kept moving, touching things, lifting a brass orrery from her shelf and setting it down a finger's width off true. Maren catalogued the offense and said nothing. Collectors were best handled like weather: acknowledged, endured, never argued with.

"Madam Oduya." The tall one produced a smile that had been folded and refolded so many times its creases had gone soft. "Collector Serle. This is my colleague, Doss. We're making inquiries."

"Make them sitting down," she said. "You're dripping on my inventory. Rainwater is ruinous to stored recollection—it bleeds the color out of childhoods."

Doss put the orrery down properly. Small victories.

Serle remained standing. From his coat he drew a square of oilcloth, unwrapped it with the care of a man handling relics, and laid it on her counter: a sketch. Charcoal on cheap paper, done fast, done well—the face of an old man with a cartographer's squint and a week of beard.

"We're looking for this gentleman," Serle said. "Last seen two nights ago near the customs quarter. He may have been carrying documents of guild interest."

Maren looked at the face and felt the bottom drop out of her evening.

She knew him. Not his name—names were currency and she spent hers carefully—but the coat. In the sketch the man wore no coat, but Maren had seen him three days ago, standing exactly where Serle stood now, dripping exactly as they dripped, wearing a surveyor's longcoat the color of wet slate with a burn scar on the left cuff shaped like a crescent moon. He had wanted to buy something impossible. He had wanted, in fact, to *sell* something impossible—a memory he described, in a voice fraying at the edges, as *a garden where children learn a country by heart.*

She had refused him. Or rather, she had quoted him a price designed to refuse him politely, because the memory he carried had frightened her. Not its content. Its *weight*. Memories came into her shop with textures, and this one had arrived dense as lead, humming, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has technically ended. She had told herself the hum was nothing. She had told herself a great many things that night.

Now two collectors stood in her shop asking after him, and the hum was back, and it had brought friends.

"This gentleman," Maren said slowly, "came to me."

Serle's smile sharpened by a degree. "Did he."

"He wanted to sell. I wasn't buying." She let distaste do some of the work, curling her lip at the memory of the negotiation. "A garden memory, he claimed. Provenance unknown. Do you know what unprovenanced memories do to a broker's stock? One bad vial and I'm selling women their neighbor's wedding night for a year. I turned him out."

"When?"

"Four nights ago. Before the storm broke." She poured herself tea she didn't want, buying time in the oldest currency there was. "He argued. They always argue. Then he left."

"Left where?"

"I don't follow clients into the rain, Collector. I have stock to protect." She sipped. The tea was terrible. She drank it like a woman with nothing to hide, which was itself a performance, and she performed it flawlessly, because she had been performing flawless composure since before either of these men could shave.

Doss drifted toward her shelves. She watched him read labels without appearing to watch, which was a skill brokers developed early or died poor. *First Snowfall, Unclaimed. The Smell of a Mother's Kitchen, Slightly Faded. Twenty Years of Marriage, Owner Deceased, Priced to Move.* Her inventory was honest in the way graveyards are honest.

"He was guild once," Serle said, conversational, watching her instead of the shelves. "Cartography division. Retired. Did he mention the guild?"

"He mentioned money. They all mention money." She set down the cup. "Collector, if you've come to ask whether I bought a stolen memory, the answer is no, and you're welcome to search my vaults with a warrant. If you've come without a warrant, then you're asking me as a favor, and favors are also stock in this shop. What's mine worth to you?"

Something flickered behind Serle's eyes—respect, maybe, or the collector's equivalent, which was recalibration. "The man is dead," he said. "Found in the canal by the Ledgerhouse gate. We'd simply like to know who he spoke to before the end."

*By the Ledgerhouse gate.* The words went through Maren like a splinter working toward the heart. She kept her face still through long practice, but inside, some part of her that she kept locked in a vault beneath the floorboards sat up and took notice.

"The Ledgerhouse," she repeated. "How dramatic. And you think I know why a retired mapmaker would drown himself at a door that doesn't open?"

"We think nothing yet, madam. That's what inquiries are for." Serle wrapped the sketch away again, unhurried. "Thank you for your time. If he mentioned anyone else—if he left anything, a parcel, a paper—"

"He left nothing but the smell of wet wool and a sense of grievance." She rose, signaling audience concluded. "Good evening, Collectors. Mind the step; it remembers being kicked."

They laughed the way men laugh at brokers' jokes—because refusing was expensive—and left. The bell rang. The rain swallowed them.

Maren stood in her empty shop for a long moment, listening to the water run down the gutters, and admitted the truth she'd been holding at arm's length since the sketch touched her counter:

She had lied to the guild. Smoothly, completely, and without hesitation—and she could not remember the last time she had done anything without hesitation about price.

* * *

She opened the vault at midnight.

Not the deep vault—the shallow one, the working drawer where she kept the trades that hadn't settled. And there it was, exactly where she'd shoved it four nights ago with the superstitious haste of a woman putting a spider under a glass: the deposit the cartographer had left as surety before she named her price. Standard practice. Clients left collateral; if the deal closed, she returned it; if it didn't, the collateral covered her time.

She had never gotten around to returning it. She told herself now that this was sloppiness. She knew better. Some instincts are just honesty arriving early.

The vial was small, hand-blown, older than any glasswork currently made in the city. The memory inside moved wrong—not the lazy drift of ordinary recollection but a slow, deliberate turning, like something pacing the walls of its prison. When she held it up to the lamp, the light came through green. Garden-green. Rain-on-leaves green.

Maren Oduya had handled ten thousand memories. She knew their grammar the way priests know liturgy. And she knew—with the certainty that lived below thought, below training, in whatever cellar of her she never opened—that she had felt this exact green before. Not one like it. This one.

Which was impossible. Which was the kind of impossibility that got brokers disappeared, which was why she should stop the vial in wax, drop it in the canal, and go back to selling other people's weddings until she died rich and hollow like every broker before her.

Instead she sat down at her desk, pulled her private ledger toward her, and began to work.

Because here was the other thing, the thing she'd been circling for four nights like a moth around a flame it refused to name: the cartographer had come to her not to sell the garden memory. Selling had been the cover story, the way a man asks directions to a house he already knows. What he'd actually said, in the last minute, when she was already turning him out—he'd said, quietly, almost gently, as if passing along a debt:

*"If you ever find you're missing rain, mistress broker—you'll know where it fell."*

And Maren Oduya, who had built a life on the clean mechanics of trade, had gone cold to the bone. Because she *was* missing rain. Had been, she realized, for as long as she could remember—which was to say, for exactly as far back as her own memory ran, a wall of fog with nothing behind it, and she had always assumed the fog was natural, the way children assume hunger is natural, until someone tells them otherwise.

Every broker had gaps. The trade ate its practitioners; you sold enough of yourself to make rent and eventually you were mostly rented out. But her gap wasn't ragged, the way sold memories left ragged edges. Hers was *clean*. Cut. Like pages razored from a book.

Like a country removed from an atlas.

She wrote in her ledger, in the cipher only she could read: *Dead man. Garden memory. Ledgerhouse gate. Green light. Rain.* Then, after a moment, because the professional curiosity had curdled into something warmer and more dangerous, she added: *Whoever took what he was carrying is now the most interesting person in this city.*

The dead man had drowned at the Ledgerhouse gate. Gates did not drown people; keepers did, or failed to, and either way there was a keeper involved. Keepers were invisible men, gray as ledger-dust, and finding one was like finding the specific grain of sand that had scratched you. But Maren had spent twenty years learning where people hid the things they couldn't afford to be seen holding, and there was a trade in the fishmarket tomorrow morning—a broker's errand, a woman selling the taste of her dead husband's cooking, meeting at the oyster stalls—and fishmarkets saw everything. Fishmongers, cutpurses, off-duty collectors, and once, she happened to know, a gray little clerk from the Ledgerhouse who bought candles in bulk and haggled like a man who counted every coin twice.

She had noticed him months ago, the way she noticed everything: a keeper doing market errands, unremarkable, forgettable by design. But nobody was forgettable to a memory-broker. She remembered faces the way other women remembered songs. And she remembered that the candle-clerk had flinched—flinched, a tiny involuntary flinch—when a fishmonger dropped a crate and shouted a word in some language that wasn't the city's language, some word that sounded like rain falling on leaves in a green place.

She hadn't thought about it since. Now she thought about nothing else.

Maren closed the ledger, wrapped the humming green vial in oilcloth, and tucked it into her coat against her ribs, where it beat faintly against her side like a second heart.

Outside, the rain kept coming down, steady and familiar and somehow accusatory, and Maren Oduya stepped into it without her umbrella for the first time in years, telling herself it was professional curiosity, telling herself it was due diligence, telling herself the story brokers tell when loneliness finally gets loud enough to dress itself up as business.

Tomorrow, the fishmarket. Tomorrow, a gray man who counted coins twice.

Tonight, she walked home through the wet streets of a city that had forgotten something enormous, carrying a stranger's garden against her heart, and if she wept a little at the smell of rain on stone—just a little, just once—she decided that too was professional.

After all, she was in the memory trade.

And somewhere in her empty vault of a past, it was very clearly raining.
