## 5. Collectors at the Threshold

The knock came at the fourth bell, which was wrong in every way a knock could be wrong.

Ines knew the sound of the Ledgerhouse's doors the way other men knew their own heartbeats. The night porters knocked twice, flat and bored. Deliveries came with three sharp raps and a shouted name. Petitioners—the rare, desperate souls who wanted to buy back a childhood or sell away a grief—scratched, hesitantly, like mice inside the walls.

This was five knocks. Evenly spaced. Patient. The kind of knocking that did not need to insist, because it already knew the door would open.

Ines set down his inventory pen mid-stroke. A drop of ink gathered at the nib and fell, blooming on the ledger page like a small black wound, directly through the word *candles*. He watched it spread and thought, with strange clarity: *I will remember this stain. I will use it to know this moment really happened.*

"Keeper Marchetti." The voice of the junior porter, Pell, drifted up the stairwell, thin with nerves. "There are—gentlemen. At the outer gate. They have papers."

Nobody had papers for the Ledgerhouse at the fourth bell. Nobody had papers for the Ledgerhouse at all, not truly; the guild issued its own documents and recognized no others. Papers were a courtesy, and courtesies from strangers were how traps dressed themselves in the morning.

He should have hidden the map first. That was the thought that arrived, cold and complete, as he rose from his desk. He had slept with it against his chest for two nights now, folded into its wrong geometry, tucked beneath his shirt where his heartbeat could warm the paper. It had seemed safe there. Secret things kept close to the body felt safe the way a held breath feels safe—right up until the moment you realize you must eventually exhale.

There was no time. The stairs were already creaking under weight that climbed without hurry.

* * *

They filled the entry hall the way water fills a hull: quietly, completely, from the bottom up.

Six of them in collector grey, standing in the attitudes of men waiting to be announced, except that they had let themselves in and the announcement was a formality they were extending to *him*. Their boots were clean. Their gloves were clean. Everything about them was clean in the particular way of things maintained by an institution rather than a person.

And at their center, examining the entry hall's single lamp with the mild interest of a man appraising a horse he might buy, stood Collector Vane.

Ines had seen him four times in eleven years. Each time had been an audit. Each time, Ines had catalogued him afterward in the private ledger of his mind, because that was how Ines processed the world—by writing it down somewhere no one could take it from him.

*Collector Vane. Age indeterminate; forty-five or sixty, depending on the light. Grey eyes the color of ledger paper before anything is written on them. Voice soft enough that you lean in to hear it, which is, of course, the point. Never raises it. Has never needed to.*

"Keeper Marchetti." Vane inclined his head precisely the depth required between a collector and a keeper—not quite equals, not quite otherwise. "I apologize for the hour. Audits keep their own schedule, I'm afraid. Time is the one commodity this house trades in that we cannot bottle."

"We do trade in it," Ines said. "Borrowed years. Shelf nine."

Something flickered across Vane's face—not a smile, but the place where a smile would be stored. "So we do. You know your stock."

"It's my stock."

"It is the guild's stock," Vane said pleasantly, "which you keep." He let the sentence sit there a moment, then gestured, and one of the grey men produced a document sealed in black wax. "A routine audit of the gate. Its mechanisms, its seals, its charter-compliance. You'll want to see the authorization."

Ines took the paper and read it with the thoroughness he gave everything, because thoroughness was his armor and he had never once been caught without it. The seal was genuine. The language was standard. The date was today.

What was not standard was the timing. Gate audits came once a year, at midsummer, when the solstice residue made the mechanisms easiest to inspect. This was autumn. The gate had been audited in the spring besides—a full audit, clean, filed under his signature.

"This is the second audit this year," Ines said. He heard his own voice and was faintly surprised by it: level, mildly puzzled, the voice of a man with nothing in his chest but ledgers. "Is there a discrepancy in the spring filing?"

"There may be discrepancies anywhere," Vane said. "That is what audits are for. May we proceed?"

*May we proceed.* As if the answer were his to give. As if six collectors in his hall needed permission from the invisible man who dusted their shelves.

"You may," Ines said. "I'll accompany you. The gate requires two keys and a spoken clause; I'm the only living keeper of both."

"I assumed as much." Vane removed his gloves—slowly, finger by finger, an unhurried ritual—and Ines understood it as a message: *we will be here some time, and nothing in this building will remain unexamined.* "Lead on, Keeper. And—" the grey eyes lifted, mild as milk, "—you needn't look so grave. Routine means routine. Unless something has occurred that would make it otherwise?"

"No," said Ines. "Nothing has occurred."

The map burned against his chest like a coal pressed there by someone who hated him.

* * *

The gate corridor ran beneath the Ledgerhouse like a spine of stone, forty paces of descending steps into a chamber where the air itself felt older than the city above. Ines had walked it perhaps ten thousand times. Tonight it felt like walking down his own throat.

Behind him, the collectors' footsteps made almost no sound. That was the training, he knew. Collectors learned to walk the way rumors traveled—present everywhere, attributable nowhere. He listened anyway, counting their steps out of habit, and found himself counting Vane's separately, because Vane's were quieter than silence had any right to permit.

At the bottom, the gate waited in its alcove of black basalt.

Most people who saw it expected a door. It was not a door. It was a doorway-shaped absence, a rectangle of darkness so complete it seemed less like a passage than like a hole cut out of the world, edged in stone carved with script that predated every living alphabet. The guild's founding charters called it *the Threshold*. The oldest marginal note Ines had ever found, in a hand eight centuries dead, called it simply *the Wound*, and had been razored out of its ledger within a generation.

He had opened it twice in eleven years—for the audits—and each time had felt the same thing: a pressure behind his eyes, a sense of being *read*. The gate did not open onto anywhere the guild admitted to. It opened onto a holding-state, a corridor of fog and half-light where nothing lived and nothing decayed, and the charter clauses that governed it were written in a legal dialect so archaic that three keepers had gone blind transcribing them.

"Beautiful, isn't it," Vane said softly. He had come to stand beside Ines, close enough that Ines could smell him—ink, cold iron, and underneath it something faintly sweet, like fruit going over. "I've stood before a hundred gates in my service. Sealed doors, warded doors, doors that eat the unworthy. None of them are as honest as this one. It doesn't pretend to be anything but a hole."

"It's a mechanism," Ines said. "Like any lock."

"Do you think so?" Vane tilted his head. "Then you'll have no objection to a full inspection. Mechanisms can be taken apart."

Ines turned to the gate and began the opening sequence, because refusing would be the loudest confession available to him. Key one from the chain at his belt. Key two from the iron box recessed in the wall, which required his palm pressed flat against cold metal while the box tasted his blood through the skin—he'd never learned how, only that it did. Then the spoken clause, in the old dialect, the words shaped in his mouth like stones:

*"Open for none. Remember for all."*

The darkness in the doorway shivered. Not moved—shivered, the way a sleeping animal shivers when it hears its name.

Behind him, quills scratched. Two of the collectors had produced ledgers and were recording the audit, and Ines kept his back straight and his voice steady through the second clause and the third, reciting the inspection litany he had performed so many times it lived below thought, while his mind ran its own parallel ledger:

*Item: they came off-schedule. Item: Vane asked if anything had occurred before anything had occurred. Item: someone told them something. Item: a man died at our outer gate three nights ago, and I buried him in the coal cellar, and I hid his map against my heart, and I am reciting gate-clauses to the man who would burn me alive for it, and my voice has not shaken once.*

That last item frightened him more than all the others combined. Not the danger—the steadiness. He had spent his whole life believing himself transparent, a grey man in a grey office, incapable of the performances that bolder men wore like coats. And now here he was, lying with every breath, and finding that the lie fit him like his own handwriting.

When had he learned this? Or worse—had he always known, and simply never had anything worth lying about?

"The seals," Vane said. "Show me the third seal."

Ines showed him the third seal. It was intact. Of course it was intact; he had checked it every morning for eleven years, part of a ritual so fixed that he performed it in his sleep, or near enough—Pell had once sworn he'd seen Ines standing before the gate at midnight, hands moving through the inspection gestures with his eyes closed.

Vane examined the seal for a long time. His fingers hovered above it without touching, tracing its circumference in the air, and Ines watched the grey eyes narrow by a degree so small it might have been imagination.

"When was this seal last disturbed?" Vane asked.

"Never. It predates my tenure. It predates the current charter."

"Everything here predates everything," Vane murmured. "That is rather the problem with old houses." He stepped back. "The visitor log, Keeper. For the gate chamber. I'd like to see the last month."

And there it was. The trap, sprung with perfect courtesy.

Because there was no visitor log for the gate chamber. There hadn't been one since Keeper Aldous Rennick's time, forty years ago—Rennick had kept meticulous logs, and then one year the logs stopped, all at once, mid-page, and no successor ever resumed them, and no auditor had ever asked. Ines knew this because Ines knew everything about this house that was written down, and because he had privately always found the missing logs unsettling, a gap in the record like a pulled tooth.

But Vane was asking for a log that did not exist, in a tone that suggested he believed it existed, and Ines understood in one cold flash that this was the test. Not the seal. Not the keys. The log. A guilty keeper would stammer. A guilty keeper would produce something—anything—to fill the silence. A guilty keeper would lie badly, because guilt makes liars of us all and most of us are terrible at it.

Ines said: "There hasn't been a visitor log for the gate chamber since Rennick's administration. Forty years. The practice lapsed and was never reinstated—it's flagged in the 1187 compliance review, appendix C, if you'd like me to fetch it. I've raised it myself, twice, in annual reports. The guild has never seen fit to fund a clerk for it."

Silence. One of the quills stopped scratching.

Vane looked at him for a long moment, and Ines met the look with the expression he imagined a man wears when he has complained about paperwork to a superior and expects sympathy, damn it, sympathy, from someone who understands the burden of institutional neglect.

It was, he reflected distantly, the best performance of his life. He had invented it whole, in the space of a heartbeat, from nothing but the truth arranged in a different order—which, he was beginning to suspect, was what all good lies were.

"Twice in your reports," Vane said at last.

"In 1190 and 1193. Both times acknowledged and deferred." He paused, and then—because he could feel the shape of the moment, feel it like a map's fold lines—added the half-truth he had decided to spend: "There *was* a disturbance at the outer gate recently. A vagrant. Sick, probably dying—I don't know, he was gone by the time the porters reached the door. Pell reported it. I filed it under irregular incidents, form twelve." He shrugged, a small tired shrug, the shrug of a man annoyed at forms. "If that's what's brought you out at the fourth bell, Collector, I wish someone had told me. I'd have had the incident file ready instead of making you wait while I dress."

He watched Vane receive this. Watched the grey eyes move over his face the way an assayer's acid moves over gold—looking for the base metal underneath, the tell, the flinch.

*He knows about the body,* Ines thought. *Or he suspects. Or he knows nothing and this is theater too, and we are two performers each convinced the other wrote the play.*

"He was gone by the time anyone reached the door," Vane repeated.

"So I'm told. I was in the archives. I don't hear the outer gate from the archives." All true. Every word true, merely arranged around the central falsehood like stones around a hollow. "Pell can confirm it. So can the incident file. Form twelve, dated three nights ago, filed the following morning. I can have it copied for you within the hour."

"No need." Vane replaced his gloves, finger by finger, unhurried again, and Ines understood that the examination was over—or paused, which was not the same thing and might never be. "You've been very helpful, Keeper Marchetti."

"I try to keep a tidy house."

"Yes." Vane smiled then, fully, and it was the worst thing Ines had seen in eleven years, because it was warm. "I've heard that about you. *Tidy.* They say nothing in this building escapes your notice. They say you know where every object in the collection has ever been." The smile remained; the eyes did not. "A useful quality. In a keeper. I wonder sometimes where such men put all that knowing. There's so much of it, after all. It has to go *somewhere*."

"In ledgers," Ines said. "That's what they're for."

"Mm." Vane turned to the gate, and for a moment—just a moment—stood before it the way a man stands before a grave he dug himself. Then it passed, so quickly Ines doubted it had been there at all. "Seal it, please. We're finished here."

* * *

He sealed it. He spoke the closing clauses and hung the keys and pressed his palm to the iron box and felt the gate settle back into its long sleep, and the collectors recorded it, and then they went up the stairs, and Ines went last, counting Vane's silent steps ahead of him and hating how easy the counting was, how natural, as though some part of him had been built for pursuit.

In the entry hall, Vane paused at the door and drew something from his coat with two gloved fingers.

"A receipt," he said, "for the audit. Filed properly, of course. You strike me as a man who values proper filing."

He pressed a small card of stiff grey paper into Ines's hand. On it, in ink still wet enough to shine, was drawn a single symbol: a circle, broken at the top, with a line descending through the break like a root growing downward through a wall.

Ines's blood stopped.

He knew the symbol. He had known it since childhood, since a kitchen table and a woman's rough hand guiding his small one through the motion of drawing it—*so you never forget, little one, so you can always find your way home*—a symbol from letters written in a script no living language used, from a grandmother who had burned her papers one winter and never said why.

He had never shown it to anyone. It existed nowhere in the guild's records. It existed nowhere in the world outside his memory and a bundle of his grandmother's letters locked in a drawer in his rooms.

And now it was drying on a collector's card in his hand.

"For your files," Vane said gently. "In case anything else occurs that would make an audit non-routine. You'll know how to reach me."

The door closed behind them. The bolts fell. The lamps guttered in the draft, steadied, burned.

Ines stood alone in the entry hall with the card in his hand and the map burning against his chest, and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only after the danger has passed, that the audit had never been about the gate at all.

Vane had shown him the symbol the way a fisherman shows the water a hook.

And Ines—who had lied well, who had survived, who had performed innocence so cleanly that even he had half believed it—stood in the empty hall and heard his grandmother's voice say *so you can always find your way home*, and realized that the hook was already set, and that the only question left was whether he would run from the man who held the line—

—or follow it wherever it led, and make the man regret ever casting it.

Upstairs, in his desk, the ink was still spreading through the word *candles*.
