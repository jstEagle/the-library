## 7. Borrowed Years

The shop smelled of cedar and cold iron, the way it always did before a storm, and Maren Oduya stood behind her counter counting vials that had not been sold because they could not be sold—memories too tangled, too grief-heavy, too strange for any buyer she'd ever served. Rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere below the street, the harbor groaned against its pilings.

She had not stopped thinking about the coat.

Three days since the collectors came through her door with their polished questions and their ledger of the dead man's face, and three days since she'd lied to them without a single stumble of the heart. *Never seen him. Cartographers don't come to me; they make their own maps of their own heads.* The taller one, Vane, had smiled as if he were thanking her for a gift, and something in his smile had told her he knew she was lying and found it more interesting than offensive.

Which meant the dead man mattered. Which meant his last weeks mattered. And Maren Oduya had built a life on the principle that other people's last weeks were, eventually, for sale.

She pulled down the trade-book from under the counter—the one no guild auditor had ever seen, because it recorded not what she bought but what her clients had tried to buy and failed. Every broker kept one. It was how you learned the shape of a city's hunger: who wanted to forget a wife, who wanted to remember a mother's voice, who wanted twenty minutes of being brave. She ran her finger down the autumn entries until she reached the one she'd half-forgotten, written in her own cramped hand:

*Hallowmas week. Man, fifty-ish, ink-stained fingers, coat good but mended twice. Asked after garden-memories. Not decorative ones. Working ones. Wanted to know if I had anything from Vessany.*

She remembered laughing at him. She remembered the exact quality of the laugh—too quick, pitched to cover the small cold shock of hearing that name spoken aloud in her shop by a stranger. Nobody said *Vessany*. The word didn't exist. She had checked, once, years ago, out of broker's thoroughness: no shipping records, no treaties, no songs, no gravestones. A country that had been subtracted so cleanly that even the subtraction was invisible. And yet this ragged cartographer had walked into her shop and said it like a man asking directions to a village two miles downstream.

*I told him no such stock existed. He paid me anyway—for the looking. Three silver crowns to search my vaults for a thing I knew wasn't there. That kind of money isn't payment. It's desperation wearing a hat.*

Maren closed the book and stood very still in the lamplight.

He had gone somewhere else after her. Brokers talked to brokers the way fishwives talked across stalls—grudgingly, competitively, but they talked—and there were only four in the lower city licensed to handle memory-stock above second grade. If he'd spent everything he had on a garden, someone had sold it to him.

She fetched her cloak.

* * *

Sella Voss kept her brokerage above a chandler's on Rope Walk, and she received Maren the way cats receive other cats: seated, unblinking, tail curled around the possibility of violence.

"Oduya." Sella poured tea neither of them would drink. "You never climb my stairs unless you want something."

"I want a name."

"Names are my whole inventory."

"A client. Hallowmas week or just after. Ink-stained hands. Bought a high-grade memory—a garden, I think. Paid ruinously."

Something moved behind Sella's eyes, quick as a minnow. "Ruinously," she repeated. "Define ruinous."

"Sella."

"He paid everything." Sella set down the pot with a click. "Not everything he carried. Everything he *had*. There's a difference, and I've been doing this long enough to know the difference when I see it walk through my door. He liquidated. Sold instruments, sold a commission, sold—I don't know what all. Came to me with a purse that weighed less than his coat and asked for one specific thing."

"A garden."

"A garden in Vessany." Sella leaned back. "And here's the part you'll want, since you climbed all those stairs in the rain. He didn't ask for it the way buyers usually ask. Buyers say *I want to feel young again*, or *I want my wedding day*, or whatever lie they tell themselves. This man put a drawing on my table. An actual drawing, done in surveyor's ink, of a walled garden with a fountain shaped like a closed hand. And he said: *I need the memory that matches this exactly.*"

Maren felt the hair rise along her forearms. "A memory to fit a map."

"That's what I thought at the time. Madman's errand. But then—" Sella hesitated, and Maren understood she was deciding how much truth to sell and at what price, and then decided, unusually, to give it away free. "Then I found it. Or something near enough. A garden-memory, third-hand, provenance murky, the kind of lot that sits in a vault for decades because nobody can bear it and nobody can use it. Children's voices. Rain on leaves. A woman teaching names. I sold it to him at the price he offered, which was criminal, and I told myself a man that desperate would only spend himself dry on something true."

"And?"

"And six days later he came back." Sella's voice dropped, and for the first time Maren saw genuine disquiet in the woman's face. "He came back and asked to return it."

Maren went cold. "Return it."

"Full refund refused—he didn't want the money, wouldn't hear of it. He wanted the trade undone. Unbought. As if the memory had gotten into him and he needed it *out*, back in the bottle, back in the dark. I told him it doesn't work that way. A memory lived-in can't be re-bottled clean; it comes back changed, it comes back *used*, and no buyer wants used goods. He argued with me for an hour. He wept, Oduya. Grown men weep in my shop weekly—it's practically a service I provide—but he wept like a man at a graveside. And then he left, and he still had it in him, and now he's dead in the canal district with his face on a collector's placard, and you're standing in my shop asking about him, which means the guild is asking about him, which means I am going to pour this tea and you are going to drink it and then leave."

Maren drank the tea. It tasted of nothing, which was appropriate.

"The lot number," she said, rising. "Of the garden-memory. The original bottle. You still have it?"

"It's empty. He took the contents; the glass is mine." Sella paused at the door of her own shop, holding it shut a moment longer than courtesy required. "One more thing, since you'll dig it up anyway. When he argued with me about returning it—do you know what his actual words were? Not *I can't live with it*. Not *it hurts me*. He said: *It isn't mine. None of these are theirs either. They've been taken and sold and taken again, and somebody has to start giving things back.*"

Sella opened the door. Rain came in sideways.

"Madman," she said. But she said it gently, the way you speak of the dead.

* * *

That night Maren opened her private vault.

Every broker had one—a warded cabinet, deeper than the wall it sat in, holding the stock that would never see a counter. Some brokers hoarded it for leverage. Some for sentiment. Maren told herself hers was for study: memories whose provenance was broken, whose ownership was disputed, whose existence raised questions she hadn't yet learned to answer. In practice, she visited them the way other people visited graves.

She lit two lamps and set out the lots one by one on the velvet cloth, and there among them—there, where it had always been, so familiar she had stopped truly seeing it—sat the fragment she'd owned eleven years, bought at an estate auction from a dead importer's effects, catalogued simply as *garden, coastal light, children reciting*.

She had never sold it. She had never been able to say why. Other lots she could price instantly, the way you price bread; this one her mind slid off of, the way a tongue slides off a cracked tooth.

Tonight she did not slide off. Tonight she held it up to the lamp and watched the amber light move through it, and thought about a dying cartographer who had spent everything he had to buy a garden, and then tried with his last strength to give it back—not because it hurt him, but because it *wasn't his*.

*They've been taken and sold and taken again, and somebody has to start giving things back.*

Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; that was the first thing they taught you, before ethics, before law: steady hands or another trade. But her breath had gone shallow, and the shop around her seemed suddenly very large and very dark, full of little glass worlds that belonged to no one.

She pressed the vial to her temple—the old way, the unguilded way, skin to glass, the crude contact that gave you only the edges of a thing, a rumor of the memory rather than the memory itself. Enough to catalogue. Enough to know what you were holding.

Rain on leaves. Salt somewhere far off. Stone warm from a sun that had set forty years ago. And voices—children's voices, chanting in a rhythm that was almost a song, reciting something with the solemnity of scripture, and beneath the chant a woman's voice correcting them gently, patiently, the way you correct children who are memorizing—

Memorizing.

Not prayers. Not lessons. Names. Streets. The color of doors. The order of the tides. Children memorizing a place, syllable by syllable, the way sailors memorize stars—as if the place could be lost, as if it *had* to be carried, as if remembering it were a labor assigned to them, a duty laid on small shoulders before they were old enough to refuse.

Maren lowered the vial. Her heart was behaving badly.

Because here was the thing she had never let herself think in eleven years of ownership: she knew that rhythm. Not the words—the words were in no language she could name, though they sat on the ear with terrible familiarity, like a lullaby heard before speech. But the *rhythm*. The cadence of the chant lived in her the way a heartbeat lives in you, unearned, unexplained, older than any memory she could account for.

She had catalogued this lot as foreign stock. Coastal, probably southern. Curious provenance. And every year since, some quiet clerk in the back of her mind had filed the same objection and been overruled: *you know this. You know this the way you know your own name.*

*Inesio,* whispered a corner of her that was not a clerk at all. *The chant ends with a name. It has always ended with a name.*

She set the vial down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off.

The trade was unfinished. That was what Sella had shown her, whether she meant to or not. The cartographer had understood something the guild's whole machinery was built to prevent anyone from understanding: that a memory could be stolen, and stolen again, and priced and bottled and shelved, and none of it would ever make it *owned*. Ownership was a story brokers told. Underneath the story was a simpler fact—that things taken must someday be returned, and that the returning was not charity but arithmetic, a debt accruing interest in the dark.

He had tried to give it back and died with it still inside him. And somewhere in her vault, in her own skull, in forty-one bottles' worth of the lower city's dusty inventory, the rest of that country waited—disassembled, dispersed, sold off in fragments to strangers who sometimes woke at night with salt on their tongues and no idea why.

Maren looked at the vial on the velvet, at the amber light pooled inside it like the last hour of a summer day.

"Who are you?" she asked it softly. "Whose are you?"

The memory, of course, said nothing. Memories never answered. That was the ache of the trade nobody warned you about at the start—you spent your life handling other people's pasts, and every one of them fit your hand a little too well, and none of them ever told you where they'd been before you got them, or what part of yourself you were really holding when you held them up to the light.

She blew out the lamps. Outside, the rain had thickened toward storm, and the harbor bell counted the hour—third bell, the dead hour, the hour when the Ledgerhouse gate stood open for no one.

But someone had stood at a gate three nights ago, bleeding, pressing a folded paper into a keeper's hands, saying a country's name like a password. Sella's description matched the placard; the placard matched the coat; the coat matched the man who'd emptied his life into a purse to buy back a piece of a place that officially did not exist.

Which meant the map was real. Which meant it was in the keeping of whoever stood behind that gate. And the guild's collectors were already circling the Ledgerhouse like gulls over a gutted fish.

Maren wrapped the vial in wool and put it away, and stood a moment in the dark with her hand flat on the cabinet door, over the lock, over the small cold heart of her hidden stock.

"Wait for me," she told the garden—or the children, or the woman with the patient voice, or whatever part of herself had been chanting in there for eleven years without her knowledge. "I'm coming to find out whose you are."

She took down her cloak again. The storm would cover her footsteps, and the fishmarket opened at fourth bell, and between here and there lay the whole sleeping city—and somewhere in it, a keeper with a map, who had no idea yet that the memory he was carrying had sisters.

She locked the shop behind her and stepped into the rain.
