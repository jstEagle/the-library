## 10. A Trade in the Fishmarket

Maren Oduya had learned long ago that the best place to watch a man was a place where he could not watch you back.

The fishmarket at Brackwater Stairs was perfect for it: loud enough to swallow footsteps, crowded enough to make pursuit invisible, and reeking so thoroughly of brine and blood that no one noticed the smell of fear. She had been coming here for three mornings now, since the collectors came to her shop with their questions and their too-clean boots, asking after a cartographer named Elio Vantre who had, they said, gone missing.

Gone missing. As if a man could bleed out against the Ledgerhouse gate and simply wander off afterward.

She had lied to them beautifully. She always did. *Vantre? He sold me two memories last spring, both mediocre, both paid for. I haven't seen him since.* And they had believed her, or pretended to, because Maren Oduya's lies were currency the guild itself had helped mint—she had brokered for them often enough. But lying well was not the same as being safe, and safety, she had decided over three sleepless nights, now depended on finding whoever had taken Vantre's map before the collectors did.

The man from the gatehouse had been easy to identify. Keepers were rare creatures—the Ledgerhouse kept them like relics, fed and shelved and never seen—and when she described the one who'd answered the door that night to a dockhand who drank at the Salt Cellar, he'd needed only one guess. *Marchetti,* the dockhand said. *Ines Marchetti. Ghost of the guild. You could stand next to him in a corridor and forget him by the time you reached its end.*

Maren had smiled at that, because forgetting things was her trade, and she knew better than anyone that nothing forgotten ever truly left.

Now she watched him come down the stairs into the market, and she understood immediately why the dockhand had used the word *ghost*. Ines Marchetti moved through the crowd the way water moved through cloth—not around people but between their attention, unremarked, unhurried, a thin gray man in a keeper's coat with his hands folded behind his back as though the whole world were an inventory he was quietly taking. His eyes went to the fish, the scales, the ice, the ledger-book a stall-keeper was marking prices in. Never to faces. Especially never to hers.

He stopped at a stall near the low tide line, where the morning's catch lay silvering on crushed ice, and spoke to the vendor about something—herring, perhaps, or the price of salt. Buying dinner. A man buying dinner. It was almost funny how completely ordinary he made himself, and almost heartbreaking, because Maren recognized the craft in it. That wasn't shyness. That was a discipline maintained over decades, a wall built brick by brick until even the builder couldn't see over it.

She gave him another minute. Then she stepped out of the shadow of the awning and stood beside him at the stall.

"Two of those," she said to the vendor, pointing at the herring. "He's paying."

Ines Marchetti turned his head slowly, the way a man turns toward a sound he has already catalogued as harmless and is now revising. Up close his face was longer than she expected, pale from years indoors, with eyes the color of wet slate that took her in with a clerk's precision—coat, gloves, the quality of her boots, the absence of any guild mark. Assessing. Filing. She watched him decide she was nobody, and enjoyed the moment just before he would learn otherwise.

"I don't know you," he said.

"No," Maren agreed. "You don't know very many people. That's rather your signature."

The vendor wrapped the fish and held out his hand. Marchetti paid without looking away from her, which told her something useful: he was rattled. A man truly in control of himself would have looked at the coins.

"Madam," he said carefully, "if this is about guild business—"

"It isn't." She took her parcel of fish and nodded for him to walk with her, along the stalls, toward the stairs where the gulls wheeled and screamed above the gutting tables. "It's about a man who died on your doorstep eight days ago. Elio Vantre. Cartographer. He carried a folded map that doesn't fold right."

Something crossed his face then—quick, quickly buried, but Maren Oduya had spent thirty years reading the small movements of people trying not to remember, and there was no expression in the world she couldn't read. There it was. The flicker of a man touching a coal in his pocket to make sure it still burned.

"I found no one on my doorstep," he said.

"Mm." She walked. He walked beside her, because refusing would have drawn more attention than complying, and he was a man who lived by the arithmetic of attention. "You hid him," she continued pleasantly. "I don't know where—somewhere in the guild's blind corridors, I'd imagine; every old building has them, and keepers know theirs best. You hid the map too. And then, four days later, Collector Vane came to audit your gate, and you performed innocence so well that even Vane half-believed you."

At the name *Vane* his step faltered, just slightly. Just once.

"You've been watching me," he said.

"Since the collectors came to my shop asking questions I didn't want them to have answers to. Yes." She stopped at the head of the stairs, where the harbor opened below them, gray-green and slapping at the pilings, and turned to face him fully. "So let us not waste each other's talents with denials. You have the map. I know what it shows—or what it tries to show. And I am the only person in this city who can tell you what it means."

Below them a boat knocked against the pier. Gulls fought over a gutted basket. Marchetti looked at her for a long moment, and she saw the war behind his eyes: the keeper who wanted to return to his ledgers and his invisibility, and whatever else had woken in him the night a dying man pressed paper into his hands and whispered a word.

"What do you want?" he asked finally.

"A partnership." She said it crisply, because she had rehearsed it, and because rehearsed words were armor. "You have the map. I have access to forty years of brokered memory—every past that's passed through my vault, including several that were bought out of a country that officially does not exist. You cannot read that map alone. Its streets move. Its names are written in a script that died with everyone who spoke it—except that memories remember what maps cannot. I can put you inside the heads of people who walked those streets. People who heard that language sung. People who—" she paused, choosing, "—who planted gardens."

His breath caught. Small, but she heard it.

"And in exchange?"

"The map. Eventually. Or copies of it. Access to whatever the guild's archives hold on the erasure—I know there was an erasure, Marchetti; countries don't fall out of records by accident, someone razored those pages, and someone kept the knife. I want to know who, and why, and what they were afraid of." She held his gaze. "I sell other people's pasts for a living. I would like, once, to buy back one that was stolen from all of us."

For a moment—one unguarded moment—she thought he might say yes. His face did something almost young. Almost hungry.

Then the wall came down again, brick by practiced brick.

"No," he said.

Just that. No apology, no explanation. He shifted the wrapped fish under his arm as if to remind them both that he was only a man running errands, and turned toward the stairs down to the harbor road.

"Inesio."

The name left her mouth before she had fully decided to spend it. She had meant to hold it back—meant to keep it as leverage, a final coin for a later negotiation—but something in his refusal, in the terrible completeness of it, made her angry. Not at him. At the erasure itself, at the neatness of it, at the way it had even managed to erase a boy's own name from his own mouth.

He stopped. He did not turn around. The gulls screamed and the tide slapped the pilings and the fishmarket roared on below them, and Ines Marchetti stood absolutely still on the third stair, as if the name had pinned him there like a specimen to a board.

"Inesio Marchetti," Maren said again, softer now, letting each syllable land. "Of Vessany. Fourth child on the manifest, smuggled out at seven months old, in a crate marked as lamp oil. Your mother's name was Serafina. She handed you over a garden wall to a woman whose face I have seen, though I don't know yet why I've seen it. You weren't born in this city at all. You were born in a country that everyone agreed to forget, and you are the last living proof that the agreement can be broken."

Slowly—he moved as if the air had thickened—he turned around.

Up close, his composure was magnificent and awful. His face had gone the color of the ice under the fish. His hands, folded at his side, had gone white at the knuckles. But he did not deny it. He did not laugh. He did not call her mad. He stood there on the stairs and let the name enter him, and Maren watched it happen: watched a man hear, for the first time in forty years, the sound of his own front door.

"How," he said. His voice came out sanded flat. "How do you know that name."

"Because I remember things I was never given," she said. "Fragments. A garden. Rain on red tiles. A woman singing in a language I don't speak, and understanding her anyway. They've been in my vault for twenty years, sealed, unsold, because I couldn't price them and wouldn't try. I thought they were stock that had spoiled." Her throat tightened, unexpectedly, and she hated it. "I think they may be mine."

"That's impossible."

"Yes," she agreed. "Most true things are. Shall we discuss impossibilities over those herrings, or would you prefer to go home to your ledgers and pretend a stranger didn't just hand you your own name on a public staircase?"

He looked at her for a long time. The crowd parted around them without seeing them—two more bodies at the rail, nothing worth a glance—and Maren saw him register that too, saw him understand that she had chosen this place as deliberately as he chose his own invisibility, that she was, in her way, a professional of the same hidden trade.

"If I say yes," he said at last, carefully, "you'll be in danger. Vane is already watching me. Anyone close to me becomes a thread he can pull."

"Collector Vane has been pulling threads in my life since before you learned to lie to him," Maren said. "I'd rather choose which ones he takes."

Another silence. Below them, the tide was turning; the boats knocked and swung; somewhere a vendor began shouting the evening's prices hours too early. And Ines Marchetti—Inesio Marchetti, fourth child of the manifest, ghost of the guild—looked down at the wrapped fish in his hand as though it were evidence of some crime, and then back up at her.

"Not today," he said. "Not here. If I'm followed—if Vane sees me speaking twice with the same stranger—everything ends before it starts. Give me three days."

"Three days." She weighed it. Every instinct she owned said take the bargain now, nail it shut while the iron was hot. But she had spent a lifetime reading people, and everything in this man said that pressure closed him like a fist and patience opened him like a hand. "Three days. Sundown, the third day, at the Salt Cellar on Tanner's Row. Ask for the back table. If you're followed, don't come in at all—walk past, and I'll find you another way."

"All right," he said. And then, as he turned to go, he paused, and something moved across his face that was not suspicion and not gratitude but some strange compound of both, alloyed with grief. "The woman at the wall," he said quietly. "The one who took me. In your fragment. What did she look like?"

Maren searched the memory—her stolen, inexplicable, unsellable memory—and gave him the truth.

"Tired," she said. "Young, but tired. Dark hair coming loose from its pins. She was crying, but she was smiling too, and she said—" Maren closed her eyes, hearing it again across twenty years and one impossible distance. "She said, *he'll keep the door.*"

When she opened her eyes, Ines Marchetti was gone. Not fled—there had been no hurry in it—but simply absent, dissolved into the crowd the way only he could dissolve, leaving Maren alone at the rail with two wrapped herrings, a pounding heart, and the growing, dreadful certainty that the fragments in her vault were not stock at all.

They were luggage. Someone had packed her past in pieces and shipped it, and somewhere in the guild's archives—in a manifest she had not yet seen but could already feel waiting for her, patient as a trap—someone had written a list of forty-one children, and left one line blank.

She went home the long way, checking her shadows, and did not open the vault until midnight. But that is not what mattered. What mattered was what she wrote in her private book, in cipher, before she slept—a single line, the first entry in a ledger of her own:

*Found the fourth. He answers to a ghost's name. Time to teach him his real one.*

* * *

Three days later, sundown, Tanner's Row, Maren sat at the back table of the Salt Cellar with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door, and Ines Marchetti did not come.

She waited an hour. Two. The tavern filled and emptied around her, and the lamplighter passed the window, and the tide outside turned and turned again. At the third hour she admitted what she had known since the second: he wasn't late. He was hunted. Something had happened at the Ledgerhouse—something with Vane's clean boots and courteous voice—and her three days had just become worth considerably less than she'd paid for them.

She was reaching for her coat when the tavern door opened and a boy came in, dockside ragged, maybe twelve years old, carrying a package wrapped in oilcloth. He scanned the room, found her by some instruction she could guess, and set the package on her table without a word.

Inside the oilcloth: a folded map that did not fold right, a page razored from a shipping manifest bearing a familiar surname, and a note in a small, precise, clerk's hand.

*They burned the annex. I have the ledger of the erased. Trust no one in the guild, including me. — I.M.*

*P.S. Tell me about the garden.*

Maren read it twice. Around her the tavern roared on, warm and oblivious, and outside the window the city went about its evening, fat and comfortable on forty years of forgetting.

Then she tucked the map into her coat, left coins on the table, and went out into the dark to find a man who had finally, at long last, begun to ask the right questions.
