## 12. Memories Not for Sale

The vault beneath Maren Oduya's shop had no lock.

That was the first thing anyone noticed, if anyone was ever allowed to notice it at all. A memory-broker's vault ought to bristle with wards and wax seals, triple-keyed doors, a door that knew its owner's heartbeat the way a dog knows a footstep. Hers had only a plain iron hatch, painted the same gray as the cellar wall, and behind it a room that looked like nothing: bare stone, a single lamp, and forty-one glass vials racked in velvet like a collection of rare insects.

The lock was in the memories themselves.

Each vial in the private rack carried a binding she had written herself, in the old broker's cipher her mother's mother had taught her before the guild standardized everything into legible commerce. The bindings were simple and absolute: *not for sale, not for trade, not for gifting, not for viewing by any hand but mine.* She had renewed them every year on the same night, kneeling on cold stone with a stylus and a pot of silver ink, and every year she told herself the same lie—that she kept them out of professional principle. A broker who sold everything, the saying went, had nothing left to broker with. The vault was inventory. The vault was capital. The vault was *stock*.

She knelt there now, past midnight, with the lamp turned low and the day's terror still humming in her wrists, and admitted to the dark that she had never once believed the lie.

* * *

It had been six days since the cartographer died against the Ledgerhouse gate, five since the collectors came to her shop wearing their courtesy like gloves over knuckles, four since she'd watched Ines Marchetti—the invisible keeper himself, of all people—slip out of the fishmarket with a dead man's map folded against his ribs. Four days of telling herself she would let it go. Let the guild have its secrets. Let the erased country stay erased; erasure was, after all, her trade's oldest customer.

Instead she had spent those four days doing what she always did when a question itched: she worked. She traced the cartographer's last deals through three ledgers and a pawnbroker with loose morals. She learned he had spent his final year buying back what he'd sold, paying ruinous premiums for his own past, vial by vial, like a man bailing out a sinking boat with his cupped hands. And she learned that the last purchase—the garden, the one he'd emptied his accounts for—had never been returned to him. The trade sat unfinished somewhere in the city's gray channels, a debt with no debtor, a memory with no owner.

Which meant, if her arithmetic was right, that the memory of that garden was still *loose*. Unowned. Drifting through the market like a coin dropped down a well.

And Maren Oduya owned a vault full of memories she could not explain.

* * *

She began, as ritual demanded, with the ones she understood.

The first vial held her mother's hands kneading dough, seen from above, from a child's height—flour dusted to the elbows, a wedding band worn thin as wire. Bought legally at an estate sale twenty years ago from a family that wanted the grief gone but couldn't bear to burn it. She had paid nine crowns and never regretted one of them. On bad nights she opened the binding just enough to feel the dough's resistance, the warmth of the oven door, the particular silence of a kitchen where someone loved you without needing to say so. It wasn't hers. She knew it wasn't hers. But she had bought it honestly, and honest things, however borrowed, steadied her.

The second held rain on a tin roof. The third, a dog named something like *Bramble*, its fur the color of wet sand. The fourth was a stranger's wedding, watched from the back row. Small things. Ordinary things. The furniture of other people's lives, which she had spent thirty years selling and thirty years secretly hoarding the pieces too small to fetch a price.

Then she reached the rack's far end, where the velvet dividers stood empty except for seven vials arranged in no order she had ever chosen, and her breath did what it always did—went shallow, went careful, went quiet.

Seven memories with no provenance.

No bill of sale. No estate record. No seller's mark, no broker's chain, no entry in any ledger she kept. They had simply been *there* when she'd inherited the shop from old Sefa Oduya—not her blood, whatever the name said, but the woman who'd raised her and taught her the cipher—and Sefa had died before Maren thought to ask, the way the important questions always arrive one funeral too late.

For years she'd told herself they were stock Sefa had forgotten to log. For years she hadn't opened them. Tonight she took down the first.

* * *

The binding broke like ice on a pond.

Rain. That was the first thing—rain, but wrong, rain falling upward off hot cobbles in the sudden way it does when a storm breaks over a city built on stone that holds the day's heat, and she was standing under an awning watching water leap off the street in silver ropes, and someone beside her was laughing, a woman, young, saying words in a language Maren's mouth almost knew—

Almost. That was the horror of it. Not foreign. *Almost.* Her ear fit the syllables the way a key fits a lock it hasn't turned in decades, and understanding hovered just past the threshold of hearing, teasing, refusing.

The woman laughed again and pulled Maren—pulled the body she was seeing from—out into the rain, and they ran, two girls running through a storm, and the streets around them were narrow and crooked and hung with lamps the color of apricots, and above the rooftops rose a tower with a clock that had no numbers on its face, only symbols, and everyone in this city could read it, everyone but—

The memory ended. They always ended too soon; whoever had cut these fragments had done it with a blunt instrument, sheared edges, no craft.

Maren sat back on her heels with the vial warm in her fist and her heart going like a trapped bird.

Vessany. There was no other explanation, and she hated explanations that arrived whole, unbidden, already dressed for dinner. The map she'd glimpsed in the fishmarket—streets that rearranged, a geography that resisted being fixed—the tower with the numberless clock matched the cartographer's ink strokes exactly, she was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of a face across a crowded room.

She had fragments of Vessany in her vault. Seven of them. Unbought, unsold, unexplained.

Brokers had a term for memories with no provenance. It was not a kind term. *Ghost stock*, they called it—goods lifted from the grieving, skimmed from the sleeping, stripped from children too young to know what was being taken. Every broker swore they'd never touch it. Every broker knew someone who had.

*Sefa,* she thought, and then, because the thought arrived with a taste like copper, *or me.*

* * *

She opened the second fragment standing up, as if the room might be safer viewed from her feet.

A harbor. Boats with high curved prows, painted eyes at the bowline—eyes, actual painted eyes, watching the water so the boats wouldn't get lost. Gulls screaming in that almost-language. A fishmarket, gods help her, a fishmarket laid out nearly identical to the one in this city, stalls and awnings and haggling voices, as if the two markets were sisters separated at birth, and she moved through it in the memory-body of someone tall enough to see over the crowd, carrying a basket, buying eels, arguing cheerfully about the price—

And then the fragment sheared, mid-argument, and the absence after it rang louder than the content. She stood in her cellar holding a dead man's country in a glass tube and understood, with the flat certainty of long experience, that these were not purchased goods. Purchased goods came whole. These had been *cut*—deliberately, crudely, by someone in a hurry, someone who'd taken a life and broken it into pieces small enough to hide.

Someone who'd hidden them inside a child.

* * *

The third fragment she saved for last, though she didn't decide to save it for last; her hands simply refused to reach for it until the others were done, the way a tongue worries every tooth in the mouth before admitting which one aches.

It was shorter than the others. Most of a minute, perhaps less.

A wall.

Not a grand wall—a garden wall, lime-washed, shoulder-high, ivy pulling at its mortar, and beyond it the suggestion of trees going gold with autumn. Evening light, low and honeyed. She was seeing from a height that made no sense until the memory tilted and she understood: someone was lifting her. Arms under her, strong, trembling. A woman's arms.

The woman's face never came fully into view—it was the curse of first-person memory that you cannot see your own self—but Maren saw her jaw, set hard; saw a smear of tears cutting through flour dust on her cheek, flour, *flour*, the same ghost-dust as the kneading-hands in the first vial, and Maren's stomach dropped through the floor of herself; saw the woman swing a small body—younger than a toddler, wrapped against the cold—up and over the wall into waiting arms on the far side, arms belonging to a man whose face was also lost, only his coat visible, dark wool, travel-stained.

And the woman pressed something into the bundle before it went over. A small thing. Flat. Square. A book? A packet of letters?

Then the fragment's final second: the woman's hands, empty now, gripping the top of the wall, knuckles white, watching the bundle disappear down the far side into the dark, and her mouth forming one word that the memory's edge chopped in half—half a word in the almost-language, half a name, and the half sounded like—

Like *Inesio.*

Maren set the vial down very carefully on the stone floor, the way you set down something that might go off.

* * *

She did not cry. She noted, distantly, that this was characteristic. Thirty years of handling other people's grief had taught her the trick of postponement—grief, like stock, could be warehoused. She filed the shaking away for later, some hour when the lamp was out and no ledger needed balancing, and she did what she did instead of feeling, which was to think.

Forty-one children. The number surfaced from nowhere and everywhere—from the shape of the rumors, from the cartographer's desperation, from the way the guild's archives had been razored clean of a whole country. Forty-one children smuggled out before the erasure. She'd heard the figure whispered in the fishmarket like a superstition, like a fairy tale with a body count.

And here, in her vault, was a fragment of one of those crossings. A woman handing a baby over a wall.

Her first thought was the professional one, the safe one: *this is evidence. This belongs with the keeper's documents. This is worth more than I am.*

Her second thought arrived slower and colder and wore no clothes at all:

*Whose arms am I looking out of?*

Because the fragments weren't recorded memories—she knew the difference the way a jeweler knows paste. Recorded memories sat flat in the mind when viewed, observed, secondhand, a window. These *immersed*. These put her behind the eyes. These remembered from the inside, with the body's knowledge—the weight of the basket, the chill of the rain through cloth, the ache in the arms that lifted. You could not buy immersion in someone else's past. Immersion was native. Immersion meant the memory had grown in the skull that now housed it.

These were her own memories. Cut out—or cut *down*—and bound shut in her own cellar, in cipher, by a woman who was not her blood and had died before explaining why.

Sefa had found her somewhere. Sefa had always refused to say where. *Foundling off a boat from the south*, was all she'd ever gotten, and the boat, and the south, and the silence.

Maren looked at the seven vials on their velvet and felt the floor of her identity tilt the way the map's streets had tilted in the fishmarket—familiar shapes sliding into new arrangements, a geography that resisted being fixed. If the fragments were hers, then she had not bought her hoard of ordinary memories to steady herself. She had bought them to *replace* something. To paper over a hole. All those years of small honest purchases—dough, rain, a dog named Bramble—suddenly legible as a woman furnishing an empty house so thoroughly she could pretend the house had always been full.

Her own past might be stolen goods. Might be sitting right here, in her own vault, priced at nothing, marked *not for sale*—by whom? By Sefa? By the person who'd cut it? Had someone stolen her childhood and then, in a fit of conscience or cowardice, locked it in the cellar of the woman who raised her, bound in cipher, waiting thirty years for her to be brave enough or desperate enough to look?

*Not for sale.* She had read those bindings ten thousand times as her own motto, her creed, her pride. Now they read like a cage label. Like a warning nailed to a door from the outside.

* * *

She was still kneeling there when the shop bell above rang—one soft silver note, muffled by floors, impossible at this hour.

Maren went still. Brokers learned stillness the way soldiers learned to sleep sitting up. The bell rang again, patient, polite, and beneath it came the sound she dreaded more than breaking glass: footsteps that did not hurry. Collectors walked like that. Guilty people hurried; the guild strolled.

She banked the lamp, slid the seven vials into the false bottom of her workbasket beneath a layer of cleaning rags, and climbed the cellar stairs with each step placed like a coin into a slot. Through the shop's peeped shutter she saw them: two figures in the street fog, and between them, taller, unhurried, gloved hands folded, a man whose courtesy preceded him into rooms like a draft.

Collector Vane. She'd sold a memory to his lieutenant once; the whole guild spoke of him the way sailors speak of weather.

They were not looking at her shop. That was the strange mercy of the moment—they stood across the lane, faces tipped up toward the fog, toward the distant bulk of the Ledgerhouse hill, and Vane was speaking quietly to his men, and though she caught no words, she caught the gesture: one gloved finger drawing a slow circle in the air. *Everything. Sweep everything.*

The sweep was coming toward the fishmarket quarter. Toward brokers. Toward anyone who might hold a piece of a country that officially did not exist.

Maren stepped back from the shutter, heart hammering against the workbasket on her hip, against the seven small truths rattling inside it. She thought of the keeper—Marchetti, Inesio, whatever his true name was—hiding in his guild with a dead man's map, thinking himself alone with the secret. He wasn't alone anymore. He had never been alone. Somewhere in her skull, in fragments, in a wall-top word chopped in half, she was carrying the other half of everything he knew.

The knock, when it came, was gentle. Three raps. Patient.

Vane's voice through the door, warm as a banked fire: "Madam Oduya. Forgive the hour. We're conducting a small audit of the neighborhood—and yours is, I'm told, the finest memory-shop in the quarter."

Maren looked down at the workbasket. At the rags. At the vials underneath, glowing faintly, the way truth glows when you finally hold it up to a lamp.

*I'm coming to find you, keeper,* she thought, with a calm that surprised her. *But first I have to survive tonight.*

She smoothed her apron, arranged her face into the smooth professional mask that had fed her for thirty years, and opened the door.
