## 13. Vane's Invitation

The summons came the way all of Vane's communications did: precisely, quietly, and at a time calculated to make waiting impossible.

It arrived as a card slipped under the door of my quarters sometime before dawn—thick cream stock, no seal, no signature. Only a time and a place. *The Long Room. Ninth bell.* And beneath that, in handwriting so controlled it looked printed: *Come alone. Come rested.*

I held the card for a long while in the grey light, turning it over as though the back might confess something the front withheld. It didn't. Collector Vane did not waste ink on reassurance.

*Come rested.* As if rest were a thing I could manufacture on command. I had not slept properly since the night the cartographer died against my gate, since I'd hidden his body beneath the coal cellar floor and buried his map inside the lining of my coat where it had lived against my skin ever since, warm as a second heartbeat. Nine days now. The map had stopped feeling like paper and started feeling like an organ—one I was smuggling inside myself, and which my body had begun, treacherously, to accept.

I dressed with care. That was the first rule of standing before Vane: never give him a wrinkle to read. I buttoned my collar to the throat, combed my hair flat, and put on the face I had spent thirty years perfecting—the face of a man who catalogued wonders without ever wondering about them.

Then I went down through the sleeping Ledgerhouse to meet the man who wanted my soul, and was prepared to offer me a very fair price for it.

* * *

The Long Room sat at the heart of the guild's administrative wing, a chamber built for negotiations too delicate for the counting floors. Its walls were lined with ledgers from the founding era, their spines bleached to the color of old teeth, and its single table was carved from a wood no one could name anymore—which was, I understood now, rather the point of everything the guild touched.

Vane was already there, of course. He stood at the far end of the room with his back half-turned, studying a ledger case as if it contained something more interesting than me. He always gave you that first moment of being unobserved—a small mercy, or a small cruelty, depending on what he meant to do with it.

"Keeper Marchetti." He turned. He was a spare man, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that aged like architecture—nothing soft ever having been permitted to form. "Thank you for coming."

"You left me little choice, Collector."

"I leave everyone little choice. It saves time." He gestured to the table, where two chairs faced each other across a distance of perhaps four feet. On the table between them sat a decanter of amber liquor and two glasses. Two. He had counted us both before I arrived. "Sit, please."

I sat. He poured—two fingers each, no more—and pushed one glass toward me with two fingertips, like a man feeding a document through a slot.

"I'll come to it directly," he said, settling into his chair. "You've been with the guild thirty-one years. Entered as a foundling at age nine. Sworn at fourteen. Never once flagged for irregularity in three decades of audits." He recited it the way I might recite an inventory—not because he needed reminding, but because he wanted me to hear how well he knew me. How completely. "Do you know how rare that is? A keeper with no flags?"

"I try to be unremarkable."

"Oh, you succeed magnificently." There was no mockery in it. That was somehow worse. "Invisibility is an art, Marchetti, and you are one of its masters. Which is precisely why the council has been watching you for promotion."

My glass stopped halfway to my mouth. "Promotion."

"Chief Keeper of the Ledgerhouse." He said it plainly, without ceremony, the way a man announces a change in shipping schedules. "Old Corvane retires at midwinter. The vote is nearly settled. Your name leads the list."

The room seemed to tilt slightly, the way a ship adjusts beneath your feet. Chief Keeper. I had spent my whole life arranging myself into the shape of a man who would never be noticed, and here was the guild offering to hand me the keys to its memory-vaults, its sealed annexes, its—

Its archives. Where every page about Vessany had been razored out by careful hands.

"The honor is unexpected," I said carefully.

"It shouldn't be. You're the best of them. You keep the gate flawlessly, you ask no questions, you want nothing." He lifted his own glass but did not drink. "Which brings me to the matter at hand. Because there is one item outstanding. One small irregularity in an otherwise spotless record."

Here it was. I felt the map shift against my chest—not physically, of course; it was folded in my quarters, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard, exactly where a careful man would keep it. But I felt it anyway, the way an amputee feels a missing limb. Guilt has excellent recall.

"Irregularity?" I let a note of mild puzzlement into my voice. Not too much. A perfectly blank response would read as rehearsed.

"A man died at your outer gate nine nights ago," Vane said. "A cartographer named Aldous Ferren. His body has not been recovered. His effects have not been logged. And you, Keeper, were the last person recorded entering the gatehouse that night."

Every clock in the world seemed to slow. I made myself take a sip of the liquor—it was very good, smoky and old—and set the glass down with a steady hand.

"I found him dying," I said. "He pressed something into my hands and said a word I didn't understand. Then he was gone. I reported nothing because there was nothing to report—a dead vagrant at the gate is a matter for the city watch, not the guild. I assumed he was a beggar who'd mistaken our door for a hospice."

"What did he press into your hands?"

"A scrap of paper. Wet with blood. Unreadable. I burned it."

Vane watched me for a long moment. His eyes were pale grey, almost colorless, and they did not narrow or widen or do any of the things eyes do when they catch a lie. They simply received me, the way a ledger receives an entry.

"Burned it," he repeated.

"In the gatehouse stove. Ashes and all."

"And the word he spoke. You didn't understand it, you say."

"No."

Something moved behind his face then—not suspicion, but something older and sadder. He rose, crossed to the ledger case against the wall, and drew out a slim volume bound in black calfskin. When he returned to the table, he did not open it. He simply laid his hand flat upon it, the way a priest lays a hand on a scripture.

"Then let me tell you a story, Keeper," he said. "Since you didn't understand the word, you deserve to understand it now. The word was *Vessany*. And the country it names was destroyed forty years ago this winter."

"Destroyed," I said. "You mean it fell? War, plague—"

"I mean it was erased. Deliberately. From maps, from records, from memory itself. Every ledger in this building that mentioned it was razored clean. Every mind that carried it was emptied and patched. Forty years ago, Vessany existed. Today, if you asked the wisest historian in this city, he would tell you there has never been such a place, and he would believe it absolutely, because we made certain of it." He paused. "We. The guild. This is the great work no chief keeper inherits until the day he must. The erasure was ours."

The fire in the grate popped. Somewhere above us, a clerk's footsteps crossed the ceiling, ordinary as rain.

"Why," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, flattened, as though it had been passed through one of the guild's memory-filters.

"Because Vessany was dying of remembering." Vane finally drank, a small precise swallow. "Not a plague of the body. A plague of the past. The country had developed—no one knows how—the practice of keeping memory outside the mind. Bottled, traded, hoarded, inherited. Whole families lived inside their ancestors' recollections. Children were born already grieving wars their grandmothers had sold them. The dead refused to stay dead because everyone remembered them too vividly. Grief became a currency, and like all currencies it inflated until it crushed the people holding it." He set the glass down. "By the end, Vessanyans were drowning in their own pasts. They couldn't walk through their capital without weeping. The guild offered a mercy: forget. All of it. The country, the name, the grief. A clean page."

"And the people?"

"Some accepted. Some were collected. Some—" his voice did not waver, but it thinned, stretched over something — "some were children, who were given no vote, because children never get votes. Forty-one of them were smuggled out before the final sealing and scattered across the world with new names. The operation was imperfect. Traces remain. Men like Ferren, who helped perform the erasure and spent his last years trying to undo it, out of guilt or madness—I've never determined which. He was trying to reopen the gate. To bring it all back. The grief, the ghosts, the plague of remembering." He leaned forward, and for the first time his composure cracked just enough to show me the load-bearing structure beneath it. "Keeper, if that gate opens, forty years of peace comes apart. Every family in this city that was quietly healed of an unbearable loss will have that loss handed back to them, gift-wrapped, still bleeding. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

I understood. That was the horror of it. I understood him completely, and some cold practical chamber of my heart even agreed with him—and underneath that agreement, underneath the whole persuasive architecture of his story, ran a single thread of knowledge he could not see: *he is lying, or he is lying to himself, because men who erase countries do not do it as surgeons. They do it as thieves.*

But I said none of that. I said, "What does this have to do with me?"

Vane opened the black calfskin ledger at last, turned it toward me, and slid it across the table.

It was a personnel record. Mine. Every posting, every audit, every commendation—and at the bottom, in fresh ink, a draft appointment: *Ines Marchetti, Chief Keeper of the Ledgerhouse, effective midwinter.* Beside it, in Vane's hand, a single condition had been written and then struck through, struck so thoroughly the words beneath were only a shadow of shadows. But I have spent my life reading damaged documents, reading what remains around a removal, and I reconstructed it in half a heartbeat:

*...contingent upon recovery of the Ferren map.*

"The offer stands regardless," Vane said, watching me read. "But the council will want the irregularity closed before the appointment is sealed. Bring me the map—or bring me proof it never existed—and you will be chief keeper by solstice. Full access. The sealed annexes. The founding vaults. Everything a man of your gifts deserves." He closed the ledger gently, reverently, as if it were a door closing on a cell whose occupant had finally agreed to behave. "Thirty-one years of invisibility, Marchetti, and I am offering to make you the most visible man in the guild. Safety. Standing. A life where no one will ever again look through you."

He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; he knew silence did his work for him.

And God forgive me, part of me *wanted* it. That was the monstrous genius of the offer—that it was genuinely generous. Thirty-one years of being furniture, of being the man clerks forgot to greet, of eating alone in a gatehouse while the guild's real life happened in rooms I was never summoned to—and here was Vane, offering me the opposite of all of it, wrapped in the language of justice. *You deserve this.* The four most dangerous words in any negotiation.

Temptation wears the face of safety. No one warns you that safety can feel like being loved.

"I'll need time to consider," I heard myself say.

"Of course." Vane rose, and I rose with him. He extended his hand, and I took it; his grip was dry and cool and exact. "Nine days, Keeper. Consider until solstice eve. But consider this as well." At the door, he paused, and did not turn around. "Ferren bled on your threshold. Blood leaves traces in a gatehouse older than this guild. We will find what we find. The only variable is whether we find it beside a loyal keeper—or beside a thief."

Then he was gone, taking the black ledger with him, and I stood alone in the Long Room with two untouched glasses of liquor and the echo of my own heartbeat, which sounded, for the first time in nine days, less like fear than like a countdown.

* * *

I did not go back to my quarters. A watched man returns to his hiding places; a clever man goes somewhere else entirely.

Instead I walked the long way through the blind corridors—the ones behind the shelving, built by some early keeper with a smuggler's heart—and let my feet carry me down to the archive's lowest tier, where the oldest ledgers sleep in their cases like reliquaries. I told myself I was going to check the manifest again, the one bearing my surname, the crack in the wall I'd found ten days ago and hadn't dared look at since.

But standing before that case, I found my hands wouldn't move. What filled them instead was Vane's voice. *Forty-one children, scattered with new names.*

Forty-one.

I thought of the razored pages. Of the shipping manifest with *Marchetti* written in a clerk's careful hand, dated forty years ago, in the season of the erasure. Of my grandmother, who never once in her long life told me where she came from, and who taught me, before she died, a strange habit I had always taken for eccentricity: *Never write down what you cannot bear to lose, Ines. Keep it in your blood.*

She had said it like a woman quoting scripture. Like a woman passing along instructions.

I stood in the dark among the sleeping ledgers and felt the shape of the trap close around me—not Vane's trap, which was crude and visible, but the older one, the one laid before I was born. Vane had offered me everything I had ever been denied, and in doing so had shown me exactly what I was worth to the guild: the price of my silence, itemized, generous, and final.

Chief keeper by solstice. Or a corpse in a coal cellar by spring.

There was a third option, of course. There is always a third option; the ledgers taught me that—every transaction has a margin, and margins are where the truth lives. But the third option required the one thing I had spent thirty-one years avoiding.

It required me to stop being invisible.

Above me, faint through the floors, the ninth bell finished tolling, and somewhere in the administrative wing a clerk was already drafting the announcement of Old Corvane's retirement, and somewhere in the city a woman I hadn't yet met was selling other people's memories out of a shop that smelled of rain.

Eleven days, Vane had said. He was wrong. I counted differently now.

Ten.
