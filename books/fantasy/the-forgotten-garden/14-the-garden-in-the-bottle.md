## 14. The Garden in the Bottle

The vault beneath Maren's shop had no lock that a thief would recognize. It opened to a question, spoken aloud, and the question changed every season. This winter it was: *What did you refuse?* She stood before the door with her palm flat against the cold iron and asked it, and the door considered her answer—the same answer she had given for eleven years—and let her in.

Inside, the shelves were not shelves of goods. Every broker kept a vault of stock: bottled first kisses, bottled griefs, the taste of a language learned in childhood, the specific weight of a father's hand on a shoulder. Maren's stock was excellent. Maren's stock was famous, in the narrow and disreputable way such things were ever famous.

But this room—this small room behind the stockroom, walled in lead and salt—held the memories she would not sell. Not at any price. Not when the Duke of Corvess had offered her a house. Not when the memory-brokers' syndicate had offered her a seat she'd spent her whole career pretending not to want.

There were nine vials on the shelf. She knew each one the way other people knew their scars.

And one of them was Vessany.

She had never been able to explain it. That was the truth she circled and never landed on. Brokers could trace provenance the way jewelerers traced stones—who had sold it, who had bought it back, whose sweat was on the glass. But this vial had come to her fourteen years ago in a crate of ordinary stock from a liquidator in the Salt Quarter, unlabeled, unpriced, and when she had uncorked it once—only once, in the dark, with wax in her ears as if that could help—she had seen rain falling on a garden wall, and she had heard children reciting something in a language she did not know and yet understood, and she had wept for an hour without knowing for whom.

She had tried to sell it afterward. That was the professional thing. An unlabeled memory of unknown origin was a liability; you either catalogued it or you moved it. Three times she had set a price. Three times, reaching for the ledger to write it down, her hand had stopped as though the ink itself had frozen.

So it lived here, among the refused. And now she was going to sell it anyway.

Maren stood in the doorway a long moment, listening to the salt walls hum their faint dry hum, and told herself the lie she had prepared: *This is different. This is not selling. This is placing it where it belongs.*

It was even mostly true. That was what made it bearable.

* * *

Ines Marchetti came to the shop at dusk, hooded, moving like a man who had learned to be furniture. She watched him cross the fishmarket's edge through her front window—the way he checked reflections without seeming to, the way his stride shortened near the corner where collectors liked to smoke. He had gotten better at this in the two weeks since the audit. Gotten worse, too. There was a new tension in him, a wire pulled tight, and wires snapped or sang.

She had named him in the fishmarket. *Inesio Marchetti of Vessany.* She still didn't know why she'd done it—whether it was leverage, or cruelty, or the simple unbearable pressure of carrying a secret that wasn't hers. All three, probably. A broker's honesty about herself was a commodity she rarely stocked.

He knocked the pattern she'd given him: twice, pause, once. She opened the door before the second knock finished, because standing in a doorway in this district was standing in a shooting lane.

"Inside. Quickly."

He slipped past her smelling of rain and lamp oil and, faintly, of paper—the Ledgerhouse clung to him like a scent he couldn't wash off. Upstairs, in the shop proper, among the labeled bottles and the brass scales and the little velvet trays where memories lay like unset gems, he stopped and looked around with the expression of a man entering a temple of a god he didn't believe in.

"You said you had something," he said. "You said it would cost everything I have."

"I said you should bring everything you have." She latched the door, drew the curtain, lit the good lamp—the one with the shuttered flame that wouldn't bleed light into the street. "Whether I take all of it depends on how much there is."

"It isn't much."

"Then we'll find out what your life has been worth." She said it flatly, professionally, and hated herself a little for how much she enjoyed the flinch. Old habits. In the trade, you established price by establishing need, and need began with shame. "Sit. And before we begin—you understand what you're asking for?"

"A memory of Vessany."

"A memory *from* Vessany. There's a difference, and the difference is the price." She sat across from him, folded her hands. "Memories aren't pictures in a bottle, keeper. They're rooms. When you open one, you live in it. For a minute or an hour, depending on the depth, you are inside someone else's skull wearing their eyes, and when it ends you come out carrying whatever they carried. If the memory holds grief, you will grieve. If it holds terror—" She shrugged. "Most brokers don't warn clients. The warning lowers the price. I'm telling you because you'll pay more knowing, and because I've seen men walk out of deep dives and never quite come all the way back."

"Whose memory is it?" he asked. "Where did you get it?"

And there it was—the question she had dodged for fourteen years, now sitting between them like a debt collector.

"I don't know its provenance," she said. "That's the truth, and it's the reason the price will be strange. Unlabeled stock is dangerous stock. It came to me years ago in a mixed lot. I catalogued everything else. This one I couldn't."

"Couldn't or wouldn't?"

"Is there a difference that matters to you tonight?"

He looked at her for a long moment. He had a way of looking that she suspected had made him an excellent keeper—patient, total, missing nothing and accusing nothing. She found she disliked being looked at that way and disliked more how much she didn't dislike it.

"No," he said finally. "Show me."

* * *

She took him down to the vault because the vault was where the ritual lived, and rituals were half of any trade worth making. She did not tell him that this room held things she'd refused to sell, or that he was the first living soul besides herself to stand inside it. Some information was not leverage. Some was just a wound you showed people so they'd trust you with theirs. She wasn't ready to show that wound yet. Maybe after. Maybe never.

The vial sat on its shelf in a cradle of black felt, smaller than a sparrow's egg, the glass dark with a sediment that moved when you weren't watching it directly. Good memories settled. Deep ones swam.

Ines reached for it and she caught his wrist—not hard, but fast, faster than she'd meant to.

"Rules," she said. "You sit. You hold it in both hands. You uncork it yourself—I can't do it for you, the memory takes the opener first, always, and I am not going into your head tonight. When it releases you—and it will release you, they always do, but it will feel like drowning feels, like the surface is a rumor—you say my name. Maren. Say it until I answer. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"Say it now, so it's already in your mouth."

"Maren."

"Good." She let go of his wrist. Her hand stayed suspended in the air a half-second longer than it needed to, and she turned away to hide whatever her face was doing. "One more rule. Whatever you hear in there—whatever voice—it isn't yours. Remember that. People come out of dives believing they've recovered something. They haven't. They've borrowed. Borrowed things must go back, or they rot in you like meat in a hot room."

"What happens if it rots?"

"You become a person who remembers something that never happened to them. There are whole madhouses full of them." She set the vial in his palms. It was warm. She had never known it to be warm. "I'll be counting your breath. If it goes past four hundred, I pull you out with the tongs and you'll hate me forever. Fair?"

"Fair," he said, and uncorked it.

* * *

Maren had witnessed a hundred dives. She knew the choreography: the pupil widening until the iris drowned, the slackening of the jaw, the small animal shiver that ran down the spine when the self stepped out of its own house. She counted his breaths the way she'd promised—one, two, three, steady, then quickening—and she watched his face the way she watched all her clients, reading the memory secondhand off his features.

Except that within thirty seconds she realized she was not reading it secondhand.

His lips parted. His brow creased. And he began, very softly, to move his mouth in time with words—to *recite*—and though no sound came out, she recognized the shape of the syllables. She had heard them once before, fourteen years ago, alone in this room with wax in her ears that had done nothing at all.

Rain on a garden wall. Children in rows. A voice like a bell saying: *again, again, from the beginning.*

Her hand found the table's edge. She gripped it and kept counting. Forty. Sixty. Ninety. His breathing went ragged at one hundred and twenty, and tears slid down his face without his expression changing at all, which was the worst thing, the thing she'd seen only in divers who had gone deeper than the memory they'd bought—into whatever waited underneath it, in the silt of themselves.

Two hundred. Two hundred and fifty. At three hundred his lips formed a word over and over, and this time sound came with it, barely, a thread of it:

"—amma. Amma. Amma—"

Not a word she knew. A name, maybe. Or a country's old word for something. She leaned close enough to hear it properly and the hair rose on her arms, because the shape of it was familiar in her own mouth, the way a stair you climbed as a child is familiar to your feet decades later.

Three hundred and forty. Three hundred and sixty.

"Ines," she said sharply. "Come up. Come up now."

His eyes flew open.

For a moment he did not see her. She watched him arrive in the room the way a diver surfaces—first the confusion, then the pain of light, then the terrible reassembly of the face into something presentable. He was holding the empty vial so hard she could hear the glass strain.

"Maren," he said. Obediently. As instructed. Then, wrecked and quiet: "She was singing. She was—there was a garden, and the children were learning the country, all of it, names and roads and songs, so that if it was erased it would still exist somewhere, in them, and she was teaching me the word for home and I—" His voice failed. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I know her voice. How can I know her voice? I was an infant. I was smuggled out in a basket. I shouldn't—"

"Borrowed things feel owned," Maren said gently. "That's the trap. That's always the trap."

But even as she said it, the professional lie curdled in her throat, because she had heard that voice too. Fourteen years ago. Before she had ever met Ines Marchetti, before the cartographer died at the guild gate, before any of it—she had heard a woman singing in a garden in a bottle, and she had wept, and she had never known for whom.

Unless.

Unless the crate from the Salt Quarter had not been a mixed lot. Unless the liquidator had been a fence for stolen goods, and the stolen goods were people, and somewhere in the arithmetic of forty-one children smuggled out of a dying country, one child's memory had been split from the rest like a share of plunder, and had drifted, unlabeled, into the hands of a girl brokering other people's pasts to buy her own future—

No. She shut the thought. Not now. Not while he was still surfacing, still raw, still looking at her with the nakedness of a man who had just heard his mother sing.

"The dive went long," she said instead, steadying her voice with craft. "Deeper than the depth I priced. Which means the price changes."

He laughed—a cracked, astonished sound. "Of course it does."

"Not for the reason you think." She took the empty vial from his hands, carefully, prying finger by finger. "A shallow dive costs what the memory costs. A deep dive costs what it *did* to you. You went under to the bottom, keeper. You heard what you heard because the memory wanted to give it to you—or because you wanted it so badly the memory had no choice. Either way, you received more than merchandise. You received—" She hesitated. "A inheritance."

"And the fee for an inheritance?"

"Grief," she said. "Paid in advance, actually. You've already paid most of it. I watched." She stood, needing to move, needing not to be looked at. "The rest you'll pay in coin. Everything you brought. I warned you."

He dug in his coat and produced it without haggling, which told her more than any interrogation could have: a keeper's savings, hoarded across years of invisible labor, counted out on her table in careful stacks. Rent money. Old-age money. The entire material proof of a life built to leave no trace, surrendered in eleven minutes of silence.

When he finished, he sat looking at his empty hands.

"It was real," he said. "The garden. It wasn't a fabrication, wasn't a composite. I know fabricated memories—I've catalogued thousands. Fabrications are smooth at the edges. This had—" he searched, "—seams. Real seams. Rain that fell wrong. A bird that interrupted the lesson and got scolded. Nobody fabricates a scolded bird."

"No," Maren said quietly. "Nobody does."

"There were other children. Dozens. Memorizing streets, harvests, the names of the dead. Like—they were the archive. Living ones. If the country was erased from paper, it would survive in them." He looked up at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "That's why the guild erased it. Not plague. Not war. They erased a country that had made itself impossible to erase, and the only way left was to scatter the archive. To scatter the *children*."

The room was very quiet. On the shelf behind her, eight refused memories sat in their felt cradles, and the ninth cradle was empty, and Maren thought: *I sold the one thing I never sold, and I still cannot say whether I robbed him or returned something.*

"Inesio," she said—his true name, the one she'd thrown at him in the fishmarket like a stone, and which she now handed over like a key. "Your mother's song. Do you remember the words?"

"Some. Not all. It's already fraying." Panic flickered under the clarity. "How do I keep it? Tell me how a broker keeps what she keeps. You have a vault. I've seen your prices; you must have methods—"

"We write them down," she said. "Immediately. Before sleep. Before conversation. Words first, then melody, then everything else. Memory survives transcription better than it survives treasuring." She pushed paper and ink across the table. "Write. Now. While it's wet."

He wrote. She watched him fill page after page in the keeper's neat, cramped hand—streets, harvests, the scolded bird, the word *amma*, the melody rendered in the only notation he knew, which was wrong and which she corrected line by line from her own fourteen-year-old recollection, and the moment she sang the first phrase aloud to check his notes, he stopped writing and stared at her.

"You know it," he said. "You know the song."

"I told you. I've heard the memory before. Tonight wasn't the first dive."

"When?"

"Fourteen years ago. The day it arrived." She should stop. Every instinct of survival said stop. But he was looking at her with his mother's country in his face, and lying to him suddenly cost more than the truth. "Ines, I need to tell you something, and I need you to stay calm, because I have not stayed calm about it for fourteen years and one of us should."

She told him. The crate. The unlabeled vial. The rain on the garden wall, the children reciting, the hour of weeping with no name attached. The three times she'd tried to price it and three times her hand had stopped. The vault of refused things. The fact that he was the first person ever to enter that room.

"So it was mine," he said slowly. "Somehow. Before it was ever yours. My mother's memory, or a piece of it, and it traveled from Vessany to the Salt Quarter to your shop, and sat on your shelf for fourteen years waiting for—"

"Don't," Maren said. "Don't say *waiting*. Things don't wait. Things drift. Meaning is what people like us sell; we don't get to believe in it ourselves." But her voice shook on the last word, and they both heard it.

He leaned forward. "Maren. If my mother's memory could reach your shelf—what else reached it? What else did the erasure shake loose? Where did the crate come from? Who was the liquidator? Because if pieces of Vessany have been drifting through this city for fourteen years, then the country isn't gone. It's *dispersed*. And dispersed things can be gathered."

She stared at him.

Because that was the thought she'd been refusing for fourteen years, the thought under the thought, the reason her hand had frozen over the ledger three times: not that the memory was inexplicable, but that it was *familiar*. The rain had fallen the way rain falls in a place you've been. The children's language had sat in her mouth like a stair under her feet.

And there was one more thing, one thing she had never told anyone, one thing she had buried so deep even she rarely visited it: the annex ledger listed forty-one children. Forty destinations recorded. One line blank.

She had read that ledger in his company two nights ago. She had said nothing.

"Ines," she said, and her voice came out strange, foreign, like a memory surfacing. "Before we gather anything. Before we plan another step. There's something in that ledger I need to show you again. Line forty-one."

He frowned. "The blank one?"

"The blank one," Maren said. "I think I know why it's blank. I think the destination wasn't recorded because the child was never sent anywhere." She swallowed. "I think she was kept. I think she grew up in this city, trading in other people's pasts, and I think—" Her hand, without her permission, rose to the empty cradle on the shelf. "I think I know her name."

Outside, the first bell of night rang across the rooftops, and neither of them moved.
