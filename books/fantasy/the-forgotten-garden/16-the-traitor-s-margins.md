## 16. The Traitor's Margins

Maren Oduya had spent twenty years learning to read hands the way other people read faces.

A hand betrayed everything. The broker who wrote his contracts in a fist so tight the ink pooled at every stroke was a man who feared loss. The widow who signed her memory-deeds with letters that trailed off at the ends, unfinished, was a woman already halfway out of her own life. Maren could look at a page and tell you whether its writer had been hurried or certain, grieving or greedy, young or old, honest or merely careful. It was the one skill the guild had never thought to tax.

So when she spread the annex ledger across her worktable at the third hour of morning, with the shutters drawn and the shop's bell silenced with wax, she did not read the names first.

She read the hand.

Forty-one entries. Forty-one children, each listed with a date of removal, a destination coded in guild cipher, and a single word in the margin that might have been a blessing or a curse: *kept*. The ink had gone brown with age, but the strokes were still legible, still alive somehow, the way good handwriting always seemed to be. And they were good. Confident without arrogance. Economical without coldness. A hand that had been trained in the old scriptorium style—she recognized the flourishes on the ascenders, the particular way the letter *s* doubled back on itself like a man glancing over his shoulder—and then had been used for decades by someone who no longer cared about beauty, only about getting the truth down before it could be taken from him again.

She knew this hand.

She had known it since she was nine years old, sitting cross-legged on the floor of her mother's stall in the fishmarket, practicing her letters on scraps of vellum discarded by the great houses. Her mother had kept a specimen book—a broker's primer, really, pages of famous signatures and contract-hands collected over a lifetime of deals—and Maren had memorized them the way other children memorized songs. There was one signature in that book that appeared more often than any other, because half the guild's founding documents bore it, because every apprentice learned to recognize it as a warning as much as a name:

*Aurelian Vess.*

Founder of the Ledgerhouse. Author of its charter. The man whose portrait hung in the council hall with scales in one hand and a closed book in the other—the closed book being, according to legend, the original ledger from which all others descended.

The man who, if the histories were to be believed, had built the machine that erased Vessany.

And here was his hand, forty years after his death, writing *kept* beside the name of a smuggled child.

Maren sat very still. The candle guttered. Somewhere below the street, a cart rattled past carrying ice, and the sound of it seemed to come from very far away, as though the room had been lifted out of the world.

Because there were two ways to read what lay on her table, and both of them broke something.

The first way: Vess had written the annex himself, which meant the founder of the erasure had also been its record-keeper, which meant he had stood in the burning heart of the thing he built and written down every child he failed to save—or every child he saved, depending on how you read the word *kept*—and then hidden the record where no one would find it for four decades. A dissenter at the center. A traitor in the margins of his own crime.

The second way was worse. The second way was that the hand was not Vess's at all, but a forgery so perfect that even Maren's eye—which had never once been wrong about a hand—could not find the seam. And if someone could forge Aurelian Vess convincingly enough to fool her, then the annex itself might be bait, and Ines Marchetti might be walking into a trap dressed as a birthright.

She turned back to the first page and made herself look properly this time, not at the names but at the pressure of the pen, the hesitations, the places where the nib had caught and dragged. Forgers were smooth. That was their flaw. They reproduced the shapes of a hand but not its weather—not the tremor of the day the writer's fingers ached, not the sudden haste where he'd heard footsteps in the corridor, not the small human stumbles that no forger thought to include because no forger understood that imperfection was the truest signature of all.

There, on the fourth entry. A blot where the pen had rested too long, the ink blooming outward like a bruise. Someone had stopped mid-word and sat thinking. Beside the third name—*Marchetti, Inesio, infant, destination withheld*—the margin note was different from all the others. Not *kept*. Something else, smaller, pressed hard into the paper as though the writer needed the words to survive even if nothing else did:

*the gate will remember him.*

Maren read it three times. Then she did something she had not done since childhood, since the last time the world had shown her its teeth: she laughed. One short, unlovely bark of a laugh, there in the shuttered dark, at the sheer audacity of history. The founder of the guild—the guild that erased countries, that sold forgetting by the ounce—had been keeping score against himself all along.

Her mother would have loved this. Her mother, who had taught her that every document was a confession if you held it to the right light.

Her mother, whose face Maren could no longer fully recall, because her childhood had been sold off in eleven transactions before her twelfth birthday, and the buyer had been discreet, and discretion was the cruelest thing money could purchase.

The laugh died in her throat. She looked at the margin note again—*the gate will remember him*—and felt the old ache shift into something sharper. Not grief this time. Direction.

She worked until dawn. She pulled every sample of Vess's hand she owned—two contracts, a letter of authentication, a page torn from a primer like her mother's—and laid them beside the annex like a physician laying bones side by side to confirm they came from one body. They matched. All of them matched, down to the drag of the nib on downstrokes, down to the way his *r*s leaned slightly left when he wrote quickly, as if hurrying away from his own words. She checked the dates. The annex was written across eleven years, if the fading of the ink told true—eleven years of a dead man's hand still moving through the world, or a living man's hand moving under a dead man's name.

Either way, someone inside the guild had been Vess's heir. Someone had inherited not just his seal and his vault but his conscience, and had waited forty years for a keeper named Marchetti to come asking questions.

Maren copied the annex. All forty-one entries, in her own neat broker's hand, working fast now because copying was time and time was the one commodity she had never managed to stockpile. When she finished, she hid the original inside the false bottom of her strongest strongbox—the one warded with a lock that answered to blood, hers—and put the copy somewhere better. Somewhere no collector would think to look because it was too obvious: sewn into the lining of the coat she wore every day, the shabby brown one, the invisible woman's coat.

Then she opened her shop.

It was a mistake. She knew it was a mistake while she was turning the key, and she turned it anyway, because caution was a currency and she had just decided to spend all of it at once. If collectors were hunting the map—and they were; the cartographer's death had seen to that—then they would come to her eventually. She was the nearest memory-broker to the Ledgerhouse. She had sold the cartographer his last purchase. Better to be found calm behind her own counter than found running.

Besides, she told herself, arranging her face into its professional mildness, she had survived audits before. She knew the choreography. Let them ask. Let her answer slowly, truthfully where truth was safe, vaguely everywhere else. She had lied to collectors in this room a dozen times and never once sweated through her collar.

The bell above the door rang at midmorning.

Three of them. Not the usual audit pair—a trio, which meant escalation, which meant someone upstairs had signed an order. The one in front was a woman with a face like a folded map herself, all creases and no expression, and Maren catalogued her in the space of a breath: senior collector, left-handed, the kind of patience that preceded violence rather than replacing it. Behind her, two younger ones took up positions flanking the door, and Maren felt the click of it in her spine. The door was closed. The door had been closed behind them.

"Broker Oduya." The senior collector's voice was pleasant. Everything about her was pleasant, the way a well-made snare is pleasant right up until the weight comes down. "Routine inquiry. We're tracing the effects of a deceased party. A cartographer."

"I don't serve cartographers," Maren said. "They're terrible clients. They want maps of their own memories, and maps are my competitors."

The joke landed the way jokes land in rooms like this: nowhere. The senior collector produced a slip of paper, unfolded it with two fingers. "He visited your establishment eleven days ago. He purchased—" she consulted the slip— "one item. We have his transaction record. What we lack is the item itself. It did not return with his body."

"His body." Maren let a broker's frown gather between her brows, the frown of a professional inconvenienced by mortality. "I heard a man died near the guild gate. I didn't know it was anyone I'd served."

"You served him. Eleven days ago. The record is quite clear."

"The record may be clear. My shelves are not." Maren spread her hands, and even as she did it—even as the lie assembled itself smoothly in her mouth—some detached part of her noted how easy it had become, how little it cost anymore, and filed that observation away in the drawer where she kept things she didn't want to examine. "I move hundreds of items a month, Collector. If you have a description—"

"We have a description." The senior collector stepped forward, and her two juniors shifted with her, a small precise geometry of menace. "But descriptions are inefficient. It would be faster simply to look."

"Look?"

"Audit. You know the form. Every licensed broker submits to inventory inspection upon request of the guild." The pleasantness did not waver. "This is a request."

Every instinct Maren owned stood up at once and began shouting. An audit meant hands on her shelves. Hands on the strongbox. Hands on the private vault downstairs, behind the second door, the one that held the memories she had never sold and never would—the fragments she couldn't explain owning, the garden, the wall, the woman's arms passing a bundle into the dark. If they touched those—

"They can't be here yet," she said aloud, and realized too late she had spoken the thought.

The senior collector tilted her head. "Who can't be where?"

"No one. Broker's superstition. We mutter to our shelves; it keeps the memories settled." Maren smiled, and the smile cost her more than any lie she'd ever told. "Of course you may audit. Give me a moment to unlock the back room."

"Of course."

She walked toward the second door with the unhurried gait of a woman fetching keys, and behind her she heard the collectors begin, quietly, professionally, to touch her life. Drawers sliding. Glass clinking against glass. A voice reading labels aloud in the flat tone of inventory. Twenty years of careful acquisition, itemized in minutes.

At the second door she paused, key in hand, and made the calculation that would define the rest of her life.

She could not take the vault. The vault was a room; rooms cannot be carried. She could take one thing from it—one vial, one fragment—and the choosing of that one thing was the hardest arithmetic she had ever done. The garden? The garden belonged to Ines now, in every way that mattered; she had sold it to him at ruinous cost precisely so it would stop belonging to her. The wall? The bundle? The woman's arms?

Or the eleventh transaction. The one her mother had signed. The one containing, if her deductions were right, the missing piece of herself—the part of her own childhood that would tell her why a memory-broker who had never been to Vessany carried its rain in her head like a stone she couldn't cough up.

She chose the eleventh transaction. Of course she chose it. Caution would have chosen the garden, because the garden was leverage and leverage was survival. But caution had just walked through her front door wearing a collector's badge, and Maren understood, with a clarity that felt almost like relief, that the time for surviving was over. The time for knowing had begun.

The vial was small, no longer than her thumb, its contents shifting with a light that had no source. She slipped it into her boot, locked the vault door behind her, and turned the key in the second lock as well—the one that didn't open anything but sealed the mechanism, jamming it, buying her perhaps a minute.

When she came back out, the senior collector was standing at her worktable.

Standing at her worktable, looking down at the place where, six hours ago, the annex ledger had lain.

Maren's heart performed a maneuver she had previously only read described in medical texts. But the table was clean. She had cleaned it. Ink stains remained—old ones, weeks old, from legitimate work—and she watched the collector's eyes pass over them and find nothing actionable.

"Your records are extensive, Broker Oduya," the collector said. "Almost extensive enough to make one wonder what you keep off the shelves."

"Everything I keep is for sale," Maren said. "That's rather the point of me."

"Mm." The collector drifted along the row of bottles, trailing one finger an inch above the glass, never touching. "You knew the cartographer. Your ledgers say so, whatever your mouth says. And the last person known to speak with him was a keeper at the Ledgerhouse. A quiet one. Marchetti." She turned. "Do you know him?"

"I know of him. He catalogs. He doesn't buy." Maren shrugged. "Invisible men make poor customers."

Something flickered across the collector's folded-map face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Invisible men," she repeated softly. "Yes. Well. We'll leave you to your shelves, Broker. Expect the formal audit notice within the week. Do be sure everything is... in order."

The bell rang. The door closed. Maren stood alone in her shop among the bottled pasts of strangers, and did not allow herself to breathe until their footsteps had faded entirely from the street.

Then she moved. Fast, methodical, without sentiment—she had learned inventory from the best, and grief could wait its turn in line. Coin into the coat. The forged papers she kept for emergencies into the coat. The copied annex already sewn into the coat. The vial warm against her ankle. She set a decoy strongbox in plain view, unlocked, filled with worthless duplicates. She left the lamps lit. She left the shop's sign hanging. Let them raid an empty stage.

At the back door she stopped, once, and looked at the room where she had lived for nineteen years. The narrow bed. The kettle. The window that faced east and caught the morning light on the good days, of which there had been fewer than she pretended.

Nineteen years of caution, and it had bought her exactly one minute's warning.

"Keep it," she told the empty shop. "Consider it a donation."

Then Maren Oduya walked out of her own life with one vial, one coat, and a founder's treason stitched against her ribs, and went to find the only invisible man in the city who owed her a partnership.

The fishmarket, she reasoned, was the last place they'd watch twice.

* * *

She found him at the stall of the eel-wives, haggling badly, which was somehow the most endearing thing she had yet learned about Ines Marchetti. He paid full price for everything; it was written all over him, in the apologetic set of his shoulders, in the way he accepted change without counting it. A man built to leave no trace, spending coins like an apology.

"Inesio," she said.

He turned, and she watched the name strike him—watched it land somewhere below the ribs, where names go when they're true. Around them the market roared on, gulls screaming over the gutting tables, the smell of brine and blood and smoke, and none of it mattered, none of it existed, because the collector's words were already in her mouth and had to come out.

"They came to my shop this morning. Three of them. They asked about the cartographer, and then they asked about you." She kept her voice low and level, a broker pricing bad news. "They'll reach you within a day. Maybe less. Vane doesn't send trios for routine inquiries."

His face did something complicated. Fear, yes—but underneath the fear, a kind of grim settling, like a house taking a storm onto its frame. "Did they find anything?"

"They found an empty shop. They'll find an empty shop forever, if I have anything to do with it." She stepped closer, close enough that the fishwives' chatter covered her words. "But listen to me, because I only have time to say this once. The annex ledger—the one listing the children. I've identified the hand that wrote it."

"You—how?"

"I read hands the way you read ledgers. It's the founder's. Aurelian Vess. The man who built the erasure sat down afterward and recorded every child it failed to swallow, and hid the record inside the guild's own walls, and next to your name he wrote—" she watched his eyes as she said it— "*the gate will remember him.*"

Ines Marchetti went very still. Stillness, she thought, was clearly his native language.

"There's more," she said. "Someone kept that annex current for years after Vess died. Which means Vess has an heir inside the guild—an heir with his conscience and his penmanship. And that heir has waited forty years for you." She pulled the coat tighter around herself, feeling the copy press flat against her side like a second heartbeat. "Which brings us to the unpleasant part. My shop is burned. I have one vial, one coat, and a talent for lying to collectors, and I am officially out of caution. So here is my new offer, keeper, and it isn't a partnership this time. Partnerships can be declined."

"What is it, then?"

"It's a fact," Maren said. "You and I are the only two people in this city carrying pieces of Vessany, and the people who erased it have just started asking about us both in the same breath. You don't get to refuse me. You can only decide whether we run in the same direction."

Above the gutting tables, the gulls rose all at once, a white scatter against the grey sky, as if something far off had startled them. Maren saw it in the corner of her eye and felt the morning tilt beneath her feet.

Down the length of the fishmarket, moving against the crowd with the unhurried precision she had stood across a counter from only hours ago, came the senior collector with the folded-map face.

And she was not alone.
