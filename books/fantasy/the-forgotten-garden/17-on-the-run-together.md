## 17. On the Run Together

**Ines**

The blind corridors had no name on any plan of the Ledgerhouse, because the plans themselves were kept in the corridors' keeping. Ines had found them at nineteen, during his third year of service, when a shelf of misfiled ledgers slid aside under his hand and revealed a passage narrow enough that he had to turn his shoulders sideways to enter. He had told no one. Telling was how things stopped being safe.

Now he stood at the hidden seam with Maren Oduya pressed against the wall behind him, her breath coming fast and shallow, one hand still gripping the strap of the satchel that held everything she owned in the world—which, as of last night, was almost nothing.

"Inside," he said. "Quickly."

She slipped past him without argument, which surprised him. He had expected her to argue. She seemed built of argument, this woman, all edges and appraisal, someone who priced everything she saw including him. But in the fishmarket she had said *your real name* like a knife laid flat on a table, and since then something between them had changed register—not trust, nothing so warm, but a kind of mutual recognition: two people who dealt in other people's secrets, now carrying their own.

The corridor swallowed them. Behind, muffled by stone and old paper, the Ledgerhouse went about its evening—bells, footsteps, the dry whisper of pages turning in the reading rooms. Somewhere above, Vane's collectors were still pulling apart Maren's shop, plank by plank.

Ines sealed the seam behind them and lit the small lamp he kept stashed in a niche, its flame catching on walls lined not with books but with the *gaps* where books should be—the Ledgerhouse's own amputations, shelves that had been emptied deliberately over decades, their contents burned or buried or worse. He had never understood why the blind corridors preserved the absence rather than filling it. Now he thought he did. Someone had wanted the gaps remembered.

Maren was looking at the shelves too. "Your guild erases things," she said quietly. "I always assumed it just sold them."

"It does both." He set the lamp down. "Selling is the honest half."

She laughed—a short, unamused sound—and lowered herself onto an upturned crate, wincing. Ines noticed then how bad her hands looked, scraped raw from prying up her own floorboards to reach the cache beneath, and how carefully she kept them out of the lamplight, the way he kept his own face neutral when audited. Two professionals, hiding their damage.

He took out the map and unfolded it on the crate beside her. The streets shifted, slow as oil, rearranging themselves when neither of them looked directly. The inscription at the margin caught the light: *the garden remembers what the country cannot.*

"And these," Maren said, "are your documents." She said it without a question mark. Then she opened her satchel and began laying out her own evidence on the stone floor with the care of a surgeon: three memory-vials wrapped in wool, a broker's ledger with pages of coded entries, and a scrap of vellum so old its edges had gone to dust.

"What's that?"

"The bill of sale for my first memory." Her voice was level, but her hands weren't. "Sold when I was six. I've spent thirty years buying back pieces of myself at market rates. Do you know what a childhood costs, Ines Marchetti? Compounded?"

He didn't answer. He watched her arrange the vials and thought: *she has been doing what I do. Cataloguing her own disappearance.*

"We compare," she said. "You have paper. I have memory. Between us we might assemble a shape."

"A shape of what?"

"Of who decided forty years ago that a whole country was worth less than the silence around it."

* * *

**Maren**

They worked through the night, and Maren learned quickly that Ines Marchetti thought the way she talked—in careful, itemized increments, never volunteering more than the transaction required. It made him slow company and good cover. It also meant that when he finally gave something away for free, it landed with weight.

It happened around the fourth bell, when she laid the oldest vial on the flagstones between them and said, "This is the one I can't sell. A garden. Children reciting something—I don't know what. Streets. Rivers. Names of towns. Like a lesson."

Ines went very still. "Say the names."

"I only hold fragments. Vessany didn't survive bottling intact—it resists, same as your map. But there's a river called—" she closed her eyes, reached into the memory the way she'd taught herself to reach into others', gently, by texture rather than force—"*the Liss.* And a hill town. Serrave. And children chanting, dozens of them, in a garden with a wall of pale stone, and a woman saying: *as long as one of you carries it whole, they cannot take it from all of us.*"

When she opened her eyes, Ines had turned his face away. His shoulders were rigid. When he spoke, his voice came out flattened, professionally neutral, and she recognized the technique because she used it daily on grieving clients: *containment*. "My grandmother drew a symbol," he said. "On letters. In a script no living language uses. The gate charter has marginalia in the same hand. And the manifest with my surname lists forty-one children smuggled out before the erasure. I'm the fourth."

"Forty-one." Maren felt the number settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well. "And the ledger lists destinations for forty of them."

"Yes."

"And the forty-first has none."

He looked at her then, and she watched him arrive at it a half-second after she did—or perhaps he'd arrived long ago and simply refused to say it, because saying made it real, and this man's entire life was a fortress against real. "You think the missing entry is you."

"I think," Maren said, and her voice betrayed her despite thirty years of practice, "that I have memories of Vessany I never bought. I checked every ledger of my own acquisitions twice. Those fragments aren't stock, Ines. They're *mine*. Which means either I'm haunted, or I was there, and someone removed me so thoroughly that even I had to purchase my own past back at retail."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from some deeper passage. Neither of them moved.

Then Ines did something she would remember longer than most transactions: he pulled the annex ledger from his coat—the one listing the forty-one children—and turned it toward her, open to the final page, and laid beside it the scrap of her own first bill of sale, and said, "Show me the handwriting."

She did. The clerk who had processed a six-year-old's dispossession in neat commercial script; the hand that had written the annex ledger, smuggling names out of a burning record; even, she realized with a cold crawling certainty, the marginalia in the gate charter—all of it shared a skeleton. The same slanted capitals. The same peculiar way of crossing t's with a single economical stroke.

"One person," Ines said softly. "One person inside the guild wrote the erasure order *and* the rescue ledger *and* processed the sales of stolen children." He exhaled. "The founder. It was always going to be the founder. You build a house of forgetting, and then you can't stop yourself from leaving doors in it."

"He was dissenting and complicit in the same ink," Maren said. "That's not a contradiction. That's how it always works. I've sold memories to the men who ordered the raids and bought memories back from their clerks the same week." She paused. "We're not looking for a villain, Ines. We're looking for a machine, and everyone who ever fed it—including us."

He didn't flinch from that, which she noted, and filed, and decided—cautiously, provisionally, the way she decided everything now—might be the beginning of something she could use.

* * *

**Ines**

By the sixth bell they had covered the floor of the blind corridor with paper: manifests, charters, Maren's coded acquisition records, the map breathing its slow rearrangements at the center like a sleeping animal. Ines knelt among the documents and felt something unfamiliar working in him. It took him a while to name it, because he had never had cause to feel it before.

It was the absence of solitude.

For twenty years his invisibility had been a discipline, and disciplines are comfortable the way well-worn shoes are comfortable—you forget they're holding your feet in a particular shape. Now a stranger was kneeling three feet away, reading his grandmother's stolen letters upside down, and the world had not ended. The guild had not materialized. He was, for the first time since the cartographer died against his gate, not alone with the secret.

"You're staring," Maren said without looking up.

"Thinking."

"Dangerous habit for a keeper."

"My oath says *open for none, remember for all*." He smoothed the charter flat. "I swore it at fifteen. I believed every word. Now I read *remember for all* and I hear it differently—as if the words were waiting forty years for someone to take them literally."

Maren glanced up. "And *open for none*?"

"That clause I'm still negotiating with."

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if she could afford one. "Practical," she said. "I like practical."

They worked on. She decoded her acquisition codes against his shipping manifests and found the pattern neither could see alone: the guild hadn't merely erased Vessany, it had *liquidated* it—memories sold off in lots, years parceled to wealthy clients, names traded as curiosities. A country converted to inventory. And running through every lot, every sale, every razored page: the founder's hand, both committing the crime and annotating it, leaving a trail of corrections like a man robbing a vault while writing apology letters to the owner.

"He wanted it undone," Ines said. "From the start. He just couldn't do it openly."

"Then he left it to whoever came after." Maren gathered the documents into stacks with quick, decisive movements. "Us. Congratulations. We're someone's contingency plan."

Ines began gathering his own papers—and stopped. At the edge of the lamplight, where the corridor bent toward the deep stacks, something hung in the air that shouldn't be there. Not sound exactly. A pressure. The particular quality of silence that means someone is listening to it.

He raised one finger. Maren froze mid-motion, and he saw her understand instantly—brokers know surveillance the way sailors know weather. For ten heartbeats neither breathed. The pressure held, then faded, retreating up through the stacks like water finding its level.

"Collector?" she whispered.

"Or junior keeper making rounds." He rose, rolling the map closed with hands that were steadier than he expected. "Either way, we move. There's a reading room two levels down that hasn't been entered since the founder's time. The seal is keyed to keeper's blood—mine will open it."

Maren stood, slinging her satchel, and for a moment they faced each other in the lamplight, two people bound together by nothing but complementary damages. He extended his hand—not warmth, just terms—and she shook it once, briskly, a merchant sealing a contract.

"For the record," she said, "I followed you for the map."

"For the record," Ines replied, "I'd have done the same."

They left the lamp burning on the floor of the blind corridor, its small flame reflected in forty years of empty shelves, and went down together into the dark—two fragments of an erased country, walking toward each other's halves of the truth.

Behind them, in the silence they'd mistaken for empty, a page turned.
