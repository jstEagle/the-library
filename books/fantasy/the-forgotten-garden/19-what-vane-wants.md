## 19. What Vane Wants

The Collector's office had no windows, and this was not an accident.

Vane had chosen the room himself, forty years ago, when the guild still called him an apprentice and he still answered to a name that ended in a vowel. He had taken the windowless chamber at the top of the Ledgerhouse because it was the one place in the building where nothing could be seen coming. No rooftops. No lantern-light from the fishmarket. No sky. Just walls of grey stone and a single lamp and the ledgers he was sworn to keep—and in all those years, nothing had ever come through the walls except questions.

Tonight the question was a dead cartographer named Ollivier Brant, and a map folded into a coat pocket that no longer existed, and a keeper with soft hands and a face like a locked drawer.

Vane sat behind his desk with his fingers steepled and did not move for a long time. This was a discipline he had learned before he learned anything else: that stillness made people confess. A man who fidgeted invited fidgeting; a man who waited invited waiting to end. So he waited, and let the lamp hiss, and let the silence do what silence had always done in this room—press down on the truth until it leaked out of the cracks.

Except there was no one here to press. Only himself, and the memory he kept in the same place he kept everything: nowhere, which is to say everywhere, which is to say in the body, where erasure could not reach.

He closed his eyes and let it rise, as he did every night, as penance or prayer—he had never decided which.

A kitchen. Woodsmoke and cardamom. A woman singing three notes over and over while she worked, a song with no words left in it, worn smooth as river stone. His mother's hands dusted with flour, pressing dough flat, saying something in a language he no longer owned the grammar of but whose music lived in him like a splinter too deep to dig out. *Sova.* She had said *sova* when she tucked him in. He knew it meant sleep. He did not know how he knew.

That was Vessany. That was all of Vessany he permitted himself: one kitchen, one song, one word. He had spent four decades building a wall around that room inside himself, brick by brick, the way the masons would soon build a wall around a gate, and he had learned to live beside it the way one lives beside a grave one refuses to visit but cannot sell.

Because he remembered what came after the kitchen. He remembered the ships. He remembered the harbor at Greywater and the quarantine sheds and the men in guild-grey who took the children aside and asked them gentle questions in a language they were careful never to name. He remembered being told, at seven years old, that the country he came from had never existed—that the fever that emptied it had been so terrible, so contagious in its way, that the only cure was forgetting, and that the guild had administered the cure, and that he should be grateful.

He had asked, once, with a child's terrible directness: *Grateful for what?*

And the kind grey man had smiled and said: *For being alive.*

Vane opened his eyes. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below him, eleven floors down, Ines Marchetti was presumably cataloguing impossible things with his immaculate hands, and somewhere in that keeper's chest, folded against his skin like a coal, was a map of a country that Vane had spent his whole life helping to keep dead.

They thought he wanted to destroy it. Let them. Destruction was a mercy he could not afford.

* * *

In the morning he summoned his two best collectors to the windowless room and gave them instructions that sounded like destruction and were not.

"The map is to be recovered intact," he said. "Do you understand me? Intact. If it burns, I will have the ashes sieved. If it tears, I want every fragment numbered. The Marchetti keeper has it—I am nearly certain of it, though certainty is not yet proof. You will not search his quarters. You will not threaten him. You will audit."

"An audit," said Corvess, the taller one, in the tone of a man tasting a word he suspected of being a joke.

"A full inventory audit of the Third Gate, its annexes, its charter copies, and its keeper's personal effects as they pertain to guild property. It is entirely routine. It happens twice a year." Vane looked up from the paper he was not reading. "It happens now because I say it does. You will be courteous. You will be thorough. You will watch his hands more than his face. Faces are trained; hands are honest. And you will report to me alone—not to the council, not to the registry. To me."

Corvess hesitated. "Sir—if the keeper is compromised, protocol says—"

"Protocol says what it always says." Vane set down the paper. "Protocol was written by frightened men who mistook burning for solving. I have read the original protocols, Corvess. Have you? The first charter, the founder's own hand? No. Of course not. Then take my word for it: the gate is not to be sealed by force, the map is not to be destroyed, and the keeper is not to be harmed. He may be the most valuable asset this guild currently possesses, and he doesn't know it yet."

"And if he runs?"

"He won't." Something moved under Vane's sternum, old and cold. "Keepers don't run. That's why we make them keepers. We take the ones who have already agreed to disappear."

When they had gone, he unlocked the third drawer of his desk—the one that officially contained obsolete tariff schedules—and lifted out a thin bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Inside were pages. Razored pages, forty years of them, rescued from the incinerator by a young collector who had been ordered to burn them and had instead, in the single act of rebellion of his long obedient life, hidden them in his boot and carried them home and never told anyone, because telling would have made it real, and real things demanded action, and action was the one thing he had sworn away.

He had told himself, back then, that he kept them as evidence. That a good collector preserved the record of his own crimes. Forty years on, he no longer lied to himself about the lying. He kept them because they were the last pages of a country, and he could not bear for the fire to finish what the razors started.

He unwrapped them now, as he did each morning, and read the same line he always read first, in the founder's cramped hand:

*Item: the city of Vessany, its streets, its names, its songs, its graves. Disposition: removal. Reason: see sealed appendix.*

There was no sealed appendix. There never had been. Vane had spent nine years of his life looking for it—bribing archivists, forging credentials, descending into cellars older than the guild—and had found instead the thing he had not been looking for: the shipping manifests. Forty-one children, listed by number, smuggled out in grain barrels and coffin carts and one memorable instance inside a hollowed statue of a saint neither country worshipped. Forty-one children scattered across the coast cities with new names and new languages and strict instructions, written into their adoption indentures in code, never to speak of where they came from.

His own number was twenty-six. He had found it on a Tuesday, standing in a flooded archive with a lamp guttering, and he had stood there until the lamp died, and then he had walked home through the rain without an umbrella, and he had not wept, because weeping required believing, and believing was the disease the whole apparatus existed to prevent.

Twenty-six. Destination: Greywater. Guardian: the guild. Notes: *subject retains language; remediation required.*

Remediation required. Four words, and beneath them, in a different ink, added later by a different hand: *remediation successful.*

Vane wrapped the pages again, slowly, along the same folds, so the oilcloth would wear evenly. Remediation successful. They had taken a boy who sang three-note songs in a kitchen and made him a collector who could walk past a grieving mother in a market and feel nothing but professional satisfaction at the absence of a disturbance. They had called it peace.

And the worst of it—the thing he admitted only in this room, to these walls, in the hour before the lamp went out—was that they had been right.

* * *

He went down to the Third Gate himself that afternoon, which he almost never did, because collectors who hovered made keepers nervous, and nervous keepers made mistakes, and mistakes were how countries came back from the dead.

But he wanted to see Marchetti. Not interrogate him—see him. There was a difference, and the difference mattered, and Vane had built his entire career on the difference between what things looked like and what they were.

The keeper was at his post behind the iron grille, entering figures into the day-ledger with a nib so fine it barely whispered. Vane watched him for a moment from the corridor's shadow before announcing himself. Watched the economy of his movements, the way his shoulders stayed low and quiet, the way he occupied space the way furniture occupies space—present, useful, unremarkable. It was masterful. It was exactly the training working as intended.

It was also, Vane thought, a performance. And lately—a very slightly strained one.

"Keeper Marchetti."

"Ines, Collector Vane." The young man rose and bowed the precise degree. "The audit concluded yesterday. I trust the reports reached your office."

"They did." Vane stepped closer to the grille, close enough to smell the ledger-ink and the cold stone breath of the gate behind it. "Your inventory was flawless. Your annotations exemplary. Your cooperation—" he paused, letting the word hang, "—impeccable."

"I do my work."

"You do. That's what interests me." Vane smiled, and made the smile warm, because warmth disarmed better than steel ever had. "I've been chief collector for nineteen years, Keeper, and in that time I have audited sixty-one keepers. Fifty-nine of them made small errors. Misfiled entries. Rounded sums. Nothing criminal—just the ordinary friction of human beings doing tedious work. Two of them were perfect." He watched Marchetti's face with the patience of a man watching ice form. "You are the second."

"Perhaps the audits are improving."

"Perhaps." Vane let the silence stretch until it creaked. Then, softly: "Tell me, Keeper. Do you dream?"

Whatever Marchetti had braced for, it was not that. Vane saw it land—the flicker behind the eyes, half a heartbeat, quickly filed away. Good. The hands stayed honest on the ledger's edge.

"I sleep adequately," the keeper said.

"That isn't what I asked." Vane leaned in, and lowered his voice as if confiding something shameful, which in a sense he was. "I ask because I've noticed something in forty years of this work. People who erase things—professionally, I mean, people like us—we don't stop dreaming about them. The mind keeps its own ledger. Unaudited. And sometimes, late at night, it opens the books." He straightened. "If you ever find yourself dreaming of a place you've never been, Keeper—a garden, perhaps, or a street with strange names—you should report it. Guild health regulation. We take dreams seriously here. They're the earliest symptom."

"Symptom of what, Collector?"

Vane held his gaze for a long, courteous moment. "Of remembering," he said pleasantly. "Good evening."

He walked away without looking back, and felt the keeper's attention on his spine like a hand, and thought: *You have it. I know you have it. Show me where you hide it, and I will show you why it must stay hidden.*

* * *

That night, in the windowless room, Vane took out the one object he allowed himself besides the razored pages: a child's wooden bird, painted blue, one wing chipped.

It had come through Greywater in the pocket of a coat belonging to a woman on the manifest—entry thirty-one, destination unknown, presumed lost at sea. The bird had washed ashore alone, tangled in kelp, six weeks after the ship was reported gone. Some dockhand had turned it in to the guild's lost-property office, and some clerk had logged it, and some collector—young Vane, twenty-three years old, still answering to another name—had signed for it and then simply never filed it.

He turned it in his fingers now. The paint was faded; the chip had softened with handling. His mother had carved birds like this. He knew that the way he knew *sova* meant sleep—not as fact but as gravity, something pulling at him from a direction he could not point to.

Here was the arithmetic he performed every night, the sum that never changed:

Forty years ago, a country died of remembering. That was the official phrasing, and like most official phrasings it was a lie built around a truth. Vessany had not died of plague. Vessany had died of its own memory—of a people who remembered so fiercely, so collectively, that the country itself had become something the world could not digest. The guild's founders had watched a nation's grief curdle into something that tore holes in the ordinary fabric of trade and time, and they had made the only choice available to frightened, powerful men: they cut the country out of the world and stitched the wound shut and called the scar peace.

And the peace held. That was the unbearable part. Forty years of harbors that ran on schedule, of borders that behaved, of children who slept without screaming. Whatever Vessany had been, whatever it had torn open in the years before the erasure, the forgetting had stopped it. Vane had read the incident reports from the final decade—the ones sealed even above his clearance, the ones he had stolen copies of at a cost that would have bought a house. Streets that rearranged themselves overnight. Rain that fell upward. A district where everyone woke speaking the same sentence. Memory, unchecked, had made Vessany a wound in the world, and the guild had cauterized it, and the patient had lived.

So when he hunted the map, he was not hunting a piece of paper. He was hunting the scalpel's return. Because a map that rearranged itself when viewed twice was not a map—it was a symptom. It was the country reaching up through the stitches, and every day it remained loose was a day the wound reopened a little wider, until one morning the fishmarket smelled of cardamom and the harbor filled with ships that had burned forty years ago and the peace—the long, hard-won, purchased-with-forty-one-children peace—came apart like wet paper.

He set the wooden bird on the desk and looked at it for a long time.

People believed he hated Vessany. Even Corvess believed it; he had heard the whispers—*the Collector despises the erased, hunts them like vermin.* He let them believe it. Hatred was simple, and simple things were safe. What he actually felt had no clean name. It was closer to what a man feels toward the house where he was happy and which burned down with his family inside it: he cannot look at the ruin, and he cannot look away, and he will fight anyone who tries to rebuild on the ashes, not because he hates the house but because the house is where they died, and rebuilding would mean agreeing they were gone.

Vessany could not come back. That was the whole of his creed, and he had paid for it with everything, and he would pay again.

But tonight—for one moment, in the dark, with the lamp out and the bird cool under his thumb—he permitted himself the heresy he permitted himself once a year, on the night of the solstice, when the gate's charter hummed in its sleep and the whole Ledgerhouse dreamed uneasily:

*What if we were wrong?*

*What if the forgetting wasn't a cure but an amputation, and the country didn't die of remembering but of being forbidden to?*

*What if my mother's song had words in it, and someone living still knows them?*

He put the bird away. He relit the lamp. He was Collector Vane again, and the wall inside him stood, and tomorrow he would find the map, and he would not burn it.

He would carry it to the gate himself, and lock it in the deepest vault, and stand guard over it for the rest of his life—the last librarian of a country that must never be read aloud.

Below him, eleven floors down, a lamp still burned in the Third Gate's office. Vane saw its glow from his windowless room's single slit of a vent, and noted the hour, and noted that the keeper was awake far too late, and understood with a calm that felt almost like tenderness:

The boy was copying the mutilated ledgers.

And somewhere in the city, a memory-broker who lied beautifully to collectors had begun asking questions about a dead man's coat.

Three threads now, all running toward the same door. Vane trimmed the lamp, opened a fresh sheet of paper, and began—at last—to plan not a hunt, but an intervention.

Eleven days to the solstice. He had read the charter too.
