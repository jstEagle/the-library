## 20. The Forty-First Child

The vial sat in Maren's palm like a held breath.

She had carried it up from her private vault three nights ago and had not yet worked the courage to open it. It was a small thing, no longer than her thumb, glass gone amber with age, the wax seal stamped with a sigil she had never been able to read. She had owned it for eleven years—bought it at an estate auction from a dead broker's effects, told herself it was inventory, and then locked it in the vault she kept for memories that were not for sale, where it had sat beside the other fragments of Vessany she could not explain: rain on slate roofs, a song sung by children in rounds, the smell of bread baked over coals, a woman's hands.

Always the woman's hands.

Maren set the vial on her worktable among her instruments—the fine silver siphons, the graduated flasks, the little brass scale she used to weigh recollection against coin—and did what she had done every night since the fishmarket. She looked at it. She did not touch it.

Touching was how brokers ruined themselves. A memory once opened could not be resealed clean; the edges frayed, the colors bled. Every time you entered your own vault of the unsellable, you came out carrying a little more of it on your shoes. She had watched older brokers go that way, women who had hoarded their unexplained treasures until they could no longer tell which griefs were theirs and which were stock. Maren had promised herself she would never be one of them.

But Ines Marchetti had stood in the fishmarket smelling of brine and ink, and when she had said his name—*Inesio*—he had flinched as though she had struck him, and something in his face had cracked open a door in her that had been shut for eleven years.

Because he had flinched *the way she would have flinched.*

That was the thought she could not put down. Not the name. The flinch. She knew that flinch from the inside. She had spent her whole life knowing things she should not know—the layout of a city that appeared on no map, the taste of a fruit she could not name if you asked her, the particular quality of light through a window she had never stood in—and she had built a career on selling such knowledge to people who assumed it was borrowed goods, secondhand, purchased from some dead stranger's skull. Everyone believed memory moved like money. Maren had let them believe it because the alternative was worse.

The alternative was that some of what she carried had never passed through anyone else's hands at all.

She lit the lamp, turned the wick low, and finally picked up the vial.

The wax crumbled under her thumbnail like old bread. Inside, the memory swam—a slow silver-gold suspension, denser than most, shot through with threads of darker color like iron filings in honey. Old. Very old. And dense in a way that made her professional heart stutter: density meant *lived*, not manufactured. You could not fake this weight. Somewhere in this thumb of glass was someone's actual afternoon, preserved whole.

She fitted the siphon to the neck, pressed the intake cup behind her ear, and opened it.

* * *

Rain first. Rain on stone, warm rain, the kind that comes in summer and makes the whole world smell green. She was small—she could feel it in the body of the memory, the way the world loomed too tall, the way her own hands were plump and dimpled, a child's hands gripping a wall's rough coping. She was standing on something. A step? A stool? Someone's cupped palms?

And there were voices below her, urgent, low, the hushed urgency of adults doing something they will later refuse to discuss.

A woman's face came up toward hers out of the dark. Young. Strands of hair plastered to her cheek by the rain. Her mouth was moving—saying words Maren could not hear, the way dreams drop their sound—but Maren *read* the lips, read them with a fluency that turned her blood cold, because the language was not one she had ever studied and she understood it anyway.

*Be brave. Be quick. Remember us.*

Then the hands—the woman's hands, lifting, passing her upward over the wall into arms that smelled of lamp oil and wet wool—and the memory tore.

Not faded. Tore. As though someone had taken scissors to the middle of it. The last thing was the sensation of being swung through air, rain hitting her face, and a sound beneath the rain that she would spend the rest of the night failing to name: a deep, resonant hum, like a struck bell heard through miles of earth.

Maren came back to herself sitting on the floor. She did not remember getting there. The lamp had burned lower. Her cheeks were wet.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, annoyed at it, and made herself think like a broker instead of a child.

Density: lived. Age: forty years, give or take. Provenance: unknown—or rather, provenance *claimed* unknown, purchased at auction, but she had checked that ledger entry a hundred times over the years and the auction house's records for that lot were, characteristically, incomplete. Condition: damaged. Deliberately damaged. That tear in the middle was not decay. Decay softened; this had been *cut*. Someone had excised the ending—the arrival, the faces on the far side of the wall, whatever came after *remember us*—with a precision she recognized, because she performed that same precision for clients who wanted a single hour removed without disturbing its neighbors.

Someone had edited her. Or edited this memory before it reached her. But the memory fit her the way a key fits its own lock—she had felt that in the siphon's pull, the perfect seat of it, no friction anywhere. Bought memories always sat slightly wrong, like another person's shoes. This one fit.

Which left only the conclusion she had been circling for eleven years and now could not avoid.

It was hers.

* * *

She did not sleep. She worked.

By lamplight, in the back room of her shop, Maren Oduya did the thing she had never done: she laid out her entire unsellable vault—all nine fragments of Vessany—and catalogued them against each other like a scholar with relics instead of a broker with stock.

The rain on slate roofs. The round-song, sung by children whose faces blurred whenever she tried to focus on them—blurred, she noted now, *edited*, the faces cut away with the same clean hand. The coal-smoke smell of bread. The quality of light through a particular window, diamond-paned, with a crack shaped like a river delta in the corner pane. The woman's hands again, in a different fragment, kneading dough, wearing a ring with a stone the color of deep water. A staircase. A garden wall with ivy that grew, impossibly, in a spiral pattern no ivy followed. A word spoken aloud in that unplaceable language, which she had once caught herself murmuring while measuring out a client's purchase, and which she had never found in any glossary because there was no glossary, because the country that spoke it had been razored out of the world.

And now the newest fragment, the oldest one, the one that fit her best: the wall, the rain, the woman saying *be brave, be quick, remember us.*

Nine fragments. She arranged them chronologically by internal evidence—the length of her own hair in each, the size of her hands, the season of the light—and got a span of perhaps two years, maybe less. Early childhood. The years a mind seals over anyway, the years almost no one keeps.

Almost no one. But brokers knew better than anyone that those years did not vanish. They sank. They became the sediment everything else settled on. And occasionally, with the right siphon and the right pressure, you could draw the sediment up and look at it.

Her whole career, she had assumed her strange stock was salvage—memories bought cheap from dying estates, mislabeled, their provenance lost. She had even sold a few of the Vessany fragments early on, before she understood what they were, and had spent years quietly buying them back at painful prices whenever they surfaced. Two were still missing. She thought of them the way you think of teeth pulled.

They weren't salvage. They were *her*. Fragments of her own first two years, cut apart—she was certain now, looking at the scissor-marks under her loupe, all nine bore the same editing hand—cut apart and scattered. Sold off. Dispersed into the market like a broken necklace, bead by bead.

Why would anyone do that?

She pulled the shipping manifest copy Ines had shown her in the fishmarket—he had pressed it into her hands half in defiance, half in need, and she had read it twice and given it back, but her memory was a broker's memory and it did not lose things. Forty-one names. Children smuggled out of Vessany before the erasure. Each entry bearing a destination: a family in the capital, a convent in the south, a keeper's household, a farm upriver. Forty entries with destinations.

The forty-first line had been different. She had noticed it even then, had felt it itch at the edge of her attention the way an unbalanced ledger itches. The forty-first entry had a name—she closed her eyes and summoned it, letter by letter, the way she summoned clients' account numbers—and no destination. Where the destination should have been, someone had written a single word in the margin, in a hurried hand unlike the rest:

*Split.*

At the time she had assumed it meant the child died en route, or the record was simply unfinished. Records were full of unfinished sentences. She had built a comfortable life on the assumption that unfinished sentences stayed unfinished.

*Split.*

She said the word aloud in the empty shop, and heard it differently now. Not a death. A procedure. You split a shipment when you do not want any single carrier to hold the whole of it. You split a cargo when the whole of it is dangerous. You split a child's first two years into nine fragments and scatter them across the memory markets so that no one—not the child, not the guild, not the collectors—can ever assemble them in one place.

Except a broker could. Brokers handled dispersed stock all the time. Consolidation was the trade.

Or—her stomach turned slowly over—except two people could. If you split a thing deliberately, you gave half to one carrier and half to another, and you sent them in opposite directions, and you trusted to distance and forgetting to keep the halves apart.

She thought of Ines Marchetti's face in the fishmarket. The ledger-keeper with his careful invisible life, who catalogued impossible things and touched none of them, who had flinched at his own name like a man hearing a bell he'd been deaf to all his life. He had documents. Manifests, ledgers, the paper skeleton of Vessany. He was assembling the country from its records.

And she had the flesh. Nine fragments of rain and bread and a woman's voice saying *remember us.*

Paper and flesh. Half and half. She looked at her neat arrangement of vials on the table and felt the shape of it close around her with an almost audible click, like a lock turning.

The forty-first child had no destination because the forty-first child *was* the destination—was both destinations, was the plan itself. One child carried out over the wall in the rain and divided in two: the records to one keeper, the remembering to another. So that when the day came—if the day ever came—the country could be rebuilt from both directions at once, and neither half alone would be enough, and whoever held the halves would have to find each other.

Someone had trusted two infants with a nation's memory.

Maren laughed, once, without any humor in it, and the laugh came out wrong, closer to a sob, and she let it, because there was no one to see.

* * *

Dawn found her still at the table, and it was dawn-light falling across the ninth fragment—the wall, the rain—that showed her the last thing.

The vial lay in the sun's first thin gold, and the light passed through the memory inside and threw a faint image onto the whitewashed wall behind her table, the way a lantern throws shadows. She had done this trick a thousand times for clients, projecting a purchased past onto a screen so they could watch their own weddings from outside. She had never done it to her own.

The image wavered, silver and grainy. The wall. The rain, falling in long bright threads. The woman's face coming up, young and terrified and resolute. And now, projected large, Maren saw what the siphon's intimacy had hidden: behind the woman, over her shoulder, a garden. Hedges in a spiral pattern. A gate of black iron. And above the gate, carved in stone, words in the language she could not help but read:

*We keep what is given. We give what is kept.*

Below it, smaller, almost worn away: a date. Not a day and month—an era-mark, the kind cartographers used, and Maren's breath stopped, because she had seen that exact era-mark four days ago, folded into a dead man's map, in the corner where the cartographer had signed his work.

The map was keyed to this garden. To this gate. The dying stranger had not pressed a random country into Ines's hands. He had pressed the way home to the precise wall where a woman in the rain had handed over a child and said *remember us.*

Two children went over that wall. Or—one child went over, and one was already on the other side, waiting in the arms that smelled of lamp oil and wet wool. The memory tore before the arrival. Cut away. Because whoever edited it had not wanted her to see who caught her.

Or had not wanted her to see *who else* was caught.

Maren stood very still in the rising light while the projection faded and the wall behind her went blank and white again. Eleven years of professional curiosity, of telling herself she hunted Vessany's fragments for the trade, for the puzzle, for the pleasure of the rare—eleven years of loneliness dressed up as expertise, and here at the end of it stood the plain truth, naked as a peeled egg:

She had been following Ines Marchetti since before either of them could walk. They had been split at a garden wall in the rain, and everything since—the guild, the shop, the ledgers, the brokering, the careful invisible lives they had each built, his of paper and hers of other people's pasts—had been two halves of one sentence drifting apart, waiting forty years for someone to die at a gate and say *Vessany* and start the clock.

Identity arrived in her the way a wound arrives when the surgeon finally cleans it: not gently, and not painlessly, but with the terrible relief of knowing exactly where it hurts. She was not Maren Oduya, memory-broker, who happened to stock curiosities from a vanished land. She was the forty-first child. Destination: split. She was the half that remembered, and somewhere across the city a man who kept a gate was the half that recorded, and the woman in the rain had said *remember us* to *both* of them, and Maren had spent her whole life selling memories because she could not bear to be alone inside her own.

She washed her face. She dressed. She packed the nine fragments into a padded case, took her coat, her siphons, her working cash, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch.

There was a knock.

Three knocks, spaced evenly, courteous—the knock of someone who has never in his life needed to force a door because doors open for him. Maren knew that knock the way she knew the tear-marks in her own memories. Collector Vane had come asking questions at her shop once before, smooth as poured cream, and she had lied to him beautifully.

Through the door, in a voice like velvet drawn over a blade, came the words she had been dreading since the fishmarket:

"Madam Oduya. I believe you have something of mine."
