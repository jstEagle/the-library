## 24. Vane's Trap

They took her in the hour before dawn, when the fishmarket was nothing but wet stone and the smell of yesterday's catch, and they took her well—no shouting, no spectacle. Four collectors in plain coats, moving like men who had done this a hundred times and would do it a hundred more. Maren Oduya had time to register the cold of the cobbles through her boots, the particular gray of the sky over the customs house roofline, and the fact that she had been careless for exactly one night.

One night of sleep in her own bed, because running made you tired and tired made you stupid, and stupid got you caught.

She should have known better. She told other people that every day, for a fee.

The room they brought her to was above a chandler's shop near the harbor, a place chosen for its ordinariness: whitewashed walls going yellow at the seams, a table, two chairs, one window with a view of rigging and gulls. Someone had thoughtfully provided a lamp, a pitcher of water, and a single glass. The courtesy of it frightened her more than manacles would have. Manacles meant passion. This meant procedure.

Collector Vane sat at the table when they pushed her through the door, and he did not look up right away. He was writing. That was the first thing she understood about him in the flesh, after weeks of hearing his name spoken in the lowered voices of people who dealt in secrets: he wrote everything down. His pen moved in small, patient strokes, and beside him on the table lay a ledger bound in gray leather, opened to a page dense with entries in a hand so neat it looked printed.

"Sit," he said, without looking up. "Or stand. It makes no difference to me. But I'd ask you not to waste both our mornings."

Maren sat. She chose the chair facing the window, so that the light fell across Vane's face instead of hers. A small thing. Brokers lived on small things.

He finished his line of writing, blotted it, closed the ledger, and only then raised his eyes. They were pale, unhurried eyes, the color of water over slate, and they examined her the way she had seen appraisers examine goods—not cruelly, not kindly, simply establishing value and condition.

"Maren Oduya," he said. "Memory-broker. Licensed under the third charter, which is amusing, given what we're about to discuss. You've been operating out of the Lantern Row shop for eleven years. Your clients include two minor magistrates, a shipping magnate, and—" he turned a page without opening the ledger, from memory, "—at least four people who sold me information about you over the years. I mention this not as a threat but as context. You are known to me. Thoroughly. Which means everything you do today will be measured against eleven years of established behavior, and deviations will be noted."

"You've been watching me for eleven years," Maren said, "and you're only now inviting me to tea."

"I've been aware of you for eleven years. Watching is a stronger word." He poured water into the glass and set it in front of her, an oddly gentle gesture. "Drink if you like. Now. Let us speak plainly, because I have very little time and you have somewhat less."

He folded his hands.

"The cartographer Aldous Renk died three weeks ago outside the Ledgerhouse gate. Before dying, he passed something to the keeper there—a map. Since then, Ines Marchetti has committed no fewer than six violations of guild law, escalating from concealment of evidence to, as of last night, the theft of documents from a sealed annex. He is currently a fugitive, and he is currently alive, which means someone is helping him. Someone who knows the city's memory trade. Someone who could read a broker's marks on a forged provenance." His voice never rose; it was the flattest, most reasonable voice she had ever heard threaten her. "Someone like you."

"And if I say I've never met him?"

"Then I'll believe you might be telling the truth, and I'll check anyway, and the checking will be unpleasant for people who don't deserve it. Your neighbor who feeds your cat. The apprentice you dismissed last spring. The widow on Lantern Row who buys memories of her husband from you twice a year because once isn't enough." He said it all mildly, cataloguing her life back to her like a list of assets about to be seized. "I would prefer not to. I am not a cruel man, Mistress Oduya. I want you to understand that. Cruelty is inefficient. I deal in efficiency."

* * *

Here was the thing about being a memory-broker that no client ever understood until it was too late: you learned to hold two truths in your mouth at once. The truth you were selling and the truth you were keeping. Eleven years of practice sat in Maren now like a second spine, and she leaned on it while Vane talked, because he was talking again, and what he was saying made her hands want to shake.

"We know what you keep in your private vault," he said. "Below your shop. Behind the false wall. Seventeen vials you have never offered for sale, which by guild reckoning constitutes hoarding of unregistered goods." He paused, and for the first time something moved behind the flat pale eyes—something almost like sympathy, which was worse than cruelty. "We know some of them contain Vessany."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped coin.

"You've wondered about them yourself," Vane went on softly. "I can see it. Fragments you can't account for. Rain on tiles the color of rust. A garden where children recite street names like prayers. A woman's hands passing a bundle over a wall." He watched her flinch and continued, merciless in his gentleness. "You've spent years telling yourself you bought them and forgot buying them. But brokers don't forget inventory, do they? And here is my offer, and I make it sincerely."

He reached into his coat and produced a case. Inside, nested in velvet, lay five vials, each stoppered with wax, each labeled in a fine clerk's hand. Even across the table she could feel them—the hum of them, the way a tuning fork hums against bone.

"Your own past," Vane said. "Piece by piece. These five first. There are more. Every fragment of Vessany that was taken out of you—and there were many, Mistress Oduya, far more than seventeen—returned to its rightful owner. In exchange, you tell me where Ines Marchetti is hiding, and you give me the map."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the chandler's boy was sweeping, the rasp of the broom steady as a heartbeat.

And oh, it was a beautiful offer. That was the horror of it. She could feel the vials the way a starving woman feels bread through a baker's window. Her whole life she had sold other people's yesterdays because she had none of her own worth keeping, and here was a polite man in a plain coat offering to give her back the child she must have been, the mother whose hands she half-remembered, the country that lived in her like a splinter too deep to dig out.

All it would cost was one man's life. One careful, invisible, stubborn man who had looked at her in a fishmarket with suspicion and then, later, with something that was almost trust.

"He won't thank you," Vane said, reading her silence correctly, as brokers read each other. "Whatever he's promised you. Whatever cause you think you're serving. What he's carrying will bring back a country that forty years of peace was built on top of, and the digging-up will bury thousands. I know you don't believe me. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to weigh it against your own face in a mirror at sixty, still wondering who gave you away."

Maren reached for the glass of water. Her hand was steady. She was distantly proud of that.

"Let me see one," she said.

Vane considered, then slid the case forward and drew out the first vial, holding it up to the lamp. The liquid inside moved sluggishly, gold shot with green, and even unopened, even at arm's length, it whispered to her. Not words. Just the sense of a door standing ajar somewhere inside her, with light behind it.

"This one," Vane said, "is a kitchen. Evening. Someone singing badly, on purpose, to make a child laugh." He set it down between them, equidistant, like a piece on a board. "Yours. It has always been yours. I am not a thief, Mistress Oduya. I am a custodian of things that were removed for good reasons. Give me Marchetti, and this comes off the table, into your hand, and I walk out of here and forget your name."

* * *

Later—much later, in other rooms, in other interrogations—people would ask her how long she had known she was going to lie to him. Maren never had a clean answer, because the truth was that she hadn't known. That was the trick of it, the thing she would carry with quiet pride for the rest of her life: she had decided in the space between his offer and her answer, the way a broker decides mid-handshake whether a client is worth the risk. Not before. Not after. In the gap, where all real trades happen.

Because here was what Vane did not know, what his eleven years of surveillance had never caught, because she had never once done it for a client and so had never left a trace: Maren Oduya could forge a memory-vial.

Not counterfeit the glass or the wax—any competent glassworker could do that. She could forge the contents. There was a technique, old and half-forbidden, that brokers called *seeding*: taking a genuine fragment, thin as onion skin, and growing it in suspension with suggestion until it bloomed into something that felt complete. A seeded memory tasted true. It tested true. It carried the texture, the temperature, the weight of the real thing, because it was built on a real thing—it was simply pointed elsewhere, like a signpost made of honest wood with the destination repainted.

She had learned it from the old broker who trained her, who had used it exactly once, to escape a man very much like Vane, and who had made her swear never to use it because, he said, *a lie that feels true is the only lie that survives testing.*

She had kept the oath for eleven years.

"Before I agree to anything," Maren said, "I want to verify. You'd expect that. I'm a professional. If I'm trading my entire past, I want to know the goods are genuine." She let a tremor enter her voice then, carefully metered, the tremor of a woman beginning to break. "If I'm going to sell him, I want to know what I'm selling him *for*."

Something satisfied moved through Vane's expression and was gone. "Reasonable. Open it."

She picked up the vial. Her fingers knew the work even as her heart hammered: thumb finding the seam in the wax, the quarter-turn that mimicked verification without breaking the seal fully, the tilt that let a thread of the contents touch the air where she could taste it. To Vane it would look like appraisal. It was appraisal. It was also theft.

The fragment bloomed against her tongue: evening, a kitchen, copper pots, a woman's voice cracking deliberately on a high note while a child shrieked with laughter. Genuine. Undeniably genuine—she could taste the age of it, the wear of having been handled by guild extractors, the faint bureaucratic aftertaste of their preservative salts. Real, and really hers.

Which meant the others were likely real too. Which meant Vane was telling the truth about the vaults, about the removals, about all of it—and that changed nothing except the depth of her fury, which now had a floor beneath it and could go down forever.

She resealed the vial with hands that did not shake, and set it down, and looked at Collector Vane with wet eyes that were also, underneath, entirely dry.

"All right," she said. "All right. But hear me first, because if I do this, I do it properly, and properly means you understand what you're getting." She swallowed, a performance of gathering courage. "Marchetti doesn't trust me. He barely trusts anyone. If I just hand you a location, you'll find it swept clean—he moves every few days, he thinks like a keeper, he seals routes behind him. I can give you a place, but I can't give you certainty unless—" she hesitated, the hesitation of a woman crossing her own Rubicon, "—unless I show you how I found him. Provenance. You people respect provenance."

Vane's eyes narrowed a fraction. "Go on."

"A broker verifies a trail the same way she verifies a vial. I followed him through a purchase. Three nights ago, desperate, low on options, he came to a contact of mine to buy something specific—a memory that would help him open the gate. My contact sold it, and my contact is talkative when paid, and I paid, and I traced the sale." She reached into her coat slowly, telegraphing every movement, and drew out a vial wrapped in cloth. "This is a copy of what he bought. Not the original—I wouldn't risk the original—but a working copy, made for verification. Look at it. Taste it. You'll find the trail in it, because that's how I found him: the memory contains where he was standing when he bought it, and where he said he was going. Memories are sloppy that way. People carry their geography with them."

It was beautiful, she thought, watching him reach for it. It was the finest work of her career. Because the vial was seeded—grown overnight in her shop's cellar from a sliver of true rain-on-tile, cultivated and bent until it held a warehouse by the tannery row, a door with a green mark, a voice saying *we move again tomorrow, before the bell.* Everything in it consistent. Everything in it textured with authentic detail, the smell of hides, the drip of eaves, the exhaustion in a man's shoulders. A signpost of honest wood.

And the cruelest elegance of it, the part she savored even now: the trail it pointed to was real. There *was* a warehouse on tannery row. There *was* a green-marked door. Ines had sheltered there nine days ago, briefly, before moving on—and Vane's collectors would storm it and find cold ashes, recent enough to confirm everything, empty enough to send him hunting the next address, and the next, each one a day stale, each one costing him hours while the solstice burned down toward its final night.

She was not giving him a lie. She was giving him a lag. And in a race counted in days, a lag was a death sentence—or a pardon, depending on which side of the door you stood.

Vane held the vial up to the lamp for a long moment. Then he did something she had not expected: he uncorked it and touched a drop to his tongue himself, eyes closing, reading it with the fluency of a man who had tasted ten thousand memories and trusted his palate absolutely.

Maren sat very still and thought about gallows.

"The tannery district," Vane said at last, opening his eyes. "Green door." He looked at her, and the flat pale gaze went through her like a probe through soft ground, looking for the hollow, the flaw, the seed. "You understand that if this proves false, I will come back for you, and the next conversation will not include tea."

"If it proves false," Maren said, "you'll know where to find me. I'm a broker, Collector. My reputation is the only asset I have. I don't spend it on things that fail."

A silence. Then Vane stood, took the vial, returned the case of five to his coat—she felt the loss of them like a physical wound, and let it show, because showing it was the last layer of the forgery—and inclined his head with terrible courtesy.

"Your five vials will be delivered within the hour," he said. "Consider our transaction concluded. And Mistress Oduya—" at the door he paused, hand on the frame, not turning around, "—for what it's worth, I hope you find what you're looking for. Most people who sell memories are looking for their own."

Then he was gone, and his collectors with him, and the broom below went on rasping, and Maren Oduya sat alone in the yellowing room with her heart finally allowed to hammer, laughing silently or weeping, she couldn't have said which, until the sound ran out of her.

* * *

They delivered the vials at dusk, as promised. A boy left the case at her shop's door and vanished, and Maren carried the five small weights upstairs and set them on her table in the last of the light, and did not touch them for a long time.

They were hers. They were bait. Both things were true, and the truth of them did not cancel out.

She thought of Vane's parting words, and of the woman in her oldest fragment, the one with the strong hands and the wall and the bundle, and she thought: *he wasn't wrong. I have spent my whole life selling other people's yesterdays because I wanted mine back.* And now she had five pieces of it sitting in velvet, gifts from the man trying to erase her country a second time, and taking them felt less like rescue than like swallowing hooks.

But she was a broker. And a broker knows that every payment is also a price, and every gift is also a debt, and the only question that matters—the only question there has ever been—is who ends up holding the ledger.

She packed the case, and her tools, and the one vial she had saved from the raid on her shop, and she blew out the lamp, and she went out into the dark to find a fugitive keeper and tell him the collectors would be tearing apart tannery row by morning, and that he needed to run, and that she had just become the most dangerous kind of liar:

the kind with nothing left to prove.

Behind her, in the empty shop, the false wall stood open on an empty vault, and on the shelf where seventeen vials had once slept, someone had already left a slip of paper she would not find for two more days, written in a neat clerk's hand:

*We kept the originals. — V.*
