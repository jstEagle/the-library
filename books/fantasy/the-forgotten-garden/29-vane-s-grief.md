## 29. Vane's Grief

The letter was written in his mother's hand.

That was the first thing Vane understood, before he understood anything else—the small, slanted script he had spent forty years trying to forget, the hand that had written *eat your porridge* and *mind the wet stones* and once, folded into his coat pocket on a morning he could no longer name, *be brave, my little lantern*. The letter lay open on the table between them, and it was not addressed to him. It was addressed to a woman named Sefa Ilves, of the Lanternmakers' Row, in a city that did not exist.

He had come to the founder's vault expecting weapons. Ines Marchetti had summoned him with the careful formality of a keeper announcing an inventory discrepancy—*Collector Vane, I've found something in the annex you should see*—and Vane had walked down into the dark beneath the Ledgerhouse with his hands loose at his sides and three collectors stationed above, because whatever the boy had found, it would need to be handled, and handling things was what Vane did.

He had not expected his mother's handwriting.

"Read it," Marchetti said quietly. "All of it."

Vane did not pick it up. He read it where it lay, the way one reads a thing that might bite, and the words assembled themselves with terrible patience:

*My dear Sefa — the order has gone out tonight. They are calling it containment, but I have seen the lists, and there is nothing in them but names. Yours is among them. I cannot stop what comes at dawn, but I can tell you that your son is recorded here as born of Vessany, and that when they ask him who he is, he will not know how to answer, and perhaps that will be a mercy. Forgive me. Forgive us all. The garden remembers even if we are made to forget.*

Below the signature was the founder's seal, pressed deep into wax gone dark as old blood.

Vane read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings refused to cohere, the way a sentence refuses to mean anything when the floor has dropped out from under the reader. His mother's name sat in the salutation like a stone in a shoe. *Sefa.* He had not spoken that name aloud in four decades. He had not been permitted to. There had been no permit to revoke; the name had simply ceased, along with the street it belonged to, the city it belonged to, the country, the language, the whole warm architecture of a childhood that had been dismantled while he slept.

"You went through my family records." His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later. Level, and cold, and precise—the voice of a man conducting an audit rather than a man drowning.

"The guild keeps everything," Marchetti said. "Even the things it erased. Especially those."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

* * *

They stood in the vault for a long time without speaking. It was not a large room—the founder had built it small on purpose, Vane realized now, small and dry and deep, the way one builds a place meant to outlast anger. Shelves of ledgers rose into shadow. The erasure order itself lay in a glass case, forty years of dust on the lid, and beside it the original charter of the Ledgerhouse, and beside that a box of letters that Vane could not make himself look at again.

His parents. His mother and father, of Lanternmakers' Row, of the city whose name he had spent his career striking from other people's ledgers.

He thought: *I am forty-six years old and I learned my parents' origin from a dead man's filing system.*

"I don't believe it," he said finally.

Marchetti tilted his head. He was younger than Vane by twenty years, this gatekeeper, this gray little ghost of a man who had spent his life cataloging bottled memories without ever opening one, and yet standing in the vault light he looked less like a clerk than like a blade someone had sheathed and forgotten to draw. "You read the letter."

"I read ink on paper. Paper lies. That is the first lesson of this house, keeper, and you'd know it if you'd been here longer than a season of trouble." Vane turned away, walked the length of the shelf, ran one finger along the ledger spines without touching them. "Anyone can forge a hand. Anyone can age wax. You want me to stand down. You want me to doubt the work of my life because of a document produced by the very conspiracy you're asking me to ignore."

"Do you doubt it?"

"Yes."

"Liar," Marchetti said, without heat.

Vane stopped walking. For a moment—one long, suspended moment—he considered the arithmetic of violence. Three collectors upstairs. A vault with one stair. He was stronger than the keeper, better armed, and far more practiced at ending things. It would be simple. It would take eleven seconds.

But the letter would remain. Copies would exist—there were always copies, that was the disease of paper—and killing Marchetti would convert a problem into a martyr, and martyrs were worse than problems. So instead Vane said, "Show me the rest," and hated himself for the hunger in it.

* * *

There was more. Of course there was more. The founder had been thorough in his guilt, and guilt kept excellent records.

A census fragment, pre-erasure, listing households of the Lanternmakers' Row. *Ilves, Sefa (née Marchetti), lampwright; Ilves, Doran, wainwright; child, Aldric, aged 4.*

Vane read his own name in a dead language and felt nothing, which was itself a kind of horror. *Aldric.* A name that fit him the way a borrowed coat fits—not badly, but with someone else's shape still in the shoulders. He had been Aldric once, for four years, in a country of lamplighters and rain. Then the guild had come with its clean instruments and its cleaner intentions, and Aldric had been dissolved, and a boy named Vane had been issued in his place: new city, new school, new history, a ward of the guild raised in the corridors of the Ledgerhouse itself, grateful, obedient, and empty in the specific way that made for excellent collectors.

"They chose you deliberately," Marchetti said. He had gone to the case with the erasure order and stood beside it like a man standing witness. "Children under five. Too young to fight the forgetting. The guild took forty-one of them out before the gate closed and scattered them across the cities as wards, apprentices, foundlings. You weren't spared, Collector. You were harvested."

"I was *saved*." The word came out of him with force enough to echo off the shelves, and Vane heard it land in the vault and understood, distantly, that it had not sounded like conviction. It had sounded like a man shouting into a well to hear something other than water.

"Saved from what?" Marchetti asked softly. "Your mother was a lampwright. Your father drove a cart. What plague were they? What contagion? Show me the crime, Vane. It's your guild's own archive. Show me what they did that deserved to have their country deleted."

And that was the wound of it, the true wound, the one Vane had carried so long he had mistaken it for a spine: there was no crime. He had spent thirty years inside the machinery of the erasure. He had audited its justifications until he knew every line of them by heart—the plague of remembering, the memetic collapse, the country that ate its neighbors' histories—and he had believed it, needed to believe it, because the alternative was that his entire life was a janitor's mop pushed across a murder.

"There was a reason," he said. "You weren't there. You've read fragments. I've read the full doctrine. The founders didn't erase Vessany out of cruelty—they contained it. Memory in that country wasn't private. It was communal, structural, self-propagating. A place where remembering was contagious isn't a homeland, keeper. It's a fire. And fires don't grieve when you put them out. Fires are grateful."

"Fires don't write letters to lampwrights."

"No," Vane said. "People do. People who were wrong about the danger, or lying about it, or—" He stopped. The founder's letter sat in his mind, *forgive us all*, and he heard in it, for one vertiginous instant, not confession but plea—a good man begging forgiveness not for what he'd done but for what he'd failed to prevent. He shut the door on that hearing. He shut it hard. "Or grieving. Grief makes forgers of us all. Your dead cartographer forged a map. Your founder forged a conscience. Why should I trust his?"

"Because he spent his last twenty years trying to undo it," Marchetti said. "What have you spent yours doing?"

The question was not cruel. That was the unbearable part. It was asked with the flat professional curiosity of a keeper checking an entry against a ledger, and it went into Vane like a probe going into a wound to count the depth.

What had he spent his life doing?

Striking names. Sealing doors. Sitting across tables from frightened people and explaining, in his courteous voice, why their memories of impossible things had to be collected, catalogued, and closed. He had told himself it was surgery. He had told himself someone had to hold the knife steady. He had told himself so many times that the telling had worn grooves, and the grooves had become the man.

"I've spent mine keeping the peace," he said.

"Whose?"

* * *

He left the vault because staying had become untenable, and he climbed the long stair with his hand on the wall like an old man, and above, in the gray administrative daylight of the audit floor, he found his composure waiting for him exactly where he'd left it, folded and pressed, and he put it back on.

The collectors on duty straightened when he passed. Good. Let them see nothing. Let them see the Collector Vane of record: unhurried, immaculate, a man whose griefs had all been filed and stamped.

But in his office, alone, with the door sealed and the privacy wards humming, Vane permitted himself ten minutes. He timed them by the guild clock, because grief unmeasured becomes indulgence, and indulgence becomes weakness, and weakness in a collector becomes a door left open somewhere that things crawl through.

He sat at his desk and let the shaking start.

It began in his hands and moved inward, and with it came the memories—not of Vessany, he had none, the guild had taken even those—but of the after. The orphanage corridor that smelled of lye. The matron who called him *boy* because his file said his former designation was deprecated. The night, aged nine, when he had tried to remember his mother's face and found that the guild's instruments had taken it cleanly, completely, leaving not absence but a smooth blankness, like a wall where a door used to be, and he had lain in the dark inventing her features over and over, testing each invented face against a feeling he could no longer verify, weeping because he could not even be sure he was weeping for the right woman.

They had done that to him. His guild. His house. The institution he had served with the devotion of a son.

And they had done it, the doctrine said, to save him. To save everyone. Because Vessany's memory was a contagion, and contagions do not care that you loved the sick.

*Or*, said a voice in him that sounded unpleasantly like Ines Marchetti, flat and patient as a ledger line, *they did it because a country of people who remember everything is a country that can never be ruled, and the guild was young then, and hungry, and it dressed its appetite in medicine.*

"Stop," Vane said aloud, to the empty office.

The clock ticked. Nine minutes remaining of his allotted grief.

He thought about his mother's hands. Not her face—that was walled—but her hands, which had survived the erasure by accident, lodged in some unindexed corner of him: hands dusted with lamp-black, turning him by the shoulders toward the window to look at the evening lights coming on along the row, one by one, a whole street of small suns. She had said something then. He would never know what. The words were gone but the warmth of the saying remained, orphaned, meaningless, a hearth burning in a house with no walls.

*The erasure spared them pain,* he told himself, and reached for the doctrine the way a drowning man reaches for rope. *Whatever came for Vessany at the end—whatever the founders saw building in that country's shared dream—it would have taken her too. Would have taken her slower. Would have made her watch. The guild gave her a clean end. An unwitnessed one. No one suffers a deletion. That is its mercy.*

The thought held for three seconds. Then he saw it for what it was: a coin he had minted himself, decades ago, and paid out so often it had worn smooth, and he could no longer tell whether it had ever been legal tender or whether he had simply decided, one night in an orphanage bunk, that if his parents' loss had to be meaningless he would make meaning the only way available to a nine-year-old—by believing the thieves were surgeons.

Ten minutes ended. The clock chimed. Vane dried his face with a linen cloth, precisely, corner to corner, and when he raised his head the man in the office was the Collector again, entire, seamless.

Almost seamless.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon with the efficiency that had made his reputation. Warrants drawn. Routes assigned. The defected keepers named, their quarters marked, their access revoked—Marchetti's little rebellion had spread further than Vane liked, three junior keepers and an archivist at least, and treason in a house of records was a fire in a library: it did not stay where it started.

But underneath the paperwork, in the cellar of himself where he kept the things that could not be filed, the letter burned.

*Sefa Ilves, née Marchetti.*

Née Marchetti. He turned that over and over through the drafting of warrants, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The gatekeeper's name was Marchetti. Inesio Marchetti of Vessany, Maren Oduya had testified before her shop was raided—Vane had read her statement twice, looking for the lie, and found instead a genealogy. Which meant the boy in the vault was not merely a smuggled child. He was kin. Blood of the same row, possibly the same house. The keeper of the gate was the grandson—or nephew, the generations blurred—of the woman who had written letters to Vane's mother.

The country had sent him back its children, and one of them had grown up to keep the last door, and now stood in the dark below the Ledgerhouse holding out his family's papers like a lamp.

*It's a trap,* Vane told himself. *Of course it's a trap. Sentiment is the oldest weapon in the broker's arsenal, and the boy has been drinking from Maren Oduya's well for weeks.*

But the handwriting. God help him, the handwriting. Forty years and he had known it instantly, the way you know a bell by its voice. No forger alive could have reproduced the particular impatient crossbar of the *f* in *Sefa*, because no forger alive had ever seen it. Only Vane had seen it. The proof existed solely in the one place the guild could not audit: the memory they had missed.

Which meant either the letter was genuine, or it had been tailored to him by someone who had guessed—guessed!—at the one seam left in his stitching. And both possibilities were intolerable, because both meant the same thing: that Ines Marchetti had seen him. Not the Collector. Him. The boy from Lanternmakers' Row, the invented man, the hollow coat. And had reached in through the seam.

Vane set down his pen.

He sat for a long moment in the lamplit office, listening to the guild breathe around him—the shuffle of clerks, the chime of the hour bells, the vast paper heartbeat of the house that had raised him—and he understood, with the clarity that arrives only at the bottom of a thing, that he could not serve two truths. Either the erasure was medicine, and the letter was forgery, and the boy was an enemy to be crushed. Or the erasure was murder, and his parents had been stolen from him twice—once from their country, and once from his own memory—and every warrant he had ever signed was a signature on their second death.

There was no third column. He had checked, over forty years, and the ledger balanced only two ways.

And Vane—who had been emptied once already, who knew exactly what was taken from a person when their past was confiscated, who had spent a childhood inventing a mother's face in the dark—Vane chose the doctrine. He chose it the way a man chooses to keep walking on a broken leg, because stopping means falling and falling means feeling the break.

Not because he believed it. He was honest with himself in this one cellar-hour: belief had nothing left to do with it. He chose it because if the erasure was murder, then grief was not a weight he could carry; it was an ocean he would drown in, and he had not survived the orphanage, the lye-smell, the blank wall where his mother's face had been, to drown now in a basement archive over a dead man's letters.

Grief, then, would become fuel. It was the only transformation available. He had watched the guild do it to lesser men for decades—take their sorrow, stamp it, issue it back as purpose—and he had always believed himself immune, being already hollow. But he was not hollow. He had never been hollow. He was full to the brim with a country he could not remember, and the pressure of it had nowhere to go except forward.

* * *

At dusk he went down to the gate.

Not the public entrance—the inner approach, the corridor of the mechanism, where the great door stood in its housing of black iron and older wood, humming faintly, the way it had hummed for forty years: the sound of a thing locked that wished otherwise. Vane had stood in this corridor a hundred times on inspections. Tonight it felt different. Tonight the hum sounded less like machinery and more like breathing.

He laid his palm flat against the door. Cold. Deep cold, the cold of a well.

"I know what you are now," he said quietly. "I grew on the other side of you. Four years. Lamplight. Rain on a cobbled row." His voice did not shake. He had spent his ten minutes; the Collector's voice was all that remained, and it was enough. "And I am telling you what I should have told you forty years ago."

He leaned close, forehead nearly touching the iron, a man at a deathbed or a confessional.

"You stay shut."

Then he straightened, and called for the master mason, and gave the order he had been composing since the vault: not a sealing this time, not brick and mortar and a vote's authority. Those were reversible; bricks could be pulled down by passionate people with nothing left to lose, and the boy had nothing left to lose, Vane had seen it in him, that terrible lightness.

No. This time it would be the old protocol, the founder's own contingency, the one written for gates that could not be trusted to stay closed: dissolution of the passage itself. Unmaking. The door would not be blocked. It would be *unremembered*—struck from the charter, struck from the mechanism, struck from the minds of every keeper who had ever sworn upon it, until the Ledgerhouse had no gate at all, only a wall that had always been a wall.

It required the council's assent. It required, technically, a keeper's cooperation—for the final clause could only be executed by the hand that held the gate's oath.

Vane smiled without warmth, and sent for the masons anyway, and drafted the summons to the council for tomorrow's session, and at the bottom of the order, in his precise collector's hand, added the clause that would bring Inesio Marchetti to the chamber in irons if necessary:

*The keeper will attend. The keeper will bear witness. The keeper will understand that some doors are closed for love.*

Above, in the corridors, the guild clocks rang the evening hour, one bell after another, a whole row of small suns going out.
