## 30. Nine Days

Maren Oduya had spent twenty years learning the exact weight of a secret—how much it cost to carry, how much it fetched on the open market—and in nine days she intended to spend every one she owned.

She started at the Drowned Lamp, because that was where memory-thieves went to drink away what they'd stolen. The tavern sat below the waterline of the old canal district, its windows fogged green with river-light, and by the third bell it smelled of tallow and wet wool and the particular sourness of people who made their living taking things that couldn't be fenced. Maren knew half the faces. The other half knew hers.

She did not buy drinks. She never bought drinks; buying was a posture, and posture invited negotiation from a position of weakness. Instead she took the corner table with sightlines to both doors, set a single memory-vial on the wood—the good glass, the kind with the silver stopper that thieves recognized the way soldiers recognized rank—and waited.

It took less time than she expected. Nine days before the solstice, and word traveled faster than she'd ever seen it move.

"You're the broker." The man who slid into the seat across from her was called Ferren, though Maren suspected he'd worn four other names that year. He was thin in the way of someone who ate when he remembered to, with burn scars laddering his left forearm—the mark of a man who'd handled vials during a vault fire and chosen the memories over his own skin. "They say you're selling work."

"I'm not selling anything." She turned the vial slowly on the table, letting the light catch it. "I'm hiring."

"Hiring." He said the word like it was a bone he'd found in his soup. "Brokers don't hire. Brokers take."

"Brokers are about to be out of business." Maren leaned forward, and let him see her eyes, and let him see that she was not afraid, which was itself a currency. "In eleven days the Ledgerhouse seals its gate for the last time. You know what's behind that gate? Everything they cut out of us. Every name they razored. And when it closes, it doesn't close for a season or a year. It closes forever."

Ferren's gaze dropped to the vial. "What's in it?"

"A street." She watched him go still. "A street in Vessany. Rain on cobbles, a woman calling someone home for supper. It's not mine. I don't know whose it is. That's the point."

The tavern noise seemed to recede around them. Thieves were superstitious about exactly one thing: unclaimed memories. A vial with no provenance was either worthless or priceless, and no one alive could tell which until they drank it.

"You're saying there's more," Ferren said carefully.

"I'm saying there's a ledger with forty-one names on it. Forty-one children carried out of a country that was then erased so thoroughly that even the paper it was written on was cut apart. Some of those children are still alive. Some of them are in this city right now, walking past the places their mothers used to shop, and they don't remember a single door of it." She stopped turning the vial. "I'm one of them. And I intend to walk back through that gate, and I intend to bring enough hands to hold the door open while the rest come home."

For a long moment Ferren said nothing. Then he laughed—not mockery, but the low, disbelieving laugh of a man watching the odds change.

"The guild will bury you," he said.

"The guild is already burying itself. Half its keepers have read the annex ledger by now. You can't show forty men a list of stolen children and expect them all to look away." Maren stood, leaving the vial on the table between them like a wager. "Drink that if you want proof. Bring me anyone who's ever been burned by the Ledgerhouse—thieves, exiled keepers, debtors, anyone with a grudge and steady hands. I'm paying in returned memories. Their own. The ones the brokers sold off years ago. I know where most of them ended up."

"That's—" Ferren's voice cracked slightly. "That's not possible. Those sales are scattered across thirty years of private vaults."

"I'm a broker." She pulled her coat on. "Scattered is my native language."

* * *

Ines Marchetti had not slept properly in six days, and he had stopped pretending this was a problem.

The blind corridors beneath the ruined archive had become a workshop. He'd claimed the old copying room—a low stone chamber where apprentice keepers had once learned penmanship by duplicating shipping manifests until their fingers bled—and filled it with everything he'd salvaged: armfuls of ledgers rescued from the fire, candle stubs, ink, and the annex ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth like a relic, which he supposed it was.

Forty years of razored pages. That was the arithmetic that kept him awake. When Vessany was erased, the erasers hadn't just destroyed the records—they'd cut them out of every ledger, every manifest, every customs roll that mentioned the country. But the Ledgerhouse was a bureaucracy, and bureaucracies make mistakes. Bureaucracies duplicate. A shipment logged in a master ledger would appear again in a quarterly summary, again in an auditor's copy, again in a keeper's private index. Cut a page from one book and you might miss its ghost in seven others.

So Ines hunted ghosts.

He worked methodically, the way he'd catalogued impossible goods for fifteen years, except now the inventory was his own country assembling itself out of scraps. A customs entry from thirty-one years ago: *grain, salt, wool—origin illegible, port code VS-4.* A ferry toll record: *twelve passengers, crossing paid in Vessany crowns, currency exchange refused.* A letter of complaint from a merchant whose trade route had simply ceased to exist mid-sentence, the destination struck through with such violence the nib had torn the paper.

Each fragment went into the copying book in its proper place. He was rebuilding Vessany the way a mosaicist rebuilds a shattered face—one tessera at a time, trusting that the whole would emerge.

By the fourth night he had streets. By the fifth, a harbor. By the sixth, he had something that made him put down his pen and press both palms flat against the table until the shaking stopped: a tax roll from the year of his birth, listing households by district, and in the Garden District—a name. *Marchetti.* His grandmother's handwriting appeared nowhere in it, but the district did. The garden did. The same garden the map folded toward, the same garden in the bottle where a woman's voice had said his name in a language he didn't know and understood anyway.

He copied it three times, in case of fire.

* * *

Maren's second alliance was harder, because exiled keepers were harder. Thieves wanted payment; keepers wanted absolution, and Maren had none to give.

She found Edouin Vance—no relation to the Collector, a coincidence that made them both flinch—in a garret above a chandler's shop, living like a man who'd decided furniture was a moral failing. He'd been expelled from the guild nineteen years ago for falsifying an intake record, and he'd spent those nineteen years drinking and insisting he'd falsified it for good reasons, though he'd never told anyone what they were.

"You want me to forge documents," he said, squinting at her through the smoke of a pipe that had seen better decades.

"I want you to do the opposite of forging. I want you to authenticate. I've got fragments of Vessany scattered through forty years of ledgers, and when we present them to the council, Vane will call them fabrications. They need a keeper's eye behind them. Someone with standing. Someone they can't dismiss."

"They dismissed me fine."

"They dismissed you for a lie. This is the truth. It weighs differently." Maren set the annex ledger on his table, unwrapped the oilcloth, and turned to the page with forty-one names. "Read the fourth line."

Edouin read it. Read it again. His pipe went out in the ashtray and he didn't notice.

"Inesio Marchetti," he whispered. "Destination: the Ledgerhouse. Intake authorized by—" He looked up, and his face had gone grey. "I know this hand. I apprenticed under it. This is Halvard's writing. The founder's own hand."

"Yes."

"He signed the erasure order. Everyone knows that. And he—" Edouin touched the page as if it might be hot. "He smuggled the children out first. He wrote the destruction and then he wrote the rescue, in the same year, in the same ink."

"Now you understand why I need you sober," Maren said. "And why I need you by solstice."

* * *

On the fifth day, Ines restored a page that changed the shape of everything.

It came from the dullest possible source—a maintenance log for the gate mechanism itself, forty years old, recording routine inspections. Most entries were oil and counterweights and hinge tolerances. But one entry, dated two weeks after the erasure, read:

*Gate sealed per charter amendment. Charter clause 12 struck. Note: clause 12 (right of personal passage) preserved in original charter, vault copy. Amendment applies to council copies only.*

Ines read it eleven times.

Clause 12. The right of personal passage. He'd found it once before, buried in the founder's vault—a provision stating that a sworn keeper, acting alone, retained the irrevocable right to open the gate for personal transit, subject to no vote, no seal, no amendment. He'd assumed it was dead letter, superseded, forgotten. But the maintenance log said otherwise: the amendment had been applied only to the copies the council consulted. The original charter—the true one, the binding one—still held the clause intact.

Someone had left a door inside the lock. Forty years ago, someone had made sure that no matter how thoroughly Vessany was erased, one keeper could always walk home.

And Ines knew who. The same hand that wrote the annex. The founder, who had built the machine of forgetting and then threaded a needle through its heart.

He sat back in the candlelight, surrounded by his mosaic of recovered pages—streets, harbors, tax rolls, names—and felt something he hadn't felt since the cartographer died against his gate. Not fear. Not grief. Momentum.

Nine days. He had nine days, a forged nothing of a resistance, a handful of allies, and a clause.

It would have to be enough.

* * *

By the seventh night, the copying room had become a war office, and neither of them had planned it that way.

Maren arrived after midnight with Ferren and six others—two exiled keepers Edouin had vouched for, a debt-broker with a grudge, a woman who'd spent a decade stealing memories out of collectors' vaults and could pick the Ledgerhouse locks in her sleep. They came in through the blind corridors, one at a time, carrying satchels, and Ines met each of them at the door with the same expression: a keeper's neutral face stretched almost, but not quite, to breaking.

"This is a restoration project," he told them, when they'd all crowded into the low stone room among the candles and the ledgers. "Not a battle. Yet."

"It's both," Maren said. "It was always going to be both."

She'd brought her own contribution: a leather case of vials, arranged in rows like eggs. Returned memories—she'd spent five days and most of her remaining capital tracking down sold childhoods, buying them back from private vaults at ruinous prices, sometimes trading two of her own for one of theirs. The exiles passed them around silently. One of the keepers, a grey-haired woman named Sefa, held a vial up to the candlelight and began, very quietly, to cry.

"What is it?" Ines asked.

"Songs," Sefa said. "My mother's songs. I sold them when I was nineteen because I was hungry and stupid, and I've been humming wrong versions of them my whole life." She closed her hand around the vial. "You're telling me these are real. That they're mine."

"I verified the provenance myself," Maren said. "Broker's guarantee."

Sefa drank it there, sitting on a crate of salvaged manifests, and the room went silent as her face changed—as forty years of a wrong-shaped absence filled in. When she finished, she looked at Ines with wet eyes and said, "Where do you need me?"

And that, Ines thought, was how a resistance assembled. Not with oaths or banners. With a woman getting her mother's songs back and deciding, on the spot, that the debt would be paid in full.

They worked through the night. Edouin authenticated fragments with a forger's inverted precision, building a chain of custody for every scrap of Vessany. Ferren mapped the guild's patrol rotations from memory. The lock-woman, whose name was Pell, studied diagrams of the gate mechanism and asked questions so precise that Ines realized she understood the mechanism better than he did. Maren moved among them all, brokering—settling disputes, allocating tasks, converting grievance into coordination the way she'd once converted memory into coin.

Near dawn, she found Ines at his table, adding the final fragment to the copying book: a harbor master's note, forty years old, listing the last ship out of Vessany. Forty-one passengers. No destinations recorded for any of them except, in the margin, in the founder's hand: *safe roads, all.*

"Do you ever sleep?" she asked.

"When the work allows."

"The work won't allow for nine days." She sat across from him, and for a moment neither spoke. Candlelight moved over the pages between them—the country reassembling itself, street by street, name by name. "Vane knows we're moving. Ferren says collectors doubled their patrols two nights ago. He's not trying to find us anymore, Ines. He's trying to find the annex ledger. If he gets it before the vote—"

"He won't get it before the vote."

"And if he gets it after?"

Ines set down his pen. Underneath it, the copying book lay open to the last page, where he'd transcribed the maintenance log's note about clause 12. Maren read it upside down, and her breath caught.

"The original charter," she said. "In the vault. It still holds."

"It still holds." He closed the book. "Whatever happens with the council, whatever Vane does—on solstice night, one sworn keeper can open that gate. No vote can stop it. He can brick the doorway, and I can still open it, because the charter says the door belongs to the keeper, not the guild." He looked at her, and let her see what he'd been carrying since the night he found the clause: not hope exactly, but its skeleton. "He knows it too. That's why he ordered the wall. He's not sealing the gate, Maren. He's sealing me in with it."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Outside, somewhere far above, the city was waking—bakers, fishwives, clerks, forty-one scattered children walking streets that had forgotten them, all of them unaware that in nine days the shape of the world would be decided in a basement by candlelight.

"Then we'd better make sure the wall comes down first," she said, and reached across the table, and turned the copying book back open, and picked up a pen.

They worked side by side until the candles gave out.
