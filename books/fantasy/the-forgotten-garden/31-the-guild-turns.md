## 31. The Guild Turns

The council chamber had not been opened in Ines's lifetime.

He knew this the way he knew everything about the Ledgerhouse—by its records. The last entry concerning the Chamber of Nine was a maintenance log dated thirty-one years ago: *floors swept, candles replaced, dustsheets removed and rehung.* No one had written *meeting held.* The room existed in the ledgers as a place that was kept but never used, like a coffin maintained by a family that refused to admit whose name was on it.

Now he stood before its doors with the annex ledger under his arm, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp of the lower stacks, and listened to his own heart count out the seconds like a clerk tallying coin.

Behind him, in the corridor that smelled of tallow and cold stone, stood eleven keepers. Not all of them would follow him inside—he had known that since the night he'd shown them the first page—but they had come this far, and this far was farther than he had any right to expect. Old Sarel, who had taught him the weight-grading of bottled grief when he was fourteen. The twins Pellam and Rook, who between them remembered every corridor of the blind levels better than the blueprints did. Junia Voss, gray-haired and iron-spined, who had audited him twice in his career and found nothing either time because there had been nothing to find. And others whose faces he had catalogued for years without ever once imagining them standing at his back.

*Forty-one children,* he had told them, in the scriptorium after midnight, laying the annex open under lamplight like a wound. *Smuggled out over nine years. Listed by hand. Someone inside these walls wrote their names down so they could not be wholly erased—and then let the erasure stand anyway.*

He had watched their faces do what faces do: flinch, calculate, harden, break. He had watched Keeper Aldous Marr—the man who had reported the annex to Vane, though Ines had not yet learned it, would not learn it until later, when the smoke was already rising—nod slowly and say, *This must go to the council,* with a sincerity that Ines would remember afterward as the most convincing performance he had ever witnessed, better than anything Vane had ever done, because Marr had believed every word.

The doors were black oak banded in bronze, and across them, in the old guild script that no one spoke anymore but every keeper could read, ran the words of the founding: *WE KEEP WHAT THE WORLD WOULD LOSE.*

Ines put his palm against the wood. It was warm. That surprised him—a door unused for three decades should have been cold as a gravestone—but then, everything about the Ledgerhouse had felt warm lately, feverish, as if the building itself had caught the sickness of remembering that Vane claimed to fear.

He pushed, and the doors swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than screaming.

* * *

The Chamber of Nine was a circle.

Not metaphorically—an actual circle, a round table of dark wood beneath a domed ceiling painted with constellations that matched no sky Ines had ever charted. Nine chairs sat at even intervals. Eight were occupied. The ninth, at the northern point, stood empty, and Ines understood without being told that it had stood empty for forty years—that it belonged to whoever had ordered the erasure, or perhaps to whoever had dissented from it, and that no one had dared to sit in it or to remove it.

The eight who waited were older than he had imagined. He had spent his life among collectors and gate-wardens and clerks; the council had been an abstraction, a signature at the bottom of directives, a name spoken in the tone reserved for weather. Now here they were, flesh and age spots and spectacles, and every one of them looking at him as if he were a ledger that had begun writing back.

"Ines Marchetti," said the woman at the head—or what served for a head, in a circle. First Chair Odile Ferrant. Her voice was dry paper. "You requested audience. You have it. You have until the candle burns to the first ring." She indicated a fat white candle on the table's center, already lit, already weeping wax toward a scribed line an inch below its flame. "State your business."

He had rehearsed this. On the walk up from the annex, through the corridors where Maren waited with the map hidden in the lining of her coat because the council could never be allowed to see it—not yet, not first—he had rehearsed the opening sentence forty times. All forty versions left him now.

So he said the true thing instead.

"Forty years ago, this guild erased a country."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"We keep what the world would lose," Ines said, and set the oilcloth bundle on the table, and unwrapped it. "That is what the doors say. I have walked past those words every day of my life. I believed them. I built my whole self on them." His hands were steady. He noticed this from a strange distance, the way he noticed everything about himself lately—as if he were both the clerk and the inventory. "But we didn't keep Vessany. We unmade it. We razored it out of our own records. We burned its maps and salted its archives and bought its memories off its survivors and sealed them in vaults. And then—" he turned the annex ledger around, so its open page faced the circle, faced the empty ninth chair, "—someone in this room, or someone who sat where you sit, wrote down the names of the children who escaped. Forty-one names. Because some part of this guild could not bear to let them vanish entirely. And then buried that part too."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and grain, that can be cut and stacked.

It was broken by Third Chair Emmerich Hale, a heavy man with a collector's flat eyes, who said quietly: "Where did you get this?"

"A dead man gave me a map," Ines said. "I followed it into the ledgers. The ledgers led here."

"The cartographer," said Ferrant, and something moved behind her face—recognition, or guilt, or simply the flicker of a file being consulted. "Vane has been hunting him for months."

"Vane has been hunting the truth for months," Ines said. "The cartographer was just the man carrying it. He spent his last years trying to undo what this guild did. He died at my gate with a country's name in his mouth." He looked around the circle, meeting each pair of eyes in turn, refusing to hurry. "I am not here to accuse you. I don't know which of you knew. Maybe none of you. Maybe the order came and was executed and filed and forgotten, the way everything is filed and forgotten here. That's what makes it monstrous—it didn't need your hatred. It only needed your procedure."

Fourth Chair, a thin man named Corvus whose fingers were stained the permanent brown of archival ink, laughed—one short, unhappy note. "You speak like someone who has never seen what remembering does. Have you read the containment reports, boy? Do you know why—"

"I know why," Ines said. "I've heard Vane's version. A plague of remembering. A country that couldn't stop recalling itself, memories metastasizing, borders dissolving because the land remembered other shapes. I've read the summaries in the sealed stacks." He paused. "I've also read the erasure order. Would you like to hear what it actually says? Not the summary. The order."

No one answered, which was answer enough.

"It says the plague was contained within Vessany's borders for six years before the guild acted. Six years. It says the neighboring kingdoms petitioned for trade sanctions, not annihilation. It says the final authorization cites, as its primary justification, not contagion—" he tapped the annex, "—but *irregularity in the guild's own holdings.* Someone had been keeping records the council hadn't authorized. Someone had been preserving what should have been destroyed. The erasure wasn't medicine. It was a cover-up wearing medicine's coat."

"You cannot know that interpretation is correct," Hale said.

"No," Ines agreed. "And you cannot know it isn't. That's the whole point. For forty years, only one version of events has existed in this building, because the other version was cut out with a razor. I'm asking you to look at both. That's all a keeper does. We look at what's written before we decide what's true." He spread his hands. "Or have we stopped doing even that?"

The candle burned past the first ring.

* * *

They argued for two hours.

Later, Ines would try to reconstruct the shape of it—the way water tries to reconstruct the shape of the stone it flowed over—and what remained was less a sequence of speeches than a weather system: fronts of outrage colliding with fronts of fear, pressure dropping, pressure rising, the whole chamber cycling through storms.

Corvus demanded the annex be surrendered for authentication. Ines slid it to the center of the table and let them pass it around, and watched Corvus's expert fingers still on the handwriting, and saw the old archivist's lips move as he recognized—Ines was almost certain he recognized—the founder's script. Corvus said nothing about it. But he stopped arguing for suppression after that, and started arguing, in a low relentless voice, for *verification*, which was a different thing entirely, and Ines understood that somewhere in the old man a door had come unlatched.

Hale argued for Vane. Of course he did. Vane's argument was clean, and cleanliness is seductive to people who have spent their lives handling things that stain. *The peace holds because the forgetting holds. Open the gate and you don't restore a country—you release forty years of grief into a world that has finished mourning it. You make forty-one adults into refugees of a place that will not hold their weight.* Hale said it well. Ines hated how well he said it.

Junia Voss—who had entered the chamber behind him and been permitted, over Hale's objection, to stand at the wall—spoke only once. "I audited this keeper twice," she said, nodding at Ines. "Both times I found his books immaculate. If he says the pages are real, the pages are real. And if the pages are real, then this council has been governing on a falsified record for four decades, and every decision made atop that falsification is void. Including," she added, with terrible mildness, "the decision to seal the annex."

Fifth Chair, a woman Ines had never heard speak—Maren would later tell him her name was Betha Quill, and that she had been the youngest member ever seated—put her face in her hands and said, to no one: "My grandmother hummed songs in a language I was told was made up. She said she invented them for me." She lifted her head. Her eyes were wet and furious. "She didn't invent them. Did she."

No one answered her either. But Ines saw it move through the circle like wind through wheat—the private reckonings, the half-remembered lullabies and unexplained heirlooms and grandparents who went quiet at certain names, forty years of small wrongnesses suddenly finding their explanation and turning, all together, to face the table.

And then the doors opened, and Collector Vane walked in as if the vote had already been held and won.

* * *

He had dressed formally, Ines noted—the gray coat with the silver gate-and-key at the collar, the dress of a man attending an execution, possibly his own. He crossed the chamber without asking leave, because Vane had never in his life asked leave, and took up a position beside the empty ninth chair, which was either insolence or homage, and perhaps Vane himself did not know which.

"Council," he said. "Forgive the interruption. I understand a matter touching on guild security is under discussion, and I felt my absence would be dereliction."

"Your presence was not requested," Ferrant said.

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly. "It rarely is. And yet here we are." He looked at the annex ledger, lying open on the table like a dissected thing, and Ines watched him look at it, and understood that Vane had already read every word—that he had probably read it years ago, that he might have been the one who hid it, or the one who failed to destroy it, or both. "Before this body does anything irreversible, I'd ask the members to consider what they're actually voting on. Not history. History is settled; nothing done in this room returns Vessany to the maps. What you're voting on is a door. One door. And what comes through it."

"And what comes through it?" Ines asked.

Vane turned to him, and for a moment the courtesy dropped, and what was underneath was so tired and so old that Ines nearly pitied him. "Grief," Vane said. "Forty years of it, preserved fresh, because the people who carry it never got to bury it. Grief with nowhere to go, looking for someone to blame. And blame, Marchetti, always finds the nearest keeper." He gestured at the walls, the dome, the whole sleeping edifice of the Ledgerhouse. "This guild exists because the world learned, long ago, that some things are safer kept than carried. We are the world's strongroom. You want to throw the doors open and hand out the contents because the contents are sad. Sadness is not a mandate."

"Neither is fear," said Betha Quill.

"No," Vane said. "Fear is just older, and it has a better record." He straightened. "I move that the question be put to a formal vote, as charter requires, and that the options be stated plainly, without poetry. Option one: the gate is sealed. Permanently. Masonry, wardwork, revocation of the passage clause—the full rite of closing, performed publicly, witnessed, minuted. Option two: the gate is opened at the coming solstice, per the original charter terms, with whatever consequences follow, borne by whoever survives them." He smiled, thin as a razor's edge. "There is no third option. There never was. The charter allows no abstention on matters of the gate. Every chair votes. Even the empty one counts as nay—I'll see to the paperwork myself."

"That is not procedural," Corvus began.

"It is precisely procedural," Vane said. "I wrote the amendment myself, eleven years ago. It passed while you were all busy with budget disputes. Check the minutes." He glanced at Ines, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was the cruelest thing yet. "You should know, keeper, that I take no pleasure in this. You were the best of them. Invisible, meticulous, faithful. I offered you a way out. You chose a country over a career. I can't even call it foolish—it's just the oldest choice there is, and it always costs the same."

The candle had burned down to its final ring. Ferrant looked at it, then at the circle, then at the empty ninth chair, and Ines saw her arrive at the understanding that arrived for all of them at once: that the vote did not matter, that it had never mattered, that Vane had called it not to decide the gate's fate but to force the guild to choose its own—to show every keeper, by roll call, exactly who among them believed in the forgetting, so that the believers could be strengthened and the doubters marked.

Or so that the doubters, seeing themselves outnumbered, would fold.

"Then let us vote," Ferrant said quietly. "By show of hands. Seal the gate."

Five hands rose. Ferrant's. Hale's. Two others. And the fifth—

Ines watched Aldous Marr raise his hand, smooth and prompt and sorrowful, and felt nothing so much as admiration, distant and cold, for craftsmanship.

"Open the gate," Ferrant said.

Three hands. Corvus. Quill. And after a moment that stretched like a wire about to part—Betha Quill again, no, wait, she had already voted; the third hand belonged to the silent Sixth Chair, old Mother Anselm, who had not spoken a single word all session and who now raised her arm with the slow deliberation of a woman lifting something very heavy, and said, in a voice like a drawer opening after decades:

"My sister was born in Vessany. I would like to know where she is buried."

The chamber absorbed this. Five to three. One seat empty, counting as nay. Six to three, if one counted ghosts, and in the Ledgerhouse one always counted ghosts.

"So recorded," Ferrant said. "The gate will be sealed. Masonry begins tomorrow at dawn. The rite of closing will be conducted at solstice, in public, per the collector's amendment." She closed the annex ledger with both hands, gently, as one closes the eyes of the dead, and slid it back across the table to Ines. "Keeper Marchetti. Your audience is ended."

The eight of them rose. The circle broke. And Ines stood alone a moment longer in the painted dark, under constellations that matched no living sky, holding a book of stolen names against his chest, understanding with perfect clarity that the vote had changed nothing except the clock.

Eleven days. And now masons, and mortar, and Vane's men measuring the corridor for brick.

He walked out through the doors of black oak, past the words *WE KEEP WHAT THE WORLD WOULD LOSE*, and found Maren waiting in the corridor with her arms folded and her eyes already reading his face.

"Well?" she said.

"They voted to seal it," Ines said. "Six to three."

Maren nodded slowly, unsurprised, unsurprisable. Then: "And the three?"

Ines allowed himself the smallest breath of something that was not quite hope. "Three," he said, "is enough to start a schism. And I know where the founder kept his vault."

Above them, faint through forty feet of stone, the Ledgerhouse began—very quietly, in corridors none of them could see—to turn.
