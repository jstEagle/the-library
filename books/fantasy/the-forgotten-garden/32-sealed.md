## 32. Sealed

The vote was not close.

Vane had expected it to be close. He had spent three nights preparing for a close vote—counting the council in his head the way other men counted coins, weighing each keeper's debts and fears and small ambitions like a moneylender at year's end. He had prepared arguments for a tie. He had prepared arguments for losing. He had even, in the worst hour of the night, prepared arguments for what he would do if he won by a margin so thin it would smell of rot for years.

He had not prepared for twenty-two votes to four.

"Then it is decided," said Elder Corvane, her voice dry as ledger-paper. "The gate is to be sealed. Permanently."

The chamber of the guild's council was a long room built to make men feel small—low ceiling, high windows, forty years of candle smoke staining the stone the color of old tea. Vane stood at its center because he always stood at its center, hands folded behind his back, face arranged into the expression he had practiced for thirty years: courteous, patient, faintly bored. The face of a man who has already thought of everything.

Around him sat the keepers of the Ledgerhouse, and they would not look at him. That was how he knew he had won completely. Men look at you when your victory is interesting. When it is merely inevitable, they study their own hands.

Four votes against. He knew all four names before Corvane read them aloud. Keeper Aldous, who was old enough to remember rumors. The twins Sera and Imre Fen, who had never agreed on anything except this. And young Pell, the junior keeper whose report had started everything—the boy who had come to Vane with shaking hands and the location of a hidden annex, and who now could not meet anyone's eyes.

*He will be trouble later,* Vane noted, filing the thought where he filed everything. *Or he will be useful. They are usually the same thing.*

"The masons," Corvane continued, "will require instruction."

"They have them," Vane said. "I drafted the specifications last night." He drew a folded sheet from inside his coat and laid it on the table with both hands, deliberately, so they could see he did not tremble. "Stone from the quarry at Hallowmere—it takes mortar slowly, which means the seal sets true. No doorframe left standing. No hinge left hanging. We brick the passage entire, floor to vault, and we carve nothing above it. A wall that looks like every other wall."

"And the keeper?" someone asked. "Marchetti is still—"

"Marchetti," Vane said, "is no longer a concern of this council."

It was not entirely a lie. Ines Marchetti had fled the burning archive two nights ago with a stolen map and a stolen book, and Vane's collectors had chased him into the city's lower streets and lost him there, in the warren of cellars older than the guild itself, where the map's shifting geography made pursuit a fool's errand. Vane knew exactly where the man was going. There was only one place left for him to go.

But the council did not need to hear that. The council needed to hear that the matter was closed, that the guild had voted and the guild had spoken and the world would continue to hold its single, survivable shape. Fear was a solvent; certainty was mortar. Vane had learned which one built walls.

"Collector Vane." It was Corvane again, and something in her tone made him turn. She was the eldest keeper, older than the erasure itself, though she claimed—loudly, often—to remember nothing of it. Her eyes were pale and patient as a fish's. "You have served this house faithfully for thirty years. You understand better than any of us why this must be done."

"I do," Vane said.

"Then perhaps you would supervise the work personally."

Every head in the room lifted slightly. It was an honor, technically. It was also, Vane understood instantly, a sentence: let the man who wanted it most stand and watch it done, so that none of the rest of them ever had to think about it again. The guild was very good at distributing its guilt the way it distributed its profits—in shares too small for any one holder to feel the weight.

"Of course," Vane said. "It would be my privilege."

*Privilege.* The word tasted like a coin found under a corpse.

* * *

They began the work at dawn, because masons are honest men and honest men prefer to begin things in daylight, and because Vane had specified dawn in his instructions without admitting to himself why.

The gate corridor ran down beneath the Ledgerhouse like a throat—a long descent of worn steps, then a passage lined with shelves of charter copies and dead ledgers, then the antechamber, and then the door.

Vane had not stood before the door in eleven years. He told himself, walking down, that this was because there had been no reason to. The truth, which he permitted himself to examine for exactly the length of the staircase and no longer, was that the door made a sound when you approached it. Not audibly. Something beneath audition—a pressure in the ears, a fullness, like the moment before a storm breaks or a word is spoken that cannot be taken back. Younger keepers called it humming. Vane had never called it anything. Naming it would have meant admitting he heard it.

Now he stood in the antechamber with six masons and a foreman named Brakka, a broad gray woman with forearms like mooring rope, and he looked at the door for the first time in over a decade.

It was smaller than memory insisted. That was the first betrayal. In his dreams—and he did dream of it, though he would have cut out his tongue before admitting that too—the gate was vast, a cathedral mouth, wide enough for a whole country to pour through. In fact it was a door. An ordinary, human-scaled door of dark wood bound in iron, set into a frame of older stone, and above the lintel a carved inscription worn nearly smooth:

**OPEN FOR NONE. REMEMBER FOR ALL.**

Someone had once tried to chisel it out. Vane could see the scars of the attempt, half-finished, abandoned. He wondered which side of the argument that anonymous vandal had been on.

"You're certain about the depth, Collector?" Brakka asked. She had a mason's bluntness; she looked at the door the way she'd look at any load-bearing problem. "Three feet of stone is generous. Two would hold."

"Three," Vane said. "And iron rod through the center, set in lead. If it is to be done, it is to be done past the reach of regret."

Brakka shrugged and turned to her crew. "You heard him. Strip the frame."

The hammers began.

* * *

There is a sound a door makes when it is being unmade, and Vane discovered he had no name for that either.

Wood does not scream, but iron groans, and the old hinges came away with a low complaint that traveled down the corridor and up through the floors of the guild above, and Vane saw two of the younger masons pause and glance at each other, and he understood that they felt it too—that everyone who worked in this house for long enough absorbed some sediment of the door's strangeness, the way a cellar absorbs cold.

They worked through the morning. The frame came out in pieces. The threshold stone, which was older than the guild, older than the city street above it, took four men and a lever to lift, and when it came free the cavity beneath was simply earth—dark, ordinary earth, smelling of rain and roots. Several of the masons laughed with relief. Vane did not laugh. He crouched at the edge of the hole and looked down into that plain black soil and thought: *forty years ago a country ended here, and underneath it is only dirt.*

That should have comforted him. It did not.

He walked the length of the corridor while the masons mixed mortar, checking the shelves out of habit, and it was there, in the third alcove, that he found the thing he would spend the rest of the day pretending he had not seen.

A ledger, shelved among the charter copies where it did not belong. Thin. Recent binding. And on the first page, in a careful clerk's hand he recognized immediately—because he had read thousands of pages in that hand across thirty years of audits, because he had once recommended its owner for promotion—

*Copied from the razored leaves. Page one of ninety-one. Let no one say the guild destroyed everything. Let no one say I did not try to save what I could.*

Ines Marchetti's hand. Copies of the erased pages, hidden here in the corridor of the door itself, tucked behind the charters like a letter slipped under a bedroom door. Vane stood holding the little book and understood two things at once, the way one understands lightning and thunder as a single event: that Marchetti had been here, inside the sealed corridor, within the last day; and that he had wanted Vane—or whoever came next—to find this. It was not hidden well. It was hidden *readably*. It was a message.

*Let no one say I did not try.*

Vane closed the book. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; it was the ground that sometimes seemed unsteady, these days, as if the whole edifice of forgetting were a table with one short leg.

He could burn it. He could lock it in his office and add it to the small, growing, unspeakable collection of things he kept and did not destroy and did not explain. He could leave it where it lay and let the masons' dust bury it behind three feet of Hallowmere stone.

He put it inside his coat, next to his heart, where he kept such things, and went back to watch the wall go up.

* * *

By late afternoon the door was gone.

Not removed—*entombed*. The masons had bricked the empty frame first, then built outward, course over course, and now the passage ended in a fresh wall of pale new stone that looked obscene against the ancient dark of the corridor, like a bandage on a wound too old to heal. Brakka's crew worked fast and well. They were proud of it. Masons are always proud of walls; it is the trade's particular innocence, Vane thought, to build divisions and call it craft.

Iron rod through the center, set in lead. Three feet deep. Past the reach of regret.

The solstice was nine days away. Vane had counted. He had counted the way a drowning man counts the distance to shore, except that Vane was not sure anymore which direction the shore lay.

"Last course," Brakka announced, slapping mortar onto the final row of stones. "You want to set the capstone yourself, Collector? Some like to."

"No," Vane said. "Finish it."

She finished it. She scraped the joints clean, stepped back, and looked at the wall with a craftsman's satisfaction, and then—because she was honest, because honest men and women always see more than they're meant to—she glanced sideways at Vane and her satisfaction faltered.

"You don't look pleased, sir, if you'll pardon me."

"I'm pleased," Vane said. "The work is good."

"It is that." She wiped her trowel and began gathering her tools. "Only my grandmother used to say, you can tell a bad wall by how the builder stares at it. Good evening to you."

She herded her crew up the stairs, their voices receding into echoes and then into nothing, and then Vane was alone in the corridor with the wall.

* * *

He stayed a long time.

The lamps burned low. Somewhere above him the guild carried on its evening business—the clink of the counting room, a junior keeper's footsteps crossing the atrium, the great front gate booming shut for the night. The sounds of a living institution, indifferent to what lay beneath it. In forty years, he thought, those sounds would not change at all. In forty years no one above would know there had ever been a door down here. The wall would grow old. Someone would hang a shelf against it. A child born in the guild's service would lean against it waiting for a friend and feel nothing, absolutely nothing, through three feet of stone and iron and lead.

That was the peace he had fought for. That was the peace he had just purchased with twenty-two votes and his own name.

So why, he asked the wall, did it feel like this?

Why did it feel like standing at a graveside he had dug himself? Why did the silence behind the fresh stone seem less like absence than like *holding*—like breath held, like a word held, like a country held under the surface of the world with its eyes open?

He thought of his parents.

He had thought of them very little for thirty years, by discipline, the way a man avoids pressing a bruise. Two faces from the earliest years, already dissolving: a woman singing in a language he could no longer pronounce, a man's hands folding paper into birds that flew badly. He had been seven when they gave him to the guild's orphan intake and walked back into a country that was, within the season, nowhere at all. He had been told they died in the plague of remembering. He had believed it because believing it was survivable and because the guild fed him, taught him, promoted him, and asked in return only one thing: that he help ensure no one else ever caught what killed them.

*A plague of remembering.* He had said those words to Marchetti himself, offered them like medicine. And Marchetti had looked at him—not with hatred, which Vane could have handled, but with pity, which he could not—and said, quietly: *Who told you it was a plague? The same people who erased the cure?*

Vane pressed his palm flat against the new stone.

It was cold. Of course it was cold; it was mortar and Hallowmere granite, hours old, in an underground corridor in winter. But the cold went through his hand and up his arm and settled somewhere behind his sternum, and he stood there like a man touching a coffin lid, and he permitted himself—one moment, one only, because he had won, because winners are owed one moment of honesty before they spend the rest of their lives defending the win—he permitted himself the doubt.

*What if I have sealed us in with them?*

*What if the wall doesn't keep the past out, but keeps us in—entombed in a peace built on a theft, breathing borrowed air, calling the tomb a house?*

His mother's song rose in him, unbidden, three notes of it, the only three notes that forty years had failed to dissolve. He did not hum it. He stood perfectly still until it passed, the way one stands still until a vertigo passes, staring at a fixed point.

The fixed point was the wall.

When the moment was over—and it did end; moments of doubt always end, that is their tragedy and their mercy—he straightened his coat, composed his face into courtesy and patience and faint boredom, and turned toward the stairs.

Behind him, the fresh stone held. It would hold for years. He had built it past the reach of regret.

But as he climbed, hand trailing along the old cold rock of the corridor wall, Vane became aware of a sound beneath the silence—faint, rhythmic, patient, coming not from the wall ahead but from everywhere and nowhere, from the stone itself, from the deep foundations of the guild and the city above it and the dark soil under the threshold where a country waited.

Not humming. He had never called it humming.

It was knocking.

Slow. Even. Like knuckles on a door. Like fingers on the inside of a lid.

Like something that had all the time in the world, and nine days, and intended to use both.
