## 1. The Valley That Refuses Magic

The Hollow did not believe in magic, and magic, being a courteous thing in its way, had long ago agreed to return the favor.

Quentin Vasquez had lived her whole life inside that mutual refusal. She was thirty-one years old and she had never seen a spell cast, never watched a charm take light, never felt the particular pressure of an enchantment settling over a room like weather. The valley saw to that. Whatever ran through the world beyond the treeline—whatever made rivers sing and hedges bloom out of season and dead men walk if they were stubborn enough—stopped at the ridgeline as though it had struck glass. The old folk said the Hollow was a cupped hand, a held breath, a place where the world's wilder currents simply declined to flow. Quentin thought of it more plainly: the valley was quiet, and quiet was the only thing she had ever trusted.

She sat at her workbench with the shutters open to the late morning, carving a memorial bone for Widow Anselm, who had died on Tuesday with her hands folded and her mouth slightly open, as though interrupted mid-gossip. The bone lay across two cloth pads on her bench—a shin bone from one of Anselm's own cattle, chosen because Anselm had loved that animal stubbornly and against all sense, and because Quentin believed a person should be remembered in something they had loved.

Her knife moved in slow curls. Thin shavings fell like pale petals onto the floorboards, and through her hands, faint as breath on a winter window, the dead murmured.

They always murmured. Not the widow—not yet; Anselm's whisper would not settle into bone for another day or two—but the others, the ones who had passed through Quentin's hands over the years, whose names she had cut into cattle bone and river stone and once, memorably, into the handle of a shovel because the family could afford nothing else. They lingered in the grain of things she had carved. They spoke to her fingers the way heat speaks to a palm held near a fire: wordlessly at first, then in fragments, then—when the carving was finished and the grief in it was true—in something very like a voice.

*Cold,* said the ghost of Harlan Pike, dead eleven years, whose memorial hung above his daughter's mantel three houses down. *It is cold where we are, carver.*

"It is October," Quentin said aloud, to no one, in the manner of a woman who had long since stopped finding it strange to answer empty rooms. "You chose the winter to die in. You might have waited for May."

A rustle of amusement, dry as leaf-fall. Harlan had been a jokester in life and had seen no reason to change professions afterward. His voice faded, and beneath it came the older murmur, the one that never quite left her—the low tide-sound of every soul she had ever carved for, layered like sediment, like rings in wood. It was not unpleasant. It was company, of a strange and one-sided kind. It was also, she knew, the closest thing to magic the Hollow permitted, which was to say it was not magic at all, or so she told herself. It was only craft. Only listening. Anyone with steady hands and patient ears might do it.

This was a lie she had been telling herself since she was nine years old, and she kept it the way other people kept saints' medals: worn smooth, carried always, never examined too closely in good light.

Because the truth was that no one else in the Hollow heard what she heard. No one else's hands ached with borrowed grief. No one else woke some nights with a stranger's name dissolving on their tongue, a name belonging to someone dead sixty years whose bones Quentin had never even touched—only carved near, only worked in the same valley, as though the dead here had all quietly agreed that she was the post office and their letters would find their way to her eventually whether she wanted them or not.

She wanted them. That was the shameful part. She wanted them the way a lonely child wants footsteps overhead.

* * *

The workshop smelled of linseed oil and tallow and the sweet dust of bone, and Quentin had arranged her life around that smell the way a snail arranges itself around its shell. One room below for work, one room above for sleeping, a cookstove that had opinions about damp weather, a garden gone half to frost. On the wall beside the door hung her tools in their places: gouges and veiners and a set of tiny gravers her mother had owned before her, handles darkened by two generations of grip. Her mother's mark was still cut into the bench's underside, deep enough that Quentin sometimes rested her thumb in it without thinking, the way you'd touch a scar.

Mireia Vasquez had been a carver too. Had been, in fact, better than Quentin would ever be, if the old folk were to be believed—and they loved to be believed, especially about the dead. Mireia had carved for half the valley before Quentin was born, and then she had stopped, abruptly, when Quentin was four years old, and taken up farming instead, and never said why, and died when Quentin was nineteen of a fever that burned through her in three days and left her daughter alone in a house full of tools and silence.

Alone. Well. Not entirely. There was Marisol Reyes next door, who was seventy-one and sharp as a new nail and had appointed herself Quentin's aunt without consulting anyone, least of all Quentin. There were the neighbors who still brought their dead to her door, sheepish and grieving, leaving cuts of meat and bolts of cloth on her step because barter was easier than thanks. There was the dog—half wolf, wholly opinionated—who had adopted her six winters ago and currently lay across the doorway like a drawn bolt.

But company was not the same as being known, and Quentin had learned early that the two things lived far apart. How could they know her? She could not explain the hearing without explaining everything, and everything included questions she had no answers to. Why her? Why here, in the one valley in the world where nothing uncanny could survive? What was she, that the dead found her even in a place that refused them?

So she wore her strangeness close, under the apron, like a second skin, and let the valley believe she was merely a skilled craftswoman with a solemn manner, and she told herself this was safety, and most days she almost managed to believe it.

* * *

By midday the memorial had begun to take shape. Quentin worked in the old style, the one her mother had used: interlaced lines first, the long unbroken band that stood for the road a soul walked, and then the smaller marks within it—one notch for each year of the life, a curl for each person who had loved the departed, a closed ring for each promise kept until death. Widow Anselm's band was filling nicely. Seventy-eight notches. Fourteen curls. Quentin paused over the promises, counting on her memory of the funeral gossip, and decided on six, though privately she suspected Anselm had kept more and simply never told anyone about them.

*She counted wrong,* whispered a voice near her wrist, thin and papery. *I kept seven.*

Quentin's knife stilled. "And who are you?" she asked softly. "I haven't carved you."

No answer. There rarely was, to that question. The dead were like birds at a window—drawn to the light of her attention, unwilling to explain how they'd found it.

She sat back and flexed her fingers, watching the light move across the yard. Frost had come in the night and silvered the bean rows. Beyond the garden fence the pastures climbed toward the treeline, and the treeline stood along the northern ridge like a wall of dark sentries, black pines shoulder to shoulder, holding back whatever the rest of the world was. Children in the Hollow grew up on warnings about the forest's edge. Don't gather wood past the marker stones. Don't follow a light into the trees, there are no lights in the trees, and if you see one, run home and don't look back. Quentin had absorbed the warnings the way she'd absorbed her letters, without ever questioning their grammar. The world beyond was magic-soaked, and magic was hunger wearing beautiful clothes, and the Hollow was safe because the Hollow was empty of it.

Safe. She turned the word over. It had the flavor of a food eaten so long one has forgotten it is food at all.

The dog lifted its head suddenly, ears forward, staring north.

Quentin followed its gaze. The ridge stood as it always stood—dark, still, indifferent. But the dog did not lie down again, and after a moment Quentin realized why the sight of it snagged at her: the pines were moving. Not swaying. There was no wind. The upper branches trembled in a long line along the crest, west to east, like a shiver passing through a body, like something running along the far side of a wall and brushing it as it went.

Then stillness. A jay called somewhere, absurdly ordinary. The dog whined once and put its head back on its paws, but its eyes stayed open.

"Nothing," Quentin told it, and herself, and the murmuring dead. "Nothing ever comes down from there."

The words settled into the workshop the way a stone settles into a well—slowly, and with a sound at the bottom that she chose not to hear.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon. This was the hour she loved best, the light going amber through the south window, the shavings piling up, her hands busy enough that the voices softened to a lullaby-murmur and she could almost pretend she was only a woman making a thing of beauty for a grieving family. She thought about Anselm's daughters, who would hang the bone above the hearth and touch it on hard evenings and feel, perhaps, a fraction less alone. That was the trade she had made with her life: her own loneliness, spent daily, converted into small mercies for others. It was not a bad trade. Some days it was even a good one.

She was setting the final curl into the seventh promise—she had decided to trust the whispering stranger and carve seven after all—when the light changed.

Not dimmed. *Changed.* The amber went thin and gray-green, the way it does when a cloud crosses the sun, except there were no clouds; the sky over the valley was hard blue from rim to rim. The dog was on its feet now, hackles up, facing the door. And through her hands—through the bone under her hands—came a feeling Quentin had never known in thirty-one years in the Hollow.

Pressure. A pushing, a leaning, like wind against the far side of a door.

The dead went silent. All of them at once, every murmur, every whisper, the whole sedimented chorus of her life's work—hushed, the way a forest goes hushes when a predator moves through it. In the sudden quiet Quentin could hear her own heart, and the tick of the cooling stove, and underneath both, faint and rhythmic and wrong, a sound from the direction of the ridge.

Footsteps. Many of them, or one thing that stepped like many. And then—closer, much closer, stumbling, ragged, human—the sound of someone running down the pasture slope toward her door, and the wet, labored drag of a breath that was not going to last.

The dog growled low. Quentin rose, knife still in her hand, and through the open shutter she saw him: a man coming down through the frosted beans, crashing, staggering, dressed in clothes that belonged to no tailor in the Hollow, dark with blood from hip to shoulder. Behind him, up at the treeline, the shadows between the pines seemed to lean together like conspirators—and stop. All at once. As though they had struck glass.

As though the valley had refused them, too.

The man fell at the edge of her garden, crawled the last few yards, and got one bloodied hand onto her doorstep just as she pulled the door wide. He looked up at her with eyes going already distant, and he did not ask for help, or water, or a priest. He fumbled inside his coat and pressed something into her free hand—a fold of stiff, cracked leather, warm from his body—and his lips moved around a name.

Not his name. Hers, or nearly. The name of a woman twenty years dead.

"…Vasquez," the stranger breathed, and the light went out of him between one syllable and the next, and in the bone still lying on her workbench behind her, the dead began, very softly, to scream.
