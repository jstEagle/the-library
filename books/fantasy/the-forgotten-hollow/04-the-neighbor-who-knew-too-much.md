## 4. The Neighbor Who Knew Too Much

Marisol Vane had lived in the Hollow for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things worth knowing: the river lied about its depth in autumn, grief kept better than salt if you wrapped it right, and Quentin Vasquez was a terrible liar.

So when Marisol saw smoke rising from the workshop chimney at an hour when Quentin should have been asleep—when she saw, through her kitchen window, a stranger staggering up the bone-dust path with his side blooming dark against his shirt, and Quentin's door opening before he even knocked—Marisol set down her tea, put on her boots, and began to plan.

She had watched the treeline all evening from her window, the way she did most evenings, though she'd never told anyone why. The Hollow's border wasn't marked by anything so honest as a fence or a stone. It was a feeling. A hush. You crossed out of it and the world got louder—not with sound, but with *presence*, like stepping from a quiet room into a market mid-argument. Marisol knew that feeling the way a tongue knows a missing tooth. She had felt it once, long ago, walking away.

And tonight she had seen something at the trees. Something that came right up to the edge of the valley and stopped, the way a dog stops at an invisible line, pacing, furious, unable to follow. It had been tall. It had been wrong. And then it had simply folded itself backward into the dark like a letter being closed.

Now there was a dead man in Quentin's workshop—or he would be dead soon; Marisol had seen enough dying to know its walk—and Quentin had not come to her, which meant Quentin intended to handle it alone, which meant Marisol would have to handle Quentin.

She let herself in without knocking. The door wasn't locked. That alone told her how rattled the girl was; Quentin locked her door the way other people said their prayers, nightly and without thinking.

The workshop smelled of bone dust and lamp oil and, beneath it, blood. The stranger lay on the long worktable among the shavings and half-finished memorials, his coat open, his breathing a wet rattle that Marisol heard from across the room. His skin had gone the gray of old tallow. Quentin stood over him with her hands pressed flat on the table's edge, knuckles white, staring at nothing.

"You saw him," Marisol said.

Quentin flinched. "I didn't hear you."

"No," Marisol agreed. "You never do." She hung her shawl on its peg, deliberately, giving the girl time to arrange her face. "You want to tell me who he is?"

"A stranger. He was bleeding. I couldn't just—" Quentin's jaw tightened. "He's dying, Mari."

"Mm." Marisol moved closer, looked at the stranger properly. Young-ish, under the pallor. Fine boots worn down at the heels, the kind of wear that came from running rather than walking. His fingers were stained with ink to the second knuckle, the mark of a man who wrote for a living, and there was a burn scar across his left palm shaped like a seal—a ring of interlocking lines, deliberate, ceremonial. Marisol's stomach dropped somewhere below her knees and stayed there.

She knew that scar. Not this man's scar. But that mark.

"Where is he hurt?" she asked, because her voice needed something to do while the rest of her went cold.

"Under the ribs. Something cut him, but the wound's—" Quentin hesitated. "It's wrong. It doesn't bleed right. It bleeds slow, like it's being poured."

*Poured.* Yes. Marisol had seen wounds like that once, in another life, in another country whose name she still could not say aloud in the Hollow without tasting iron. Wounds made by things that hunted souls and were careless with the meat they traveled in.

"He said something," Quentin said quietly. "Before he went under. He said my mother's name."

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft. Marisol watched Quentin watch the stranger, and saw on the girl's face the thing she had seen there since Quentin was nine years old and first asked, *Mari, do you remember what my mama sounded like?* — hope and dread braided so tight you couldn't tell which was holding which together.

"Sit down," Marisol said.

"I'm fine."

"*Sit down*, Quentin Vasquez, before I sit you down myself, and I've still got the arm for it."

Quentin sat. Marisol took the good chair, the one Quentin's mother had carved with her own hands back when her hands still did what she told them, and folded hers in her lap, and looked at the girl she had helped raise, and decided—finally, after thirty-one years of careful silence—that some silences cost more than they saved.

* * *

"I'm going to tell you a story," Marisol said. "And you're going to let me finish it before you ask your questions, because I know you, and you'll have forty of them, and some of them I can't answer yet."

Quentin said nothing. Her eyes went to the stranger's face, then back.

"When I was nineteen," Marisol began, "I left the Hollow."

She felt the words land. In the Hollow, leaving was not a thing people did. Leaving was a thing that happened to you—a fever took you, a winter took you, the river took you—and the valley absorbed the absence the way sand absorbs rain. Nobody *left*. Nobody talked about the ones who tried.

"There was a boy," Marisol said. "There's always a boy in these stories, isn't there? His name was Elio, and he was a ferryman's son from beyond the pass, and he used to come to the border in spring when the meltwater ran high, and we'd talk across it. Just talk. He couldn't cross in—the magic wouldn't carry him past the treeline, same as whatever chased your friend here couldn't follow him in. And I couldn't cross out. Or I thought I couldn't." She smiled, and knew it was a thin smile. "But wanting is a kind of key, querida. Nobody tells you that. Want hard enough, long enough, and one morning you wake up and the hush lets go of you, and you're standing outside the valley with wet grass on your boots, and you don't even remember deciding."

The lamp flame steadied. Quentin had gone very still.

"I went with him," Marisol said. "Three days' travel to his father's ferry. And the world out there—" She stopped. Started again. How did you describe color to someone who had only ever seen the Hollow's muted palette? How did you describe *loudness*? "Out there, everything has a voice. The stones. The rivers. The dead, most of all—they're everywhere out there, Quentin, thick as midges over the marsh, and every one of them wants something. Wants to be remembered, wants to be avenged, wants to finish the sentence they died in the middle of. I don't have your gift. I never heard them the way you hear bones. But I *felt* them. Like walking through cobwebs. Like walking through hands."

"What happened?" Quentin asked. Softly. Almost kindly.

"Elio's father sold passengers two ways," Marisol said. "The cheap way, and the way where you arrived whole. Most people paid coin for the cheap way and never knew the difference. But some travelers paid in other coin. Memory. Years. And there was a third thing the ferry carried, sometimes, at night, in boxes that breathed." She looked at the stranger on the table. At the seal-scar on his hand. "Souls, Quentin. Carried south, toward the mountain, in boxes. I saw one of the boxes opened. I shouldn't have looked. But I looked, and what was inside looked back at me, and it knew my name, though I'd never told anyone out there my name."

The workshop was silent except for the stranger's ragged breath and the small tick of cooling bone dust settling.

"I ran," Marisol said. "Not cleverly. Not bravely. I ran like an animal, and I lost two fingers to the frost coming home over the pass, and I lost—" she flexed her right hand, the one with the crooked knuckles, "—other things. Things I've never gotten back. When I crawled across the border into the Hollow, the hush closed over me like water, and everything out there went quiet, and I swore I would never look at that treeline again as long as I lived."

"But you do," Quentin said. "Look at it. Every night. I've seen your lamp in the window facing the trees."

"Yes." Marisol laughed, short and dry. "Because I'm a fool, and because fools keep watch. Because I promised myself that if the world ever came looking for this valley—if it ever found a crack in our walls—I would see it coming. And tonight I saw it, querida. Whatever chased that man walked right up to our border and *paced*. Do you understand? It wanted in. Things from out there don't want into the Hollow. There's nothing here for them. No magic to harvest, no souls to trade. Unless—" and here her voice went flat and hard, "—unless what they want is carrying something. Or someone."

Quentin's gaze dropped to the map.

Ah. So the girl had it already. Marisol had wondered. She'd seen the shape of folded paper in Quentin's pocket when the girl answered the door, seen her hand twitch toward it twice in the last five minutes like a tongue finding the gap in its teeth.

"Show me," Marisol said.

* * *

Quentin unfolded it on the table beside the stranger, weighting the corners with four smooth carving stones, and Marisol leaned over it and felt the bottom fall out of the last comfortable hour of her life.

The map was drawn on vellum gone soft with handling, inked in a hand that shook and steadied and shook again. It showed the lands east of the Hollow in obsessive detail: the river with its ferry crossing, the road south through country Marisol had run through with frostbite eating her hands, the salt city with its lanterns, and finally the mountain itself—drawn not from above but from within, a cutaway view, the peak peeled open like a fruit to show the chambers inside. And in the deepest chamber, rendered in ink so dense it looked like a hole burned in the page, was a single word:

**LEDGER.**

Beside the mountain, in fresher ink, someone had written: *thirty years. the debt comes due. find the carver's daughter.*

Marisol read it three times. The words did not improve with repetition.

"The carver's daughter," she said.

"That's me." Quentin's voice was steady. Her hands were not; Marisol saw them trembling where they braced on the table. "My mother was a carver. Everyone knows that. But Mari—*debt*. What debt? My mother never left the Hollow. She was born here, she died here, she's buried behind the meetinghouse under the rowan. She never owed anyone anything."

"Querida." Marisol reached out and covered one of those trembling hands with her own crooked-fingered one. "Your mother was born here. Are you certain? Certain the way you're certain of your own name?"

Quentin opened her mouth. Closed it. Marisol watched the question work its way down into the girl like water into a foundation, finding every crack.

"She never talked about before," Quentin said at last. "I asked, when I was little. She'd say the Hollow was the beginning and the end and that was all any soul needed. I thought—" She swallowed. "I thought she was just private."

"Everyone in this valley is private," Marisol said. "That's half of why they came. People wash up in the Hollow the way driftwood washes up on a beach, and none of us ask each other what sea we fell out of, because we know we'd have to answer the same question." She tapped the map, gently, beside the word *ledger*. "But driftwood doesn't get debts called in. Debts mean somebody kept accounts. And there's only one place out there that keeps accounts like this."

On the table, the stranger stirred.

They both turned. His eyes had opened—not fully, just to slits, gray-rimmed and fever-bright—and they fixed on Marisol with an intensity that had nothing left in it but purpose. His lips moved. Marisol bent close, close enough to smell the strange sweetness of the wound, the smell she remembered from thirty years ago and had spent thirty years forgetting.

"...Vane," the man whispered. "Marisol Vane. Brist said... Brist said if I couldn't make the crossing... find the Vane woman. She remembers the ferry."

Ice walked down Marisol's spine, vertebra by vertebra. "Who is Brist?"

"Caravan master. Exiles. He'll take you..." A cough racked him, deep and ruinous, and when it passed there was blood on his lips, blackish in the lamplight. "The ledger page. I stole a page. They know. They always know. The page names names—names they'll never free unless..." His hand crept across the table, fumbling, and caught Quentin's wrist. "Your mother. Elena Vasquez. Ask the ledger. Ask it what she bought. Ask it what she *paid*."

"My mother paid nothing," Quentin said, but her voice cracked in the middle of it, and the stranger smiled—a terrible, gentle smile, the smile of a man who had already left his body and was watching all this from somewhere the pain couldn't reach.

"Nobody pays nothing," he said. "That's the whole... that's the entire business model..."

His eyes rolled back. His grip on Quentin's wrist loosened, slid, and Marisol caught the man's shoulder and felt the tremor running through him like a struck bell. He wasn't dead yet. He was going to be, and soon, and there was nothing in Marisol's thirty-one years of mending broken neighbors that could stop what was pouring out of him.

"Listen to me," she said fiercely, leaning over him. "Brist. The caravan. Where do we find them?"

"South road. River ferry. New moon." Each phrase cost him visibly now, coins counted out of a nearly empty purse. "Tell him... tell him the page survived. Tell him I got it to the daughter." His gaze slid to Quentin, and something in it softened. "Elena's girl. She has your mother's... stubborn..."

And then he said one more thing, so quietly that Marisol almost missed it, so quietly that later she would convince herself she'd imagined it, except that Quentin went white as fresh-carved bone and stayed that way for an hour:

"He's still at the treeline. It doesn't leave. It *waits*. It knows the debt isn't settled until the daughter crosses."

The breath went out of him after that, slowly, like air from a punctured bellows, and did not come back.

* * *

They sat with the body for a long time. Marisol lit a second lamp, because the room felt darker than two lamps could fix, and went through the stranger's pockets with Quentin watching. They found little: a stub of pencil, a tin of matches, three coins from countries Quentin had no names for, and a scrap of paper bearing a single line in that shaking-steadying hand: *the shelf remembers what the court denies.*

No ledger page. Marisol searched twice, then a third time, feeling along every seam of the coat.

"It's not on him," she said finally.

"It's in the workshop somewhere." Quentin was already on her knees, checking under the table, behind the tool chest. "He had time. Before I found him—he was conscious when he stumbled past the border post, the shepherd's boy saw him. He had minutes. Maybe more. He hid it."

"Or whoever sent him took it back."

"Then why send him at all?" Quentin stood, dust on her knees, and there was something new in her face—something Marisol recognized from mirrors, from thirty years ago, from the girl she had been walking out of the Hollow with wet grass on her boots. Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, a terrible bright willingness. "Why die to deliver a map and a message if the thing they were delivering was already gone? The map's real. The warning's real. *Find the carver's daughter*—that's real, Mari. Which means the page might be too."

Outside, the wind came down off the ridge and combed through the valley, and for a moment—only a moment—Marisol could have sworn she heard it catch on something at the treeline. Something patient. Something that waited the way debts wait.

She looked at Quentin, this girl she had bandaged and fed and scolded and loved without ever once saying so, this girl who was about to do exactly what Marisol had done at nineteen and walk out of the only safe place in the world.

"Well," Marisol said, taking her shawl back off the peg, because if the girl was going to be a fool, then a fool would need company, and Marisol had thirty years of watching that treeline to answer for. "New moon's eleven days off. We'd best start packing."

Quentin stared at her. "You're coming?"

"Someone has to keep you from lying to yourself," Marisol said. "You're terrible at it."
