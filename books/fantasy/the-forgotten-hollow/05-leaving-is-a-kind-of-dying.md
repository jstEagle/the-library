## 5. Leaving Is a Kind of Dying

Quentin had always believed the Hollow would be the last thing she ever saw.

She had imagined it wrong, of course—imagined herself old and bent over her carving bench, the smell of bone dust in her lungs, the murmur of the dead gone quiet at last because there would be no one left who remembered them to whisper about. She had imagined dying here the way her mother had died here: slowly, completely, belonging to the valley the way a stone belongs to the riverbed it has settled into.

She had not imagined packing before dawn with shaking hands, choosing which knives to take as though she were choosing which fingers to keep.

The workshop was cold. She had let the fire die two nights ago, after the stranger's body stopped whispering and started simply being a body, and now the chill had worked itself into everything—the mortar between the stones, the grain of the bench, the marrow of the bones laid out in their careful rows. She moved through the gray half-light by memory, gathering what she needed without letting herself think too hard about any single object, because thinking would be the death of leaving.

Her carving tools first. The roll of oiled leather that had been her mother's, its ties gone soft as cloth from forty years of hands. Inside it lay the set like a family portrait: the fine gouges for detail work, the heavy chisel for roughing out a form, the little hooked knife her mother had called *the listener* because it was the one you used when you wanted to hear what the bone had to say. Quentin touched each blade once, the way you'd touch a sleeping child's head, and rolled them up.

Then the harder choices. She could not carry everything. The Hollow taught you to travel light or not at all—it wasn't a place that rewarded ambition—but she had never actually traveled anywhere, so all her lightness was theoretical. She took the whetstone. She took the small tin of rendered tallow she rubbed into finished pieces to make them glow faintly warm in candlelight, a trick that had nothing to do with magic and everything to do with craft. She took three days of bread and the smoked fish Marisol had left on her step yesterday morning without a word, wrapped in cloth, arranged with pointed neatness—a message in the language of food that said *I know, and I am not going to stop you, and I am furious about both*.

The map she carried against her chest, folded into eight creases, inside her shirt where it lay like a second heartbeat.

And the stranger's bones. She stood over them a long moment, laid out on the bench in anatomical order because she could not bring herself to bury him yet—not until she understood what he had died trying to tell her, not until she could put him in the ground knowing his whispering was finished rather than interrupted. His skull watched her with the patient blankness of the dead. *Go*, it seemed to say. Or perhaps that was only her own guilt, wearing his voice.

"I'll come back," she told him, and heard how young she sounded, how much like a promise made to a grave. "I'll come back and finish this."

The bones said nothing. In the Hollow, they never said much—that was the whole point of the place, the whole mercy of it. Magic could not follow you past the treeline, and neither could most of the dead's noise. What Quentin heard through her hands here was a fraction of what existed elsewhere; she'd known that the way you know the sun is larger than it looks. A rumor of a truth. She had built an entire life on a fraction, and told herself the fraction was the whole sky.

She took her mother's shawl off its peg. She banked the cold hearth so no spark could wander. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at the workshop that had been her entire world: the bench scarred by thirty years of blades, the shelves of finished memorials waiting for families who would come looking for her and find only dust and absence, the window where the stranger's blood had dried to a brown crescent on the sill.

Leaving is a kind of dying, her mother used to say. You have to be willing to be someone else on the other side of the door.

Quentin had thought that was poetry. Standing in the doorway, she understood it was instructions.

* * *

Marisol was waiting at the edge of her garden, exactly as Quentin had known she would be, wrapped in a coat too heavy for the season, holding a lantern she didn't need because the sky was going pale at the east like a bruise healing.

"You're taking the river road," Marisol said. Not a question.

"The map says the ferry."

"The map." Marisol's mouth went thin. "A dead man's map. Drawn by who knows whom, leading to who knows what, and you're following it because—" She stopped herself. Quentin watched her do it, watched her swallow whatever came next, and loved her fiercely for it. "Because your mother's name was in his mouth when he died. Yes. All right."

"I didn't say that."

"You've been saying nothing else since he bled out on your floor. You say it with your face." Marisol pressed something into her hands: a small bundle, oilcloth-wrapped, heavier than it should have been. "Dried meat. Apples from the tree your mother planted, which I expect you to eat before they rot out of spite. And this." From her own pocket, a flat stone on a leather cord, drilled through and polished smooth. "It's nothing. It's just a rock. But my grandmother swore river-stones keep you steady when the ground stops being trustworthy, and I find I believe her this morning more than I believe in anything sensible."

Quentin closed her fingers around it. It was warm from Marisol's pocket, and that warmth undid her more than tears would have.

"Come back," Marisol said. It came out harsh, almost angry, the way she said everything that mattered. "I don't care what you find under that mountain. I don't care if it's your soul in a jar or your mother's ghost or the answer to every question you've ever choked down. You come back. Do you hear me? This valley has taken enough people. It doesn't get you too."

"It already got me," Quentin said quietly. "That's the problem, Mari. I think it got me before I was ever born."

They held each other for a moment in the freezing garden, among the black canes of the raspberry bushes, and then Marisol pushed her away with both hands, firmly, the way you push a boat off from shore, and turned her back, and did not watch her go. That was the kindness of it. That was Marisol all over: she gave you the dignity of not being seen while you were still afraid enough to turn around.

* * *

The treeline stood dark against the graying east, and Quentin walked toward it through a world she was seeing for the last time—or telling herself she was, so that every ordinary thing would burn itself into memory. The well with its moss-slicked lip. The crooked fence post her father had never gotten around to straightening. The long meadow where the grass grew silver with frost, each blade edged in light, the whole field glittering like it was made of the same stuff as the stars going out overhead. Her breath hung in front of her and drifted apart. Somewhere a rooster complained about the hour. Ordinary things. Unbearable things.

At the border of her own yard she stopped and did the thing she had promised herself she wouldn't do: she knelt and pressed her bare palm flat against the frozen earth, fingers spread, listening.

The dead of the Hollow murmured back, faint as voices through a wall. Old Tomas, whose memorial femur she'd carved eleven years ago, humming his one remembered song. The children lost to the fever winter, all together, all the time, a soft chorus like wind in eaves. And beneath them, deeper than the others, threaded through the valley's silence like a vein of ore through stone: her mother. Not words. Never words, anymore. Just presence, the way a hearth stays warm in a room hours after the fire dies. Thirty years dead and still the Hollow held her shape.

"I'm going to find out what happened to you," Quentin whispered into the ground. "Whatever it cost. Whatever it means. I'm going to know."

The earth kept its cold counsel. She stood, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and walked toward the trees.

The last hundred paces were the longest of her life. She felt the valley's attention on her—not magic, the Hollow had none, but something older than magic, the plain gravity of home, pulling at her heels. Every instinct she owned screamed at her to stop, to turn, to go back to the bench and pick up a bone and carve until the questions drowned. The Hollow had raised her on safety the way other places raised children on stories, and safety, she realized with a strange clarity, was just fear that had learned good manners.

She crossed the treeline.

For ten steps, nothing. Only pine needles underfoot, the smell of resin and rot, the dark closing over her like water. Then—

The world *inhaled*.

There was no gentler word for it. One moment the air was air; the next it was thick with presence, saturated, alive, and Quentin staggered as if she'd stepped from a dim room into full noon. Sound poured into her through the soles of her boots, through her palms, through the skin of her face. The trees were *loud*—not the trees themselves but everything that had ever happened beneath them, soaked into root and soil like rain into cloth. A deer had died here once, years ago; she felt the small bright fact of it like a struck bell. A man had carved his lover's name into bark and she felt the carving still singing. The ground hummed with roots talking to each other in slow chemical sentences, with water moving in the dark, with the vast patient machinery of living things, and threaded through all of it, everywhere, *everywhere*, the dead.

Not the polite murmur of the Hollow. Not a fraction. The whole sky.

Quentin dropped to her knees and clapped her hands over her ears, which was useless, which had always been useless, because she had never heard the dead with her ears. They came through the bones of her own skull, through the delicate architecture of her inner ear, through the cage of her ribs. Voices layered on voices layered on voices, near and far, old and new, some pleading, some singing, some simply repeating the last thing they'd ever said the way a clock repeats the hour. A woman counting. A child calling a name that might have been *Mama* or might have been *Mara*. Something vast and wordless moaning somewhere north, deep under the land, like the mountain itself dreaming aloud.

She was going to be sick. She was going to be deaf, if deafness worked that way, if you could go deaf to a sense that lived in your marrow. She curled forward over her knees and breathed, the way she breathed when a carving fought her, slow and deliberate, finding the rhythm of her own body and holding onto it like a rail in floodwater.

*You are Quentin Vasquez,* she told herself. *You were born in the Hollow. You carve the dead into shapes that let the living say goodbye. You are not a door. You are a person.*

Slowly—minutes, or hours—the roar resolved into layers she could sort. Near and far. Loud and faint. She had spent her whole life hearing through a wall and calling the wall the world; now the wall was gone and she was standing naked in the open air of the true world, and it was terrible, and it was the most alive she had ever felt.

Terror and exhilaration, braided so tightly together she couldn't tell which end was which. She laughed, once, a cracked and startled sound, kneeling in the pine needles with her arms wrapped around herself, laughing at the sheer unbearable size of everything.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one, to the loud green dark. "Oh, it's *all real*."

Somewhere ahead, water spoke. A river, wide and unhurried, saying its one long sentence over the stones.

* * *

She walked all morning, and by noon she had learned three things.

First: distance helped. Within a mile of the border the dead were a crowd pressing at every window; by five miles they thinned to passersby, and she could almost pretend the Hollow's silence had followed her. Almost. The land beyond the valley was never silent. It was a room full of sleepers, and she had walked in without shoes.

Second: her hands were louder than her feet. When she touched things—bark, stone, the handle of her pack—their histories rang up her arms. She learned to walk with her fists closed, then to relax, then to close them again when a fallen log shrieked its death into her palms. By midday her fingers were raw from clenching and her head ached like a fever, but she was building walls, clumsy ones, brick by exhausted brick. The Hollow had never given her reason to learn control. Out here, control was survival.

Third: the map was warm.

She discovered it at the stream where she stopped to drink, when she drew it from her shirt and the folded paper practically leapt against her fingers, humming with a faint heat like a stone kept too near a fire. She spread it on a flat rock with hands that weren't quite steady.

She had studied it for two days and knew its geography by heart: the Hollow marked as a blank space, a hole in the world, ringed by mountains drawn like sleeping beasts. Beyond them, inked in a fine sure hand, the lands she was walking through now—and a road, dashed, running west and south toward a river drawn in careful blue-gray strokes. Where road met river sat the first mark: a small circle, and beside it, in cramped letters, a single word.

*Ferryman.*

Below the word, smaller, almost an afterthought, another line of script that she had read a dozen times and still could not make sit still in her mind:

*He remembers what he is owed. Pay honestly. Pay quickly.*

The paper pulsed gently against her fingertips, warm as a living thing, and around her the forest listened with its thousand dead throats, and the river called from the west like something that knew her name.

Quentin folded the map, tucked it back against her heart, and turned her face toward the water.

The ferryman was waiting. She could feel it already—a thread of attention, patient and old, stretched across the miles between them like a line between hook and fish. And some deep-buried instinct, older than the Hollow, older than her mother's lullabies, whispered that whatever toll he asked, she would pay it.

Everyone did.
