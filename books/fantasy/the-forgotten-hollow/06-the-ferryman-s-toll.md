## 6. The Ferryman's Toll

The river announced itself before Quentin saw it—a low rushing murmur that grew until it filled her ears like a crowd of whisperers. After two days walking through country where the dead spoke constantly and loudly, she had learned to sort their voices the way one sorts rain from wind: background, texture, nothing to answer. But the river was different. The river did not speak in words at all. It hummed.

She crested a ridge heavy with gorse just as the sun came up behind her, throwing her shadow long and thin across the valley below, and there it was: a broad grey ribbon of water cutting the land from east to west, wide enough that the far bank looked soft with distance. Mist clung to its surface in ragged sheets. And moored at a rickety wooden jetty, half-swallowed by fog, sat a flat-bottomed ferry with a single lantern burning green at its prow.

Quentin stopped and let herself look for a long moment. She had never seen so much water in her life. In the Hollow, streams ran shallow over stone, and you could cross them without wetting your knees. This was something else—something with weight and appetite. It moved the way time moved, she thought. Without any interest in what it carried.

The map had been clear about this place. *Cross at the Greywater. Pay what is asked. Do not haggle.* The stranger's cramped handwriting had underlined the last instruction twice, the ink pressed hard enough to tear the paper slightly, as if he'd learned the lesson himself and wanted no one else to repeat his mistake.

She descended toward the jetty through wet grass that soaked her boots to the ankle. The closer she came to the water, the louder the humming grew, and the more her hands began to ache.

That was new. Or rather—it was old, but changed. In the Hollow, the bones whispered only when she held them, skin to carved surface, a conversation she could open or close like a door. Out here, past the treeline, everything was louder. The land itself was full of the dead, unquiet and uncontained, and her gift—her curse—had sharpened until she could hear them whether she wanted to or not. A field they'd crossed yesterday had been thick with voices, soldiers or farmers or both, murmuring under the soil like people talking in their sleep. She hadn't slept well after that.

But the river didn't whisper. The river *hummed*, a deep continuous note that vibrated in her teeth, and beneath the hum she caught something worse: an absence. The water was full of things that had once been souls and were no longer. Dissolved. Unmade. Her hands ached the way teeth ache before a storm.

*Don't think about it,* she told herself, and walked out onto the jetty.

The planks were silvered with age and slick with mist. Halfway along, she noticed the wood was carved—faintly, worn nearly smooth by decades of feet and weather—with patterns she almost recognized. Spirals. Knots. Little running figures. Someone had decorated this jetty the way she decorated memorial bones, and the thought made her feel less alone than anything had since she'd left the Hollow.

At the end of the jetty, the ferry waited. There was no one aboard.

"Hello?" Her voice fell flat into the mist.

The green lantern flickered. Then, from the shadow beneath the ferry's small canopy, a figure unfolded itself—slowly, joint by joint, like something that had been sitting still for a very long time and resented being asked to move. It was tall. It wore a coat of layered grey cloth that might have been robes or might have been feathers, and its face was hidden beneath a wide-brimmed hat from which water dripped steadily, though the morning was not raining.

"You're early," it said. Its voice was neither male nor female, young nor old. It sounded like the river did, if the river could be bothered to form words. "Most who come to the Greywater arrive at dusk. They like to see the far bank before they commit."

"I couldn't sleep," Quentin said. "I figured I might as well walk."

"Honesty." The ferryman tilted its hat back just enough to reveal a chin—or the suggestion of one, smooth and grey as driftwood. "That will cost you extra."

Her stomach dropped. "The map said—"

"The map said don't haggle. I'm not haggling. I'm naming my price." It gestured with one long arm toward the bench in the center of the boat. "Sit. We'll discuss payment. You have coin?"

"A little."

"Keep it. Coin is for bridges and innkeepers. I carry souls across moving water, and moving water doesn't recognize currency." The ferryman settled onto the stern bench with the creak of old wood, though Quentin was increasingly unsure how much of the ferryman was flesh. "I take memories. Small ones, usually. The color of your first snow. The smell of a particular bread. Things people hoard without knowing why, and miss without knowing what's gone."

"That's—" Quentin started to say *barbaric*, and stopped herself. She carved the dead into keepsakes and listened to their whispers. Who was she to call any trade in the self barbaric?

"It's fair," the ferryman said, as if reading her. "You keep everything you are, minus one small piece, and you get across. Most travelers thank me on the far bank because they can't remember why they were sad." It extended a hand, palm up. Grey fingers, too many knuckles. "So. What will you give me?"

Quentin stared at the offered hand. Her mind raced through her inventory of memories the way she'd once inventoried her coin purse before market day. Which ones were safe to lose? Her first carving—no, that was load-bearing; everything she was had grown from that splintered piece of deer bone. Marisol's laugh—absolutely not. The smell of her workshop, cedar shavings and linseed oil—that felt expendable until she imagined losing it and felt a cold panic she couldn't explain.

"I need a moment," she said.

"Take it. The river has all the patience in the world. That's rather its defining feature."

She closed her eyes. And immediately regretted it, because closing her eyes opened her ears, and her ears were full.

The dead were everywhere here—not in the water, but along the banks, in the reeds, in the jetty's old carved planks. Generations of travelers. And among them, faint as a thread pulled through heavy cloth, she heard something that stopped her breath.

*Straight across the water, little bird, straight across and home.*

Her mother's voice.

Not a memory of her own—she knew the difference now, painfully, having spent two days sorting her own recollections from the ambient chorus of the dead. This was something recorded somewhere outside her, some echo lodged in the wood or the water or the mist, thirty years old. Her mother had stood on this jetty. Her mother had paid this toll. Her mother had crossed this river carrying a child in her belly and a bargain in her blood, heading home to the Hollow, heading toward the silence that would raise her daughter.

Quentin's eyes were still closed. Tears slid down her cheeks, and she let them, because opening her eyes meant explaining them.

"Interesting," said the ferryman.

"What?"

"You're listening. Most people plug their ears against this place. You've gone and opened every door you have." A pause, and when it spoke again, something in the flat river-voice had shifted—curiosity, maybe, or appetite. "You hear them. The dead."

"They talk," Quentin said. "It's not always a gift."

"No," the ferryman agreed. "It rarely is. But it is rare." The grey hand was still extended, patient as driftwood. "Your price, then. What memory do you part with?"

And Quentin, still hearing that thread of her mother's voice winding through the mist, heard something else beneath it—the rest of the lullaby, the verses her mother must have sung on this very crossing, the ones Quentin had never known because her mother had died before finishing the teaching of them. She had three verses by heart. She'd spent her whole life assuming there were only three.

There were five. She could feel the shape of the missing two the way you feel a missing tooth.

An idea arrived whole, the way ideas sometimes did when she worked bone—as if the material itself suggested the form.

"Not a memory," Quentin said. "A song."

The ferryman went very still. Even the dripping from its hat brim seemed to pause.

"Explain."

"My mother sang me lullabies. One of them—I only know part of it. The rest exists somewhere I can't reach. If I pay you with the song, you get all of it. Every note, every verse, including the parts I've never heard. A whole thing instead of a fragment. Isn't a complete memory worth more than a scrap?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere out on the water, something large turned over and sank again.

"That is not how the toll works," the ferryman said slowly.

"You said honesty costs extra. Consider this ambition."

Another silence. Then, unexpectedly, the ferryman laughed—a sound like water over stones, brief and genuine. "Ambition. On my jetty." It withdrew the grey hand and laced its fingers together, considering her. "Songs are dangerous payment. A song isn't stored in one place. It lives in the throat, the ears, the bones. To take a whole song, I'd have to take it from all of you at once—and leave you the silence where it lived."

"I know," Quentin said. "I'm asking anyway."

"Why? What song is worth a hole in you?"

Because her mother had crossed here. Because her mother had paid whatever price this creature asked, and Quentin wanted—needed—to know what that transaction had felt like from the other side of the ledger. Because if she was going to spend herself, piece by piece, on the road to the mountain, she wanted the spending to mean something.

"Because it's the truest thing I own," she said. "And I'd rather lose it whole than keep it broken."

The ferryman regarded her for a long moment. Then it rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and gestured her aboard.

"Sit," it said. "Sing. And we'll see what the river thinks of your arithmetic."

* * *

The ferry pushed off from the jetty with a groan of rope and timber, and the mist closed around them like a held breath. Quentin sat on the center bench with her hands flat on her knees, and the ferryman took up a long pole and began to work them out into the current, unhurried, the green lantern swaying gently at the prow.

The water was colder than she expected—not the temperature of it, but the feeling. Cold rose off the surface in waves that pressed against her skin like attention. Beneath the hull, she could sense the drowned things, the dissolved things, drifting like silt. She fixed her eyes on the lantern and did not look down.

"Whenever you're ready," the ferryman said.

She wasn't ready. She would never be ready. She took a breath anyway.

"*Sleep, my bird, the hollow's deep,*" she began, and her voice wavered on the second word, because singing it aloud on this water, in this mist, was like handing her mother over piece by piece. "*The stones are warm, the shadows sleep.*"

The effect was immediate and strange. The mist drew inward, gathering toward the boat like a listener leaning close. The hum of the river lowered, softened, found a key. And in her chest, Quentin felt the song begin to move—felt it lift out of her the way a splinter lifts when the flesh finally lets go.

"*Straight across the water, little bird, straight across and home—*"

And there it was. The fourth verse rose in her throat unbidden, arriving from wherever songs keep their hidden rooms, and her eyes flooded as she sang words she had never known and somehow had always known:

"*The ferryman takes what you won't miss, / the mountain keeps what you won't name, / but love goes where it lists, little bird, / and comes back all the same.*"

The ferryman had stopped poling. It stood motionless at the stern, hat brim dripping, and though she couldn't see its face, she felt it listening with its whole grey self.

The fifth verse came harder. It resisted, the way the deepest splinters resist, and Quentin understood dimly that this verse was the heart of the song, the reason her mother had carried it across a border where magic couldn't follow, the reason it had survived in a valley that refused enchantment—because it wasn't enchantment at all. It was just a woman's voice, and a promise.

"*When they ask you what you're owed, little bird, / tell them nothing, tell them none, / for what was sold was never yours, / and what is yours was never won.*"

The last note left her throat and hung in the mist, trembling.

Then the hole opened.

It didn't hurt, exactly. It was more like a door swinging wide in a house she'd lived in all her life, revealing a room she'd never noticed—except the room was empty, and she could feel the emptiness, a small cold absence shaped exactly like a lullaby. She knew the song was gone. She could not remember how it went. She remembered that it had existed, remembered singing it, remembered crying—but the melody itself had been lifted out clean, leaving smooth silence behind, like a bone polished bare.

She put her hand over her mouth. The ferry rocked. The mist began, slowly, to loosen.

"Well," said the ferryman.

"Was it enough?" Her voice came out rough.

"It was more than enough." For the first time, the ferryman sounded uncertain, and Quentin clung to that uncertainty like a handhold. "The river hasn't heard a whole song in— I don't know how long. Travelers don't offer wholes anymore. They offer scraps and call it sacrifice." It resumed poling, slower now, thoughtful. "You'll want to know what I'll do with it."

"Do I want to know?"

"Probably not. But I'll tell you regardless, since you paid honestly. Sometimes, on quiet nights, I sing to the water. The drowned like songs. It settles them." The pole dipped, pulled, dipped. "Yours will be in the rotation. Every verse. Including the ones you didn't know you knew."

Quentin nodded, unable to speak. Grief and relief were tangled in her chest so tightly she couldn't tell which was which. Her mother's lullaby would live on in the mouth of a grey creature on a grey river, sung to the drowned on quiet nights. It was a strange afterlife. But then, she thought, most afterlives were strange. She'd spent her life listening to them complain about it.

The far bank emerged from the mist gradually—a dark line of trees, a shingle beach, a path leading up into hills she'd have to cross. As they drew near, the ferryman spoke again, quieter.

"One more thing, carver. Since you hear the dead—you should know the river gave something back. It does that, occasionally, when it's pleased. Listen to the mist behind us."

Quentin turned. The mist was closing over their wake, and in it, faint and fading, she heard singing. Five verses. All of them. Her mother's voice, or something wearing it, trailing across the water like a ribbon unspooling.

She listened until the mist swallowed even that, and then she faced forward and wiped her face with her sleeve, and when the hull ground softly against the shingle, she stepped onto the far bank with one fewer song and one more certainty: she was going to find the court beneath the mountain, and she was going to make it answer for every verse of every life it had taken.

* * *

She was checking the map—*east road, three days to the salt city, trust no broker*—when she heard the sneeze.

It came from beneath the tarpaulin piled in the ferry's bow, among coils of rope and a stack of crates stamped with markings she didn't recognize. A very human, very young sneeze, quickly smothered in fabric.

The ferryman's head turned with the slow inevitability of a weathervane.

"Ah," it said. "My other passenger."

Silence. Then, from under the tarpaulin, a voice—high, defiant, and thoroughly unconvincing: "I have paid. Full fare. Don't check, you won't find anything wrong."

"I wasn't going to check." The ferryman folded its arms. "I was going to say that stowaways disembark last, and also that they sneeze."

The tarpaulin erupted. Out from under it scrambled a figure—small, wiry, perhaps Quentin's age or a year younger, wrapped in a patched traveling cloak that had been dyed an ambitious purple and faded to the color of a bruise. Red hair stuck out in every direction from under a knit cap. Freckles everywhere. The newcomer landed on the shingle in a crouch, straightened, brushed off the cloak with great dignity, and looked directly at Quentin.

"Oh," the stranger said. "You're not the ferryman."

"No," Quentin said.

"Pity. You look like someone who'd be reasonable." The stranger stuck out a freckled hand. "Tam. Hedge-witch, formerly apprenticed, currently between situations. And before you ask—yes, I paid the toll, and no, I'm not telling you what it was, because it's private."

Quentin shook the hand. Tam's grip was quick and strong, calloused in odd places—fingertips, palms—in ways that spoke of charms and knots and small fires tended in secret.

"Quentin. Carver."

"Carver." Tam's eyes flicked to the tool roll slung across Quentin's back, then to her face, and narrowed with sudden sharp interest. "Bone-carver? Like, memorial-work? Whisper-bones?"

The question landed strangely. "You've heard of that?"

"Heard of it? My gran used to trade with a bone-listener, years back, before the Hollow closed itself off. Said they could settle a haunting in an afternoon that would take a hedge-witch a week of sage and shouting." Tam grinned, and there was something desperate and bright underneath the grin, the look of someone who had run a long way and not yet decided where to stop. "What's a bone-listener doing on this side of the river? Heading to the mountain, I'd wager. Everyone's heading to the mountain lately. Bad idea, generally. Wonderful story, though."

"I'm not looking for company," Quentin said.

"Neither am I. Terrible at it. Ask anyone." Tam shouldered a battered satchel that clinked with glass bottles. "Which is why it's lucky we're not company. We're just two people walking the same road at the same time in the same direction, which is completely different, and statistically much safer than either of us alone, given that the east road has bandits, brokers, and at least two things the locals won't name after dark."

The ferryman, coiling its ropes, made a sound that might have been a laugh.

Quentin looked at the hills. Three days to the salt city, alone, with her hearing turned up so loud she could barely sleep and no idea what waited for her at the end of the map. She looked at Tam—small, loud, armed with bottles and nerve.

"I walk fast," she said.

"I nap while walking. It's a gift." Tam was already scrambling up the shingle toward the path. "Come on, then, carver. Daylight's wasting, and I heard the mist singing earlier, and when the mist sings, the road gets interesting."

Behind them, the ferryman pushed off from the shore without farewell, the green lantern shrinking into the fog. And Quentin, adjusting her tools, followed the runaway hedge-witch up into the hills—telling herself this wasn't company, wasn't companionship, wasn't anything at all.

She didn't quite believe herself. But the road was long, and the dead were loud, and for the first time since the stranger died in her workshop, she was not entirely alone with the noise.

By midday, she'd learned three useful things about Tam: she could light a fire with a snap, she lied fluently about where she'd gotten her supplies, and whenever the subject of *why* she'd run came near, she changed it faster than a card sharp. By dusk, camped under a leaning oak with the first stars pricking through, Quentin heard the thing she'd been dreading all day—a low, rhythmic sound rising from the valley behind them, like drums played underwater, like something vast beginning to move.

Tam heard it too. The hedge-witch went still, firelight catching the fear she'd been hiding all afternoon.

"That," Tam said quietly, "is why I ran."
