## 7. Roadside Ghosts

Tam had learned to count the dead the way other children learned to count sheep.

Not that he'd ever slept well enough for sheep. Sleep was a door, and doors were where they waited—his grandmother's voice in the wall, the drowned boy from the millpond humming through the floorboards, all of them pressing at the thin places between the world and whatever came after. A hedge-witch's apprentice learned early that the dead were not gone. They were only quiet, or not.

The land beyond the Hollow was *not*.

He walked a half-step behind Quentin Vasquez because it was easier to watch her than to watch the fields. She moved like someone carrying something breakable—shoulders drawn in, hands curled near her ribs as though her own bones might crack if she swung her arms too wide. Three days out from the ferry and she still flinched every time the wind changed direction.

"You're doing it again," he said.

"Doing what."

"Listening to everything at once." He nodded ahead, to where the road bent through a valley of barley gone feral, gold stalks leaning over the ditches like gossips over a fence. "You have to pick. One voice at a time, or you'll drown."

Quentin's jaw tightened. He'd noticed she did that when she didn't want to admit he was right—which, so far, was most of the time. "It isn't voices," she said finally. "Not like yours describe. It's more like—" Her hand made a vague, frustrated shape in the air. "Weather. Pressure. Like standing under a waterfall of *static*."

"That's because you've never been anywhere with this many dead in one place." Tam kicked a stone off the road and immediately regretted it; somewhere beneath the barley, something sighed at the disturbance. "In the Hollow there's nothing to hear, right? Silence your whole life. Then you cross the border and—" he snapped his fingers, "—the whole world turns on at once. No wonder you look like a woman being shouted at by an ocean."

"I *am* being shouted at by an ocean."

"Then I'll teach you to swim." He said it lightly, but his stomach knotted as he said it, because teaching meant touching, and touching meant letting her feel what he felt, and he hadn't let anyone do that since Master Ellery died with her hand on his wrist and her whole lifetime of ghosts pouring into him at once. He still dreamed about it. He still woke up with names in his mouth that weren't his.

But Quentin stopped walking. Turned. And there was something in her face—not desperation exactly, but the next thing down from it, the thing desperation wore when it wanted to keep its dignity.

"Please," she said. Just that. Just once.

Tam exhaled. "All right. Sit down before you fall down."

* * *

They sat in the ditch below the barley, out of the wind, and Tam showed her what his mother had shown him before his mother left, which was what her teacher had shown her, which was older than any of them: the shuttering.

"It's not a wall," he explained, taking her hands. Her fingers were cool and ink-stained and calloused in strange places, the pads worn smooth from years of carving. "Everyone thinks shielding is building a wall, and walls fail, and then everything comes through at once and you're worse off than before. It's a door. You make a door with a latch, and you decide what comes through."

"I don't know how to make a door out of nothing."

"You don't make it out of nothing. You make it out of something true." He pressed her palms together, folded her fingers inward until her hands became a small closed shape against her sternum. "Think of your workshop. The Hollow. The quietest place you know. Not the idea of it—the *smell* of it. Bone dust and lamp oil. The sound of your knife."

Her eyes went distant. He watched her breathing slow, watched the constant tiny tension around her mouth begin to unknot.

"Now hold that," he murmured, "and imagine it has a threshold. A doorstep. Anything that wants to reach you has to knock first. And you—" he tapped her folded knuckles, once, gently, "—are the one who answers."

For a long moment nothing happened. Then Quentin shuddered, one full-body tremor like a horse shedding rain, and her eyes flew open, wet and astonished.

"It's *quiet*," she whispered. "Oh. Oh, it's—I can still feel them, but it's like—they're behind glass. Behind a window." She laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound. "I haven't heard silence since we crossed the treeline. I'd forgotten what my own thoughts sounded like."

"There she is," Tam said. "There's the actual you, under all that noise."

She looked at him then—a long, level look that made him want to fidget—and said, "Who taught you this?"

"My mother. Before she decided raising a hedge-witch's brat wasn't the life she'd signed up for." He shrugged, aiming for careless and mostly hitting it. "Master Ellery after that. She's dead now. Most of my teachers end up dead. You should be careful."

"Noted," Quentin said dryly. But she didn't let go of his hands right away, and neither did he, and the barley whispered above them in a language neither of them tried to translate.

They practiced through the afternoon. Opening the door a crack. Closing it. Letting one voice through at a time—an old man mourning a lost mule two fields over, patient and puzzled, calling a name that wasn't a name anymore. Quentin wept a little at that one, quietly, without seeming to notice she was doing it, and Tam pretended not to see, because some kindnesses only work if nobody mentions them.

By dusk she could hold the shutter for nearly a minute at a time. By full dark, when the road crested a low hill and they saw the fires below, she was holding it steady enough to smile.

"Travelers?" she asked.

"Worse," Tam said, squinting. "A caravan."

* * *

He smelled them before they reached the wagons: woodsmoke, tallow, wet wool, and underneath it all the particular sourness of people who had been walking away from something for a very long time. Exiles. He knew the breed. Half the hedge-craft in the borderlands was carried by people no village would keep.

There were eight wagons in a loose ring, painted in faded colors that had probably meant something once—indigo and rust and a green like pond water. Mules grazed on picket lines. Children too old to be children and too young to be anything else watched the newcomers from between the wheels with the flat, weighing stares of kids who had learned that strangers were weather: sometimes gentle, always worth preparing for.

An old woman rose from the largest fire. She was built like a root—short, dense, gnarled, impossible to imagine upright anywhere but exactly where she stood. Silver hair cropped close to her skull. A coat made of patches that had themselves been patched. When she looked at Tam, he felt catalogued, filed, and shelved.

"Road's public," she said. Her voice was gravel rolled in honey. "Fire's shared. Trouble stays outside the ring or it doesn't stay at all."

"We're not trouble," Quentin said.

"They all say that." But the old woman's gaze had already moved past them, snagging on Quentin's satchel, on the corner of something stiff and pale protruding from it—a carved bone, half-finished, that Quentin had been working at their rest stops the way other people prayed. "You're a carver."

"Memorial work," Quentin said. "Mostly."

"Hm." The old woman jerked her chin toward the fire. "Sit. Stew's thin but it's hot. I'm Brist. Old Brist, if you're feeling formal, which you shouldn't, formality being the first symptom of trouble."

They sat. The stew was thin and it was hot and it was the best thing Tam had tasted since the ferry, though that said less about the stew than about the ferryman's toll, which still sat in him like a splinter. He kept reaching for the memory of his mother's second lullaby—the one about the fox and the moon—and finding the place where it had been swept clean, smooth as river stone. Paid. Gone. He wondered if Quentin's mother's song ached in her the same way. He hadn't asked. Some tolls you don't discuss across a campfire.

Around the fire, the caravan told itself in pieces, the way caravans do. A farrier with burn-scarred arms who'd fled a duke's levy. Twin sisters who spoke in a private shorthand of glances. A man who claimed to be a tax collector between positions, which everyone understood to mean he'd collected from the wrong lord. They were loud and warm and careful, all at once, the way people are when home is a thing they carry instead of a place they have.

And the dead here—Tam kept his shutter mostly closed, but even through the latch he could feel them. The road behind the caravan hummed with them. People who'd died on this stretch of ground across centuries, layered like sediment. A battle, maybe, long ago; the earth remembered iron. He caught Quentin glancing at him twice, and each time he tapped his own sternum—*doorstep, latch*—and each time she nodded and settled.

It was later, much later, when the fires burned low and most of the caravan slept, that Old Brist looked at the map.

Quentin hadn't meant to show anyone. Tam saw it happen: she'd taken the map out to check tomorrow's route by firelight, unfolding it just so far, and Brist's shadow fell across it before either of them could move.

The old woman went still.

Not startled-still. Tam knew startled. This was different—this was the stillness of a deer that has scented the hunter and is deciding, in the space of a heartbeat, whether to run or stand its ground. Every line of her body locked. The firelight found her face and Tam watched the color leave it in stages, like a tide going out.

"Where," Old Brist said, and her voice had lost all its honey and kept only the gravel, "did you get this."

Quentin's hand closed over the map. "Why?"

"*Where did you get this.*"

"A dying man," Tam said, because Quentin had gone rigid and someone had to talk. "At the treeline of the Hollow. He was bleeding out. He gave it to her and died before he could say why."

Brist did not look at him. She was looking at the seal—or the ghost of it, the wax impression stamped in the map's corner, half-crumbled but unmistakable to anyone who knew what they were seeing. Tam didn't know what they were seeing. But he knew what fear looked like on a woman built like a root, and this was it.

"Inside," Brist said abruptly. "Not here. My wagon. Now, and quiet about it."

* * *

Her wagon smelled of cedar and old paper and, faintly, of something burnt that had never quite finished burning. Shelves lined every wall, crammed with jars and bundles and books whose spines had been sewn back on more than once. A single lantern hung from a hook, swaying with the wagon's small motions, throwing moving shadows that made the little room feel like the inside of a bell.

Brist bolted the door. Then she stood with her back against it for a moment, both hands flat on the wood, as if holding something out.

"Show me the seal," she said. "Properly. All of it."

Quentin unfolded the map on the small table between them. Tam leaned in. In the lantern light he could finally see it clearly: a circle of interlocking figures, each one gripping the next, wound tight as a knot—and at the center, a shape like an open hand holding a flame that was also, if you squinted, a key.

"Under-mountain," Brist breathed. "That's the court's mark. That's the *seal of the Under-Court*, girl, and you've been carrying it through three provinces like a peddler's flyer." She looked up, and her eyes were wet, which frightened Tam more than the anger would have. "Do you know what this is? Do you know what you're holding?"

"A map," Quentin said steadily. Too steadily. "To the court beneath the mountain. A dying man traded it for my help and died saying my mother's name. So yes, I know roughly what I'm holding. What I don't know is why an exile caravan leader goes white as bone-dust at the sight of it."

The silence stretched. Outside, a mule shifted; somewhere a child laughed in sleep. Through the shuttered door of his hearing, Tam felt the dead of the roadside press closer, curious, like moths against glass.

Brist crossed to a shelf. Her hands shook—not much, but visibly—as she worked a false panel aside and drew out a slim packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft with age. She set it on the table beside the map without unwrapping it.

"I served them," she said. "Thirty years ago. The court beneath the mountain. Not as a magistrate—I was never that. A clerk. An archivist. I kept their accounts." Her jaw worked. "Their ledger. Every soul they hold, every contract, every debt, entered in bone and bound in skin. I wrote entries in that book for eleven years. Names. Dates. Prices paid."

Tam's mouth had gone dry. "Prices paid for *souls*."

"What else does the Under-Court deal in?" Brist laughed, and it was the worst sound he'd ever heard from an old woman, dry and cracked and hollow as a winter reed. "People come to them desperate. Sick children, dying parents, debts of honor, debts of blood. And the court says: we can fix this. We can mend this. All it costs is—" she spread her hands, "—a piece of you. A sliver. A memory-shaped thing. Most folk never miss it. That's the genius of it. That's the *horror* of it. You sell a piece of your soul and wake up the next morning feeling exactly like yourself, because how would you know the difference?"

Quentin had gone very pale. Tam watched her hand drift, unconsciously, toward her own sternum—the way he'd taught her to anchor the shutter—and something cold walked down his spine.

"And you ran," he said.

"I stole a page first." Brist touched the oilcloth packet, not lifting it. "One page. Proof. Leverage. Insurance. Call it what you like—it's the only thing I took that they want back, and it's the reason I've spent thirty years moving, always moving, keeping good people around me because lone travelers vanish and caravans are harder to make disappear." She finally looked up at Quentin, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Now tell me, girl. Tell me true, because I'll know if you lie. Why did a dying man carry this court's seal into the magic-dead Hollow—the one place in the world their contracts can't follow—and put this map in *your* hands? Of all the hands in all the world?"

Quentin was quiet for a long moment. The lantern swayed. Shadows swung across her face like the phases of something turning.

"He said a debt was unpaid," she said at last. "Thirty years. He said—" her voice caught, steadied, "—he said my mother's soul was sold there. And then he died saying her name."

Old Brist closed her eyes.

When she opened them again, she looked ten years older, and she reached across the table and gripped Quentin's wrist with a strength that seemed impossible in those gnarled hands.

"Then it's worse than I thought," she said, "and it was already bad enough to stop my heart. Because I read that ledger for eleven years, child. I wrote in it. And I remember—" her voice broke, and she pushed through it, "—I remember every entry marked *partial*. Every soul sold in pieces. And there are names in that book I have prayed thirty years to forget."

She turned the oilcloth packet slowly, deliberately, until it sat between the three of them like a third person at the table.

"So before we go one mile further," Old Brist said, "you're going to tell me your mother's name. And God forgive me, girl—but I think I already know it."
