## 8. Old Brist's Bargain

The fire had burned down to its red heart by the time Old Brist decided to speak, and he was glad of it. Darkness made confession easier. Darkness meant you could look at the flames instead of the faces of the people who would have to decide what your guilt was worth.

He sat with his back against the wagon wheel, his bad knee drawn up, the stolen page wrapped in oilcloth beneath his shirt where it had lived for thirty-one years, warm as a second heartbeat. Across the fire, the girl—Vasquez, Quentin, the bone-carver from the dead valley—was showing Tam how to wrap a shielding knot in cord, her fingers quick and certain in the way of hands that had done a thing ten thousand times. Marisol kept watch at the edge of the camp, lantern-charm swaying from her belt, and every few minutes she glanced back at Brist with eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

She knew. He could see it in the set of her shoulders. She'd seen him go pale when the map's seal caught the light that afternoon, and she had been waiting ever since for him to explain himself.

Well then. Better to bleed now than to let the wound go sour.

"Girl," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended, like a hinge long unswung. "Bone-carver. A word, if you'd give it."

Quentin looked up. In the firelight her face was all planes and shadow, her mother's face, though she didn't know yet how well he remembered it—or why. That knowledge sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. *Not yet,* he told himself. *One sin at a time.*

Tam drifted closer without pretending not to listen, and Marisol abandoned her watch to stand at Quentin's shoulder, arms crossed, a wall in a wool coat. Fine. Let them all hear. A secret this old deserved witnesses.

Brist drew out the oilcloth bundle and unwrapped it slowly, letting the ritual of it slow his hammering heart. Inside lay a page of vellum gone soft and yellow at the edges, covered in columns of script so fine it looked like frost-work, each line written in ink that still gleamed faintly silver when it caught the light. It did not catch the firelight. It never had. The page drank light the way a drunkard drinks coin.

"This," he said, "is a page of the Ledger."

* * *

He had been young once. That was the part no one believed about him anymore—the bent spine, the clouded eye, the hands knuckled like tree roots—but he had been young, and clever, and hungry in the particular way of boys born to nothing who want everything. When the court beneath the mountain put out word that it wanted clerks, scriveners, keepers of accounts, Brist had walked three hundred miles through snow to answer.

They had liked him. That was the horror of it, looking back. They had *liked* him.

"The Under-Court keeps accounts," the Master of Names had told him on his first day, leading him down corridors of black stone where the walls sweated cold. "Every soul that enters our keeping is entered here. Every debt, every sale, every trade. Nothing is forgotten. Nothing is forgiven. The Ledger is the law, and the law is the Ledger." The Master had smiled then, and Brist had spent years learning not to flinch at that smile. "You will write nothing yourself, clerk. You will only witness what is written. Do you understand the difference?"

He hadn't. Not then. He'd thought witnessing was a small thing, a clean thing. He'd sat at his desk in the Scriptorium with its lamps that burned without flame, and he had copied entries in his neat clerk's hand while the glass vessels lined the walls hummed their low, sweet, terrible humming, and he had told himself: *I am only writing down what is true.*

Entry by entry. Year by year.

*A soul, whole, sold by its bearer for passage across the Salt Waste.* Witnessed.

*A soul, partial, taken in payment of a debt contracted before the debtor's birth.* Witnessed.

*A soul, whole, tender-aged, surrendered by the mother in exchange for—*

He had stopped copying that one. His pen had hovered over the line, and the ink had waited, patient as the court itself, and somewhere down the corridor a vessel had hummed a note that sounded almost like a lullaby, and Brist had set down his pen and thought: *I cannot.*

But he had. Of course he had. You always did, in that place. The court did not need chains. It needed only the Ledger, and the Ledger needed only witnesses, and witnesses needed only to be tired, and hungry, and afraid, until one day they were old, and the walls were all they knew, and leaving was unthinkable because leaving meant admitting what you'd been all along.

It took his daughter to make him think it.

Liesl. His Liesl, born in the mountain's shadow, in the clerks' quartering, with her mother's dark hair and her father's hunger for open sky. She used to stand at the one high window of their rooms and watch the birds cross the glacier and say, *Papa, where do they go?* And he would say, *South, little bird. Where it's warm.* And she would say, *Take me there someday.*

He might have stayed forever, otherwise. Might have grown old copying other people's damnation and calling it honest work. But Liesl sickened the winter she turned nine—a fever, the physicians said, though Brist knew better; he had read enough entries to know what the mountain took from those who lived too long inside its contracts—and the physicians offered the usual remedy. A tithe. A portion of the girl's soul, held in trust, in exchange for her health.

*In trust,* they said. As if the court had ever given anything back.

And Brist—who had witnessed a thousand such bargains, who knew precisely what *in trust* meant in that silver script—signed anyway, because the fever was burning his daughter hollow and the alternative was watching her die. He signed, and the fever broke, and Liesl woke pale and quiet with a piece of herself missing, a piece she would spend her whole life reaching for and never finding, the way you reach in the dark for a stair that isn't there.

She died at nineteen. Fell from the cliff path above the glacier, or so they said. But Brist had been a clerk for twenty years by then, and he knew how to read between the lines of an official account, and what he read there was this: the court did not return what it borrowed, and souls held *in trust* past the death of their bearers became the property of the Ledger entire. His daughter's missing piece had not gone with her. It was still there. On a shelf. In the dark. Humming.

That was the night he stole the page.

* * *

"It was the page my daughter's entry appeared on," Brist said aloud, and found his voice steadier than he expected, the way a man's voice goes steady when he has finally stopped fighting the fall. "Her tithe-entry. Her name. And around hers, the names of others—hundreds of others, entered across thirty years. Souls held in trust. Souls sold. Souls taken." He turned the vellum toward the fire, though the fire gave nothing back. "I copied it out in my own hand over six nights, hid the true page in my boot, and walked out through the salt gates during the Changeover, when the wards are thinnest. They caught my scent two days later. I've been running for thirty-one years."

Silence, but for the fire and the wind combing the high grass.

"Why run?" Tam asked softly. "Why not just—hide the page? Burn it?"

"You can't burn it." Brist laughed, and there was no humor in it, only rust. "Boy—I'm sorry, *girl*—you can't burn a page of the Ledger any more than you can burn a law. I tried, the first year. Fire won't take it. Water won't drown it. It wants to exist, do you understand? The court wrote it into being, and it holds itself together the way a soul holds a body together. All I can do is keep it from them. Because as long as the page exists outside their walls, the entries on it are—" he searched for the word, the precise word, the clerk's word— "*contested*. Unenforceable at distance. Every soul named on this page is a soul the court cannot fully own while I carry it away from them. They've sent hunters after me eleven times. I buried the first three. After that I got faster."

Marisol spoke for the first time, her voice flat as a frozen lake. "And the carver's mother. Where does she sit in this story of yours?"

There it was. He had known it was coming, and still it went through him like a spear.

"I never met her," Brist said. "But I witnessed her entry." He looked at Quentin, and made himself hold those dark, level eyes. "Thirty years ago, a woman came to the court with child, fleeing something south of the Salt Waste—I never learned what. She bargained for passage home to a valley the magic couldn't follow her into. The price was steep. She paid it in the only coin the court accepts." His throat worked. "The entry reads: *A soul, partial, taken from the unborn issue of the bearer, in exchange for safe conduct to the Hollow.*"

Quentin did not move. Did not breathe. The fire cracked, and somewhere off in the grass a night-bird called once and went silent, as if it too were waiting.

"Your mother bought your life with a piece of your soul, girl," Brist said gently. "And I stood at a desk and wrote it down. So you see, when I saw that map's seal today, I didn't go pale from fear." He pressed the oilcloth bundle into her hands, closing her fingers around it with his own ruined ones. "I went pale because I recognized the debt. This page has names on it. Hers is one. Yours is another. And mine—mine is the hand that recorded them both."

"What do you want for it?" Quentin asked at last. Her voice was very quiet, and very steady, and Brist thought: *she is her mother's daughter, every inch.*

"Not coin. Not memory. I've nothing left worth taking." He knelt then, bad knee and all, because some things must be asked from below. "Only this. If you reach the court—if you find the shelves where they keep what they've taken—find my Liesl. Find what's left of her, and free her. Free her the way I couldn't. That's my price. That's the whole of it. Thirty-one years of running, weighed against one promise from a stranger with good hands."

Quentin looked down at the page in her lap, at the silver script that drank the firelight, and Brist watched her thumb trace the edge of it the way she must trace the bones she carved—reading, listening, hearing whatever the dead and the damned had to say.

"And if I fail?" she asked.

"Then I'll have lost nothing but the lie I've been living," Brist said. "Which is more than I deserve to keep."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind. Then Marisol crouched beside them both, took the page's corner in two fingers as if testing a fish for freshness, and said, "Then we'd best plan how to get four fools and a stolen page up a mountain full of things that hunt stolen pages. There's a city three days south—Salt and Lanterns, they call it. Soul-brokers thick as flies. We'll need papers, disguises, and a story that holds water."

Brist felt something loosen in his chest that he had carried tight for thirty-one years, and did not trust it, and was grateful for it anyway.

Quentin folded the page along its old creases and tucked it inside her coat, over her heart, where the ledger-page and the living beat against each other like two clocks refusing to agree.

"Rest," she said, rising. "We leave at first light."

And far behind them, far below, in the vaulted dark beneath the mountain, Brist did not doubt that a lamp had just guttered, and a clerk had looked up from a desk, and a voice like dry paper had said: *Find the page.*

The hunt, he knew, had already begun.
