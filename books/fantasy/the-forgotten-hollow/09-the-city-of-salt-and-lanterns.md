## 9. The City of Salt and Lanterns

The city announced itself by smell before sight: salt and smoke and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in the sun. Quentin smelled it from the ridge, where the road bent around a shoulder of grey rock, and her stomach tightened with a hunger she hadn't felt since the Hollow—hunger for people, for noise, for the press of strangers who owed her nothing.

"Saltmere," Old Brist said, and spat, though his mouth was dry. He'd been doing that since the pass. "Last city before the mountain. Last place a body can buy bread without selling something else along with it."

"It smells like the sea," Tam said.

"There is no sea," Brist said. "There hasn't been sea within three hundred miles since before my grandmother's grandmother. That's salt off the mountain. Off the *court*." He said the word the way other men said plague. "They mine it. They trade it. Everything that comes out from under that rock comes out crusted white."

Quentin looked down at Saltmere and understood why pilgrims had once called it the City of Lanterns. It sat in a bowl of hills like a handful of embers poured into ash: ten thousand lights strung on wires and poles and balconies, gold and green and a blue so deep it looked cold to touch. Above the rooftops, higher than anything built by human hands, the mountain rose—a black wedge against the darkening sky, its peak lost in cloud, its slopes glittering faintly, as if dusted with frost.

Salt. Even from here she could see it shining.

She touched the map through her shirt, where it lay folded against her sternum. Three marks remained between them and the court. The third mark was the city itself; the fourth, if the map could be believed, was a door.

* * *

They came down into Saltmere as the lanterns were being lit, which Quentin suspected was deliberate on Brist's part—he walked like a man who knew exactly what hour a city showed its kindest face. The streets were narrow and loud, cobbled with pale stone that crunched faintly underfoot, and everywhere there were sellers: fish from mountain streams, wool dyed impossible colors, candied roots, knives, prayers. Prayer-sellers worked the corners in pairs, one chanting, one holding out a wooden bowl painted with eyes.

And everywhere, threaded through all of it, the dead.

In the Hollow, the dead whispered. Here they *sang*. The whole city rang with them, a low chord beneath the market-noise, rising from the gutters and the doorways and the pale cobblestones themselves. Quentin had learned on the road to keep her hands closed, her fingers still, but here even her skin listened. The stones were bone-white because they were not stone at all—or not only stone—and every step pressed another murmur up through her boot-soles.

"You're limping," Marisol observed, falling into step beside her.

"I'm listening."

"Don't." Marisol took her elbow, firm as a splint. "You listen here, girl, you'll walk straight into a wall or worse. Eyes forward. Mouth shut. We look like traders, we act like traders, we buy a room and we sleep."

"We're not traders," Quentin said. "I'm a carver. That's the point."

Marisol's grip tightened. "That's the part I hate."

They found lodging above a noodle house in the Lantern District, where the landlord charged them in coin and didn't ask questions, which Brist said made him either honest or very good at pretending. From the window of their shared room, Quentin could see the mountain, closer now, filling half the sky. Its snowfields glowed faintly in the dark. Not snow, she thought. Salt.

She slept badly, and dreamed of a ledger opening itself page by page, and woke with her mother's name caught in her throat like a fishbone.

* * *

In the morning, Marisol braided Quentin's hair back tight and rubbed walnut stain into her hands until the carver's calluses looked like a laborer's instead of an artist's. Brist produced a battered trade-tally board from his pack—the kind traveling merchants used—and coached her until she could hold it without looking guilty.

"You're a bone-carver from the hill country," he said. "Combs, buttons, needle-cases. You've come to sell to the lantern-makers, who use bone fittings. You know nothing about souls, you've never heard of brokers, and if anyone asks about the mountain you spit, like this—" he demonstrated, "—because hill folk think the court steals weather."

"Do they?"

"No idea. But everyone believes someone else's superstitions more readily than their own." He fixed her with his milky eye. "One more thing. In this city, when a broker asks your price, you never say a number first. You say, 'What is it worth to you?'"

"And if they don't take no for an answer?"

Brist smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen on him yet. "Then you run, girl. And you hope your legs are younger than theirs."

* * *

The broker-houses kept to the Salt Quarter, in the city's oldest streets, where the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators and the lanterns burned green. Quentin went alone. That had been argued over until midnight, and in the end logic won: a young carver browsing wares drew no eyes; a party of three travelers with a stolen ledger page sewn into Brist's coat hem drew every eye in the district.

So she walked the Salt Quarter alone, past shopfronts where glass vessels sat in velvet rows, each one lit from within by something that was not flame. She did not let herself listen. She could not afford to hear what was in the glass.

The stranger's map marked its fourth point with a symbol she'd come to understand as *broker*: a circle crossed by a single line, like an eye drawn shut. She found it chalked small beside a door halfway down Lantern Row, above a shop whose sign read VESS & DAUGHTER — APPRAISALS HONESTLY RENDERED.

The bell over the door was made of bone. It did not ring so much as *murmur*, and Quentin's spine prickled at the sound.

Inside, the shop was dim and crowded and immaculate. Shelves rose to the ceiling, filled not with soul-glass but with ordinary things: clockwork birds, ivory chessmen, combs, reliquaries, a wall of hand-mirrors each wrapped in cloth. A woman sat behind the counter, sixty-odd, silver-haired, with spectacles pushed up on her forehead and a jeweler's loupe hanging at her neck. She was mending a music box with tweezers held in fingers steady as a surgeon's.

"Closed to browsers," the woman said, without looking up.

"I'm not browsing. I'm selling." Quentin set her sample case on the counter and opened it: combs, buttons, a needle-case, six weeks of work done by firelight on the road. Good work. Her hands had needed something to do besides shake.

The woman set down the tweezers and picked up a comb. She turned it in the lamplight, tested the teeth against her wrist, and raised one eyebrow.

"Hill work," she said. "Good hill work. Who taught you?"

"My mother."

"Mm." The woman set the comb down. "Vess," she said, offering nothing else. "You'll want the lantern-makers' guild, east side. They pay fair and don't haggle."

"I'm told you deal in rarer stock."

Something shifted in the woman's face—not much. A shutter moving a finger's width. "Rare stock has rare buyers. And rare risks."

"So I've heard." Quentin kept her voice level, bored, the way Brist had drilled her. "A man came through here recently. Traveling scholar. Tall, grey coat, coughed like his lungs were leaving ahead of schedule. I have something of his to return to interested parties, and I'm told this is the street for finding them."

Silence. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked.

Vess removed her spectacles, polished them slowly on her sleeve, and put them back on. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—warmer, softer, and somehow far more dangerous.

"Sit down, child."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit *down*." It wasn't a command; it was pity, and pity frightened Quentin more. Vess glanced toward the stairs, then back, and lowered her voice. "Tall man, grey coat. Came in eight days ago, sweating, asking me to appraise a map. Wanted to know if I'd buy it." She shook her head. "I told him no. Not because the map was worthless. Because he was already sold. You can't do business with a man whose soul is collateral—the court holds his paper, and anything he touches, they can claim. I told him to run south, fast, and lose himself." Her eyes, magnified behind the lenses, were terribly kind. "He didn't run south, did he."

"He ran north," Quentin said quietly. "Into the Hollow."

Vess closed her eyes briefly. "Then he's dead, and whatever he gave you, you should burn it."

"What if I can't?"

"Everyone can burn something, child. The question is whether they can burn it *before* someone comes asking." Vess stood, and Quentin saw that her hands had begun, very slightly, to tremble. "Listen to me. You came in here playing a carver, and you're good—I nearly believed you. But you asked about a dead man by description, in a broker's shop, and you flinched when my bell murmured. So here is my appraisal, honestly rendered, free of charge: somewhere in this city, right now, there are men who are paid to notice exactly what you just showed me. And the tall man's creditors will already be asking who inherited his debts." She reached beneath the counter and pressed something into Quentin's palm—a small brass token stamped with the shut-eye symbol. "Show this at the back door of any shop on this row after dark, and you'll get a cellar to sleep in and no names asked. That's the only kindness I can afford you. Now go out the front, smile, and walk slow. Running marks a person worse than anything."

Quentin's hand closed around the token. It was warm from the counter, or from Vess's grip, or from something else entirely.

"The map," she said. "You appraised it. What did you see?"

Vess's face did something complicated—grief and fear and an old, old tiredness.

"I saw a route to the court," she said, "drawn by someone who wanted to reach it alive. And I saw the seal of the Under-Court at the bottom, which means whoever follows that map isn't a visitor, child. They're expected."

* * *

Quentin walked slow, as instructed. She smiled at a fruit-seller. She admired a string of paper lanterns. All the while she counted the watchers the way Brist had taught her to count exits: the man leaning in a doorway with clean boots and dirty hands; the pair by the fountain who were talking to each other but watching her reflection in the water; the boy on the corner who looked away a half-second too late.

Four. Maybe five.

Her heart hammered, but her feet stayed slow. *Walk slow.* She turned down a side street, then another, following Brist's instructions for losing a tail—never twice the same direction, always end where you began. She bought a bag of roasted chestnuts she didn't want, because a woman carrying chestnuts was a woman with errands, and errands were invisible.

By the time she reached the noodle house, her shirt was soaked through despite the cool air.

Marisol took one look at her and started packing.

"Brist," Quentin said. "It's the map's seal. Whoever follows it isn't a visitor. We're *expected*."

Old Brist sat down heavily on the bed, and for a long moment he looked older than the road had ever made him. Then he began, methodically, to unpick the stitching at his coat hem.

"Then we stop hiding the page," he said. "We stop pretending we're smugglers. If the court expects us—" he drew out the folded scrap of ledger, thirty years hidden against his body, and laid it on the table between them, "—then we walk in the front door."

Outside, the lanterns of Saltmere were lighting one by one, ten thousand small fires against the dark of the mountain. And among them, Quentin knew, at least four sets of eyes had already found her, and were even now carrying her description home to whoever paid them.

Somewhere below, faint through the floorboards, a bone bell murmured.
