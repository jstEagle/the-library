## 10. Hunted Through the Night Market

Tam had learned to count the dead the way other children counted sheep.

It was a hedge-witch's habit, drilled into her by a grandmother who'd been more superstition than spell: *count what follows you, girl, and you'll never be surprised by it.* In the city of Salt and Lanterns, counting was a full-time occupation. The dead here didn't whisper—they *shouted*, layered over one another like gulls fighting over a fish head. A beggar's ghost argued with a merchant's about a debt three centuries old outside the chandlery. Something drowned paced the canal in slow, wet circles, muttering tide-tables to no one. And beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, came the mountain—a vast, patient hum that Tam tried very hard not to think about.

Her shielding charm helped. It was a crooked little thing, knotted from red thread and a river-stone with a hole worn through its middle, and it sat against her sternum on a leather cord, warm as a held hand. Without it she would have been deafened by now. With it, the city's chorus of the unquiet dead came through muffled, like voices heard through a closed door.

Quentin walked beside her through the night market with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, and Tam kept glancing at her, because Quentin was the strangest thing in a city full of strange things: a woman who could hear everything the charm kept out, and who wore that hearing like a coat two sizes too small.

"You're doing the thing again," Quentin said, without turning her head.

"What thing?"

"The counting thing. Your lips move."

"I'm counting exits," Tam said, which was true. "And ghosts. Mostly exits."

The night market of Salt and Lanterns opened when the sun went down and the honest merchants went home. It filled the long square between the salt-warehouse district and the canal steps—hundreds of lanterns strung on wires overhead, their light the color of weak tea, swaying so that every shadow in the place leaned and righted itself, leaned and righted, like a congregation at prayer. Stalls sold things that couldn't be sold by daylight: bottled breath, secondhand names, luck scraped off the boots of the fortunate. The air smelled of frying onions and tallow smoke and, underneath, the mineral stink of the salt vats, which got into everything in this city, including, Tam suspected, the souls.

Old Brist had wanted them to stay at the caravan's camp beyond the walls. Marisol had wanted them to stay too—or rather, Marisol had planted herself in front of the workshop door they'd rented and said, in a voice that could have planed wood flat, "You are not going into that market without me," and then Brist had needed someone to help him with his bad hip and his worse maps, and somehow it had shaken out that Quentin and Tam went alone, because Quentin had insisted the broker would only talk to a carver, and a carver's apprentice doesn't bring an entourage.

*We'll be back before the third bell,* Quentin had promised.

Tam had known even then that it was a lie. Not because Quentin lied—but because the city lied around them, constantly, and promises made inside Salt and Lanterns were like footprints in the salt flats: gone by morning.

They found the broker's stall where the stranger's fragments had said it would be, in the crook of the market where the lantern-light thinned to almost nothing. The broker was a narrow man with rings on every finger and a face like something left out in weather—smoothed down, feature-worn, pleasant in the way that old bone is pleasant. His stall displayed no goods at all. Just a ledger, chained shut, and an empty chair across from him, waiting.

Quentin sat in it. Tam stood behind her shoulder and did what she did best, which was be small and watch everything.

"Carving work," Quentin said, and set a bone on the counter—a memorial piece, blank, beautifully finished. "I'm told you deal in commissions for the bereaved."

The broker turned the bone over once with one ringed finger. "I deal in *commissions*, yes." He smiled. His teeth were very even, which Tam found worse than if they hadn't been. "Though I confess your face is familiar, mistress carver. Have we done business?"

"No."

"Mm. Perhaps it's your hands I know. Hands are memorable, in my trade." He slid the bone back across the counter, and his eyes—pale, lidless-seeming, the eyes of a fish or a saint in an old painting—lifted to Tam's face, then past her, scanning the crowd behind. "A friend of mine passed through this market some days ago. A traveler. He carried a map, and he owed me a fee, and he died before paying it. Debts don't die with their debtors, you understand. They go... looking." He laced his fingers together. "You wouldn't know anything about a map, would you? Or about a woman named Vasquez?"

Tam felt Quentin go still in a way that wasn't stillness at all—it was the stillness of a drawn bow.

"Vasquez is a common name," Quentin said.

"Here? No." The broker's smile widened by one tooth. "But in the Hollow, perhaps. In the valley where magic can't follow. Where a certain kind of person might hide for thirty years and think herself safe." He leaned forward, and the lantern above his stall guttered though there was no wind. "Debts go looking, mistress carver. And mine has very good eyes."

Tam's hand found the cord at her throat without her deciding it should. The charm pulsed warm against her knuckles.

"Come along, master," Quentin said lightly, rising, gathering her bone. "He's mad, and I've work to do."

They were ten steps into the crowd when Tam counted the wrongness.

Four men had detached themselves from the market's edges—not following, exactly; *converging*, the way water finds a drain. They moved badly, in the loose-jointed way of bodies that weren't being steered entirely from inside, and when Tam let her charm slip—just a hair, just enough—the sound hit her like a wave over a seawall. Every one of the four was *wearing* a ghost. Dead men wrapped around living ones like wet cloaks, and the dead were all murmuring the same word, over and over, in the broker's voice:

*Looking. Looking. Looking.*

"Quentin," Tam breathed. "Left alley. Now. Don't run—running tells them."

Quentin turned without breaking stride. They slipped between a stall selling bottled sighs and another selling the shadows of famous people, into the alley behind, where the lantern-light didn't reach and the smell of salt was thick enough to chew. Tam pressed her back to the cold brick and pulled Quentin after her and thought, wildly, *this is the part where I find out whether I'm brave or just fast.*

Footsteps in the market lane. Not passing. Pausing.

"They're listening," Quentin whispered. Her hand found Tam's wrist. "Can you hear what they hear?"

"That's not—" Tam swallowed. "That's not how it works."

"Try."

So Tam closed her eyes and did the hardest thing she knew: she loosened the knot of her charm, thread by thread, letting the city's dead come flooding in—the beggar and the drowned thing and the mountain's patient hum and now these four borrowed corpses standing in the lane, their resident ghosts straining toward the alley like dogs on a scent. She listened *through* them, the way you listen through a wall, pressing her attention against the membrane of their stolen hearing—

—and heard the broker's voice, thin and clear, speaking somewhere behind those four pairs of ears: *"The small one carries a charm. Take the charm first. Then the map-bearer. The court wants both alive. The charm is worth more than the girl."*

Tam's eyes flew open. "They want my charm," she whispered. "And you. Alive. Both of us."

"Then we don't get caught," Quentin said, with a calm that Tam would later realize must have cost her everything, and pulled her deeper into the dark.

* * *

The alleys of Salt and Lanterns were a maze built by drunkards and improved by liars, and Tam knew maybe half of it, and the half she knew was trying to eat them.

They ran. Not the panicked sprint that would call every hunter in the district, but the fast, low, weaving run of people who grew up in places where running was sometimes necessary. Behind them the footsteps multiplied—four became six became eight, hunters peeling off stalls and doorways, always ahead as often as behind, because the ghosts they wore didn't need to see. They listened for the living the way Tam listened for the dead, and the market was loud with life, but two women breathing hard in an alley cut through the noise like a knife through cloth.

Tam's charm burned against her chest. She fed it everything she had—her fear, her focus, the memory of her grandmother's kitchen—and the dead recoiled from her in a stuttering radius, buying seconds, never enough seconds.

"Roofline," she gasped. "Stairs by the dye-house—we can—"

The net closed.

They came out of three doorways at once, in the little square behind the salt-vats where the lanterns didn't reach and the only light was the pale glow rising off the brine pools themselves, ghost-light, corpse-cold. Eight figures. Maybe nine. Their faces were ordinary faces, dockworkers and porters, and their eyes were all wrong—too steady, too empty, lit from behind by something that wasn't theirs.

The nearest one spoke with the broker's voice sliding under his own like a blade under skin. "The charm, little witch. Hand it over and walk away whole. The court has no quarrel with hedge-trash."

"Don't," Quentin said, stepping in front of Tam. "Whatever they offer—"

"Oh, I know what they offer." Tam's mouth had gone dry but her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Nothing. You give them nothing and they take everything anyway. That's how courts work. That's how *brokers* work." Her fingers closed around the river-stone. It was hot now, almost too hot to hold. "Grandmother said this charm took her three winters to make. Said it would outlive me. She was wrong about a lot of things."

The hunters moved all at once.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do, each fragment sharp and separate as a dropped plate:

Quentin going low, sweeping a vat-pole into the knees of the nearest hunter, who folded with a sound like wet rope.

Tam throwing a hex she'd only ever practiced on rats—*stumble-blind, stumble-blind*—and watching it take two hunters at once, their borrowed ghosts shrieking out of their mouths like steam from a kettle.

Hands. Too many hands. Someone catching her braid, wrenching her backward, the world tilting into brine-glow and grasping fingers.

And then—light.

Not lantern-light. *Charm*-light, gold and furious, pouring into the square from the alley mouth like sunrise arriving early and angry, and in the middle of it stood a short, broad, gray-streaked woman in a traveling coat, holding a lantern-charm aloft in one fist the way you'd hold a sword, and her voice cracked across the square like a whip finding its mark:

"*Get your hands off my girls.*"

Marisol.

Later, Tam would try to reconstruct how one woman with one charm scattered eight hunters, and she never quite managed it. There was the light, which the wearing-ghosts hated worse than fire, streaming from the lantern-charm in pulses timed—Tam realized with a jolt—to a heartbeat, a living heartbeat, steady and furious and *refusing*. There was Marisol's voice, which did not stop, calling out names and threats and, absurdly, instructions—"Quentin, LEFT, girl, the pole, use the POLE"—with the authority of a woman who had spent decades telling neighbors what to do about their fences and their griefs and had never once been disobeyed. And there was the moment, seared into Tam forever, when Marisol waded directly into the middle of the hunters, a grandmother-shaped storm, and drove the lantern-charm's light into the face of the man holding Tam's braid, and the ghost riding him tore free with a sound like tearing linen and fled shrieking into the salt-dark, and the man simply *collapsed*, emptied, human, weeping.

"Run," Marisol said, hauling Tam upright by the collar. "Now admire me later."

They ran. Quentin grabbed Tam's hand; Marisol brought up the rear, walking backward, swinging the lantern-charm in great golden arcs that kept the remaining hunters at bay the way a torch keeps wolves off. Through the dye-house yard, over the canal footbridge, up the wet stone steps two at a time, the city blurring around them into smears of lantern-tea and salt-white and shadow. The hunters followed to the bridge and no farther—whatever bound them to the broker's will, Marisol's charm outranged it, or the broker's reach ended at the water, or luck, for once, had decided to sit on their side of the table.

At the top of the steps, in the dark above the warehouse district, they stopped and breathed.

* * *

It was only there, bent double with her hands on her knees, that Tam noticed the silence.

The city's dead still shouted. The beggar still argued, the drowned thing still paced. But it was all *distant* now, muffled twice over, as though she'd stuffed wool in her ears—and cold. A cold spot on her chest, exactly the size of a river-stone.

She looked down. The cord hung intact against her shirt. The stone was gone. Somewhere in the scramble—in the grip of the man with her braid, or the press of the crowd, or the moment Marisol's light had flared—the knot had broken and the charm had fallen, three winters of her grandmother's work, lying in the salt-muck of that square, and eight hunters had seen exactly where it fell.

"No," Tam said. It came out very small. "No, no, no—"

"Hey." Quentin crouched in front of her. "Hey. Look at me. You're bleeding." She was—Tam's palm came away red from her own scalp, a shallow line behind her ear she hadn't felt at all. "You're bleeding and you're here and we're all three of us here. The charm bought us the street. That's what it was for."

"You don't understand, that was—" Tam's voice cracked and she hated it. "She made that for me. Before she died. She said—" *She said it would outlive me.* "It was the last thing she ever—"

"I know," Quentin said quietly. "I know what it is to keep the last thing someone made you." And Tam remembered, with a strange sideways lurch, the workshop in the Hollow, the bones on every shelf, each one a voice Quentin refused to let go silent.

Marisol crouched beside them, setting the lantern-charm down between the three of them. Up close Tam could see her properly for the first time since the square: gray-faced, winded, a bruise already rising on her forearm where a hunter had caught her. Seventy years old if she was a day, and she had run through a strange city at night and driven off soul-hunters with a lamp and her voice.

"How," Quentin said. It wasn't really a question. It was the beginning of several.

"You were gone too long," Marisol said flatly. "Third bell came and went. So I asked Brist what the broker's stall looked like, and I put on my good boots, and I followed the noise." She touched the lantern-charm, and her expression did something complicated—grief and pride tangled together. "Your grandfather made this, Quentin. For crossing the passes, he said. For the things that live in the dark between here and the mountain." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and absolutely unbending. "I told him someone in this family would need it someday. I hate being right."

"The hunters saw where the charm fell," Tam said. Her voice was steadier now, or performing steadiness, which amounted to the same thing. "When they recover it—if they give it to the broker—he'll know what it is. Court-make, pass-grade. He'll know exactly which road we're taking."

"Then we don't take it slowly," Marisol said. She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and offered Tam a hand up. Her grip was iron wrapped in flour-sack softness. "Camp's two miles north. We wake Brist, we break caravan within the hour, and we're in the foothills by dawn." She paused, and for a moment the iron softened into something older and wearier. "I'm sorry about your charm, child. Truly. But hear me: charms can be remade. People who love you—" her gaze moved from Tam to Quentin and back, and something passed across her face like weather, "—people who love you are harder to replace. Tonight I watched two fools run straight into a trap because neither would leave the other behind. That's worth more than any knot of thread."

Tam looked at Quentin. Quentin looked at Tam. Neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that Marisol hadn't just said better, and because saying it would mean admitting it, and admitting it was dangerous in a way no hunter could match.

Above them, far to the north, the mountain stood blacker than the sky, and its hum—no longer filtered through the charm, reaching Tam raw and unmediated for the first time—rose through the soles of her feet like a second heartbeat.

It sounded, she thought, almost pleased.

"Move," Marisol said. "While the moving's good."

They moved. And three streets later, at the edge of the lamplight, Tam glanced back once and saw, on the rooftop behind them, a figure that did not move as the living move—standing perfectly still, watching them go, with a faint golden gleam cupped in its hands like a stolen piece of morning.

Her grandmother's stone. Already found.

Already in the wrong hands.
