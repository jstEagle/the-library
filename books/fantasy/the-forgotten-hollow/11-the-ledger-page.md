## 11. The Ledger Page

The caravan made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, its stones furred with moss that glowed faintly green when Tam's lantern-charm passed over it. Quentin had not yet grown used to such small magics—the way light could be *asked* to stay, the way fire burned without wood—but tonight she barely noticed them. Tonight she had other things to think about.

Old Brist sat apart from the others, on a broken lintel with his back to the tower wall, and in his lap he held a leather roll tied with a cord of braided hair. He had been holding it since dusk. He had not spoken since dusk either, which for Brist was its own kind of omen; the old man filled silence the way rivers fill valleys, constantly, without effort.

Quentin crossed the camp to him, her boots crunching on gravel that whispered underfoot—everything here whispered, everything here had something to say, and she had learned in three days on the road to keep her shields up or drown in the chatter of buried things.

"You said you'd show me when we were safe," she said. "Marisol says this is as safe as we get."

Brist looked up at her. In the lantern-light his face was a map of old roads, every line leading somewhere he didn't want to go. "Safe," he repeated, tasting the word like spoiled milk. "Girl, there is no safe from what's written on this page. There's only far, and only for a while." His hands worked at the cord anyway. "But you've a right to see it. More right than I ever did."

The cord came free. The leather unrolled with a sound like a sigh.

Inside lay a single sheet of vellum, thin as onion skin, and even before Quentin touched it she felt it—not with her hands, but behind them, in that place where the dead spoke through bone and blood. The page *hummed*. It hummed the way a struck bell hums after the sound has supposedly ended, a vibration living in the material itself, patient and terrible.

"It's written on soul-stuff," Brist said quietly. "Not ink. Ink can be washed out. This can't."

Quentin knelt across from him and looked down at the page.

At first glance it was almost ordinary—a ledger column, ruled lines, entries in a hand so fine and regular it might have been set by machine rather than man. Each line held a name, a date, a sum, and a notation. She read the first few aloud in her head, and each one landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well:

*Harrow, Edda — sold whole — 41st year of the Under-Court.*
*Merrick, Josiah — sold whole — 43rd year.*
*Vale, Sera — sold partial (two-sevenths) — 44th year.*

Sold whole. Sold partial. As casually as a grocer noting flour and salt.

"Whose names are these?" she asked, though she already knew the shape of the answer.

"Every soul the court beneath the mountain holds a claim on," Brist said. "That page is one of hundreds. Thousands. I stole it the night I ran—thirty years ago now. I told myself I'd use it to find my daughter's name and buy her back." His voice caught on something old and rusted. "I never found the courage to read past the first column. Some cowardices keep a man alive."

Quentin reached out and turned the page flat against his knee. Her fingers hovered above the writing, not touching—she had learned that much about soul-work already, that some things should not be handled until you were ready to be handled by them.

She began to read.

She read the way a woman walks through a house where someone has died, quickly and carefully, eyes sliding over details she doesn't want to see and catching them anyway. Names she didn't know. Dates in a calendar she'd never been taught. Prices listed in currencies of memory and years and love—she saw one entry marked *paid in first laughter*, another *paid in the color blue*, and she understood, with a lurch, that the court beneath the mountain dealt in stranger coin than gold.

Then, halfway down the second column, a name stopped her breath.

*Vasquez, Quentin.*

Her own name. Written in that fine, patient hand. Thirty years old.

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. The crickets outside the camp kept singing, the lantern-charm kept its soft green pulse, Tam laughed at something Marisol said over by the cookfire—and none of it reached her. She was underwater. She was reading her own name on a list of the owned.

Below the name, the notation: *sold partial (one-third) — entered prior to birth — consideration paid: passage, two souls, westward crossing.*

"*Partial,*" she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like a bone carved too thin. "It says partial. It says—" She couldn't finish. Her eyes kept snagging on the same words, going back to them like a tongue to a broken tooth. *Entered prior to birth.* Before she was born. Before she had drawn a single breath, someone had stood in whatever cold hall housed this ledger and signed away a piece of her.

Brist leaned close, following her finger, and when he read the entry she heard him inhale sharply through his teeth. "Ah," he said softly. "Ah, girl. I'm sorry."

"Sorry?" The word cracked out of her sharper than she meant. "Sorry for what? You didn't write it."

"No. But I know what it means." He tapped the notation gently, his nail clicking against nothing—the ink sat a hair's breadth above the vellum, she realized, hovering, alive. "One-third. That's not a trimming. That's not a lock of hair off the top. A third of a soul is your courage, or your grief, or your joy in making things. A third of a soul is a room in your house you'll walk past your whole life knowing the door is locked and you've never once seen inside."

Quentin stared at the entry. *Consideration paid: passage, two souls, westward crossing.*

Two souls. Passage westward. Westward led to the Hollow. The valley that refused magic. The valley no one crossed into lightly, because there was nothing in it for the magic-soaked world—no power, no trade, no purchase.

Except refuge. Except a place to hide.

Her mother had come home to the Hollow thirty years ago, pregnant, alone, and silent about everything that had come before. Quentin had grown up thinking her mother's silence was grief for a father never named, a life never described. She had built stories around it—scandal, heartbreak, exile. Ordinary human sorrows.

Not this. Not a bargain struck in a vault beneath a mountain, with her unborn self as currency.

"She bought passage home," Quentin said. The words tasted like ash and copper. "With me. With part of me. She was running, and she paid the toll with—" Her hand rose to her sternum, pressed flat against it, as if she could feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth. And maybe she could. Maybe she always had. All those years of standing at the edge of the Hollow's border feeling like a door slightly ajar. All those years of hearing the dead through her hands and wondering why the gift felt less like hers than like something *lodged* in her.

"Oh," she said, very quietly. "Oh, that's why."

"That's why what?" Brist asked, gentle.

"The bones." She was still looking at the page. "I hear the dead through my hands. Everyone in the Hollow thinks it's just—me. A quirk. A trick of the valley. But it isn't, is it? A third of me is *theirs*. Court-stuff. Soul-stuff. I've been carrying a piece of that mountain inside me since before I took my first breath, and it's been listening back."

The thought arrived whole and terrible, and she let it in because there was no shutting it out now: every whisper she had ever carried for a grieving neighbor, every name she had carved into memorial bone, every comfort she had given—had it been her? Or had it been the missing third, reaching toward its own kind? Was her gift a lamp she'd lit, or a window someone else had left open?

She thought of the stranger dying in her workshop, pressing the map into her hands, whispering her mother's name. *He knew,* she thought. *He came to me because he knew what I was. Because we were both on this page.*

And there it was—she made herself look, made herself run her finger down the columns until she found it, four entries below her own name:

*Alder, Wren — sold whole — 29th year.*

Wren Alder. The stranger whose corpse lay wrapped in oilcloth in the back of the caravan wagon, waiting for ground deep enough to hold him. Sold whole. No fractions for him, no careful arithmetic of loss. They had taken all of him—or claimed all of him—and somehow he had gotten loose enough to run, and bleed, and die at her door with a map in his fist.

"He wasn't running to escape," she said slowly. "He was running to deliver. To me. Because my name was next to his."

"Ledgers have their own gravity," Brist murmured. "Names written side by side pull at each other. I've seen it before." He paused. "I've *felt* it. My daughter's entry—I never found it, but some nights I swear I could feel the page leaning toward wherever she is, like a compass needle."

Quentin folded her hands in her lap so they would stop trembling. It didn't work. She looked at the camp instead—at Tam's bright head bent over a whittling knife, at Marisol stirring the pot with the fierce economy of a woman who had fed people through worse nights than this—and tried to feel like the person they thought she was. The carver. The quiet one with steady hands. The woman who had walked out of the Hollow with a map and a dead man's errand.

Was any of that hers?

If a third of her belonged to the court—if that third had been shaping her hearing, her instincts, her pull toward the dead—then who had chosen this road? Who was sitting here reading this page? The self she'd built, or the piece that had been sold before she could object?

"Girl." Brist's voice cut through the spiral, rough and low. "Whatever's happening behind your eyes, stop it."

"You don't know what's—"

"I know exactly what's happening. I watched myself do it for thirty years." He rolled the page closed between them, and the humming dimmed to almost nothing, and Quentin realized she'd been clenching her jaw against it. "You look at that entry and you think it rewrites you. You think everything you are is suddenly suspect. Every choice, every skill, every stubborn bone in your body—you're asking yourself which ones the court put there." He shook his head. "Don't. That way lies being owned twice. Once by the ledger, and once by the fear of it."

"And what way would you suggest?" Her voice shook. "Pretending I read my own name on a slave list and feel fine?"

"No. Feel however you feel. But remember this." He leaned forward, and the lantern-light caught the wet in his old eyes. "They wrote your name down. They wrote *one-third* down. Which means they themselves reckon two-thirds of you was never theirs to touch. Two-thirds of you they couldn't buy, couldn't sign for, couldn't enter in their fine book. Whatever they took, girl—they left most of you standing. And most is more than some of us ever got to keep."

Quentin wanted to believe him. She reached for the belief the way a drowning woman reaches for driftwood, and found it wouldn't quite bear her weight yet. Not tonight. Tonight the entry sat in her mind like a splinter working deeper: *entered prior to birth.* She had never been asked. She had never been given the chance to be anything other than partly purchased.

Over by the cookfire, Marisol looked up. Of course she did—Marisol missed nothing, had missed nothing since the night she'd appeared on the road behind them with a packed bag and a lantern-charm and the flat declaration, *"You didn't think I'd let you walk into that world alone."* Their eyes met across the camp, and Marisol read something in her face, because Marisol always did, and started to rise.

"Not yet," Quentin called softly. "Later. Let me—let me sit with it a minute."

Marisol held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded and sat back down, though her eyes stayed on Quentin like a hand on the shoulder.

Quentin unrolled the page again.

She made herself read it properly now, all of it, every name—because these weren't strangers anymore, not entirely. Somewhere on this page was Brist's daughter, though he'd never found the entry. Somewhere was Wren Alder, sold whole. Somewhere were dozens of others, sold in fractions and wholes, their griefs and joys and first laughters itemized in a hand too beautiful for what it recorded. If she was going to carry this page to the mountain, she would carry it knowing what it weighed.

Near the bottom of the last column, one final entry caught her eye. Not because of the name—she didn't recognize it—but because of the notation beside it, written in a different ink, darker, newer:

*Claim contested. Holder advised: the partial Vasquez asset has crossed the border. Recovery recommended before the debt matures.*

Below that, a date. Eleven days hence.

The cold that moved through her then had nothing to do with the night air. She read the entry three times, and each time the meaning assembled itself more completely, more horribly. *Recovery recommended.* Someone at the court knew she was coming. Had known since she crossed the treeline. And eleven days—

"Brist," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "When does the pass close for winter?"

He frowned. "Another month, why—"

She turned the page toward him and watched him read. Watched his face do what hers must have done minutes ago: go still, then pale, then old.

"Eleven days," he said. "That's not a warning. That's an appointment." He looked up at her, and for the first time since she'd known him, Old Brist looked frightened all the way through. "They're not waiting for you to come to the mountain, girl. They're counting down to come collect."

Behind them, at the edge of the lantern-light, the moss on the fallen watchtower flickered—all at once, all together, green to grey to green—as if something passing in the dark had brushed against it on its way east, toward the mountain, moving fast.

Tam stood up from the cookfire, whittling knife forgotten, staring at the tower.

"Did anyone else," he said slowly, "just feel that?"
