## 12. Whose Hands Are These

Quentin did not sleep.

She sat by the banked fire with the ledger page spread across her knees, and she read her own name until the letters stopped looking like letters. *Quentin Vasquez. Partial.* Two words in a stranger's ink, thirty years old, and they had undone her more thoroughly than any wound.

The camp around her breathed. Tam curled in his bedroll like a comma, one hand tucked under his cheek, his shielding charm—a cracked amber bead on a leather cord—glinting faintly where it had escaped his fist in sleep. Old Brist snored with the rhythm of a man who had made peace with his guilt decades ago and only argued with it now on special occasions. Marisol's breathing was the steadiest of all, slow and deliberate even in sleep, as if she refused to let even unconsciousness take her by surprise.

And Quentin sat with her hands open in her lap, palms up, staring at them as if they belonged to someone else.

*Whose hands are these?*

They were carver's hands. She knew every callus, every scar: the white line across the left thumb where a chisel had skipped when she was nine; the burn from the day she'd learned that bone, freshly boiled, holds heat like a grudge; the fine nicks along both index fingers, dozens of them, a lifetime of small surrenders to the work. Her mother had held these hands and taught them. Her neighbors had trusted them. The dead had whispered through them for as long as she could remember, and she had built an entire life on that whispering—her trade, her name, her place in the Hollow.

But if a piece of her soul had been sold before she was born—if some clerk beneath the mountain had entered *partial* beside her name while her mother's blood still warmed the ledger's table—then what exactly had been listening through her all those years?

The question had been circling her since lamplight, patient as a vulture, and now it landed.

Because here was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing she had barely admitted to herself: the bones did not always feel like hers. When she carved, there were moments—deep in the grain, when the whispers rose and her chisel moved without her deciding to move it—when she felt less like a listener and more like a doorway. A hinge. Something opened in her and something else passed through, and afterward she would surface from the work gasping, unsure how long she'd been gone, unsure who had been holding the tools.

She had always called it gift. She had called it grace. She had wrapped it in the language of vocation because the alternative was too frightening to name.

But what if it was neither? What if the hollow place inside her—the missing piece, the *partial*—was not empty at all but resonant, a struck bell's absence, and everything she had ever heard was just the echo of her own incompleteness ringing against the world?

What if she had never heard the dead at all? What if the dead had simply been pouring through the hole in her, the way wind moans through a broken window, and she had mistaken the draft for a voice?

Her stomach turned over. She pressed her palm flat against the cold ground and felt nothing—not the murmur of buried things, not the low chorus of the earth's dead that lived everywhere except the Hollow. They had crossed out of the valley days ago, and the land here was loud, so loud, layered with the unquiet and the waiting, and normally she would have been cataloguing it, learning its dialects. Tonight she heard it all and trusted none of it.

Because if the hearing wasn't hers, then maybe nothing was.

Maybe her patience was the patience of something missing. Maybe her stillness was a vacancy. Maybe the way people in the Hollow brought her their griefs—their dead fathers, their drowned daughters, their bones wrapped in linen—and said *you understand, Quentin, you've always understood*—maybe they had all been talking to a gap. Comforted by an absence. Laying their sorrows into a hollow like stones into a well, and calling the silence wisdom.

Her mother's face rose behind her eyes, young again, the way it appeared in the oldest memory Quentin owned: bent over a carving block, hair tied back with a strip of red cloth, humming. Humming the lullaby she had paid at the ferry, the one the ferryman had taken from her like a coin, and she had given it gladly because it was the last of her mother she carried that wasn't bone.

Had her mother known? Had she sat in this same posture, decades ago, staring at her own hands, knowing what she had signed? *Partial.* Not stolen. Sold. Someone had weighed Quentin's soul on a scale before Quentin drew breath and decided how much of it the court beneath the mountain could have, and her mother had let the ink dry.

*I'll ask her,* Quentin thought, and then the thought turned to ash, because her mother's soul-echo waited in the archive under the mountain, shelved like a jar of preserves, and to reach it Quentin would have to walk into the mouth of the thing that owned a piece of her.

Unless—

Unless the hearing wasn't hers, then maybe the wanting wasn't either. Maybe this whole journey, the map, the dying stranger, the ledger page—maybe none of it was choice. Maybe she was a contract walking itself toward fulfillment, a debt with legs, drawn up the mountain by the gravity of the missing piece the way water is drawn downhill. The herald's smile on the glacier came back to her. *Then come claim it from the shelf.* As if the court had been expecting her. As if she were not a traveler but a return.

Her breath went thin. The fire collapsed into itself with a soft sigh, and the darkness leaned closer, and Quentin Vasquez—who had spent her whole life steady-handed over other people's dead—found her own hands shaking like a frightened animal's.

She didn't hear Marisol wake. She never did. One moment she was alone with the spiral, and the next her surrogate aunt was settling onto the ground beside her, wrapped in her shawl, close enough that their shoulders touched. Marisol never asked *are you all right*, because the answer was always obvious and usually no. She just arrived, the way weather arrives.

"You're doing the thing," Marisol said quietly.

"What thing."

"The thing where you sit very still and convince yourself you're nobody." Marisol looked into the coals. "Your mother did it too. Right before she—" She stopped. Started again. "It runs in the family, apparently. The convincing."

"Marisol." Quentin's voice came out scraped raw. "What if it was never mine?"

Marisol didn't answer right away. That was one of the things Quentin loved about her, though she rarely let herself use that word even in the privacy of her own skull: Marisol refused to hand out comfort like cheap bread. She made you wait for the real loaf.

"The hearing?" Marisol said at last.

"All of it." Quentin turned her hands over, showed her the calluses as evidence, though of what she couldn't say. "If part of my soul was sold before I was born, then I've been living next to a hole my whole life. And maybe the hearing isn't a gift. Maybe it's just... the hole. Wind through a broken window. Echo off the emptiness. Every bone I ever carved, every grieving family I comforted—I might have been a doorway, Marisol. A hinge. Not a person. Just the shape of what's missing in me, and everything I thought I was poured through it."

She ran out of air. The confession hung between them, ugly and naked, and Quentin braced for Marisol to deny it—to say *don't be absurd, of course it's yours*—the kind lie, the easy lie, the one anyone would tell.

Marisol took a slow breath instead.

"When you were six," she said, "you found a sparrow dead under the eaves of my kitchen. Do you remember?"

"No."

"I do. You wouldn't bury it. You sat with it for half a day and then you went and got your little knife—you could barely hold it—and you carved it a marker. A stone, actually, because you couldn't carve bone yet. You scratched a bird into it. Crude as anything, wings like two ladders. And then you said—" Marisol's voice caught, just slightly, and she cleared her throat. "You said, *it should be remembered properly. It worked hard.*"

Quentin stared at her. "I don't remember that."

"I know. But I do. And I've watched you do it ten thousand times since, with bigger birds." Marisol turned to face her fully, and in the dim light her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "A hole doesn't grieve, Quentin. A hole doesn't decide a sparrow deserves a marker. A hole doesn't spend half a day sitting with something dead because *it worked hard*. Whatever echoes through you—that's the wind. But the kneeling down? The carving? The staying? That's you. That has only ever been you."

"But the hearing—"

"Might be the hole. Fine. Say it is." Marisol shrugged, brutal and gentle in the same motion. "Say every whisper you ever heard was just the draft through your brokenness. You still chose what to do with it. Nobody sold your kindness. Nobody carved your markers. Whoever signed that page, whoever took a piece of you before you had a name—" her jaw tightened— "*they* decided what you'd be missing. They never once decided what you'd do with what remained. Thirty years, mija. Thirty years of choices, all yours. You don't get to hand them back now just because the truth is heavy."

Quentin's eyes burned. She looked down at her hands again—at the calluses, the scars, the nicks—and tried, really tried, to see them as Marisol saw them. Not hinges. Not doorways. Hands that had knelt. Hands that had stayed.

"And if I go up there," she whispered, "and take the piece back, and the hearing stops—"

"Then you'll be deaf and whole," Marisol said, "instead of haunted and halved. And you'll still be the woman who carved a ladder-winged bird for a sparrow. The hearing was never the best part of you, querida. I promise you that. I've known you since you were six, and the best part of you has always been what you do with what you hear."

The spiral didn't vanish. Quentin doubted it ever fully would; some questions keep their teeth. But it loosened, the way a fist loosens, and she could breathe around it. She folded the ledger page with care—her name facing inward, against her heart, where it could not look at her—and slid it into her coat.

"Whoever sold me," she said slowly, testing the words like new-carved bone, "made a hole in me. But the life I built around the hole..." 

"Is yours," Marisol finished. "Every splinter of it. Now come. Brist wants to move before the light does, and Tam will pretend he's been awake for hours, which means he fell asleep again on watch."

Despite everything, Quentin huffed something that was almost a laugh.

* * *

They climbed.

The valley behind them had been gentle country, rolling and green, but the mountain did not believe in gentleness. The trail narrowed to a shelf of stone stitched to the mountainside, wide enough for one, with a drop on the left that went down and down into cloud. Above them the peaks wore their snow like old lace. The air thinned until each breath felt borrowed, and the silence of the high places pressed against Quentin's ears—which was its own strange torment, because the silence was not silent. Not to her.

The dead grew sparse up here, but they grew *strange*. She caught fragments as they climbed: a shepherd's ghost keeping pace with them for an hour, humming a song about wool; something old and enormous sleeping under the glacier, dreaming in colors rather than words; and once, faintly, from far below and far ahead at the same time, a sound like pages turning. Thousands of pages. She did not mention it to the others. Some things you carry alone until you understand them.

Tam walked behind her, chattering to fill the thin air. He had strung a new cord for his shielding charm after losing the original in the night market, threading the cracked amber bead alongside three river pebbles and a button off Brist's coat, and he narrated the repairs like a saga. "—and the pebble from the western fork is for grounding, obviously, and the button is for luck, and honestly the button might be doing the most work, Brist's buttons have survived things buttons should not survive—"

"TAM," Brist called from the front, without turning. "Breath. Save it. You'll want it for the switchbacks."

But Quentin noticed that Tam's chatter had a second purpose, and she loved him a little for it: he was watching her hands. Every time the path narrowed, every time the drop yawned on her left, his eyes flicked to her grip on the rock, ready to catch, ready to anchor. He had heard enough of her nightmare-murmurs two nights ago to know she was carrying something heavy, and he had decided, in his hedge-witch way, that the remedy was noise and proximity. He was not wrong.

By late afternoon they reached the shoulder where Brist called the halt: a hollow beneath an overhang of gray stone, sheltered from the wind that screamed past the lip of the cliff like a choir with no congregation. While Tam coaxed a fire from scrub pine, Brist stood at the edge, looking up the trail toward the pass they'd reach tomorrow.

Quentin joined him. For a while neither spoke. Below, the clouds filled the valleys like milk in a bowl. Ahead, the mountain reared into a sky gone the color of iron, and somewhere on its far side—or inside it, or beneath it—waited the court, the archive, the shelf, the echo of her mother's voice, and the piece of herself she had come to reclaim or to free or perhaps, if the spiral won, to leave forever.

"Brist," she said. "When you served them. The clerks who kept the ledger—did they ever talk about partials? Souls entered incomplete?"

His jaw worked. He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"They talked about them like inventory," he said finally. "Like a bolt of cloth cut short. But there was one clerk, old Vess, near the end, who used to mutter that the court had it backward. Said a partial soul wasn't lesser. Said the missing piece made a *hunger* in a person, and hunger was the only thing the court truly feared." He glanced at her sideways, and his old eyes were unreadable. "He said the hungry ones were the only souls he'd ever seen walk back out."

Quentin turned that over as they ate—hard bread, hard cheese, snowmelt—and as the fire died and Tam dropped mid-sentence into sleep, and as Marisol's breathing settled into its deliberate rhythm beside her.

Hunger. She lay back against the stone and considered it. All her life she had thought of the hollow in her as a wound. Tonight, for the first time, she wondered if it might also be an engine. The thing that had driven her out of the safest valley in the world. The thing that had made her pick up a dying stranger's map. The thing that was carrying her, choice by choice, up a mountain that ate souls.

Not a doorway. A compass.

She slept, and dreamed of nothing at all, which felt like mercy.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold, and with it came the bells.

They heard the Pass of Hanging Bells before they saw it: a sound like rain on tin, distant and arrhythmic, drifting down the glacier wind. Tam stopped mid-stride. Brist went pale in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"That's tomorrow's crossing," Quentin said. "Isn't it. We're a day early."

"We're not," Brist said quietly. His eyes stayed fixed on the notch in the ridge ahead, where, as the light strengthened, they could make out hundreds—thousands—of small shapes suspended on lines strung across the pass, swinging and glinting. "The court knows we're coming. It moved the bells."

Marisol's hand found the lantern-charm at her belt. "Moved them how?"

Brist didn't answer. High above, on the ridgeline, something slender and robed unfolded itself against the brightening sky and stood watching them—a silhouette thin as a needle, still as winter.

Waiting to see who would speak truth first.
