## 14. The Court's Herald

The glacier began at the place where the last green thing gave up.

Quentin felt the change before she saw it—a thinning in the world's voice, as if the land itself were holding its breath. For days the road had been loud with the dead: farmers murmuring under their fields, travelers whispering from the shoulders of the road where they'd fallen, whole villages humming beneath the stones like bees in a wall. Tam had woven her a new shielding charm after the night market, a knot of red thread and river-clay that sat warm against her sternum, and even through it the dead pressed close, curious, hungry for someone who could hear them.

Then the trees stopped. Then the grass stopped. Then there was only the ice, blue-white and ancient, climbing toward the mountain in a long frozen tongue, and the silence of it was so total that Quentin's ears rang.

"No one dies up here," Old Brist said quietly. He walked with his staff planted careful as a man crossing a frozen pond, though the ice held them all without complaint. "That's the point of it. The court doesn't want its approach cluttered with ghosts. Nothing up here has ever been allowed to die."

"Nothing lives either," Marisol muttered. She had her lantern-charm swinging from her belt, and its little flame burned strangely on the glacier—straight and colorless, like a needle of light. "I've never seen a place so empty it *hurts*."

"It doesn't hurt," Quentin said. Her breath came out in silver plumes. "That's what frightens me. It's not empty. It's *kept*. Like a room swept before company arrives."

Tam glanced back at her, wind-whipped and pale, his hedge-witch's braid half-undone. "You can feel that? Through the charm?"

"I can feel the absence." She touched the knot of thread at her chest. "It's like pressing your hand over your own heartbeat and finding nothing there."

They climbed. The glacier rose in slow gray-blue terraces, crevassed and groaning somewhere far beneath, and the mountain above them grew until it filled half the sky—a black mass shouldering into the clouds, its peak lost in weather that did not move. Quentin had seen mountains all her life from the rim of the Hollow, but those had been distant guardians, blue and benign. This one was a door standing closed, and every step brought them closer to the hinge.

It was near midday—or what passed for midday under that motionless cloud—when they saw the figure waiting on the ice ahead.

* * *

It stood alone on a shelf of glacier perhaps two hundred paces off, and it had been standing there, Quentin understood at once, for exactly as long as it needed to. It wore no coat against the cold. Its clothing was the color of deep water, or of ink diluted drop by drop, and it moved when it moved the way reflections move—perfectly, and only when you were not quite looking.

"The herald," Brist said. His knuckles had gone white around his staff. "Don't take anything from its hands. Don't answer any question twice. And whatever it offers—and it will offer—remember that the court has never once given a gift."

"You've met it," Marisol said. It wasn't a question.

"I served under it for eleven years." Brist's voice was flat as slate. "It remembers me. That may be the most dangerous thing on this ice."

They walked on, because there was nowhere else to walk, and the herald waited with the patience of stone, and the cold came up through Quentin's boots like a rising tide.

Up close, the herald resolved into something almost human and entirely wrong. It had a face—the shape of one, smooth and symmetrical as an egg—but the features seemed applied rather than grown, eyes like polished garnets set too evenly, a mouth that was simply a line until it chose otherwise. When it spoke, its voice came from everywhere at once, from the ice beneath their feet and the air inside their lungs.

"Quentin Vasquez." It said her name the way a jeweler names a stone. "Daughter of Elena Vasquez. Carver of the Hollow. Half-soul." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down on silk. "Welcome home."

"This isn't my home," Quentin said.

The garnet eyes did not blink. "All roads are home to what the road was made for. But we needn't quarrel about geography." The herald turned—not its body, which stayed perfectly still, but its attention, sliding across each of them in turn like a lamp being carried down a row of cells. "Old Brist. Thief of page nine-and-forty. Eleven years in our service, thirty-one years running from us. You have aged badly."

"I've aged honestly," Brist said. "Which is more than I could say for myself back then."

"And the hedge-child who shields her, and the woman with the lantern-charm who followed three hundred miles because she loves this carver more than she fears the mountain." The herald's mouth became, briefly, something that was not a smile. "How touching. How *useful*. Attachment makes such clean leverage."

Marisol stepped forward, and Quentin caught her arm. "Don't," she said softly. "It wants you angry. Angry people make bad bargains."

The herald inclined its head, a gesture of exquisite courtesy. "The Hollow taught you caution. Good. Then let us be cautious together." From the folds of its sleeve—it had sleeves now; Quentin was certain it hadn't a moment ago—it drew out a small object and held it cupped in both palms.

Light spilled between its fingers. Not firelight, not sunlight—something warmer than both, something that made Quentin's chest ache before her mind understood why. It was the light of a hearth seen through a window from a dark road. It was the light of a kitchen at dawn. It was the particular gold of a memory she did not have and had missed her entire life without knowing its name.

"Do you know what you are missing?" the herald asked gently. "Of course you don't. That is the cruelty of it. One cannot miss what one has never been whole enough to perceive. And yet—" it tilted the cupped hands slightly, and the light shifted, and Quentin heard, faint and far away, a sound like her own laughter, "—you have spent your whole life listening for something. Through bone, through stone, through the bodies of the dead. Always listening. Always leaning toward a voice just past hearing." The garnet eyes fixed on hers. "You have been listening for yourself, Quentin Vasquez. For thirty years, a piece of you has sat on a shelf beneath this mountain, singing to itself in the dark. And it knows you're close. It is singing louder now. Can you feel it?"

She could. God help her, she could. Beneath the shielding charm, beneath the silence of the glacier, something was pulling at her like a tide pulls at driftwood—not from outside but from *inside*, from the hollow space she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for her shape. Her eyes stung. Her hands, her steady carver's hands, had begun to tremble.

"What do you want?" she said, and hated how thin her voice came out.

"To give you a gift." The herald opened its hands fully, and the light bloomed, and for one vertiginous moment Quentin saw the ice around them reflected in it, and herself reflected in the ice, and in her reflection there was a fullness, a wholeness, a face without that faint unfinished quality she'd never been able to name in mirrors. "Your missing piece. Freely given. No toll, no contract, no ledger-line. Walk away from this mountain and be whole for the rest of your life. Every soul you'll ever meet says the half-souls are the loneliest people alive—they lean toward voices because their own is incomplete. You could stop leaning. You could finally stand still inside yourself."

The temptation was not a claw or a hook. That was the terrible thing. It was a warm bath. It was rest. Quentin stood on the freezing ice and felt thirty years of loneliness rise in her like floodwater—every memorial bone carved alone by lamplight, every neighbor who kept a polite distance from the girl who heard the dead, every night she'd lain awake listening to the murmur of the valley and wondering why none of it ever quite reached her, why she was always translator and never addressed. Whole. The word opened in her like a door into a lit room.

"And the price?" she managed.

"There is no price." The herald's voice was honey poured slow. "There is only a choice. The page of the ledger that the thief Brist carries. Leave it here on the ice, and leave these three at the foot of the mountain—they go home, unharmed, with my word upon it. You may even keep your companions' friendship, if they offer it. We ask only that you not climb. Some doors are better left shut by the people they were built to keep out."

"And the souls in the glass," Quentin said. "The ones on the ledger. My mother among them. What happens to them?"

The herald was silent for a moment, and in that silence Quentin understood that this, precisely this, was the question the herald had hoped she would not ask—or hoped she would ask and then decide not to mean.

"They are assets," the herald said at last. "Held in trust. Their disposition is not your concern."

"Not my—" Quentin laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle like bad ice. "You're offering me *myself*, and asking me to abandon everyone else who's ever been stolen. My mother bought my passage into this world with a piece of her own soul and a piece of mine, and you keep her in a *jar*, and you tell me her disposition is not my concern?"

"She consented to the terms."

"She was dying and desperate, and your contracts are written to be unreadable. I've read the page Brist stole. I know how your ledger works." Quentin stepped forward, and Marisol made a small sound behind her, and Tam's hand hovered near his charm-pouch and did not interfere. "Here is my answer, and I'll say it once, so listen well, because I'm told you people put great stock in spoken words. I will not leave the page. I will not leave them. And I will not take my piece from your hands, because anything you give freely, you give to bind. I've carved enough bones to know that the dead always pay for gifts eventually."

The light in the herald's hands flickered.

For the first time, something moved behind the garnet eyes—something quick and cold and old, the way a fish moves under lake ice. When the herald spoke again, the honey was gone from its voice, and what remained was worse: pleasantness worn thin over machinery.

"A stirring speech. The court does so enjoy the stubborn ones. They make the archive more interesting." It closed its hands, and the warm light went out, and the cold rushed back in like water filling a footprint. "But I confess I expected refusal. The court expects everything; that is why the court endures. So let us dispense with negotiation." The herald smiled then—truly smiled, the mouth curving with terrible patience—and gestured with one pale hand up the glacier, toward the black shoulder of the mountain, where far above, barely visible in the unmoving cloud, a seam of amber light marked some vast doorway.

"Then come claim it from the shelf."

The words fell onto the ice and lay there.

"Every soul in our keeping sits where it was placed, Quentin Vasquez. Yours included. If you want what is yours, you must walk into the dark and take it—as your mother took her passage, as the stranger took his map, as every soul in that mountain took its bargain. The court does not come to you. The court *receives*." The smile widened by a degree. "And the court receives so few visitors. We will prepare a room."

It stepped backward—one step, two—and with each step it grew less there, its edges dissolving into the white, until on the third step there was nothing on the glacier at all except four travelers and a circle of ice where the frost had melted in the perfect shape of footprints, already refreezing.

* * *

No one spoke for a long time.

The wind had begun again, or perhaps it had never stopped and they had only now noticed. Tam sank down onto his heels, breathing hard, though he had not exerted himself. Marisol's lantern-charm flame had gone from its needle-straight burn to a low, guttering shiver.

"It offered you yourself," Marisol said finally. "Freely. And you said no." She looked at Quentin with an expression caught between awe and grief. "Child, I watched you look at that light. I watched your face. Do you know what you gave up just now?"

"Yes," Quentin said.

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Inside her, the hollow space ached like a tooth, and somewhere far above, behind the amber seam in the mountain, she could still feel it—that faint, persistent singing, her own voice calling to her across thirty years of dark. It would have been so easy. That was the horror of it. Not a trap she could have spotted, not a lie she could have caught. Just rest, offered by a courteous monster on a field of ice.

"But here's what I learned carving memorials," she went on. "The dead don't regret the things they suffered. They regret the things they abandoned. Every bone I've ever held, the whisper is the same at the bottom of it: *I left them. I left them. I left them.*" She turned to face the mountain, and its shadow fell across all four of them like a hand. "I am not going to spend eternity whispering that I traded my mother's soul for my own comfort. I'd rather stay half of myself forever than be whole and hollow."

Brist made a sound—half laugh, half sob—and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Eleven years I worked for that thing," he said thickly. "Eleven years, and I never once heard anyone refuse it. They told us it couldn't be done. They told us the offer was irresistible by design." He lowered his hand and looked at her, and his eyes were wet and fierce. "Page forty-nine, carver. It's been riding against my heart since the salt city. Whatever happens on that mountain—whatever we find up there—you remember that you said no on the ice. Because the court will spend everything it has making you forget it."

Above them, the amber seam in the black rock brightened.

It happened slowly, the way dawn comes, but there was nothing gradual about its meaning: the mountain was opening. Light spilled down the glacier in a long gold path, straight as a carpet, ending precisely at their feet. And borne down on the wind—faint, threaded through the cold like a smell of bread from a distant house—came the sound of singing.

Not many voices. One voice. Hers.

Quentin stood at the bottom of the golden path with her found family gathered around her and her missing self calling down from the dark, and she picked up her pack, and she started to climb.
