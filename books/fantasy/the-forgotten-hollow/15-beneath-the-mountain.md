## 15. Beneath the Mountain

The door at the bottom of the glacier was older than doors.

Tam knew that was a foolish thought the moment he had it, but he couldn't shake it. Doors were things people made—hinges and handles, thresholds and locks. This was a seam in the mountain's flank, tall as three men and wide as a chapel, its edges worn smooth as river stone, as if the rock itself had decided, ages ago, to open and had never quite finished deciding. No handle. No lock. Only a faint warmth breathing out of the dark beyond it, like the exhale of something enormous and patient.

"Charming," Marisol said behind him. Her voice was steady, but Tam had learned to hear the wire under her steadiness, the way you could hear wire under wax in a good candle. "No hinges. No lock. Very welcoming."

"It doesn't need a lock," Old Brist said. He stood closest to the seam, closer than anyone else dared, his scarred hands hanging loose at his sides. In the blue light of the glacier morning his face looked like something carved and left out in weather. "Nothing that goes in wants to leave. The door only has to keep the world out."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No," Brist said. "It's supposed to make you careful."

Quentin said nothing at all. She stood with her pack straps gripped in both fists, knuckles white, her eyes fixed on the dark seam with an expression Tam had seen on her face only twice before—once over the stranger's corpse, once over the ledger page with her own name on it. It was the look of someone reading a letter they'd been dreading their whole life.

*Tam,* she'd say if he asked. *I'm fine.* And she would be, mostly, because Quentin Vasquez was made of the same stubborn flint as the valley that raised her. But fine wasn't the same as unafraid, and Tam had spent enough years running from things to know the difference.

He stepped up beside her. Not touching—he'd learned that about her too, that touch had to be offered before it was taken—but close enough that his shoulder nearly brushed hers, close enough that she'd know he was there.

"The shielding charm's still holding?" he asked quietly.

"For now." She turned her wrist, where the little woven band sat against her pulse—a hedge-witch's knotwork, ugly as a cart rope and worth more than gold down here. It had cost them dearly in the night market; the escape from the broker's hunters had torn through half of Tam's kit, and this charm was what remained of it. "It'll fray by tomorrow. Maybe the day after."

"Then we move fast."

"We always move fast." Almost a smile. It didn't reach her eyes, but it reached the corner of her mouth, and Tam counted that a victory.

Brist raised one hand, and the party went silent. He pressed his palm flat against the stone seam—and pushed.

The mountain opened.

Not with grinding or thunder, which Tam had braced for, but with a sound like a held breath finally released, long and low and felt more than heard, rising up through the soles of his boots into his teeth. Cold air poured out around them, carrying a smell he couldn't name at first—stone and lamp-oil and something sweeter underneath, something like the air inside a candle shop, like tallow and honey and—

And then he named it, and wished he hadn't.

It smelled like souls.

* * *

The outer halls of the court beneath the mountain were vaulted dark, corridor after corridor of black stone rising into arches no lantern could find the top of. But they were not unlit.

They were lit by glass.

Lanterns hung from iron chains in their hundreds, thousands, receding down every hall into distances the eye refused to measure—and inside each lantern burned not flame but a soft, shifting glow, pale gold, pale green, pale as moonlight through milk. Souls. Kept in glass, burning slow, the way Brist had described it on the road: *like candles that remember being people.*

Tam had imagined it, on the long climb. He'd thought he was prepared.

He was not prepared for the sound.

The murmuring began softly, at the edge of hearing, the way rain begins at the edge of a roof. A whisper here. A sigh there. A voice saying something that might have been a name, might have been a prayer, might have been nothing at all. And beneath it all, so deep it was less heard than felt, a drone—the sound of ten thousand voices all murmuring at once, harmonized by distance into something like a hymn, something like a tide.

Tam stopped walking.

"Tam." Quentin's voice came from somewhere far away, though she was only a step ahead. "Tam, look at me."

He was looking. That was the problem. He was looking at the lanterns, and the lanterns were looking back—or that's how it felt, how it *was*, because each little glow shifted when he passed it, leaned toward him like flowers toward sun, and the murmuring rose to meet him, and he could almost—

Almost make out the words.

*—cold, it's cold in the glass, isn't it cold, we were warm once, weren't we warm—*

*—my daughter, has anyone seen my daughter, she was just here, she was just—*

*—the ledger, check the ledger, my name is in the ledger, I'm owed, I'm owed, I'm—*

Hedge-witch. That was what they called him, what he called himself, runaway apprentice, dabbler, fraud on his worst days. His magic was small magic: charms against chilblains, knots to find lost keys, a shielding song his grandmother taught him before she died and before he ran. Small magic for a small life. He had never once, in all his years of half-learned craft, been *listened to* by anything larger than a house spirit.

But the souls in the glass were listening. They had been listening for decades, some of them, for centuries, sealed in their lanterns with nothing to do but listen—and here came a living boy walking through their halls, warm-blooded and loud-souled, humming with the little leftover magic of the world above, and they turned toward him like the starving turn toward bread.

*—warm, he's warm, oh, oh, he can hear us, he can hear—*

*tell someone, tell someone my name is Aldric and I had a farm above Vail and I—*

*—don't go, don't go, stay, stay, it's warmer where you are, stay—*

Tam's feet stopped obeying him. Or rather, they started obeying something else. He drifted toward the nearest chain of lanterns, hand rising without his permission, fingers reaching for the glass, and the soul inside flared bright as a struck match, pressing itself against the barrier between them, and the murmur became a chorus became a roar—

*STAY*

—and then a heartbeat slammed into his ear like a fist.

Not his own. Quentin's.

She had taken his hand—no. She had pressed his palm flat against her chest, over her sternum, her own hand clamped over his fingers, and beneath the wool of her shirt her heart was beating: steady, stubborn, unhurried. *Thump. Thump. Thump.* The rhythm of someone who had spent thirty years carving bones in a silent valley and had never once, not ever, let the noise of the world hurry her.

"Breathe," she said. Her voice was low, rough, right beside his ear. "You hear that? That's real. That's mine. Everything else in this hole is a lie wearing a pretty light. Listen to me instead."

"I—" His voice cracked. The murmur was still there, still pulling, but the heartbeat cut through it like a plow through frost, furrow after furrow, and he found he could hold onto it. *Thump. Thump.* "I can hear them, Q. All of them. There are so many—"

"I know."

"They're *asking*. They're asking me to—" His throat closed. His eyes were stinging. "Nobody asks me for anything except to leave."

"I know," Quentin said again, softer. She didn't let go of his hand. She didn't look away from him either, and her eyes in the lantern-light were fierce and wet at the edges. "That's exactly why we're here. So come on. Walk with my heart. One step per beat. That's all you have to do."

One step per beat. *Thump,* step. *Thump,* step. It should have been absurd, two grown people shuffling through a haunted hall in time to a pulse, and maybe it was, but Tam didn't care. The murmur lost its grip one finger at a time. The lanterns dimmed behind them, or perhaps he simply stopped turning toward them, which amounted to the same thing.

Marisol fell in on his other side, close enough that her shoulder touched his, and said nothing at all, which from Marisol was its own kind of shelter. Brist walked ahead with his eyes fixed forward and his jaw set like a man passing through a room he'd helped build and could not bear to look at.

They walked that way for what might have been an hour. Might have been three. Time moved strangely under the vaulted dark, stretched thin and slow as honey off a spoon. The halls branched and branched again, and Brist chose turns without hesitating, following routes he had walked in another life, another name. Once they passed a gallery where the lanterns hung in perfect rows, floor to unseen ceiling, thousands upon thousands, and Tam kept his eyes on Quentin's back and counted heartbeats and did not stop until the gallery was behind them.

When the murmur finally faded to something survivable, Quentin released his hand.

Tam immediately missed it. He said so, or started to, and then found he couldn't, because his voice wasn't working right and because some things said aloud become too true to carry.

"You'll get it back," Quentin said, misreading him, or pretending to. "The charm. When we're out of here, I'll help you reweave it. My stitching's decent."

"It's terrible," Tam managed. "Your stitching is a war crime."

"There it is," Marisol muttered. "The boy lives."

Tam laughed—a wet, cracked sound, but a laugh. His hands were shaking. He looked at them, at the tremor in his fingers, and thought about the soul in the last lantern they'd passed, the one that had flared and whispered *thank you* as they walked away, and he made himself hold the thought carefully, the way you hold a coal, because it hurt and because letting it go would be worse.

They were going to break them out. All of them. That was the whole mad point of this mad pilgrimage—that Quentin would stand in front of the Under-Court with a stolen page and a carver's hands and refuse to leave without every name in the book. Tam had agreed to it on a mountainside, in daylight, when it had seemed like a story rather than a plan. Down here, in the murmuring dark, surrounded by ten thousand small burning hopes, it seemed like the only sane thing he had ever been part of.

* * *

They made camp in a side alcove Brist remembered—a guard post once, he said, though the guards were long gone and their brazier stood cold and full of ash. Tam warded the entrance with the last of his knot-charms, three strands of twine and a whispered word, knowing full well it wouldn't stop anything that truly meant them harm, but it would give warning, and warning was worth having.

Quentin sat apart, sharpening her carving knives. She did that when she needed to think—the whetstone's rhythm steadied her, she'd said once, the same way her heartbeat had steadied him. Tam watched the lamplight slide along the blades and worked up his courage.

He crossed the alcove and sat down across from her, close enough to speak low.

"How are your ears?" he asked. "With the charm fraying."

Her hands paused on the whetstone. "Loud," she admitted. "Even shielded, I can hear them. Like standing next to a river. You get used to the sound until you try to sleep, and then—" She shrugged, a small tight motion. "Then you don't sleep."

"Q." He turned the little woven band on her wrist gently with one finger. "When this goes. When we're deep in there and it goes. What happens?"

She was quiet a moment. The whetstone resumed, slower.

"Then I hear everything," she said. "Every soul in this mountain, all at once, no wall between us. Back home, in the Hollow, the dead are quiet. Thin. Like voices through a wall. Out here—" She looked up at him, and Tam saw something in her face he'd never seen there before: fear she wasn't bothering to hide. "Out here, magic is loud, Tam. And I'm made of the stuff. Half of me, anyway. When the charm goes, I won't be standing next to the river anymore. I'll be in it."

"Then we anchor you," he said. "Same as you anchored me. One step per beat."

Something moved through her expression—surprise, then something warmer and more frightened than surprise. "You'd do that? Walk the whole archive like that? In front of the court?"

"In front of the whole Under-Court," Tam said. "In front of whatever's worse than the Under-Court. Q, you put my hand on your heart and walked me past ten thousand ghosts. You think I'm going to let you drown in the same water alone?"

For a long moment she didn't answer. Then she set down the knife, took his hand—the same hand, the one that had reached for the lanterns—and pressed it briefly, hard, over her sternum. *Thump. Thump.* Steady as stone.

"There," she said gruffly, releasing him and returning to her blade as if nothing had happened. "Deposit made. Don't spend it all in one place."

Tam grinned at her, and lay back on his bedroll, and discovered he was tired all the way down to the floor of himself.

Sleep came fast and strange. He dreamed of lanterns, but in the dream they were empty, all of them, thousands of glass bells swinging silent in a windless dark, and somewhere far off a great bone creaked like a ship's mast, and a voice that sounded like Quentin's mother said: *the shelf is waiting, little witch, and your name is already written.*

He woke to Brist's hand on his shoulder and to a sound that froze the blood in his veins.

Footsteps. Many of them. Unhurried, echoing down the main hall, accompanied by a light that was not the soft gold of the soul-lanterns but something colder, whiter, moving like a procession.

"They know we're here," Brist breathed. "They've known since the door."

From the darkness of the corridor came a voice, resonant and courteous and utterly without warmth:

"Quentin Vasquez. Daughter of Elena. The Court Beneath the Mountain bids you welcome—and requests the pleasure of your audience."

Beside Tam, Quentin rose slowly to her feet, knives sheathed, chin lifted, and Tam pressed his palm briefly to his own chest—to the borrowed rhythm still lodged there, steady as stone—and followed her into the light.
