## 16. The Shelf of Names

The archive breathed.

That was the only word Quentin could find for it. The great hall beneath the mountain did not echo the way stone halls should; instead it inhaled and exhaled in a slow rhythm she felt against her skin, a tide of warm air rising from somewhere far below, carrying with it a smell like candle smoke and old paper and something sweeter underneath—something like the inside of a ripe fig, or the moment before rain. Glass cases lined the walls from floor to vaulted ceiling, thousands upon thousands of them, each one lit from within by no flame Quentin could name. And in each case, something glowed.

Souls. She had seen them in the outer halls on the long descent—small captive lights, blue-white as foxfire, some steady and some flickering like moths beating against their jars. But here, in the archive proper, they were not kept in glass at all. They were kept in *shelves*.

Rows of them stretched away into the dark, longer than any cathedral Quentin had ever imagined, longer than sense allowed. Each shelf was carved from bone so old it had gone the color of honey, and on each shelf sat objects: rings, lockets, coins, combs, buttons, teeth, small carved figures, letters sealed with wax gone black with age. Each object held a light inside it, dim or bright, and each light murmured.

The murmur was the worst part. In the Hollow, the dead spoke through her hands only when she touched their bones, and even then their voices came thin and distant, like songs heard through a wall. Here there was no wall. Here the whole archive sang at once—a low, layered drone, hundreds of voices braided together, none of them words yet, all of them *wanting* to be words. It pressed against her hearing the way deep water presses against the ears.

Tam walked close enough that his shoulder brushed hers. He had not let go of her sleeve since the descent began. "It's loud," he whispered. "Even with the charm Brist gave me. It's like standing inside a hive."

"Then don't listen," Marisol said from behind them. Her voice was flat and steady, the voice she used when fear was chewing at her and she refused to show the bite marks. "Listen to my boots. Count my steps if you have to."

Quentin almost smiled. Almost. Then a herald in silver-gray robes gestured them forward with a hand that had too many joints, and the smile died where it stood.

"The Shelf of Names is this way," the herald said. Its voice was pleasant the way a snare is pleasant until your foot is in it. "You were promised an audience. Before audiences, the court permits... preparation. Grief is disorderly. We prefer it filed."

*Filed.* Quentin's hands curled at her sides. She thought of the stranger dying in her workshop with her mother's name crumbling off his lips, and she made herself walk.

* *

The Shelf of Names occupied its own alcove at the heart of the archive, and it was smaller than Quentin expected—almost humble. A single long shelf of pale bone, perhaps forty feet, holding perhaps three hundred objects. No glass. No locks. The lights here burned steadier than anywhere else in the hall, and quieter, as though these souls had been handled so often they had learned reserve.

A ledger lay open on a reading stand before the shelf. It was enormous—the size of a door laid flat—and bound in what Quentin recognized, with a lurch, as the same honey-colored bone as the shelves themselves. Its pages were not paper. They were thin sheets of ivory, and the writing on them moved. Not flickered, not shimmered—*moved*, crawling slowly across the surface like insects under ice, rearranging themselves as debts accrued and interests compounded.

"Your names," the herald said, and made a gesture that might, in a kinder court, have been courtesy.

Quentin approached the ledger the way she approached a bone she meant to carve: slowly, with both hands visible, with respect for what lay inside. The ivory was cool under her fingertips. She found the stranger's entry within moments, because she had read his face for two days after his death and knew the shape of his name in her mouth before she saw it written.

**VESZELY, ILLIAN. SOLD IN FULL. PURCHASER: THE UNDER-COURT. TERM: PERPETUAL. BALANCE: UNPAID.**

Beneath the entry, in smaller script, a note: *Collateral retained pending settlement of associated debt.*

And beneath *that*, cross-referenced in ink that gleamed wet and fresh though it must have been thirty years old:

**VASQUEZ, QUENTIN RAE. ENTERED IN UTERO. SOLD: PARTIAL.**

Partial. The word sat on the page like a stone in a shoe. She had known it since the night with Brist's stolen page, had turned it over a hundred times since, but seeing it here—in the court's own hand, in the court's own house—made it real in a way that scraped.

Beside her name was a second notation, and this one stopped her breath:

*Piece held in trust. Vessel: maternal. See: VASQUEZ, SURENA.*

Her mother's name.

"She's here," Quentin said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, as if someone else were using her throat. "She's not just listed. She's *here*."

The herald inclined its head. "The court preserves certain echoes. Sentiment has archival value." It paused, and its too-many-jointed fingers folded together. "You may speak with it. Once. This is not generosity. It is procedure. Echoes decay without use."

Marisol's hand landed on Quentin's shoulder, heavy and warm. "You don't have to," she said quietly.

"Yes, I do." Quentin heard herself say it and was surprised by how little doubt lived in the words. "I've been talking to her grave for twenty years. She never once answered. I'm not walking past the one place she can."

* *

They found her mother's object near the center of the shelf, because of course they did; Quentin's hands knew the way before her eyes did, drawn along the row like a needle finding north. It was a comb. A plain wooden comb, cheaply made, the kind sold in valley markets for a copper, with two teeth broken—and Quentin knew those breaks, had traced them a hundred times as a child sitting behind her mother while Surena Vasquez pinned up her hair for market days. The comb glowed softly, and inside the glow something shifted, restless, like a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice.

Quentin picked it up.

The archive's drone fell away. The hall fell away. There was only the warmth of the wood in her palm and then—

*—cold morning, frost on the window, a baby crying in the next room and the crying sounds wrong, too thin, like the sound is missing a layer—*

*—the broker's parlor, lanterns green as pond water, and her own voice saying* how much for passage home, all of it, the long way, safe passage, *and the broker smiling with teeth like keys—*

*—she is pregnant and terrified and the valley will not let her magic follow her home, which is exactly why she needs to go home, because out here everything has a price and in the Hollow nothing does—*

*—the price is named. A piece. A sliver. The child will never miss it, the broker says. Everyone is missing something. That is what makes them people—*

*—her mother's hands, signing. Her mother's hands, shaking. Her mother's hands, later, combing a daughter's hair with a comb that now held a piece of her forever, humming a lullaby about a river that runs uphill home—*

Quentin came back to herself on her knees on the cold archive floor, the comb clutched to her chest, tears running freely down her face. Tam was crouched beside her saying her name. Marisol stood over them both like a wall.

"I saw her," Quentin managed. "I *saw*—"

"You saw what the court wanted you to see," Marisol said, but gently, and her hand on Quentin's head was as soft as it had ever been.

"No." Quentin pushed herself upright. Her legs shook. She held the comb out in front of her, and the glow inside it had brightened, agitated, reaching. "No, that was her. That was *her memory*. Marisol, she didn't sell me because she was cruel, or careless, or—" Her voice cracked. "She sold me to buy *passage home*. To bring me here. To the Hollow. Because the Hollow was the only place in the world where nothing could put a price on me again."

The irony of it landed like a blow, and she laughed—a broken, astonished sound. "And I grew up there thinking I was safe. Thinking the silence of the valley was a kindness. And all the time the price had already been paid. I was already *spent*."

The glow in the comb pulsed. And then, for the first time in Quentin's life outside the whisper of dead bone, she heard her mother's voice—not through her hands, not through a carving, but directly, the way living voices travel. It was thin. It was worn smooth by thirty years of preservation, the way river stones are worn. But it was unmistakably Surena Vasquez, and it said:

*"Little wren."*

Only that. Two words. Quentin made a sound that was half sob and half laugh.

"Mama. Mama, I'm here. I came to get you. I came to get all of you—I'm going to break the ledger, do you hear me? Whatever they took, whatever they're keeping, I'm going to—"

*"Hush."* The voice was fond and tired and firm, the exact voice that had once ended childhood arguments with a single syllable. *"Hush, little wren. Listen. I don't have much left that isn't worn through. So listen."*

Quentin listened. The whole archive seemed to lean in.

*"I was afraid,"* her mother said. *"All my life I told myself I left the wide world because it was wicked, but the truth is I left because I lost there. Your father. My sister. The court took its tithe of everyone I loved and called it law. When I carried you, I swore you'd be born somewhere the court's hand couldn't reach. And then I learned the hand reaches everywhere, and the only question is what you feed it."*

*"So I fed it a piece of you. God forgive me. I told myself a piece is not a person. I told myself you'd never know. I told myself a thousand lies in that green-lit room, and every one of them smelled like figs and candle smoke, and I signed anyway, because the alternative was raising you out here where they'd come back for more. They always come back for more."*

The glow flickered, guttering like a candle in wind. Quentin gripped the comb tighter. "Mama, stop, you're wearing yourself out—"

*"Let me spend what's left right."* A pause. When the voice resumed it was softer. *"Wren. What they took from you—it wasn't the part of you that loves. Or grieves. Or carves. I've watched you from in here, through the thin places, whenever the valley's silence thinned. You are not less. Do you hear me? You were never less. You were a girl who learned to listen so well that the listening became a kind of wholeness all its own."*

*"But if you find the missing piece—if you take it back—know this: it will change you. Not because the piece is large. Because you will finally be able to stop wondering. And wondering is a hard thing to set down when you've carried it all your life."*

The glow was dimming now, truly dimming, and terror seized Quentin's chest. "There has to be another way. There has to be something I can give them, something I can trade—"

*"You already carry the trade."* The voice was fading, going thin as thread. *"The page. The boy's debt. And wren—one thing more. One thing I paid extra for, that night, the last coin I had. Ask the court about the flaw in the vault. Ask them why they never repaired it. Ask them who built it."*

*"Ask them about Old Brist."*

The glow went out.

Not faded—*out*, like a pinched wick, and the comb in Quentin's hands was suddenly just wood, old and broken-toothed and ordinary, and colder than the floor.

"No," Quentin whispered. "No, no—come back—I didn't ask about Papa, I didn't ask about—"

"Wren." Marisol knelt and gathered her in, comb and all, and Quentin broke against her the way she hadn't broken since she was nine years old and her mother went into the ground. Tam wrapped his arms around them both, and the three of them knelt together on the bone-pale floor of the enemy's house, holding on.

* *

It was Tam who noticed, later, when the shaking had slowed. He was still holding Quentin's hand—he hadn't let go since the descent, and she suspected he wouldn't for some time—and his thumb had gone still against her knuckles.

"Quentin," he said quietly. "What did she mean? About Brist?"

Quentin looked down at the dead comb. *Ask them who built it. Ask them about Old Brist.* Brist, who had served the court and fled. Brist, who carried a stolen page and a daughter's name like a wound. Brist, who had gone pale at the map's seal and offered everything he had for a single promise.

*Ask them why they never repaired it.*

"He built something," Quentin said slowly. "Before he ran. Something in the vault. Something they've never fixed." She looked up, and through the ruin of her tears something new was kindling—hot, sharp, and steady as a forge. "He's been carrying the key this whole time and doesn't know it. Or maybe he does. Maybe that's what his promise was really for."

The herald reappeared at the alcove's mouth, silver robes whispering, its jointed hands folded in patient ceremony.

"The court will see you now," it said. "Bring your grief. Bring your page. Bring your questions." Its smile was a snare closing. "The magistrates are so very eager to meet the woman who thinks she can break a thirty-year ledger."

Quentin rose. She tucked the comb inside her coat, over her heart, where it weighed nothing and everything. Behind her, three hundred quiet lights kept their watch on the Shelf of Names, and somewhere among them, she chose to believe, her mother rested easier than she had in thirty years.

"Then let's not keep them waiting," Quentin said, and walked toward the dark.
