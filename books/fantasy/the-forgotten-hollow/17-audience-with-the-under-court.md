## 17. Audience with the Under-Court

They came for her at what might have been dawn, though beneath the mountain there was no dawn—only a slow brightening of the glass-lanterns along the corridor walls, as if the court itself had decided to open its eyes.

Quentin had not slept. She sat on the edge of a cot in a room of smooth black stone that was neither cell nor guest chamber, turning Brist's stolen ledger page over and over in her hands until the lamplight wore grooves in her vision. Tam slept fitfully on the second cot, curled around his satchel like it might be taken from him. Marisol stood watch by the door with her lantern-charm cupped in one palm, its light guttering against her weathered face.

When the knock came—three soft raps, courteous as a knife laid on a table—Quentin folded the page into her bodice, over her heart, where it lay warm as a second heartbeat.

"They're ready for you," said the herald.

The herald looked the same as before: silver-robed, face pleasant and unfinished, like a portrait someone had abandoned halfway through. It did not look at Tam or Marisol. It looked only at Quentin, and its smile held the patience of something that had waited thirty years and could wait thirty more.

"Only you," it said.

"She doesn't go alone," Marisol said flatly.

"Mother." Quentin touched her arm. "If they wanted me dead, I'd already be dead. They want to *bargain*." She let a breath out slowly. "Watch Tam. Keep the page's twin safe—" she nodded at the copy Tam had made, laborious and imperfect, in charcoal on birch bark "—and if I'm not back by the time your charm burns down to the wick, burn everything and run."

Marisol's jaw worked. Thirty years of silence and stubbornness lived in that jaw. "You come back," she said finally. "You hear me? You come back whole."

Quentin thought of the word *partial*, inked beside her own name, and could not answer honestly. So she lied, gently, the way you lie to the dying and the frightened: "I always do."

* * *

The way down was long, and the court made sure she felt every step of it.

Corridors of veined black marble opened into galleries, and galleries into vaulted halls where souls hung in glass—not cruelly, Quentin noticed, and that was somehow worse. The vessels were beautiful. Blown glass in colors like deep water and evening, each one cradling a soft light that pulsed faintly, rhythmically, the way a sleeping chest rises and falls. Some of the lights pressed against their glass when she passed. Some turned away.

She heard them. Even muffled behind bone-shields Tam had taught her to raise, even dulled by the mountain's weight, she heard them—a vast low murmur, like wind through a forest of reeds, like ten thousand people whispering *please* in languages dead and living.

One voice cut through all of it, thin and familiar as a scar.

*Quentin-love. Quentin-love, don't listen too hard.*

Her mother. Not the soul-echo from the archive—that had been a recording, a preserved moment—but this was no different, really. Just another light in another glass. Still, Quentin's step faltered, and the herald glanced back with that patient smile, and she understood: they had placed her mother's vessel along this route deliberately. Everything in this place was deliberate.

"I know why I'm here," Quentin said aloud, to cover the shaking in her hands.

"Do you?" the herald asked pleasantly. "Most visitors believe they are here to retrieve something. In truth, they are here to be *appraised*."

* * *

The hall of the magistrates was round, and cold, and lit from below.

Light rose through a floor of translucent stone, pale gold, sourceless, so that the nine figures seated in a crescent before her seemed to float upon it. They were old—not elderly, but *old*, the way mountains are old, the way debt is old. Their robes were the color of unlit candles. Their faces were different ages, different peoples, but all of them shared the same stillness, the same terrible economy of movement, as though blinking were an expense they resented.

In the center of the floor, between Quentin and the crescent, stood a table. On the table lay a single object: a bone the length of her forearm, white as milk, dense with carved script so fine it looked like frost.

The great ledger. Or a piece of it. Her fingers ached toward it before her mind caught up—the carver's instinct, the listener's hunger. She could hear it from here. It did not whisper. It *sang*, low and continuous, a chord made of every name ever entered into it, and underneath the chord ran a pulse like a heartbeat in a locked room.

"You are Quentin Vasquez," said the magistrate at the center. A woman, or the shape of one, with a voice like dry paper folding. "Daughter of Elena Vasquez. Carver of the Hollow. Partial owner of one soul, outstanding balance of thirty years."

"I'm here about the balance," Quentin said. Her voice came out steadier than she'd expected. Rage will do that—it packs itself down tight, like snow, and holds you up. "I have something you want."

"We know," said the magistrate. "You have carried it against your heart since you woke this morning. We do not require theatrics."

"Then let's skip yours too." She pulled the ledger page from her bodice and held it up—not offering it, just showing it, letting the gold underlight catch the ink. "Every name on this page belongs to me now. That's how your contracts work, isn't it? Possession transfers. Brist stole it; I received it; the stranger before him carried it out of this mountain in his own two hands." She let that last phrase sit. She'd learned, these past weeks, that the court respected precision. Precision was their native tongue. "So here is my offer. The page—for every soul listed on it. All of them. Freed, released, contracts voided, whatever language you use when you're being honest. Every name, or nothing."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere far above, water dripped in a rhythm like a patient clock.

The center magistrate tilted her head. "You speak as though the page gives you power over us."

"It gives me power over *them*." Quentin's hand closed around the paper. "Your whole edifice runs on names properly recorded. I've read your page. I've read your contracts. A soul without its entry isn't owned—it's just haunted. Shatter the record and you don't have a debtor anymore. You have a ghost with no leash." She took a step forward, onto the gold light, and let them see her face. "I spent my whole life listening to bones. Do you know what bones say, magistrates? Bones say *everything ends*. Flesh ends. Memory ends. Even names end, unless someone keeps carving them—and I am very, very good at carving."

Something moved in the crescent—one of the outer magistrates leaning toward another, murmuring. The sound of it was like stones shifting underwater.

"You would destroy value," the center magistrate said. "Yours included. Your own partial soul is bound in our greater ledger. Break the system and you break your own claim to wholeness."

"I know." And she did know; she'd lain awake turning it over. "That's the price. I've paid worse ones." She thought of the Hollow, of thirty years of loneliness worn like armor. "But here's what you seem not to understand about me. I came down here ready to lose my piece. What I'm *not* ready to do is walk past all those lights"—she gestured upward, toward the galleries, the glass, the murmuring dark—"and trade a few of them for myself. You offered me that bargain once already, through your herald. My answer hasn't changed. All of them. Or I burn the page, and then I start carving copies of every contract I can find, and I scatter them so wide that your clerks spend the next thousand years chasing paper instead of collecting souls."

It was mostly bluff. She had no idea whether she could do any of it. But rage sharpens a person wonderfully, and lies told with a steady voice have a way of becoming true enough.

The magistrates conferred. Not in whispers—they simply went still, all nine at once, and the stillness stretched so long that Quentin's heartbeat grew loud in her own ears, loud enough that she wondered if they could hear it too, if they were reading the rhythm of her blood the way she read the grain of bone.

At last the center magistrate spoke.

"The Under-Court accepts negotiation," she said, "within the terms of our founding law. Hear them, and understand that they are not negotiable in turn." She raised one hand, and the great ledger-bone on the table drifted upward, rotating slowly, its frost-fine script catching the light. "Contracts may be discharged by three means only. Payment. Forgiveness of the creditor. Or physical removal of the debtor by the creditor's own agency." Her eyes, gray and bottomless, fixed on Quentin. "We will not forgive. You cannot pay. Which leaves removal."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," the magistrate said, with the first flicker of something almost like warmth, almost like cruelty, "that we will open the vaults. We will unlock every vessel belonging to a name on your page—all forty-one of them. And you may carry out of this mountain whatever you can carry out *in your own two hands*. Two hands, carver. Two vessels at most, given the weight of glass and soul. Choose wisely. The rest remain ours, and the page returns to us, and your little rebellion becomes a footnote."

The words landed like stones dropped into Quentin's chest, one after another.

Two. Out of forty-one. Out of the hundreds in the deeper galleries, the thousands perhaps, the whole humming archive of bought and sold humanity stacked in beautiful glass. She thought of Brist's daughter, whose name he'd whispered like a prayer for six hundred miles. She thought of the stranger who'd died on her workshop floor with her mother's name in his mouth. She thought of her mother, whose light had pressed toward her through the glass as she passed.

*This is the counter,* she thought. *This is how they win even when they lose.* They would give her exactly enough mercy to break her heart with it.

"That's monstrous," she said quietly.

"That's *law*," the magistrate replied. "Monstrousness is merely the shape law takes when it grows old enough. You of all people should appreciate the distinction—you carve the dead into keepsakes and call it love."

Quentin's hands shook. She let them. There was no shame in it; the shaking was honest, and honesty was the one weapon they hadn't priced yet.

"Then I'll need time," she said. "To choose."

"One night."

"And the page stays with me until I've chosen."

A pause. The magistrates' stillness deepened, if such a thing were possible.

"Agreed," said the center magistrate. "Guarded, but with you. We are not fools, carver—but we are also not, despite reputation, without honor. Our law binds us as surely as it binds the dead." She inclined her head, and the gesture was almost gracious. "Choose well. Most who stand where you stand choose themselves. It is the oldest choice there is, and we have never once blamed anyone for making it."

"I'm not most," Quentin said.

"No," the magistrate agreed, and for just a moment her ancient gray eyes held something that might, in a younger face, have been called sadness. "We counted on that."

* * *

They led her back up through the galleries, and this time the herald walked beside her rather than ahead, matching her pace with that same infuriating courtesy.

"You did well," it said.

"Don't talk to me."

"As you wish." A dozen steps of silence, glass lights pulsing softly in their alcoves. Then, because it apparently couldn't help itself: "For what it is worth—I hoped you would refuse the easy bargain. It has been four hundred years since anyone did."

Quentin stopped walking.

The gallery stretched away in both directions, vessels glowing blue and green and amber, and among them—third alcove on the left, she'd marked it on the way down—her mother's light burned steady and warm as a hearth seen through a window.

"Why?" Quentin asked. "Why hope for that?"

The herald's unfinished face did something complicated. "Because I am on a shelf too," it said. "And no one has ever come for me."

Then it bowed—actually bowed, robes pooling on the black marble—and left her alone in the corridor of glass.

Quentin stood a long while, the ledger page warm against her sternum, forty-one names heavy as stones in her mind, and two hands, only two hands, hanging empty at her sides.

Above her, far above, through a mile of mountain, she could just barely feel it: the tremor of Old Brist moving through the vault levels, doing something to the walls, humming an old working song under his breath.

He had built a flaw into this place decades ago.

And he had run out of time to tell her how to use it.
