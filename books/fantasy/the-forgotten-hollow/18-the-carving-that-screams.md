## 18. The Carving That Screams

Old Brist had spent forty years learning how courts beneath mountains kept their secrets, and he had learned the most important lesson of all in his first week as a junior archivist: nothing down here was ever truly locked. Locks were for thieves. The Under-Court did not fear thieves; it feared its own servants remembering what they had done.

So it had built no locks at all. It had built habits.

Brist walked those habits now, slow and stooped, a gray man in a gray robe among the deliberating magistrates' outer halls, carrying a tray of salt-cakes and lantern-oil that no one would ever eat or burn. Servants were furniture. Furniture was invisible. He had been furniture for three days since the girl Quentin had stood before the dais and demanded the impossible with her chin up and her dead mother's name in her mouth, and in those three days he had memorized the rhythm of the court's attention the way he had once memorized it as a young man with quick hands and a coward's heart.

The magistrates deliberated in the Chorister's Hall, where the walls were made of pressed soul-glass and hummed faintly, like wasps dreaming. They would deliberate for hours yet. They loved deliberation the way moths loved flame—helplessly, fatally, until it consumed them. Every hour they spent weighing the ledger page against the girl's insolence was an hour the vault below stood guarded by exactly two wardens who had not been relieved in a day and a half, because relief schedules were also a habit, and habits could be counted on like gravity.

Brist set down his tray beside a pillar of black basalt and straightened his back, which hurt, because he was old, and because he had not straightened it honestly in forty years.

*Wren,* he thought, the way he thought it every morning when he woke and every night before sleep took him under. *Wren, Wren, my Wren.*

His daughter's soul sat on a shelf four levels down, in a jar of green glass, and had sat there for thirty-one years, and every day of those years Brist had told himself the same lie: *not yet, not yet, there will be a better moment.* There was never a better moment. There was only this one, worn smooth as a river stone by all the worse moments that had come before it.

He checked the oil flask in his sleeve. He checked the page of the ledger hidden against his chest—he had given Quentin a copy, but the original still rode over his heart, because some things you did not hand to a girl you had known for two weeks, however steady her hands were with a knife. He checked the flaw.

Ah, the flaw. His one masterpiece.

They had made him build the vault's bone-lattice when he was twenty-six, a carver of small talent and large desperation, and they had watched him work, because the court watched everything, and so he had hidden his treachery inside his competence, which was the only hiding place the court never thought to search. A vault meant to hold souls needed a resonance chamber—a great carved femur of some extinct god-beast, sunk into the floor's center, that amplified the ledger's binding-song through every shelf and jar. He had built it beautifully. He had built it to sing.

And he had built it, in a hollow no inspection had ever found, with a fracture line. Hairline. Patient. Waiting forty years for the right note struck in the right place by the right traitor's thumb.

The court believed the lattice indestructible because Brist had told them so, and the court believed what its servants told it about craft, because magistrates did not dirty their hands with carving. That was the second lesson of his first week: pride was a bigger door than any lock.

* * *

The descent took him past the shelves, and he made himself look at them, because he owed them that. Jar after jar after jar, green glass glowing soft as foxfire, each one holding a soul folded small. Some of the souls pressed against the glass as he passed, sensing—what? Not warmth; he had none left to give. Perhaps they sensed intention. Perhaps souls, folded that long in the dark, learned to read the shape of a passing heart the way blind folk read faces.

*I'm sorry,* he did not say, because he had said it ten thousand times to jars that could not answer, and apology without action is just noise with good manners. Tonight the apology would be made of stone and fire and his own old bones, and it would be legible at last.

On the third tier, in the row he knew better than his own prayers, a jar flickered as he went by. Green light stuttering, like a candle catching wind. Like a child waking.

Brist stopped. His knees cracked. He put his palm flat against the glass, and the glass was cold, and beneath the cold something turned toward him the way a sunflower turns toward a window.

"Wren," he whispered. "Papa's here."

The light steadied. Held. Thirty-one years, and she still knew his voice. He had sung to her through the glass on the nights the wardens changed shift, lullabies from the salt country, and perhaps that was all she had of him now—song and the vibration of a father's guilt coming through the glass like weather.

"I'm going to be loud," he told her. "It's going to be very loud, my love, and then it's going to be quiet, and then—" His voice failed him, an old bridge finally dropping its last plank. "Then someone is going to open every jar in this room. A girl with kind hands. Her name is Quentin. If you see her soul go by yours, you make friends, do you hear me? She's good people. Better than her father, anyway."

The jar glowed. He chose to believe it was laughter.

He kissed the glass, which tasted of dust and cold, and walked away without looking back, because looking back was for men who intended to survive the night, and he had finished being one of those somewhere around the second tier.

* * *

The two wardens stood at the vault door as predicted, spears of black iron, faces like closed books. They knew him. Everyone knew Old Brist, the gray servant, the tray-carrier, the relic. That was the whole architecture of his survival: he had made himself so harmless for so long that harmlessness had become armor.

"Oil delivery," he rasped, lifting the flask. "For the resonance chamber. Magistrate Vell ordered it maintained during deliberation—the binding-song's gone thin, she says. She says the girl's page has the song out of tune."

Vell had ordered no such thing. But Vell was three floors up, mid-deliberation, and the wardens were tired, and the order was plausible, and plausibility was the currency the court minted best.

One warden waved him through. The other looked at him a moment too long—not suspicion, only the dull curiosity of the exhausted—and Brist gave him the smile of a man whose feet hurt, and the warden looked away, and that was that. Forty years of invisibility, cashed out in a single glance refused.

The vault door closed behind him with a sound like a held breath.

Inside, the resonance chamber rose from the floor's center: the god-beast femur, taller than two men, carved over with forty years of binding-script, humming its low patient note that kept every soul in every jar folded small. Brist crossed the floor slowly, savoring the ache in his knees, the smell of cold stone and old magic, the weight of his own body—all of it, every ordinary sensation, catalogued like a miser counting coins he was about to give away.

He knelt at the base of the femur, at the spot where the script spiraled inward to its origin, and pressed his thumb into the hollow he had carved when he was twenty-six and terrified and already grieving a wife the court had taken as collateral for his debts.

The hollow fit his thumb perfectly. Of course it did. He had made it from his own hand's memory.

"Now," he said to the dark, to the jars, to Wren, to nobody. "Now, now, now."

He struck the fracture line with the heel of his palm, and spoke the note.

Not a word. A note—one clean tone from deep in his ruined chest, pitched to the frequency he had calculated across four decades of nights, the exact frequency at which a hairline flaw becomes a screaming chasm. The sound left him and entered the bone, and the bone listened.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The vault held its silence around him like water.

Then the femur sang.

It began as a tremor in the floor, a shiver in his teeth, and then the flaw opened—not all at once but the way ice lets go of a lake, in a long groaning surrender—and the resonance chamber's song broke key. All of it. Every shelf, every jar, every thread of binding-script woven through the vault's architecture caught the broken note and shattered along it, script flaking off the walls like burning paper, and the hum that had held thousands of souls folded small for centuries twisted into something else entirely.

A scream. The carving screamed. It screamed with forty years of stored voices, and the sound was terrible and gorgeous, a cathedral organ played by a storm, and Brist knelt in the middle of it with his palms flat on the shaking floor and wept like the young fool he had once been, because it was working, it was *working.*

Above him, alarms he had never heard before began to ring. Boots hammered in the corridors. The wardens were shouting at the sealed door, and then striking it, and then doing something worse—summoning help, real help, magistrates with power enough to rebind the lattice before the break spread past the vault.

Which was why the flaw was only half the plan. The flaw bought minutes. The girl had to use them.

He had left her the way in—scratched into the copy of the ledger page, in the carver's cipher only the two of them shared, a route through the maintenance channels he had crawled as a young man: *down the oil shaft, past the cistern, third door, follow the screaming.* She was clever. She was stubborn. She had her mother's chin and, gods forgive him, probably her mother's talent for walking into hell on behalf of other people.

"Come on, girl," he said to the chaos. "Run. Don't be dignified. Dignity is for gravestones."

The door behind him glowed white-hot as something on the far side began to unmake it. Brist got to his feet, joints protesting, and turned to face it, because someone ought to be standing between the court and the flaw for as long as standing was possible, and he was, at last, after forty years, exactly the right man in exactly the right place.

The door came apart like sugar in rain.

Magistrates poured through—or things shaped like magistrates, tall and robed and wrong at the edges, their faces smooth as eggs, their eyes full of ledger-light. The foremost raised a hand and the air itself bent toward Brist, pressure like deep water, a voice that was not a voice saying words that were older than words: *Servant. Kneel. You are owned.*

"No," Brist said, pleasantly. It mattered to him to say it pleasantly. "I really rather aren't."

He had one more thing in his sleeve besides the oil flask. He had taken it from the tray an hour ago: a salt-cake, pressed dense, soaked in lantern-oil. Kitchen chemistry, the humblest magic in any world. He threw it at the base of the resonance chamber, where the fracture breathed, and the flask of oil followed it, and he opened his mouth and gave the broken bone the note again, louder, his last full breath spent on it like a coin.

The chamber detonated.

There was no pain, or rather there was all pain at once, so total it passed through agony and out the other side into something almost like music. Brist felt himself come apart in the blast-light, felt the old bones finally relieved of their long duty, and in the white roar he heard—everywhere, above him, below him, inside him—the jars beginning to open. Not all of them. Not yet. But the first ones, the nearest ones, souls rising out of broken glass like birds startled off a winter field, streaming upward through the shattered lattice toward a sky none of them had seen in lifetimes.

And one light, green as spring, green as a name, circled him once before it went.

*Wren,* he thought, and the thought was the last thing he was, the final shape of him, held a moment in the burning air like a note held in a singer's throat.

*Wren, fly home. Papa's right behind you.*

Then the light took her, and the dark took him, and far above, in the maintenance channels, a girl with kind hands heard the screaming stop and started to run.
