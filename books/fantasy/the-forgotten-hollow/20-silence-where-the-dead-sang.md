## 20. Silence Where the Dead Sang

Marisol had spent sixty years learning to read Quentin's silences, and this one frightened her more than any of them.

They had made camp on the glacier's shoulder, in a hollow of stone where the wind couldn't reach, and Quentin sat apart from the fire with her hands open in her lap—palms up, fingers loose, the way she held them when she was listening. Marisol knew that posture the way she knew her own heartbeat. She'd watched it through a thousand evenings in the Hollow: Quentin by her hearth, head tilted, hands cupped around some scrap of bone, hearing what no one else could hear.

But now there was nothing to hear. Marisol could see it in the way Quentin's fingers drifted closed, slowly, like a door shutting on an empty room.

* * *

The mountain was still shuddering behind them when they'd come down off the ice. Not collapsing—the Under-Court's unraveling was quieter than that, a great exhalation rather than a scream—but the passes rang with it for hours, bells tolling without hands, and the freed souls went up past them like weather. Like birds. Like a river running backward toward the sky.

Marisol had stood in the snow and watched them go, thousands upon thousands, lights rising out of the mountain's broken mouth, and she had felt something she hadn't felt since she was a girl leaving the Hollow: small, and glad, and terrified all at once.

Quentin had not watched. Quentin had knelt in the snow with her hands pressed flat against the ice, as if she could listen through it one last time, and Tam had crouched beside her saying her name, and Marisol had seen the moment it landed—the moment Quentin understood that the silence wasn't temporary, wasn't shock, wasn't the mountain's doing.

It was hers. She had carved it into herself when she broke the ledger. The price and the payment in the same stroke.

She hadn't cried. That was the worst of it. Marisol had braced herself for weeping, for rage, for the kind of grief that throws things and breaks things and needs holding down. Instead Quentin had risen, dusted the snow from her knees, and said, in a voice scraped smooth and empty, "We should get below the treeline before dark."

And she had walked. All night, steady as a mule, while Tam stumbled and Marisol's old knees screamed, because walking was a thing you could do instead of feeling, and Quentin Vasquez had always been fluent in that language.

Now, two days down, camped among pines that whispered ordinary wind-whispers, she sat apart from the fire with her hands closing on nothing, and Marisol decided she had watched long enough.

* * *

"Move over," Marisol said, and dropped onto the log beside her without waiting to be invited.

Quentin didn't move. She was looking at her own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. "I used to be able to tell you what kind of wood this is," she said. "By the dead in it. Every tree keeps its rings twice over—once in the grain, once in whatever's left of the light it drank. I could put my palm on that log and tell you if it grew in shade or sun. Whether lightning ever touched it." Her thumb moved across her own wrist, back and forth, a carver's unconscious motion. "I can't even tell if it's pine."

"It's pine," Marisol said. "I have eyes."

"That's not the same."

"No," Marisol agreed. "It isn't."

The fire cracked and settled. Over by the tents, Tam was pretending very badly to sleep; Marisol could see the boy's silhouette sitting bolt upright inside the canvas, watching them through the gap in the flap. She let him watch. Some things needed witnesses.

"I keep reaching for it," Quentin said. Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "All day. Walking, eating, trying to sleep—I reach for it the way you reach for a stair that isn't there. And there's just—" She turned her hand over, studied the calluses, the old knife scars, the lines of thirty years of work. "Nothing. Just my hands. Just me."

"Just you," Marisol repeated.

"You know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean. And I'm going to say something you're going to hate." Marisol shifted so she could look at the girl directly—her girl, half-raised, stubborn as a mule and twice as likely to kick. "You said 'just me' like it's a wound. And it is a wound, querida, I won't argue that. But do you remember what you told me at the ferry? When you were crying about your mother?"

Quentin's jaw tightened. "That was different."

"It was exactly the same. You said—" Marisol closed her eyes, dredged it up, because she had carried the words carefully all this way and meant to spend them now. "You said, 'Every soul in that ledger is someone who was never allowed to be whole.' You said it angry, with snow in your hair, and then you walked into a court full of monsters and broke the thing that made them rich." She opened her eyes. "You were never allowed to be whole either, Quentin. Not since before you were born. Everything you heard through those bones—some of it was your gift. But some of it was the piece they took, echoing across the distance, calling home. You've been listening to a stranger's voice your whole life and calling it yourself."

"And now the stranger's gone," Quentin said, "and I don't know how much of me was her."

The words hung there, raw and true, and Marisol did not rush to fill the silence. That was another thing sixty years taught you: some wounds need air before they need bandages.

"I'll tell you what I know," Marisol said finally. "I know a girl who came to my door at nine years old with a carved sparrow in her fist and wouldn't say whose grave it was for. I know she learned every neighbor's name and every neighbor's grandmother's name, and remembered which ones liked honey and which ones couldn't abide it. I know she sat with dying people because she couldn't stand for anyone to go alone, even before she could hear what they carried. That wasn't the missing piece, mija. That was you. The piece they stole didn't make you kind. It just made you lonely."

Quentin's breath caught—a small, involuntary sound, quickly smothered.

"You're grieving," Marisol went on, gentler now. "You're allowed to grieve. You lost something real. But you don't get to use that grief as a door to walk out of your own life. I've done it. I left the Hollow once, and I came back broken, and I spent twenty years telling myself the brokenness was the whole of me." She reached over and took Quentin's hand—the scarred one, the carver's hand—and held it the way you hold something that might pull away. "So here's what's going to happen. Tomorrow, and the day after, and every day until it stops being true, when you start retreating into that silence, one of us is going to sit down next to you. You don't have to talk. You don't have to be fine. But you are not going to be alone in it. That's not a request."

For a long moment Quentin didn't answer. Then her fingers turned under Marisol's and gripped back, hard enough to hurt, and she bowed her head, and the sob that came out of her was nothing like the smooth empty voice from the mountain—it was ragged and ugly and thirty years deep, and Marisol pulled her in and held on while the girl who had shattered a court wept against her shoulder like the child she'd once been.

Tam slipped out of the tent at some point and sat down on Quentin's other side without a word, close enough that his shoulder touched hers, and stayed.

* * *

Grief, Marisol thought over the following days, is a country with terrible roads. You cross it slowly, and you backtrack constantly, and sometimes you find yourself in a place you swore you'd already left.

Quentin wept on the third day, raged on the fourth—threw a tin cup into the trees and shouted at the sky, at the mountain, at her mother, at God or whatever kept the ledger of such things—and by the fifth day she had gone quiet again, but it was a different quiet. A quieter quiet. The kind that comes after a storm has passed through and knocked things down and left room to breathe.

She started carving again on the sixth day, working a piece of fallen birch with her belt knife, no design in mind, just the motion of it. Marisol watched her hands move and saw that they still knew their work. Whatever had been taken, the skill remained. The patience remained. The care remained.

"She's going to be all right," Tam said quietly, coming to stand beside her at the fire. He was sharpening his hedge-witch's little knife against a stone, though it didn't need it. He always sharpened it when he was thinking. "Aren't she?"

"She's going to be herself," Marisol said. "Which is harder, and better."

He nodded, chewing on that. Then: "Do you think they know? The ones that got free. Do you think any of them... remembers her?"

Marisol looked at him. The boy had lost his shielding charm in the night market and never replaced it—he'd told her once that he didn't want to, that after hearing the souls in the vault he wanted to stay open to the world even when it hurt. He was young enough to make choices like that and mean them.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because of this." He fished in his pocket and brought out something wrapped in cloth, and unwrapped it with the particular care people give to things that might be holy. "Found it this morning. Outside her tent. I didn't touch it beyond picking it up, and I've been sitting on whether to show her all day, because—" He stopped, swallowed. "Because I think it might be from them."

It was a token, carved from bone. Small enough to close in a fist. Marisol took it gingerly and turned it in the firelight, and her breath went out of her slow.

It was a sparrow.

Not fine work—not the way Quentin's work was fine, all precision and polish. This was rougher, older, the cuts made by someone remembering the shape of a bird rather than looking at one. But there was no mistaking it. And Marisol knew—she knew with a certainty that raised the hair on her arms—whose memory had shaped it. Because Quentin had told her the story once, years ago, the only time she'd ever spoken of it: the first memorial bone she ever carved, at nine years old, a sparrow for a grave she wouldn't name.

A grave that, Marisol now understood, must have been her mother's.

Someone had remembered. Someone who had been in that ledger, who had poured out of the mountain like a river running home, had stopped—or lingered, or sent something ahead—and had carved, in the only language the dead had ever shared with Quentin Vasquez, a thank-you.

Or perhaps not a thank-you. Marisol turned it over. On the underside, in tiny scratched letters, was a single word:

*STAY.*

* * *

They gave it to her together, which felt right. Tam held it out on his open palms and Marisol stood at his shoulder, and Quentin looked at the little bird for a long time before she touched it.

Her fingers trembled. "This is my work," she whispered. "Or—it's the shape of my work. Who—"

"We don't know," Marisol said. "It doesn't say."

"The stranger?" Tam offered. "Or Brist's daughter, maybe. Or—" He shook his head. "Could be any of them. Could be all of them."

Quentin closed her hand around the sparrow, and Marisol watched her face do several things at once—grief and wonder and something new, something that hadn't been there since the mountain. She pressed the token to her chest the way you press a hand to a wound that has finally begun to heal, and when she spoke, her voice was rough but it was hers, wholly hers, with no echo in it at all.

"They heard me," she said. "Even without me hearing them. They heard me anyway."

"Yes," Marisol said.

"And they're telling me to stay." Quentin laughed—a wet, broken, astonished sound. "Stay. As if I'd go anywhere. As if—" She stopped, and looked east, toward the low hills and the long road home, toward the valley that refused magic and the workshop with the cold hearth and the graves that needed tending. "I have to bury the stranger properly," she said. "And Brist. And I have to—" Her hand tightened around the sparrow. "I have to take this home."

"Then we pack at first light," Marisol said, and it was settled, the way things were settled between them now, three survivors of a war nobody else would ever know had been fought.

That night Marisol lay awake in her tent listening to the pines, and near dawn she heard footsteps, soft, and the flap of Quentin's tent whispering open. She peeked out and saw the girl standing in the blue-grey light with the sparrow token in her fist, held up toward the mountains, toward everything that had gone up out of them and would not come back.

"I'm staying," Quentin said to the silence, to the world, to whoever might still be listening anywhere at all. "You hear me? I'm staying. And I'm going to keep carving. Not to hear you. Just because it's mine."

The wind moved through the pines. Nothing answered.

But Quentin smiled—small and unsteady and real—and Marisol realized, watching her, that for the first time in all the years she had known her, the girl was not leaning toward the dead.

She was leaning toward the living. Toward the road. Toward home.

Toward them.

And far to the north, faint as a memory, Marisol would have sworn—though she never told a soul—that the morning wind carried something like birdsong down off the melting snow, as if the mountain itself had finally learned to sing.
