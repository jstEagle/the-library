## 22. The Carver's New Apprentice

The year turned the way water turns a millwheel: slowly, and then all at once.

Quentin felt it most in her hands. They had changed. Not in shape—the knuckles still ached before rain, the left thumb still carried the scar from the first knife she'd ever held—but in their silence. For thirty years her hands had been instruments of listening, conduits through which the dead murmured their unfinished business into her bones. Now they were only hands. They carved, they kneaded bread, they braided Tam's hair when she let them, and when they touched bone they heard nothing at all.

She was learning to call that peace instead of loss. Some mornings she succeeded.

The workshop smelled of juniper smoke and oiled wood, as it always had. Spring light came through the south window in a long gold bar, catching the dust that rose whenever Tam's mallet fell. The girl worked at the second bench now—her own bench, though she'd never say so aloud, because saying so would mean admitting she was staying, and Tam Voss did not admit to staying anywhere. She'd been in the Hollow eleven months, and she still kept her pack by the door, still slept with her boots within reach, still flinched at the word *home* the way other people flinched at a struck match.

But she had a bench. And she had not left.

"Again," Quentin said.

"You said that last time."

"And you'll hear it again until it's right." Quentin crossed the room and stood over her shoulder. On the workbench lay a memorial bone—a sheep femur, sanded smooth, half-covered in the fine spiral script that marked a name for keeping. Old Wenna had died in February, quietly, in her sleep, and her son had come to the workshop with red eyes and a lamb for payment, and Quentin had taken the commission the way she took all of them now: as craft, not communion. The dead could not tell her what to carve anymore. She had to ask the living instead, which turned out to be harder and better.

"Your lines are too deep," she said. "You're fighting the bone."

"I'm *carving* the bone."

"You're arguing with it. There's a difference." Quentin reached down and closed her hand over Tam's, guiding the graver back to the surface. "Feel how it wants to curve here? Bone has a grain, same as wood. It remembers being alive. You don't cut across a memory—you follow it, and it carries your line for you."

Tam went still under her hands. "That's the kind of thing Marisol says about people," she muttered.

"Marisol says it about everyone because it's true about everything." Quentin stepped back. "Softer. Let the bone help you."

The mallet fell again, lighter this time. The line curved where it should, and something in Tam's shoulders unknotted by a degree, and Quentin turned away before the girl could catch her smiling, because Tam Voss would rather swallow gravel than be caught being taught.

Outside, the Hollow went on refusing magic, as it had always done. The valley sat in its bowl of grey stone and green terraces, and beyond the treeline the world shimmered faintly where enchantment pressed against the border and slid off, like rain against glass. Quentin had crossed that line twice now—once going out, once coming home—and she understood it differently than she had as a girl. The valley wasn't a prison. It was a wound that had scabbed over into a sanctuary. Magic could not enter; therefore the court beneath the mountain could not reach in; therefore the people who fled here could set down their packs and, eventually, stop sleeping in their boots.

Most of them. Not all.

*Some inheritances are chosen,* Marisol liked to say, usually while shelling peas or sharpening knives or doing any of the hundred small violent domestic things she did with terrifying competence. *Blood gives you what it gives you. Everything else, you pick yourself.* Marisol had picked Quentin thirty years ago, when Quentin's mother came home from beyond the treeline hollow-eyed and pregnant and silent about why, and the sharp-eyed woman next door had simply begun appearing—with soup, with firewood, with opinions delivered like verdicts. She had never stopped appearing since. Even now she lived in the little house between theirs, and her lantern-charm hung by Quentin's door, dark and inert in the magicless air, kept anyway, the way you keep a sword on a wall.

The bell above the workshop door rang.

It was an ordinary bell, bought from a tinker two summers ago, and it rang for customers and wind and cats with equal indifference. Quentin wiped her hands on her apron and turned, already composing the polite refusal for whatever farmer wanted a fence-post charm she couldn't make anymore—

And stopped.

A child stood in the doorway.

She was small, seven perhaps, maybe eight, thin in the way of children who have learned to eat slowly and trust slowly. Her hair was dark and cropped close, unevenly, the way hair gets cut on the road with a belt knife. Her dress had been good once, embroidered at the collar in a style Quentin didn't recognize—not Hollow work, not lowland work either. Something from farther away. From over the mountains.

And clutched against her chest with both arms, the way children clutch dolls or bread or the last thing they were told to save, was a bone.

It was a long bone, a femur by the look of it, polished dark with handling. And even across the room, even in the valley where nothing sang, even through the wool of Quentin's apron and the thick quiet of her own rebuilt soul—Quentin felt it.

Not heard. *Felt.* A hum, faint as a fly against a window in another room. A pressure behind her sternum, the ghost of a sense she no longer owned, waking up and straining toward the thing in the child's arms like an old dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep.

Tam had gone rigid at her bench. Her hand had drifted, casual and not casual at all, toward the shielding charm that hung empty on its cord around her neck—they hadn't yet found anyone who could recharge it inside the valley, and Tam wore it anyway, bare, the way you wear a scar.

"Hello," Quentin said carefully. She crouched slightly, making herself smaller, the way Marisol had taught her to approach anything frightened. "Are you lost?"

The child looked at her for a long moment. Then past her, at the benches, the tools, the half-finished memorial bone with Wenna's name spiraling across it. Then at Quentin's hands.

"You're the carver," the child said. Her voice was hoarse, road-worn. "In the city of salt and lanterns, the broker's man said there was a carver who broke the ledger. He said it angry, like a curse. I walked a long time." Her arms tightened around the bone. "I didn't know if it was true. But my grandmother said, if it was true, find her. She said—" The child's voice cracked, steadied. "She said the carver would know what this is."

She held it out.

Quentin took it.

The hum swelled the moment it touched her skin—that phantom pressure, that almost-sound, rising like water in a dry well. She turned the bone in the light. It was old, older than the child, older than the road. Carved into its length, worn nearly smooth by centuries of thumbs, ran a script she knew. She had read it on a stolen ledger page by lamplight while her whole understanding of herself came apart in her hands. She had carved its sister-form into the great ledger-bone itself, the day she shattered it.

Court-script. The writing of the under-mountain. And beneath it, deeper, older cuts: a name.

"This is a soul-vessel," Quentin said quietly. "Or it was meant to be. Someone started the binding and never finished it." She looked up. "Whose name is on it?"

"Mine," the child said. "My grandmother sold me. Before I was born. Same as—" She stopped, hearing herself, and her eyes went wide and wet and furious all at once. "Same as you. That's what the broker's man said. He laughed about it."

The workshop was very silent. Somewhere outside a cart rattled past on the valley road, and someone called a greeting, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, and none of it mattered at all.

Quentin looked down at the bone in her hands. At the unfinished binding. At the small name, hers-not-hers, waiting in the grain like a seed in frozen ground.

When the ledger had shattered, every completed contract had voided. Every soul held in glass had poured free. But bindings unfinished—bindings interrupted, half-made, sealed nowhere—those had simply been left hanging. Orphans of the old law. She had thought of them sometimes, in the first raw months, the way you think of a door you didn't lock. She had told herself the court was gone, its magistrates scattered, its archive dust. She had told herself the loose threads would stay loose.

The child stood in her doorway with one of those threads in her arms, and she had walked out of the mountains alone to hold it.

"What's your name?" Quentin asked.

"Sefi."

"Sefi." Quentin set the bone gently on the workbench, between Wenna's memorial and the jar of carving knives, and crouched fully now, eye to eye with her. "My mother sold a piece of me before I was born. To buy passage home. I spent a long time being angry about it, and then a longer time understanding it, and neither one of those things was wrong." She held out her hand, palm up, the way you offer it to a bird. "Nobody in this house will finish that binding. Nobody in this valley can. What we *can* do is teach you to carve it free yourself, someday, if you want. It'll take years. It'll take you learning the grain of things. It'll take you staying."

Sefi stared at her hand. Behind her, Tam had risen silently from her bench, and Quentin could feel the girl vibrating with a dozen questions and a longer history—runaway hedge-witch, stowaway, survivor of the night market, keeper of an empty charm she refused to remove.

"The broker's man," Tam said suddenly. "The one who laughed. Did he follow you?"

"He tried." Sefi's chin lifted. "He stopped at the treeline. He stood there a long time. Then he went away." A pause, and something young and fierce moved under the exhaustion in her face. "They all stop at the treeline. My grandmother told me. She said it's the one place in the world they can't go."

*The valley that refuses magic.* Quentin had spent her whole life thinking of it as the thing that kept her small. She was beginning, finally, after thirty years and one shattered ledger, to understand it as the thing that had kept her safe—and now, apparently, the thing that would keep this child safe too. The wound that had scabbed into a sanctuary. Still taking in the fleeing. Still doing its quiet, stubborn work.

"Well," Quentin said. "You'd better come in properly, Sefi. It's nearly midday, and Marisol will be here soon with food whether we've earned it or not, and she'll want to look at those feet, and she'll pretend to be stern about it, and you should let her. That's just how she loves people."

Sefi didn't move. Her eyes had gone to the workbench—to the memorial bone, to the tools laid out in their careful rows, to the long gold bar of light falling across the wood.

"You really broke it?" she whispered. "The ledger? All of it?"

"All of it."

"Then the others—" Her breath hitched. "There were others like me. Half-made. In the outer vaults, my grandmother said, waiting for contracts that never got finished. When it broke—"

"They went free," Quentin said. "All of them. Whatever they were becoming, they became themselves instead."

Sefi began to cry then, silently, enormously, the way children cry when they have been holding it in for a very long road. And Quentin gathered her in, this stranger-child with a stranger's name on a stranger's bone, and held her in the smell of juniper smoke and oiled wood, and over the small bowed head she met Tam's eyes across the workshop.

Tam's face did something complicated. Then she sighed the sigh of the permanently doomed, took off her apron, and said, "I'll get Marisol. And feed the goat, since apparently we're collecting people now." She paused at the door. Her hand rested a moment on the empty charm at her throat. "For the record," she added, not looking back, "I'm not staying."

"Of course not," Quentin said.

"I have a pack by the door."

"It's a very nice pack."

Tam snorted, and the bell rang as she pushed out into the spring light, and Quentin heard her boots on the path—unhurried, unhurried, taking her time all the way down to Marisol's house, taking her time the way people do when home has stopped being a word that flinches.

* * *

By evening the workshop smelled of stew, because Marisol had arrived within the hour, assessed Sefi with one long look, bathed her, fed her, wrapped her feet, and installed her in the spare room with the efficiency of a general taking a hill. Now the three of them sat in the lamplight—four, counting the bone, which lay on the table between them like a guest who had not yet been introduced.

"She'll need clothes," Marisol said, darning a sock with lethal calm. "And lessons. Real ones, letters and numbers, not just your bone-mysteries. And she'll need time before anyone asks her a single question about that road. I don't care whose grandmother sent her. Children get to arrive before they have to explain."

"Yes," Quentin said.

"And you'll want to study that vessel. Don't think I didn't see you looking at it during supper like it owed you money."

"I always look at things like they owe me money."

"Mm." Marisol bit her thread. "Your mother looked at things like that too."

It was said lightly, and it landed softly, the way Marisol's hardest truths always did these days—worn smooth by a year of grief shared and grief survived. Quentin's mother's grave lay up the hill under the rowan tree, and beside it now a small carved token, Brist's token, the gratitude of a freed daughter for a freed father. Quentin visited it most Sundays. She brought no offerings, asked no questions, heard no whispers. She just sat, and was her mother's daughter in the only way that remained: wholly, entirely, every piece of her accounted for and her own.

Across the table, Sefi had fallen asleep sitting up, her cheek on her folded arms, her breath gone slow and deep. The lamplight moved on her cropped hair. One hand had crept out in sleep to rest against the dark bone, as if even dreaming, she needed to know where it was—as if she had carried it so long it had become less a burden than a heartbeat.

Tam looked at the child, then at Quentin, and something passed between them that needed no words. *This one stays,* the look said. *However long it takes. However many questions. This one stays.*

Quentin reached across and drew the bone gently from under Sefi's fingers, and the child stirred but did not wake, and Quentin turned the femur once more in the light. The court-script gleamed. The small name waited in the grain. Years from now, when Sefi's hands had learned the grain of things, when she could follow a memory instead of cutting across it, she would sit at that second bench—or a third one, they'd need a third one—and she would carve her own name free with her own two hands, and it would be the truest memorial ever made in this valley, because it would be the opposite of a memorial. A beginning.

Quentin set the bone on the shelf beside her mother's oldest tools, where the lamplight would find it every morning.

Outside, the valley lay quiet under the stars, magicless and stubborn and safe. Beyond the treeline the world went on shimmering, full of broken bindings and unfinished names and roads that led, eventually, to doors. Somewhere out there, she had no doubt, others were walking. Others had heard the story of the carver who broke the ledger, told angry like a curse or hopeful like a prayer, and were turning their feet toward the Hollow.

Let them come. The bell above the door would ring. There was room at the benches. There was stew enough, and Marisol's sternness, and Tam's pretended leaving, and the long patient lesson of the grain.

Some inheritances are chosen. Quentin blew out the lamp, and in the dark the house breathed around her—child asleep, girl pretending, old woman darning—and she chose hers again, gladly, the way you choose a door and leave it open.
