## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The boundary stones did not want tending. Sebastian had known this since he was nine years old, when Guildmaster Odile first put a whetstone and a jar of lamp oil in his small hands and told him to walk the circuit alone. The stones were older than the guild, older than the village of Hollowmere that huddled in the valley's green lap, older — Odile claimed — than any kingdom whose banners had ever flown over the ridgeline. They were black basalt, taller than a man, and they did not weather. Rain slid off them without staining. Moss refused to take root. In two hundred years of recorded guild service, not one stone had cracked, chipped, or so much as dulled.

And yet every evening at dusk, the Valeward Guild walked the circuit and tended them.

Sebastian knelt at the seventh stone with his cloth and his oil, performing the motions he had performed four thousand times before: three passes along the eastern face, clockwise, always clockwise; a whispered word in a language no living member spoke fluently; a bow of the head toward the valley's heart. His breath fogged in the autumn cold. Behind him, the sun was going down over the western ridge in a spill of copper and ash, and the shadows of the stones stretched long across the meadow like the fingers of drowned men reaching for shore.

*Why?* he asked the stone, as he asked it every evening.

The stone said nothing. It never did.

He rose, brushed the damp from his knees, and looked down the line of his own life: eight more stones to the north, then the river crossing, then home. Somewhere behind him, smoke from the guildhouse chimneys rose straight up into the windless sky, carrying the smell of bread and woodsmoke and the particular bitterness of the elder tea Odile brewed every night without fail. It smelled like safety. It smelled, he thought sometimes, like a lid on a pot.

He was twenty-three years old, the youngest oath-keeper in the history of the Valeward Guild, and he could not remember a time when he had not felt both held and kept — the way one holds a bird, or keeps a prisoner.

* * *

The guildhall had been a granary once, or a temple, depending on which elder was telling the story. Its beams were black oak gone silver at the edges, its floor worn into shallow valleys by generations of feet walking the same paths. Seven chairs stood around the long table, though only six were ever occupied now. The seventh — the high-backed seat at the head, carved with a circle of nine interlocking hands — had been empty for eleven years, since the death of the last guildmaster before Odile. No one sat in it. No one explained why.

Sebastian came in with the cold still clinging to his coat and found the others already gathered.

Tomas had claimed the corner nearest the hearth, as always, his enormous frame folded into a chair built for smaller men, mending a fishing net that did not need mending. Tomas mended things that did not need mending the way other men prayed. Beside him sat his sister Petra, sharp-faced and sharper-tongued, arguing with old Merric about the ledger even though the ledger was balanced — it was always balanced; balancing it was simply where their arguments went to live. At the far end, quiet Sister Anwen was stitching a new hem onto a ritual robe that would be worn by no one, because the robes only ever needed hems and never needed wearing. And near the window, half in the last light, sat Odile.

Guildmaster Odile Vane was sixty-one years old and looked carved rather than aged, all planes and angles, her grey hair bound back so tightly it seemed to hold her thoughts in place. She was watching Sebastian come through the door with an expression he had learned to read the way sailors read weather: attention without warmth, scrutiny without accusation.

"The northern stones?" she asked.

"Tended." He hung his coat on the peg. "All eight."

"And the word?"

He gave it. The word was *Vaelun*, or close enough — none of them could truly pronounce it. It was the same word every keeper whispered at every stone every dusk, passed mouth to mouth down two centuries until its meaning had worn away entirely, leaving only the shape of it, like a coin rubbed smooth by a thousand thumbs.

"Good," Odile said, and rang the small bell that opened the evening rite.

They rose together. Six voices spoke the Litany of the Vale — *We keep what was given. We guard what was charged. We forget what must be forgotten* — and Sebastian, as always, stumbled slightly on the third line, because it had always struck him as an odd thing to promise. *We forget what must be forgotten.* How could you promise to forget? Forgetting wasn't a duty. It was a failure you dressed up in ceremony.

But he said the words. He always said the words. That was what belonging meant here: you said the words whether they meant anything or not, and after enough years, the saying itself became the meaning.

Afterward there was bread and stew and Merric's complaint that the tithe carts from Hollowmere had come up two sacks short, and Petra's counter-complaint that Merric counted sacks the way misers count sins, and Tomas laughing so hard at some private joke that he knocked his spoon into the fire. Ordinary sounds. Warm sounds. Sebastian sat among them and felt the familiar ache beneath the warmth, the way you might feel a draft in a warm room — barely there, impossible to ignore once noticed.

Six keepers, tending a border no one remembered why it existed, for a charge no one could name, in a valley that the maps forgot to draw.

*Dead ritual,* he thought, and hated himself for thinking it, and thought it again anyway.

* * *

He couldn't sleep. He told himself it was the moon — full and low and insistent, pouring silver through his shutters — but that was a lie he offered kindly to himself, the way you'd offer a blanket to a shivering child. The truth was the ache again, grown louder in the dark. So he pulled on his boots and his coat and went out into the blue-black night, and his feet, knowing him better than he knew himself, carried him west to the stones.

The first stone stood at the edge of the barley field, and beside it — this was the thing visitors never believed, though no visitors ever came — the world simply changed.

Inside the valley: barley, orchards, the river, the village, the guildhall, the whole soft ordinary machinery of a life. Outside the stones: the same grass, the same soil, the same sky. But between them lay a line you could feel with your palm held flat, a faint pressure like standing too close to a waterfall, a hum in the teeth. The elders called it the Veil. The villagers called it nothing at all, because the villagers did not know it existed. That was part of the keeping, too — the guild remembered so the valley didn't have to.

Sebastian pressed his hand against the air above the stone and felt the hum answer, faint and patient, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"Still there," he murmured. "Whatever you are."

He walked the circuit under the moon, not tending tonight, just walking, counting the stones the way he'd counted them as a boy — one through nineteen, and the gap where a twentieth should have been. There had always been nineteen stones and a gap. When he'd asked Odile about the gap as a child, she had gone very still, in that way of hers, and said only: *Some doors are closed from both sides.* He hadn't understood then. He didn't understand now. But lately the question had begun to live in him like a splinter working toward the surface.

Because here was the truth he could say to no one, not even Tomas, not even in the dark of his own skull where he tried most nights to bury it:

The rituals were hollow. The guild was dying. Not loudly — nothing in the valley died loudly — but the way a fire dies when the wood is spent: fewer flames each year, more ash. Once the guild had numbered thirty. Then twelve. Now six keepers and a chair no one sat in. The villagers brought their tithes out of habit and their children out of politeness, and no young person had taken the oath in fourteen years. Odile trained Sebastian as though the future depended on it, but the future, Sebastian suspected, was a room with one candle in it, and the candle was him.

And yet.

And yet when he stood at the stones at dusk with his cloth and his oil and his worn-smooth word, he felt — beneath the hollowness, beneath the melancholy — a rightness he could not deny. As if the ritual were a key that fit a lock somewhere far below the floor of the world, and even if no door answered anymore, the key still knew its shape. Belonging to something dead, he thought, was still belonging. A grave is a kind of house.

He reached the ninth stone — his favorite, facing east toward the pass, toward the world beyond the ridges he had crossed perhaps six times in his life — and stopped.

Something was wrong.

It took him a moment to name it, the way it takes a moment to notice a missing picture in a familiar room. The hum. The hum beneath the stones was different tonight — not louder, not softer, but *quicker*, urgent, like that heartbeat through the wall beginning to run. He laid his palm flat against the basalt and the vibration climbed up his arm and settled in his chest, and for one vertiginous instant he had the mad certainty that the stone was not humming at all.

It was calling.

Then the wind shifted, coming down off the eastern pass, and it brought with it a smell that did not belong in the Valeward Valley and had never belonged there in all his twenty-three years.

Smoke. Woodsmoke and something fouler underneath — burnt hair, burnt flesh.

And on the wind, thin and ragged and unmistakably human, a scream.

Sebastian's hand dropped from the stone. Far to the east, past the barley fields, past the river bend, at the very edge of the wardline where the world's rules bent and broke, a figure was staggering down through the dark — stumbling, falling, rising again, trailing something dark behind it across the silvered grass.

Moving toward the stones.

Moving, Sebastian realized with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with everything else, *through* them — the wardline rippling around the stranger like water around a wading leg, which should have been impossible, which had never happened, which broke every law the guild existed to keep —

— and the stranger fell at the foot of the ninth stone, and did not rise.
