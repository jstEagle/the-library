## 2. A Proclamation in Ash

The raven came at dawn, and Odile knew before she opened the message tube that it carried ruin.

She had been awake for hours, as she was most mornings now, standing at the window of the guildhouse's upper room with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea gone cold. Below her, the valley lay silvered in early light — the terraced gardens, the crooked rooftops of Millbrook, the boundary stones marching up the slopes like grey sentinels. Forty years she had watched this valley wake. She had buried two guildmasters before her and sworn to a third on his deathbed that she would keep the Vale safe.

She had not expected to fail so soon.

The raven beat against the bars of its cage, feathers dark with road dust and something darker still — ash, Odile thought, though the nearest city lay three days' ride away. The courier who had loosed it at the border relay would have been one of theirs, a watcher paid in old coin to send word of anything unusual moving through the lowlands. The tube was sealed with black wax, stamped with the sigil of the crown: a broken tower beneath a rising sun.

Aldric's sun. Aldric's tower, broken by his own hand when he took the throne.

Odile broke the seal with a thumbnail that trembled more than she liked. She read the proclamation once, standing. Then she sat down on the edge of her narrow bed, because her knees had opinions of their own, and read it again.

*By decree of His Majesty King Aldric, First of His Name, Protector of the Realm: let it be known that all persons gifted with sorcery, hedge-craft, or any art forbidden under the Edict of Ashes are hereby declared enemies of the crown. Such persons shall be seized where found and delivered to the king's justice, or burned where they stand. No sanctuary shall be offered them, neither in hold nor hamlet nor holy ground. Those who shelter the gifted share their crime and their pyre.*

*Let every lord, every magistrate, every honest subject search out these abominations and give them up. Reward shall be given for information. Punishment shall be given for silence.*

*So it is written. So it shall be done.*

Below the royal script, in the watcher's cramped hand, a single line had been added: *They are burning them in Corvale already. The roads are full of hunters. God keep you.*

Odile set the paper down on the bed beside her and looked at her hands. They were an old woman's hands, spotted and strong, hands that had kneaded bread and bound wounds and pressed shut the eyes of friends. They did not shake now. That was something. That would have to be enough.

* * *

By midmorning the elders were gathered in the round chamber beneath the guildhouse, the one with no windows and a floor of packed earth older than any of them. Seven chairs ringed the room. Six were filled. The seventh — Sebastian's place, at the foot, where the youngest always sat — stood empty, because Odile had given him the morning's rounds along the eastern stones, and he had gone gladly, whistling, glad of any errand that took him into the open air.

She felt the lie of it sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"Read it again," said Marta. Marta was the eldest, keeper of the stores, a woman built like a root cellar who had not raised her voice in thirty years and did not need to.

Odile read it. She did not soften the words. They deserved the whole weight of them, and besides, softening changed nothing. When she finished, the silence in the round chamber was so complete she could hear the earth itself settling.

"They can't mean it," said Bren, youngest of the elders after Sebastian, a big gentle man who mended the valley's fences and wept at lambing season. "Burn them *where they stand*? Whole families? There are children—"

"There are children in Corvale," Odile said quietly, "and the watcher says the fires have started."

Bren closed his mouth. His hands, resting on his knees, curled slowly into fists.

"The wards will hold," said Halric. He said it the way he said everything, flat and certain, a man who had tended the boundary stones for twenty years and believed in them the way other men believed in scripture. "They've held for two hundred years. Nothing crosses. Nothing sees in. Let Aldric burn every city from here to the sea — he'll never find this valley. We don't exist to him. We've never existed to anyone."

"That," said Marta, "is precisely what worries me."

Odile let them argue. It was her craft as much as anything — knowing when to speak and when to let the room wear itself raw against the truth. Halric held that the wards were absolute. Marta observed, mildly, that wards were maintained by keepers, and keepers were mortal, and there were only seven of them left, and the youngest had never once been permitted to learn what the rituals actually *did*. Tomas pointed out — Tomas, who saw everything, who missed nothing — that the proclamation spoke of *searching*, which meant hunters, which meant men on the ridgelines with dogs and cold iron, and wards kept things out but had never been tested against a man patient enough to walk the border for a month, watching, counting, learning.

"And what," Tomas asked, looking directly at Odile with his pale, unhurried eyes, "do we do about the charter?"

The room went still.

"What about it?" Halric said.

"It says what it says," Tomas said. "And if hunters come, and the wards break, and our people are dragged before some captain who asks why seven farmers in a forgotten valley perform rites at standing stones twice a season — I'd like to know what story we're telling. Because the charter tells a different one than the one we tell Sebastian."

"We don't discuss the charter," Halric said. "That was decided before any of us took our seats."

"Decided by whom?"

"By the guild. By tradition. By—" Halric's jaw tightened. "It doesn't matter. The girl's question stands regardless: can the wards hide us? Yes or no."

Every head turned to Odile.

She looked around the ring of faces — Marta's grim patience, Bren's anguish, Halric's brittle certainty, Tomas's terrible quiet — and she understood that whatever she said next would become the truth they all lived inside. A guildmaster's words were load-bearing. She had learned that young, watching her predecessor lie beautifully to a dying woman about her son's fate, and understanding, even at fifteen, that some lies were beams holding up a roof over other people's heads.

"No," Odile said.

Halric started forward in his chair. "The wards—"

"I have read the maintenance ledgers since before you took your first watch, Halric." Her voice stayed level, almost gentle, which made it worse and she knew it. "The wards are not failing, but they are not what they were. Every year the rituals cost more and hold less. Every year the stones drink deeper and give back less. Two hundred years ago, a guild of forty maintained this border. Thirty years ago, we were twelve. Now we are seven, and one of those seven is a boy we've taught to sweep stones without telling him why." She paused. "If Aldric's hunters come in force, the wards will slow them. They will not stop them. And they will not hide us forever, because forever requires keepers, and we are dying faster than we can be replaced. You all know it. You've known it for years. This proclamation has only made it impossible to keep pretending otherwise."

"So we run," Bren said hoarsely. "We take the children and go east, to the free cities—"

"With what? Our people are farmers. Half of them have never seen the river, let alone crossed it. And the roads, Bren — you heard the proclamation. *No sanctuary.* The free cities are a rumor and a hope, and between here and there lies a kingdom hunting for exactly what we might be carrying."

"Carrying," Marta repeated softly. Her eyes had gone sharp. "Are we carrying something, Odile?"

And there it was. The second stone in her chest, heavier than the first.

Odile rose. She walked to the old iron-bound chest that sat in the chamber's center, the one none of them ever opened, and she rested her hand upon its lid, and she felt — as she always felt — the faint wrongness beneath, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"The charter stays sealed," she said. "That is my decision as guildmaster, and I will bear the weight of it alone. What matters now is this: we say nothing to the valley. Not yet. Panic moves faster than riders. We double the watches on the stones. We prepare quietly — carts, stores, routes east — so that if the day comes, we move in an hour instead of a week. And Sebastian—" her voice caught, barely; she covered it with a cough "—Sebastian continues his rounds. He knows nothing. He suspects nothing. That is how we keep him safe."

"He has a right—" Bren began.

"He has a right to be alive at the end of this," Odile said, and for the first time her voice cracked like green wood. "You didn't see his face when his mother left, because none of us did — she went in the night, and we told him she'd abandoned him, and we let him believe it, because the truth would have put a target on his chest that even these wards couldn't hide. I have spent nineteen years building a wall of small lies around that boy. I will not tear it down the week the kingdom starts lighting fires."

The silence that followed was long. At last Marta nodded, once, the way she acknowledged hard weather coming. One by one, the others followed — Halric last, and grudgingly, staring at the sealed chest as if it had spoken.

Meeting dismissed, Odile remained alone in the round chamber. She knelt, stiff-kneed, before the chest, and laid both palms flat on the cold iron.

*I'm sorry,* she thought — to the chest, to the thing behind the walls of the world, to a dead friend whose face she could no longer fully recall. *I'm sorry. Not yet. Let him have a little longer.*

Beneath her palms, faint as a moth against glass, something stirred.

* * *

She found Sebastian at dusk on the eastern ridge, re-setting a leaning boundary stone, dirt to his elbows, hair plastered to his forehead. He worked the way he did everything — completely, cheerfully, as if tending a dying tradition were simply a good day's labor. When he saw her he grinned, and the grin went through her like a knife through wet cloth.

"Guildmaster. Come to inspect my stonework?"

"Come to bring you supper, since you'll forget it otherwise." She sat on the flat rock beside the path and unwrapped the bread and cheese she'd carried up, and they ate while the light drained gold out of the sky. He talked — about the stone, about Tomas's terrible map-drawing, about whether the orchard would yield this year — and she listened, and memorized him, the way she had begun memorizing things lately without admitting to herself why.

"Odd thing," he said, mouth half-full, nodding westward. "The birds. Have you noticed? Flocks coming over the ridge at odd hours, going east. Like they're leaving somewhere."

Odile followed his gaze. Against the darkening sky, a ragged line of birds crossed the valley's rim, silent, purposeful, wrong.

"Birds fly east in autumn," she said.

"It's not autumn." He frowned at them, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes — something quick and unplaceable, like a fish turning under ice — and then it was gone, and he laughed and stole the last of her cheese. "Well. If the birds know something we don't, I suppose they'll tell us eventually."

*Yes,* Odile thought, wrapping the empty cloth with steady hands. *I imagine something will.*

She walked down the ridge ahead of him, into the valley, into the last ordinary evening any of them would have, and behind her, far to the west beyond the mountains, she could almost hear the fires beginning.
