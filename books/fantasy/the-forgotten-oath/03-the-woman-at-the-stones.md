## 3. The Woman at the Stones

The boundary stones had never once surprised Sebastian Pemberton.

For eleven years he had walked the circuit of them — dusk and dawn, in rain, in snow, in the long amber light of harvest evenings — and for eleven years they had done precisely what stones do: nothing. He would press his palm to each one, speak the words Odile had taught him before his voice had finished changing, and feel the faint hum beneath the granite like a sleeping animal's heartbeat. *We hold. We ward. We keep.* The ritual was as familiar as his own hands, and just as unremarkable.

So when the seventh stone spoke back, he nearly fell into the bracken.

It was not a voice, exactly. It was a shudder that traveled up through his palm and into his teeth, a discordant note struck somewhere deep inside the valley's old silence. The air above the stone rippled — there was no better word for it, though ripple was wrong too; it *bent*, the way heat bends over a forge — and out of that bending came a woman.

She did not walk through the wards. She fell against them, the way a moth falls against glass, and then she simply collapsed, folding down along the stone's mossy flank until she lay half in the valley and half out of it, as if the border itself could not decide whether to admit her or spit her back.

Sebastian stood very still, his hand still pressed to the granite, his heart doing something arrhythmic and painful against his ribs.

She was young — younger than him, perhaps, though it was hard to tell. Her hair had been dark once; now it was matted with ash and dried blood, plastered to her cheek in ropes. One sleeve of her grey traveling coat was soaked through from wrist to shoulder, and the ground beneath her arm drank the red out of it in slow, greedy pulses. In her right hand she clutched a leather scroll case so tightly that her knuckles had gone the color of tallow, as if letting go of it would require a strength she no longer possessed.

"Hello?" Sebastian said, and immediately hated how small his voice sounded.

Her eyes opened. They were grey-green, fever-bright, and they fixed on him with an intensity that made him take an involuntary step backward. Her lips moved. No sound came. Then, on the second attempt:

"You're one of them." Her voice was cracked clay. "A keeper. You're — gods, you're *young*."

"I'm the youngest oath-keeper of the Valeward Guild," Sebastian said, because when frightened, he retreated into rote. "State your name and your business at the—"

She laughed. It was a terrible sound, more cough than laughter, and it left a bright thread of blood at the corner of her mouth. "Business. Yes. My business is that I've run four days ahead of men who want to burn me, and your pretty little wall is the only door in the world that doesn't open."

"The wards don't open," Sebastian said. "That's rather the point of them."

"They opened for me." She lifted her free hand — trembling, blistered at the fingertips, the skin around her nails raw and split as though something had burned its way *out* of her — and pressed it flat against the stone. The hum beneath Sebastian's palm stuttered. "Because I asked nicely. Because I'm carrying something your guildmaster will kill to see. Or die protecting. I've honestly stopped being able to tell which."

Sebastian's mouth had gone dry. Everything about her was wrong — the blood, the burns, the way the air near her fingers seemed faintly, impossibly warm — and yet the thing that frightened him most was the simplest: magic could not cross the borders of the Vale. Every child raised in the valley knew it the way they knew the sun rose in the east. And this woman was *made* of whatever the wards kept out, leaking it from her wounds like light through a cracked lantern.

He should call for Odile. He should call for anyone. Instead he heard himself say, "You're bleeding on the boundary stone."

"I'm bleeding on a lot of things." Her eyes were losing their focus, sliding past him toward the darkening sky. "Pemberton," she murmured. "You have the look. The jaw. Gods, they said the line had thinned to nothing, but here you are, standing your watch like a good little—"

"How do you know my name?"

But her eyes had closed. Her breathing went shallow and ragged, and the scroll case slipped from her fingers at last, rolling to rest against Sebastian's boot with a soft, final knock.

He looked down at it for a long moment. Then he crouched, hooked his arms under hers, and dragged her across the border — over the line no magic could cross, into the valley where nothing strange had ever happened, hauling the strangest thing that had ever happened directly into the heart of home.

* * *

They laid her in the infirmary room behind the meeting hall, on the cot that hadn't held a patient since old Merrick's bad lung two winters past. Mira the healer worked over her in grim silence, cutting away the ruined sleeve, cleaning wounds that were worse than they'd looked — a gash along the ribs, a burn across the forearm shaped like a handprint, as if someone had gripped her with a fist full of coals.

"It's not natural, this hurt," Mira said quietly, not looking up. "Some of it isn't even *wounds*. Look here." She turned the woman's blistered fingertips toward the lamplight. "This is what happens when the gift gets forced through a body that's been holding it back too long. Like a river through a cracked dam."

"The gift," Sebastian repeated. The word felt foreign in his mouth, a coin from a country he'd only read about.

Mira glanced up at him, and her expression softened into something between pity and apology. "You were raised behind these wards, love. There's a whole world of things you were raised not to know."

By the time Odile arrived, the stranger had drifted back to consciousness, and the meeting hall's long table had filled with every member of the Valeward Guild — all seven of them, which was to say, everyone. Tomas hovered by the door with his arms crossed and his jaw set. Old Fenwick sat with his hands flat on the table like a man bracing for weather. The twins, Petra and Ansel, whispered together in the corner. Odile stood at the head, and her face, when Sebastian searched it, gave him nothing at all.

"She crossed the wards," Sebastian said. "On her own. They—they let her through."

"That's not possible," Fenwick said flatly.

"And yet." Odile came to stand at the foot of the cot. For a moment she only looked at the sleeping woman, and Sebastian saw something flicker across his guildmaster's face that he had never seen there before: recognition. Not of the woman herself — of *what* she was. "Wake her," Odile said. "Gently."

The woman surfaced slowly, like something rising through deep water. Her gaze found Odile first, then swept the room, counting them — seven faces, eight with Sebastian — and something in her shoulders loosened by a fraction.

"The Valeward Guild," she rasped. "All that remains of it. Seven keepers and a wall." She tried to push herself upright, gasped, and settled for propping herself on one elbow. "My name is Yara. I come from Corvane, from the archives beneath the old temple. Three nights ago, King Aldric's witch-hunters burned those archives with everyone still inside them. I am what walked out."

"Aldric," Petra breathed. Even in the valley's isolation, the name had weight. The usurper. The king who had taken the throne over his nephew's body and spent three years tightening his grip on everything east of the mountains.

"He has declared the Purification Edict," Yara went on. Her voice was steadier now, worn smooth by repetition, by the telling and retelling of horror. "Every gifted person in the realm is to be seized, catalogued, and either bound to his service or put to the flame. His hunters have lists. His hunters have *names*. And his hunters followed me here." She let that settle over the room like frost. "I didn't lead them to you. But they know the direction I ran, and they know what I carry, and they will come to this valley the way water comes to a low place."

"What do you carry?" Odile asked. Her voice was level. Too level.

Yara's eyes moved — deliberately, unmistakably — to Sebastian. "The founding charter of your own guild. The true one. The one your guildmaster keeps sealed in the iron box beneath her floor and has never shown a single one of you." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Has she?"

The silence that followed was the loudest sound Sebastian had ever heard.

He turned, slowly, and looked at Odile. Odile looked back at him, and for the first time in the eleven years he had known her — since the day she'd found a hollow-eyed orphan boy at the south gate and told him he had a home — her composure cracked. Just at the edges. Just enough.

"Guildmaster," Sebastian said quietly. "Is that true?"

"We are not discussing charters," Odile said. "We are discussing whether we shelter a fugitive whose presence may bring the king's army down on every soul in this vale. That is the question before us. Nothing else."

"It's always the same question, though, isn't it?" Yara said softly. "What does the Valeward Guild actually *do*? You tend your stones. You speak your little words. You guard a border no one remembers the reason for. Don't you ever wonder? Any of you? Eleven years, boy—" her eyes found Sebastian again, and there was something almost gentle in them now, almost sorry, "—eleven years walking that circuit, and you never once asked what you were keeping *in*?"

"Enough," Odile said.

"The Oath," Yara persisted, her voice gaining strength as Odile's lost it. "Your founders swore it in blood, nine names on a single page, and eight of those names are dust. Ask her about the ninth. Ask her why the charter names a Pemberton. Ask her—"

"*Enough!*" Odile's palm cracked against the table. The lamp flames shuddered. And in the ringing quiet after, she drew herself up and said, in a voice gone cold and formal as winter stone: "The guild will vote. All in favor of sheltering this woman within our walls, speak now."

Tomas voted yes — of course he did; Tomas would shelter a viper if it looked cold enough. Mira, reluctantly, yes. Petra and Ansel exchanged a long look and split their votes, yes and no. Fenwick said no, flat and final: "We are seven old fools and a boy guarding a fence. We cannot fight a king."

Odile herself said nothing for a long moment. When she finally spoke, she did not vote at all. "The guildmaster abstains," she said, "pending matters the guild is not yet prepared to discuss."

Which meant, Sebastian understood with a sick lurch, that she had already decided — and that her decision was no.

Four votes to shelter. Two against. One withheld. And a rule as old as the guild itself, older than any of them: no fugitive crosses the guildhall threshold without unanimous consent.

"So that's it," Yara said. She laughed again, that broken-glass laugh, and lay back against the cot. "I ran four days and bled half my life out to reach the one place in the world sworn to keep the very secret I'm carrying, and you'll turn me out at dawn to burn. Your oath must be very proud of you."

No one answered her. Odile turned to go, and the others began to file out after her, and the lamp guttered low, and Sebastian stood alone beside the cot of a dying woman who knew his name without being told.

"Ninth name," she whispered, catching his sleeve as he turned. Her grip was feeble and burning hot. "Ask her about the ninth name, Sebastian Pemberton. Ask her who wrote it. Ask her why your mother signed a charter your guildmaster swears doesn't exist."

The room tilted. The lamp flame bent sideways as though a wind had passed through sealed walls.

"My mother," Sebastian said, "died before I could walk."

"Yes," Yara said, and smiled with bloody teeth. "That's exactly what she wanted everyone to believe."

Outside, far off beyond the ridge, a nightbird cried — or something that sounded almost like a horn.
