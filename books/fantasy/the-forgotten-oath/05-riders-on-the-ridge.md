## 5. Riders on the Ridge

Yara woke to the smell of beeswax and feverfew, and for one merciful moment she did not remember where she was.

Then the pain arrived, patient as a debt collector, and with it everything else: the ridge, the arrows, the river of her own blood steaming on frozen stone. The three days of running. The way she had crawled the last mile on her elbows because her legs had simply stopped answering.

She was lying in a narrow bed in a stone room that smelled of old wood smoke. Someone had washed her wounds and bound them with linen so clean it must have been hoarded. A cup of something bitter sat on the stool beside her, still warm.

Alive, then. Hidden, then. For now.

She lay still and listened — a habit beaten into her by two years of Aldric's purges. Voices drifted up through the floorboards, low and urgent, the cadence of people arguing about her without saying her name. Beneath the voices, beneath even her own heartbeat, she felt it: the null-field, pressing against her gift like a hand pressed over a mouth. She had crossed into the valley half-dead precisely because the deadening made the hunters' scrying slide off her like rain off slate. But lying here inside it, wrapped in its silence, she felt what she had not let herself feel on the road.

Small. Cut off. Buried alive.

*You chose this,* she reminded herself. *You crawled here on your elbows. Remember why.*

* * *

They came for her at dusk — the young one first, Sebastian Pemberton, moving with the careful gentleness of a man approaching a wounded animal. He set a tray of bread and broth on the stool and did not sit until she nodded.

"You're Pemberton," she said. Not a question. She'd heard the name whispered through the floorboards, had seen the way the older woman — Odile, the guildmaster — looked at him when she thought no one watched. Like looking at a lit fuse.

"I am." He hesitated. "The others said I shouldn't tell you anything. About the guild. About the valley."

"But you're going to anyway."

He almost smiled. "I'm going to ask instead. You said things, when they found you. About an oath. About a ninth name." His voice stayed level, but his hands had gone still in a way that told her how much the stillness cost. "I've tended those boundary stones my whole life. Every dusk. And I don't know what they're for."

Yara studied him. Twenty-three, maybe twenty-four years old, with ink stains on his fingers and the hollow-cheeked look of someone who had been hungry in childhood and never quite believed the eating was over. The youngest oath-keeper of the Valeward Guild. The last keeper. She had carried proof of it across half a kingdom pressed flat between the pages of a stolen book, and now the proof itself was sitting across from her, offering her broth.

"Before I answer," she said, "I need to know something. When the riders come — and they will come — will you give me to them?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped knife. Sebastian didn't flinch, which she noted, and didn't answer immediately, which she respected.

"No," he said finally. "But I'm one vote of seven."

"One vote," Yara repeated softly. "In my experience, one vote is where every salvation and every damnation begins."

* * *

She saw the riders two mornings later, from the window of the infirmary, and her whole body turned to ice water.

They crested the western ridge in a column of twelve, black cloaks dusted grey with road frost, moving with the unhurried discipline of men who had never once needed to hurry. At their head rode a woman on a dun mare — tall, straight-backed, her hair bound back so tightly it seemed to stretch the skin of her face smooth and expressionless. Even at this distance Yara knew her.

Captain Vess.

Yara's hands began to shake before her mind caught up to why. Not the purge-fires. Not the interrogations in the undercroft of the Ashen Keep, though she carried those scars too. Vess specifically. Vess, who had stood in the doorway while her teacher Brenholt burned, and had watched with eyes like winter water, and had said afterward, quietly, to no one in particular: *the gifted always run toward other gifted. It is their nature. It makes them easy.*

Vess had tracked her across four hundred miles of bad country. Of course she had. Yara had run toward other gifted. It was her nature.

"They're stopping at the boundary stones," Sebastian said beside her. She hadn't heard him come in. "They always stop there. They have to."

"Do they?" Yara kept her voice flat. Inside, the animal part of her was screaming — *run, hide, burrow* — and she held it down the way you hold down a lid on a boiling pot. "Look at their captain. Look where she's looking."

Vess had not stopped with her men. She had ridden past the first boundary stone, dismounted, and was standing before it with her head tilted, reading it — or reading whatever her instruments told her about it. One of her soldiers handed her something small and bright. A lodestone, maybe. Or worse.

"She knows the valley is warded," Yara said. "She doesn't know yet that it's warded against *her*. There's a difference, and she'll find it within the day."

* * *

The guild's deception, when Odile unveiled it that evening in the low hall, was so simple it might have worked.

Seven guild members in plain wool, performing the dusk ritual at the western stones exactly as they performed it every dusk — as they had performed it, Yara gathered, for generations. To any observer, the Valeward Guild was what it appeared to be to the whole kingdom: a dying order of ceremonial caretakers, sweeping leaves off old rocks and muttering liturgies no one remembered the meaning of. Nothing to burn. Nothing worth a captain's time.

Yara would be hidden in the cold cellar beneath the hall, behind a false wall, dosed with valerian to keep her gift — her fear, her *presence* — from flickering through the null-field in ways a sensitive tracker might read.

"You'll be drugged and buried," Tomas said to her apologetically, as he showed her down the cellar stairs. He was a big, soft-spoken man with flour on his sleeves even though the baking was long done. "I'm sorry. It's the best we could—"

"It's the best anyone could do," Yara said. "I've been drugged and buried before. It beats the alternative." She managed a smile. "Though if you're hiding me behind a false wall, I'd rather it were behind the bread."

That earned her a real laugh, brief and startled, and she clung to the warmth of it all the way down into the dark.

But lying on the pallet in the false-walled cell, listening to the valerian creep warm and heavy through her blood, Yara found the terror waiting for her anyway. It always did. In the dark, in the silence, the animal mind took over: *they are above you, they are walking the floors above you, they are burning people like you in squares from here to the coast.* Her heart hammered. Sweat prickled along her bound ribs. The null-field pressed down and the valerian pulled her under and between them she dreamed in fragments — Brenholt's fire, the stolen book's pages, a voice saying *the ninth name, the ninth name* — and woke each time not knowing which century she was in.

Once, through the fog, she heard boots on the cellar stairs, and a voice — Vess's voice, cool and unhurried, asking questions in the hall above. Asking about strangers on the road. Asking about *a woman, wounded, seen crossing the ford.*

And Odile's voice answering, steady as bedrock: "We keep the stones, Captain. We see no one. No one sees us."

The lie was beautiful. Yara loved her for it and feared for her with equal measure, and slept at last like a stone in a wall.

* * *

They let her out on the third night, after the riders withdrew to camp beyond the ridge, and it was Sebastian who came for her, hauling at the false panel with his face pale and strange.

"They're not leaving," he said. "Vess sent a rider east two days ago. Odile thinks it's for orders. I think—" He stopped. "Come upstairs. There's something you need to see. Something *we* need you to see."

The guild hall was dim, most of the others already gathered around the long table. On it lay the charter — the sealed charter, the one Yara had bled and burned and killed to carry news of — unrolled and weighted open at its final page.

Yara's breath caught. She had only ever seen a copy, transcribed in a scholar's hurried hand. This was the original, and it was worse and more wonderful than the copy: the vellum ancient and soft as cloth, the ninth line of names written in faded brown ink that had once been red.

Eight names. Eight keepers of eight generations, each sworn in blood, each binding themselves to the vessel below the valley — the prison that was not a prison, the null-field that was not a ward but a *wound*, the great sealed heart of the old war's ending magic, sleeping under their feet since before the kingdom had a name.

And beneath the eighth name, a ninth, fresher, written in a woman's careful hand:

*Sebastian Pemberton. Last keeper. Sworn at birth by Maren Pemberton, that the vessel may sleep past her son's years, or wake by his word alone.*

"That's my mother's writing," Sebastian said. His voice sounded like someone speaking from the bottom of a well. "I never knew her handwriting. I never knew her at all."

Yara touched the edge of the page, gently, the way you touch a wound. "Then hear the rest of it," she said, "because Vess has already guessed half, and Aldric will guess the rest. The vessel is waking. I felt it crossing the mountains — the null-field stuttering, thinning, like a sleeper turning toward morning. It isn't failing because the wards are old. It's failing because it was never meant to sleep forever. Your oath was a lullaby, Pemberton, and lullabies end."

"And the ninth name?" Odile asked quietly, though Yara saw in her eyes that she had known — had perhaps always known — and had carried that knowledge the way you carry a weight too heavy to set down.

"The ninth name is the hinge." Yara looked at Sebastian, and made herself say it plainly, because he deserved plainness and because plainness was all she had left to give. "A Pemberton blood-oath can seal the vessel again — another generation, maybe two of sleep, bought with your life's work and your children's inheritance. Or it can free it. Open the wound and let the old magic back into the world — all of it, everywhere, for everyone. Including for Aldric, if he reaches it first and finds a Pemberton to force."

The silence that followed was total. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines, and far off on the ridge, faint as a thought, a hunting horn sounded once and fell silent.

Sebastian stared down at the name his mother had written, at the word *alone*, and Yara watched him understand — really understand — that the valley he had tended all his life was a lock, and he was the key, and both of them had been built by someone who loved him enough to decide his life's shape before he drew his first breath.

"The horn," Tomas said into the quiet. "Did anyone else hear—"

It sounded again. Closer. Twice now, and answered by a third from the eastern ridge, where no rider should have been.

Odile rose from the table, and her face in the lamplight was the face of a woman counting her people and finding the number too small. "They're not waiting for orders," she said. "They're closing the ring. Everyone to your stations. We ride for the east road at dawn — or we don't ride at all."
