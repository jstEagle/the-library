## 6. The Breaking of the Vale

### Vess

Captain Ilse Vess had burned forty-one mages in the king's name, and she had learned long ago that fire was the least of her instruments. Fear traveled faster than flame. So did hunger, and rumor, and the particular desperation of people who believed themselves hidden.

The valley hid itself well. That was the trouble.

From the ridge above the Valeward border, she studied it through her spyglass: a green cup of farmland cradled in grey stone, smoke rising from honest chimneys, sheep dotting the lower pastures like spilled rice. Too peaceful. Peace, in the third month of Aldric's purge, was a confession.

"There," said her tracker, a lean man called Dunmore with a nose for residue the way hounds had a nose for blood. He pointed to a line of standing stones that wound along the valley's throat like a crooked fence. "The wards run between them. Old work. Older than the kingdom."

"Can you breach it?"

Dunmore's mouth twisted. "Not break it, Captain. But a wall is only as strong as its foundation, and foundations can be *asked* things." He drew a knife of black iron and knelt at the nearest stone, pressing the blade's point into the earth. "Null-fields don't push magic away. They drink it. All we have to do is give it more than it can swallow."

Vess watched him begin his ugly arithmetic — blood, iron, borrowed power from the caged charms at her belt — and felt the old cold satisfaction settle over her like armor. Somewhere in that valley was a woman named Yara, wounded, hunted, carrying documents the king wanted ash. And somewhere in that valley, if the archives were honest, was something older than documents.

"Captain," Dunmore said quietly, sweat beading on his brow despite the chill. "This ward isn't keeping people out."

"No?"

"It's keeping something *in*." His voice had gone thin. "There's a well down there, Captain. A deep one. And I think we're about to pull the cork."

Vess lowered her spyglass. Below, the valley lay golden in the late light, utterly unaware.

"Then pull it," she said.

### Sebastian

Sebastian heard the boundary stones before he understood them — a sound like a struck bell heard underwater, rolling up from the valley's throat where the stones ran oldest.

He was in the east pasture with Yara, who could now walk unaided and insisted on walking everywhere, as though her legs needed convincing they belonged to her again. She stopped mid-stride. Her head came up like a deer's.

"That's not supposed to happen," she said.

"What isn't?"

"The stones are *singing*. Null-fields are silent, Sebastian. Silence is the whole point." She grabbed his wrist, and her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "Something is feeding them."

He felt it then — not heard so much as felt, in his teeth, in the scar tissue of his palms where twenty years of ritual polish had worn grooves. The valley's famous stillness, the hush he had always thought of as peace, was trembling. Like a held breath about to break.

They were halfway to the guildhall when the world ended politely, without violence, the way an old man falls asleep.

The null-field failed.

It did not shatter. It *drained*, the way color drains from a sunset, and into the space it left rushed everything it had ever swallowed. Sebastian went to his knees in the clover. Light poured out of the ground itself, pale and gold and green, threads of it rising from the soil like heat-shimmer made visible — and with the light came memory.

His memory.

He saw his mother's face. He had not seen his mother's face in twenty years; the guild had told him she died of fever when he was four, and grief had blurred her into a shape, a voice, a smell of rosemary. But now she stood before him whole and burning, kneeling at the ninth boundary stone with a knife in her hand and his small hand in hers, and she was saying words in a language that predated the kingdom, and the words were:

*"By my blood the door is shut. By my blood the sleeper sleeps. Pemberton after Pemberton, until the last keeper chooses."*

Until the last keeper *chooses.*

"Sebastian." Yara was crouched over him, her own eyes wide and streaming, magic crawling visibly along her arms like lightning trapped under skin. "Breathe. Whatever you're seeing—"

"My mother," he said, and his voice came out cracked down the middle. "She didn't die of fever. She *sealed* something. With me watching. I was four years old and I watched her seal it and then—" The memories kept coming, merciless. Guildmasters bending over a child. Odile's face, younger, wet-eyed, murmuring *forgive me* as she pressed her thumb to his forehead. The forgetting settling over him like snowfall. "They took it out of my head. They took *her* out of my head."

Above them, the sky over the eastern ridge lit orange. Not sunset. Fire.

Aldric's witch-hunters had fired the treeline, and the wards that would have smothered such a fire for a thousand years were gone.

### Odile

Guildmaster Odile Vane had spent thirty years preparing for this day, and discovered in the first minute of it that preparation was worthless against the truth.

The hall was chaos. Maren was screaming at the window — the eastern ridge, the fires, the riders coming down the switchback road two hundred strong. Tomas had Yara by the arm, demanding to know what was happening to the fields, because the wheat beyond the kitchen garden was glowing, every stalk crowned with faint blue light, and the bees were rising in a column of gold. Old Fen had collapsed in his chair with his hands over his ears, weeping, saying a name over and over — *Lira, Lira, Lira* — a name none of them knew, which Odile now understood had been his wife, erased from all their memories when Fen had once tried to leave the valley and been caught at the stones.

And Sebastian stood in the doorway with his mother's dying words in his eyes, looking at Odile the way a man looks at a gallows.

"You knew," he said. Not a question. "You've always known what I am."

"I knew what you were *for*," Odile said. There was no time for the apology that would take a night to say properly, and no version of it that would survive the next hour anyway. "Listen to me, boy. All of you. The field is failing and it will not come back — whatever the hunters did at the rim, they've opened the vessel's cage, and everything this valley buried is coming up like floodwater. In an hour this place will be visible from the moon. Every gifted soul within fifty miles will feel it. So will Aldric's entire army."

"Then we fight," Maren said, and her hand was already on her axe.

"We *lose*," Odile said flatly. "Two hundred trained witch-hunters against seven keepers and a wounded mage. We were never soldiers. We were *wardens.* And the thing we were warding is awake." She looked at Sebastian, and let herself say it plainly at last, because lies had bought them nothing but this hour. "Your mother bound the vessel with a Pemberton oath. You are the last Pemberton. Which means either you re-seal it, or you free it, or you run — and I have spent thirty years reading that charter, and I believe your mother left you a fourth option she wrote in cipher, and I believe Yara knows how to read it."

Every face turned to Yara.

Yara's jaw tightened. "I know where the cipher key is," she said. "In the free cities. Three weeks' ride east."

"Three weeks we don't have," said Tomas.

"Then we'd better start riding," said Yara.

Odile took the guild charter down from behind the warded glass — the glass whose ward had just died with everything else — and pressed it into Sebastian's hands. The vellum was warm. It should not have been warm.

"The Valeward Guild is dissolved," she said, and her voice did not shake, though it wanted to. "Seven members, one fugitive, one horse apiece, and whatever we can carry. East. Now."

"And the valley?" Sebastian asked.

Odile looked out the window at the wheat glowing blue, at the bees ascending like a prayer, at the fires on the ridge, at the home she had given her whole life to and lied her whole life for.

"The valley remembers us now," she said. "That's more than most graves get. *Go.*"

* * *

### Yara

They rode out through the western pasture as the eastern sky burned, and Yara, at the rear, allowed herself one look back.

The valley was unmaking itself into something magnificent. The null-field had drunk magic for six hundred years, and now it was giving all of it back at once: rivers running silver with light, the standing stones humming audibly, wildflowers opening out of season in waves that followed the horses like a blessing. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it was a beacon, and it was a grave, and she could not separate the three.

Beside her, Sebastian rode like a man asleep with his eyes open.

"The memories don't stop," he said, without preamble. "Do they? Once they come back."

"No. They settle. Eventually they stop feeling like drowning and start feeling like weather." She nudged her horse closer. "I'm sorry. What they did to you — erasure is a violence, Sebastian. The guild told themselves it was mercy."

"Was it?" He turned to look at her, and in the strange light his eyes had flecks of gold in them that had not been there a week ago. "My mother sealed a prison with her life to keep me safe. They erased her to keep me *small*. Those aren't the same thing, are they?"

"No," Yara said. "They aren't."

Behind them, a horn sounded from the ridge — three notes, repeated, the hunting-call of Aldric's witch-finders. Then a fourth note, wrong, off-key, and Yara's stomach dropped, because she knew that signal too. It meant the hunters had found the valley empty and the trail leading west-to-east, toward the free cities, and it meant Captain Vess had not burned the guildhall after all.

Vess never burned what she could follow.

"Tomas!" Yara shouted forward. "Riders on our back trail! Two miles, maybe less!"

Tomas wheeled his horse, squinted back down the valley road, and grinned the terrible grin he got when a plan was forming that would cost someone dearly. "Then we split the trail," he called back. "Maren, Odile — take the river fork south. Make noise. Be loud. Sebastian, Yara, Fen — you're with me on the high road east."

"You're not coming with us?" Sebastian's voice cracked on it.

Tomas clapped him on the shoulder as the horses bunched together at the fork, breath steaming, the valley glowing behind them like a lamp being carried into a dark country. "Someone has to make sure the story ends with all of us in it," he said. "Go east, little brother. Don't look back until the mountains. And Sebastian—" his grin faltered, just for a heartbeat, into something rawer, truer, "—whatever your mother started, finish it *your* way. Not theirs. Yours."

Then he wheeled south with Maren and Odile, and their hoofbeats faded toward the river, loud and deliberate, a drumbeat laid down for hunters to follow.

Yara urged her horse alongside Sebastian's. Ahead, the high road climbed toward the mountain pass and the free cities beyond, and the first stars were coming out over peaks that had never once been inside a ward.

"They'll follow Tomas," Sebastian said hoarsely. "They'll catch him."

"Yes," Yara said, because lying to him now would have been a second erasure, and she would not be party to that. "So we'd better get where we're going fast enough to matter. Ride."

They rode. Behind them, the Valley That Forgets blazed with everything it had remembered, and ahead, in the dark, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep — and, for the first time in six hundred years, dreamed of waking.
