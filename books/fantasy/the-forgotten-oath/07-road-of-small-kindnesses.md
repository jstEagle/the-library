## 7. Road of Small Kindnesses

Tomas had always believed that a person's true character showed in how they ate when they were afraid. Fear made some people greedy — hands snatching, eyes measuring the shares. It made others generous in a desperate, reckless way, giving away food they could not spare because generosity was the last thing they still controlled.

He watched the six of them around the fire and learned them all over again.

They had been four days out of the valley, east along the old drover's road that wound through the Ashfell hills like a gray thread someone had dropped and never picked up again. Behind them, the Vale burned its slow green burn as the null-field died; ahead of them lay two hundred miles of Aldric's kingdom and, somewhere past it, the free cities with their salt-wind walls and their stubborn refusal to kneel.

Odile sat apart from the fire, as she always did now, mending a strap that did not need mending. The guildmaster's hands were steady but her eyes kept going to the dark southern sky, where no rider should have been visible yet and where Tomas, watching her watch, understood that she saw one anyway.

Yara slept closest to the flames, wrapped in Sebastian's cloak, her breathing still wet and labored. The witch-hunter's blade had gone deep under her ribs before they'd pulled her from the stones, and though her own magic had knit most of the damage, magic spent healing left a body hollow as a drum.

And Sebastian — Sebastian was sitting cross-legged across the fire from Tomas, holding a loaf of bread like it was a holy relic.

"Go on," Tomas said. "Eat it. It won't judge you."

"It's the first thing I've baked since—" Sebastian stopped. Turned the loaf over. "Since everything."

"Then eat it for everything." Tomas tore off his own piece and chewed. The bread was dense, a little burnt at the base, faintly sweet from the wild honey Odile had traded for at the last farmstead. It was, without competition, the best bread he had ever tasted, and he told Sebastian so.

"You're lying."

"I'm a liar about many things," Tomas said, "but not bread. Bread is sacred to me. Ask anyone."

Nobody asked anyone. But Wren, curled against Brenna's shoulder with her bow across her knees, smiled into the firelight, and Brenna laughed — actually laughed, a short startled sound like she'd surprised herself — and for a moment the circle around the fire was warm in a way that had nothing to do with coals.

That was the thing about ruin, Tomas thought. It stripped a family down to its bones, and if you were lucky, you found the bones were good ones.

* * *

The maps were the morning's war.

They spread them on a flat stone by the roadside: three charts of overlapping age and disagreeing opinion, inked by cartographers who had apparently never met the same river twice. The old drover's road bent south toward the ferry at Marlow's Crossing. The pilgrim track climbed north through the hills toward the stone bridge at Vael Ford. Between them lay forty miles of forest with nothing but deer paths and, according to Wren, an excellent chance of getting thoroughly lost.

"Ferry," said Brenna. "We're not mountain goats. Yara can't climb."

"Bridge," said Wren. "Ferries keep records. Records get read. I'd rather be a tired goat than a caught one."

"The bridge is watched," Odile said quietly, and everyone went quiet, because Odile only spoke quietly now, as if loudness might carry all the way back to the valley rim and the woman hunting them there. "Vess will assume we go north. She'll put riders on the high ground. We take the ferry, and we pay the ferryman to forget us."

"And if she assumes we assume she assumes—"

"Wren." Odile's voice stayed soft. "Ferry."

Tomas let them argue. He liked listening to them argue. There was a particular music in found people fighting over a map — the bickering of people who had chosen each other and therefore could not stop choosing, even now, even bleeding, even hunted. In the guildhall back home they had argued too, but those arguments had been about ritual timings and the proper angle of candle-smoke, ceremonies performed for a border no one remembered. These arguments were about survival. They had weight. They meant something.

Sebastian stood at the edge of the argument, holding the maps' corner down against the wind, and Tomas watched him watch the others with an expression Tomas had never seen on him before the valley broke: hunger. Not for food. For the future. For the idea that there might be one.

* * *

It happened that evening, in a clearing where the trees opened onto a meadow gone gold with late summer, and Tomas would remember it for the rest of his life — however long that turned out to be.

Yara was well enough to sit up, which meant she was well enough to teach.

"Hold out your hand," she said. "No — flat. Like you're catching rain."

Sebastian held out his hand. His face wore the expression of a man catching rain for the first time in a country where rain had been declared illegal.

"You've felt it already," Yara said. "Don't lie to me. The night the wards failed — when the memories came back — you felt something move through you. Something that wasn't memory."

Tomas saw Sebastian swallow. "Like a door opening in my chest."

"That door was locked your whole life. The valley locked it. Your mother helped lock it." Yara's voice gentled. "She wasn't punishing you, Sebastian. She was hiding you. There's a difference, and someday you'll understand how large that difference is." She placed her palm beneath his, not touching. "Now. Don't reach for anything. Don't *want* anything. Wanting is grabbing, and magic doesn't like being grabbed. Just notice. Notice the warmth in your hand. Notice that the air above your palm is a little bit warmer than the air beside it."

A long silence. Crickets. Somewhere a nightjar calling its two-note question.

"I feel—" Sebastian frowned. "Nothing. I feel nothing, I'm sorry, maybe the valley took it all, maybe I'm just—"

The grass around his knees stirred.

Not wind-stirred. Stirred outward, in a ring, blades leaning away from him like courtiers bowing, and above his open palm the air shimmered — barely, the way heat shimmers over a summer road — and then a spark. One small orange spark, no bigger than a firefly, turning slowly in the air above his hand.

Sebastian made a sound Tomas had never heard from him. Half laugh, half sob.

"Don't grab it," Yara whispered. "Just let it know you."

The spark drifted down and settled in his palm like a tame bird, glowed once, and went out. Sebastian stared at his empty hand as though it belonged to a stranger — a better stranger, a stranger with a future.

Tomas found his eyes burning and blamed the woodsmoke. Around the fire, Wren had both hands pressed over her mouth. Brenna was grinning like a girl. Even Odile had come close, drawn despite herself, and the look on her face was grief and pride braided so tightly together you couldn't tell which strand was which.

"Well," Tomas said, because someone had to say something and it was clearly going to be him. "When we get to the free cities, you're baking every loaf with your mind. I want floating bread. I want bread that arrives pre-buttered."

The laughter that went around the circle was ragged and real, and Tomas thought: *this*. Whatever they lost behind them, whatever waited ahead — this was worth the road.

* * *

On the sixth day, Tomas slipped away from camp before dawn.

He told himself it was to check the back-trail, which was even true, mostly. He took the pony — poor patient Bramble, who deserved better than all of this — and rode south along their own tracks until the light came up gray and thin, and then he did what he had been turning over in his mind for two days.

At the fork where the drover's road met the shepherd's path, he dismounted. He took off his boots — his good boots, the ones Odile had re-soled herself — and walked in his stockinged feet up the muddy slope, dragging his heel, making a trail plain enough for a child to follow. South. Toward Marlow's Crossing, away from the route they had actually chosen: north, through the hills, the way Wren had wanted after all, because Odile had changed her mind in the night and told no one but the map.

At the top of the slope he turned Bramble around and led her back down on the far side, over bare stone where hooves left nothing, and circled wide through the trees to rejoin the road.

Anyone following the false trail would find fresh bootprints and pony tracks heading for the ferry. Anyone following them would find a man waiting alone at the crossing, buying passage for one, telling any rider in a red cloak exactly what a frightened fool would tell them: *they went south, they took the boat, I paid double to get across first.*

They would find Tomas.

He had done the arithmetic on the ride out, the cold honest arithmetic he was good at and hated. Someone had to be the bait. Wren was their eyes; Brenna was their shield; Odile was their spine; Yara was the secret itself; Sebastian was — Tomas didn't have a word yet for what Sebastian was becoming, but it was certainly not expendable. That left the liar. The one whose gift was a quick tongue and a quick exit and knowing which of those to use.

He could do this. He had lied to tax collectors, to press-gangs, to a customs officer in Corvane with a crossbow and a bad attitude. He could lie to a witch-hunter.

He just had to believe it harder than the fear.

* * *

He heard the horses an hour past noon.

Four of them, coming up the ferry road at a trot, and Tomas made himself keep walking — unhurried, a traveler stretching his legs while his horse drank — until they crested the rise and he saw the red cloaks and the gray steel beneath them.

The woman at their head reined in ten paces away. Captain Vess. He had glimpsed her once, far off, on the ridge above the valley, and distance had made her merely a shape. Up close she was worse: a narrow, patient face, eyes the color of a frozen pond, the kind of eyes that weighed a man the way a butcher weighs meat and arrived at the same number.

"Tomas Ferrow," she said.

His name in her mouth went through him like a fishhook. They knew him. Of course they knew him — the guild rolls, the valley records, whatever informants Aldric's people had cultivated in the market towns. Knowing your name was the first tool of every hunter.

"That's me," he said, and gave her the smile he used on customs officers. "Am I in trouble, Captain? I'm just crossing to see my sister in Corvane. Well — not my sister. My sister's husband's cousin. It's complicated. Families usually are."

"Where are your companions?"

"What companions? It's just me and Bramble, and Bramble doesn't talk much."

Vess looked at him for a long time. Long enough for the birds to start up again in the riverside alders. Then she swung down from her horse, walked to the mud at the roadside, and crouched beside the bootprints he had planted that morning.

"Stockinged feet," she said. "You walked uphill in stockinged feet to make these prints, and then you fetched your boots back. Clever. Sloppy at the top of the rise, where the dew hadn't dried — but clever." She rose, brushing mud from her fingers, and looked at him with something that was almost, horribly, respect. "You sent us a false trail, Master Ferrow. Which means the truth runs the other way. North. The hills."

Tomas's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"But," Vess continued, mounting again, gathering her reins, "a lie is also information, and you are a very committed liar, which tells me the people you're protecting matter a great deal to you. So here is what happens next. You're going to ride with us. You're going to think, very carefully, about whether your life is worth more than their secret." The frozen-pond eyes held his. "And if you decide it isn't — well. The king pays for names, Master Ferrow. He pays very well."

The soldiers closed around him. Somewhere north, beyond the hills, beyond the gold meadow where a boy had held a spark in his open hand, his family was walking unknowing into the path of four riders — and Tomas Ferrow, tied at the wrists and smiling his customs-officer smile, began searching frantically for the lie that would save them all.
